DISCERN: inferring drug sensitivity from single-cell transcriptomes using cell-type-specific genetic interaction networks
Authors: Mingyue Liu, Yu Tian, Yuchao Jia, Jiewei Zhang, Xi Yi, Shaocong Sang, Nan Zhang, Kaidong Liu, Yunyi Peng, Yuncong Wang, Xin Li, Bo Chen, Haihai Liang, Yunyan Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGenetic interactions, including synthetic lethality (SL) and synthetic viability (SV), are crucial for understanding tumor-specific vulnerabilities and mechanisms of drug resistance. However, predicting drug response at single-cell resolution based on SL and SV remains challenging.MethodsHere, we construct a large-scale atlas of cell-type-specific SL and SV networks across 14 human cancers using scRNA-seq datasets. Based on this atlas, we develop DISCERN (Drug response Inference from Single-Cell gEnetic inteRactioNs), a novel computational framework designed to infer single-cell drug sensitivity by utilizing malignant cell-specific genetic interactions. We also establish CellGIdb, an interactive portal that provides the cell-type-specific genetic interaction networks.ResultsWe reconstruct cell-type-specific genetic interaction networks across cancers, revealing both shared and distinct patterns among cell types. Notably, SL and SV interactions derived from malignant cells and T cells exhibit prognostic value and correlate with response to immunotherapy. DISCERN effectively infers tumor cell-specific drug sensitivity in scRNA-seq datasets from lung and breast cancers. DISCERN demonstrates improved predictive performance compared with existing computational methods. CellGIdb provides user-friendly analytical tools to facilitate the exploration of genetic interactions’ roles in drug response and immunotherapy.ConclusionsCollectively, this study provides a comprehensive atlas and a novel computational framework, DISCERN, for interpreting drug responses in the context of genetic interactions at single-cell resolution. The publicly available CellGIdb (https://biodata.hrbmu.edu.cn/CellGIdb/index.html) resource will support further exploration of cell-type-specific vulnerabilities in cancer therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13073-026-01688-w.

Background

Genetic interactions, including synthetic lethality (SL) and synthetic viability (SV), refer to a phenomenon where the combination alterations of two genes produce an effect not observed when either gene is altered individually [1, 2]. SL refers to a lethal outcome resulting from the co-occurrence of specific genetic alterations [3, 4]. SL provides a new avenue to explore therapeutic targets and biomarkers that are sensitive to drug response in cancer [5, 6]. SV, defined as the combination of gene alterations that can rescue the lethal effects of a single gene alteration, has recently been documented to play roles in drug resistance response [7, 8].

Currently, approaches for identifying genetic interactions primarily rely on bulk transcriptomic data and CRISPR/Cas9 screening. For example, the SELECT framework predicts SL interactions by integrating drug sensitivity data with multi-omics tumor profiles [9]. Similarly, SPAGE-finder utilizes patient expression and survival data to identify SL interactions across cancers [10]. However, these approaches average gene expression across diverse cell types, thereby obscuring cell-type-specific effects. The diversity of cellular states and compositions within the tumor microenvironment complicates the identification of cell-type-specific genetic interactions. Several computational tools, including CS-CORE and MAST, have been developed to identify gene-gene co-expression relationships from single-cell data [11, 12]. Current approaches for predicting drug response often focus on established sensitivity markers, and may potentially overlook critical associations between genetic interactions and drug effects [13]. Specifically, SL is generally associated with drug sensitivity, whereas SV contributes to drug resistance [14]. Despite their importance, the systematic analysis of SL and SV interactions at single-cell resolution remains limited. Therefore, reconstructing SL and SV networks using scRNA-seq data enables the precise mapping of these genetic interactions within their specific cellular context, offering a resolution unattainable with bulk expression data.

Predicting drug response at single-cell resolution is essential for dissecting tumor complexity and revealing distinct responses across different cell clones [15, 16]. Nevertheless, the development of such predictive models is hindered owing to the limited availability of large-scale preclinical or clinical training datasets [17]. Recent computational tools, including DREEP, Beyondcell, and PERCEPTION, have attempted to address this challenge by leveraging scRNA-seq data and pharmacogenomic resources [18–20]. However, these methods often require complex modeling and extensive parameter tuning, limiting their practical applicability. Furthermore, a significant gap remains in the integration of SL and SV interactions for predicting drug efficacy at single-cell resolution.

To address these above challenges, we curated a large-scale single-cell atlas to systematically reconstruct cell-type-specific SL and SV networks across human cancers (Figure S1A). We then developed DISCERN (Drug response Inference from Single-Cell gEnetic inteRactioNs), a computational framework that integrates malignant cell-specific SL and SV networks to infer single-cell drug responses (Figure S1B). To facilitate data accessibility and analysis, we further established CellGIdb (https://biodata.hrbmu.edu.cn/CellGIdb/index.html), an interactive database for exploring genetic interaction networks across cell types (Figure S1C).

Methods

Data sources

SL and SV gene pairs were downloaded from CGIdb 2.0 [21]. Cancer-related and hallmark-related genes were obtained from the Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer [22], whereas hallmark pathway-related gene sets were retrieved from the Molecular Signatures Database [23]. We collected 235 preclinical SL interactions from Schäffer et al. and 220 high-confidence SV gene pairs from our previously published work [6, 22].

ScRNA-seq data for tissues were collected from Gene Expression Omnibus (Table S1). Single-cell data for cell lines treated with drugs at different time points are presented in Table S2.

Drug-response profiles for cancer cell lines were integrated from the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) [23], Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) [24] and PRISM [25]. We used the area under the dose-response curve (AUC) as the response value for each compound and interpreted smaller AUC values as greater sensitivity. Dataset information, including the number of drugs and cell lines, was summarized in Table S3.

Genome-scale CRISPR-Cas9 dependency profiles were retrieved from Dependency Map (DepMap) release 21Q4 [26]. These fitness scores measure the effect of gene perturbation on cell proliferation, where increasingly negative values correspond to more reductions in cell viability and thus indicate stronger loss-of-fitness phenotypes. These data were incorporated into the filtering of SL and SV interactions [27]. The anti-tumor immune gene set was sourced from the Immune-related CRISPR screen Analyzer of Functional Targets (ICRAFT) database [28].

For immunotherapy-related analyses, we collected transcriptomic profiles and matched clinical response information from ICI-treated cohorts. Cohort source, tumor type, and sample number are listed in Table S4. Clinical benefit was defined as complete response (CR), partial response (PR) or stable disease (SD), whereas progressive disease (PD) was considered non-response. Bulk RNA-seq expression and survival data across 32 cancer types were obtained from TCGA via UCSC Xena [29] and cBioPortal [30].

Single-cell data processing and cell type annotation

All single-cell expression matrices were analyzed in Seurat version 5.4.0 [31]. Cells failing the quality-control criteria (fewer than 200 detected genes, count exceeding three median absolute deviations, or mitochondrial gene fraction above 20%) were removed before normalization. The retained expression counts were normalized using the “NormalizeData” function.

Dimensionality reduction and graph-based clustering were performed, and clusters were assigned to major cell types according to the marker genes listed in Table S5. To distinguish malignant from epithelial cells, we generated large-scale copy number variation (CNV) profiles at single-cell resolution by applying inferCNV version 1.10.0 with default thresholds, using T cells as the reference population [32]. Next, cells were clustered into seven groups according to the consistency of their CNV scores. Clusters of epithelial cells with T cells were classified as normal, while the remaining epithelial cells were classified as malignant.

Identification of cell-type-specific SL and SV networks

We employed a five-step screening process to identify cell-type-specific SL and SV networks. (i) For a given cancer-type dataset, we applied “inferCNV” to identify malignant epithelial cells (Figure S2A). Distinct cell types were annotated using curated marker gene sets. (ii) Differentially upregulated genes were identified for each cell type with average log2 fold-change > 0.5 (Figure S2B). (iii) We filtered the SL and SV gene pairs from CGIdb 2.0 to keep only those gene pairs containing at least one cell-type-specific upregulated gene (Figure S2C). (iv) To identify co-expressed SL and SV gene pairs, we first benchmarked four co-expression inference methods, including Pearson correlation, CS-CORE [11], propR [33], and Dozer [34]. For each method, we computed a co-expression correlation coefficient for every gene pair. True positive pairs were defined as those SL or SV gene pairs that were identified across multiple sources. We then evaluated the ability of each method’s correlation coefficient to discriminate true positive from other pairs using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and recall. PropR, which achieved the best performance, was selected for subsequent cell-type-specific co-expression analysis. Using propR, co-expressed SL and SV gene pairs were identified at a significance threshold of P < 0.05 and a coefficient greater than 0.3 (Figure S2D). (v) CRISPR-Cas9 fitness scores from DepMap were used to filter SL and SV gene pairs in malignant epithelial cells. For a given cancer type, we retained an SL pair only if its combined perturbation led to a lower fitness score in cell lines; conversely, an SV pair was retained only if it was associated with a higher fitness score. Detailed criteria are provided in the Supplementary Methods. We extracted anti-tumor immune-related SL and SV gene pairs through ICRAFT (Figure S2E) [35].

Topological specificity and edge similarity evaluation

In each network, the weight of an edge was defined as the co-expression correlation coefficient between the two genes it connects. Topological specificity was calculated to quantify a gene’s influence within a network, following a previous study [33]. For each cell-type-specific SL/SV network, we measured gene centrality w(i), defined as the sum of interaction weights over all neighbors directly connected to gene “i”. Next, we generated a null distribution for each gene by randomly permuting the edge weights across the network, while leaving the underlying topology unchanged. The topological specificity score for gene “i” was then computed as topS(i) by comparing the observed value with the mean µR(i) and standard deviation σR(i) of the randomized distribution, as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$topS(i)=\frac{w(i)-\mu R(i)}{\sigma R(i)}$$\end{document}

Pairwise network similarity was evaluated from two complementary perspectives, edge-level and node-level similarity, following a previous study [33]. For edge-level similarity, we calculated the Spearman correlation coefficient between the interaction weights of edges that were present in both networks. For node-level similarity, we computed the Spearman correlation coefficient between the topological specificity scores of genes shared across the two networks.

The DISCERN framework

DISCERN (Drug response Inference from Single-Cell gEnetic inteRactioNs) was a computational tool that integrated SL and SV interactions from malignant epithelial cells to predict the drug sensitivity of individual cells. The DISCERN workflow comprises three steps:

Step 1: Data collection

SL and SV gene pairs derived from malignant epithelial cells were collected, along with drug efficacy AUC values and gene expression profiles from cancer cell lines (Table S3, Figure S3A).

Step 2: Model training

First, alteration profiles of SL and SV interactions were constructed. An SL or SV pair was considered as an alteration when both genes were simultaneously activated or inactivated. Subsequently, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to identify sensitivity-associated SLs and resistance-associated SVs (details in Supplementary Methods). Cell lines harboring altered SLs exhibited significantly lower AUC values, whereas those with altered SVs displayed higher AUC values. For each drug, we generated collections of SL and SV gene pairs and integrated them to construct the DISCERN Drug Score (DDS) for predicting single-cell drug sensitivity. The DDS was calculated as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$DDS=\frac{\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^M\delta_{SL_i}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^N\delta_{SV_j}}G$$\end{document}

The DDS represents the fraction of altered SL interactions and reversed SV interactions associated with drug sensitivity. Here, “G” represents the total number of gene pairs related to drug response, “M” represents the number of SL gene pairs associated with drug sensitivity, and “N” represents the number of SV gene pairs associated with drug resistance. Binary indicators “\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta_{SL_i}$$\end{document}”and “\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta_{SV_j}$$\end{document}” represent the status of the “i”-th SL and “j”-th SV gene pair, respectively, where “1” denotes an SL alteration or SV reversal, and “0” denotes no alteration. The criteria for defining SL alterations and reversed SV status were provided in the Supplementary Methods (Figure S3B).

Step 3: Model validation

The predictive performance of the DDS was validated using the SCP542 dataset, which comprises single-cell data from 198 cell lines. For each cell line, the DDS was defined as the median of the single-cell DDS values across all cells within that cell line. Drug-sensitive cell lines were defined as those with AUC values in the lower tertile across all cell lines. Additionally, single-cell data collected at multiple time points after treatment were employed for further validation (Table S2). In this validation, cells collected on day 0 of drug treatment were defined as the true-positive group (i.e., sensitive cells) [18]. Predictive performance was evaluated by the AUROC using the pROC version 1.19.0.1 [34], and group comparisons were performed with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Figure S3C).

Estimation of DISCERN performance

DISCERN’s performance was assessed using drug screening data from CTRP, GDSC, and PRISM. To build the “gold standard” for each drug-effect screening dataset, we defined a cell line sensitive to a drug only if its AUC value was below 1/3 (i.e., if it was in the bottom tertile across specific cancer cell lines in the same dataset). The DDS for a cell line was defined as the median of the scores across all cells within that cell line. Next, cells from day 0 were defined as sensitive, while those from subsequent days were classified as resistant. Finally, AUROC and recall on each drug effect dataset were computed with the “pROC” R package [34].

Implementation of CellGIdb

The CellGIdb web resource (https://biodata.hrbmu.edu.cn/CellGIdb/index.html) is publicly accessible without user registration. We built CellGIdb using a backend comprising Django 5.2 (Python 3.12) and MySQL 8.0.40, running on a Linux operating system. The database stores curated genetic interaction pairs, cell-type annotations, and downstream analysis outcomes. On the front end, we employed jQuery, DataTables, and ECharts to render dynamic tables and interactive plots. Browser compatibility was verified across Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Safari [36].

Analysis of survival and immunotherapy efficacy

The prognostic relevance of SL/SV interaction states was evaluated using univariate Cox proportional hazards models. Patients were grouped according to the presence of SL/SV interaction alterations, and survival outcomes were compared using log-rank statistics, with results visualized by Kaplan-Meier estimates. Gene activation and inactivation states were defined relative to expression distributions within each cancer type. The criteria for defining SL/SV alterations and reversals were described in the Supplementary Methods. Associations between SL/SV interactions derived from non-malignant epithelial cells and immunotherapy response were evaluated using Fisher’s exact test.

Results

Reconstruction of cell-type-specific genetic interaction networks of cancer

We assembled a single-cell transcriptomic atlas comprising 1,086,649 high-quality cells from 289 patients across 10 solid tumor types following stringent quality control. Then, we annotated cells to distinct cell types, while ensuring expression of canonical cell-type marker genes (Figure S4A-J). A total of 15 different cell types were identified in solid tumors (Figure S4K). Malignant epithelial cell counts ranged from 361 in ovary cancer to 6,344 in colorectal cancer (Figure S4L). The malignant epithelial cells identified in this study exhibited substantial overlap with the malignant cell annotations provided by the TISCH2 database (Figure S5) [37].

Utilizing this single-cell atlas across 10 tumor types, we compared four computational methods, CS-CORE [11], Dozer [38], Pearson and propR [39], for reconstructing SL and SV networks specific to malignant epithelial cells. Our evaluation showed that propR yielded higher AUROC (Fig. 1A-B) and recall (Fig. 1C-D) values than other methods. Furthermore, propR identified a larger number of SL and SV gene pairs that were validated by CRISPR/Cas9 screening (Fig. 1E-F, P < 0.05, one-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test). PropR comprised a greater proportion of clinically validated SL and high-confidence SV pairs (Fig. 1G).

In the resulting networks, cancer-related and cancer hallmark genes exhibited significantly higher degrees than other genes in the SL and SV networks (Fig. 1H, P < 0.05, one-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test). Analysis of SL networks across the 10 cancer types revealed that hub genes had significantly lower fitness scores than non-hub genes in malignant epithelial cells (Fig. 1I, P < 0.05, one-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test). Lower gene fitness scores indicate reduced cell viability after gene knockout. Recurrent SL and SV sub-networks across multiple cancer types included key genes such as EGFR, BRCA1, and TP53 (Fig. 1J-K). Taken together, these analyses indicated that malignant epithelial cell-specific SL and SV networks reconstructed by propR were robust and informative for downstream analysis.

We also observed that immune-associated genes had higher degrees than other genes in both SL and SV networks (Figure S6A-B, P < 0.05, one-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test). Additionally, marker genes for T cells and endothelial cells were enriched among high-degree genes in the SL network (Fig. 1L, P < 0.05, one-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test). Thus, these observations suggest that genetic interaction networks in non-malignant cell types within the tumor microenvironment may also be relevant, warranting further investigation.

The commonality and difference among cell-type-specific networks

We applied propR to construct SL and SV networks for T cells, B cells, endothelial cells and fibroblast across various cancers. Malignant cell-specific SL networks were then compared across solid tumor types. The SL networks of malignant cells exhibited variable sizes across cancer types (Fig. 2A). Comparative analysis revealed that only a small fraction of edges and nodes were shared across cancer types (Fig. 2B-C), yet these shared components showed strong correlation (Fig. 2D). Shared nodes were enriched for functional terms related to the MAPK signaling pathway, cellular senescence, and cell cycle (Fig. 2E).

This study revealed that SL interactions are associated with a poor prognosis in lung cancer patients (Fig. 2F). Specifically, patients with alterations to the SL gene pairs CD4-IGFBP3 and LCK-MAPK12 had poorer survival (Fig. 2G-H, P < 0.05, log-rank test). In bladder and breast cancer datasets, alterations in the SL pairs LCK-NOS2 and CD36-PCYOX1L, respectively, were correlated with a better response to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy (Fig. 2I-J, P < 0.05, Fisher’s exact test). Conversely, patients with alterations in SV pairs such as IL12RB1-SPHK1 and LCK-CDC14B were associated with resistance to immunotherapy (Figure S6C-D, P < 0.05, Fisher’s exact test).

Moreover, similar patterns were observed in the T cell-specific SL networks (Figure S7A-D). Glycolysis, central carbon metabolism, and EGFR inhibitor resistance were enriched among nodes in T cell SL networks (Figure S7E). Analysis of hub genes in the SL subnetwork in T cell indicated that TGIF2 participates in multiple SL interactions to modulate patient prognosis (Figure S7F-G). A substantial number of overlapping edges were observed between breast and gastric cancer in T-cell-derived SL and SV networks (Figure S8). Analysis of node overlap indicated that the edges of the endothelial cell SV network exhibit a relatively high degree of overlap across different cancer types (Figure S9). Furthermore, we examined whether SL-related genes mediate ligand-receptor interactions across distinct cell populations. Interestingly, the SL gene SDC4 in malignant epithelial cells and the SL gene COL1A1 in fibroblasts were found to engage in ligand-receptor pairing across multiple datasets (Figure S10A-D). Additionally, we observed receptor-ligand interactions between the SL gene HLA-DRB1 in malignant epithelial cells and the SL gene CD4 in macrophages (Figure S10E-G), as well as between malignant epithelial cells and mast cells (Figure S10H). Overall, our findings imply the potential for deconvolving tumor prognostic SL into specific cell types, and for identifying key drug effect signatures within these cell-type-specific networks.

DISCERN predicts drug sensitivity in single-cell datasets of lung cancer

Here, we proposed the DISCERN framework to predict drug sensitivity at the single-cell level. DISCERN integrated drug effects and bulk gene expression data of cancer cell lines from CCLE [40], GDSC and PRISM datasets to train SL and SV. DISCERN identified SL associated with sensitivity and SV associated with resistance. SL and SV were subsequently integrated to compute a DDS. Then, single-cell expression data were used to validate the predictive performance of DDS. To validate DISCERN, we applied it to the SCP542 dataset, which comprises 12,842 single-cell transcriptomic profiles from 34 lung cancer cell lines [41] (Figure S11A). DISCERN generated predictions for a total of 892 drugs using DDS, comprising 219 from CTRP, 115 from GDSC1, 65 from GDSC2 and 493 from PRISM datasets (Figure S11B). Analysis by mechanism of action (MoA) showed that inhibitors constituted the largest class of effective drugs across datasets (Fig. 3A), with HDAC and EGFR inhibitors being most prevalent (Fig. 3B). Analysis of EGFR inhibitor targets revealed that the target genes of erlotinib and afatinib were present in the corresponding drug-associated SL/SV networks (Figure S12). Furthermore, we evaluated the concordance between the SL/SV genes and the MoA pathways delineated by Carli and colleagues [13]. Our findings indicate that SL/SV network genes are involved in a broad spectrum of MoA-related pathways (Figure S12). DISCERN yielded an AUC of 0.7 in predicting erlotinib response across lung cell lines (Fig. 3C). Among the 31 cell lines in the SCP542, 15 were classified as erlotinib-sensitive (Fig. 3D), and these exhibited significantly higher DDS than resistant lines (Fig. 3E).

We next assessed DISCERN’s ability to recognize adaptation to drug treatment over time using the GSE149214 dataset [42], which profiles 7,440 cells from a PC9 cell line-derived clone at baseline (Day 0) and after 11 days of erlotinib exposure (Fig. 3F). DDS was significantly lower at Day11 compared to Day0 (Fig. 3G-H, P < 0.05, one-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test), suggesting a transcriptomic shift consistent with the development of an erlotinib-resistant state.

Similar findings were observed in the GSE134839 and GSE134841 datasets [42], wherein DDS was correlated with the development of erlotinib resistance across different treatment time points (Fig. 3I-L, Figure S11C-D, one-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test). Additionally, a time-dependent decrease in DDS was also observed in osimertinib-treated cells (Fig. 3M-O, one-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test). Furthermore, DDS showed high predictive performance for multiple drugs at the single-cell level, with AUROC values ranging from 0.88 to 0.99 (Fig. 3P). Together, these results support the ability of DISCERN to capture drug sensitivity and its temporal dynamics in single-cell data.

Benchmarking DISCERN versus state-of-the-art methods

To further evaluate the performance of DISCERN in an additional cancer type, we applied it to a single-cell breast cancer dataset (SCP542), which comprises 3,285 cells derived from 24 breast cancer cell lines. Among these, 15 cell lines possessed gold-standard labels for drug sensitivity (Fig. 4A). We selected 269 drugs for which sensitivity was predicted using the DDS in breast cells, comprising 66 from CTRP, 58 from GDSC1, 34 from GDSC2 and 264 from PRISM datasets (Figure S11E). Inhibitors represented the most abundant drug class across all four databases (Figure S11F), with topoisomerase and PI3K inhibitors constituting the majority (Fig. 4B). At the cell line level, DDS achieved AUROC values of 0.92 and 0.87 in predicting sensitivity to afatinib and bortezomib, respectively (Fig. 4C).

We also observed that bortezomib-sensitive breast cancer cell lines in SCP542 exhibited higher DDS scores (Fig. 4D-E). Moreover, the DDS for bortezomib progressively decreased as the treatment duration increased (Fig. 4F). Figure S13 showed the SL and SV networks associated with the efficacy of erlotinib in lung cancer and of afatinib and bortezomib in breast cancer. We further validated the predictive efficacy of DDS using the GSE173634 dataset, which comprises 32 breast cancer cell lines, 14 of which contain sensitive labels (Fig. 4G-H). In sensitive cell lines, the DDS for bortezomib was significantly higher (Fig. 4I), and DDS achieved an AUROC of 0.803 in predicting drug sensitivity (Fig. 4J). Additionally, this work revealed that the AUROC values for SL and SV were surpassed by the co-expression networks for predicting sensitivity to erlotinib and bortezomib (Figure S14). Finally, we performed a comparative analysis of DISCERN against DREEP [18] and Beyondcell [19] on both lung and breast cancer datasets. Our analysis showed that DISCERN attained higher predictive accuracy for targeted drugs compared to DREEP [18] and Beyondcell [19] across multiple datasets (Fig. 4K-L). Collectively, these results support the potential of DISCERN to predict drug sensitivity at single-cell resolution across diverse cancer types and suggest its favorable performance relative to existing benchmark methods.

CellGIdb: Cell-type-specific genetic interactions database

We developed CellGIdb, a data portal for querying and visualizing cell-type-specific SL and SV networks. CellGIdb contains 93,473 SL and 14,038 SV from the CGIdb 2.0 [21], 1,086,649 cells from 42 single-cell datasets across 14 cancer types, and includes information on 2,693 drugs and 726 cancer samples treated with ICIs (Fig. 5A). For each dataset, we performed standardized data processing and quality control. The resulting datasets contained between 14,599 and 171,696 cells each. Major cell types, including malignant cells, T cells, macrophages, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells, were present across all datasets (Figure S4K). Next, we selected cell-type-specific SL and SV networks (Fig. 5B). To facilitate the interpretation of these networks, CellGIdb implemented versatile functional tools. CellGIdb provides six analytical tools, including the “GI-Network”, “GI-Function”, “GI-Hallmarker”, “GI-Survival”, “GI-Drug”, and “GI-Immunotherapy” tools (Fig. 5C).

The web interface of CellGIdb

CellGIdb provides an intuitive interface for data access and interactive visualization. On the “Browse” and “Search” pages, users can flexibly retrieve SL and SV networks by selecting specific cancer types and cell types (Fig. 6A-B). CellGIdb includes six analytical tools for exploring cell-type-specific networks (Fig. 6C). Query results are presented in a summary table, with each row corresponding to the SL and SV networks for a specific cell type. The table lists key attributes, including cell type, cancer type, dataset ID, cell count, the numbers of SL/SV edges and nodes, and counts of genes linked to prognosis or immunotherapy response (Fig. 6D). Each entry in the results table is linked to the corresponding analytical tool.

The “GI-Network” tool provides visualization of SL and SV networks, including the edge weights and node information (Fig. 6E). Discussion

In this study, we reconstructed cell-type-specific SL and SV networks using single-cell transcriptomic data from 14 cancer types. We further developed DISCERN, a novel computational method that integrates SL and SV interactions to predict single-cell drug sensitivity. DISCERN surpasses existing methods in predictive sensitivity across multiple cancer datasets. To support broader research efforts, we built CellGIdb, an interactive portal for exploring genetic interactions linked to presognosis, drug response, and immunotherapy outcomes https://biodata.hrbmu.edu.cn/CellGIdb/index.html.

Previous studies like SELECT [9], ENLIGHT [43], and ISLE [44] have used transcriptomic data to identify SL and SV relationships. Here, we applied a co-expression-based strategy to infer genetic interactions within distinct cell types. Our study evaluated four methods for identifying gene co-expression at the single-cell level and found propR to perform well for predicting both SL and SV interactions. PropR identified SL and SV networks in malignant epithelial cells from ten cancer types, including a recurrent SL between PARP1 and RAD51 (Fig. 1G). This finding recapitulates a known mechanism, as PARP1 and RAD51 are involved in DNA damage repair, and extensive literature has established that their concurrent alteration can induce SL [45]. We also detected recurring SV interactions between the paralogous CDKN2A and CDKN2B (Fig. 1K), which is consistent with studies reporting frequent genetic interactions among paralogs [46, 47].

DISCERN leverages integrated SL and SV networks to compute a DDS to quantify single-cell drug sensitivity. SL interactions are generally associated with drug sensitivity [44], while SV interactions may contribute to drug resistance [48]. Therefore, cells with a higher proportion of altered SL interactions and a lower proportion of reversed SV alterations are expected to be more sensitive to a given drug. Based on this rationale, the DDS was designed to quantify the relative contribution of altered SL and reversed SV interactions among all SL and SV interactions associated with drug response. Accordingly, a higher DDS indicates greater drug sensitivity, whereas a lower DDS suggests drug resistance. Evaluated across multiple lung and breast cancer datasets, DISCERN showed accuracy in predicting sensitivity to HDAC and EGFR inhibitors. In breast cancer, DISCERN predicted sensitivity to bortezomib across the SCP542, GSE114461, and GSE173634 datasets. When benchmarked against Beyondcell [19] and DREEP [18], DISCERN showed improved performance for targeted agents including afatinib and erlotinib. These results suggest that DISCERN can capture drug response heterogeneity from single-cell transcriptomes. Although DISCERN identifies SLs and SVs associated with drug response across multiple cancer cell lines, the single-cell datasets used to validate the predictive performance of DDS were limited to breast and lung cancer. Therefore, the applicability of DDS to other cancer types remains to be further established. In future studies, we plan to include single-cell data from additional cancer types to further assess the predictive performance of DDS. DISCERN is publicly available at https://github.com/liumingyue0820/CellGIdb [49].

Our study has several limitations. Although reference-guided inference improves biological relevance, it may overlook novel cell-type-specific interactions. In addition, the cell-type-specific interactions constructed in this study based on gene expression are not classic mutation-level interactions. Genomic alterations can lead to downstream changes in gene expression. Consistent with this concept, Eytan and colleagues developed computational frameworks, including SELECT [9] and ENLIGHT [43], that utilize bulk transcriptomic profiles to identify SL and SV interactions. In the present study, we inferred interactions from transcriptomic data derived from malignant epithelial cells. Although this approach does not directly establish causal, non-additive relationships in the same way as genomic perturbation screens, it provides a transcriptionally informed strategy for identifying candidate SL and SV interactions. In future work, we will integrate single-cell genomic alteration data to further refine and validate the discovery of SL and SV interactions. Moreover, DISCERN depends on SL and SV derived from pharmacogenomic screens, which are limited in drug and cancer type coverage. Because the drug response database does not include efficacy data for immune checkpoint inhibitors, DISCERN is currently unable to predict immunotherapy efficacy. This represents a limitation of the current study. In future work, we plan to integrate transcriptomic data from patients receiving immunotherapy to develop a single-cell model for predicting immunotherapy response. This may constrain its applicability to novel drug and non-cancerous disease contexts. Spatial transcriptomics preserves spatial organization and enables the investigation of how intercellular interactions influence drug response. Perturbation sequencing can be used to validate causal relationships between genetic perturbations and phenotypic effects. In the future, this work will consider applying DISCERN to predict drug efficacy by employing spatial transcriptomics and perturbation datasets.

Conclusions

In summary, we reconstructed genetic interaction networks at single-cell resolution, enabling systematic comparison of SL and SV patterns across diverse cell types. We developed DISCERN, a computational approach that integrates these interactions to predict single-cell drug sensitivity. Additionally, we built CellGIdb, an interactive portal for exploring genetic interactions in single-cell contexts. Together, these resources suggest that cell-type-specific genetic interactions may refine our understanding of drug response and could provide a useful framework for precision medicine.
