GYPC methylation performance in detecting cervical (pre)cancer in high-risk HPV-positive women using liquid-based cervical scrapes
Authors: Xing Fan, Zhengjiao Tong, Saiping Mao, Fang Yu, Rong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMethylation of host genes is promising for the triage of women with high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) infections. This study aimed to validate the potential value of Glycophorin C (GYPC) methylation (GYPCm) in the early detection of cervical cancer.MethodsWe recruited HR-HPV-positive women at the hospital outpatient clinic between August and December 2023, using cytology triage. The remaining exfoliated cervical cells were subjected to GYPCm testing. A total of 549 cases were finally included for analysis: 156 cervicitis, 303 cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN)1, 49 CIN2, 37 CIN3, and 4 CC.ResultsThe difference of GYPCm ΔCp values between CIN1 and CIN2 was statistically significant in the pathology results of 549 participants (P < 0.001). The optimal threshold value of GYPCm for detecting CIN2 + was 6.35. The GYPCm adjusted ORs for CIN2 + and CIN3 + were 31.23 (95%CI: 16.53–58.99) and 34.68 (95%CI: 11.90–101.11), respectively. In all individuals, the sensitivity of GYPCm for CIN2 + and CIN3 + was consistent with that of cytology (CIN2 + 83.3%; CIN3 + 90.2%), with higher specificity than that of cytology (CIN2 + 85.8 vs. 29.4%; CIN3 + 79.7 vs. 28.7%, all P < 0.001). The sensitivity and negative predictive value (NPV) of HPV16/18 combined GYPCm for CIN3 + were not statistically different from those of HPV combined with cytology (sensitivity 95.1 vs. 97.6%, P = 0.317; NPV 99.4 vs. 99.0%, P = 0.613). The net reclassification improvement for HPV16/18 combined GYPCm and its combined cytology to classify CIN2 + vs. < CIN2 was 48.8% (95%CI: 39.7–57.9%, P < 0.001), with a relative colposcopy referral rate of 50.1% (95%CI: 45.5–54.7%).Conclusion GYPCm has a higher CIN2 + OR and higher specificity and positive predictive value than cytology in HR-HPV-positive women and is a potential molecular biomarker for triaging HR-HPV (+) to detect cervical (pre)cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13027-025-00699-3.

Background

In 2022, more than 661,000 cervical cancer (CC) cases and 348,000 deaths were recorded globally [1], and CC is the fourth most common cause of cancer-related mortality and morbidity in women. The latest statistics show that 111,800 new cases and 61,500 deaths occur annually in China [2]. A number of high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) infection may result in cervical abnormalities that are histologically graded as cervical intraepithelial neoplasia ((CIN), Grades 1–3) or cancer (mostly squamous cell carcinoma) [3]. To further reduce the incidence of CC, primary screening has shifted toward HR-HPV testing [4]. HR-HPV genotypes typically include 14 types (HPV16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, and 68) [3]. HR-HPV testing is a more sensitive screening test than cytology for the diagnosis of high-grade cervical lesions and more effective prevention of invasive CC [5]. However, HR-HPV testing is not as specific as cytology, and most HR-HPV, including HPV16/18, have a transient infection that resolves spontaneously [6]. Consequently, colposcopy referrals and associated treatments are unnecessary [7]. Additionally, a positive HR-HPV result requires multiple rounds of follow-up testing to monitor disease progression [8]. The aforementioned problems caused by HR-HPV testing can be reduced using better triage strategies. Various triage strategies, such as cytology, extended HR-HPV genotyping [9], dual staining (p16/Ki67) [10], and HPV E6/E7 mRNA testing [11], have been proposed. However, the optimal methodology has not been determined.

Recent studies have shown that epigenetic events significantly regulate the HPV life cycle and cervical lesion progression [12]. Silencing of tumor suppressor genes (TSGs) that contribute to cancer development through the local hypermethylation of CpG-rich promoter regions has been widely recognized and strongly demonstrated [13]. The DNA methylation levels of host TSGs increase with CIN severity and are high in CC [14]. DNA methylation tests are being performed to triage patients into colposcopy-driven biopsies or ablative treatments across CC screening clinics worldwide [15]. Glycophorin C (GYPC) is an integral membrane glycoprotein [16]. Previous studies have reported that low-or hypermethylated GYPC may be associated with the development and progression of lung [17], ovarian [18] and uterine cancers [19].

Therefore, we recently developed the GYPC methylation (GYPCm) test, a real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based assay, to assess the levels of methylated alleles in the GYPC gene. In this study, we explore the performance of GYPCm as a triage strategy for detecting CIN2 + and CIN3 + in HR-HPV (+) women who were opportunistically screened as outpatients. A direct comparison was also performed between GYPCm and cytology.

Methods

Study participants and specimen collection

Participants enrolled at the colposcopy clinic of Changsha Maternal and Child Health Hospital who had tested positive for HR-HPV and subsequently underwent biopsy procedures between August 2023 and December 2023 were collected. Figure 1 shows a flowchart of the study design. The inclusion criteria were participants who were ≧ 18 years old and had positive results for the HR-HPV test in 1 month. The exclusion criteria were pregnancy, previous cancer history, previous treatment for CIN or cancer, immunodeficiency, and HPV vaccination history. Furthermore, no cytological or pathological data were excluded from the analysis. A total of 146 women were excluded from the study due to the absence of biopsy procedures, as no lesions or abnormalities were observed during colposcopy, and some patients refused participation. Finally, a total of 550 participants underwent biopsy and pathology; however, one individual was excluded from the analysis due to insufficient methylation data. Consequently, 549 participants were included in the study’s analysis.

The residues of exfoliated cervical cells preserved in a cytopreservation solution (PreservCyt solution) were collected from participants following the cytology examination for the purpose of conducting methylation testing. The DNA extracted from remaining exfoliated cervical cells were stored in a freezer at -80 ℃. This study was approved by the Research and Clinical Trial Ethics Committee of the Changsha Hospital for Maternal & Child Health Care (No. EC-20230726-02), and all patients provided written informed consent.

HR-HPV DNA genotyping test

HR-HPV DNA genotyping was performed using the HPV gene array test kit (21 subtypes; HybriBio Ltd., Guangzhou, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The HPV DNA extracted using Nucleic acid extraction kit (HybriBio Ltd., Guangzhou, China) from the sample was amplified using PCR, and then the DNA amplicons were hybridized to a fixed HPV-specific probe on a HybriMem using HybriBio’s proprietary flow hybridization technology. An enzyme immunoassay was performed for colorimetric identification of the 21 HPV genotypes. Only 14 h-HPV types (HPV 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, and 68) of positive patients were included in the study.

Liquid-based cytology (LBC)

Exfoliated cervical cells were preserved in a cytopreservation solution (PreservCyt solution, Hologic, MA, USA) and processed into thin smears using the ThinPrep cytologic detection system. Cytological sampling was performed at least 5 days after menstruation, and vaginal douching or medication was not permitted 3 days before sampling. Cytological diagnoses were classified as no intraepithelial lesions or malignancy, atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance (ASC-US), low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (LSIL), atypical squamous cells that cannot exclude high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (ASC-H), atypical glandular cells (AGC), high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions, and CC.

GYPC methylation assay

Genomic DNA was extracted from the exfoliated cervical cells. DNA quality and quantity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, DE, USA). The bisulfite conversion of 200 ng of gDNA was performed using a DNA bisulfite conversion kit (Hoomya, Changsha, China). The GYPCm test was performed in a certified DNA laboratory (Hoomya Medical Laboratory, Changsha, China), where the operators and staff were blinded to clinical information, including basic participant information, cytology, HPV genotyping, colposcopy, and pathology results.

GYPCm was assayed by running 50 cycles on the LC480 quantitative PCR system (Roche Applied Science, CA, USA). COL2A1 was used as an internal reference gene, not only as an indicator of the amount of DNA in the samples but also as a baseline for determining the level of GYPCm. The results were considered invalid if the Cp value of COL2A1 (CpCOL2A) was > 35 or undetectable. When CpCOL2A ≤ 35, the difference between the two Cp values was used to determine the methylation level of the GYPC gene (ΔCp = CpGYPC-CpCOL2A1). Lower ΔCp values were associated with higher levels of GYPC methylation and vice versa.

Colposcopy-directed biopsy

All participants underwent colposcopy impression examination, during which 2–4-point colposcopy-directed biopsies were performed on the identified lesions, Additionally, quadrant biopsy of the normal cervix with the patients’ consent. The final diagnostic results were classified as cervicitis, CIN1, CIN2, CIN3, and CC. Two senior associate pathologists independently evaluated the pathological biopsy results. If the two pathology results were inconsistent, a third pathologist was invited for diagnosis.

Statistical analysis

R (version 4.4.1; Vienna, Austria) was used for all the statistical analyses. All differences were considered two-sided and were statistically significant at P < 0.05. Participant characteristics were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Non-normal variables were recorded as medians [Inter-quartile ranges], and count variables were recorded as percentages (n). Violin plot of the distribution of GYPCm in histopathological and cytopathological findings using the ggpubr package, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for the comparison of ΔCp of GYPCm. The significance of GYPCm in distinguishing < CIN2 vs. CIN2 + and < CIN3 vs. CIN3 + was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (pROC package). The GYPCm threshold was selected based on the maximum Youden index (YI), which distinguishes CIN2 + from CIN2. A positive result of the GYPCm assay was determined when ΔCp ≤ 6.35 and a negative result when ΔCp > 6.35. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses with the stats and autoReg packages were used to compute odds ratios (OR).

HPV16/18 combined GYPCm (HPV16/18 or GYPCm) and its combined cytology (HPV16/18 or cytology) are both OR rules, in which the diagnosis is positive if either is positive. Both must be negative for the diagnosis to be negative. The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of the tests for detecting CIN2 + or CIN3 + were calculated, and the CIs were calculated using the Wilson score interval (DescTools package). The differences in sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were assessed using the DTComPair package. The relative colposcopy referral rates were calculated. Net reclassification improvement (NRI) was assessed using the method described by Alba et al. (2017) [20].

Results

Participant characteristics

This was a prospective cross-sectional study. From August to December 2023, 824 women were tested for HR-HPV during the hospital’s outpatient CC screening, with the highest prevalence of HPV52 infection at 33.3% (274). This was followed by HPV16 in 19.3% (159), HPV58 in 14.9% (123), HPV51 in 12.5% (103), and HPV18 in 8.6% (71). Supplementary Table 1 presents the prevalence of other HPV types. LBC was performed on 701 h-HPV-positive patients. A total of 696 patients completed colposcopy, of which 550 underwent colposcopy-directed biopsy pathology. Residual LBC specimens were tested for GYPCm; one (0.2%) specimen was invalid for methylation testing because of an insufficient amount of DNA. A total of 549 cases were included in the analysis. Table 1 presents the clinical characteristics of the individuals. Table 2 presents the prevalence of HPV types in different cervical lesions. HPV 16/18(+) accounted for 21.1% of cases (116). The median age of the individuals was 40.0 [33.0–51.0] years old. Cytologic ASC-US was present in 43.7% of the patients (240). Median ΔCp of GYPCm was 13.4 [6.2–20.3]. There were 156 cases of cervicitis, 303 of CIN1, 49 of CIN2, 37 of CIN3, and four of CC. Figure 1 shows a flowchart of the study.

ORs of GYPCm, cytological for CIN2+, and CIN3+

For CIN2+, the crude ORs (cOR) of HPV16/18, cytology, and GYPCm were 1.90 (95%CI: 1.15–3.15, P = 0.012), 2.08 (95%CI: 1.16–3.76, P = 0.015), and 30.31 (95%CI: 16.41–55.96, P < 0.001), respectively. The adjusted ORs (aOR) of HPV16/18, cytology, and GYPCm were 2.80 (95%CI: 1.41–5.56, P = 0.003), 1.48 (95%CI: 0.71–3.11, P = 0.299), and 31.23 (95%CI: 16.53–58.99, P < 0.001), respectively.

For CIN3+, the cOR of HPV16/18, cytology, and GYPCm were 2.61 (95%CI: 1.34–5.07, P = 0.005), 3.73 (95%CI: 1.31–10.65, P = 0.014), and 36.37 (95%CI: 12.68–104.35, P < 0.001), respectively. The aOR of HPV16/18, cytology, and GYPCm were 3.79 (95%CI: 1.69–8.53, P = 0.001), 3.09 (95%CI: 0.96–9.97, P = 0.059), and 34.68 (95%CI: 11.90–101.11, P < 0.001), respectively (Table 3).

Clinical performance of GYPCm and cytology

Regarding CIN2+, the sensitivity of both GYPCm and cytology in all HR-HPV-positive individuals was 83.3% (95%CI: 74.3–89.6%). The specificity of GYPCm and cytology were 85.8% (95%CI: 82.3–88.7%) and 29.4% (95%CI: 25.4–33.7%), and the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.001). The PPV and NPV of GYPCm were 53.6% (95%CI: 45.3–61.6%) and 96.3% (95%CI: 94.0–97.8%), respectively, which were higher than the PPV (18.8%) and NPV (90.0%) of cytology (Table 4). In analyses grouped by HPV16/18 and age (≥ 30 vs. < 30y) subgroups, there was no statistically significant difference in sensitivity between GYPCm and cytology and between the specificity of GYPCm and cytology (Table 4).

The sensitivity and specificity of HPV16/18 were 31.1% (95%CI: 22.5–41.3%) and 80.8% (95%CI: 77.0–84.2%) for detecting CIN2+, respectively. The sensitivity and specificity of detecting CIN2 + was 92.2% (95%CI: 84.8–96.2%) and 20.3% (95%CI: 16.8–24.2%) for cytology combined with HPV16/18, respectively. The sensitivities of GYPCm and HPV16/18 combined cytology for detecting CIN3 + were 90.2% (95%CI: 77.4–96.1%) and 97.6% (95%CI: 87.4–99.6%), respectively, and the difference between them was not statistically significant (P = 0.083). Improved performance of GYPCm compared with that of cytology

In the all-individuals population, the NRI for GYPCm than for cytology was 56.4% (95%CI: 45.9–66.9%) for differentiating < CIN2 from CIN2+. The colposcopy referral rate for GYPCm was 35.1% (30.6–39.9%) of that for cytology colposcopy. The NRI for HPV16/18 combined with GYPCm compared to that for HPV16/18 combined with cytology was 48.8% (95%CI: 39.7–57.9%), and the relative colposcopy referral rate was 50.1% (95%CI: 45.5–54.7%). In the subgroups based on HPV16/18 and age (≥ 30y vs. < 30y), NRIs were all improved, and colposcopy referral rates were all decreased in GYPCm compared to those in cytology (Table 5). The NRI for GYPCm compared to that for HPV16/18 combined with cytology was 56.7% (95%CI: 45.1–68.3%). The relative colposcopy referral rate was 31.2% (95%CI: 27.9–38.0%). GYPCm missed three CIN3 and one CC case, and HPV16/18 combined cytology missed one CIN3 and 0 CC cases. Supplementary Table 2 presents the improvements in the detection of CIN3+.

Discussion

We reported a cross-sectional assessment based on outpatient CC screening that triaged HR-HPV-positive women using automated molecular testing (GYPCm; HPV16/18 combined with GYPCm). We studied the triage method for HR-HPV-positive women, and the HPV genotypes were inconsistent with those observed in a previous investigation [21]. This may be because we followed the patient’s choice of whether to undergo HR-HPV testing or cytology in the outpatient clinic. After adjusting for cytology and HPV16/18 factors, the risks of immediate CIN2 + and CIN3 + were more than 30 times higher in the GYPCm (+) group than in the GYPCm (-) group, and the cytologic aORs were 1.48 and 3.09, respectively (Table 3). This may be due to the cytological cut-off value of ASC-US, as the rate of cytological abnormalities was higher in cervicitis and CIN1 (57.1 and 77.6%, respectively; Table 2). Lower ORs were also recorded in other studies of Chinese cohorts [22–24]. HPV-positive patients with hypermethylated oncogene status had a higher risk of progression in the short term than those with hypomethylation [25], and CIN3 with hypermethylation status had a higher proportion of invasive CC after conization [26, 27]. These findings suggest that hypermethylation carries a higher risk of developing cervical precancerous lesions and CC. They also suggest that clinical management of methylation-positive patients should be more aggressive.

Alterations in the DNA methylation status are considered determinants of genomic instability. Several studies have been conducted to determine their potential as biomarkers in cancer diagnosis [28]. DNA methylation involves the covalent addition of methyl groups to carbon 5 of the cytosine ring, especially on several CpG islands in gene promoters (promoter hypermethylation) [29]. Changes in DNA methylation levels are usually associated with the aberrant expression of genes that promote carcinogenesis, primarily by regulating cell growth and differentiation [30]. Alterations in DNA methylation play a decisive role in maintaining the malignant phenotype, and their diagnostic properties have been evaluated in colon, endometrial, cervical, and lung cancers [31–33].

GYPC are highly associated with the biological processes and pathways associated with cancer development and metastasis. GYPC gene is associated with various cancers, including breast cancer [34], lung adenocarcinoma [17], endometrial cancer (EC) [35], ovarian cancer [18], and CC [36]. Wang et al. reported that GYPC expression was correlated with the depth of invasion of lung adenocarcinoma and was regulated by DNA methylation [17]. In women with abnormal uterine bleeding, the sensitivity and specificity of the WID-qEC test, which combines GYPCm with ZSCAN12m for the diagnosis of endometrial carcinoma are 90.9% and 92.1%, respectively [37]. In addition, the WID-qEC test demonstrated 100% sensitivity and 92.9% specificity within the CC cohort. amongst the hospital-cohort (n = 330), the ROC AUC is 0.99 (95% CI: 0.98-1) with a sensitivity and specificity of 100% and 82.5% for the WID-qEC test, respectively. In conclusion, the WID-qEC test demonstrates a high level of accuracy in detecting both endometrial and CC through the utilization of ThinPrep-based liquid cytology samples [19]. For HR-HPV-positive women, we found that the detection rates for CIN2 + and CIN3 + in GYPCm were 83.3% and 90.2%, respectively, which is consistent with the cytology results. The specificity of GYPCm was higher than that of cytology (CIN2 + 85.5 vs. 29.4%, P < 0.001; CIN3 + 79.7 vs. 28.7%, P < 0.001). Owing to the high specificity of GYPCm, its PPV was also higher for both CIN2 + and CIN3+. Regarding reliability of negative predictions, GYPCm had a higher NPV for CIN2 + than cytology in all analyzed populations, non-16/18 HR-HPV-positive, and age ≥ 30 populations (all 96.3% vs. 90.0; non-16/18 HR-HPV (+) 97.8 vs. 93.0; Age ≥ 30 97.0 vs. 91.1). The differences in the NPV of GYPCm and cytology under other conditions were not statistically significant (Table 4). These properties are consistent with the methylation properties of other genes [38–40]. Our data showed the ability to detect advanced CIN when GYPCm triage HR-HPV positivity is comparable to cytology; the specificity and PPV are higher than those of cytology. The reliability of negative results is consistent with or even better than that of cytology.

A nationwide population-based study in China reveals that the most prevalent HR-HPV genotypes were HPV-52, 58, and 16, while the infection rate of HPV-18 is low. There are variations in HPV genotype infections across different age groups and regions [41]. The sequencing of these genotypes in the study are consistent with those reported in previous studies. According to the 2025 Guidelines for cervical cancer screening in China Ⅱ [42], a direct referral for colposcopy is recommended if the initial screening result indicates HPV 16/18. In cases where HR-HPV genotypes other than HPV 16/18 are detected, it is advised that cytology or DNA methylation testing be conducted. HPV types 52 and 58 rank among the top two HR-HPV genotypes in Chinese women. However, their carcinogenic risks are lower than HPV − 16 and − 18. Consequently, in accordance with China guidelines in China, cytological examinations and DNA methylation tests continue to be conducted. Individuals who receive positive results are referred for colposcopy, while those with negative results undergo re-examination after a period of twelve months.

The GYPCm test demonstrated a sensitivity of 82.9 (67.9–92.8) and specificity of 83.5 (79.2–87.2) for HPV-positive women with NILM, ASC-US, and LSIL cytology [43]. In contrast, this study reported a higher sensitivity of 83.3 (74.3–89.6) and specificity of 85.8 (82.3–88.7) for the GYPCm test in HPV-positive women, with no statistically significant difference observed between the two studies. These findings highlight the robustness of the GYPCm test, which is specifically linked to cervical CIN2+. Additionally, further population studies are necessary for additional validation.

Our clinical study exclusively focused on cervical diseases and did not encompass a cohort of EC patients. Consequently, we are unable to assess whether our GYPCm test can accurately detect EC. We designed three primer pairs and selected the most effective one through clinical samples to establish a standardized detection procedure for the study. The various primer pairs targeting methylation regions may exhibit differing clinical performance. The WID-qEC study demonstrated that GYPCm exhibits remarkable efficacy in both endometrial and CC. Consequently, it is plausible that we may pursue related research on EC in the future.

In the past decade, the application of host cell DNA methylation analysis in the detection of high-grade cervical intraepithelial neoplasia in women has been increasing. Dozens of human genes have sustained elevated methylation levels in pre-CC and most cancers, which can be used as a tool for triaging HR-HPV-positive women to detect cervical (pre)cancer [44, 45]. The AUC of GYPCm in the diagnosis of CIN2 + and CIN3 + was 0.846 (0.7899–0.903) and 0.872 (0.806–0.938), respectively, indicating good ability to distinguish high-grade from low-grade cervical lesions and cervicitis.

Five DNA methylation kits designed for the triage detection of 13/12 genotypes of HR-HPV, excluding HPV types 16 and 18, have been certified by the National Medical Products Administration in China as Class III medical devices. The available methylation kits include GynTect, which specifically targets six distinct methylation genes [22]. Additionally, there are the PAX1 methylation kit [46], the JAM3 combined with PAX1 methylation kit [47], the SOX1 and PAX1 methylation kit [24], as well as the PCDHGB7 methylation kit [48]. The study of the GYPCm test demonstrates high diagnostic performance and low cost through a single gene design. The introduction of various CC methylation kits available in the Chinese market provides an additional clinical option for precise CC detection and fertility preservation interventions.

This study has two main limitations. First, according to the study flowchart, some patients were not included in the analysis because they did not undergo cytology or colposcopic biopsy, which could have biased the results. Second, this study was cross-sectional, and there was no follow-up to determine the short-term risk of GYPCm, which creates a clinical dilemma in the follow-up interval. Despite these limitations, our results are reliable. The clinicians, individuals, and GYPCm testers were triple-blinded. The individuals were unaware of their GYPCm status, the clinicians who treated the patients were completely unaware of the GYPCm, and the GYPCm testers had no clinical information regarding the individuals. Further prospective large-scale studies are required to evaluate the accuracy of different triage strategies for HR-HPV positivity.

Nevertheless, this study can guide CC screening strategies in China. HPV16/18 has a higher risk of carcinogenesis and is generally recommended for colposcopy, whereas non-16/18 HR-HPV positivity is triaged using cytology [3]. Therefore, this study analyzed and compared HPV16/18 combined with cytology and GYPCm alone. The difference in sensitivity between the two groups for CIN2 + and CIN3 + was not statistically significant; however, the specificity and PPV of HPV16/18 combined with GYPCm were higher than those of HPV16/18 combined with cytology (Table 4). Both triage strategies missed seven cases of CIN2+; HPV16/18 combined with GYPCm improved the correct classification rate of differentiating CIN2 + from < CIN2 with an NRI of 48.8% (Table 5) and CIN3 + from < CIN3 with an NRI of 41.5% (Supplementary Table 2), with a relative reduction in the colposcopic referral rate of approximately 50% (Table 5). We conducted the study in a real-life hospital outpatient setting. The sensitivity and NPV of GYPCm is comparable to that of cytology but its sensitivity and PPV are higher than those of cytology in triaging HR-HPV (+) women ≥ 30 years old or < 30 years old (Table 4). Therefore, implementing the GYPCm test in hospitals enables fully automated CC screening based on molecular testing and reduces the number of unnecessary colposcopy referrals, preventing the inefficient use of healthcare resources.

Conclusion

In conclusion, GYPCm as a tool for triaging HR-HPV-positive women effectively detected CIN2 + and CIN3+ (AUC > 0.8). Compared with those for cytology, higher ORs, specificity, and PPV were achieved while showing no difference in sensitivity. Thus, the GYPCm assay based on cervical specimens can be used to identify cervical (pre)cancer in HR-HPV-positive women.
