Evaluation of functional capacity and clinical outcomes in healthy female college students: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Tarek M El-gohary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study was aimed at comparing step-up-and-down (SUD) test performance between the dominant and non-dominant lower limbs, and examining the association between isokinetic strength measures and functional performance in college students.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in 39 healthy females. Participants performed the SUD test on both lower limbs. The isokinetic strength of the knee extensors was assessed at angular velocities of 60°/s and 180°/s with a calibrated isokinetic dynamometer.ResultsNo significant differences were observed in SUD test performance between the dominant and non-dominant limbs (p > 0.05). Significant moderate negative correlations were found between SUD performance and isokinetic strength measures. SUD time with the dominant limb correlated with peak torque (ρ = −0.40, p = 0.02), average torque (ρ = −0.36, p = 0.04), and average power (ρ = −0.61, p < 0.001), whereas SUD time with the non-dominant limb correlated with average power (ρ = −0.41, p = 0.02). Therefore, greater muscular strength was associated with faster functional performance.ConclusionsAlthough no significant functional differences were observed, integrating isokinetic strength testing with functional assessments enables more comprehensive evaluation of lower-limb performance, enhances the detection of subtle strength–function discrepancies, and supports targeted, individualized rehabilitation and preventive strategies.Keywords: Cross-sectional studies, Female, Lower extremity, Physical fitness, Quadriceps muscle, Torque

Introduction

Lower-limb strength and endurance are fundamental components of physical fitness that play critical roles in maintaining functional health. A comprehensive evaluation of lower-limb function requires integration of both functional and clinical assessments.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Functional assessments typically emphasize dynamic balance and postural control during task-specific activities, whereas clinical assessments focus on quantifying muscular strength, endurance, and power, which are key determinants of optimal lower-extremity performance.2,3,6 Together, these complementary approaches provide a holistic understanding of lower-limb functionality and are essential for evaluating physical performance and designing effective intervention strategies.1,7, 8, 9

The step-up-and-down (SUD) test, a practical and efficient functional assessment, is frequently used to evaluate lower-extremity performance. The SUD replicates the everyday task of stepping onto and off a raised surface, thereby offering valuable insight into dynamic balance, neuromuscular control, and lower-limb coordination. This test is particularly advantageous because of its ease of administration, minimal equipment requirements, and adaptability across a variety of clinical and field settings. Because its simplicity enables widespread use in both healthy and clinical populations, the SUD provides a convenient and effective tool for identifying functional limitations and monitoring rehabilitation outcomes. The test has been applied in various formats, including stepping to a predetermined height, descending from a platform, and incorporating mini squats. It has also been used across diverse age groups, and among both healthy individuals and clinical populations. However, the findings in the literature remain inconsistent. The test serves as a practical, clinician-friendly tool for assessing lower extremity movement quality during functional activities.10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15

Isokinetic testing offers a precise, objective assessment of lower-extremity muscle performance.16 Measurements at 60°/s evaluate maximal strength, whereas those at 180°/s assess endurance and power.17 This method aids in detecting muscle imbalances, monitoring rehabilitation, and guiding individualized exercise interventions.

Whereas isokinetic assessment at 60°/s and 180°/s provides valuable data on lower-limb strength and endurance, understanding of how these measures relate to functional performance remains limited. Few studies have examined this relationship in healthy college students, a population warranting focused investigation, because of the physical and lifestyle transitions occurring during this stage. Therefore, gaining preliminary insights into performance patterns within this population is essential. Addressing these knowledge gaps is critical to improving the interpretation of lower-extremity outcome measures and enabling clinicians to infer clinical performance from easily administered functional tests. This approach supports increasing the accessibility of screening strategies across various healthcare and performance settings. The primary objective of this study was to compare SUD test performance between the dominant and non-dominant lower limbs. The null hypothesis was that no statistically significant difference in test performance would be observed between sides. The second objective was to examine the association between isokinetic clinical outcome measures and functional performance measures in healthy college students; no significant associations were hypothesized.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional measurement study was conducted between January and April of 2024. Ethical approval (CMR-PT-2024–10) was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of the College of Medical Rehabilitation Sciences (November 22nd 2023). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

A priori power analysis was conducted in G∗ Power software (version 3; University of Kiel, Germany), to determine the appropriate sample size for the repeated measures design comparing performance across paired conditions (e.g., dominant vs. non-dominant leg, and different isokinetic angular velocities). The analysis was based on a paired-samples t-test, according to assumptions of a moderate effect size (Cohen's d = 0.5), an alpha error probability of 0.05, and a desired statistical power of 80%. This calculation yielded a minimum required sample size of 34 participants, depending on the anticipated effect size and within-participant variability. A total of 39 participants were ultimately included in the analysis, thereby allowing for a margin of error, and enhancing both the statistical power and the generalizability of the study findings. This sample size adjustment provided a buffer and strengthened the overall robustness of the study outcomes.18,19

Participants

A total of 39 female participants from the CMRS-TU, KSA, voluntarily participated in the study. Recruitment was conducted consecutively with a convenience sampling approach. On the basis of body mass index (BMI), participants were classified into two groups: normal weight (n = 32) and overweight (n = 7). Eligible participants were healthy, physically active, right-leg–dominant college students. Lower-limb dominance was determined with the ball-kick preference test. Only participants who identified the right leg as the dominant limb were included in the study. The exclusion criteria included notable mobility impairments, inability to perform the test exercises, obesity, a history of lower limb surgery, recent musculoskeletal injuries, or a pain intensity score above 2 out of 10 in the lower back or lower extremities.

Procedure

The principal investigator assumed primary responsibility for screening eligible participants and collecting all relevant data with a standardized collection form. Participants received a thorough explanation of the study protocol and measurement procedures, and provided written informed consent before participation. To minimize order effects, reduce potential bias, and prevent systematic influence from fatigue or learning, the order of measurements was counterbalanced, thereby maintaining internal validity. All personal and outcome data were handled with the highest confidentiality and securely maintained to ensure participant privacy. Before testing, participants underwent a brief familiarization session to ensure proper technique and understanding of the protocol.

Step-up-and-down test

The SUD test was used to assess lower-limb functional performance by comparing the dominant and non-dominant limbs. Participants began the SUD test in an upright stance, with the feet positioned shoulder-width apart, facing a standardized 20 cm step platform. The test was performed barefoot, while allowing free arm movement, and was executed at a self-selected, comfortable pace to ensure natural and reproducible functional performance.

The dominant leg stepped up onto the platform while the non-dominant leg remained elevated; this was followed by stepping down with the non-dominant leg while the dominant foot remained on the step (Figure 1). The sequence was performed five times as quickly and safely as possible, and total time was recorded with a digital stopwatch. A minimum 30-s rest was conducted between limbs. Testing was discontinued if participants experienced fatigue or instability, and continuous supervision ensured adherence to protocol and safety. The SUD test consisted of five repetitions, in agreement with established brief functional screening protocols used in clinical practice. This format was selected to ensure standardization, feasibility, and applicability within a multijoint assessment battery.

Isokinetic strength testing

Right knee extensor concentric strength was measured with a Biodex System 4 dynamometer (Biodex Medical Systems, Shirley, NY). A sample of 39 healthy female college students participated in the assessment. Calibration of the dynamometer was performed according to the manufacturer's specifications before testing. For assessment of neuromuscular capacity in a near-resting state, no formal warm-up was conducted. Participants performed two submaximal familiarization repetitions at each velocity, thus ensuring safety and reducing variability while preserving baseline muscle condition for accurate maximal performance measurements.

Participants were seated with the hip flexed to approximately 85°–90°, and the lateral femoral epicondyle was aligned with the dynamometer's axis of rotation. Stabilization straps were applied across the torso, pelvis, and right thigh. The lower leg was secured to the lever arm just above the lateral malleolus, while avoiding excessive pressure on the tibia. Knee joint range of motion was set from 0° (full extension) to 90° of flexion, and was adjusted according to individual comfort and mobility. Gravity correction was performed with the leg in full passive extension.

The isokinetic assessment consisted of concentric contractions of the knee extensors at two angular velocities: 60°/s to evaluate maximal strength and 180°/s to assess muscular endurance and power.17,20,21 A rest period of 2 min was implemented between isokinetic test velocities to minimize fatigue and ensure reliable performance. Standardized instructions and verbal encouragement were provided to ensure maximal effort. Outcome measures at both angular velocities included peak torque (Nm), average torque (Nm), average power (W), and total work (J). These variables provided a comprehensive profile of knee extensor performance under controlled conditions.17

Statistical analysis19

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Normally distributed quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD, non-normal data are presented as median (IQR), and qualitative variables are presented as frequency (%). Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test.

The related-samples Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare SUD test performance between the dominant and non-dominant legs. This non-parametric test was used to evaluate within-participant differences across paired observations. Specifically, the isokinetic parameters including peak torque, average torque, and average power showed significant deviations from normality at the angular velocity of 180°/s, whereas these measures did not significantly deviate from normality at 60°/s. Although total work at both angular velocities did not show significant deviation from normality, these measures were also analyzed with the Wilcoxon test to align with the study's overall non-parametric approach. This decision was made to enhance the robustness and consistency of the statistical interpretation. Spearman's rank-order correlation was used to examine variable relationships, with a significance threshold set at p ≤ 0.05. Analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Discussion

The primary findings of this study indicated no significant differences between the dominant and non-dominant legs during the SUD test. However, significant differences in isokinetic knee extension performance were identified between angular velocities (60°/s and 180°/s) for the dominant leg. The weak-to-moderate correlations observed between isokinetic strength and functional performance suggested that muscle strength contributes meaningfully, although not exclusively, to functional lower-limb outcomes.

The present findings correspond to those of Nyberg et al.,11 who investigated the validity and reliability of the step-up test in a sample of 60 healthy middle-aged participants. Their results supported the psychometric soundness of the test, by demonstrating a significant correlation between step-up test performance and isokinetic knee extension peak torque. The authors highlighted the importance of a comprehensive evaluation approach integrating both clinical and functional outcome measures.

The absence of a significant difference between sides probably reflected symmetrical lower-limb function, as expected, given the participants' healthy status and absence of musculoskeletal impairments. Healthy individuals without prior injury typically demonstrate balanced neuromuscular control and strength across limbs. This symmetry might explain the comparable performance outcomes observed in both legs. Therefore, in healthy populations, limb dominance does not appear to meaningfully affect performance on the SUD test, thus reinforcing the test's value as a practical and balanced measure of lower-extremity function. The test requires no specialized equipment except a stopwatch, and it can be administered quickly in clinical, athletic, or field settings. Therefore, it provides a valuable tool for early functional screening and performance monitoring. Our findings are consistent with those from the literature. Moran et al.22 have determined that leg dominance does not significantly influence performance on the balance error scoring system (p = 0.79), particularly among individuals without a history of lower-extremity injury. Their investigation, which included both collegiate and high school athletes, supports functional symmetry between lower limbs in uninjured populations. In agreement with these findings, our study demonstrated no significant differences between sides and further supported that, in the absence of prior injury, limb dominance might not substantially affect performance in lower-extremity functional assessments.22, 23, 24, 25

The SUD test was intentionally implemented with five repetitions to align with widely used functional screening assessments, such as the five-times sit-to-stand test and the five-times squat-to-stand test. These brief protocols are frequently used in both clinical and research settings to evaluate lower-extremity functional performance. In the present study, the SUD test was not designed to elicit maximal performance but to contribute to a comprehensive multijoint screening framework. Although a ceiling effect might be expected in healthy young populations, the test remains appropriate for its intended purpose, by prioritizing feasibility, standardization, and clinical relevance, rather than as a standalone performance discriminator.26

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 46 studies, reported by Schorderet et al.,25 has demonstrated that leg dominance does not significantly affect balance in healthy adults. Similarly, El Gohary et al.24 have found no differences in single-leg stance performance, thereby supporting the lack of influence of leg dominance on static balance. Similarly, our findings from the SUD test revealed no significant effect of leg dominance, despite assessment of a different aspect of lower-limb function. Together, these results suggested that leg dominance might have minimal to no effects on balance-related performance in healthy young individuals, regardless of the specific task being evaluated.22, 23, 24

The isokinetic assessment demonstrated velocity-dependent differences in muscle performance, in agreement with the force–velocity relationship. Greater peak torque, average torque, and total work were observed at 60°/s than 180°/s, a finding reflecting muscle capacity to produce higher force at slower contraction speeds. In contrast, the higher average power at 180°/s reinforced the influence of movement velocity on power generation. These findings highlight the value of assessing muscle performance across multiple angular velocities. In agreement with previous research, we observed no bilateral knee strength asymmetry in this cohort.6,17 Our findings align with those from Harbili et al.,20 who have likewise reported no bilateral knee strength asymmetry in young athletes. In that study, concentric peak torque was assessed at 60°/s and 180°/s with an isokinetic dynamometer. The findings suggest that a well-balanced development of lower-limb musculature might protect against sport-related injuries in young athletes.

Similarly, Jones and Bampouras27 have compared isokinetic and functional approaches for assessing bilateral lower-limb imbalances in young athletes and highlighted the value of field-based tests in detecting strength asymmetries. They have emphasized the selection of assessment methods reflecting the physical requirements of the sport to ensure ecological validity and performance relevance, and have also underscored the importance of muscular strength and balance in reducing the risk of sport-related injuries.

Noh et al.28 have assessed peak torque at 60°/s with an isokinetic dynamometer as a primary measure of lower-limb muscle performance. Their findings, in agreement with ours, support isokinetic testing as a reliable method for quantifying muscle strength. Concentric knee extension at 60°/s and 180°/s is frequently used to evaluate strength across different contraction velocities.21,29 Kabacinski et al.30 have highlighted the assessment of quadriceps concentric peak torque at 60°/s and 180°/s to detect strength asymmetries between the involved and uninvolved knees in female athletes. Lutz et al.23 have reported that concentric strength ratios change with angular velocity but have found no significant limb-to-limb differences.

These findings emphasize the value of incorporating multi-velocity isokinetic protocols in both clinical and research settings, to enable more comprehensive assessment of muscle performance.29 Integrating objective strength measurements alongside functional assessments is supported by evidence demonstrating that eccentric resistance training can produce greater strength gains than traditional training, even when the functional outcomes are similar, thus highlighting the value of including both strength and functional evaluations.31

Several limitations should be considered in interpreting our findings. The relatively small sample size, narrow age range, and unequal BMI distribution might limit the generalizability of the findings. The use of a convenience sample from a single institution further restricted external validity. In addition, the familiarization-only protocol and potential minor postural variations during testing might have influenced performance outcomes. Finally, the SUD test might exhibit a ceiling effect in healthy young adults, thus potentially decreasing its sensitivity to detect subtle limb differences.

Conclusions

Although no statistically significant functional differences were observed, integrating isokinetic strength testing with functional performance assessments has potential to offer enhanced insights into lower-limb capacity. However, given the potential ceiling effects of the SUD test in healthy young adults, this combined approach should be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, incorporating objective strength measures alongside functional assessments might enable a nuanced understanding of performance and inform individualized training or rehabilitation strategies.
