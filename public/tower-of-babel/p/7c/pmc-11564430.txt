Geometry of Modern Presbyopia-Correcting Intraocular Lenses and Changes in Haptic–Capsular Bag Behavior According to Compression and Different Well Diameters: A Bench Study Using Computed Tomography
Authors: Andreas F Borkenstein, Eva-Maria Borkenstein
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe geometry of an intraocular lens (IOL), the design of the haptics, and the optic–haptic junction play a role in initial and long-term visual outcome after cataract surgery. Knowledge of the behavior of an implant in the capsular bag and under compression is of major importance. Our laboratory experiment analyzed in-depth the geometry of acrylic, single-piece, premium, presbyopia-correcting intraocular lenses and changes in haptic–capsular bag relationships according to capsular bag size using a range of compression well diameters.MethodsOne hydrophilic (RayOne Trifocal® RAO603F) and three hydrophobic intraocular lenses (AcrySof IQ PanOptix®, Synergy DFR00V™, and AT ELANA 841P®) were scanned with computed tomography (CT) in a dry, uncompressed state for quantitative analyses of haptic and optic–haptic junction (OHJ) dimensions and qualitative assessment of geometry. In the second part of the experiment, CT was performed after sample placement into a series of compression wells (9.0, 10.0, 11.0, 11.5 mm) for analyses of length of contact (LoC) between the haptics and the wells. Axial alignment and haptic–capsular bag relationships were assessed.ResultsThe qualitative and quantitative evaluations highlighted differences in haptic and OHJ geometry and dimensions across the samples. The mid-zone of the optic was thickest in the hydrophilic sample (RayOne Trifocal® RAO603F) with a maximum of 0.880 mm compared to the thinnest hydrophobic sample (AT ELANA 841P®) with 0.564 mm. The AT ELANA 841P® showed the largest OHJ surface area (3.86 mm2) and OHJ volume (0.60 mm3) of the hydrophobic samples. The TECNIS Synergy DFR00V™ showed the thickest OHJ (0.51 mm), while the AcrySof IQ PanOptix® showed the thinnest OHJ (0.21 mm). The LoC values decreased with increasing well size for all tested samples. The AT ELANA 841P® showed the largest LoC and largest contact zones of hydrophobic samples in all wells.ConclusionThe laboratory experiments highlight differences in the haptics, the OHJ geometric characteristics, and behavior of samples in different well diameters. The results support the idea that specific IOL designs may have advantages or disadvantages depending on anatomical dimensions. We cannot make any classification or rating (good versus bad) for clinical practice on the basis these experimental results, as many other factors play a role. However, knowledge of IOL geometry seems important to select the best option in each individual case.Keywords: Presbyopia-correcting intraocular lens, Geometry of intraocular lens, Behavior in wells, Optic–haptic junction, Length of contact

Key Summary Points

This laboratory experiment shows that the geometric dimensions of the haptics and optic–haptic junction in modern acrylic, one-piece intraocular lenses differ.Computed tomography revealed that the tested intraocular lenses showed different behavior in compression wells with varying diameters. Moreover, the measured length of contact (LoC) between the haptics and the wells was different.Knowledge of intraocular lens geometry seems important to select the best option in each individual case.

Introduction

Intraocular lenses (IOLs) were primarily invented to replace the natural lens in case of visual impairment caused by cataract. Meanwhile, a huge variety of IOLs are available that additionally can correct ametropia and presbyopia, the age-related loss of accommodation, and therefore, near vision. In 2020, 1.09 billion people worldwide were estimated to be affected by presbyopia [1]. Most common methods of correction are the use of reading or multifocal glasses or contact lenses. However, considering the increasing demand of patients for more comfortable spectacle-free vision in all distances, more and more IOL designs were developed with the aim to deliver either more than one focus point (multifocal) or an extended depth of field (EDOF) or even change their focus point (accommodative IOLs). With those possibilities, it is getting more common to exchange the clear lens before the advent of cataract called clear-lens exchange or refractive lens exchange. In addition, cataract surgery is often performed at an earlier stage of lens opacification to take advantage of the new IOL optics.

The overall proportion of premium IOL implantation is still rather low but continuously increasing. Over the last decade, it increased from 2.8% in 2012 to 6.3% in 2022 (Fig. 1) [2]. Clinical and market observations show an increment in uncorrected presbyopia and, with it, a steadily growing demand for presbyopia-correcting IOLs (PC-IOLs), including multifocal IOLs (MIOLs) [3–6]. Various presbyopia-correcting IOL designs have their own advantages and disadvantages. Refractive and diffractive PC-IOLs still induce more side effects such as photic phenomena, while EDOF IOLs cannot deliver the same quality of near visual performance as typical multifocal IOLs. The most crucial point seems to be to choose the most suitable lens for the individual patient to achieve high satisfaction [7–10]. To keep this satisfaction, patients must maintain the newly gained quality of vision over a long period of time. With regards to the IOL, several factors seem to be crucial, among them the biomechanical stability of the IOL and the prevention of posterior capsule opacification (PCO) development, which again are both influenced by multiple factors such as those explained in more detail in a previous study [11], the IOL’s haptic design, and the design of the optic–haptic junction (OHJ), the part of a one-piece lens where the optic merges with the haptic [12–16]. Professor Apple identified OHJ as a weak point that could render an IOL’s ability to block the ingrowth of lens epithelial cells (LECs) and subsequent PCO development and called it the Achilles’ heel of an IOL [17]. Furthermore, the design of the haptic and the resulting function as a barrier against LECs has also been discussed as an important factor to minimize the risk for anterior capsule opacification (ACO) and subsequent lens dislocation due to fibrosis [18]. Taking into account that dislocation/decentration is still the leading cause of IOL explantation, it becomes quite obvious that identifying and eliminating the sources are of major interest for patients, surgeons, and manufacturers [9]. A proper location in the capsular bag is even more important for PC-IOLs than for standard IOLs. On the one hand, according to the laws of physics, decentration and tilt have a greater influence on multifocal optical designs; on the other hand, patient expectations are often higher in a population willing to pay for premium IOLs.

Overall proportion of premium IOL implantations between 2011 and 2022. The steady growth in PC-IOLs is remarkable, but their share of the total number (< 10%) is still low (Source Market Scope 2022 IOL Market Report [6])

As for all kinds of IOLs, within the group of MIOLs, there are a variety of haptic designs on the market. Available designs include planar haptics with a 0° angle or a step-vaulted configuration in which the haptics are offset from the optic plane. OHJ volume and area, as well as haptic thickness and area also vary between IOLs. All those factors may contribute to the response to force exerted by capsular bag compression and subsequently an IOL’s ability to maintain a stable optic position over a long period [11, 12, 14].

Unfortunately, variations depending on well-known differences in anatomy and capsular bag size are currently not covered by routine tests [19, 20]. Though measurements of OHJ dimensions and haptic are part of the manufacturer’s routine practice, the haptic angle of contact according to ISO 11979-3 only needs to be measured in a single-size (10 mm) compression well, which cannot represent the implantation in eyes with extraordinary small or large capsular bags. It is well known that there are anatomical variations among myopic, emmetropic, and hyperopic eyes. The capsular bag size and diameter depend on the degree of filling. A study reported an increase of the capsular bag size and diameter of approximately 10% after removal of the lens substance in postmortem eyes [21]. An experimental study using magnetic resonance imaging to predict the lens diameter concluded that it is likely that these changes also occur after cataract surgery [22].

In a previous experimental study, the geometry of commonly used hydrophobic acrylic monofocal IOLs was evaluated by means of computed tomography (CT) for characterization and comparison of the design of the haptics and OHJ. CT images of the IOLs in different compression wells with 9.0, 10.0, 11.0, and 11.5 mm diameters were used to explore changes in the haptic–capsular bag relationships according to capsular bag size for five IOLs from different manufacturers. The wells served as a surrogate model for the capsular bag to provide a standardized setting that allowed comparisons of the investigated parameters projected angle of contact (AoC) according to ISO standards and simulated capsular bag contact angle (CBC), reflecting the true contact zone [11].

The purpose of this new bench study is to evaluate the geometry of currently available PC-IOLs. CT was used to characterize and compare the design of the haptics and OHJ of four one-piece, acrylic IOLs made of hydrophilic and hydrophobic materials and to explore changes in the haptic–capsular bag relationships for a given IOL and between IOLs according to capsular bag size. Compression wells of different diameters were used as a surrogate model for the capsular bag that would allow comparisons of the projected contact length of the haptic with the wells. The projected length of contact (LoC) is intended to reflect the behavior of the lenses with different capsule bag sizes and give the surgeon additional information to select the best possible option in individual cases.

This article does not contain any studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors but is solely based on laboratory/bench evaluations.

Methods

Samples Under Investigation

The laboratory experiment included four presbyopia-correcting IOLs currently on the market and frequently used worldwide. All samples had the same refractive power of 20.0 D.

One hydrophilic acrylic IOL (RayOne Trifocal® RAO603F by Rayner) and three hydrophobic acrylic IOLs (AcrySof IQ PanOptix® by Alcon, TECNIS Synergy DFR00V™ by Johnson & Johnson, and AT ELANA 841P® by Carl Zeiss Meditec) were analyzed. All hydrophobic models have an optical diameter of 6.00 mm and an overall diameter of 13.00 mm with a classic C-loop design. All samples are acrylic, single-piece IOLs. The hydrophilic RayOne Trifocal® has the same optical diameter but has an overall diameter of only 12.50 mm and a different haptic design. CT Measurements

All CT measurements were conducted by the laboratory Westcam-Datentechnik GmbH in Austria with the Zeiss Metrotom 1500 device, a high-precision CT system suitable for industrial use and non-destructive 3D inspection and measurement of components. The system uses X-rays to generate high-resolution 3D images of components. These images make it possible to identify internal structures, cavities, faults, edges, and defects. All images of the components can be visualized and analyzed in high resolution.

Before CT was performed, each IOL sample was stored in liquid at 30 °C to ensure exactly the same conditions for hydrophobic and hydrophilic lenses.

All samples were cleaned with deionized water before the CT measurement. All CT measurements were conducted using a new sample in air and in dry ambient conditions at an ambient temperature of 21 °C. A series of compression wells for analyses of haptic contact were 3D-printed with different diameters of 9.0, 10.0, 11.0, and 11.5 mm. IOL placement was done under microscope visualization, whereby a crosshair was added in the center to each well as an alignment guide to achieve the most accurate centration of the sample.

Image Analysis

GOM Inspect software was used to process 3D scan data, carry out measurements, perform error analysis, and create reports. VGStudio Max 3.4 (Volume Graphics) was used to analyze OHJ volume and OHJ surface area. With the help of this software, IOL and environment could be segmented (separated) by extended surface determination. Furthermore, the open/close function was used to remove filtering particles and artifacts. The optic body was virtually removed from the IOL to enable measurement of volume and total area of the remaining haptics.

Test Procedures

As the first step, geometry of the samples, including design parameters and their differences, thickness, diameter, and edges of the IOLs were examined. A distinction was made between three components of the samples: optics, OHJ, and haptics. All evaluations were based on CT measurements of the uncompressed IOLs with the same refractive power. Therefore, the individual IOLs were positioned on a low-density foam block in their natural non-compressed state.

In the second step, behavior of the compressed IOLs in the wells was investigated and compared. CT scans were acquired of compressed IOLs placed into 3D-printed wells with 9.0, 10.0, 11.0, and 11.5 mm diameters as described in a previous study by the authors [11]. For all four IOLs, the same set of wells was used to obtain results that are as comparable, accurate, and as objective as possible.

The main focus of the analysis was on the following topics: geometry of the sample including optic, OHJ, and haptics geometry, in depth evaluation of haptics with analysis of haptic surface area, haptic thickness, and haptic volume.

Overlays of top-view CT images were prepared to visualize geometrical differences between IOLs. The lens with the most distinct OHJ was identified and used as a baseline image, and the geometry of the other lenses was compared to it. Here, the main focus of the analysis included OHJ surface area and volume, haptic contact measurements, measurement of LoC for both haptics of each IOL, axial alignment and position in the wells, and 3D images. LoC was defined as the contact length between the haptics of the samples and the inside of the well to provide a means of comparison for the different geometry and behavior of the samples in different wells. The overall LoC of a sample in a specific well was calculated from the sum of the LoC of haptic 1 and haptic 2.

Results

Overall Geometry

All samples had the same optic diameter of 6.0 mm. With respect to the optical area, the hydrophilic RayOne Trifocal® by Rayner was the thickest IOL in this study (Fig. 3). In some areas, it had almost twice the material thickness compared to hydrophobic samples. This can be explained by material properties. Maximum thickness in the mid-zone of the RayOne Trifocal® was 0.880 mm versus 0.564 mm of the AT ELANA 841P®, respectively. Minimum thickness in the outer region of the RayOne Trifocal® was 0.261 mm versus 0.111 mm of the AT ELANA 841P®. The minimum thickness in the outer region of the AcrySof IQ PanOptix® and TECNIS Synergy DFR00V™ was 0.234 mm and 0.330, respectively.

Haptic Geometry Analyses

Haptic Thickness

The closed C-loop haptic of the hydrophilic RayOne Trifocal® was the thinnest of all samples, with a thickness of around 0.38 mm over the whole haptic area. All hydrophobic samples have an open C-loop haptic design. The AcrySof IQ PanOptix® was nearly as thin as the RayOne in the haptic area (0.39 mm versus 0.37 mm). The haptics of the TECNIS Synergy DFR00V™ and AT ELANA 841P® were thicker, around 0.48 mm (Fig. 3).

Haptic Surface Area

The hydrophilic RayOne Trifocal® showed the largest haptic surface (14.68 mm2; Table 2). Among the hydrophobic samples, the AT ELANA 841P® showed the largest surface area (12.64 mm2), followed by the TECNIS Synergy DFR00V™ (11.87 mm2) and the AcrySof IQ PanOptix® (10.84 mm2).

Optic-Haptic Junction Geometry Analyses

Surface Area and Volume

The differences were even more pronounced in the geometry of the OHJ. The hydrophilic RayOne Trifocal® showed the largest OHJ surface area (5.96 mm2) and OHJ volume (1.09 mm3). The AT ELANA 841P® showed the largest OHJ surface area (3.86 mm2) and OHJ volume (0.60 mm3) of the three hydrophobic samples. The AcrySof IQ PanOptix® and the TECNIS Synergy DFR00V™ showed much lower values with an OHJ surface area of 2.08 mm2 and 2.06 mm2 and an OHJ volume of 0.21 mm3 and 0.36 mm3, respectively. This means that the OHJ volume for a hydrophobic sample (AT ELANA 841P®) was almost three times as large as for two other hydrophobic samples (TECNIS Synergy DFR00V™ and AcrySof IQ PanOptix®). As a result of the design, the OHJ surface area of the hydrophilic sample (RayOne Trifocal®) was almost three times as large as that of two hydrophobic samples. When we compared the absolute thickness of the OHJ, the TECNIS Synergy DFR00V™ showed the thickest junction (0.51 mm), while the AcrySof IQ PanOptix® had the thinnest junction (0.21 mm).

LoC length of contact, Ø diameter

The RayOne Trifocal®, the only lens in our study with a double C-loop haptic design, a hydrophilic, softer material, and a smaller overall diameter (12.5 mm), showed, in some cases, an asymmetric contact of the haptics to the wells. This is probably the result of the material properties. The side views (Figs. 8, 9, 10, and 11) clearly show how the material deforms and partially bends during compression. As a result, there was sometimes no clear identifiable continuous contact between haptics and the inside of the wells. However, the best contact could be observed in the 10.0 mm and 11.0 mm wells. In the largest well (11.5 mm), the RayOne Trifocal® had the smallest total LoC (< 2.0 mm).

When we compared the three hydrophobic IOLs with identical open C-loop haptics design and identical overall diameter, the AT ELANA 841P® showed the largest LoC in all four wells. In the 9.0 mm well, the AT ELANA 841P® showed a total LoC of 9.470 mm, whereas the TECNIS Synergy DFR00V™ had a LoC of 7.560 mm and the AcrySof IQ PanOptix® 5.802 mm. In the 10.0 mm well, the AT ELANA 841P® had a total LoC of 7.106 mm, whereas the TECNIS Synergy DFR00V™ had a LoC of 4.358 mm and the AcrySof IQ PanOptix® 2.699 mm. The differences between the three samples regarding behavior in the wells and contact to the wells (LoC) were particularly impressive in the larger diameters. In the well with a diameter of 11.0 mm and an inner circumference of 33.46 mm, the AT ELANA 841P® showed a total LoC of 5.341 mm, whereas the AcrySof IQ PanOptix® showed a total LoC of 1.26 mm in the same well. In the well with a diameter of 11.5 mm and an inner circumference of 35.82 mm, the AT ELANA 841P® showed a total LoC of 4.476 mm, whereas the AcrySof IQ PanOptix® showed a total LoC of 0.854 mm in the same well. It could also be proven for all samples that the LoC decreases continuously as the well diameter increases.

Axial Alignment and Position

The IOLs’ axial position and alignment in different well sizes were evaluated with top-view and side-view CT images (Figs. 8, 9, 10, and 11). It should be emphasized that the results of a laboratory experiment are shown here. The samples were positioned exactly centered on the crosshairs of the wells under a microscope. The real-world conditions may differ because of various influencing factors. The shape (asymmetric natural anatomy of the capsular bag versus artificial perfectly round crafted well) and possible postoperative shrinkage may vary and influence results. The positioning of the IOL during implantation process, residual viscoelastic remnants in the capsular bag, and the capsulorhexis size may play another important role.

In our laboratory experiment, all four lenses under investigation showed good axial alignment behavior and no decentration. Samples with large contact zone to the well showed slight movement (shift) in smaller well diameters. This could be shown very well with thicker, large OHJ and prominent haptic design, and large LoC (Figs. 8, 9, 10, and 11). The RayOne Trifocal® and the AT ELANA 841P® showed this behavior in the smallest wells (Fig. 8). Samples with more fragile, thinner OHJ designs like the AcrySof IQ PanOptix® did not show this behavior. The evaluation of the geometry of OHJ and haptics showed a good correlation to the behavior of the samples in the wells. The samples with more contact with the wells (larger LoCs) had more stability in the larger wells.

Discussion

With an increasing proportion of presbyopia-correcting (premium) IOLs being implanted, reliable stabilization of the lens in the capsular bag is becoming more important to avoid side effects caused by decentration, rotation, and tilt. As shown in experimental studies, negative effects of incorrect centration, at least in terms of technical performance, are higher for aspheric and PC-IOLs than for standard monofocal, spherical IOLs, especially in eyes with a history of corneal refractive surgery [23–27]. Can et al. investigated modulation transfer function (MTF) of five diffractive IOLs as representative parameter for their optical behavior. In their study, the authors included lenses such as the TECNIS Synergy (Johnson & Johonson) and the AT LISA tri (Zeiss, Meditec). The hydrophilic AT LISA tri is very similar in terms of trifocality to the new hydrophobic AT ELANA 841P®. However, there are differences with respect to the aspheric profile as the AT ELANA 8341P® has a SA neutral design [24, 28]. In their bench test, they introduced 5° tilt or 0.5 mm of decentration and determined MTF for varying apertures from 2.0 to 4.5 mm. Outcomes showed significant differences among IOLs; overall MTF oscillations were observed as decentration and tilt affected the energy distribution [24]. The resulting assumption that multifocal IOLs would be more sensitive to decentration and tilt is not yet clearly demonstrated by clinical data [24]. However, an unsystematic review of 30 articles on the topic concludes that a deep understanding of IOL tilt and decentration is of major importance for individual estimation of individual patient postoperative outcomes, and IOL design should be chosen carefully, especially if existing preconditions indicate uncertain postoperative position accuracy [7]. The surgeon is not only responsible for the procedure itself but also for the correct selection of the implant. Therefore, good knowledge of lens geometry is crucial in selecting the best option for each individual case. Unfortunately, manufacturers only publish very general data such as optical diameter and total diameter of their lenses. Therefore, it seems understandable that IOLs of similar design (e.g., hydrophobic, C-loop, one-piece) are often rated as identical. However, our laboratory in-depth evaluation of the geometry clearly showed how different parameters can be within a model group.

Furthermore, patient expectations on postoperative visual acuity in general rise with modern possibilities and lifestyle, and the trend towards lens exchange at a younger age combined with increasing life expectancies prolongs the time IOLs stay in the eye and are expected to maintain a stable performance. Therefore, the choice of the most suitable IOL design is becoming more important.

All four IOLs investigated in our laboratory study are based on established haptic designs. Multifocal designs of the IOLs and/or respective parent models have already proven overall good performance in many clinical studies in the past [29–33].

Differences displayed in the current experiment do not express any quality criteria of any lens. The laboratory results purely show how different IOLs behave depending on compression after implantation and could serve as guidance in choosing the most suitable IOL for an individual patient. However, the laboratory results presented here cannot be transferred to clinical practice without a specific study.

The experiments are also intended to show that there are differences even between lenses of similar design. These geometric variations are developed together with the material properties and optical principles during the development process of the lens and an optimization of A-constants (calculation formula) is carried out within premarket studies. Therefore, the geometry of a specific lens and the desired position in the capsular bag has to be considered together, as an angulation of the haptics or a movement towards the posterior capsule (posterior capsule opacification prevention) is intended by the manufacturers.

Therefore, the results of the laboratory experiment should not be used to give any rating. One lens with more fragile haptics and smaller OHJ could offer advantages in anatomically small capsular bags, as well as providing benefits in the implantation process through small incisions. One lens with pronounced, thick haptics and more pronounced OHJ could provide more stability in larger capsular bags but show different implantation behavior and unfolding. In addition, the material and its properties always play an important role.

Our data are in alignment with those of Zhang et al., who investigated the correlation of the LoC, called “arc of contact” in their publication, with capsular bag size in postmortem eyes. Six different IOL models were implanted successively in 11 donor eyes as long as capsular bag or capsulorhexis was intact. As in our study, lower LoC was observed in larger capsular bags. Statistical calculations showed a relatively strong, positive, and linear correlation between those parameters. Furthermore, investigation of capsular bag stretching revealed no significant differences in capsular bag size before and after IOL implantation. The authors recommended to base IOL selection on IOL design to avoid rotation and misalignment in eyes with a large capsular bag [34]. Unfortunately, the Miyake-Apple posterior view technique for capsular bag size measurements is not available in clinical settings. Within daily practice, the capsular bag size can preoperatively best be estimated on the basis of lens diameter (LD), lens thickness (LT), and lens volume (LV). In the past, those parameters were assumed to be correlated with overall eye size and respective refractive error. However, latest research indicates that LD, LT, and LV seem not only (not in all cases) correlated with axial length (AL) and refractive error [7, 22, 35]. Therefore, based on our data, no general advice should be given on which IOL might be best suited for large myopic or small hyperopic eyes. Ideally, lens parameters should be determined preoperatively. As only limited devices allow suchlike measurements, researchers also tried to find solutions for estimation of lens variables based on commonly available optical biometry or ultrasound B-scan data. Erb-Eigner et al. described a prediction method for customized IOL diameter based on those data [22]. The authors stated that the partial least square regression model that included the globe diameter and the axis length resulted in the best prediction for the horizontal lens diameter. Similar to the horizontal lens diameter, globe diameter was the best predictor for the vertical lens diameter followed by the distance of the ciliary sulcus. Gu et al. and Langenbucher et al. presented promising methods of decentration and tilt prognosis based on multivariate regression [36] and a neural network approach [37], respectively. But until suchlike software support is available to assist the surgeon in the choice of the most suitable IOL, comprehensive knowledge of the complex interaction of anatomic properties, IOL, especially haptics, design, and postoperative physiological processes, is important to find the best lens for every individual patient. This seems most important in cases of unusual anatomy such as extremely large or small eyes as well as large or small lens equatorial plane and capsular bag diameters. Apart from presurgical variations, postoperative anatomical alterations such as fibrosis might occur unpredictably and lead to late-onset changes in IOL position [18, 38]. The exact relationship and effect as well as clinical implications should be targeted in large multicenter trials with diverse IOL models in the future. In any case, every surgeon should be aware that even IOLs with the same diameter or same haptic principle can behave very differently according to anatomical conditions. Manufacturing companies should also disclose more specific data about their IOLs to allow surgeons to choose the option that best suits their individual case. In addition, there should be more experimental studies to analyze IOL design, dimensions, and material behavior that could provide the basis for clinical evaluations. It is clear that visual outcomes after cataract surgery depend on IOL position in the capsular bag. The effective lens position post-implantation and the potential for postoperative shift, including axial displacement, decentration, tilt, and axial rotation, are affected by multiple anatomic and IOL-related features, among which capsular bag size and haptic/OHJ design have prominent roles. Ideally, the surgeon can select the right implant from a large portfolio and select a specific optic design with a specific haptic design and material property to combine the most advantages.

In clinical routine, the lack of optic decentration can be used as proof of good construction, and the anterior displacement of the IOL can be used as one possible cause of postoperative refractive surprise.

Limitations

The laboratory tests were performed under exactly the same conditions for all IOL samples. Care was taken to ensure that the temperature during IOL insertion in the wells was always constant at 30 °C and that the samples were always kept in a moist state. The only exception was the CT measurement, where the IOLs had to be briefly removed from the liquid.

It should be emphasized that the parameters evaluated in our laboratory study are only some of the factors affecting the intraocular performance of a lens. Other issues, including material properties, optic design, and surgical factors, also play an important role. Capsular bag stretching and pathophysiological reactions like capsular bag shrinkage and phimosis cannot be considered in this laboratory experiment. Factors relating to the surgeon, rhexis size, and the accuracy of the implantation process of the lens in the capsular bag were not considered.

The results of this bench study cannot be used to draw conclusions about the clinical relevance of the qualitative and measured differences in haptic and OHJ characteristics or to rank any IOL as a superior option. All four IOL models have been demonstrated to be safe and effective through premarketing and postmarketing clinical studies. The laboratory results presented here cannot be transferred to clinical practice without specific clinical studies.

Conclusion

The laboratory experiments showed differences in the geometry of modern acrylic, single-piece, presbyopia-correcting IOLs. Moreover, the behavior of the samples under compression in wells with different diameters varied. The collected evidence refutes opinions that acrylic IOLs with the same optic or overall diameter, which appear similar at first glance, are identical. It supports the idea that a customized and individual selection of an implant can have advantages. Knowledge of geometry and the understanding how it impacts position is important to have the opportunity to select the best option in each individual case.
