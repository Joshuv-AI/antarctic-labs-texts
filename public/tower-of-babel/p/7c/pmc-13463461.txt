Relationships between shift work, mental workload, and needlestick injuries among nurses
Authors: Hamideh Bidel, Alireza Kazemian Talkhooncheh, Bahareh Heidari, Maryam Hasandaee Talkhooncheh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNurses often engage in shift work, which disrupts their circadian rhythms and increases mental workload and fatigue. These factors may associate with and elevate the risk of needlestick injuries. This study aimed to model the pathway from shift work to needlestick injuries through the mediating roles of predictor variables.MethodsIn this descriptive-analytical study, 243 nurses from a hospital in Isfahan, Iran, were selected based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. After obtaining informed consent, data were collected using the NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) to assess mental workload, the Wallace and Chen Cognitive Failures Questionnaire, and a standardized Needlestick Injury Questionnaire. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software.ResultsThe mean age and work experience of the nurses were 35.85 and 11.61 years, respectively. The mean mental workload and cognitive failure were 81.75 and 63.46, respectively. The results of the bootstrap and Sobel test show that workload (p-value = 0.227) and cognitive failure (p-value = 0.895) do not have a mediating role in the effect of shift work on needlestick incidence in nurses.ConclusionThese findings support a growing body of evidence indicating that occupational safety in healthcare is a multidimensional phenomenon shaped by organizational conditions as well as individual factors. It is important to consider strategies such as work schedule management, training, supervision, and compliance with safety and health procedures and standards among nurses to reduce the incidence of needlestick injuries.

Background

Nurses are the largest occupational group in hospitals; 80% of the health care system employees are nurses. They are more important than other groups because they provide medical care to patients and have more contact with them. The level of efficiency of nurses affects the quality of health care services and, as a result, the health state of the people in the community [1]; therefore, these people must be fast, accurate, and error-free in their work. These job characteristics cause mental workload. Mental workload is a general concept and is the amount of mental effort that a person needs while performing a task. From a cognitive perspective, accidents caused by human error, especially in intellectual tasks, do not occur due to the person’s ignorance, but rather a person’s attention and concentration decrease in a moment, so that the things he knew or studied, or even actions he has performed several times, do not interfere with his decision. In some professions, such as nursing, this problem is more evident, which is interpreted as mental workload. Every year, about 44,000 to 98,000 people die in the United States due to medical errors caused by psychological stress in various medical departments [2]. Mental workload is one of the important factors affecting patient safety, quality of treatment, and consequently the quality of services provided by nurses, as excessive mental workload can cause confusion in individuals, reduce their performance in processing information, and increase the likelihood of medical errors and mistakes [3]. High mental workload is one of the most important causes of stress and cognitive failures in nurses. Cognitive failures can be defined as errors related to the mind in tasks that individuals can perform flawlessly and completely [1]. These errors and mistakes include problems caused by memory, perception, and performance. Evidence shows that there is a significant relationship between accidents and cognitive failures [4]. Due to the nature of their jobs, nurses face cognitive failures due to various professional and occupational stresses and challenges, which may have detrimental effects on their mental, physical and performance health [5]. Factors such as work-life conflicts and challenges, as well as dealing with patients with complex conditions, lead to cognitive failure in nurses by creating feelings of helplessness and inefficiency [6]. Excessive workload affects the thinking process and causes risky behaviors. Fatigued individuals are more likely to choose risky behaviors such as taking shortcuts to complete their tasks [7]. High-workload jobs can cause memory impairment, impairment of mental processes, irritability, and decreased learning due to fatigue, which can be considered risk factors for occupational accidents [8]. Needlestick injuries are one of the most common occupational accidents among nurses. Statistics show that approximately 3 million of the 35 million healthcare workers worldwide suffer from needlestick injuries annually [9]. Needlestick injuries refer to any superficial or deep skin injury caused by sharps contaminated with blood or body fluids. At least 25 different pathogens are transmitted by needlesticks [10], which cause infectious diseases [11]. In a meta-analysis, findings reported a 43% incidence of needlestick injuries [12]. The studies examined the factors affecting the occurrence of needlestick injuries showed that many factors play a role in the occurrence of this incident.

Shift work is a social phenomenon that is increasing for economic and technological reasons. Many service professions, including medicine, nursing, firefighting, etc., using this work system [13]. Despite the economic and social benefits of shift work, it can cause physical, psychological, cognitive, and social problems and diseases by affecting the body’s circadian cycle [14]. As much as 80% of shift workers have reported psychological problems, including impaired concentration and learning, inattention, boredom, and arousal [15]. Nursing, is one of the most sensitive, risky, and important jobs in the health system of any society. According to studies, shift work is one of the most important and main risks in this job group, which disrupts biological balance and, as a result, causes adverse psychological, social, cognitive, and physical consequences [16]. Previous studies suggest that shift work is associated with adverse outcomes such as increased mental workload, fatigue, stress, and burnout, all of which can impair cognitive functioning and safety performance [17]. In nursing, where exposure to complex clinical tasks and high job demands is routine, accurate workload assessment is essential, as persistent stressors can hinder effective task management and decision-making. Moreover, the combination of excessive workload, supervisory responsibilities, and irregular working hours-particularly rotating or night shifts-may reduce vigilance, delay recognition of critical situations, and increase the likelihood of human error. Among these consequences, needlestick injuries represent one of the most serious occupational hazards faced by healthcare workers, with significant physical and psychological implications [18]. Considering the high prevalence of needlestick injuries among nurses and their detrimental outcomes, it is crucial to identify the underlying mechanisms that contribute to their occurrence. Accordingly, the present study aims to model the pathway from shift work to needlestick injuries, with a focus on the mediating roles of mental workload and cognitive failures among nurses.

Methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted in Al-Zahra hospital in Isfahan, Iran in 2025. The study population consisted of all nurses working in the hospital during the study period. Due to the limited number of accessible participants and to avoid sampling bias, a census method was applied and all nurses who met the inclusion criteria were invited to participate. Therefore, no sample size calculation was conducted. Of the total 250 eligible nurses, 243 completed and returned the questionnaires and were included in the final analysis. Participation in the study was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to completing the questionnaires. Non-attendance, unwillingness to participate in the study were as exclusion criteria, and the inclusion criteria were defined as nurses who were formally employed by the hospital, including both permanent (formal) and contractual staff, and had at least one year of work experience.

A researcher-made demographic questionnaire was used to collect the demographic and occupational characteristics of the participants. In order to determine the relationships between research variables, three questionnaires were used: workload, cognitive impairment, and needlestick frequency. In order to investigate workload among nurses, the NASA-TLX1 questionnaire was used. This questionnaire determines the workload imposed on an individual by different tasks. NASA TLX is a multidimensional assessment tool that has 6 subscales. These six subscales are: (1) Mental Demand, (2) Physical Demand, (3) Temporal Demand, (4) Performance, (5) Effort, (6) Frustration. This questionnaire consists of two parts. In the first part, each subscale is scored by the individual from 0 to 100. Then, in the second part, these subscales are compared in pairs and the dimension that has had a greater impact and importance on the individual is determined by him. The process of assessing mental workload using the questionnaire also consists of three stages: in the first stage, the weighting of the load is determined and in the second stage, the rating of each of the six scales is determined by the individual. In the third stage, the weight and degree of the determined scales are entered into the workload software and the final workload score of the individual is determined and calculated numerically between 0 and 100 [19]. According to the final score of the questionnaire, the level of mental workload is divided into 4 levels: low (0–25), moderate (26–50), high (51–75), and very high (76–100). Also, based on a study conducted to assess the mental workload of nurses in ICU, the reliability of this questionnaire was obtained with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.83 [20]. The Wallace and Chen questionnaire was used to measure cognitive failure among nurses [21]. This questionnaire has 30 questions that examine three dimensions of memory, attention, action and behavior. Each dimension has 10 questions and is rated using a Likert scale (1 = never, 2 = rarely, 3 = sometimes, 4-often, 5 = very much). The minimum score of this questionnaire is 30 and the maximum is 150. A score between 30 and 60 indicates a low level of cognitive failure, a score between 61 and 90 indicates a moderate level of cognitive failure, and a score above 90 indicates a high level of cognitive failure. This questionnaire has been used in several studies among nurses to assess cognitive failures and its validity has been confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.81 [18]. To assess needlestick injuries, the questionnaire “Assessment of the Prevalence and Reporting of Sharps Injuries in Surgeons” used in the Thomas and Murray study was used [22]. In order to determine the validity of the instrument, the content validity method was used, and for this purpose, this questionnaire was reviewed by 12 nursing faculty experts to ensure content relevance and clarity. Based on their feedback, minor modifications were made, including rephrasing a few items and adjusting the wording to reflect local clinical practice. No items were added or removed from the original questionnaire. These changes were made to improve comprehension and contextual appropriateness while maintaining the integrity of the validated instrument. To evaluate the reliability of the instrument, Cronbach’s alpha was calculated for all participants in the study, which was equal to 0.78. All questionnaires were self-administered and were distributed and collected by the research team during the participants’ work shifts. Participants completed the questionnaires at their workplace, during or immediately after their shifts.

After data collection, data analysis was performed using SPSS software, employing both descriptive and inferential statistical methods. Relationships between variables were examined using Pearson correlation analysis and other appropriate statistical tests. The normality of the data was assessed using skewness and kurtosis indices. The significance level for all statistical analyses was set at 0.05.

Discussion

The present study was conducted to modeling the pathway from shift work to needlestick injuries by mediating the role of mental workload and cognitive failures in nurses of a hospital in Isfahan, Iran. The mean age and work experience of the nurses studied were 35.85 and 11.61 years. In the present study, the incidence of needlestick injuries among nurses was 72%, indicating a remarkably high occupational exposure rate. Although this figure is higher than the findings of most previous studies, similar patterns of concern have been reported in both national and international research. For example, Saeednia reported that 37.5% of nurses in Sanandaj and Saqqez hospitals have suffered needlestick injury during the past year [23]. Consistent findings were also observed in other studies, where Ghanei reported the prevalence of needlestick injuries among nurses as 44.2% [24], Salmanzadeh et al. reported this as 38.18% [25], Abebe et al. reported this as 43% [26], Rashidi et al. reported this as 53.7% [27]. The prevalence of needlestick injuries observed in this study (72% over a six-month period) was notably higher than rates reported in several previous studies. Differences in measurement periods, reporting methods, workload intensity, staffing ratios, and institutional safety practices may contribute to this discrepancy. Furthermore, as this study was conducted in a single hospital, contextual factors specific to the setting may have influenced the reported rate. Therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing this finding to other healthcare environments. Overall, the literature consistently indicates that nurses experience a higher rate of needlestick injuries compared to other healthcare personnel. This elevated risk may be attributed to the nature of nursing responsibilities, which involve frequent and direct patient contact, continuous handling of injurious medical devices, and performance of invasive procedures such as venipuncture, injections, and intravenous cannulations. Additionally, nurses constitute the largest segment of hospital staff, which increases their cumulative exposure to sharp instruments compared to other professional groups. The findings of this study indicated that there is a significant relationship between marital status, employment status, type of work shift, and work experience with the occurrence of needlestick injuries among nurses. These findings are consistent with the conceptual framework in demonstrating a significant association between shift work and needlestick injuries. However, the proposed indirect pathways via mental workload and cognitive failure were not statistically supported, and thus mechanistic interpretations should be considered speculative and based on previous literature rather than on the current data. The results showed that needlestick injuries were more frequently observed among nurses working rotating shifts. This finding is consistent with reports by Persaud et al., who demonstrated that irregular work shifts are associated with fatigue and impaired cognitive performance, increasing the risk of needlestick injuries by up to 1.5 times [28]. In the same vein, Pellicani stated that the mental workload induced by irregular shift schedules directly increases sharp-related injuries [29]. The results demonstrated a significant inverse association between work experience and needlestick injuries. Although greater experience has been linked in prior literature to improved clinical skills and safety compliance. This result is in line with the reports of Hambridge et al. and Abbasi et al., who identified work experience as a protective factor against occupational injuries [6]. Likewise, Halimi et al. confirmed in a national meta-analysis in Iran that nurses with fewer years of experience are significantly more likely to experience needlestick injuries [30]. The results also showed that needlestick injuries were more prevalent among married nurses. This finding may be due to increased psychological pressure and multiple family–work responsibilities, which could reduce work focus. This result is consistent with the findings of Elmi et al., who demonstrated that psychosocial factors are significant predictors of nursing errors [31]. According to the findings, employment status was also associated with the occurrence of needlestick injuries; contract-based and temporary staff were more likely to experience such injuries. This may be due to job insecurity, higher workload pressure, and reduced access to safety training [32].

The findings of this study revealed that nurses experienced a very high level of mental workload (M = 81.75, SD = 9.8) and a moderate level of cognitive failure (M = 63.46, SD = 7.92). According to NASA-TLX interpretation guidelines, mental workload scores above 70 indicate a critical workload level that may negatively affect performance. The high workload found in this study confirms that nursing is a cognitively demanding profession that involves complex decision-making, multitasking, time pressure, and emotional demands [33]. Furthermore, analysis of workload dimensions indicated that mental demand received the highest score among all subscales (M = 89.88), suggesting that the cognitive nature of nursing tasks contributes more to workload than physical effort. This is in line with study findings which reported that mental demand was the most dominant dimension of workload among hospital nurses [34]. Similarly, Mohammadi et al. in Iran found that mental workload was significantly higher among nurses working in emergency and intensive care units [35]. Although the mental workload was significantly high, the level of cognitive failure among nurses was moderate. Cognitive failures-such as memory lapses, attention slips, and action errors-often occur in high-demand environments where workers experience information overload and fatigue. In agreement with the findings of this study, Pourtalemi et al. and Ghahramani et al. reported moderate levels of cognitive failure among Iranian nurses, emphasizing that even moderate cognitive failure can seriously compromise patient safety [36, 37]. Overall, the high mental workload observed in this study may be attributable to multiple occupational stressors, including staff shortages, shift work, inadequate staffing ratios, and increased patient acuity-problems commonly reported in developing healthcare systems [38]. These findings highlight the urgent need for interventions such as workload redistribution, reduced shift length, improved staffing policies, and cognitive support strategies to prevent performance decline and enhance safety. The findings of this study showed that the mean level of mental workload among nurses working in rotating shifts was significantly higher than those with fixed shifts. This result is consistent with previous research demonstrating that rotating shifts impose greater psychological and cognitive demands due to irregular sleep patterns, circadian rhythm disruption, and increased work pressure. Similar findings have been reported in Iranian studies, where rotating shift nurses experienced significantly higher workload compared to day-shift nurses [36]. One explanation is that rotating shift schedules often involve high patient turnover, frequent emergencies, and limited managerial supervision, which increase task complexity and decision-making load [34]. Despite this significant difference in mental workload, the findings indicated no significant difference in cognitive failure between nurses working rotating shifts and those in fixed shifts. This result may initially seem contradictory, as earlier studies linked rotating shift work with increased cognitive failures, reduced vigilance, and attention lapses. However, this finding may be interpreted in several ways. First, nurses with rotating shifts may gradually develop compensatory strategies and professional coping mechanisms to maintain cognitive performance despite workload challenges [39]. Second, cognitive failure may be more strongly influenced by mental fatigue and burnout accumulated over time rather than short-term workload variations [40]. Third, it is possible that organizational and social support at work (such as teamwork and work experience) acts as a moderator that prevents cognitive decline despite high workload levels [41]. From the theoretical perspective of this study, these findings reinforce the assumption that shift work increases mental workload, but its effect on cognitive failure is indirect and may be mediated by individual resilience, workload management, and fatigue accumulation. This implies that while shift structure clearly affects workload perception, cognitive performance may remain stable in the short term but deteriorate if high workload persists without preventive measures. These findings highlight the need for ergonomic interventions such as optimized shift scheduling, structured rest breaks, staffing adequacy, and fatigue management programs to prevent cognitive overload and related consequences such as medical errors and needlestick injuries [29].

The findings of this study provide insight into the determinants of needlestick injuries among nurses. Logistic regression analysis indicated that mental workload associated with the occurrence of needlestick injuries, whereas cognitive failure did not show a significant direct effect. Mediation analyses using both Sobel and bootstrap methods demonstrated that neither mental workload nor cognitive failure significantly mediated the relationship between shift work and needlestick incidence. Therefore, the hypothesized mediation pathways were not supported by the data. Although shift work showed a direct association with injury occurrence, the absence of significant indirect effects suggests that the relationship operates independently of the cognitive variables examined in this study. Potential mechanisms such as circadian rhythm disruption, sleep disturbance, and fatigue accumulation have been proposed in prior literature; however, these explanations should be interpreted as theoretical considerations rather than empirical findings of the present research. Also, it is important to distinguish between a direct association and a mediating effect. A variable may be significantly associated with an outcome as an independent factor, yet not meet the statistical criteria required for mediation. In the present study, although mental workload was significantly associated with needlestick injuries, the indirect pathway linking shift work to injuries through mental workload was not statistically significant. Therefore, mental workload appears to function as an independent associated factor rather than as a mediating variable in this relationship.

The significant role of mental workload aligns with the Job Demands–Resources model and the Job Demand–Control framework [42], which posit that high job demands consume cognitive and emotional resources, compromising safety behaviors. Elevated workload can reduce adherence to standard precautions, accelerate procedural steps, and increase fatigue, collectively heightening the risk of sharps-related injuries [43]. Iranian studies also corroborate this pattern, showing that nurses with higher perceived workload are more prone to needlestick injuries, particularly in high-acuity units such as ICUs and emergency departments [35, 44]. Interestingly, cognitive failure did not significantly predict needlestick injuries, despite theoretical expectations. While cognitive failures are generally linked to human error and task mistakes [21, 41], needlestick injuries appear to be more task-based and environment-driven, often resulting from high patient loads, insufficient staffing, procedural shortcuts, and inadequate safety equipment. These findings suggest that cognitive failure alone may not be sufficient to explain injury risk unless combined with operational and organizational stressors. Additionally, cognitive failure may act indirectly through fatigue, burnout, or safety climate, which were not captured as mediators in the current study [29]. The lack of a mediating effect of workload and cognitive failure in the relationship between shift work and needlestick injuries underscores the multifactorial nature of occupational hazards. Shift work directly affects injury risk, likely through circadian disruption, sleep deprivation, psychomotor impairment, and decreased vigilance [38]. Other unmeasured contextual factors such as safety climate, management support, and adherence to protective protocols may also modulate this relationship [45]. Collectively, these findings reinforce the notion that organizational and structural interventions are paramount in preventing needlestick injuries. Recommendations include optimizing shift schedules, implementing fatigue risk management programs, providing adequate staffing and resources, ensuring safety-engineered needles, and fostering a positive safety climate. While individual cognitive training and awareness remain important, the direct effect of workload and shift work highlights the necessity of systemic solutions that address the underlying occupational hazards. From a managerial perspective, healthcare administrators should prioritize organizational policies and workflow designs that mitigate workload peaks, particularly in rotating shifts. Proactive monitoring of staffing levels, scheduled rest breaks, rotation planning, and ergonomically informed task assignments can reduce exposure to sharps injuries. These findings provide evidence-based guidance for hospital leadership to enhance nurse safety, reduce injury rates, and maintain patient care quality while supporting occupational well-being [46].

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, Given the cross-sectional design of this study, causal and temporal relationships cannot be established. The observed associations should therefore be interpreted with caution, and longitudinal research is required to determine the directionality and underlying mechanisms of these relationships. Second, the study was conducted in a single hospital in Isfahan, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings with different organizational structures, staffing patterns, or safety protocols. The other limitation of this study was the potential for recall bias and over-reporting associated with self-administered questionnaires. Participants may have overestimated the frequency of needlestick injuries due to the high-stress environment or difficulty recalling specific incidents over a 6-month period. Consequently, the reported prevalence of 72% should be interpreted with caution, although the contextual factors strongly suggest a genuinely high risk of injury in this setting. Additionally, some potential confounding variables such as safety climate, staffing levels, and compliance with standard precautions were not assessed and may have influenced the results. Also, there was the temporal mismatch between the assessment of needlestick injuries and the predictor variables. While needlestick injuries were assessed retrospectively over a 6-month period, mental workload and cognitive failure were measured at a single time point. This cross-sectional design limits our ability to establish the temporal ordering required to infer causality or confirm mediation pathways. Therefore, the mediation analysis presented here should be interpreted as exploratory, and the findings cannot support definitive mechanistic interpretations or causal claims regarding the role of workload and cognitive failure in precipitating needlestick injuries. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify these temporal relationships. Finally, participation was voluntary, and a small number of eligible nurses declined involvement, which could introduce non-response bias. Future multicenter studies with larger and more diverse samples are recommended to enhance external validity and explore other organizational and psychological predictors of needlestick injuries.

Conclusion

The present study modeled the pathway from shift work to needlestick injuries among nurses by examining the potential mediating roles of mental workload and cognitive failures. The findings revealed a notably high prevalence of needlestick injuries, suggesting that exposure to sharp-related hazards remains a persistent occupational challenge in nursing practice. Shift work emerged as a significant predictor of needlestick injuries, exerting a direct effect independent of individual cognitive factors. Furthermore, mental workload demonstrated a non-significant and contribution to injury occurrence, indicating that excessive cognitive demands may compromise adherence to safe practices and increase vulnerability to occupational accidents. Although cognitive failures were conceptually expected to intensify the risk of injury, their direct effect was not statistically significant, suggesting that acute errors may arise more from task overload and environmental stressors than from enduring cognitive limitations. The mediation analysis offered additional insight into the underlying mechanisms of occupational injury risk. Contrary to the hypothesized model, neither mental workload nor cognitive failures mediated the effect of shift work on needlestick incidence. This suggests that the influence of shift work on safety outcomes is more structural than cognitive, likely linked to circadian rhythm disruption, sleep deprivation, and fatigue accumulation. These findings support a growing body of evidence indicating that occupational safety in healthcare is a multidimensional phenomenon shaped by organizational conditions as well as individual factors. Overall, this study underscores the importance of addressing work design and systemic constraints as key determinants of nurse safety. While cognitive performance remains an essential component of safe care delivery, the results emphasize that reducing excessive workload and optimizing shift allocation are more critical in preventing needlestick injuries than interventions targeting individual cognitive enhancement alone. Future research should extend this model by incorporating organizational variables such as safety climate, staffing adequacy, and fatigue management to develop a more comprehensive understanding of injury pathways in clinical settings.
