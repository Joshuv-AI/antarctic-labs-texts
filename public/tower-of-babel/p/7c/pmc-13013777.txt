Introduction

Prurigo nodularis (PN) is a rare neuroimmune inflammatory skin condition characterized by intense, chronic pruritus lasting > 6 weeks and nodular lesions distributed symmetrically on the arms, legs, and trunk [1–4]. PN is estimated to affect 37 to 72 per 100,000 people in the US [4–6] and is more common in females, older adults, and Black populations [1]. The chronic pruritus in PN is the most intense of the various inflammatory dermatoses and can greatly reduce quality of life, especially by disrupting sleep and causing psychological distress [2, 7, 8].

Treatment of PN aims to reduce itch, heal the skin, and improve quality of life [1, 9, 10]. A variety of medications may be used off-label, including locally acting agents, phototherapy, systemic immunomodulators, and systemic neuromodulators; however, there are no formal guidelines [1, 11, 12]. Two monoclonal antibodies—dupilumab, approved in 2022 [13], and nemolizumab, approved in the US in 2024 [14]—are currently the only treatments approved for PN, although other drugs are in development [15]. Treatment decisions are based on clinical judgment, considering individual patients’ characteristics, disease severity, lesion distribution, comorbidities, potential treatment side effects, and impact on quality of life [10, 15].

Therefore, understanding patient perspectives about their symptoms and their impact can help guide treatment strategies. The US Food and Drug Administration considers that people with lived experience are “uniquely positioned to inform the understanding of the therapeutic context for drug development and evaluation” [16, 17]. This information is usually collected through qualitative research and can be summarized in a conceptual disease model (CDM), which can help clarify the concepts most important to patients and visualize how the signs and symptoms of a disease relate to functional status, quality of life, health perception, and outlook [18]. This information can also help select clinical outcome measures for clinical trials and support treatment decisions in clinical practice. Here, we developed a CDM for PN symptoms and impacts based on our previous qualitative research of US adults and their perspectives on living with severe PN combined with the results of a targeted literature review [19].

Methods

An initial CDM for PN symptoms and impacts was developed based on previous qualitative research [19] and further informed by a targeted literature review. All concepts synthesized for the CDM were restricted to prurigo nodularis and were not extrapolated from studies of other pruritic conditions. The framework for the development of the CDM for PN considered previous CDMs for chronic itch [20] and atopic dermatitis [21] and was informed by the Wilson and Cleary conceptual model of patient outcomes, which links clinical variables to health-related quality of life through five hierarchical components: (1) biological and physiological variables, (2) symptom status, (3) functional status, (4) general health perceptions, and (5) overall quality of life, with two modifying factors, individual characteristics and environmental characteristics [18]. As the model is intentionally focused on patient-reported symptoms and impacts, the first component of biological and physiological variables underlying PN, such as neuroimmune inflammation, is considered an upstream driver of disease and will not be depicted explicitly. The CDM was finalized with input from clinicians specializing in PN treatment (authors SGK and CP).

For the targeted literature review, Medline and Embase were searched via Ovid for published or in-press articles on PN in English from January 1, 2011, to April 11, 2025, excluding case studies or reports, in vitro studies, and animal studies. The Ovid search algorithm and results are shown in Table S1. Titles and abstracts were screened by a first reviewer to ensure their relevance to the objectives. To be considered for inclusion, articles had to include studies on adults (≥ 18 years old) who had been diagnosed with PN and had to report patient views, perceptions, and experience of symptoms and their impact on patients’ mental state or functioning/quality of life, including more distal outcomes such as productivity or time lost from work. Studies could be qualitative (qualitative interviews, focus group discussions), clinical trials, observational, cross-sectional, or systematic literature reviews or meta-analyses of observational studies. Rejected records were checked by a second reviewer to confirm exclusion.

Ethical Approval

Studies involving human participants (the concept elicitation/cognitive debriefing interviews) were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from an institutional review board and/or ethics committee. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Results

Studies Included

The automated search identified 285 peer-reviewed publications, of which 31 articles describing symptoms and impacts in patients with PN were included (Fig. 1). These included 5 reviews [22–26] and 26 qualitative observational studies (Table S2): 18 prospective [7, 19, 27–42], 7 retrospective [43–49], and 1 study that could not clearly be classified as either retrospective or prospective [50]. Most of the studies (19/31) assessed disease burden, quality of life, symptoms, and neuropsychiatric comorbidities related to PN using questionnaires [7, 19, 27–38, 40–42, 45, 50]. Seven studies used patient history retrieved from patient records or national patient registries [39, 43, 44, 46–49] (Table S2).

Symptoms of PN

Patients with PN may experience a combination of different symptoms, but the main symptom is the presence of hyperkeratotic nodules, papules, or plaques that are crusted or excoriated and accompanied by chronic, severe continuous, paroxysmal, or sporadic pruritus [25, 26] (Table S2). Patients characterized the itch as severe, disturbing, agonizing, or intractable, and they considered it their most bothersome symptom [19, 35, 37, 44]. In one study, itch was described as the worst of all symptoms [19]. Patients described PN-associated itch as constant [27], lasting all day [27], or lasting for > 12 h every day [30]. As measured by numerical rating scales, itch from PN was perceived as more severe than itch from other dermatological conditions [45].

Other common burdensome symptoms of PN can include pain, burning, dry skin, xerosis, bleeding, prickling, and stinging [19, 35, 44]. Patients with PN may also experience sensations of tingling, hot, or cold [19].

Impacts of PN Symptoms

PN has profound impacts on patients’ quality of life, with individuals experiencing sleep disturbance, psychological distress, depression, interference with daily activities, reduced work productivity, presenteeism, absenteeism, psychiatric disorders, and social isolation [26, 35, 38]. PN is associated with more frequent psychiatric disorders, including eating disorders, self-harm, attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder, schizophrenia, depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation/self-harm, and psychosis [5, 22, 26, 40, 42, 44]. Two studies have suggested that psychological and psychiatric disorders are experienced by nearly half of patients with PN [38, 42]. Low self-esteem, difficulty concentrating, and difficulty enjoying oneself due to anxiety and distress are also common in patients with PN [42]. A study by Pereira et al. found that one in five patients considered visibility of skin lesions among the most burdensome symptom of PN. Almost half reported that the disease negatively affects their mood, and approximately one in three reported that it interferes with personal relationships [35]. The same study found that sleep was impaired in just under half of patients with PN [35]. The extent of sleep disturbance in PN correlates with the severity of nocturnal itch [50] and appears to be worse than in other dermatologic diseases [45]. Other studies suggest that depression [40] or feelings of worry [42] are linked with sleep disturbance in PN, although another study found that “depression/sadness” and “anger/irritability” did not significantly correlate with PN or sleep disturbance [50]. Lastly, one study found that the extent of impairment in quality of life by PN correlates with itch severity [35].

Clinicians Input

Following the synthesis of qualitative and literature-derived concepts, the draft CDM was reviewed by two dermatologists with extensive experience in treating PN (SGK and CP). Their input focused on confirming the clinical relevance of symptom groupings, validating the central role of the itch-scratch cycle, and ensuring that the directional relationships among symptoms, mediating factors, and impacts depicted in Fig. 2 were consistent with real-world clinical presentation.

Conceptual Disease Model

In the present CDM (Fig. 2), symptom status is represented by core PN manifestations, including pruritus and itch-related characteristics (e.g., intensity, frequency, duration), skin lesions, and associated sensory symptoms (e.g., pain, burning, stinging, bleeding). Functional status is reflected through impacts on sleep and daily activities, while broader emotional, social, and work-related impacts correspond to the general health perception and overall quality-of-life components of the Wilson-Cleary model. Mediating factors included in the CDM (e.g., exposure to triggers and environmental conditions) align conceptually with the individual and environmental modifiers described by Wilson and Cleary, although these are not modeled as independent outcome domains.

Discussion

This study developed a CDM for PN symptoms and impacts based on our previous qualitative research [19] coupled with a targeted literature review. In line with our previous qualitative research, the targeted literature review found that itch is the most bothersome and frequent symptom of PN. Other burdensome symptoms include pain, stinging, burning, and bleeding. These symptoms have a tremendous impact on patients’ quality of life, including affecting emotional wellbeing, sleep, and daily functioning. In the CDM (Fig. 2), the primary clinical manifestations of PN were itchy lumps or bumps and lesions or sores, but the various symptoms and impacts of PN are all interlinked: itching leads to scratching, which in turn results in bleeding/scabbing, inflammation/infection, unpleasant sensations, and physical changes in the skin. Most impacts of PN, such as those on sleep and emotions, are directly linked to itching, although for some impacts the link is indirect. For example, daily activities and work productivity can be indirectly affected by itching and scratching at night because of sleep disturbance. Collectively, the current CDM outlines the significant effects of the itch-PN scratch cycle on patients' lives, identifying these effects as crucial targets for a comprehensive treatment management plan. Moreover, the present findings could serve as a basis for developing a patient-reported outcome tool specifically designed for studies in patients with PN.

Content validity for the CDM from a clinical perspective was provided by experienced clinicians who thoroughly evaluated the model and determined that it was suitable and comprehensive for understanding and addressing the complexities of PN [51]. The clinicians' expertise ensured that the model accurately reflects the multidimensional character of the disease, incorporating essential clinical observations and patient experiences. Their contributions have validated the model's relevance and applicability in clinical settings, providing a robust foundation for further research and therapeutic interventions in PN.

To our knowledge, this is the first CDM for PN and is aligned with another developed by Silverberg et al. for chronic itch, in which biologically and physiologically driven factors have direct and indirect impacts, including pain and sleep disturbance, which, in turn, can lead to impaired function, task avoidance, stigma, disruption of social life and relationships, emotional disturbances, and increased treatment burden [20]. Our CDM is also in accordance with one for chronic itch of unknown origin, which centers on the itch-scratch cycle [52], and two others for atopic dermatitis, where nocturnal scratching affects skin appearance and sleep, which in turn influences daily activities [53, 54].

Interpretation of our findings should consider the study’s limitations. This study focused exclusively on prurigo nodularis, and all concepts included in the CDM were derived from studies of patients diagnosed with PN. However, PN shares overlapping symptoms and impacts with other chronic pruritic conditions, such as atopic dermatitis. As a result, some patient-reported experiences described in the literature may reflect broader pruritus-related burdens rather than PN-specific features alone. Nonetheless, the synthesis and interpretation of findings were restricted to PN-relevant concepts, consistent with the study objective. The qualitative research on the proposed CDM of PN used a modest sample of patients; nevertheless, saturation of symptom concepts was achieved. In addition, the population consisted of a convenience sample of mainly college-educated and White populations, thereby limiting the generalizability of results to broader populations. A strength, however, is that the qualitative findings were supplemented by a targeted literature review covering a broader period, extending until very recently. Also, by grounding the CDM in the Wilson-Cleary framework while prioritizing concepts most salient to patients, the model provides a transparent, patient-centered representation of the lived experience of adults with PN.

Conclusions

This CDM can help in understanding how PN-associated pruritus disrupts quality of life in patients. This model can also help in the selection of key endpoints in clinical trials; for example, measuring sleep disturbance with a patient-reported outcome measure may be useful in assessing the efficacy of treatments for PN.
