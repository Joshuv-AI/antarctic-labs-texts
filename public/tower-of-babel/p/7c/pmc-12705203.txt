Effect of digital navigation during anterior-approach total hip arthroplasty on patient outcomes
Authors: Manjot S Birk, Panayiotis D Megaloikonomos, Bryan Heard, Teresa Nguyen, Rajrishi Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Fluoroscopic guidance is a well-established technique in anterior-approach total hip arthroplasty (AA-THA) to enhance appropriate implant size and positioning, and to address potential complications, such as leg-length discrepancy (LLD). The primary objective of this study was to assess whether the implementation of a noninvasive fluoroscopy-assisted digital-navigation software improved the accuracy of postoperative leg length in AA-THA.Methods:We conducted a retrospective cohort study involving patients who were identified from the prospectively collected patient-level provincial administrative data repositories. We extracted outcome data and measured leg lengths on postoperative radiographs.Results:A total of 435 patients were identified. After exclusion, 400 patients were included in the study, with 247 in the navigation-software cohort. There was no significant difference between the groups with respect to demographic data. In the navigation-software cohort, we found no significant difference between the achieved LLD and zero LLD, whereas we found a significant difference in the fluoroscopy-only cohort (p < 0.001). The navigation-software cohort required more fluoroscopy time and significantly reduced case time.Conclusion:The use of navigation software in AA-THA is safe and effective to achieve more accurate postoperative leg length. Despite the increase in fluoroscopic time, there was a small but statistically significant reduction in operative time. The value of such technology would be expected to substantially increase if used during the learning curve when introducing AA-THA to contemporary practice.

Arthritis is the most common form of joint disease, with radiographic evidence estimated in 80% of the population by the age of 65 years and a lifetime risk of developing hip osteoarthritis of 25%.1–3 In Canada, total hip arthroplasty (THA) is among the most performed inpatient surgeries.4 Before pandemic-related shutdowns, 62 016 THAs were performed in 2018/19 in Canada, an annual increase of 20.1%.4 Total hip arthroplasty is a highly successful surgery with increasing demand. However, complications can occur, leading to revision surgeries and, consequently, a substantial cost to health care systems. The most common reasons for revision surgery are prosthetic joint infection, aseptic loosening, and instability.4,5 Another important complication is leg-length discrepancy (LLD), which has a reported incidence of 1% to 27%.4–6 Leg-length discrepancy has been associated with back pain, sciatica, neuritis, gait disorders, dislocation, component loosening, and negative patient-reported outcomes.7 Unsurprisingly, it is one of the most common reasons for litigation after THA.8,9

Conventionally, orthopedic surgeons rely on preoperative templating to estimate several parameters, such as approximate limb lengthening, acetabular cup position, acetabular cup size, neck cut, stem position, and stem size. The ideal position of the implants is an ever-debated topic, and the need to customize position based on several patient factors, such as anatomy, spinopelvic relationship, and other such factors, is well accepted; however, placing implants outside of the accepted position can increase complication risks.10–13 The accuracy of digital 2-dimensional templating has been shown to be greater than 70% for within 1 prosthesis size, and evidence supports continued routine use.14,15 However, cup placement and leg symmetry are reported to be accurate in only 63% and 67% of cases, respectively.16 Further, traditional free-hand methods of implantation of the prostheses within 5 degrees of the intended target can be as low as 21.5%.17 These limitations may be reduced by fluoroscopic-assisted anterior-approach THA (AA-THA), as the benefit of the supine positioning with a radiolucent table and fluoroscopy can greatly assist in component positioning and verification.18

Novel techniques have their inherent limitations that restrict surgeons from performing this anterior approach, such as the time and dedication required to overcome the learning curve and potential increased complication during this time. The learning curve varies on surgeon experience and confidence, but the literature suggests the learning curve to be between 20 and 100 cases, with 50 cases as the best identified number.19–21 Further, newer, noninvasive digital-navigation software can provide information to help surgeons fine-tune implant position and further reduce potential complications. The role of digital fluoroscopic navigation has been shown in the literature to improve implant position and LLD; however, further studies are required.20–22

The primary objective of this study was to assess whether the implementation of a noninvasive fluoroscopy-assisted digital-navigation software improved the accuracy of postoperative leg length in AA-THA. Secondary outcomes included length of operative time, fluoroscopy time, 30-day readmission, and postoperative mechanical and medical complications after the first 25 cases of AA-THA.

Methods

We performed a retrospective cohort study on a single fellowship-trained surgeon (R.S.) between 2016 and 2022. We collected deidentified data from the prospectively collected patient-level provincial administrative data repositories that are stored and maintained by the Alberta Bone and Joint Health Institute (ABJHI). We performed the investigations at Peter Lougheed Centre, Calgary, Alberta.

We included all patients older than 18 years undergoing primary AA-THA for osteoarthritis, inflammatory arthritis, and posttraumatic arthritis. We excluded patients who underwent any other surgical approach or revision surgery, or with known infection. Additionally, we excluded the first 25 patients to remove effects of the learning curve for AA-THA. Another 10 patients were excluded after the implementation of the navigation system to remove the impact of the learning curve for the navigation system.

The Depuy Synthes VELYS Hip Navigation was used for the digital-navigation software, which is a noninvasive, fluoroscopic-assisted system. An intraoperative radiograph with 10 degrees of internal rotation of the patient’s legs, which includes the greater trochanter, femoral diaphysis, pubic symphysis, and supra-acetabular pelvic bone, was taken. The interoperative radiograph was then transferred to the Depuy Synthes VELYS Hip Navigation workstation. The software was used to place a reference at the greater trochanter for a landmark (Figure 1). The centre of rotation was approximated by placing a circle of best fit around the femoral head (Figure 2). The long axis of the femur was aligned and then a reference line was generated from arbitrarily identifiable, selected points from the supracetabular pelvis to the pubic symphysis (Figure 3). The teardrop was marked for reference (Figure 4). Component sizes were entered into the software, and trial component images were imported. The greater trochanter, shoulder of femoral implant, pelvis reference line, and teardrop were marked on intraoperative images. The surgeon then confirmed point placement and satisfaction with component placement and sizing (Figure 5).

Patients were classified into 2 separate consecutive cohorts: those who underwent surgery without the use of a navigation system and those who underwent surgery with navigation. We extracted outcome data from the ABJHI and fluoroscopy time from the radiology department software. Standard 6-week postoperative radiography was performed, and leg lengths were measured, duplicated, and verified by all authors. We resolved any discrepancy by consensus.

We performed the leg-length measurement primarily using inferior acetabular teardrops to superior aspect of lesser trochanter distance whenever possible. If we were unable to assess the teardrop, we used the transichial line. We compared patient demographic data, including age, sex, and body mass index, using the t test for continuous variables and χ2 or Fisher exact tests for the different accuracy thresholds, with p values set at less than 0.05 for significance. We recorded operative time in minutes and complications, including 30-day readmission, mechanical events, and medical events, for all cases.

Ethics approval

The Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board at the University of Calgary approved the study (REB22-0181) and waived the requirement for individual consent. Data were deidentified before release to the investigators.

Results

A total of 435 patients were identified. After exclusion, a total of 400 patients (400 hips) were included in this study: 247 patients (247 hips) were designated to the cohort receiving AA-THA with fluoroscopy-assisted digital-navigation software, and 153 patients (153 hips) were designated to the cohort receiving conventional AA-THA with fluoroscopy without digital-navigation software (Figure 6 and Table 1). There was no significant difference between the groups with respect to age (mean 66 ± standard deviation [SD] 10 yr, p = 0.9), sex (230 female patients [57.5%], p = 0.5), and body mass index (mean 28 ± SD 5, p = 0.7). Further, there was no statistical difference in preoperative LLD between the groups (p = 0.5).

The cohort receiving conventional AA-THA with fluoroscopy only (without digital-navigation software) had a wider spread of achieved LLD when compared with zero LLD, which was significantly different (Figure 7). The cohort receiving AA-THA with fluoroscopy-assisted digital navigation had a narrower spread of achieved LLD when compared with zero LLD, which was not significantly different (Figure 8). Therefore, in the digital-navigation cohort, use of the software increased accuracy by allowing closer achievement of zero postoperative LLD. The navigation software required more fluoroscopy time, with median times of 0.33 minutes before implementation and 0.55 minutes after implementation of digital navigation (p = 0.004). However, the digital-navigation cohort had a significantly reduced median case time of 103 minutes, compared with 107 minutes in the fluoroscopy-only cohort (p = 0.02). Further, with the use of the digital-navigation system, there was no increase in 30-day readmission (p > 0.9); no increase in mechanical events, including fracture and dislocation; and no increase in medical events (p = 0.5).

Discussion

Total hip arthroplasty remains a largely successful intervention with complication rates reported at 4.2% for major complication and 2.17% for minor complication.23 The demand for hip arthroplasty continues to rise in Canada as the population continues to age, which is in line with worldwide trends. Before pandemic-related shutdowns, there was an annual increase in THAs of 20.1%, and this number is expected to increase.4 Although the rate of complication remains low, the total number of cases with complications will increase with an increasing number of THA surgeries. Surgeons have turned to the use of intraoperative fluoroscopy to improve outcomes of THA by optimizing implant position. Previously, Leucht and colleagues showed that fluoroscopy during anterior approach significantly decreased LLD compared with posterior approach without fluoroscopy.24 Further, some have implemented digital-navigation software in conjunction with fluoroscopy to improve implant position and reduce complications. In this study, we sought to compare outcomes of a group who underwent AA-THA with fluoroscopy-assisted digital-navigation software compared with a group with fluoroscopy without digital-navigation software. We investigated whether the use of fluoroscopy and digital-navigation software would reduce LLD compared with intraoperative fluoroscopy alone.

Our data showed a statistically significant difference between the 2 groups; the cohort receiving AA-THA with fluoroscopy-assisted digital navigation had increased accuracy in achieving zero postoperative LLD compared with the cohort receiving AA-THA with fluoroscopy without digital navigation. These results support the use of digital-navigation software with fluoroscopy when performing AA-THA. O’Leary and colleagues found similar results supporting digital navigation in reducing the incidence of LLD.25

In our study, the overall median fluoroscopy time was increased from 0.33 minutes to 0.55 minutes for the digital-navigation cohort. Jinnai and colleagues reported 0.83 minutes of intraoperative fluoroscopy for AA-THA without the use of digital navigation.26 Furthermore, Jinnai and colleagues showed that intraoperative radiation exposure and fluoroscopy time for AA-THA is significantly lower than that of osteosynthesis for proximal femoral fracture and does not exceed the annual cumulative tissue radiation dose limit.26 Other studies have reported on radiation exposure during AA-THA with variable measurement methods; however, these studies report minimal risk.27–30

Tischler and colleagues reviewed 341 cases undergoing primary total hip arthroplasty with and without intraoperative fluoroscopic guidance, and assessed accuracy of prothesis placement, operative time, and postoperative complications.31 The mean operative time (skin-to-skin time) was significantly higher in the fluoroscopic-guidance cohort, from 59.8 minutes to 52.8 minutes. As such, the authors discouraged the use of fluoroscopy in primary total hip arthroplasty. In our study, the use of digital navigation significantly reduced case time (patient in and out of room) from 107 minutes to 103 minutes. Although this finding is statistically significant, the difference of 4 minutes is likely not clinically relevant. However, it addresses previously reported concerns of increased operative time and cost.

Limitations

This is a single-surgeon retrospective study of prospectively collected data conducted at a single institution. However, we hope that the large sample of 400 patients may reduce this inherent potential bias. Surgeon experience is another concern as the cohorts were consecutively treated.

The authors examined leg-length measurements using the inferior acetabular teardrop as a pelvic reference to the superior aspect of lesser trochanter distance, which is superior to transischial line as a pelvic reference.32 However, if the authors were unable to assess the teardrop, the transischial line was used; thus, the possibility of variability in measurements exists. However, by duplicating the measurements and achieving consensus, we hope to have reduced the variability.

Conclusion

We showed that there is a significant difference in achieved postoperative LLD between conventional fluoroscopy alone and fluoroscopy-assisted digital navigation compared with zero postoperative LLD. Therefore, our results support the use of digital navigation during AA-THA. We believe that the value of this technology will help reduce the learning curve when introducing AA-THA to contemporary practice.
