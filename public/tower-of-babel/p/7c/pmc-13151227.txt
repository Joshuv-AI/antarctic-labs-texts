Bayesian multilevel analysis of multimorbidity among women in Somalia: prevalence, patterns, and determinants
Authors: Jama Mohamed, Ahmed Ibrahim Farah, Ahmed Ismail Mohamed, Azizur Rahman, Saralees Nadarajah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultimorbidity is a growing public health concern globally, yet remains under-researched in fragile and conflict-affected settings, including Somalia, a country in the Horn of Africa with limited health infrastructure. This study investigates the prevalence, regional patterns, and individual- and contextual-level determinants of multimorbidity among women aged 15–49 years in Somalia.MethodsA cross-sectional analytical study design was applied using nationally representative data from the 2020 Somalia Demographic and Health Survey (DHS). The final sample included 8,172 women after excluding cases with missing or incomplete information. Multimorbidity was defined as the presence of ≥ 2 self-reported chronic conditions. Bayesian multilevel logistic regression was employed to account for hierarchical data structure— individuals (Level 1) nested within regions and residence types (Level 2). Random intercept and slope models were compared using Deviance Information Criterion (DIC), with posterior distributions estimated via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulation. Associations were assessed using odds ratios (ORs) and 95% Bayesian credible intervals (CrIs).ResultsThe prevalence of single morbidity was 7.2% (95%CI: 6.64%–7.76%), while multimorbidity was 2.1% (95%CI: 1.78%–2.40%), with higher burden among urban residents and certain regions (e.g., Banadir, Bay, Sool). Hypertension, kidney disease, and chronic headache were the most common conditions. Model III (random intercept and slopes) provided the best fit (DIC = 1525.051). Key determinants of higher multimorbidity risk included food insecurity (OR = 2.939, 95%CrI: 2.280–3.775), obesity (OR = 1.601, 95%CrI: 1.175–2.093), older maternal age (OR = 1.047, 95%CrI: 1.026–1.066), lack of internet access (OR = 1.254, 95%CrI: 1.084–1.471), and longer water-fetching time (Up to 30 min, OR = 1.798). Protective factors included rural (OR = 0.658, 95%CrI: 0.464–0.809) and nomadic (OR = 0.349, 95%CrI: 0.250–0.453) residence, non-smoking (OR = 0.641, 95%CrI: 0.434–0.988), lack of agricultural land ownership (OR = 0.563, 95%CrI: 0.484–0.653), and toilet non-sharing (OR = 0.664, 95%CrI: 0.570–0.813).ConclusionsMultimorbidity affects a notable proportion of Somali women, with considerable geographic and socioeconomic variation. The findings underscore the need for regionally tailored, multisectoral interventions targeting modifiable risk factors such as food insecurity, obesity, and access to healthcare infrastructure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-026-01905-3.

Background

As life expectancy rises globally, multimorbidity (i.e., defined as the presence of two or more chronic diseases in an individual) has emerged as a pressing public health concern, challenging health systems that were primarily designed to manage single diseases [1]. The global burden of chronic conditions is increasing rapidly, with non-communicable diseases (NCDs) accounting for the majority of cases [2]. Notably, approximately 80% of NCDs occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where health systems are often under-resourced and less prepared to handle the complex needs of individuals living with multiple conditions [3]. The growing prevalence of multimorbidity places immense strain not only on individuals affected by it but also on healthcare professionals and systems that are already overburdened [4].

Traditionally, research on multimorbidity has focused on high-income countries (HICs), where population aging first highlighted the health and economic implications of managing multiple chronic conditions [5]. However, there is now a global recognition that multimorbidity is also a critical issue in LMICs, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where urbanization, epidemiological transition, and increasing life expectancy are driving a surge in NCD prevalence [1, 4]. Despite SSA having a relatively young population, demographic shifts are underway, and the region is experiencing rising rates of chronic disease and multimorbidity, which are expected to exert additional pressure on fragile healthcare systems [6]. Addressing this challenge requires person-centered, integrated, and holistic approaches to prevention and management, as well as a deeper understanding of the shared risk factors and disease clusters most relevant to these settings [1].

The socioeconomic impact of multimorbidity in developing countries is profound. Chronic illnesses and their associated disabilities reduce productivity, increase healthcare costs, and can exacerbate poverty at both household and national levels [7]. Recent systematic reviews [8–10] have revealed significant variation in the prevalence of multimorbidity depending on study design, population characteristics, and setting. Pooled prevalence estimates suggest that multimorbidity affects 42.4% of individuals in HICs, 43% in Latin America [8] and the Caribbean [9], and 36.4% in LMICs [10]. Moreover, multimorbidity is consistently associated with higher mortality, increased healthcare utilization, polypharmacy, and reduced quality of life [11, 12]. A meta-analysis has shown that individuals with two or more chronic conditions are 1.73 times more likely to die than those without any chronic illness [13]. In African contexts, evidence suggests important sex disparities. A study conducted across four African countries (Burkina Faso, Ghana, Kenya, and South Africa) reported a higher prevalence of multimorbidity among women, with nearly 50% affected compared to 35% of men [4]. A study conducted in India reported that environmental, social, and behavioral determinants, including lifestyle, socioeconomic status, and access to care, play crucial roles in the development of multimorbidity [14].

In the African region, the proportion of deaths due to NCDs increased from 24.2% to 37.1% between 2000 and 2019, illustrating how the growing burden of chronic illnesses intensifies multimorbidity trends [15]. Globally, cardiovascular and metabolic conditions, as well as mental health and musculoskeletal disorders, are the most frequently co-occurring diseases, while depression, hypertension, and diabetes are often highlighted as common multimorbid clusters [11]. These conditions are not only medically complex but are also associated with social consequences such as loneliness and economic hardship, which are exacerbated in resource-limited environments [12].

Somalia, located in the Horn of Africa, has experienced decades of political instability and protracted conflict since the collapse of its central government in 1991, which has severely weakened public institutions, including the health system. The country is characterized by diverse geographic, social, and economic conditions, comprising urban, rural, and nomadic populations across multiple climatic zones, with varying access to health services and infrastructure. It has a largely young population, widespread poverty, and is highly vulnerable to climate-related shocks such as recurrent droughts and floods, which exacerbate food insecurity, malnutrition, and overall disease burden. In Sub-Saharan Africa, including Somalia, local data on multimorbidity remain scarce. Nevertheless, the rising prevalence of NCDs suggests that similar patterns of disease clustering and health system strain are emerging. Understanding the distribution, trends, and determinants of multimorbidity in these settings is essential for informing early prevention strategies, integrated care models, and policy responses. Against this backdrop, the present study aims to assess the determinants, trends, and prevalence of multimorbidity among Somali women, providing much-needed evidence to inform health system planning and interventions in the region.

Materials and methods

Study design and settings

This study employed a cross-sectional analytical design using data from the 2020 Somalia Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) (https://microdata.nbs.gov.so/index.php/catalog/50), which collected data between 2018 and 2019 and is the first nationally representative health survey conducted in the country [16]. The DHS was implemented by the Directorate of National Statistics of the Federal Government of Somalia, in collaboration with international partners. The survey covered 16 of the 18 administrative regions of Somalia, excluding Middle Juba and Lower Shabelle due to security concerns. The detailed map illustrating the regions included in the analysis, as well as those excluded due to missing or unavailable data, is provided in Fig. 1. The data collection targeted women aged 15–49 years residing in sampled households across all included regions and residence types. This design allowed for the examination of multimorbidity prevalence and its associated factors within and across different geographic and social contexts in Somalia. Therefore, a multilevel modeling approach was adopted in this study, with individuals (Level 1) nested within regions and residence type (Level 2) to account for spatial heterogeneity in multimorbidity patterns and determinants.

Sampling procedure

The sampling strategy for this study followed a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design, consistent with the methodology used in the 2020 Somalia Demographic and Health Survey (DHS). In the first stage, 1,433 enumeration areas (EAs), also referred to as clusters, were randomly selected from a national sampling frame of 9,136 EAs, which covered 16 out of the 18 regions in Somalia (excluding Middle Juba and Lower Shabelle due to insecurity). In the second stage, 16,360 households were systematically selected from the sampled clusters. All women aged 15–49 years who were usual residents or who had stayed the night before the survey in the selected households were eligible for interview. A total of 18,155 eligible women aged 15–49 years were identified in the survey. Of these, 8,172 women were included in the final analysis after excluding participants with missing or incomplete data on the outcome variable or key covariates, while 9,983 women (approximately 55%) were excluded due to missing information on study variables. The sample selection process is illustrated in Fig. 2. To assess the potential for selection bias resulting from these exclusions, a supplementary comparison of key sociodemographic characteristics between included and excluded participants was conducted (Supplementary Table S1).

Study variables

Outcome variable

The primary outcome variable in this study was multimorbidity, defined as the presence of two or more self-reported chronic conditions in the same individual, and coded as a binary variable (1 = multimorbidity present, 0 = no multimorbidity). Chronic conditions were identified using self-reported diagnoses or symptoms recorded in the DHS household member (PR) file, an approach widely used in population-based multimorbidity studies, particularly in low-resource settings where clinical records and biomarkers are limited [17, 18]. Given the absence of a universally agreed list of chronic diseases for multimorbidity research, all chronic conditions available in the survey that reflected long-term or recurrent health problems were included. In total, 19 chronic conditions were used to construct the multimorbidity measure (Table 1). Each condition was coded as present/absent and all conditions were weighted equally in the multimorbidity count (i.e., no differential weighting was applied).

Predictor variables

Predictor variables were selected based on a conceptual framework informed by the multimorbidity and social determinants of health literature, rather than solely by data availability. Prior research indicates that multimorbidity is shaped by a broad range of sociodemographic, socioeconomic, behavioral, household, and environmental determinants across diverse settings, including both high- and low-income contexts [19–27]. For example, global systematic reviews have identified age, sex, socioeconomic position (education, income), and behavioral risk factors (e.g., smoking, obesity) as consistent predictors of multimorbidity prevalence and accumulation [19, 20, 22, 23], while other studies emphasize the role of environmental and social conditions in influencing disease clustering and health inequalities [21, 24–26].

In line with this evidence, several variables in our analysis represent well-established determinants of multimorbidity, including age, education, socioeconomic status (wealth index), marital status, and place of residence (urban, rural, or nomadic). Health-related behaviors and conditions such as tobacco use and obesity were included given their robust associations with the development and progression of multiple chronic conditions. Household and environmental characteristics — including household assets (mobile phone, agricultural land, livestock), access to information (radio, television, internet), sanitation and hygiene conditions (toilet type, toilet sharing, presence of handwashing agent, water access), type of cooking fuel, and household food insecurity status — were included to capture material circumstances and living conditions that plausibly influence health exposures and resources. Contextual factors, such as region and urban–rural–nomadic residence, were also included to account for geographic and lifestyle differences linked with multimorbidity patterns in population studies. Some of these variables are exploratory or context-specific due to limited multimorbidity evidence in Somaliland, but their inclusion is supported by broader research on social determinants of chronic disease risk. All variables were appropriately recoded and structured for inclusion in the multivariable models.

Data processing and statistical analysis

Data were obtained from the household member file (SOPR71SV) of the 2020 Somalia Demographic and Health Survey (DHS), which contains detailed information on sociodemographic characteristics, household conditions, and health-related indicators. The dataset was examined for completeness and internal consistency, and observations with missing, inconsistent, or implausible values for the outcome variable or key predictors were excluded using complete-case analysis approach. To evaluate potential bias associated with complete-case analysis, key characteristics including region of residence, education, and household wealth quintile were compared between women included in the analysis and those excluded due to missing data (please see the supplementary file associated with this manuscript). The comparison indicated statistically significant differences between the two groups suggesting that missingness was not completely random and should be considered when interpreting the study findings. All variables included in the analysis were recoded where necessary to ensure consistency and interpretability, and new binary indicators were generated for derived measures including multimorbidity, obesity, and food insecurity. Categorical variables were coded as factors, while continuous variables such as maternal age and age of the household head were assessed for plausibility and distributional consistency.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the study population. Frequencies and percentages were reported for categorical variables, while means and standard deviations were used for continuous variables. Graphical summaries, including bar charts and pie charts, were used to illustrate the distribution of key variables such as region, type of residence, and the prevalence of multimorbidity.

The Somalia DHS employs a complex sampling design involving stratification, clustering, and unequal probabilities of selection. In the present study, descriptive statistics and prevalence estimates were calculated using the unweighted analytical sample to describe the characteristics of women included in the analysis. Because the study excluded a substantial proportion of participants due to missing data and focused primarily on examining associations and determinants of multimorbidity using a Bayesian multilevel modeling framework, sampling weights were not applied. Clustering was accounted for in the regression analysis through random intercepts for region and residence type.

MCMC estimation and convergence assessment

All models were estimated using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods as implemented in Stata 17 via the bayes: melogit command. Due to the extreme computational cost of the full hierarchical Bayesian model, we used a single long MCMC chain with 10,000 iterations, of which the first 2,500 iterations were discarded as burn-in. No thinning was applied. Convergence was assessed using visual inspection of trace plots and effective sample size diagnostics, which showed no evidence of lack of convergence and indicated adequate sampling efficiency for all key parameters. While multiple chains are generally recommended in Bayesian practice, the present approach was adopted given the high computational cost of the model and is acknowledged as a limitation.

Model comparison and statistical inference

Models were compared using the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC), with lower values indicating a better balance between model fit and complexity. The model with the lowest DIC was selected for final inference. Posterior summaries are reported as posterior means, posterior medians, and 95% Bayesian credible intervals (CrI). Results are presented as posterior odds ratios, and associations were considered statistically significant when the 95% credible interval did not include 1.

Rationale for the Bayesian approach

The Bayesian multilevel modeling framework was chosen over frequentist alternatives because it allows coherent propagation of uncertainty across hierarchical levels, improves estimation of cluster-level effects through partial pooling, and provides full posterior distributions for all parameters [28]. These properties are particularly advantageous for complex survey data with unbalanced cluster sizes and sparse data structures [29], such as the Somalia DHS, and enable more stable and interpretable inference on both fixed and random effects.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study population

A total of 8,172 women from the 2020 Somali Health and Demographic Survey were included in this analysis, and their sociodemographic characteristics are summarized in Table 3. The majority of participants resided in Banadir (22.5%), followed by Woqooyi Galbeed (8.7%) and Togdheer (8.4%), and most lived in urban areas (69.5%), with 27.4% residing in rural areas and 3.1% in nomadic settings. The mean maternal age was 26.1 years (± 8.6), while the average age of household heads was 43.5 years (± 14.4).

Overall living conditions and socioeconomic indicators reflect substantial structural vulnerability. Only 14.9% of households reported internet access, 5.1% had a bank account, and 30.9% were food insecure. Nearly half of the women had never attended school (50.1%), and 13.1% were classified as obese. Access to improved sanitation was reported by 72.3% of households, but most relied on solid fuels for cooking (90.5%), and 69.3% lacked cleansing agents at handwashing facilities.

Prevalence and distribution of multimorbidity

Among the 8,172 women included in the study, the prevalence of single morbidity was 7.20% (95% CI: 6.64%–7.76%), while the prevalence of multimorbidity was 2.09% (95% CI: 1.78%–2.40%) (Fig. 3). Although multimorbidity was less frequent than single morbidity, the presence of multiple coexisting chronic conditions among more than 2% of women represents a meaningful public health burden, particularly in a relatively young population.

Considerable geographic variation was observed (Fig. 4). The highest prevalence of multimorbidity was recorded in Bay (3.50%), Banadir (3.42%), Sool (3.20%) and Hiraan (3.14%), whereas Gedo (0.25%), Bakool (0.30%) and Lower Juba (0.49%) exhibited the lowest levels. By place of residence, multimorbidity was most prevalent in urban areas (2.39%), followed by rural (1.52%) and nomadic populations (0.40%) (Fig. 5). Bayesian multilevel modeling of factors associated with multimorbidity

Results from the final model (Table 5) indicate that multimorbidity among Somali women is shaped by strong geographic, demographic, and socioeconomic gradients. Substantial regional variation persisted after adjustment, with Sool showing significantly higher odds of multimorbidity compared to Awdal (OR = 1.42, 95% CrI: 1.09–1.86), while most other regions exhibited significantly lower odds. Place of residence was also strongly associated: rural (OR = 0.66, 95% CrI: 0.46–0.81) and nomadic women (OR = 0.35, 95% CrI: 0.25–0.45) had markedly lower odds than urban residents.

Age and deprivation-related factors emerged as key determinants. Both maternal age (OR = 1.05 per year) and household head age (OR = 1.01 per year) were positively associated with multimorbidity, indicating accumulation of chronic conditions with age. Food insecurity was one of the strongest predictors (OR = 2.94, 95% CrI: 2.28–3.78), and obesity was also associated with substantially higher odds (OR = 1.60, 95% CrI: 1.18–2.09). Other factors associated with multimorbidity include lack of internet access (OR = 1.26, 95% CrI: 1.08–1.47), and longer fetching time (Up to 30 min, OR = 1.84, 95% CrI: 1.09–2.74). Toilet non-sharing exhibited lower odds OR = 0.67, 95% CrI: 0.57–0.81). Residual between-region and residence-level variance remained (σ² = 0.008), suggesting that unmeasured contextual factors continue to influence multimorbidity risk beyond individual and household characteristics.

Discussion

The findings from this nationally representative study of 8,172 Somali women provide important insights into the patterns and predictors of multimorbidity in Somalia, a context that has received limited attention in global health literature. The overall prevalence of multimorbidity (2.09%) was considerably lower than that of single morbidity (7.20%). While this trend may partially reflect underdiagnosis and limited access to healthcare services, which constrain the detection of coexisting chronic diseases in low-resource and fragile contexts [30], it is important to note that these factors could also affect the detection of single morbidities. Multimorbidity may be disproportionately underestimated because it requires the identification of multiple coexisting conditions, which can be more challenging than detecting a single condition in such settings. Compared to findings in more stable low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where multimorbidity prevalence often exceeds 10% among adults [9, 11, 31], the lower rates observed in Somalia may reflect both genuine demographic health patterns and significant underreporting. By “genuine demographic health patterns,” we refer to population characteristics that inherently influence disease prevalence, such as a relatively young age structure, lower life expectancy, and differing patterns of risk factor exposure compared to older or more urbanized populations. The relatively young maternal age (mean, 26.1 years) in our sample may also contribute to lower multimorbidity estimates, as the risk of multimorbidity increases with age [9, 32–35]. Other factors, such as rural residence, lifestyle behaviors, and varying access to healthcare, may also shape these demographic patterns and partially explain the lower observed prevalence of multimorbidity.

The regional variations in multimorbidity—highest in Bay, Banadir, and Sool—underscore the spatial inequities in health burdens across Somalia. These regions are often affected by conflict, displacement, and fluctuating humanitarian support, which can influence both disease prevalence and access to diagnosis [36, 37]. For example, Banadir’s relatively high multimorbidity may result not only from urban risk factors but also from better diagnostic availability in Mogadishu compared to rural or insecure areas, suggesting that some of the observed differences may reflect detection rather than true disease burden [38]. Similarly, high multimorbidity in Sool, despite moderate single morbidity, may indicate the clustering of vulnerabilities, such as poor water access, inadequate sanitation, and limited health infrastructure, which collectively exacerbate disease risk [39]. These findings align with recent analyses in Nepal and Somalis in Ethiopia, which point to region-specific syndemics—where conflict, displacement, and chronic disease interact [40].

The type of residence significantly influenced disease patterns, with urban women experiencing the highest levels of multimorbidity. Urbanization in Somalia, particularly in areas like Banadir, has been rapid and unplanned, leading to overcrowded informal settlements with poor living conditions, high stress, and limited access to preventive care [38, 41, 42]. At the same time, urban areas may provide better access to healthcare and diagnostics than rural or insecure areas, which could contribute to the higher observed multimorbidity in cities. Our findings are consistent with a growing body of literature suggesting that urban environments in fragile settings do not necessarily confer health advantages due to structural inequalities and service fragmentation [43, 44]. The lower prevalence among rural and nomadic populations could reflect both true differences in lifestyle (e.g., more physical activity, less processed food) and systematic underdiagnosis due to limited contact with health systems [45, 46]. This supports prior calls for mobile and culturally sensitive diagnostic services for nomadic groups, whose needs are often overlooked in national health planning [47].

Taken together, the low overall prevalence of multimorbidity, the pronounced regional and residential disparities, and the consistent indications of underdiagnosis among rural and nomadic populations point to substantial unmet diagnostic need in Somalia. In fragile and conflict-affected settings where facility-based care is limited and unevenly distributed, reliance on clinic-based detection is likely to miss a large proportion of chronic conditions, particularly among women in remote, mobile, or underserved communities. Community-based and mobile screening approaches, integrated into existing primary health care, nutrition, and humanitarian outreach programs, could therefore play a critical role in improving early detection of hypertension, diabetes, anemia, and other chronic conditions, reducing diagnostic inequities and enabling earlier and more cost-effective intervention [48]. Similar approaches have been shown to improve detection of NCDs in other low-resource and fragile settings where access to formal health services is constrained and disease burden is often hidden [49–52].

Hypertension’s predominance across all regions and residence types, especially in urban and northern regions, echoes findings from other LMICs, where it is now the most common non-communicable disease (NCD) among adults [53, 54]. The regional clustering of kidney disease, anemia, and ulcers suggests unique etiological factors and social determinants at play. For instance, the prevalence of anemia in Bay and Bakool may be related to chronic undernutrition and parasitic infections, while kidney disease in Hiraan and Galgaduud may reflect untreated hypertension or water contamination—an emerging concern in many Somali regions [55]. This supports recent studies showing that chronic NCDs in conflict-affected regions often present atypically and are exacerbated by environmental and infrastructural failures [56]. The geographic variation in chronic disease types should inform region-specific interventions, such as blood pressure screening in urban centers and nutritional anemia programs in rural food-insecure zones.

Socioeconomic and infrastructural determinants also emerged as important predictors. Interestingly, lack of internet access was associated with higher odds of multimorbidity, likely reflecting broader socioeconomic disadvantage and barriers to health information and preventive care [57]. However, reverse causality cannot be ruled out, as individuals with chronic health concerns may be more motivated to seek connectivity when it becomes available to access health information and support online [58]. Moreover, evidence from a longitudinal study suggests that regular internet use is linked with lower incidence of certain chronic diseases and improved healthcare access, indicating that digital exclusion itself may contribute to poorer health outcome [59]. On the other hand, there may be a possibility where people with internet access have less physical movement, social withdrawal, which could cause obesity, depression, and other health issues [60, 61]. This apparent contradiction may reflect differences in how internet access is used, as while it can improve access to information and care, more passive or excessive use may also promote sedentary behaviour and social withdrawal, increasing chronic disease risk. Apart from the internet access, lack of land and livestock ownership, traditionally linked to vulnerability, was associated with lower multimorbidity. This counterintuitive finding may reflect urban-rural differences: many urban women lack land but face higher multimorbidity due to lifestyle and stress factors. This supports a growing body of evidence from sub-Saharan Africa showing that conventional rural–urban wealth indicators often obscure substantial intra-urban and inter-regional inequalities and fail to adequately capture the evolving health risks associated with rapid urbanization and informal settlements in transitional societies like Somalia [62]. Moreover, the strong positive association between obesity and multimorbidity reinforces global evidence of obesity as a major driver of NCDs, especially in urban and food-insecure settings where caloric intake is high but nutritional quality is poor [63–65].

Food insecurity emerged as one of the strongest predictors of multimorbidity, reflecting the complex interplay between malnutrition, chronic stress, and susceptibility to illness. This aligns with growing evidence that links food insecurity to hypertension, diabetes, and mental health disorders [66–68]. In the context of Somalia’s prolonged drought, conflict, and inflation, food insecurity not only contributes to acute malnutrition but also increases the risks of chronic diseases. Limited access to affordable, nutritious food often results in reliance on energy-dense, nutrient-poor diets that contribute to obesity, insulin resistance, and hypertension, particularly among vulnerable populations [69, 70]. Additionally, the chronic stress associated with food insecurity can lead to dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, resulting in elevated cortisol levels, systemic inflammation, and immune suppression—biological pathways linked to the development of metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and depression [71]. Studies using biomarkers have further demonstrated that food-insecure individuals have higher levels of inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein and cortisol, underscoring the physiological toll of sustained food-related stress [72]. Moreover, food insecurity is consistently associated with increased risk of anxiety, depression, and sleep disturbances, which can further exacerbate physical health conditions and complicate disease management [73, 74].

Additionally, our finding that longer water collection times were associated with significantly higher odds of multimorbidity reinforces a growing body of evidence linking limited access to basic services, particularly reliable water, to chronic disease burdens through interrelated pathways. Extended water-fetching imposes physical strain and leads to time poverty, reducing opportunities for rest, employment, and caregiving—thereby elevating chronic stress levels. For instance, Geere et al. [75] found that prolonged water-carrying durations were associated with musculoskeletal damage and related disability in South Africa. Water insecurity contributes to psychological distress, emotional strain, and disruptions in daily life [76]. In Namibia, longer distances to water points were associated with higher levels of mental distress among informal settlement residents [77]. Inadequate water access also compromises hygiene and sanitation, increasing susceptibility to infectious diseases and exacerbating existing chronic conditions. Recent findings further highlight that inadequate water access is a structural determinant of multimorbidity, with adults in disadvantaged settings experiencing significantly higher burdens of coexisting non-communicable diseases [78].

Despite the robust Bayesian multilevel modeling and use of a nationally representative sample, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference; associations observed between predictors and multimorbidity may reflect reverse causality or unmeasured confounders. Second, the study relied on self-reported chronic illness data which may underestimate the true burden of multimorbidity, especially among rural and nomadic populations with limited diagnostic access or health literacy. Third, recall and social desirability biases may have influenced responses to sensitive questions such as tobacco use, internet access, or educational attainment. The unexpected finding that uneducated women had lower odds of multimorbidity may be due to reporting bias or differential access to diagnosis. Fourth, although the dataset was assessed for completeness and internal consistency, a substantial proportion of observations had missing values for some study variables and were excluded through a complete-case analysis approach. Excluded participants differed from included participants in several sociodemographic characteristics, suggesting that missing data may have introduced some degree of selection bias. Fifth, while the model adjusted for key sociodemographic variables, unobserved factors such as diet, physical activity, environmental pollution, and genetic predispositions were not included due to data limitations. Some clinically relevant conditions (e.g., COPD or biomarker-defined chronic kidney disease) were also unavailable in the dataset. In addition, the list of chronic conditions used to define multimorbidity was constrained by the survey questionnaire and does not represent a fully validated or comprehensive multimorbidity panel used in clinical epidemiological studies. Therefore, the estimated prevalence and patterns of multimorbidity should be interpreted within the context of the conditions captured in the survey, and comparisons with studies using broader clinical condition lists should be made cautiously. Moreover, due to the high computational cost of the model, posterior inference relied on a single long MCMC chain; although trace plots and effective sample size diagnostics suggested adequate convergence, this precluded the use of between-chain convergence measures and should be considered a methodological limitation. Finally, while the multilevel model captured regional clustering, more refined spatial or longitudinal analyses would further improve understanding of multimorbidity patterns. Finally, although Bayesian modeling effectively captured regional clustering, more advanced spatial models or longitudinal data could further enhance the understanding of multimorbidity patterns. Future research should incorporate clinical validation of self-reported conditions, qualitative assessments, and longitudinal data better to inform health planning in fragile settings like Somalia.

Conclusion

This study provides the first nationally representative analysis of multimorbidity among women in Somalia, revealing a low but notable prevalence with significant geographic and socioeconomic disparities. Urban residents and regions such as Banadir, Bay, and Sool exhibited higher burdens, while food insecurity, obesity, older age, lack of internet access, and limited access to clean water emerged as key risk factors. These findings underscore the pressing need for comprehensive, context-sensitive health policies that prioritize the prevention and management of chronic diseases through enhanced nutrition, sanitation, and access to healthcare. We recommend expanding community-based screening and education programs, particularly in high-risk regions, and enhancing diagnostic capacity in rural and nomadic areas where underreporting is likely to occur. Regionally tailored interventions, coupled with investments in health infrastructure and further longitudinal and qualitative research, are essential for addressing the growing burden of multimorbidity and achieving equitable health outcomes for women across Somalia.
