Validation of an Artificial Intelligence-based Tool – The Screening Corneal Objective Risk of Ectasia Integrated into Anterion for Detection of Corneal Ectasia/Risk of Ectasia
Authors: Ramya S. Raju, C. V. Gopal Raju
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
PURPOSE:The purpose of this study was to validate the artificial intelligence-based Screening Corneal Objective Risk of Ectasia (SCORE) for the detection of corneal ectasia/risk of ectasia and to find the mean SCORE value in normal eyes.METHODS:This prospective observational study included 320 eyes of 160 patients undergoing screening for refractive surgery and keratoconus. All were subjected to Anterion (Heidelberg, Germany) corneal tomography. The maps were read by trained cornea specialists masked with respect to system-generated SCORE. The SCORE values were retrieved by another masked observer. The SCORE was labeled true positive if the eye was tomographically positive for ectasia/ risk of ectasia with or without clinical evidence of ectasia (i.e., clinician’s diagnosis as ectasia/ risk of ectasia) and SCORE was >/=0. The SCORE test was labeled as true negative if all the maps were normal and SCORE was <0. A false-positive test was where the eye was normal but SCORE was ≥0. A false-negative test was one tomographically there was definite evidence of ectasia/ risk; with or without clinical evidence of ectasia (i.e.,clinician's diagnosis as ectasia/ at risk of ectasia) but SCORE was <0. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated.RESULTS:The mean SCORE in normal eyes was −0.7 ± 0.6. Sensitivity was 83.3% and specificity was 80%. PPV and NPV were 46% and 96% respectively. In the keratoconus subgroup, specificity and PPV were 100%; sensitivity was 79% and NPV was 76.3%.CONCLUSION:SCORE in Anterion has moderate sensitivity and specificity for detection of ectasia/risk of ectasia. It performs best in the detection of corneal ectasia. It needs to be interpreted with caution.

Introduction

Corneal ectasias are a group of disorders characterized by noninflammatory, progressive corneal steepening and thinning of the cornea. They comprise keratoconus, keratoglobus, pellucid marginal degeneration, post-LASIK ectasia, and postpenetrating keratoplasty ectasia.[1] Of these, keratoconus is the most common with a global prevalence rate of 1.38 per 1000 population.[2] Amsler coined the term “Forme Fruste Keratoconus” (FFKC) for subclinical forms of keratoconus.[34] However, unified criteria for the definition of FFKC are absent.[5] Post-LASIK ectasia, albeit rare, is a devastating complication of corneal refractive surgery which ought to be prevented. It has a prevalence rate of 0.04%–0.6%.[6] A thorough preoperative evaluation including corneal tomography to rule out any abnormality is essential for its prevention.[6] These ectasias can result in significant ocular morbidity and affect the patient’s quality of life. Hence, it is imperative that they are detected early and their progression prevented.

Artificial intelligence (AI) is the ability of a machine to learn and display intelligence.[7] It is emerging as a major frontier and has been quite beneficial in the management of various ocular disorders.[89] In this era of digital transformation, there have been multiple AI based systems that have been used to detect the risk of ectasia and early keratoconus, like the Belin-Ambrosio enhanced ectasia display- total deviation (BAD-D), topographic keratoconus classification, Sirius Phoenix, OPD-scan III corneal navigator etc.[10] The Pentacam random forest index is reported to have 100% sensitivity for clinical ectasia and BAD-D 87.3% sensitivity and 97.5% specificity.[11] KeratoDetect and ectasia status index were developed using tomography and ASOCT.[121314] Convolutional neural network-based systems are also being developed and 100% sensitivity and specificity have been reported for keratoconus detection using them.[15] The tomographic and biomechanical index combines corneal tomography and biomechanics and has 100% sensitivity and specificity for ectasia detection at a cutoff of 0.79.[16] Yet, there is not one universally accepted system. There is a necessity to develop these AI-based algorithms and validate them so that they will help clinicians make decisions faster and easier as compared to the conventional tedious manual interpretation of the maps by the clinician themselves. Gatinel and Saad developed a similar ectasia risk scoring system called the Screening Corneal Objective Risk of Ectasia (SCORE) in the year 2010. It was an AI-based system designed to diagnose early ectasia and quantify it and to detect eyes at risk of ectasia after a refractive corneal procedure just by looking at a value rather than going through complex maps.[17] It was incorporated into Orbscan (Technolas Perfect Vision, Germany) previously. Recently, in the year 2022, it has been optimized and integrated into Anterion (Heidelberg, Germany).[18] To date, there is no literature on validation of this SCORE integrated into Anterion, or about the mean SCORE value in normal eyes. The purpose of the present study is to find the accuracy of this AI-based SCORE integrated into Anterion for the detection of corneal ectasia or risk of postrefractive surgery ectasia and to find the mean SCORE in normal eyes.

Methods

This study was approved by the ethics committee of the institute. Informed consent was taken from the patient or their parents in case of children. The sample size was calculated using standard formulae with 80% power and 95% confidence intervals. A prospective, observational study was conducted for a period of 2 months. All consecutive cases of corneal ectatic disorders, suspects, and patients undergoing screening for refractive surgery were included. A routine comprehensive ophthalmic examination including visual acuity assessment, refraction, slit-lamp examination, and dilated fundus examination was performed. Soft contact lens use was discontinued for a period of at least 2 weeks prior to tomography. There were no rigid lens users. All cases underwent corneal tomography using the Cornea App in Anterion. Those patients whose conditions precluded a reliable image acquisition, or those patients whose conditions would have probably affected the results as in cases of ptosis, ocular surface diseases including active vernal keratoconjunctivitis, pterygium etc; and those who were uncooperative; were excluded from the study. The tomography maps were deciphered by trained cornea specialists. The maps included were pachymetry, anterior and posterior elevation, and anterior curvature as in any tomography machine. This display given out as a print-out did not contain the SCORE value. Hence, these specialists were masked with respect to the SCORE. The patients were classified as ectatic/nonectatic or risk of ectasia present/absent accordingly. The cases were appropriately managed according to the clinical signs, tomographic maps, and the clinician’s judgment. An independent masked observer was used to obtain the SCORE values from the Anterion machine separately using the patient identity numbers alone. A third observer compared these two, i.e., the clinical decision and the SCORE value. The test (SCORE) was declared as true positive (TP) if the eye was tomographically and/or clinically positive for ectasia (clinician’s diagnosis as ectasia/risk of ectasia) and the SCORE was≥0. The test was true negative (TN) if all the maps were normal and there was no clinical suspicion at all (clinician’s diagnosis as no ectasia/no risk) and the SCORE was <0. A false-positive (FP) test was where clinically and tomographically, the eye was normal (clinician’s diagnosis as no ectasia/no risk) but had a SCORE of ≥0. The test was classified as false negative (FN) where tomographically and/or clinically and there was definitive evidence of ectasia (clinician’s diagnosis as ectasia/risk of ectasia) but the SCORE values <0. The trained cornea specialist’s decision based on not just the clinical examination but also on the tomographic evaluation was considered to be the gold standard. Data were entered into Excel sheets, and analysis was done using SPSS version 24 (IBM Corporation, New York, USA). Sensitivity, specificity, Positive Predictive Value (PPV), and Negative Predictive Value (NPV) were calculated using standard formulae.[19]

Results

320 eyes of 160 patients were included in this study. The age range was 4–31 with a mean age of 21.9 + 2.96 (95% confidence limits). 72 eyes (22%) underwent the testing for keratoconus/suspicion whereas the rest underwent screening for refractive surgery (242, 78%). The mean SCORE value in normal eyes without clinical or tomographical suspicion of ectasia was −0.7 ± 0.6. 91 (57%) were males and 69 (43%) were females. Discussion

SCORE is an AI-based system developed by Dr. Gatinel and Dr. Saad and has been incorporated into Anterion.[17] It is an objective method meant to aid ectasia diagnosis and to pick an eye at risk for post-LASIK ectasia just by looking at a single value. It can range from – 4 to a value that can be any value more than 20. In normal eyes, the SCORE is <0 whereas in eyes at risk of ectasia or in ectatic eyes the SCORE is ≥0. The SCORE bar displayed on top of the SCORE value shows the range from − 4 to 20.[20] In this study, the mean SCORE of the normal eyes (without ectasia) was −0.7 ± 0.6. This mean SCORE has not been reported earlier. Although all the parameters on which it is based have not been revealed clearly, some of them that are used are anterior K max (maximum curvature of the anterior cornea), anterior K max-opposite K (keratometry of the hemisphere opposite to the K max), thinnest pachymetry value, posterior elevation, irregularity in the 3 mm zone, anterior inferior-superior mean K value, (difference between the mean keratometry of the inferior and the superior halves of the cornea) as depicted in the radar map below the SCORE value.[20] This radar map is a graphical color-coded representation which provides the clinician with a striking image of the condition of the eye [Figure 4]. It shows each of these indices plotted on radii radiating from a common point.[21] All these values are joined together to form a polygon and this is filled with color where yellow indicates a value of 2 standard deviations from normal.[21]

Radar map showing various indices and a color-coded polygon. Note the Screening Corneal Objective Risk of Ectasia (SCORE) bar above and the value from −4 to 20 and the SCORE above it. Also seen are the averaged and relative pachymetry maps toward the thinnest point

The overall sensitivity and specificity of SCORE were 83.3% was 80%, respectively. Chan et al. used an earlier version of SCORE incorporated in Orbscan in a Singaporean population and found a sensitivity of 70.8% and a specificity of 98.1% for FFKC and post-LASIK ectasia risk detection.[22] The parameters which were used in this earlier version of SCORE in Orbscan were different from those which were used in the present study using SCORE software in Anterion. They were: 1)central thickness- thinnest pachymetry (TP) 2) inferior displacement of Y coordinate, 3) anterior elevation, 4) posterior elevation, 5) thinnest pachymetry, 6) irregularity in the 3 mm zone. This could be one of the reasons why there was a difference in the sensitivity and specificity of the SCORE in the above study when compared to the present study. Saad and Gatinel found a much higher sensitivity of 92% and specificity of 96% in the detection of FFKC in a French population.[21] Again, this was the earlier version using Orbscan and the parameters were different.

PPV was low at 46% and NPV was high at 96% in the present study. Thus, this system would be more important in ruling out rather than ruling in ectasia/risk of ectasia. Chan et al. found a PPV of 89.5% and NPV of 93.6%.[22] They retrospectively analyzed and compared the SCORE values in FFKC Eyes and in eyes after LASIK who did not develop keratectasia. We differ from this group as we assessed the SCORE performance in comparison to the clinician’s judgment of the tomographic maps alone.

In the subgroup of eyes which were screened for detection of keratoconus, the SCORE performed best with a specificity of 100%, PPV of 100%, and a sensitivity and NPV of 79% and 76.3%, respectively. In the refractive surgery screening group, there was a high number of FPs leading to poor PPV at 17.2%. The sensitivity was 100%, NPV was 100% and specificity was moderate at 77.6%.

It is an established fact that any inferior-superior asymmetry of >1.5D and superior-inferior asymmetry >2.5 D at the 5 mm region are indicative of an ectatic disease.[2324] We found a certain pattern that happened to recur. This system happens to miss superior steepening even in frank keratoconus cases. The reason for this could be because one of the parameters incorporated in the SCORE system is a difference between inferior mean keratometry and the superior mean keratometry value (That is, Inf K mean-Sup K mean). In classical cases of inferior steepening, this value would be positive and would reflect a larger value in the SCORE. However, in superior steepening, this value would be negative, thus causing an erroneous interpretation as a lesser SCORE value and lesser risk of ectasia. This is a novel finding which has never been reported so far. However, it is uncertain as to how exactly the system calculates the K mean values of the inferior or the superior half. This is depicted in Figure 1a where the SCORE value was only 0.7. The value should have been much higher as this was a case of frank ectasia as the K max was 55 D and posterior elevation was 55 µ. Furthermore, the steepening of LE was not picked [Figure 1b]. In addition, in three cases with unilateral keratoconus, this AI system failed to pick up the FFKC in the fellow eye. Hence, it might not be useful as a predictor for the development of keratoconus. One of these cases is shown in Figure 3a and b. However, the sample size of this cohort of unilateral keratoconus was too small for drawing further deductions from it.

The reason for large number of FPs could be because it identifies every thin cornea (<510 µ) as at risk for ectasia. This was the reason for the low PPV of 17.2% in the refractive surgery screening group. A lower cutoff value of about 470 µ would lead to a reduction in the number of FPs. Because of surface ablation, refractive surgery is being performed in mild to moderate myopia cases even where the corneal thickness is < 510 µ up to about 470 or 480 µ depending on the surgeon’s preference. In instances where a practicing refractive surgeon would advise laser vision correction without any second thoughts, the AI system labels the eye as at risk for ectasia which may not be true. Furthermore, the clinician’s judgment would also depend on the amount of refractive error to be corrected and also a comparison between the two eyes, which is not present in the above system. This could be another reason for the discrepancy between clinician’s and machine’s judgment. False negativity could partly be due to missing of the superior steepening pattern as discussed above and lack of comparison of the two eyes of the same patient.

There is also considerable ambiguity in the literature with regard to the cutoff value of SCORE. The manufacturer’s user manual suggests that 0 be considered the cutoff value, which is what we have taken into consideration.[20] However, another article from Heidelberg says that the normal population may have values −0.5 or lesser.[18] Furthermore, the bar which is shown above the SCORE value has a green color from −4 to −0.5, yellow from −0.5 to 1, and orange and red for values above 1 [Figure 4]. This would suggest that normal population would have values between −4 and −0.5, suspects would have values between −0.5 and 1 and frank ectasia cases would have values either equal to or >1. We suggest that further research be done on this aspect so that the cutoff becomes standardized.

The strengths of this study were that observers were masked and this would have minimized the bias and its large volume of 320 eyes. The limitations were that multiple (three) cornea specialists were involved to decipher the tomography maps which could have led to inter-observer variations. Multiple technicians were involved in taking the Anterion images. However, the machine had its reliability settings which were strictly adhered to. Repeatability and inter-test variability were not performed. Furthermore, double checking of cases labeled as false positive or false negative, using another system like the BAD-D of the corneal tomographer pentacam was not performed. This was not performed because the authors believed that the anterion was a well established and standard corneal tomography system. The clinicians also were well trained and experienced, hence the room for errors would have been minimal. Nevertheless, there may be concerns about the clinical acumen of the specialists themselves. The authors also seek to follow up these cohorts of patients to look for the development of any probable post-LASIK ectasia in those who underwent refractive surgery. To our best knowledge, this is the first study that validates this version of SCORE after its incorporation into Anterion. So far, there is no study that provides the SCORE values in normal population either.

Conclusion

To summarize, SCORE in Anterion has moderate sensitivity and specificity in ectasia or risk of ectasia detection. In ectasia detection, it is a highly specific tool with a high PPV. In refractive surgery screening, it is highly sensitive but has poor PPV. At this juncture, caution needs to be exercised while interpreting it. We recommend that these algorithms also take inter-eye symmetry into consideration, which might facilitate FFKC detection. Inclusion of corneal biomechanics; better detection of superior cones by incorporation of superior-inferior asymmetry also, instead of the routine inferior-superior asymmetry alone, which is designed to detect inferior cones only; advanced algorithms where in the manifest refraction and visual acuity of patients could be entered and interpreted; are all means by which further improvements may be designed in AI based detection systems. It would be too early to draw an inference that this AI system would eventually replace human intelligence in ectasia screening. Extensive studies are required to develop this tool better so that it may revolutionize corneal ectasia management and risk detection in refractive surgery screening, not only in hospitals with trained specialists but also in remote locations.
