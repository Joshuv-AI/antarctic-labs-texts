Technology-enabled telerehabilitation for Parkinson’s disease: a scoping review of digital rehabilitation systems, delivery architectures, and implementation challenges
Authors: Chiara Longo, Lorenzo Gios, Susanna Pardini, Raffaello Ferrari, Anita Daves, Giuseppe Jurman, Maria Chiara Malaguti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDigital technologies are increasingly integrated into neurorehabilitation programs for Parkinson’s Disease (PD), enabling remote delivery of therapy, continuous monitoring of motor performance, and adaptive feedback during rehabilitation training. Telerehabilitation systems incorporating wearable sensors, virtual reality platforms, mobile applications, and artificial intelligence (AI) have expanded rapidly in recent years. However, the evidence base remains fragmented across heterogeneous technological configurations, clinical contexts, and delivery models, limiting a comprehensive understanding of how digital rehabilitation systems are implemented in PD care.MethodsThis scoping review maps the current literature on telerehabilitation for PD with a focus on the technological architectures, care settings, and delivery models used in digital rehabilitation programs. Peer-reviewed studies published between 2020 and 2025 were identified through searches in PubMed with reference to Scopus and Web of Science. Eligible studies investigated remote rehabilitation interventions for PD using digital technologies such as wearable sensors, mobile health applications, virtual reality systems, and AI-supported monitoring tools. Evidence was analyzed across three domains: (i) technological components and digital rehabilitation systems, (ii) rehabilitation setting, and (iii) delivery model.ResultsFifty-three studies met the inclusion criteria. Most interventions were home-based and implemented multi-component digital architectures combining teleconferencing platforms, wearable sensors, and mobile applications. Wearable sensing technologies were used in nearly half of the studies to quantify gait, balance, or tremor, while video platforms and mobile applications supported remote supervision and exercise delivery. Virtual reality systems and serious games were used to enhance engagement and taskspecific training, whereas AI techniques were increasingly integrated to support movement detection, monitoring, and adaptive feedback. Despite generally high usability and acceptability, substantial heterogeneity was observed in outcome measures, terminology, and safety reporting. Few studies explicitly described care pathways, delivery architectures, or long-term clinical outcomes.DisscussionTelerehabilitation for Parkinson’s disease is evolving toward integrated digital rehabilitation ecosystems combining wearable sensing, software platforms, and AI-enabled monitoring. Although feasibility and patient acceptance are consistently reported, current evidence remains limited by heterogeneous reporting standards and insufficient integration between technological systems and clinical workflows. Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by a combination of motor and non-motor impairments that lead to disability and reduced quality of life [1–3]. Alongside pharmacological treatment, rehabilitation is a core component of PD management, aiming to preserve functional abilities, support participation in daily activities, and mitigate disease-related complications [4, 5].

In recent years, advances in neuroengineering and digital health technologies have enabled the development of technology-supported rehabilitation systems capable of delivering therapy beyond traditional clinical environments [6]. Digital platforms, wearable sensors, and interactive rehabilitation software increasingly allow remote monitoring of motor performance, real-time feedback during training, and personalized adjustment of rehabilitation protocols.

However, traditional in-person rehabilitation is often limited by accessibility, continuity, and resource constraints, particularly for individuals with advanced disease stages or those living in underserved areas. In this context, telerehabilitation (TR), defined as the remote delivery of rehabilitation services through digital technologies, has emerged as a viable strategy to extend care beyond conventional clinical settings. Evidence from neurological populations suggests that TR can be feasible, safe, and comparable to in-person rehabilitation for selected outcomes [7–10]. In PD, a growing body of studies has explored TR interventions using video conferencing, motion-tracking systems, wearable sensors, and virtual reality platforms, reporting improvements in gait, balance, and patient satisfaction. Despite this progress, the evidence base remains heterogeneous and less mature compared with other neurological conditions, such as stroke. Long-term effectiveness, implementation feasibility, and transferability across care contexts are still insufficiently understood [11].

For PD specifically, interventions such as non-immersive virtual reality platforms, video-conferencing, and motion-tracking systems have also shown promise in improving balance, gait, and other motor outcomes [12–14]. Studies consistently report that TR is safe, feasible, and well-accepted by patients, with satisfaction levels generally matching those of traditional rehabilitation [10].

Nevertheless, research focusing on TR in the field of PD remains limited compared to stroke or other clinical areas, and findings are somewhat heterogeneous [7, 15]. Moreover, evidence regarding the long-term effectiveness and feasibility of implementation remains unclear [16, 17]. While some studies demonstrate comparable outcomes in Quality of Life (QoL) and patient satisfaction between TR and in-person treatment, others highlight variability in results depending on the type of intervention, technological modality, and outcome measures applied [10]. Importantly, TR may also support the maintenance of functional improvements over time, which is particularly relevant in the management of chronic conditions such as PD. For PD, telerehabilitation could offer continuous, personalized, and scalable interventions adapted to motor and cognitive fluctuations [7, 18].

In recent years, advanced digital technologies—including wearable sensors, mobile health applications, artificial intelligence (AI), and virtual or augmented reality—have increasingly been incorporated into TR programs for PD. These technologies offer opportunities for continuous monitoring, adaptive feedback, and personalized exercise delivery. These developments highlight the growing role of human–technology interaction in neurorehabilitation, where digital systems support both clinical decision-making and patient engagement in rehabilitation processes [19]. At the same time, organizational aspects such as care setting (home-based versus clinic-based) and delivery approach (clinician-guided versus self-managed or blended models) vary widely across studies and are often poorly defined or inconsistently reported.

Most existing reviews focus on specific technologies or isolated clinical outcomes, without adopting a framework that integrates technological solutions with rehabilitation settings and delivery models. This fragmented perspective limits understanding of how these elements interact to influence patient engagement, safety, equity of access, and scalability of TR interventions [20–22].

In line with the core constructs emerging from the recent literature [10, 22] and to address this gap, the present scoping review aims to map and synthesize the literature on technology-enabled telerehabilitation systems for PD. Specifically, the review examines: (i) the types of digital and technological solutions adopted, (ii) the contexts in which TR is delivered, and (iii) the delivery models characterizing professional involvement and patient autonomy. By integrating these dimensions, this review seeks to identify patterns, strengths, and critical gaps in current evidence and to inform future research and development of effective, scalable, and patient-centered telerehabilitation models for PD.

Methods

Study design

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). The protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) [23, 24]. A scoping approach was selected to map key concepts, types of evidence [25], and research gaps related to telerehabilitation (TR) for Parkinson’s disease (PD), rather than to assess comparative effectiveness.

Eligibility criteria

Peer-reviewed original studies published in English between January 2020 and July 2025 were eligible if they: (i) focused on TR interventions for individuals with PD; (ii) involved digital or remote rehabilitation technologies (e.g., virtual reality, artificial intelligence, wearable devices, or mobile applications); (iii) reported the rehabilitation setting (home-based, outpatient, or hospital-based); and/or (iv) described the level of professional involvement (clinician-guided, self-managed, caregiver-supported, or blended). Study protocols, conference abstracts, non-peer-reviewed publications, and studies not specific to PD or lacking a remote rehabilitation component were excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

Literature searches were conducted in PubMed as the primary database, with reference to Scopus and Google Scholar to ensure broad multidisciplinary coverage. Search strategies combined terms related to PD, telerehabilitation, digital rehabilitation technologies, care settings, and delivery models. Searches were limited to publications dated up to 31 July 2025.

Study selection

All records were imported into an electronic data matrix and duplicates were removed. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by all authors, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible studies. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. Reference management was supported by reference management software and AI-assisted tools, while screening, eligibility assessment and coding were performed manually by the authors, followed by manual verification. The selection process is reported using a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Data charting and analysis

Data were charted using a structured extraction form covering three analytical domains: (i) technologies used, (ii) rehabilitation setting, and (iii) delivery model. Data synthesis followed a qualitative, iterative approach, allowing themes and patterns to emerge across studies. Given the scoping nature of the review, no formal risk-of-bias assessment was performed, and findings are reported descriptively to map trends and research gaps. AI-supported tools were used solely to cross-check wording within the matrix and related representations, based on the selection done by the authors, and the matrix was subsequently manually cross-checked.

The highest return for the broad telerehabilitation query suggests a substantial body of PD-specific TR research post-2020, likely reflecting the pandemic-accelerated shift to remote care. The second query’s focus on AI/VR/wearables/mobile/chatbots still retrieves a sizable subset (n = 29), indicating sustained innovation activity.

By contrast, lower yields for care settings (n = 15) and particularly for the delivery model (n = 7) point to a comparative scarcity of work that explicitly operationalizes the setting (home vs. clinic) and labels delivery as synchronous/asynchronous/hybrid, highlighting potential need for expanding research and knowledge on these specific constructs.

Technological innovations

Overview

When focusing on technological interventions, most studies report home-based programs (nearly two-thirds), indicating a shift from laboratory or clinic settings to domestic environments. Randomized controlled trials account for approximately one third of study designs. Wearables or inertial measurement units are used in 48% of studies, mainly as smartphone-integrated or body-worn sensors to quantify gait, turning, or tremor. Video platforms (51%) and mobile applications (38%) represent the core software components, whilst virtual reality or serious games are used in 31% of interventions. AI is frequently reported, primarily to support monitoring and adaptive feedback (Table 2).

Usability and acceptability outcomes are reported across all studies and are generally positive. Interventions predominantly target motor outcomes (79%), with cognitive or mixed programs reported in roughly one third of cases, often combining gait or balance training with dual-task or executive function exercises. Caregivers are involved in approximately one third of interventions, mainly for setup and safety; clinicians provide remote supervision or periodic review in nearly half of the studies.

Wearables and inertial measurement units are widely used to provide objective, high-frequency measures and biofeedback (e.g., cadence or step symmetry). Differently, mobile applications primarily support exercise coordination, instruction delivery, and capture of adherence or patient-reported outcomes. Video platforms are commonly used for therapist observation, progression, and safety checks. Virtual reality or serious games are mainly adopted to enhance engagement and task specificity but often require dedicated space and caregiver support. In some cases, artificial intelligence integrates multimodal data to detect gait events, monitor deterioration, or adapt exercise progression.

SWOT—technological innovations

The innovation corpus consistently demonstrates high feasibility and acceptability, with a home-first, multi-component approach — combining wearables/IMUs, apps, and video/tele-platforms (including VR) — to complete and boost the continuous monitoring and to this extent to improve tailored and personalized training. In this scenario, AI appears to be increasingly used for event detection and real-time feedback.

Key limitations include heterogeneous outcomes that hinder cross-study synthesis, limited explainability/external validation of AI pipelines, and access barriers to VR/sensor setups (cost, space, cybersickness), which sometimes necessitate caregiver support. Priorities are core outcome sets (with PROs and structured safety), explainable AI integrated into clinical workflows, and multi-sensor fusion for adaptive dosing and early warning.

Considering the different papers, major threats include digital inequities and caregiver burden, interoperability gaps/data silos, and heightened privacy–security bias, which could slow the adoption of technologies and tech-enabled services, particularly those without robust governance (Table 3).

Care settings

Overview

Most studies are conducted in home settings (87%), typically with remote oversight and occasional in-clinic assessments. Randomized controlled trials account for 60% of study designs. Technologies used in home-based programs are consistent with those reported overall, with frequent use of wearables (53%) and video platforms (60%), followed by mobile applications (27%). Artificial intelligence features are described in approximately half of the studies, with varying levels of detail. Usability and acceptability outcomes are consistently reported, with caregiver involvement in about 40% of interventions and professional supervision in 47%, often via scheduled tele-visits.

Home-based telerehabilitation programs commonly include video calls and remote assistance, reducing travel demands. Safety procedures typically involve pre-session checks, wearable-based alerts where available, and predefined escalation rules. When clinical settings are used, they mainly support baseline or periodic assessments, while some hybrid models include initial supervised sessions to calibrate home-based exercises.

SWOT — care settings

Evidence from domiciliary trials suggests robust feasibility and non-inferiority of this approach when compared to other approaches (Table 4). Caregiver and clinician involvement seems to improve setup quality, adherence, and safety, while home delivery expands access to service when reducing travels. However, workflow heterogeneity across papers limits insights in terms of reproducibility; safety reporting is inconsistent (including adverse events and near misses), and the cost/organizational impact remains under-reported, making these studies less useful for assessing the potential sustainability of the models. Priorities in research appear to be hybrid effectiveness, including robust assessment of costs and sustainability, as well as caregiver-inclusive protocols.

Threats stem from reimbursement variability, workforce digital readiness/change fatigue, and persistent access gaps (in rural areas and with device availability), requiring a multi-layered intervention to avoid widening disparities (e.g., device loan programs).

Delivery models

Overview

Video platforms (71%) and mobile applications (57%) are commonly reported in studies addressing delivery models; however, explicit classification of delivery style is uncommon, with synchronous, asynchronous, and hybrid approaches each reported in 14% of studies and most remaining unspecified (71%). Study descriptions nonetheless indicate that home-based, asynchronous self-guided programs are frequently supplemented by periodic synchronous tele-visits. Artificial intelligence features are described in nearly half of the studies, mainly to support remote monitoring or adaptive progression. Usability and acceptability outcomes are consistently reported and generally positive, and randomized controlled trials represent a substantial proportion of study designs.

Synchronous sessions enable real-time observation and progression but are resource-intensive and dependent on connectivity and scheduling. Asynchronous programs appear more scalable and supportive of self-management but require reliable monitoring and clear escalation procedures. Hybrid models, combining asynchronous training with scheduled live check-ins, are reported less frequently but appear to balance efficiency, safety, and engagement.

SWOT—delivery modes

Evidence across studies indicates that asynchronous and hybrid delivery can scale clinician time and foster patient autonomy while preserving periodic professional oversight. Consistently positive usability/acceptability suggests strong patient buy-in, and apps, along with video, reduce onboarding friction while supporting both motor and non-motor targets. However, safety pathways (fall detection, stop criteria, rapid escalation) are unevenly described; dose-personalization rules (progressions/regressions/intensity caps) vary widely; and terminology for “asynchronous,” “synchronous,” and “hybrid” remains non-standard, complicating comparison. Priorities include codifying dose/intensity algorithms and check-in cadence (e.g., AI-guided flags that trigger live sessions), embedding patient-reported outcomes and therapeutic alliance measures, and designing equity-oriented delivery (such as device-loan programs, offline-capable content, multilingual UI, and adaptive/low-bandwidth video). Key threats are overreliance on asynchronous care without robust monitoring (risking missed deterioration), platform lock-in with shifting terms, and privacy/security incidents that could erode trust and participation. Discussion

General insights based on current evidence

The goal of this review was to identify emerging trends, technological innovations, and opportunities for developing integrated models of care. From a neuroengineering perspective, the reviewed studies also illustrate the emergence of technology-supported rehabilitation ecosystems in which wearable sensing, software platforms, and remote communication systems interact to support rehabilitation delivery and monitoring in PD.

The first key finding emerging from the literature is a clear “home-first” orientation. Across the reviewed studies, 69–87% of interventions are delivered in home settings, confirming a shift toward rehabilitation in patients’ everyday environments [22]. This trend supports the role of familiar contexts in enabling more personalized and potentially sustainable rehabilitation pathways [7].

A second area of relevance concerns the adoption of multi-component approaches. Wearable devices and IMUs are used in up to 53% of studies, mobile applications in 27–57%, and video platforms in 40–71%. Together, these technologies form integrated toolsets that support monitoring, feedback, and remote interaction [7, 10]. Virtual reality is less frequently adopted but contributes to engagement and task-specific training when included [16, 26]. Clinically, this integration of multiple digital tools could support more comprehensive, flexible, and patient-centered care, potentially improving adherence, outcomes, and the overall rehabilitation experience. From a systems perspective, these configurations can be interpreted as distributed rehabilitation architectures in which sensing, training, and feedback components are integrated across multiple digital platforms.

A third aspect relates to the growing role of AI in telerehabilitation and adaptive digital rehabilitation systems. AI is reported in 43–53% of studies addressing settings or delivery models and in 48% of technology-focused studies, primarily to support detection and adaptive feedback rather than full automation. Key challenges include accessibility, training requirements, algorithm robustness, data security, and regulatory considerations [27].

A fourth element concerns patient and caregiver roles. Most interventions rely on asynchronous, self-guided delivery, often supplemented by periodic synchronous tele-visits. This hybrid approach increases flexibility and accessibility while preserving professional oversight, supporting adherence over extended treatment periods [28].

Finally, usability and acceptability are consistently reported, and the substantial proportion of randomized controlled trials (31–60%) indicates that PD telerehabilitation has progressed beyond early feasibility testing. Lower usability in older patients or those with advanced disease suggests the need for stratified approaches, with targeted deployment of technologies according to patient capabilities.

Despite the expanding literature on TR for PD, several important gaps seem to persist.

First, the lack of standardized outcome measures, including patient-reported outcomes (PROs) and safety indicators, limits evidence synthesis and benchmarking. Second, few studies report implementation-science metrics such as adoption, fidelity, or cost, which are essential to guide scale-up and sustainability. A third area is related to the equity strategies that are rarely articulated: in fact, interventions seldom mention device lending, digital coaching, or low-bandwidth options, raising concerns about digital exclusion among those who could benefit most. Fourth, the absence of consistent terminology for asynchronous, synchronous, and hybrid models reduces comparability and creates ambiguity around clinical involvement and check-in frequency. Clearer definitions and reporting standards are needed for safe and scalable implementation.

Finally, evidence on long-term efficacy, adherence, and usability remains scarce. Only one study followed participants for nine months, underscoring the need for longitudinal evaluation.

When focusing on implications for practice, policy, and research, future research should focus on bridging efficacy and implementation through pragmatic, multicenter RCTs that include process evaluations to understand contextual success or failure. Standardization of outcome measures—covering clinical, functional, and patient-reported indicators—is essential to enable comparability and benchmarking and so far this seems not widely adopted in the scientific literature. Building clinical trust in AI and digital technologies requires systematic evaluation of explainability, calibration, bias, and external validity, with studies comparing AI-guided and clinician-guided interventions, and often this is not the case, at least in the papers assessed in this exercise.

Finally, scalability should be planned from the outset by applying implementation frameworks such as CFIR or RE-AIM, ensuring interoperability (e.g., FHIR), and embedding cost-effectiveness and environmental sustainability considerations. In addition, aligning innovation with regulatory frameworks, including MDR 2017/745 and the EU AI Act (in case of EU contexts), will be key to achieving safe, effective, and regulation-ready TR models.

Strengths and limitations

In terms of strengths, this work utilizes a recent (2020–2025) curated dataset to map PD TR along three practical axes, providing concrete SWOT analyses and an implementation-oriented agenda. It foregrounds home-based care, multi-component stacks, and the emergence of AI while explicitly considering usability and equity.

Considering the main limitations of the study, it is worth noting that, as a non-systematic mapping, the search may be prone to selection and classification biases. All strings adopted for the study were selected to improve specificity, which may lead to the potential exclusion of studies describing relevant interventions without canonical terms (e.g., “telecoaching,” “remote exercise,” “digital physiotherapy”). At the same time, the setting and delivery queries rely on relatively narrow lexical markers that were purposely selected for this mapping exercise (e.g., “home-based,” “clinic-based,” “self-guided,” “blended”). This improves precision but likely underestimates studies that describe these features implicitly rather than as indexed keywords. In addition, the restriction to English-language publications should be mentioned.

Conclusions

PD telerehabilitation is entering a deployment phase: technology stacks are converging (wearables, apps, video, and VR), delivery is predominantly home-based, and AI is transitioning from novelty to practical support for detection and feedback. With usability consistently high and RCTs increasingly common, the field is poised for pragmatic, implementation-aware trials, standardized outcomes, and equity-first scale-up. Aligning programs to these priorities can turn PD TR into routine, sustainable care that is safe, effective, and accessible.
