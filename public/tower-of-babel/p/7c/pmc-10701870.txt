Complementary Pocket and Network-Based Approach to Search for Spike Protein Allosteric Pocket Sites
Authors: Ronny
L. Cheng, Jerwin C. Quirante, Louise Erika Z. Vargas, Abigael F. Gatchalian, Ricky B. Nellas
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
COVID-19 is a persistent public health concern due to the emergence of more virulent and contagious variants resulting from mutations in the spike protein. The spike protein in newer variants, including Delta and Omicron, may be less sensitive to neutralizing antibodies and have a more favorable binding environment to the human ACE2 receptor. In the interest of identifying anti-COVID-19 allosteric drugs, a network-based approach based on coarse-grained molecular dynamics (CGMD) simulations, in complement to pocket-based analysis, is used to identify the possible allosteric pathways of the wild-type, Delta, and Omicron BA.1 spike proteins. Three pockets around 30 Å away from the spike–ACE2 interface are identified underneath the three receptor-binding domain (RBD) chains, which are potentially druggable due to favorable hydrophobicity and surface accessibility. Meanwhile, the network-based approach reveals intrinsic changes within the coupling between the three RBD chains, which could affect the overall communication between the spike–ACE2 interface active site and the three pockets, in particular between the stronger coupling between RBDA and RBDB for the wild type, versus the stronger coupling between RBDA and RBDC in Omicron BA.1. These results are to be used in subsequent drug discovery studies in targeting the spike protein allosterically as part of the search for COVID-19 drugs and as part of the toolbox against future pandemics.

Introduction

The ongoing COVID-19 pandemic, caused by SARS-CoV-2 coronavirus, continues to be a significant public health concern. As of May 2023, there have been over 700 million confirmed cases and nearly seven (7) million deaths.1 The SARS-CoV-2 is a β coronavirus first detected in Wuhan last December 2019 that is rapidly transmissible through inhaled droplets, saliva, direct contact between humans, and airborne spread.2−5 SARS-CoV-2 is closest phylogenetically to two bat coronaviruses, HKU4 and HKU5,6,7 and is also similar to the SARS-CoV-1 coronavirus, which caused the 2003 SARS outbreak, in terms of genetic similarity (79.5% similarity) and its target receptor, the human angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (hACE2).6

The fight against COVID-19 is further complicated by the continuous emergence of COVID-19 variants of concern (VOCs), described through the amino acid mutations within the spike protein.8 For RNA viruses such as SARS-CoV-2, mutations naturally occur during replication due to natural selection and increased chances of errors in transcription compared to DNA viruses due to a lack of proofreading within RNA polymerases.9 Over time, frequent mutations in the spike protein led to the genesis of newer VOCs that outcompete older variants through increased viral adaptation, more effective binding toward hACE2, and better antibody escape.10−16

Structurally, the spike protein is a homotrimeric glycoprotein embedded in a viral membrane which varies in conformation depending on the accessibility of the RBDs toward the hACE2 receptor.17,18 In particular, the binding of the SARS-CoV-2 receptor-binding domain (RBD) against the hACE2 receptor is a significant virus–host interaction that triggers the viral release and subsequent replications within the host cell.19 As a major mutation hotspot with critical functions in the virus, the spike protein remains to be a crucial target for understanding the virus and drug discovery.20 However, the traditional targeting of only one domain (specifically, the RBD) currently does not suffice for the urgent need for emergency drugs and vaccines against COVID-19. Several studies have led to designing compounds using different domains of the spike protein besides using only RBD for drug development. These include the design of drug-like compounds, neutralizing antibodies and vaccines targeting the epitopic regions within the N-terminal domain and C-terminal domain within the S1 subunit,21,22 and the heptad repeat regions 1 and 2 within the S2 subunit to inhibit membrane fusion.23−25 As the virus evolves over time, the demand for targeting various domains of the spike protein can greatly increase the chances of finding the right target for the candidate drugs against the coronavirus.26

Search for anti-COVID-19 allosteric drugs remain of interest as these could offer several advantages over orthosteric drugs such as better spatiotemporal selectivity,27 lower risks for side effects,28 and being less susceptible to drug resistance from target proteins.29 However, the search for allosteric drugs is complicated by the need to identify allosteric pockets and their associated allosteric pathway to the active site owing to the unique topography of proteins.30 Once identified, an allosteric pathway can provide valuable insights into how perturbations in the allosteric site can affect the dynamics of the active site of the protein, including the critical residues involved in the allosteric pathway and the degree of correlation between allosteric and active sites.31

Recent advancements in computer simulations and algorithms have allowed researchers to computationally predict allostery. These approaches include the use of topology-based analysis or simulation-based analysis,32 which can be used in tandem with algorithms including normal-mode analysis (NMA),33 residue–residue contact correlation analysis,34 spectral graph methods,35 perturbation-response scanning method,36,37 and network-based approaches.38−40

While network-based approaches are already being applied to model the communication hotspots and the allostery of the spike glycoprotein,41−43 as well as the spike–RBD complexes of the wild-type, Delta, and Omicron variants,44 a comparative network analysis of the trimeric spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 variants could provide additional insights about the residues that could potentially affect protein dynamics. In this paper, a complementary pocket and network-based approach is used as a means to explore allostery in the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein variants. From coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations of SARS-CoV-2 wild-type, Delta, and Omicron BA.1 spike proteins, potential allosteric pockets are identified from geometric-based algorithms, followed by a network-based analysis to identify allosteric pathways and, likewise, critical residues and/or mutations that can affect the allosteric pathways between spike protein variants. These findings can be applied in future drug discovery studies in the continuous search for anti-COVID-19 drugs.

Methods

System Preparation

Coarse-grained systems were prepared using the all-atom structure of the wild-type trimeric spike protein from the CHARMM-GUI COVID-19 database,45 which is based on the existing EM structure of the trimeric spike protein at the open 1-up conformation (PDB ID: 6VSB, GenBank Accession ID: YP_009724390.1).46,47 In this conformation, the RBDA domain is at the up conformation, while the RBDB and RBDC domains are in the down conformation.

Loops that are absent in the crystal structure were added to the models using template-based modeling with the GalaxyTBM and FALC (Fragment Assembly and Loop Closure) programs.48−50 The spike protein tail, membrane, and associated glycosylations were removed from the initial model, leaving only the residues that comprise the spike protein head (residues 1–1146).

The amino acid mutations corresponding to the Delta (GenBank Accession ID:QTW89558.1)51 and Omicron BA.1 (GenBank Accession ID: UFO69279.1)52 spike protein variants were introduced based on their respective reference amino acid sequence deposited in the NCBI GenBank database www.ncbi.nih.gov and is described in Table 1.53

Coarse-grained representations of the spike protein were generated from the all-atom systems with the Martini 2.2 force field using the martinize.py script, producing a representation composed of ∼7600 CG beads.54,55 Subsequently, the coarse-grained systems were embedded in a 1.0 nm rectangular box, adding ∼40,000 CG water beads corresponding to ∼120,000 water molecules. The systems were solvated in 0.15 M KCl solution, with the appropriate Na+ or Cl– ions added to maintain charge neutrality within the system. The water parameters were defined by the Martini 2.2 force field, while the ions were defined by the Martini 2.0 force field. A coarse-grained representation of the wild-type S–ACE2 complex was also prepared to validate the methodology in the presence of hACE2, producing a representation composed of ∼9000 CG protein beads and ∼41,000 CG water beads corresponding to ∼123,000 water molecules.

Coarse-Grained Molecular Dynamics Simulations

Coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS 5.0,56 starting from energy minimization using the steepest descent algorithm, followed by a 1 ns NPT equilibration with a time step of 20 fs. The temperature and pressure were controlled during equilibration using the velocity rescale (v-rescale) thermostat57 and Berendsen barostat using isotropic coupling,58 respectively. A two microsecond (2 μs) NPT production run was then performed. The temperature was controlled at 310 K using the v-rescale thermostat57 with a temperature coupling time constant of 1.0 ps, while the pressure was controlled at 1 atm using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat59 with a pressure coupling time constant of 12.0 ps. The change in the barostat between equilibration and production run was necessary because the Parrinello–Rahman barostat tends to oscillate during equilibration in coarse-grained molecular dynamics (CGMD).60 The coordinates were saved in the trajectory every 20 ps during the production run.

All simulations were conducted under periodic boundary conditions. Long-range van der Waals interactions were accounted by applying a potential shift within the Verlet cutoff scheme, using a cutoff distance of 1.1 nm.61 Coulomb interactions were treated using a reaction field with a relative electrostatic screening value of 2.5 to represent water electrostatics. These conditions were applied to significantly accelerate the CGMD simulations while preserving the energy conservation of the system.60 The LINCS algorithm62 was applied to handle constraints for ring systems and stiff bond constraints.

Pocket Analysis

Pocket analysis was conducted to investigate the druggability potential of pockets present in the three variants of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein. Snapshots were extracted from the CGMD trajectories at 10 ns intervals and were converted to an all-atomistic representation, resulting in 200 snapshots.63 Before cavity/pocket detection was performed, the solvent molecules and added counterions were removed from the 200 frames of the back-mapped trajectory.

MDpocket was employed to characterize the detected pockets in the three SARS-CoV-2 spike variants.64 To carry out pocket characterization, a threshold value of 0.5 was set to extract pocket-like grid points with at least 50% frequency. The resulting grid points representing each pocket are then taken to facilitate the necessary calculations for evaluating the mean local hydrophobic density of every pocket to assess their druggability potential.65 The trajectories were aligned with reference to the loops and domains surrounding the three pockets.

Community Network Analysis

For community network analysis, the CGMD trajectories were first aligned by superimposing the trajectories with respect to the backbone beads of the spike protein central scaffold. The central scaffold includes an α helix represented by residues 747–783, the heptad repeat region 1 (912–984), and the central helix (987–1032) in each chain. A dynamical cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) was first calculated from the coarse-grained trajectory to measure the correlation of atomic displacements between the backbone beads of residues i and j (cij).66 A cutoff cij value of 0.4 was applied to simplify the network configuration, setting the |cij| value of an element in the DCCM to 0 if it falls below the cutoff. From the DCCM, an initial weighted correlation network representation was constructed, where each node represents one protein residue. A contact map filter was incorporated to ensure that network nodes are physically in contact with each other, using criteria for where backbone CG-bead atoms are within 15 Å of each other in at least 75% of snapshots.

Hierarchical clustering was then performed on the initial network configuration to cluster the nodes into communities, which are clusters of highly intraconnected nodes within the cluster and loosely interconnected with other communities. Community detection was done using the Girvan–Newman algorithm, which uses the edge betweenness through nodes as partition criteria.67 The algorithm removes nodes with the highest edge betweenness iteratively until no nodes remain, resulting in a community network. In this network, each community is represented by one node, connected by edge weights equal to the logarithm of the correlation coupling −log(|Cij|) between two communities. A final filtering of the network was performed to remove isolated nodes or nodes with only one residue as a member. The resulting network with the highest modularity, indicating an optimal clustering efficiency, was used for subsequent analysis. Visualization of these networks in relation to the protein was done using VMD. All calculations were performed using Bio3D in R.68

Suboptimal Path Analysis

Included in the Bio3D package, suboptimal path analysis is done to identify paths from a source node, representing a residue of a candidate pocket, to a sink node, representing a residue within the spike–ACE2 interface.69,70 For each spike protein variant, 200 paths between the assigned source and sink nodes were analyzed. Path lengths were compared among the networks of the three variants to assess the strength of correlated protein motion along each pathway. The path length between residues i and j is equal to −ln(|cij|), where cij is an element within the DCCM. Residues with a node degeneracy exceeding 10% were identified as “on-path” residues, indicating their importance in the potential allosteric pathway of the spike protein variants.

Results

Overview of the Dynamics of Spike Protein Mutants

Given the large size of the trimeric spike protein, coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations were utilized for enhanced conformational sampling as this provides a balance between computational time and exploration of unique conformations especially for larger and more complex biomolecules.71 As such, conformational changes in all-atomistic details (i.e., H-bonding interactions) are not considered in this approach.

An initial look at the CGMD trajectories through a root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) plot confirms the global stability of all trajectories. For trajectories representing the three variants, the protein RMSD ranges between 4 and 5.2 Å after 0.25 μs, with the wild type exhibiting the lowest RMSD, followed by Delta and then Omicron BA.1 (Figure S1). As the RMSD values start to stabilize after 1 μs, there is a sufficient sampling of the spike proteins in the open conformation, which can be used in the subsequent biophysical analysis.

A closer look into the flexibility of the spike proteins is done by calculating the root-mean-square fluctuations (RMSFs) of the backbone coarse-grained beads in each residue relative to their respective average structure. The Debye–Waller factor, commonly used in crystallography, is used as a measurement of atomic fluctuations through the equation , with B being the B-factor and ⟨ u2⟩ as the RMSF, both in Å2.

The N501Y mutation significantly improved the affinity of the viral spike proteins to cellular receptors, thereby resulting in a substantial increase in transmissions of the Omicron variant. Interestingly, the B-factors along the S–ACE2 interface in the three spike proteins studied remain similar (Figure 1), except for the higher B-factor along the N501A residue in Delta and Omicron BA.1, which contains the N501Y mutation in both variants.

The focus is on changes in the B-factor within the receptor-binding domains and the central scaffold, as these residues are important in the composition of the three pockets being studied. The lower half of these pockets include the central scaffold, wherein the α helices of 747–783 and the HR1 can be involved in two pockets, as depicted in Figure 1. Also, these pockets still exist within the S–ACE2 complex, as depicted in Figure S2c. As these pockets are around 30 Å away from the spike–ACE2 interface, these pockets also represent potential allosteric targets that are distant from the orthosteric binding site toward hACE2.

Meanwhile, higher B-factors were observed for the Omicron BA.1 spike protein in chains A and C of the receptor-binding domain (Figure 2a,2e) and the HR1 in chain B (Figure 2d). Asymmetry is observed in the change in B-factor across chains in the spike protein variants, in line with an existing study that shows increasing distortion in the symmetry of the spike protein at the receptor-binding domains, especially in the Omicron variant.72

The visualization of the relative B-factor using a three-dimensional (3D) B-factor worm plot allows for the comparison of conformational flexibility among different spike protein variants. The ratio between the B-factor of a spike protein variant and that of the wild type is plotted along the structure of the wild-type spike protein, revealing the more flexible regions in the variants compared to the wild type. Figure 3 demonstrates changes in relative B-factor in the Delta and Omicron BA.1 spike proteins relative to wild-type spike protein in pockets A, B, and C. While increased flexibility at each pocket in the Delta and Omicron BA.1 spike proteins is generally observed, pockets B in Delta and pockets B and C in the Omicron show multiple loops with increased flexibility, enhancing the number of conformations these pockets can achieve. Since pocket druggability can be affected by conformational changes within the pocket (i.e., opening and closing of transient pockets), it is important to contextualize the changes in pocket dynamics and correlate these with physical or chemical descriptors that bring light into pocket druggability.

Pocket Analysis Reveals a Potential Druggable Pocket between Wild-Type, Delta, and Omicron BA.1 Spike Proteins

Plausible druggable sites were identified beneath the receptor-binding domains (RBDs) and near the spike protein central scaffold (CS). The CS region represents a conserved protein segment. Three pockets (as shown in Figure 1) were observed near the CS, signifying their inherent conservation. However, the emergence of variants due to specific mutations may lead to subtle alterations in these pockets.

Similar studies have also investigated the pockets within this region.73,74 Di Paola et al. examined the region at the junction of the fusion peptide located at the RBDC/subdomain region 1, and the flexibility for peptide binding in this region is attributed to the presence of β sheets and unfolded traits in the binding site of their probed peptide.73,74 However, during the employed MDPocket simulations and analysis, the aforementioned region was not observed, likely due to parameters optimized for detecting finer pockets for accommodating small organic molecules. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that similar interactions were still detected in the analysis.

Pocket detection using MDPocket relies on Voronoi tessellation, an algorithm that utilizes Voronoi vertices detected in open space as α sphere centers.75 α spheres are pseudoatoms that are in open space and in contact with four protein atoms, which can be assigned as either polar or apolar based on the hydrophobicity of contact protein atoms per sphere.75 Given this approach, the calculation of volume is underestimated compared to other sphere-filling algorithms such as SPACEBALL,76 which also takes into account rotations within a density grid for more accurate volume calculations.77 Also, since the calculation of associated pocket properties is based on the location and identity of α spheres with respect to the protein, the method is sensitive to protein alignment, which we set to be residues within 15 Å of the three identified pockets.

The main focus of the analysis is on the mean local hydrophobic density of the detected pockets, as it is found to be the main descriptor for druggability in MDpocket benchmarks. The mean local hydrophobic density (ρ) is a significant descriptor, as it indicates the presence of significant hydrophobic amino acid residues within the pocket along with its associated compactness. It is a unitless descriptor, calculated as the total number of neighboring apolar α spheres over the total number of apolar α spheres within every pocket.75 As the descriptor is commonly used as an index of binding site druggability,64 analyzing the mean local hydrophobic density in each pocket can provide insights into the druggability of these sites.

Across the three variants, the reported mean local hydrophobic density for all pockets ranges between 0 and 170. The relatively high standard deviation observed could be a result of the difference in the assignment of polar versus apolar α spheres within the ensemble of structures. The highest mean local hydrophobic densities are observed in the wild type of pocket A and the Delta spike protein in pockets B and C. In general, these results demonstrate the high degree of compactness in the pockets of the Delta spike protein and the relatively lower hydrophobicity of the pockets in Omicron BA.1 compared to other spike protein variants. The difference in compactness between the pockets can be attributed to the number of hydrophobic amino acid residues present in each pocket, with pockets A and B in the Delta variant having a higher number of leucine residues in their structure. Leucine, being a nonpolar amino acid residue, can facilitate hydrophobic interactions with the ligand, potentially increasing its binding affinity toward the spike protein. Furthermore, the pocket analysis of the spike–ACE2 complex reveals that pockets A, B, and C remain conserved in the presence of ACE2, with a difference in mean local hydrophobic density ranging between 15 and 30% compared to the wild-type spike protein.

In summary, a favorable environment for protein–ligand interactions can be evident in the three pockets below every RBD chain due to the hydrophobic environment of these pockets along with its compactness. Based on the results obtained from the descriptors, it is suggested that the three pockets found across different variants have the potential to serve as druggable allosteric sites for small ligand-like molecules. However, these pockets in Omicron BA.1 are found to be less hydrophobic compared to its wild-type and Delta counterparts, which is an effect of the amino acid mutations in Omicron BA.1, especially with the lysine mutations surrounding the pockets.

Correlation Network Analysis Reveals Community Coupling of Different Strengths within Spike Protein Variants

Corresponding correlation networks for wild-type, Delta, and Omicron BA.1 spike proteins are produced from the community network analysis in Bio3D.68 As the DCCM is calculated based on atomic displacements, changes contributed by rotations and/or torsions are not considered. For a simpler comparative analysis between the three networks, a correlation value cutoff of 0.4 was applied as community networks with almost identical numbers of communities (between 29 and 31) and edges (between 70 and 78). The maximum modularity of these networks, ranging from 0.7 to 0.8, signifies the high quality of community clustering. While the protein residues assigned to each community vary between variants, alignment of these networks from common residues between the three network representations can be done. In comparison, using a correlation value cutoff of 0.5 produces networks with varying numbers of communities (between 52 and 93), which would make it difficult for comparative analysis of community networks. A similar community structure was observed for the S–ACE2 complex with the ACE2 complex grouped into one community node.

Figures 4 and S3 show a network representation of wild-type, Delta, and Omicron BA.1 spike proteins relative to their respective protein structures at the top view and side view, respectively. In these figures, only major communities are depicted, which are defined here as communities with 100 or more protein residues, or communities involved in the potential allosteric communication between pockets A, B, or C toward the spike–ACE2 interface. In this depiction, 80–90% of the residues for each spike protein variant are included in 10–15 major communities in their respective network representations. A depiction of the community representation of the spike protein from the top view is represented in Figure 4. Most residues of the N-terminal domains of chains A and B are represented by the blue and purple nodes, respectively. In the case of NTDC, most of its residues are represented by one major community in wild type and Omicron BA.1 (orange node), while it is composed of two communities for Delta (orange and dark orange nodes). The entire RBDA domain can be generally represented by the red node in the wild-type and Delta spike proteins. The community clustering for RBDB and RBDC is more complex, as its residues are clustered into two to four major communities per domain for each spike protein variant. Succeeding discussion will focus on the β-sheets forming the RBD core (β1–β4 and β7) and the secondary structures included in the receptor-binding motif (α4 and α5 helices, β5 and β6 sheets) for clarity. Community clustering of the remaining secondary structures is shown in Figure 4. Overall, the RBDB of the wild type is distributed along the black, orange, and yellow nodes. The black nodes include most of the core structure of RBDB (β1–β3 and β7 sheets), while the yellow node includes the β4 sheet along with its receptor-binding motif. For the Delta spike protein, the domain is split along the orange and yellow nodes, with the β sheets forming the RBDB core and most of the RBM inside the orange node. The α5 helix, meanwhile, is assigned with the yellow node. The residues of RBDB in Omicron BA.1 are distributed along the black, yellow, and orange nodes. In particular, the RBDB core is assigned to the orange node, while α2–α5 helices and the β6 sheet are assigned to the yellow node.

The RBDC residues in the wild type are distributed along the white, red, blue, and yellow nodes. In particular, its RBDC core β-sheets and RBM are part of the red and yellow nodes. The networks of Delta show the distribution of RBDC residues along the white, blue, violet, red, and yellow nodes. Its RBDC core β-sheets are part of the yellow node, while the RBM is distributed along the red and yellow nodes. For Omicron, RBDC is distributed along the blue, red, and yellow nodes, with the RBDC core and RBM mostly assigned with the yellow node, except for the β5 sheet, which is assigned in the yellow node.

The important communication hubs within the overall spike protein can be assessed from the edge betweenness of residues within the networks, as these represent the number of edges passing through a particular node within a network prior to community clustering. The edge betweenness of each network was calculated, and the residues with the highest betweenness are distributed along the NTD and S2 subunit across variants, in particular, the region defining residues 858–911 and the central scaffold, in each monomeric unit. This is in contrast to an existing network-based study of the wild-type protein where residues with high edge betweenness exist along the NTD and subdomain region 1 (between residues 529 and 590) of the wild-type spike protein,42 which could be due to the looser criteria in this paper for determining edges. Interestingly, some RBD residues are included in residues identified with high edge betweenness, which are found to be in either the red or yellow nodes. Given the topographic location of these two nodes and the presence of amino acid mutations across variants along the RBD, the differences in community coupling across these two nodes are further discussed.

The strength of community coupling in networks can be qualitatively assessed from the network edge weights, as these are derived from the DCCM values calculated for each coarse-grained trajectory. The edge weight between two communities is calculated as −log|Cij|, where Cij is the correlation value between two different communities. Thus, a lower edge weight corresponds to a stronger coupling between the two communities. From the perspective of the red and yellow nodes, comparison of the three networks show stronger intercommunity couplings in the wild type between the RBDA and NTDA (blue/red nodes) and the center of the RBDB and RBDC cores toward the rest of RBDB (yellow/black node), as depicted by the red edges in Figure 4a. Enhanced couplings involving the subdomain region 1 of chain C toward the RBDC core (white/yellow) in the Delta spike protein is observed, as depicted in the red edges in Figure 4b. Meanwhile, tighter intercommunity couplings that involve the RBDC core toward RBDA (yellow/red), NTDA (yellow/blue), and RBDB core/NTDC (yellow/orange) are generally observed in the Omicron BA.1 network, as depicted in the green edges in Figure 4c.

Taken together, with the yellow node representing RBDB and RBDC residues at the center of the spike protein topographically, preferential couplings across different communities are observed in the networks, with the coupling of the rest of the RBDB more favored in the wild type, coupling with the subdomain region 1 preferred at the Delta, and the Omicron BA.1 having enhanced coupling toward the RBDA, NTDA, and NTDC. However, this does not yet take into consideration the communication pathways from the pockets toward the RBD, which can be assessed further through suboptimal path analysis.

Suboptimal Path Analysis Reveals Different Communication Hotspots between Spike Protein Variants

Suboptimal path analysis was performed to provide a finer description on the possible change in communication for each variant from the three candidate pockets toward the spike–ACE2 interface. From the 200 shortest paths, information about path length, node degeneracy, and on-path residues is obtained to provide insights on the identity and relative strength of atomic displacement correlation between critical residues within the communication pathway. The path length is taken as the summation of all paths between source and sink residues, with the path between two residues corresponding to their cij element from DCCM, i.e., a shorter path corresponds to a stronger correlation between the motions of two residues.

The residue representing the spike–ACE2 interface is set to be the Q493 of the wild-type spike protein as the residue represents an important residue involved in the binding between spike protein and hACE2, forming charge–charge interactions with the hACE2.78,79 The Q493K mutation in Omicron BA.1 is also implicated as a factor in the weaker binding affinity of the Omicron BA.1 spike protein toward hACE2 compared to the wild-type and Delta variants.80 The source residues for pockets A, B, and C are set to be I973B, E748A, and Q755B, respectively. These residues, which are all located within the central scaffold, are found in pocket analysis to be common in their respective pockets across the wild type and its variants.

The suboptimal path analysis between pocket A and the spike–ACE2 interface is shown in Figure S4. While the possibility of other alternate pathways aside from identified pathways in Figures 5 and S4 and S5 cannot be discounted, these would correspond to pathways of longer path lengths and may involve interactions with weaker protein correlations versus identified pathways in suboptimal path analysis. This is a consequence of the Yen algorithm, which is used to calculate suboptimal paths based on the framework, wherein these paths can share edges and paths with the shortest identified path.69 Thus, diffusion of the allosteric signal relative to the shortest path can be addressed.

To address the question of the sufficiency of analyzing two hundred paths, suboptimal path analysis was repeated by identifying 500, 1000, 2000, and 5000 paths of the communication pathway between pocket B and the S–ACE2 interface for the wild type, where similar path lengths and node degeneracies were obtained in comparison to the results in Figure 5a. The histogram of the path lengths is shown in Figure S5.

As path lengths are taken solely from the correlation elements within the DCCM, it only gives an idea on the correlation strength but not the probability of a certain path being favored over the other. These results are also limited by the degree of conformational sampling, as the 2 μs CGMD trajectories are limited to capture dynamics within the RBD up conformation, i.e., global conformational changes toward the closed, RBD 2-Up, RBD 3-Up conformation or any of its related intermediates are not considered. While the possibility of pathways of shorter path lengths at longer time scales cannot be eliminated, these results remain helpful in understanding the possible changes of communication within the spike protein variants. The question of whether or not this communication can lead to protein allostery within the spike protein variants can be further addressed in the presence of simulations with the hACE2 receptor, along with ligands with favorable binding to any of these pockets.

As pocket analysis of the wild-type S-hACE2 complex shows the conservation of the three pockets, the potential communication pathways of the wild-type spike protein in the presence of ACE2 were compared. Interestingly, the communication pathways slightly differ across the three pockets, as depicted in Figure S6. This could be a result of dynamic changes along the RBDA in the presence of hACE2 or due to differences in motions that can vary on different time scales. Despite this observation, the path lengths from pockets A and B to the spike–hACE2 interface remain similar (Figure S6c). However, in the case of pocket C, shorter path lengths are observed in the presence of hACE2, implying stronger communication in the holo form.

Overall, the suboptimal path analysis from the three pockets to the spike–ACE2 interface shows the communication between RBDA with RBDB or RBDC as important factors in the possible allosterism within the spike protein, as the shorter paths which correspond to more dynamic protein coupling indicate stronger interdomain interactions between the three RBD units in the wild type. In the lack of direct communication from the pocket toward either RBDB or RBDC, communication via either the NTD or CTD is possible, which can be seen from the pathways from pocket B in Delta and the pathway from pocket C in the wild type. However, in comparison to the path lengths from the same pockets, these pathways exist as weaker pathways compared to pathways involving RBDB or RBDC.

Discussion

As proteins are dynamically moving biomolecules, it is important to take into consideration the conformational changes within the spike protein variants, especially along the pockets, which could serve as possible druggable target regions against the spike protein. Three pockets underneath the RBD of the spike proteins were analyzed and identified to be more flexible in the Delta and Omicron BA.1 variants compared to the wild type due to the added flexibility of the RBD loops surrounding the pockets and in some cases, the α helices within the central scaffold.

Given the effects of conformational flexibility on site druggability, the mean local hydrophobic density of every pocket was calculated. It was observed that pockets located in the Delta and Omicron BA.1 variant, especially on pocket B, demonstrate a substantial potential for druggability, owing to their enhanced mean local hydrophobic density. This characteristic is highly attributed to the abundance of leucine residues present in the pocket. The presence of hACE2 however could affect the druggability of the pockets, as shown by 15 to 30% decrease in mean local hydrophobic density in the wild type.

Community network analysis based on the correlation of protein motions provides a depiction of the differences in the strengths of communication within the spike protein variants. Motions included are limited to the domain motions within the spike proteins at the up conformation, as the conformational sampling is not enough to consider a global conformational change to conformations where multiple RBDs are in the up conformation. The differences in community membership and degree of intercommunity coupling between community networks, especially involving RBDB and RBDC, show intrinsic changes in the communication between the spike protein variants. This is evident, especially in the couplings involving the RBDC core, which is located geometrically above the central scaffold of the spike protein with multiple residues within the core identified as residues with high betweenness values in network analysis. Better coupling toward the RBDA and NTDA were observed in Omicron BA.1 networks, while better coupling to the RBDB core is observed in the wild type.

The suboptimal path analysis reveals the possible communication pathways from pocket toward the spike–ACE2 interface, under the assumption that a residue within the central scaffold is perturbed upon interaction or the presence of drug-like ligand. The analysis show the case of possible communication between pocket B and the spike–ACE2 interface passing to RBDB in the wild type, while RBDC residues are involved in the communication within the Delta and Omicron BA.1 variants, albeit as a longer path in the absence of a direct route from pocket B directly to RBDB. Interestingly, the subdomain region 1, identified as a hinge in controlling the RBD toward either the up or down conformation,17 can be involved in the communication pathway as an alternative and weaker path in the absence of a direct path between pocket and RBD, as seen in the case of Delta in pocket B and the wild type for pocket A. These residues are also identified as a putative allosteric site in the search for spike protein peptide inhibitors,73,74 which further suggests the importance of this RBD hinge for potential allosteric modulation of the spike protein using small ligand-like drug molecules or peptides.

As these pathways are based on MD trajectories, it is limited by the degree of conformational sampling, and the possibility of more efficient allosteric pathways arising cannot be discounted, which could be facilitated by rarer events, including global motion changes and H-bond switching within the suggested pathway that are not captured in 2 μs CGMD simulations. Also, the effects of hACE2 on the conformational change of spike protein should be addressed, especially if pocket C is to be targeted. Further validation of these changes in communication pathways to confirm allostery within the spike protein can be done as a part of in silico drug discovery upon conformational sampling of the ligand-bound protein in apo or holo form.

Conclusions

This approach highlights the importance of considering the dynamics of the entire trimeric spike protein in the search for potential allosteric pockets and pathways that can be exploited in the quest for allosteric anti-COVID-19 drugs. The amino acid mutations in Delta and Omicron BA.1 spike proteins affect the spike protein as a potential allosteric target versus COVID-19. The search for potential druggable pockets has revealed three pockets located underneath the RBD that could be exploited in drug discovery studies due to their favorable hydrophobic environment. These enhanced properties are attributed to the greater number of leucine and isoleucine residues within the pockets, especially in the case of Delta and Omicron BA.1 variants. However, there is a less favorable hydrophobic environment in Omicron BA.1 compared with the wild-type and Delta spike proteins. As these pockets are conserved in the wild type when bound to hACE2, these observations indicate that the identified pockets may constitute viable targets for drug development, particularly in the context of combating the prevalent COVID-19 variants.

Community network analysis and suboptimal path analysis, on the other hand, reveal changes in potential allosteric communication between these pockets toward the spike–ACE2 interface. Specifically, in the case of pocket B, the stronger correlation between motions of RBDB toward the RBDA in wild-type pathways is observed, while coupling from RBDC toward RBDA is more favored in the Delta and Omicron BA.1 spike proteins, but these pathways exhibit weaker overall coupling compared to the wild-type pathway. Further investigation of the dynamics on the presence of hACE2 could strengthen the understanding of spike protein allostery, in particular with the case of pocket C.

While there could be lesser causation between the dynamics of the pockets and the spike–ACE2 interface in the spike protein variants due to weaker coupling of protein motions, the increased flexibility of the loops surrounding the pockets, along with the changes in mean local hydrophobic density within the pockets, could provide an alternative opportunity to target the spike protein in the continuous search for mutation-proof allosteric COVID-19 drugs.
