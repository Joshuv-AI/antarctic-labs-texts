Comparative Effectiveness of Antiarrhythmic Drugs on Early Atrial Tachyarrhythmias in the Blanking Period After Atrial Fibrillation Catheter Ablation
Authors: Asami Kashiwa, Yukio Hosaka, Yuka Hayashi, Komei Tanaka, Kazuyuki Ozaki, Masaomi Chinushi, Yoshifusa Aizawa, Kazuyoshi Takahashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEarly recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmia (ERAT) after ablation for atrial fibrillation (AF) is common during the blanking period; however, no standardized management approach exists. This study aimed to identify effective antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs) during the blanking period and to examine the association between AAD efficacy and long‐term outcomes.MethodsInitially, 497 patients who underwent catheter ablation for AF between April 2017 and December 2022 were included. Among these, patients who experienced ERAT after their first AF ablation were analyzed for this retrospective study. During the blanking period, the effectiveness of three AAD regimens, pilsicainide, bepridil, and bepridil plus aprindine, was compared. Patients were followed for 2 years after AAD discontinuation and stratified according to late recurrence.ResultsAmong the 75 patients analyzed, ERAT was suppressed in 29.6% (8/27) with pilsicainide, 38.1% (8/21) with bepridil, and 77.8% (21/27) with bepridil plus aprindine (p = 0.0009). Among patients without late recurrence, ERAT suppression was higher with bepridil‐based regimens: pilsicainide, 27.8% (5/18); bepridil, 66.7% (6/9); and bepridil plus aprindine, 89.5% (17/19) (p = 0.0006). Poor response to bepridil‐based therapy was associated with a high late recurrence rate (73.7%, 14/19), whereas lack of response to pilsicainide was not (31.6%, 6/19) (p = 0.009).ConclusionBepridil, particularly in combination with aprindine, was effective for managing ERAT after AF ablation. A poor response to bepridil or to bepridil plus aprindine during the blanking period may serve as a marker for identifying patients at higher risk of late recurrence.Keywords: antiarrhythmic drugs, atrial fibrillation, catheter ablation, early recurrence

Introduction

Early recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmias (ERAT) is a common phenomenon after ablation for atrial fibrillation (AF), with rates reported in up to 50% of patients following the procedure [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. The 2024 EHRA/HRS/APHRS/LAHRS expert consensus statement [6] defines this interval as the “blanking period,” during which ERAT episodes are not considered when evaluating ablation success. The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying ERAT are complex and multifactorial [7, 8], involving myocardial edema [9, 10], inflammation [11, 12], oxidative stress [13], and transient ischemia [14], factors that contribute to arrhythmogenesis during the early post‐ablation phase. These temporary changes generally resolve over time, leading to a reduction in ERAT, supporting the rationale for the blanking period. However, ERAT may also result from pulmonary vein (PV) reconnection or non‐PV triggers, which may indicate true AF recurrence and should be differentiated from transient ERAT.

Although ERAT does not necessarily reflect treatment failure and can be temporary during the blanking period, its presence has been reported as a predictor of long‐term outcomes [5]. Currently, no standardized approach exists for managing arrhythmias during this period [15], and the relationship between the efficacy of antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs) for ERAT and long‐term outcomes remains unclear.

This study aimed to address these gaps by identifying and comparing AAD strategies for ERAT during the blanking period.

Methods

Study Population

In this retrospective analysis, we included 497 consecutive patients who underwent initial catheter ablation for AF between April 2017 and December 2022. Patients who developed ERAT during the blanking period and received AAD therapy were selected for analysis. AADs were initiated after the recurrence of atrial arrhythmias during the blanking period.

Ablation Procedure

All AADs were discontinued for five half‐lives before ablation. Procedures involved standardized PV isolation using radiofrequency ablation or cryoballoon ablation, selected at the operator's discretion based on left atrial anatomy assessed by preoperative cardiac computed tomography.

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia using thiopental sodium and dexmedetomidine. Heparin infusion was adjusted to maintain an activated clotting time > 300 s. Esophageal temperature was continuously monitored. Three‐dimensional electroanatomical mapping was performed using CARTO (Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA, USA) or Ensite (Abbott Cardiovascular, Plymouth, MN, USA). An irrigated contact force‐sensing catheter was used for radiofrequency ablation.

Acute PV isolation success was confirmed by demonstrating a bidirectional block. The left atrium was mapped using a multipolar catheter to verify complete electrical isolation of all PVs. Adenosine triphosphate was administered to assess dormant conduction; if present, additional ablation was performed until conduction was eliminated. After PV isolation, induction testing was performed using isoproterenol infusion and atrial burst pacing to assess AF inducibility and the presence of non–pulmonary vein foci. When sustained AF persisted after PV isolation, left atrial linear ablation or superior vena cava isolation was optionally performed based on clinical judgment.

Definitions of ERAT and AAD Efficacy

ERAT was defined as any AF or atrial tachycardia episode lasting > 30 s, documented by electrocardiogram or cardiac monitoring within 2 months after ablation [6]. When ERAT occurred, AADs were initiated to suppress episodes until the end of the blanking period.

For pharmacological management, pilsicainide (Class Ic antiarrhythmic drug), bepridil (multichannel blocker), or combination therapy with bepridil and aprindine (Class Ib antiarrhythmic drug) were commonly used as first‐line therapy. Patients were categorized into three groups according to the initial AAD regimen (pilsicainide, bepridil, or bepridil plus aprindine), and the effectiveness of each first‐line strategy was evaluated (Figure 1). If the initial AAD showed inadequate efficacy, medication adjustments were allowed. Typically, patients unresponsive to Class I agents (e.g., pilsicainide) were switched to bepridil, and aprindine was added if bepridil alone was insufficient. The effectiveness of second‐line therapy after modification of initial treatment was also assessed. Initial doses of AAD in the blanking period were as follows: pilsicainide at 100–150 mg/day, bepridil at 100 mg/day, and aprindine at 20 or 40 mg/day, depending on body weight. Drug selection was determined by the treating physician based on prior exposure, clinical experience, and safety considerations.

Study flow diagram. Among 497 patients who underwent catheter ablation for atrial fibrillation (AF), 104 developed early recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmia (ERAT). Of these, 84 patients who received one of the following first‐line antiarrhythmic drug (AAD) regimens—pilsicainide, bepridil, or bepridil plus aprindine—were included in the analysis.

AAD therapy was considered effective if: (1) ERAT recurrence was suppressed after initiation, (2) incessant ERAT episodes were markedly reduced and eventually eliminated, or (3) sinus rhythm was maintained without ERAT recurrence following a successful electrical cardioversion. AAD efficacy was compared among treatment subgroups.

AAD Efficacy and Long‐Term Outcomes

All AADs prescribed during the blanking period were discontinued at the end of the period. Patients were scheduled for follow‐up at 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months after the procedure, including 12‐lead electrocardiogram and/or 24‐h Holter monitoring. Additional monitoring was performed when recurrence‐related symptoms were reported. All patients were followed for over 2 years, and late AF recurrence was compared across first‐line AAD groups based on blanking period ERAT suppression.

Because ERAT may arise from transient causes (e.g., inflammation, tissue edema) or persistent triggers (e.g., PV reconnection, non‐PV foci), patients with ERAT were stratified according to the presence or absence of late recurrence after AAD discontinuation to differentiate transient arrhythmias from persistent recurrence‐related arrhythmias.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate, based on data distribution. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were compared among the three groups using one‐way analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages and were compared using the chi‐square test. Recurrence‐free survival was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards analyses were performed to identify predictors of long‐term AF recurrence. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariate analysis and clinically relevant covariates were entered into the multivariable Cox regression model. Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons. Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10 (version 10.6.0) and JMP Pro (version 19.1.1, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Patients With ERAT and AAD Selection

Of the total cohort undergoing AF ablation, 104 patients (20.9%) developed ERAT during the blanking period (Figure 1). Among them, 84 patients (80.8%) received AAD therapy: pilsicainide (27 patients, 32.1%), bepridil (21 patients, 25.0%), bepridil plus aprindine (27 patients, 32.1%), and other agents (9 patients, 10.7%). Other medications included cibenzoline (7 patients), amiodarone (1 patient), and aprindine alone (1 patient). Sixteen patients who received bepridil for ERAT had already been on bepridil before ablation.

AAD efficacy and long‐term outcomes were assessed in three treatment groups: pilsicainide (n = 27), bepridil (n = 21), and bepridil plus aprindine (n = 27). Among these 75 patients, the mean age was 61.5 ± 9.2 years, and 73.3% were male. Paroxysmal AF accounted for 64% of cases, and persistent AF for 36%. Radiofrequency ablation was performed in 36% of patients, while 64% underwent cryoballoon ablation. Successful PV isolation was achieved in all cases. Additional ablation beyond PV isolation was performed in eight patients. Baseline characteristics and procedural details did not differ significantly among the groups (Table 1).

ERAT occurred within 2 days post‐ablation in 54 patients (72.0%). Twenty patients (26.7%) developed ERAT within 1 month after discharge, and one additional patient experienced ERAT just within 2 months post‐ablation period.

AAD Efficacy in ERAT

ERAT was effectively suppressed in 37 of 75 patients (49.3%) using one of the three AAD strategies. Among them, 21 patients (56.8%) had no ERAT recurrence, 9 (24.3%) showed suppression of incessant episodes, and 7 (18.9%) remained recurrence‐free after a single electrical cardioversion. ERAT suppression rates were 29.6% (8/27) in the pilsicainide group, 38.1% (8/21) in the bepridil group, and 77.8% (21/27) in the bepridil plus aprindine group (p = 0.0009) (Table 2 and Figure 2). Combination therapy was significantly more effective than monotherapy with either bepridil (p = 0.005) or pilsicainide (p = 0.0004).

Two‐Year Late AF Free Survival After the Discontinuation of AADs

After discontinuation of AADs at the end of the blanking period, 46 of 75 patients (61.3%) remained free from late recurrence during the 2‐year follow‐up. Two‐year late AF‐free survival did not differ significantly among treatment groups (p = 0.06): pilsicainide, 66.7% (18/27); bepridil, 42.9% (9/21); and bepridil plus aprindine, 70.4% (19/27).

Factors associated with long‐term AF recurrence in patients receiving AAD for ERAT were investigated. In the univariate analysis, a duration of more than 5 years from the initial AF diagnosis to AF ablation was significantly associated with long‐term AF recurrence (HR 3.52, 95% CI 1.69–7.35, p = 0.0008). In the multivariate analysis, this variable remained an independent predictor of recurrence (HR 2.99, 95% CI 1.38–6.45, p = 0.0053). In contrast, the type of AAD used for ERAT management was not significantly associated with long‐term recurrence (Supporting Information 1). Additionally, there was no significant difference in the 2‐year recurrence‐free rate between patients who received any of the three AADs and those who did not receive any AAD therapy (with AAD [n = 75]: 61.3% vs. no AAD [n = 20]: 70.0%, p = 0.45, Supporting Information 2).

AAD Efficacy in ERAT According to Late Recurrence Status

Among patients without late recurrence, bepridil‐based therapy demonstrated higher ERAT suppression during the blanking period (Table 2 and Figure 3A): 66.7% (6/9) for bepridil and 89.5% (17/19) for bepridil plus aprindine, compared with 27.8% (5/18) for pilsicainide (p = 0.0006). Combination therapy was more effective than pilsicainide monotherapy (p = 0.0001).

In contrast, among patients who later experienced recurrence, ERAT suppression rates during the blanking period did not differ significantly across groups: 33.3% (3/9) for pilsicainide, 16.7% (2/12) for bepridil, and 50.0% (4/8) for bepridil plus aprindine (p = 0.28) (Table 2 and Figure 3B). Efficacy of Second‐Line Antiarrhythmic Drug Therapy

Among patients who received pilsicainide as first‐line therapy, 19 initially showed an insufficient response. Of these, 12 patients were switched to bepridil therapy (including 3 who simultaneously started aprindine), and efficacy was observed in 9 patients (75%). Furthermore, 8 of these 9 patients (89%) who responded to bepridil remained free from late recurrence after AAD discontinuation. Among the nine patients who showed an inadequate response to bepridil monotherapy, aprindine was added in three cases. This treatment escalation successfully suppressed ERAT in all patients (3/3, 100%), and no late recurrence was observed during follow‐up (Figure 4).

Efficacy of second‐line antiarrhythmic drug (AAD) therapy for early recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmia (ERAT). Among patients in whom pilsicainide was ineffective (n = 19), switching to bepridil resulted in ERAT suppression in 9 of 12 patients (75.0%). Among patients in whom BEP was ineffective (n = 9), addition of aprindine (APR) was performed in 3 patients, and all of them resulted in ERAT suppression in all cases (3/3).

Predictive Value of Response to Bepridil‐Based Therapy

Among patients who responded poorly to bepridil‐based therapy as first‐line treatment (monotherapy or combination with aprindine), late recurrence occurred in 73.7% (14/19), significantly higher than in those unresponsive to pilsicainide as first‐line treatment (31.6%; 6/19) (p = 0.009) (Table 3). Conversely, among patients who achieved effective ERAT suppression with bepridil‐based therapy, long‐term sinus rhythm was maintained in 79.3% (23/29). These findings suggest that early response to bepridil‐based therapy during the blanking period may predict late recurrence risk, whereas response to pilsicainide showed no prognostic association. In the analysis limited to patients who received bepridil or bepridil plus aprindine as first‐line therapy, poor response to bepridil‐based therapy remained the only independent predictor of AF recurrence in both univariate and multivariate analyses (HR 6.28, 95% CI 2.19–17.99, p = 0.0006) (Table 4). In contrast, among patients who received pilsicainide as first‐line therapy, poor response to pilsicainide was not associated with subsequent AF recurrence and did not emerge as a significant predictor in the analysis (Supporting Information 3).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that bepridil, particularly in combination with aprindine, showed superior efficacy in managing ERAT compared with pilsicainide. Furthermore, failure to respond to bepridil‐based therapy during the blanking period was associated with a higher rate of late AF recurrence. Thus, early efficacy of bepridil in ERAT may serve as a predictor of long‐term outcomes.

Pathogenesis of ERAT and Drug Efficacy

The post‐ablation period represents a unique pathophysiological state characterized by distinct arrhythmogenic mechanisms. The myocardium undergoes thermal injury, inflammation, and edema, creating conditions that differ substantially from the native AF substrate [6, 7, 8].

Because of these complex and heterogeneous mechanisms, ERAT after AF ablation is often clinically challenging and causes anxiety for both patients and clinicians regarding whether it will resolve or persist. Although ERAT requires management, no standardized therapeutic approach currently exists [6]. The European Heart Rhythm Association survey [15] has reported that blanking period management largely depends on individual physician preference, with Class I AADs commonly used empirically. Therefore, identifying effective AAD strategies for ERAT has important clinical relevance.

Mechanistic Insights for AADs

In this study, the addition of aprindine enhanced the antiarrhythmic effect of bepridil in ERAT. Previous studies have reported that sequential therapy with bepridil followed by aprindine restored sinus rhythm in persistent AF, with cumulative success increasing from 31% with bepridil alone to 69% after adding aprindine [16, 17]. Antiarrhythmic conversion of long‐standing AF may partly result from reversal of atrial remodeling [18], and sinus rhythm restoration is accompanied by an increased fibrillation cycle length [19].

In the present study, however, we observed rapid clinical responses to aprindine (with bepridil) in the majority of cases; termination or suppression of tachyarrhythmias occurred within 24 h of bepridil with or without aprindine. This rapid response is unlikely to be related to the reversal of atrial remodeling and is more likely attributable to the direct pharmacological effects of AADs on the atrial myocardium. With respect to the mechanism of aprindine, the drug may act preferentially on damaged myocardium after ablation, as cardiomyocytes are partially depolarized owing to cellular injury or ischemia. Aprindine may bind more effectively to sodium channels during the inactivated state, which predominates in partially depolarized myocardium [20]. Bepridil prolongs the duration of the inactivated state of sodium channels, potentially enhancing aprindine binding, and the combination prolongs atrial refractory periods [21]. In addition, similar to other Class Ib antiarrhythmic drugs such as mexiletine, aprindine counterbalances QT prolongation induced by bepridil [17]. In addition, bepridil has been suggested to inhibit ATP‐sensitive potassium channels, thereby attenuating the shortening of myocardial action potential duration during the early phase of ischemia [22]. Taken together, these pharmacodynamic interactions support bepridil plus aprindine as a rational therapeutic combination for ERAT management. No adverse events related to this combination were observed in the present study.

Clinical Impact of Early Recurrence

Previous randomized trials have shown that early recurrence after ablation predicts late recurrence [23, 24, 25, 26]. In particular, atrial tachyarrhythmias emerging later in the blanking period strongly correlate with subsequent AF recurrence [27]. Achieving ERAT control and distinguishing transient post‐ablation arrhythmias from those that predict subsequent AF recurrence are of considerable clinical importance. A randomized control trial also reported that empirical AAD therapy during the blanking period reduces clinically relevant arrhythmias and hospitalization without increasing serious adverse events [28]. Consequently, effective ERAT suppression may reduce clinical burden during this vulnerable phase. Effective control of ERAT may benefit not only patients, through reductions in symptoms and healthcare utilization, but also clinicians by reducing the need for repeated evaluations, emergency visits, and treatment adjustments during the blanking period.

Clinical Significance of AAD Responsiveness in ERAT

Early AAD use has not consistently demonstrated protective long‐term benefits [24, 29]. In this study, with patients developing ERAT, long‐term AF recurrence occurred in 38.7% over 2 years, and the AAD use during the blanking period did not significantly affect late outcomes. Two‐year AF‐free survival did not differ significantly among the pilsicainide, bepridil, and bepridil plus aprindine groups, nor between patients who received AADs and those who did not, suggesting that prognosis was not determined by the use of a specific AAD. While the primary objective of this study was to compare first‐line AAD strategies, a secondary observation was that patients who did not respond to bepridil‐based therapy during the blanking period had a markedly higher recurrence rate (73.7%). These findings suggest that responsiveness to specific AAD therapy during the blanking period may have prognostic value. The mechanisms underlying this association remain uncertain. One possible explanation is that transient or reversible arrhythmogenic conditions are more responsive to bepridil‐based therapy, and that bepridil‐resistant ERAT may reflect a more persistent arrhythmogenic substrate associated with late recurrence. This interpretation remains speculative because the present study did not directly evaluate the underlying atrial substrate or electrophysiological mechanisms. Further mechanistic studies and larger prospective cohorts are required to determine whether early drug response can reliably serve as a marker of long‐term recurrence risk.

Study Limitations

This study had some limitations. First, it was a single‐center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size. Second, AAD selection was not randomized, raising the possibility of selection bias. Third, ERAT suppression was defined clinically based on arrhythmia disappearance after drug initiation; spontaneous recovery due to tissue healing may also have contributed. Therefore, the influence of natural recovery cannot be completely excluded. In addition, the composite definition of ERAT suppression may include heterogeneous clinical responses. Furthermore, because rhythm monitoring was based on 12‐lead electrocardiogram and/or Holter monitoring, asymptomatic or intermittent ERAT episodes may have been undetected, and objective quantification of AF burden was challenging. Finally, in the era of pulsed field ablation, differences in lesion formation mechanisms compared with thermal ablation may result in distinct blanking period characteristics and drug responsiveness, warranting further investigation [30].

Conclusions

AAD therapy with bepridil, particularly in combination with aprindine, may be useful for managing ERAT after AF ablation. Response to bepridil‐based therapy during the blanking period may serve as a useful indicator of long‐term prognosis. Effective early ERAT control could reduce short‐term clinical burden and improve patient experience during post‐ablation recovery.
