Predicting microbially mediated plant coexistence is sensitive to vital rate identity and soil conditioning history
Authors: Petr Dostál
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding the mechanisms that maintain the coexistence of plant species is critical to addressing the global biodiversity crisis. Increasing attention has been paid to interactions between plants and soil microbes (plant–soil feedback, PSF), which can not only promote plant coexistence by increasing stabilizing effects but also hinder it by generating competitive fitness differences. However, the predictive power of the PSF has been questioned in recent studies because estimates of microbially mediated coexistence have correlated poorly with the outcomes of plant interactions observed in the field. This discrepancy may be due to the approaches typically used in PSF research, such as measuring PSF effects on a single vital rate or using soil conditioned for a short time period and without considering abiotic contexts. Here, I examined the effects of soil inoculum with different training histories and training environments (with and without added nutrients) on germination, seedling survival, and biomass of four grassland species. I then examined whether predictions of microbially mediated coexistence of four species pairs were sensitive to the vital rate identity, conditioning history, and soil training environment. I found that conspecific inoculum trained for longer had increasingly positive and negative effects on germination and biomass, respectively, although the effects of inoculum history varied across species and training environments. Estimates of microbially mediated outcomes were directly related to the vital rate used: when based on biomass and seedling survival, all four pairs were predicted to coexist, but only two pairs could do so when based on germination due to much reduced or even negative stabilization. Although coexistence predictions were not significantly related to conditioning history (including the effects of both variable conditioning durations and combinations of conditioning species) or nutrient treatments, both factors had a significant effect on stabilization. These results suggest that predictions of microbially mediated coexistence may be biased when based on a single vital rate, such as plant growth. To obtain more realistic and accurate outcome estimates, PSF effects should be integrated across different life stages, considering the temporal and abiotic contexts of these effects specific to a focal study system.

INTRODUCTION

Plant–soil feedback (PSF) is increasingly recognized as an important determinant of plant species abundance (Klironomos, 2002; Mangan et al., 2010) and community‐level processes, including plant community dynamics, structure, and functioning (Maron et al., 2011; van der Putten et al., 2013; van der Putten et al., 2016). PSF is characterized by host‐specific effects on soil microbial communities and soil abiotic properties, which in turn determine not only the performance of conditioning individuals but also of their neighbors and subsequent plant colonizers (Bever, 2003; Bever et al., 1997; Hannula et al., 2021; Ke & Levine, 2021). Typically, plant performance is reduced more in soils conditioned by conspecifics than by heterospecifics (Kulmatiski et al., 2008), suggesting the accumulation of host‐specific detrimental soil microbiota. Subsequently, several studies have shown that plant species that experience more negative PSF are less abundant (Kempel et al., 2018; Klironomos, 2002; Mangan et al., 2010). Furthermore, the fact that a species experiences more negative effects from self‐trained soil than from soil trained by its competitor(s) is thought to be a key mechanism for maintaining plant species coexistence (Bever, 2003; Bever et al., 1997; Crawford et al., 2019; Petermann et al., 2008; Stein & Mangan, 2020).

However, despite existing evidence that species facing more detrimental PSF effects are less abundant (Kempel et al., 2018; Klironomos, 2002; Mangan et al., 2010), many studies failed to find any association or found only weak associations between PSF and local or regional abundance (Klinerová & Dostál, 2020; Reinhart, 2012; Reinhart et al., 2021). PSF also appears to be an unreliable predictor of plant species coexistence. Beckman et al. (2023) measured performance in self‐ and heterospecific‐trained soil for each of six plant species and modeled their coexistence using pairwise PSFs, a method developed by Bever et al. (1997). When the modeled predictions were compared with the measured outcomes from the long‐term pairwise competition experiment, no agreement was found. Similarly, Chung et al. (2023) found no support for PSF as a mechanism underlying coexistence in a sagebrush steppe community. These results may indicate that the importance of PSF in determining plant species abundance and structuring plant communities may be overestimated. However, the discrepancy between predicted PSF‐driven patterns and observed patterns may be due to the approaches typically used in PSF research, which do not fully account for factors affecting PSF strength and direction (De Long et al., 2019; Ke et al., 2024).

One of the possible reasons for the weak predictive power of PSF is the use of a single vital rate in most PSF studies, that is, plant growth (e.g., Kulmatiski et al., 2008). However, plant population dynamics and the outcomes of pairwise interactions do not depend on a single vital rate, but are determined by a range of demographic rates, including seedling germination and survival, adult survival, or fecundity. However, the strength and direction of PSF effects can vary widely across different demographic vital rates (Kardol et al., 2013). Indeed, Dudenhöffer et al. (2018) showed that conspecific‐trained soil (compared to heterospecific‐trained soil) promoted juvenile biomass but reduced adult biomass and seed production in three grassland perennials (see also Aldorfová et al., 2022, who also demonstrated variable PSF effects across different life stages). Thus, estimating coexistence prospects or plant species abundance from PSF effects on isolated vital rates may lead to highly variable or even opposite results (Dostálek et al., 2022).

Another problem may be that short‐term experimental studies can underestimate the strength of PSF effects under field conditions (Adler et al., 2018; Beckman et al., 2023). It is often assumed that reciprocal effects between plants and soil microbes develop rapidly but remain constant over time. It is also assumed that host‐specific microbial effects disappear soon after the host plant dies. However, these assumptions can be oversimplified and lead to inaccurate predictions of plant population dynamics and species coexistence (Ke & Levine, 2021). As shown previously, soil microbial communities and abiotic conditions can change with the duration of the soil conditioning phase, as can the reciprocal effects on the conditioning species over time (Ke et al., 2021; Lepinay et al., 2018; Wubs & Bezemer, 2018). Specifically, Dostál et al. (2013) and Ke et al. (2021) showed that soil conditioned by conspecifics for a longer time had more negative effects on plant performance, most likely due to the accumulation of soil pathogens. Wubs and Bezemer (2018) then showed that different sequences of species used for soil conditioning modified the strength and even the direction of PSF effects on a focal species. In terms of microbial community decay following the death of conditioning plants, Hannula et al. (2021) found long‐lasting legacies of the previous species occupying the patch exemplified by the legacies of soil fungal communities. Therefore, ignoring past legacies or using soil conditioned for a short time period relative to the timescale on which demographic processes opeate in the field may be another source of inaccuracy in predicting PSF effects on outcomes of plant interactions or species abundance.

Finally, predictions of microbially mediated coexistence may differ across distinct environmental contexts. For example, Dudenhöffer et al. (2022) found that increasing moisture levels improved the performance of plants grown in soils conditioned by conspecifics more than in soils conditioned by heterospecifics, thereby reducing the coexistence potential of interacting species. Similarly, nutrient addition has repeatedly been shown to reduce (or even turn into positive) the negative effect of self‐trained soil (Klinerová & Dostál, 2020; McCarthy‐Neumann & Kobe, 2019; Petermann et al., 2008). These findings suggest that measuring PSF in an abiotic context other than the focal field system may lead to unrealistic estimates of PSF effects and thus incorrect coexistence predictions.

In this study, I examined whether predictions of microbially mediated coexistence of four plant pairs were sensitive to (1) the demographic vital rate used, (2) the history of soil conditioning, and (3) the nutrient context during the training phase (see Appendix S1: Figure S1 for the research framework of this study). For that, I measured the effects of PSF on three demographic vital rates, including germination rate, seedling survival rate, and seedling biomass. These vital rates were measured in soils with different conditioning histories, including no, past, recent, and continuous conspecific training with up to 7 years long of conspecific conditioning in control and fertilized conditions. Microbially mediated coexistence was then predicted using the framework of Kandlikar et al. (2019). The effect of the three factors (vital rate identity, conditioning history, and training nutrient context) was estimated on two key components of species coexistence (Chesson, 2000), namely stabilization (niche differences) and fitness differences. Stabilization in the context of microbially mediated coexistence refers to the effects of soil microbes that reduce the performance of their plant hosts, to a greater extent the greater its relative abundance in a plant community. There are also examples of destabilization, where soil mutualists make an abundant plant host even more abundant at the expense of its competitors. Microbially mediated fitness differences are due to interspecific differences in mean sensitivity to microbial antagonists (or mean benefits provided by mutualists), regardless of relative host abundance (Kandlikar et al., 2019; Yan et al., 2022).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study species and species pairs

In the test phase of this experiment, four long‐lived perennial species typical of Central European grasslands, two grasses (Alopecurus pratensis and Koeleria macrantha, hereafter Alopecurus and Koeleria and abbreviated as ALO and KOE) and two herbs (Plantago media and Galium mollugo and hereafter Plantago and Galium and abbreviated as PLA and GAL), were selected (Appendix S1: Table S1). Microbially mediated coexistence was then predicted for four species pairs, always including a grass and a forb species: Alopecurus–Plantago, Alopecurus–Galium, Koeleria–Plantago, and Koeleria–Galium pairs (Appendix S1: Table S1). Previous research made the current study possible, as it provided soil inocula with different training histories for the focal species and pairs (Dostál, 2023; Klinerová & Dostál, 2020; see also below).

Seeds of the study species used in the training and test phases were obtained from a local seed supplier (Planta Naturalis, Czech Republic). All experimental phases were conducted in the experimental garden of the Institute of Botany of the Czech Academy of Sciences, Průhonice, Czech Republic (322 m above sea level; 49.99° N, 14.57° E).

Soil training history

The soil inoculum used in this study originated from a long‐term soil conditioning performed in two consecutive periods for a total of almost 7 years (2015–2022; Appendix S1: Tables S1 and S2). The first training period was conducted between June 2015 and July 2019 and consisted of conspecific training of pot‐grown monocultures (Klinerová & Dostál, 2020). The second training period (July 2019–April 2022) was part of the pairwise competition experiments presented in Dostál (2023, 2025b). The interactions of the four species pairs also included in this study were studied in a nonspecific inoculum and a host‐specific inoculum, including one belonging to a target species and the other to a competitor species, in control and fertilized conditions (Dostál, 2025b). This procedure resulted in different combinations of species used for soil training in the first and second training periods, including no conspecific training to continuous conspecific training for a period of 7 years (Appendix S1: Tables S1 and S2; see also description of the test phase). Further details on soil inoculum preparation are provided in Appendix S1: Section S1.

Test phase

In the test phase, seeds of each species were added to soils with four types of conspecific training histories (Appendix S1: Tables S1 and S2): (1) “continuous” type, consisting of soil trained by conspecifics in two subsequent periods (June 2015–June 2019 and July 2019–April 2022); (2) “recent” type, when the soil was trained by conspecifics in the second period (July 2019–April 2022) but by heterospecifics in the first one; (3) “past” type, if the soil was trained by conspecifics in the first period but by heterospecifics in the second; (4) “no” type, if the soil was trained by heterospecifics in both periods.

Using Alopecurus as an example, I will illustrate the specific treatments representing the four conspecific training histories (Appendix S1: Table S2): (1) “continuous” history means that Alopecurus seeds were added to soil inoculated with an inoculum trained from 2015 to 2022 by Alopecurus (ALO‐ALO combination, indicating a conditioning species used in the first and second phases); (2) “recent” history is a setting when Alopecurus seeds were added to soil trained, for example, by a Plantago monoculture in 2015–2019 but by Alopecurus monoculture in 2019–2022 (PLA‐ALO). It also includes a case where a nonspecific inoculum (mixture of 40 species inoculum) from the first phase was used before Alopecurus monocultures were used in the second phase (indicated by a MIX‐ALO combination in Appendix S1: Table S2); (3) “past” history consists of training by an Alopecurus monoculture during 2015–2019, but by Plantago training, for example, during 2019–2022 (ALO‐PLA); (4) finally, “no” history is a setting where Alopecurus seeds were added to the soil without a conspecific training history in any of the two phases (e.g., PLA‐PLA; Appendix S1: Table S2).

Seeds were sown in 1‐L pots (20 seeds to each pot) in a steam‐sterilized 1:2 mixture of compost and sand. Prior to seed addition, 150 mL of soil inoculum harvested after the second training period was added to each pot (15% of the pot volume). Soil inoculum was with one of the four conspecific soil training histories (“continuous,” “recent,” “past,” and “no” types) and the number of replicates is shown in Appendix S1: Table S2.

The test phase experiment consisted of 752 pots, and the inoculum was obtained from 208 pots of the second training phase (from the competition experiment conducted in a study by Dostál, 2025b). One pot for each of the 208 soil sources was always left without added seeds to check for spontaneous seedling emergence of the focal species.

The test phase was conducted in the unheated greenhouse from April 21, 2022, to June 9, 2022. The first census of germinated seedlings was conducted on May 5, 2022. Further censuses were performed at weekly intervals, and the last (sixth) census was performed on June 9, 2022. On June 9, 2022, aboveground biomass of the largest seedling in a pot was harvested. It was then dried (for 2 days at 60°C) and weighed. Pots were maintained by regular spray irrigation. Emerging weeds (nonfocal species) were carefully removed from the pots throughout the test phase.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were performed with R software version 4.1.2 (R Development Core Team, 2021).

Effect of the conspecific training history on vital rates

First, I examined the germination of the focal species in unsown pots. In the case of Koeleria, Galium, and Alopecurus, zero, one, and two seedlings, respectively, were observed in unsown pots, suggesting that spontaneous germination was not an issue. In the case of Plantago, 44 seedlings were found in unsown pots, with significantly more seedlings found in pots with the “recent” inoculum history (χ2 = 24.48, p < 0.001). However, this observation was not considered further as soil training history did not have a significant effect on germination in pots with added Plantago seeds (Appendix S1: Table S4).

I then analyzed the effect of training history on three vital rates: (1) germination rate, expressed as the maximum number of seedlings recorded during six censuses divided by the number of seeds sown; (2) seedling survival, calculated as the number of seedlings at the last census divided by the maximum number of seedlings; and (3) aboveground biomass of the largest seedling. Germination and survival rates were analyzed using generalized linear mixed‐effects models with a binomial error distribution after combining the numbers of successes (number of germinating and surviving seedlings, respectively) and the number of failures into a single object (Crawley, 2007). In the case of seedling biomass (which was ln‐transformed prior to analysis), a normal error distribution was used, and the number of seedlings at the last census was included as a covariate. Inoculum type (conditioning history), nutrient addition (in the second conditioning phase), species, and their interactions were included as predictors in the mixed‐effects models (lmerTest package of R; Kuznetsova et al., 2017). Species combinations used for soil training (Appendix S1: Table S2) were used as a random term. The significance of the fixed effects was then tested using the likelihood ratio test. When the effect of inoculum or species was significant, multiple pairwise comparisons were estimated using the emmeans package (Lenth, 2019). Analyses were then performed for individual species with inoculum type and nutrient addition as predictors.

Effects of vital rate, training history, and environmental context on species coexistence

The PSF framework of species coexistence was developed by James Bever and was summarized in Bever (2003) and Bever et al. (1997). It assumes that the outcome of pairwise species interactions can be predicted by the reciprocal effects of microbial communities they cultivate. Specifically, each species is expected to cultivate host‐specific microbiota and alter the microbial community to a greater extent as its host plant species becomes more frequent in a community. Coexistence is possible when a species experiences more negative effects from self‐trained microbiota than from the microbiota trained by its competitor (Bever, 2003; Bever et al., 1997), that is, when plant–microbe interactions generate a stabilizing effect on the plant community.

However, James Bever's approach did not take into account the fact that interacting species may differ in their average sensitivity to the effects of the soil microbes they cultivate, independent of plant host frequencies. For example, although both interacting species can be stabilized by their pathogens, the intrinsic growth rate of one species may be reduced in the presence of host‐specific soil microbes to a greater extent than the growth rate of the other species (Kandlikar et al., 2019).

Therefore, Kandlikar et al. (2019) developed a method that follows the framework of modern coexistence theory (Chesson, 2000). Specifically, it predicts species coexistence based not only on the basis of microbially mediated stabilization (and is analogous to an earlier model developed by Bever et al., 1997) but also on the basis of microbially mediated fitness differences. Coexistence is then possible when stabilization is greater than fitness differences (Chesson, 2000; Kandlikar et al., 2019). The model developed by Kandlikar et al. (2019, see also Yan et al., 2022) was also used in this study.

Specifically, microbially mediated stabilization was calculated for each of the four pairs (Appendix S1: Table S1) using Equation (1): (1) Stabilization=−12lnP1A−lnP1B−lnP2A+lnP2B

Fitness differences were calculated using Equation (2): (2) Fitness differences=12lnP1A+lnP1B−lnP2A−lnP2B−lnP1R+lnP2R

The variance of stabilization and fitness differences were calculated according to Yan et al. (2022) using Equation (3) and Equation (4), respectively: (3) σ2Stabilization=14σ1A2P1A2N1A+σ1B2P1B2N1B+σ2A2P2A2N2A+σ2B2P2B2N2B (4) σ2Fitness differences=14σ1A2P1A2N1A+σ1B2P1B2N1B+σ2A2P2A2N2A+σ2B2P2B2N2B+4σ1R2P1R2N1R+4σ2R2P2R2N2R where P 1A and P 2B are the performances of species 1 and species 2, respectively, in their own soil. P 1B and P 2A are the performances of species 1 and species 2, respectively, in soil conditioned by the other species of a pair. P 1R and P 2R are the performances of species 1 and species 2 in soil conditioned by a species different from species 1 and 2 (Kandlikar et al., 2019; Yan et al., 2022). σiK2 and NiK are the variance and sample size used to obtain mean plant performance in the respective soil environment. The fitness differences for each species pair were always positive, as I set species 1 as the fitness superior (following an approach of Yan et al., 2022).

I used three measures of plant performance P (i.e., three vital rates used separately in the models) to calculate stabilization and fitness differences: (1) germination rate, (2) seedling survival rate, and (3) aboveground seedling biomass. Finally, I modeled coexistence with soil microbial effects integrated across the three vital rates. To accomplish this, I employed an approach developed by Chung et al. (2023). This approach models the total biomass expected from an initial 100 seeds given the stage‐specific effects of the soil environment on germination, seedling survival, and surviving plant biomass. First, I obtained bootstrapped samples of each vital rate for each species and soil training history separately (by random resampling of the data with replacement; N = 6 per sample). Then, I calculated the mean germination rate, survival probability, and seedling biomass for the bootstrapped sample, and their product (and multiplied by 100, representing the initial 100 seeds). This procedure was repeated 5000 times. During each run, stabilization and fitness differences were calculated according to Equations (1) and (2), respectively. Finally, I calculated the mean and 95% CIs (from 5000 estimates) of stabilization and fitness differences, and used them to predict coexistence.

I estimated the microbial effects on the coexistence of four species pairs (Alopecurus–Plantago, Alopecurus–Galium, Koeleria–Plantago, and Koeleria–Galium; Appendix S1: Table S1). For each species pair, I used the species performance in three combination types of conspecific training history, denoted as “continuous,” “recent_G,” and “recent_S” (Appendix S1: Table S1). In the case of the “continuous” type, I used species performance in inoculum trained by conspecifics (and in inoculum trained by a competitor species) in both training phases (2015–2019, 2019–2022). Thus, in the case of an Alopecurus–Plantago pair, I used Alopecurus and Plantago performance in the ALO‐ALO and PLA‐PLA inoculum types shown in Appendix S1: Table S2. For the “recent_G” type, self‐trained inoculum was obtained by using conspecifics in the second training phase, preceded by a preparation of nonspecific (generalist) inoculum during 2015–2019 (mixture of 40 species inoculum containing none of the focal species). For an Alopecurus–Plantago pair, the “recent_G” type was represented by the MIX‐ALO and MIX‐PLA inocula shown in Appendix S1: Table S2. Finally, the “recent_S” type was prepared by self‐conditioning in the second training phase, preceded by soil training by a competitor species during 2015–2019 (for an Alopecurus–Plantago pair, PLA‐ALO and ALO‐PLA inocula were used; Appendix S1: Table S2). Thus, all three combination types were identical in soil conditioning during the second training phase (2019–2022), but differed in the species identity used during the first conditioning phase (2015–2019) and also in the duration of conspecific conditioning. For the calculation of fitness differences, performance in soil not trained by either species of a pair was used (in MIX‐OTHER inoculum type; Appendix S1: Table S2).

All calculations were performed separately for control and nutrient‐treated pots in the second training phase (Appendix S1: Table S1). I also accounted for uncertainty in the stabilization and fitness differences by using a 95% CI.

Finally, I analyzed the effects of vital rate identity (germination, seedling survival, and biomass), soil training history, and nutrient addition (in the second training phase) and their interactions on the proportion of species pairs that can coexist, stabilization, and fitness differences (ln‐transformed). I used (generalized) linear mixed models with binomial (coexistence) and normal (stabilization, fitness differences) error distributions. A species pair identity was included as a random term (lmerTest package of R; Kuznetsova et al., 2017). The significance of the fixed effects was tested using the likelihood ratio test.

RESULTS

Effect of soil training history on vital rates

The highest germination rate was observed in the “continuous” inoculum type, that is, inoculum trained by conspecifics for 7 years (Figure 1). However, the inoculum effect was only marginally significant (χ2 = 6.622, p = 0.085; Appendix S1: Table S3) and was not observed in all species: the most positive effect of the “continuous” type was found in Alopecurus, where it increased the germination rate by almost 10% compared to the other inoculum types (Appendix S1: Figure S2, Table S4).

Vital rates averaged over the four focal species (germination and seedling survival rates, and aboveground biomass) in inoculum with different conspecific training histories. Effects are shown separately for inoculum treated (+Nutrients; left column) or not treated (−Nutrients; right column) by nutrient addition in the second training phase (2019–2022).

Seedling survival was most reduced in the “recent” type (conspecific training 2019–2022), which was on average 6% lower than the “no” type (heterospecific training; χ2 = 13.34, p = 0.004; Figure 1, Appendix S1: Table S3). More seedlings also survived in the inoculum exposed to nutrient addition during the second training phase (Appendix S1: Table S3; χ2 = 3.866, p = 0.049). The effect of inoculum and nutrients was species‐specific, yielding a significant three‐way interaction (Appendix S1: Table S3).

The lowest aboveground biomass was found in pots with the “continuous” inoculum type (Figure 1): Compared to heterospecific training (“no” type), the “continuous” type reduced biomass by 25% on average (χ2 = 11.369, p = 0.010). The observed pattern was mainly driven by a significant inoculum effect found in grass species (Alopecurus and Koeleria), while such an effect was absent in forbs (Galium and Plantago; Appendix S1: Table S4). Nutrient addition in the second training phase significantly reduced seedling biomass in the test phase by an average of 12% (Figure 1, Appendix S1: Tables S3 and S4).

Effects of vital rate, training history, and environmental context on species coexistence

Predictions of microbially mediated coexistence (i.e., when stabilization is greater than fitness differences) were influenced by the identity of the vital rate: While all four species pairs were predicted to coexist when based on aboveground biomass and seedling survival, only two pairs were likely to coexist when based on germination and on an integrated variable (Figure 2). In terms of the number of combinations, coexistence was possible in 13 combinations when based on biomass, 8 combinations when based on survival, 4 combinations when based on an integrated variable, and 3 combinations when based on germination only (out of a total of 24 combinations: 4 species pairs × 2 environments × 3 types of training histories; Figure 2).

Whether species in a pair can coexist is determined by microbial effects on stabilization and fitness differences (means and 95% CIs are shown), based on germination rate (a), seedling survival rate (b), aboveground seedling biomass (c), and integrated over all three vital rates (d). Outcomes are shown separately for three inoculum types with different training histories: the largest, medium, and smallest symbols represent the “continuous,” “recent_G,” and “recent_S” types, respectively (see Appendix S1: Table S1 and Materials and methods for details). Furthermore, results are shown separately for inoculum treated or not treated by nutrient addition in the second training phase (+Nutrients, −Nutrients). Three domains are distinguished in the plots: coexistence (stabilization > 0, and stabilization > fitness differences), exclusion (abs(stabilization) < fitness differences), and priority effects (stabilization < 0, and abs(stabilization) > fitness differences). Note that the axis scales are different in each panel. Also note that predicting coexistence based on an integrated variable (d) is more uncertain due to the bootstrapping procedure.

Differences in coexistence predictions due to different vital rates were associated with differences in stabilization. In particular, stabilization was weakest and even negative for germination (mean = −0.046). This contrasted with a large and positive stabilization (0.20) for biomass (Figure 3, Appendix S1: Table S5). Since the fitness differences were similar for germination and biomass (0.21 vs. 0.25; Figure 3, Appendix S1: Table S5), the different coexistence predictions between the two vital rates were mainly due to the different strength of stabilization.

Mean values (±SEs) of stabilization, fitness differences, and proportion of coexisting pairs based on germination rate (a–c), seedling survival rate (d–f), aboveground biomass (g–i), and all three vital rates (j–l). Values are shown separately for the three inoculum types with different training histories (“continuous,” “recent_G,” and “recent_S” type; see Appendix S1: Table S1 and Materials and methods for details) and for the inoculum treated or not treated by nutrient addition in the second training phase (+Nutrients, −Nutrients). Note that the axis scales are different in each panel.

Coexistence was also influenced by soil training history, although its effect was marginally significant (χ2 = 5.536, p = 0.063; Appendix S1: Table S5): While coexistence was found in 10 combinations in the “continuous” and “recent_G” types (out of 24 combinations: 4 species pairs × 2 environments × 3 vital rates), it was found in only 4 combinations in the “recent_S” type (Figures 2 and 3). These differences were due to the weakest stabilization found for the “recent_S” type. Specifically, the stabilization corresponded to a mean value of 0.09 and 0.10 in the “continuous” and “recent_G” histories, respectively, but was an order of magnitude smaller (0.009) in the “recent_S” type (Appendix S1: Table S5).

Although nutrient addition in the training phase did not have a significant effect on coexistence predictions, it did have an effect on stabilization. Specifically, it increased stabilization in the case of biomass (0.14 vs. 0.27), but reduced it in germination (−0.02 vs. −0.07) and seedling survival (0.06 vs. 0.02), resulting in a significant interaction of nutrient addition and a vital rate (χ2 = 8.031, p = 0.018; Figure 3, Appendix S1: Table S5).

DISCUSSION

Effect of soil training history on vital rates

In this study, I showed that PSF effects varied in strength and even direction across different demographic vital rates. While seed germination tended to be higher in conspecific versus heterospecific‐trained soil, seedling survival and seedling biomass were lower in self‐trained soil. That PSF effects can change during ontogeny was suggested in a review by Kardol et al. (2013) and demonstrated in several previous studies (Aldorfová et al., 2022; Dostálek et al., 2022; Dudenhöffer et al., 2018), indicating a more general pattern.

The mechanisms underlying these differences between PSF effects on juvenile and later stages remain unclear. However, it is hypothesized that they may be due to changes in microbial communities during the response (test) phase, or to ontogeny‐dependent plant physiology or chemistry that make plants more or less sensitive to soil microbial effects (Dudenhöffer et al., 2018). Understanding these mechanisms remains to be explored in future research. Future research should also investigate whether germination is indeed more enhanced by self‐trained soil compared to non‐self‐conditioned soil, as our current understanding is largely limited to the effects of PSF on plant growth (Kardol et al., 2013; Kulmatiski et al., 2008).

I also found that the effects of PSF on vital rates were influenced by the length of the soil conditioning phase. In fact, the effect of conspecific training was significantly different from heterospecific training only when it lasted 7 years (Appendix S1: Table S3; in the case of germination rate and seedling biomass analyzed across species). Demographic consequences of the duration of the conditioning phase have been repeatedly shown in previous research (Dostál et al., 2013; Ke et al., 2021; Lepinay et al., 2018; Wubs & Bezemer, 2018). However, how soil biotic and abiotic characteristics change with prolonged conspecific and heterospecific conditioning remains less studied. Existing evidence suggests that dynamic PSF effects are due to time‐dependent effects of self‐ versus non‐self‐training on soil chemical properties (Lepinay et al., 2018). Another explanation is related to changes in the soil microbial community (Ke et al., 2021), although evidence of time‐dependent demographic effects of PSF, which would be accompanied by the knowledge of population dynamics of host‐specific pathogen(s), is surprisingly lacking. Here, I used a 7‐year period for soil conditioning, which is a relatively long period in PSF research. According to Ke et al. (2024), the median conditioning period used in 59 experimental studies included in the meta‐analysis of Crawford et al. (2019) and Yan et al. (2022) was 3.5 months. However, exploring the demographic consequences of PSF based on such short‐term conditioning can be insufficient in some plant community types.

A final aspect considered was nutrient treatment in the second phase of the training period. When analyzed across species, previous nutrient additions increased seedling survival but reduced seedling biomass in the test phase. I also found that added nutrients did not interact with inoculum type, that is, the effects of inocula with different conditioning histories were similarly modulated by fertilization (Appendix S1: Table S3). Nutrient availability is considered a factor with strong potential to alter the strength or even direction of PSF effects (De Long et al., 2019; Lekberg et al., 2018), but whether PSF becomes more negative (as nutrient‐rich environments are thought to favor soil pathogens over mutualists) or more positive (due to improved plant immunity and defense) in more fertile environments is still a subject of ongoing debate (Johnson et al., 1997; Smith‐Ramesh & Reynolds, 2017; van der Putten et al., 2016). Most experimental evidence suggests that nutrient addition attenuates the negative effects of PSF on plant growth (Klinerová & Dostál, 2020; McCarthy‐Neumann & Kobe, 2019; Petermann et al., 2008), although a thorough meta‐analytic study would be required.

Variation in outcomes of microbially mediated interactions

The fact that PSF effects varied at different vital rates was also reflected in different coexistence predictions. That self‐trained soil improved germination also led to destabilization and a prediction that only two species pairs could coexist. This was in contrast to outcomes using seedling survival and biomass, which were characterized by negative effects of self‐trained soil and also greater stabilization, especially in the case of seedling biomass. As a result, coexistence was predicted for all four species pairs in the two latter vital rates and was also more common across combinations of the factors studied.

Although coexistence predictions were not directly influenced by soil training history, it did influence stabilization. Specifically, longer (7‐year long) conspecific conditioning increased stabilization compared to shorter (3‐year long) conspecific conditioning preceded by a competitor's training. Interestingly, this effect was absent when shorter conspecific conditioning was performed on soil trained previously by a mixture of species (Figure 3). This finding suggests that it is not only the duration of soil conditioning but also the sequences of species used in this process that determine the effect of soil training history on coexistence predictions (Ke et al., 2024; Wubs & Bezemer, 2018).

A key question is which combination of the three factors (vital rate, conditioning history, and nutrient context) is a setting that provides more realistic and accurate predictions of coexistence. Unlike Beckman et al. (2023) or Chung et al. (2023) who had long‐term field data on observed outcomes for interacting species, no such information was available for the species included in this study. However, the coexistence potential of some of the study pairs can be inferred from database records or large grassland vegetation surveys (Busch et al., 2019; Chytrý & Rafajová, 2003). For example, A. pratensis and G. mollugo are the two species typical of productive habitats that are often observed growing together, suggesting that they can indeed coexist. This observation was further supported by the previous study which predicted coexistence of the two species based on resource competition (Dostál, 2023). In the present study, coexistence of the Alopecurus–Galium pair was predicted when based on PSF effects on biomass or seedling survival (although not in all training histories and training nutrient contexts). However, when predictions were based on germination, the two species were unlikely to coexist (Figure 2). This suggests that using germination alone to predict microbially mediated coexistence can poorly inform on whether interacting species can persist in the field.

Chung et al. (2023) and Dudenhöffer et al. (2018) recommended that more realistic and accurate coexistence estimates can be obtained by integrating PSF effects across multiple demographic rates and, if feasible, including PSF effects on seed production (Dudenhöffer et al., 2018). This recommendation contrasts with the most commonly used method, which only examines PSF effects on plant growth. It is likely that this typical approach can predict coexistence well in systems composed of perennial plants with dominant vegetative (clonal) reproduction, but less well in systems where seedling recruitment contributes significantly and frequently to population dynamics, such as annual grasslands. Given the positive and negative effects of self‐trained soil on germination and biomass, respectively, as found in this study, ignoring the former vital rate may potentially overestimate the coexistence potential of interacting species. It was also demonstrated here as all four species pairs were predicted to coexist when based on biomass, but only two pairs could do so when an integrated variable was used (Figure 2).

The most rigorous approach to addressing the microbial impact on the different life stages is likely the one proposed in a recent study by Ke et al. (2024). They modified the plant population dynamics model of Uricchio et al. (2019), which describes the competition between an exotic annual plant and a native perennial plant. Ke et al. (2024) used this demographically explicit model to simulate microbial effects integrated across five different life stages (of a perennial plant), and to identify the life stage with the strongest effect on population dynamics when impacted by the microbes. They found that the relative abundance of a perennial species is strongly reduced (from 75% to almost zero) when microbes affect all five demographic parameters considered simultaneously, but this effect was largely driven by a microbial impact on a single transition (adult survival). Ke et al. (2024) acknowledge that estimating all demographic parameters together with information on how they are influenced by soil microbes can be difficult, but may improve the realism of models exploring demographically complex systems. A possible simplification lies in sensitivity analysis, that is, identifying the most critical parameter(s) (such as adult survival in the simulation of Ke et al., 2024) that should be prioritized in the assessment of microbial effects.

Identifying the importance of PSF for plant species coexistence

Identifying the key mechanisms that determine the outcome of plant interactions and community structure is an ongoing task in plant community ecology research (HilleRisLambers et al., 2012; Levine & HilleRisLambers, 2009). These efforts are particularly important in the era of anthropogenic drivers such as exotic plant invasions, climate change, or eutrophication affecting native biodiversity (Dostál, 2023; Godoy & Levine, 2014; Van Dyke et al., 2022). Interestingly, many studies focus on microbially mediated interactions as the sole mechanism determining whether plant competitors can coexist or not (Beckman et al., 2023; Chung et al., 2023; Crawford et al., 2019; Yan et al., 2022). Some recent studies have begun to explore how human drivers, such as changes in precipitation patterns, alter PSF effects and thus interaction outcomes (Dudenhöffer et al., 2022). However, most of these studies estimated predicted outcomes based on the method of Bever et al. (1997) or the more recent method developed by Kandlikar et al. (2019) without reference to field‐observed coexistence patterns. Studies by Beckman et al. (2023) and Chung et al. (2023) are rare exceptions, as they compared predicted PSF‐based outcomes with observed outcomes based on long‐term field observations or experiments. However, the PSF was found to be a poor predictor of pairwise species coexistence in these two studies.

As discussed by Beckman et al. (2023) and several previous studies (e.g., Adler et al., 2010), there may be several mechanisms that determine plant species interactions and coexistence. Besides PSF, it may be spatial or temporal heterogeneity and differences between species in their demographic responses to this environmental variation that help maintain species coexistence and community diversity (Angert et al., 2009). However, it is probably resource competition and the fact that different species are limited by different abiotic resources (Tilman, 1982) that most often explain niche partitioning among coexisting species. Researchers are aware that these mechanisms do not operate in isolation, but that several of them may simultaneously determine the outcomes of plant interactions. Specifically, in the case of PSF and resource competition, there is a call for a more complex design of future studies, which would consist of studying species interactions in uncultivated soils and soils cultivated by competing species (Crawford & Knight, 2017; Kandlikar et al., 2019; Ke & Wan, 2020; Lekberg et al., 2018). Such a design would allow quantification of the contribution of PSF to the coexistence of competing plants, specifically how PSF alters intrinsic growth rate and the strength of intra‐ and interspecific competition, and thus stabilization and fitness differences (Kandlikar, 2024).

The question remains whether microbially mediated interactions alone can be a reliable predictor of coexistence of the species studied in this study, or whether resource competition should also be considered. Resource‐based interactions could be ignored if interacting species were equal competitors, that is, if they did not differ significantly in intrinsic growth rate and the strength of intraspecific and interspecific competition in the absence of soil microbes. Although these parameters have not been previously measured in sterile soil, the focal species did differ, namely in the strength of intraspecific competition (between fast‐ and slow‐growing plants cultivated in soil with nonspecific inoculum; Dostál, 2023). Second, resource competition could be ignored if host‐specific inocula did not alter the strength of competitive interactions. In the species pairs studied, this assumption does not hold, as interspecific competition was less intense when interactions were measured in the inoculum of a competitor (Dostál, 2025b). Therefore, coexistence of the species included in the study is likely to be better predicted by the combined effects of resource competition and microbially mediated interactions, rather than by their isolated effects.

CONCLUSION

In this study, I showed that the effects of conspecific‐trained soil on plant performance differed across vital rates and were also related to the history and nutrient context of the conditioning phase. I then showed how these differences led to variable outcomes of microbially mediated interactions. These results suggest that estimates of PSF effects on coexistence and abundance patterns may be inaccurate if derived solely from the effects of short‐term soil conditioning on plant growth. Future research should focus on the role of PSF relative to other mechanisms for maintaining plant species coexistence. In this effort, predictions based on experimental approaches should be confronted with existing long‐term observations of plant communities.
