Co-pyrolysis development of waste tire-sludge adsorbent by mixed of waste tires and oily sludge
Authors: Chao Tang, Jiaojiao Guan, Shuixiang Xie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo facilitate resource utilization of waste tires (WT) and oily sludge (OS), waste tire-sludge adsorbent (WTSA) was developed using co-pyrolysis technology, and its effectiveness in adsorbing crude oil was investigated. The study revealed that the optimal preparation conditions for WTSA included a 1.5:1 mass ratio of WT to OS, a co-pyrolysis temperature of 600 ℃, a co-pyrolysis holding time of 2 hours, and a co-pyrolysis heating rate of 15 ℃/min. The surface of WTSA exhibited numerous pores and cracks with varying shapes and sizes. The dominant pore structures were found to be mesopores and macropores. The carbon content of WTSA was measured to be 89.95%. Moreover, the BET specific surface area, pore volume, and average pore size were determined to be 686.81 m2/g, 0.74 cm3/g, and 5.91 nm, respectively. In the crude oil adsorption test, WTSA demonstrated a comparable adsorption capacity to activated carbon (AC), but with a more attractive initial adsorption rate. Furthermore, thermal regeneration treatment was found to significantly enhance the lipophilic properties of WTSA, leading to an increase in its initial adsorption rate. The adsorption capacity of regenerated WTSA was also found to be relatively stable, making it an ideal solution for emergency crude oil spill cleanups. Compared to AC, WTSA can be recycled and reused multiple times, making it a more sustainable and cost-effective option.Subject terms: Environmental sciences, Energy science and technology

Introduction

Tires are an essential component of countless vehicles worldwide, ensuring smooth rides and safe journeys. However, to meet the global demand, the mind-boggling number of tires sold each year. It’s estimated that approximately 1.6 billion tires are sold and 1 billion are discarded worldwide each year. The durability of these tires means that when they are either dumped or stacked, they significantly waste land resources, pose fire hazards, and contribute to environmental pollution. Consequently, finding effective methods for disposal of waste tires (WT) has become a key focal point for national governments and enterprises alike1,2. Pyrolysis is a thermal decomposition reaction that occurs in the absence of oxygen. While pyrolysis is an effective treatment mechanism for sewage sludge, biomass, and municipal solid waste3–5, it is particularly well-suited for WT due to its ability to effectively separate primarily carbon black-derived carbon (which produces char after pyrolysis) from the rubber and textile-derived carbon compounds (which produce oil and gas). This enables more effective recovery of both product streams. The resulting carbon can be used for a variety of applications, including activated carbons (ACs), battery anodes, and catalysts6–13.

Oily sludge (OS) is a hazardous waste that commonly generated in the petroleum industry. In China alone, over 3 million tons of OS are produced annually from various stages of crude oil exploration, production, transportation, storage, and refining. Furthermore, globally, the production of OS exceeds 60 million tons per year, and the accumulated amount of OS has surpassed 1 billion tons14–16. Despite being a hazardous waste, OS contains valuable hydrocarbon components such as saturates, aromatics, asphaltenes, and resins. Therefore, pyrolysis is a promising method to convert OS into useful products such as fuels, char, and ACs17,18.

Recently, there has been a growing interest in co-pyrolysis of OS and organic materials due to its remarkable properties. In a study conducted by Xue et al., they utilized OS and corn stover as feedstock for co-pyrolysis, resulting in production of sludge-based biochar. The biochar exhibited a significant presence of large pore structures, predominantly mesoporous in nature. Moreover, the surface of the biochar was found to contain various functional groups such as –CH, C=O, –OH, and C=C. The specific surface area of the biochar was measured to be 71.8069 m2/g, and it displayed an impressive maximum adsorption capacity of 99.32 mg/L for petroleum pollutants present in oily wastewater 19. Wang et al. produced aqueous phase sulfamethoxazole (SMZ) adsorption materials from mixtures of different biomass materials (walnut shell, coconut shell or cottonwood sawdust) and carbon-enriched OS adopted co-pyrolysis technology. The maximum BET surface area was 1342 m2/g for the ACs prepared from the mixture of walnut shell and OS by one-step activation and it had the maximum SMZ adsorption capacity up to 361.9 mg/g20. Hu et al. studied the co-pyrolysis of OS and waste wood chips, Which found that the higher the content of wood chips, the higher the yield of oil and coke after co-pyrolysis, and the H/C ratio in the oil produced by co-pyrolysis was higher21. Lv et al. conducted a study on co-pyrolysis of OS and WT, which revealed a synergistic effect between the two materials during the process. The study found that the maximum pyrolysis gas was obtained when the mass fraction of WT was 50%. Additionally, the co-pyrolysis of both materials resulted in an improvement in the quality of the pyrolysis oil22.

Based on the characteristics of WT and OS, this study utilized co-pyrolysis technology to develop waste tyre-sludge adsorbent (WTSA), and subsequently investigated the adsorption of crude oil by WTSA. This approach effectively achieved the resource utilization of two types of solid waste, aligning with the principles of ecological environmental protection and high-quality economic and social development.

Materials and methods

Materials

WT used in this study were sourced from car tires after removal of steel wire. Prior to the experiment, they were washed, dried, and crushed to a 10-mesh size. The elemental composition of WT revealed mass fractions of C, H, O, N, and S at 68.85, 5.18, 24.17, 0.58, and 1.22% respectively. OS was obtained from an oilfield sewage treatment and was characterized as black and viscous, with water, oil, and solid content of 81.4, 9.8, and 8.8% respectively. Activated carbon (AC) used in the experiment was sourced from Dongguan Hongsheng ACs Co., Ltd., with water and ash content not exceeding 10 and 5% respectively. The crude oil used in the study was obtained from Jingzhou Tianhe Technology Company, it was described as black with good liquidity.

Preparation of WTSA

WT and OS were thoroughly mixed in a specific mass ratio and introduced into a pyrolysis equipment as shown in (Fig. 1) for co-pyrolysis. The co-pyrolysis process was carried out under nitrogen atmosphere. Once the co-pyrolysis was completed, the resulting solid product was subjected to a washing process until all ash and oxide residues were completely dissolved. The washed product was then dried and finely ground using a 50-mesh sieve, resulting in the formation of WTSA. In the process of prepaing WTSA, the key influencing factors include the mass ratio of WT to OS, co-pyrolysis temperature, co-pyrolysis heating rate, and co-pyrolysis holding time23,24. In order to obtain the best conditions for WTSA preparation, the key influencing factors of WTSA preparation were analyzed by combining orthogonal test and single-factor test.

Analysis and test methods

Elemental analysis of the samples was conducted using Quantax 200XFlash5000-10 EDS. Surface characterization and SEM analysis were performed using N2 adsorption ASAP and Quanta250 tungsten filament scanning electron microscope. Determination of iodine number was carried out in accordance with the China’s national standard “Test methods of activated carbon - Determinaton of iodine number” (GB/T 12496.8-2015). The method summary was as follows: a certain amount of adsorbent sample and iodine solution were adsorbed by oscillation, filtered, the filtrate was taken, and the amount of residual iodine in the filtrate was titrated with sodium thiosulfate solution. The amount of iodine adsorbed per gram of adsorbent under the remaining iodine concentration of 0.02 mol/L was taken as the iodine number.

Method of adsorbing crude oil

Based on the test method for dynamic adsorption of ACs25, plexiglass adsorption columns with scale lines should be designed and fabricated to facilitate the adsorption test of crude oil by adsorbents. To maintain a constant temperature of 28 ℃ throughout the test process, the entire adsorption process was conducted within a temperature-controlled water bath. The test device was shown in (Fig. 2), During the test, 200 g of adsorbent was added to the adsorption column, followed by the quick injection of 400 mL of crude oil. Time recording began immediately upon injection of crude oil, with corresponding measurements of downward scale of crude oil taken at regular intervals. The time of the first drop of crude oil flowing out of the adsorption column was recorded accurately, along with the corresponding falling scale of crude oil. Through volume-mass conversion, the cumulative adsorption capacity of adsorbent to crude oil at different time points from the initial adsorption to the first drop of crude oil flowing out of adsorption column was obtained, and then the adsorption rate of adsorbent at the corresponding time was calculated. The bulk density of WTSA and AC was 0.48 and 0.42 g/mL, respectively.

Crude oil adsorption device: (a) schematic diagram; (b) experimental drawings. 1-Thermostatic water tank, 2-Circulating pumps, 3-Crude oil, 4-Adsorbent, 5-Tick marks, 6-Collection bottle.

Regeneration method of adsorbent

The adsorbent that had adsorbed crude oil was subjected to pyrolysis in a pyrolysis furnace at 550 ℃ for 1 h. Upon cooling, the regenerated adsorbent was obtained.

Results and discussion

Orthogonal test of WTSA preparation

Thermogravimetric analysis can effectively determine the temperature range of co-pyrolysis of WT and OS. According to relevant studies26–28, WT and OS usually complete the pyrolysis process at around 550–650 and 500–600 ℃ respectively. Therefore, in orthogonal test for preparation of WTSA, it is appropriate to set the temperature range of WT and OS co-pyrolysis to 450–650 ℃. In this study, an orthogonal test of 3 levels and 4 factor L9 (34) was designed, the adsorption properties of WTSA were evaluated using iodine number under varying influencing factors. The levels of orthogonal test factors and orthogonal test results are presented in Tables 1 , 2, respectively. The results indicated that in preparation of WTSA, co-pyrolysis temperature emerged as the most significant influencing factor, followed by co-pyrolysis holding time and the mass ratio of WT to OS, with co-pyrolysis heating rate being the least influential. Based on analysis of the orthogonal test results, it is evident that the optimal levels for four influencing factors are A2, B2, C3 and D2. Hence, the ideal conditions for preparation of WTSA, as determined from the orthogonal test are as follows: a mass ratio of WT to OS of 1:1, co-pyrolysis temperature of 550 ℃, co-pyrolysis heating rate of 10 ℃/min and co-pyrolysis holding time of 2 h.

Single-factor test of WTSA preparation

The three primary factors (mass ratio of WT to OS, co-pyrolysis temperature and co-pyrolysis holding time) were tested as single-factor in order to gain a deeper understanding of their effects on adsorption performance of WTSA.

Mass ratio of WT to OS

To investigate the influence of the mass ratio of WT to OS on iodine number of prepared adsorbent, experiments were conducted under the following conditions: co-pyrolysis temperature of 550 ℃, co-pyrolysis heating rate of 15 ℃/min, and co-pyrolysis holding time of 2 h. The results as shown in Fig. 3 indicated that the prepared adsorbent exhibited the highest iodine number when the mass ratio of WT to OS was 1.5:1. This can be attributed to the synergistic effect of co-pyrolysis process after mixing WT and OS, which facilitated the co-pyrolysis reaction and enhanced the full release of volatiles. Moreover, there exists an optimal ratio of WT to OS for co-pyrolysis synergistic promotion22,29. Based on the findings of this study, the most effective WT and OS co-pyrolysis synergistic promotion ratio was 1.5:1.

Impact of mass ratio of WT to OS on iodine number of prepared adsorbent.

Co-pyrolysis temperature

When the mass ratio of WT to OS was 1.5:1, with co-pyrolysis heating rate of 15 ℃/min and co-pyrolysis holding time of 2 h, the study investigated the impact of co-pyrolysis temperature on iodine number of prepared adsorbent. The results as shown in Fig. 4 indicated that at lower co-pyrolysis temperature, the reaction was not sufficient. This led to incomplete decomposition of the main components in WT and OS, resulting in the inability to form an effective pore structure30. Consequently, the iodine number of prepared adsorbent was low. On the other hand, at excessively high co-pyrolysis temperature, the pores of prepared adsorbent were reconstructed, leading to the collapse and blockage of microporous walls. This caused an increase in proportion of mesopores and macropores31, resulting in a decrease in iodine number. Therefore, the suitable co-pyrolysis temperature for preparation of adsorbent was found to be 600 ℃.

Impact of co-pyrolysis temperature on iodine number of prepared adsorbent.

Co-pyrolysis holding time

To investigate the impact of co-pyrolysis holding time on iodine number of prepared adsorbent, a specific set of conditions was defined. These conditions comprised a mass ratio of WT to OS at 1.5:1, co-pyrolysis heating rate of 15 ℃/min, and co-pyrolysis temperature of 600 ℃. The findings presented in Fig. 5 indicate that the ideal co-pyrolysis holding time for adsorbent preparation was 2 h. If the co-pyrolysis holding time was too brief, the reaction would not be sufficient, the pore expansion would be inadequate, and the resulting pores would not be connected internally and externally, leading to a low iodine number. If the co-pyrolysis holding time was excessively long, the reaction would become excessive, leading to a reorganization of the pore structure and an expansion in proportion of mesopores and macropores31, consequently resulting in a decrease in iodine number.

Impact of co-pyrolysis holding time on iodine number of prepared adsorbent.

Characterization of WTSA

WTSA was prepared under the optimal conditions, which included a mass ratio of WT to OS of 1.5:1, co-pyrolysis temperature of 600 ℃, co-pyrolysis heating rate of 15 ℃/min and co-pyrolysis holding time of 2 h. The prepared WTSA was then characterized and AC was used for comparative study. Table 3 presented the element composition and surface characteristics data of WTSA and AC. The data indicated that the mass fraction of carbon in WTSA was very high, reaching approximately 90%. Oxygen and sulfur were the next most abundant elements in WTSA. In terms of elemental composition, WTSA was very similar to AC. For the surface characteristics, the BET specific surface area of WTSA was significantly lower than that of AC. However, the pore volume and average pore size of WTSA were higher than those of AC, indicating that the pore structure of WTSA is mainly mesoporous and macroporous, while AC is dominated by micropores, with relatively small pore volume and average pore size32,33. Figure 6 showed the SEM images of WTSA and AC. The SEM image of WTSA revealed tightly packed particles with a relatively messy surface, containing numerous pores and cracks of various shapes and sizes. In contrast, the surface of AC appeared smooth, with a tighter texture and smaller, more uniformly distributed pores.

Adsorption of crude oil by WTSA

To explore the potential applications of WTSA, we conducted a study on adsorption performance of WTSA on crude oil, with a comparison to AC of 50 mesh sieve.

Adsorption rate

Figure 7 illustrated the adsorption rates of WTSA, AC, and its regenerated agent on crude oil over time. The results indicated that under the same test conditions, within the first minute, the adsorption rates of WTSA and AC on crude oil were 47.38 and 29.16 g/min, respectively. As the adsorption time increased, both WTSA and AC exhibited a significant decrease in the adsorption rate of crude oil. However, throughout the entire adsorption process, the adsorption rate of WTSA remained consistently higher than that of AC. Furthermore, it was worth noting that the WTSA regenerator consistently outperformed the AC regenerator in terms of the adsorption rate of crude oil. This observation suggested that WTSA had the ability to adsorb crude oil at a faster rate compared to AC. Consequently, if WTSA is employed to mitigate the spread of crude oil resulting from accidental leakage, its emergency adsorption effectiveness would be significantly stronger than that of AC. Moreover, it was worth mentioning that the WTSA regenerator exhibited a faster initial adsorption rate of crude oil compared to WTSA alone. As the number of regenerations increased, the initial adsorption rate also increased. This can be attributed to the fact that after WTSA adsorbs crude oil, it undergoes thermal regeneration which enhances its surface lipophilicity. As a result, it is able to exhibit faster adsorption of crude oil through strong capillary action, thereby improving the initial adsorption rate.

Adsorption rate on crude oil of WTSA, AC and its regenerated agent.

Cumulative adsorption capacity

Figure 8 illustrated the cumulative adsorption capacity of crude oil over time for WTSA, AC, and their regenerated agent. The results indicated that under identical test conditions, WTSA exhibited a first drop of crude oil flowing out of the adsorption column at 37 min, with a cumulative adsorption capacity of 202.93 g. While, AC showed a cumulative adsorption capacity of 211.39 g for crude oil when the first drop flowed out of the adsorption column. Notably, the cumulative adsorption capacities of both materials for crude oil were similar when the first drop flowed out of the adsorption column. However, it was important to note that AC took a significantly longer time for the initial adsorption to the first drop of crude oil flowing out of the adsorption column, and the cumulative adsorption capacity of WTSA for crude oil was substantially higher than that of AC within the same adsorption time. Similarly, the cumulative adsorption capacity of crude oil within the same adsorption time was consistently higher for WTSA regenerator compared to AC regenerator. Furthermore, the cumulative adsorption capacity of crude oil by WTSA regenerator did not show a significant decrease with an increase in the number of regenerations. On the contrary, the cumulative adsorption capacity of crude oil by AC regenerator decreased significantly as the number of regenerations increased. This indicated that WTSA can maintain a relatively stable cumulative adsorption capacity for crude oil even after regeneration, making it suitable for emergency adsorption of crude oil spills. Moreover, WTSA can be effectively used for multiple cycles and repeated use.

Conclusions

WTSA was developed using WT and OS as raw materials through co-pyrolysis technology. The optimal preparation conditions for co-pyrolysis were determined to be the mass ratio of 1.5:1 for WT to OS, co-pyrolysis temperature of 600 ℃, co-pyrolysis holding time of 2 h and co-pyrolysis heating rate of 15 ℃/min. WTSA exhibited a high carbon content, with the highest recorded number being 89.95%. The surface particles of WTSA were observed to be densely packed, and the material displayed multitude of pores and cracks of varying shapes and sizes. The predominant pore structure was found to be a combination of mesopores and macropores. The BET specific surface area, pore volume, and average pore size were measured to be 686.81 m2/g, 0.74 cm3/g, and 5.91 nm, respectively.

During the adsorption test of WTSA on crude oil, it was observed that the initial adsorption rate of crude oil was 47.38 mg/min. The time taken for the first drop of crude oil to flow out of the adsorption column was 37 min, and the cumulative adsorption capacity of crude oil at that time was measured to be 202.93 g. The WTSA was subjected to thermal regeneration method after adsorption of crude oil. It was observed that with the increase number of regeneration cycles, the oleophilicity of WTSA was enhanced, leading to improvement in the initial adsorption rate of crude oil. Additionally, the cumulative adsorption capacity of crude oil by WTSA remained relatively stable throughout the regeneration process.
