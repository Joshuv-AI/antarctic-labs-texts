Introduction

The confluence of human immunodeficiency virus/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (HIV/AIDS) and tuberculosis (TB), specifically multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) excluding extensively drug-resistant strains, has profoundly shaped the epidemiological landscape of Africa since the early 1990s. HIV/AIDS, driven by immune suppression from HIV, significantly elevates susceptibility to co-infections, with TB being a primary cause of death among individuals living with HIV (PLHIV) [1]. MDR-TB, marked by resistance to key first-line drugs (isoniazid and rifampicin), complicates therapeutic strategies, prolongs infectious periods, and heightens mortality risks, particularly in settings with limited healthcare resources [2]. Africa accounts for a substantial share of the global HIV/AIDS burden, harboring approximately 67 % of the 39 million PLHIV in 2023, and a significant proportion of TB cases, with 2.5 million new cases reported in 2022 [3,4]. The synergistic interaction between HIV and MDR-TB has amplified mortality, necessitating a comprehensive analysis to guide targeted public health interventions.

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study, curated by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), provides robust age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) estimates, facilitating longitudinal and regional comparisons [5]. From 1990 to 2021, Africa underwent significant public health advancements, including the expansion of antiretroviral therapy (ART), adoption of rapid TB diagnostics (e.g., GeneXpert), and global initiatives such as the President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) and the Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tuberculosis, and Malaria [6,7]. These interventions have reshaped the epidemiological trajectories of HIV/AIDS and MDR-TB, yet disparities persist due to variations in healthcare access, socioeconomic factors, and conflict-related disruptions [8]. Southern Africa, with HIV prevalence rates approaching 20 % in some nations, faces distinct challenges in managing HIV-TB co-infection, while Northern Africa exhibits a contrasting epidemiological profile with lower HIV prevalence [8,9].

Gender-specific disparities further influence the epidemic’s dynamics. Women are disproportionately affected due to physiological vulnerabilities (e.g., immunosuppression during pregnancy), socio-structural barriers (e.g., stigma, gender-based violence), and interventions like prevention of mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) programs [10]. Men, however, face elevated TB risks through occupational exposures (e.g., mining) and lower adherence to ART, impacting mortality outcomes [11]. Understanding these patterns is critical for advancing global health objectives, such as the World Health Organization’s (WHO) End TB Strategy and the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) 95–95–95 targets by 2030 [12,13].

This study employs GBD data to examine the temporal trends and mortality burden of HIV/AIDS and non-extensively drug-resistant MDR-TB across African subregions from 1990 to 2021. Using joinpoint regression, we quantify trends through Annual Percent Change (APC) and Average Annual Percent Change (AAPC), stratified by region and gender, to identify critical inflection points and drivers of epidemiological shifts. Our findings aim to inform evidence-based strategies to alleviate the dual burden of HIV/AIDS and MDR-TB in Africa.

Methods

Data source

This study draws upon the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 Study, a comprehensive, collaborative effort led by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) to systematically estimate disease burdens worldwide, including detailed mortality metrics for conditions such as HIV/AIDS and multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) without extensive drug resistance (GBD cause ID: 949) [5]. We specifically extracted age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) data—defined as the number of deaths per 100,000 population adjusted to a standard age distribution to account for demographic variations across populations and time periods—for HIV/AIDS and non-extensively drug-resistant MDR-TB across the African Union. The data encompass five key subregions: Central Africa, Eastern Africa, Northern Africa, Southern Africa, and Western Africa, covering the period from 1990 to 2021 (as visualized in Fig. 1). This temporal span was selected to capture the evolution of the dual epidemics from their explosive growth in the early 1990s through to recent advancements in treatment and prevention, allowing for a robust assessment of long-term trends [5].

The ASMR metric is particularly valuable in epidemiological research because it eliminates confounding from age structure differences, thereby enabling meaningful comparisons over time and between regions with varying demographic profiles [14]. For instance, without age standardization, rising mortality in an aging population might be misinterpreted as a true increase in disease burden, whereas ASMR provides a more accurate reflection of underlying epidemiological shifts. Data were stratified by gender (male, female, and combined) to facilitate the exploration of sex-specific patterns, which are crucial given the documented biological, behavioral, and social factors influencing HIV/AIDS and MDR-TB outcomes differently between sexes.

The GBD 2021 estimates are derived from an extensive, multi-source dataset that integrates vital registration records, disease surveillance systems, verbal autopsy studies (which retrospectively determine causes of death through interviews), and health facility-based reports. To enhance accuracy, the IHME applies sophisticated Bayesian meta-regression models—known as DisMod-MR—to adjust for underreporting, misclassification of causes, and incomplete data coverage, particularly in low-resource settings like much of Africa where vital registration is often fragmentary [15]. This rigorous modeling approach ensures that the estimates are not only comprehensive but also comparable across diverse contexts, making the GBD database an ideal foundation for our analysis of regional disparities in HIV/AIDS and MDR-TB mortality.

Statistical analysis

To rigorously investigate the temporal dynamics of HIV/AIDS and MDR-TB mortality, we employed joinpoint regression analysis—a sophisticated statistical technique widely used in epidemiology to identify and quantify abrupt changes (or “joinpoints”) in linear trends within time-series data, thereby revealing underlying shifts in disease patterns that might be obscured in simpler models [16]. Unlike traditional linear regression, which assumes a constant rate of change over time, joinpoint regression divides the data into distinct segments where the trend is approximately linear, fitting piecewise log-linear models to the logarithm of the rates. This approach is especially apt for infectious disease trends, as it can pinpoint inflection points corresponding to pivotal events, such as the introduction of new interventions or changes in transmission dynamics [17]. By modeling the natural logarithm of ASMR, the method assumes multiplicative changes over time, which aligns well with the exponential growth and decay often observed in epidemics.

All analyses were conducted using the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.0.2), developed by the Surveillance Research Program of the National Cancer Institute, a gold-standard tool for trend analysis in public health surveillance [18]. To determine the optimal number of joinpoints—balancing statistical fit with model parsimony to avoid overfitting—we utilized the Weighted Bayesian Information Criterion (WBIC), which penalizes excessive complexity while rewarding goodness-of-fit, supplemented by permutation testing. Permutation testing involves randomly reshuffling the data multiple times to generate an empirical distribution of test statistics under the null hypothesis of no joinpoints, providing a non-parametric assessment of significance that is robust to non-normal data distributions [18]. We conservatively limited the maximum number of joinpoints to six per analysis, a threshold that maintains interpretability and computational feasibility while accommodating the complexity of 31-year trends, consistent with established practices in GBD-based trend studies [19].

For each subregion and gender stratum, we computed two core trend metrics to encapsulate both segment-specific and overall changes:

1.Annual Percent Change (APC): This metric quantifies the average annual percentage change in ASMR within each joinpoint-defined segment, offering granular insights into how mortality rates evolved during specific periods—such as rapid epidemic growth or intervention-driven declines. Gender-stratified analyses were a cornerstone of our approach, enabling the detection of differential trends that reflect well-documented disparities in HIV/AIDS and TB epidemiology—for example, higher female vulnerability to HIV acquisition juxtaposed with male predominance in TB notifications due to diagnostic biases or behavioral factors [23]. Consequently, APC and AAPC estimates were generated separately for combined genders, males, and females, yielding a multifaceted view of regional variations. To mitigate risks from multiple comparisons across subregions and genders, we adhered to a stringent significance threshold of p < 0.05 and conducted comprehensive sensitivity analyses. These included re-running models with alternative joinpoint maxima (e.g., five joinpoints) to verify stability of identified trends and exploring permutations with different grid searches for joinpoint placement, ensuring that our findings were not artifacts of model specification [24]. All computations strictly followed GBD protocols for data extraction, handling of uncertainty intervals, and maintenance of statistical rigor, thereby upholding the integrity and reproducibility of the results [15].

Ethical considerations

Given that this investigation relied exclusively on publicly accessible, de-identified aggregate data from the GBD 2021 Study, no individual-level information was involved, obviating the need for institutional ethical approval or informed consent procedures. Nonetheless, all data utilization conformed to the IHME’s terms of use, including appropriate attribution and adherence to dissemination guidelines that promote open access while protecting data provenance [25].

Discussion

The analysis of HIV/AIDS and MDR-TB mortality trends across Africa from 1990 to 2021 reveals a dynamic epidemiological trajectory, marked by sharp increases in the 1990s due to the uncontrolled HIV/AIDS epidemic and the emergence of MDR-TB, followed by significant declines in most regions driven by transformative health interventions. However, regional and sex-specific variations highlight persistent challenges in achieving equitable progress, with implications for global health targets. Below, we discuss these findings in detail, contextualizing trends, exploring drivers, addressing study limitations, and outlining future directions.

Regional trends and drivers

The African Union’s non-significant overall increase (AAPC: 1.9526 %; 95 % CI: −1.7682, 6.3941; p = 0.278344) reflects the balance between early epidemic surges (e.g., APC: 99.8137 %; 1990–1992) and later declines (e.g., APC: −10.3478 %; 2006–2014). The dramatic increases in the 1990s, particularly in Southern Africa (APC: 105.5506 %; 1990–1993), were driven by high HIV prevalence, limited ART access, and weak TB control systems, which amplified HIV-TB co-infection [26]. MDR-TB’s rise during this period was exacerbated by inadequate treatment regimens, poor infection control, and delayed diagnostics [27]. The inflection points post-2003 align with global interventions, including the WHO’s Directly Observed Treatment, Short-course (DOTS) strategy, PEPFAR’s ART scale-up, and the Global Fund’s investments in TB diagnostics and MDR-TB treatment [28,29]. For instance, Central Africa’s steep decline (APC: −15.7512 %; 2016–2021) reflects improved ART coverage, which reached 76 % of PLHIV in sub-Saharan Africa by 2022, and the rollout of GeneXpert for rapid MDR-TB diagnosis [30].

Regional disparities underscore heterogeneous health system capacities. Northern Africa’s significant increase (AAPC: 3.5641 %; p = 0.014397) contrasts with non-significant declines in Central (AAPC: −1.5891 %), Eastern (AAPC: −0.5294 %), and Western Africa (AAPC: −0.1334 %). Northern Africa’s lower baseline HIV prevalence may have delayed intervention prioritization, while cultural barriers and stigma hindered testing and treatment uptake [31]. Southern Africa’s non-significant increase (AAPC: 2.2739 %) reflects its high HIV burden, with countries like South Africa and Botswana facing challenges in managing co-infection despite robust ART programs [32]. The slower declines in 2014–2021 across regions (e.g., African Union, APC: −5.4735 %) suggest emerging challenges, including ART resistance, MDR-TB treatment stockouts, and health system strain from population growth and conflict [33].

Sex-specific patterns

Sex-specific trends reveal critical disparities. Females exhibited steeper declines in most regions (e.g., African Union, APC: −11.4648 %; 2006–2014 vs. males, APC: −9.7190 %; 2006–2012), likely due to targeted interventions like PMTCT, which reduced HIV transmission and improved female ART access [34]. However, non-significant plateaus in early periods (e.g., Central Africa, APC: 0.9223 %; 1998–2004) indicate delays in addressing female-specific needs, possibly due to stigma, gender-based violence, or rural healthcare access barriers [35]. In Southern Africa, females’ sustained decline (APC: −12.9742 %; 2007–2014) reflects gender-sensitive programs, but the slower decline post-2014 (APC: −4.3587 %) suggests persistent adherence challenges [36].

Males, with higher AAPCs in most regions (e.g., African Union, 2.6147 % vs. 1.4705 % for females), faced greater risks, likely due to occupational exposures (e.g., mining in Southern Africa) and lower ART uptake [37]. For instance, Western Africa’s male AAPC (0.5383 %) was positive, contrasting with females’ negative AAPC (−0.6602 %), reflecting male-specific barriers like delayed healthcare-seeking behavior and stigma [38]. Northern Africa’s significant increases for both sexes (female AAPC: 3.8169 %; male AAPC: 3.4344 %) highlight gender-neutral challenges, such as low testing rates and cultural resistance to HIV/TB care [39].

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, GBD data rely on modeled estimates, which may introduce uncertainty in settings with incomplete vital registration or underreported TB cases, particularly in conflict-affected regions like Central Africa [40]. Second, joinpoint regression assumes linear trends within segments, potentially oversimplifying non-linear epidemiological shifts driven by abrupt policy changes or outbreaks [41]. Third, the analysis aggregates MDR-TB without distinguishing specific resistance patterns (e.g., pre-XDR-TB), which may obscure treatment challenges [42]. Fourth, subnational variations, such as urban–rural disparities or hotspot regions (e.g., mining communities), are not captured, limiting granularity [43]. Finally, the study does not account for social determinants (e.g., poverty, education) or co-morbidities (e.g., diabetes), which influence HIV/TB outcomes [44].

Future directions

Future research should address these limitations by integrating primary data from national TB and HIV registries to validate GBD estimates [45]. Subnational analyses, using geospatial modeling, could identify high-burden areas and inform targeted interventions [46]. Incorporating genomic surveillance data for MDR-TB strains would enhance understanding of resistance patterns and guide treatment strategies [47]. Longitudinal cohort studies exploring social determinants and co-morbidities could elucidate drivers of sex-specific disparities [48]. Predictive modeling, using machine learning or compartmental models, could forecast HIV/TB mortality trends to 2030, aligning with WHO and UNAIDS targets [49]. Policy-wise, strengthening health systems, scaling up community-based testing, and addressing gender-specific barriers (e.g., male-friendly clinics, female empowerment programs) are critical [50,51]. Innovations like shorter MDR-TB regimens and integrase inhibitor-based ART could further reduce mortality [52].

Conclusion

From 1990 to 2021, Africa made remarkable progress in reducing HIV/AIDS and MDR-TB mortality, driven by ART scale-up, improved TB diagnostics, and global health initiatives. However, regional disparities, with Northern Africa’s increasing trends and Southern Africa’s high burden, alongside sex-specific challenges, underscore the need for tailored interventions. While females benefited from targeted programs, males faced persistent risks, necessitating gender-equitable strategies. Despite significant declines, the slowing progress post-2014 highlights emerging challenges, including resistance, stockouts, and health system constraints. Addressing these through strengthened health systems, innovative treatments, and granular epidemiological analyses is essential to achieve the WHO’s End TB Strategy and UNAIDS’ 95–95–95 goals by 2030, ensuring equitable reductions in the dual burden of HIV/AIDS and MDR-TB across Africa.
