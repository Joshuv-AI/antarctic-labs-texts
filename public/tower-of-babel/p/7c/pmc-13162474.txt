Integrated multi-omics analysis and functional validation reveal the role of TRIM2 as a potential novel biomarker in breast cancer
Authors: Rumei Deng, Yifei Ren, Kaiheng Hu, Yanxuan Zhang, Yihui Xu, Qi Zhang, Yong Li, Xuan Huang, Feng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast cancer (BRCA) remains a major global health challenge due to its high recurrence rates and resistance to therapy, underscoring the need for reliable biomarkers and novel therapeutic targets. Tripartite motif-containing protein 2 (TRIM2), an E3 ubiquitin ligase, has been implicated in tumorigenesis across multiple cancers, yet its role in BRCA remains poorly understood.MethodsWe integrated multi-omics datasets from TCGA, GEO, and METABRIC, and validated TRIM2 expression using proteomic data from the Proteomic Data Commons and Human Protein Atlas. Single-cell RNA sequencing was employed to characterize the transcriptional heterogeneity and cell–cell interaction patterns of TRIM2+ tumor cells. Ubiquitin-proteomic and proteomic profiling were conducted to identify TRIM2-regulated substrates. Drug sensitivity prediction and molecular docking were applied to assess TRIM2-related therapeutic responses, and in vitro experiments were performed to validate its biological functions.ResultsWe found that TRIM2 is consistently downregulated in BRCA tissues at both transcriptomic and proteomic levels, with low expression significantly associated with advanced stage and poor overall survival. Clinically, elevated TRIM2 expression correlated with improved responsiveness to immunotherapy. Drug sensitivity and molecular docking analyses identified 64 TRIM2-sensitive compounds, including five with strong binding affinities, suggesting potential for targeted therapeutic development. Genomic alterations and promoter hypermethylation were identified as potential mechanisms driving TRIM2 dysregulation. Multi-layered functional enrichment analyses, including transcriptomic, proteinomic, and ubiquitin-proteomic profiling, suggested that TRIM2 regulates cancer- and immune-related pathways including TGF-β, Hippo, TNF, and IL-17 signaling pathways. Single-cell RNA sequencing further demonstrated that TRIM2+ and TRIM2− tumor cell subpopulations engage in distinct cell–cell interactions, highlighting TRIM2’s role in shaping tumor-immune communication. Functional assays confirmed that TRIM2 suppresses BRCA cell proliferation and anchorage-independent growth in vitro. Immune profiling revealed that high TRIM2 expression is associated with enhanced immune checkpoint activity, greater infiltration of antitumor immune cells, and increased immune recruitment scores.ConclusionsCollectively, our findings identify TRIM2 as a potential tumor suppressor and novel prognostic biomarker in BRCA, with potential utility for guiding personalized immunotherapy and targeted treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-026-02263-2.

Background

Breast cancer (BRCA), a malignant tumor originating from the breast epithelium, remains a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality among women worldwide [1]. Despite advances in screening, surgery, systemic therapies, and targeted interventions, patient outcomes are still hindered by residual tumor burden, heterogeneous drug response, therapeutic resistance, and others [2, 3]. The identification of robust biomarkers and actionable therapeutic targets is therefore essential to improve diagnosis, guide treatment, and overcome resistance. However, clinically validated biomarkers and effective molecular targets remain scarce, highlighting the urgent need to elucidate the functional relevance of potential candidates. Such insights are critical for developing precision strategies that can enhance patient outcomes and reduce the global burden of BRCA.

The tripartite motif-containing (TRIM) protein family, the largest subfamily of E3 ubiquitin ligases, plays crucial roles in diverse oncogenic processes, such as cell proliferation, invasion, migration, and apoptosis. TRIM proteins can exert either oncogenic or tumor-suppressive functions primarily through the ubiquitin–proteasome system [4–6]. As a member of the TRIM family, TRIM2 is a typical E3 ligase with a “RBCC” domain, comprising an N-terminal RING domain, a B-box type 1 domain, a B-box type 2 domain, and a coiled-coil region [7]. These domains confer catalytic activity, enabling TRIM2 to mediate ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of specific substrates, thereby influencing tumorigenesis [8]. TRIM2 has been implicated in the progression of various cancers, including osteosarcoma [9], rectal cancer [10], lung adenocarcinoma [11], and renal clear-cell carcinoma [12]. Notably, Yin et al. reported that TRIM2 suppresses the pro-apoptotic protein Bim, thereby enhancing the sensitivity of estrogen receptor (ER)-positive BRCA cells to tamoxifen [13]. This finding highlights TRIM2’s potential as a therapeutic target to improve treatment outcomes in BRCA. However, the roles and molecular mechanisms of TRIM2 in BRCA remain largely unexplored.

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive investigation of TRIM2 in BRCA using multi-omics databases and in vitro experiments. We first assessed TRIM2 expression patterns and their associations with diverse clinical features, including overall survival (OS), immunotherapy response, drug sensitivity and therapeutic interaction. The present study sought to further explore the relationships between TRIM2 expression, genetic mutations, and DNA methylation. Functional enrichment analyses were also performed to characterize TRIM2’s biological roles at the transcriptomic, proteomic, and post-translational levels. Furthermore, the tumor-suppressive role of TRIM2 was confirmed through in vitro cell proliferation assays, and its association with immune infiltration and cell–cell interactions within the tumor microenvironment was examined. Collectively, our findings explore the potential of TRIM2 as a novel prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in BRCA, while providing new insights into the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Raw data acquisition

To investigate the molecular characteristics of BRCA, we collected multiomics datasets from publicly available repositories. Bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), DNA methylation, copy number variation (CNV), iTRAQ-based proteomics, and corresponding clinical data were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) via the TCGAbiolinks R package [14]. Additional bulk RNA-seq datasets, including GSE22820, GSE10797, GSE45827, and GSE65194, were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). The METABRIC cohort was accessed via the MetaGxBreast R package. To validate protein expression levels of candidate genes in normal and tumor tissues, we utilized proteomic data from the Proteomic Data Commons (PDC000120) and the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) [15–17]. Furthermore, to capture transcriptomic heterogeneity at single-cell resolution, we retrieved a single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset (GSE195665) from the GEO database [18]. In addition, two publicly available immunotherapy BRCA microarray cohorts were included to assess treatment response. The CTR_Microarray_126_l cohort consists of BRCA patients treated with a combination of MK-2206, paclitaxel, and trastuzumab, whereas the CTR_Microarray_170_l cohort includes BRCA patients treated with paclitaxel in combination with pembrolizumab, an anti-PD-1 immune checkpoint inhibitor.

Prognostic association analyses

After excluding patients with survival times of less than 30 days, a total of 962 samples were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the median TRIM2 mRNA expression level. Fisher’s exact test was subsequently applied to assess associations between TRIM2 expression and various clinicopathological characteristics, including age, histological type, menopause status, pathological stage, race, and TNM stage. Survival analyses were conducted using the survival package (v3.2.7) in R. Kaplan–Meier curves were generated to compare survival outcomes between high and low groups, and statistical significance was evaluated using log-rank tests.

Drug response and sensitivity analysis

The Cancer Treatment Response Gene Signature Database (CTR-DB, http://ctrdb.ncpsb.org.cn/) was used to investigate clinical transcriptomic profiles associated with anticancer drug responses. To further assess drug sensitivity, the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer 2 (GDSC2) dataset was employed to predict patients’ half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) scores for 198 therapeutic agents using the R package oncoPredict. In addition, molecular docking was performed to evaluate the potential interactions between candidate drugs and proteins encoded by TRIM2. Protein receptor structures were mainly obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Ligand information was collected from ChEMBL and converted into suitable formats using OpenBabel. Docking simulations were carried out with Dockey and QuickVina-W, and the binding affinity was assessed based on calculated binding free energies. Complex structures were further visualized with PyMOL to illustrate potential interaction modes.

Genetic alteration analysis

Genetic alterations of TRIM2 across various cancer types were examined using the cBioPortal platform, which utilized data from the TCGA PanCancer Atlas studies. Specific mutation sites and alteration regions were explored through the “Mutations” module. The associations between TRIM2 expression and copy number variation (CNV) levels were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, with statistical significance defined as an adjusted p < 0.05. In addition, TRIM2 promoter methylation levels across clinical subgroups were evaluated using the UALCAN platform, with results displayed as box plots.

Functional analysis of TRIM2 based on bulk RNA-seq and proteomic datasets

Differential expression analysis was performed on TCGA RNA-seq data using the limma R package to identify significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between TRIM2-high and TRIM2-low groups, applying thresholds of p < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change|> 1. Functional enrichment was assessed with Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using the clusterProfiler R package. Pathways with p < 0.05 and q < 0.2 were considered significantly enriched. The Linkedomics platform (http://www.linkedomics.org/login.php) was applied to identify the top 10 genes most strongly correlated with TRIM2 expression in BRCA. Co-expression patterns of these genes across various cancer types were visualized using the Tumor Immune Estimation Resource 2.0 (TIMER2.0) platform. Additionally, pathway activities associated with TRIM2 and its co-expressed genes were evaluated across various tumors using GSCALite (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/web/GSCALite/).

Proteomic profiling of iTRAQ datasets from TCGA was used to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) comparing the TRIM2-high and TRIM2-low groups using thresholds of p < 0.05 and absolute fold change > 1.5. Enrichment and protein–protein interaction (PPI) analyses were performed with Metascape, and TRIM2-interacting network was constructed using the STRING database. Finally, expression differences of TRIM2-interacting proteins between tumors and adjacent normal tissues across pan-cancer datasets were further assessed with the Cancer Proteogenomic Data Analysis Site (cProSite) (https://cprosite.ccr.cancer.gov/).

LC-MS/MS sequence, quantification, and ubiquitinated peptide enrichment

The 3 biological replicates of MCF-7 cells and MCF-7 cells overexpressing TRIM2 were treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 for 4 h. Cells were lysed in 1% SDS/100 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH8.5) by sonication, and protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay. The 1 mg of protein was incubated at 95 ℃ for 10 min with Tris-2-chloroethyl-phosphate (TCEP) and 2-chloroacetamide (CAA). After digestion with trypsin overnight at 37 ℃, ubiquitinated peptides were enriched using anti-K-ε-GG antibody beads (PTMScan Kit, Cell Signaling Technology) and then subjected to ubiquitinated proteome sequencing.

LC–MS/MS analysis was performed on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer connected to an EASY-nLC 1200 system. Peptides were separated on a 75 μm × 25 cm C18 column using a gradient of 0.1% formic acid (A) and 80% acetonitrile (B) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. MS spectra were acquired in DDA mode with one full scan (R = 60 K, 350–1500 m/z) followed by 20 MS/MS scans (R = 15 K). Raw data was processed using MaxQuant against the SwissProt human proteome database. Label-free quantification was employed with variable modifications (Oxidation [M], GlyGly [K], Acetyl [N-term]) and fixed modification (Carbamidomethyl [C]). False discovery rate (FDR) was controlled at 1% for both protein and peptide levels.

Differentially ubiquitinated sites (DUS) between the TRIM2 overexpression and control groups were identified with a threshold of p < 0.05 and |fold change|> 1.2. Functional enrichment analyses, including KEGG and GSEA, were performed on proteins containing differentially ubiquitinated sites (DUPs) following the same procedures used in RNA-seq analysis.

Single-cell data preprocessing, clustering, and cell type annotation

A total of 235,614 cells were collected from 11 normal tissues and 38 BRCA tissues, and the scRNA-seq dataset was analyzed using the Scanpy package (version 1.9.4). Potential doublets were removed using the Scrublet package, with expected_doublet_rate set to 0.05 and doublet score threshold set to 0.25. Cells with fewer than 500 expressed genes, cells with mitochondrial gene expression greater than 20%, genes expressed in fewer than three cells, and mitochondrial, ribosomal, heat shock, and dissociation-related genes were filtered out. A total of 235,614 cells and 28,460 genes were kept for further analysis. After normalization and scaling, batch effects from different sources were corrected using the BBKNN algorithm [19, 20]. Dimensionality reduction was carried out using PCA and UMAP, followed by cluster identification using the Leiden algorithm with a resolution of 0.8 [21].

Cell types were annotated based on common cell identity markers, defining the following major subpopulations: epithelial cells (KRT17, KRT19), T & NK cells (CD3D, CD3E), macrophages & monocytes (C1QA, LYZ, APOC1), fibroblasts (ACTA2, COL1A1, DCN), endothelial cells (PECAM1, ENG), plasma cells (MZB1), B cells (MS4A1), dendritic cells (TCF4, GZMB), and mast cells (TPSAB1, TPSB2). Epithelial cells were further sub-clustered using the Leiden algorithm at a resolution of 0.8. To distinguish tumor cells from normal epithelial cells, inferCNVpy (version 0.5.0) was applied to infer CNVs, using normal epithelial cells as a reference. Based on inferred CNV scores, epithelial cells were first classified as malignant or non-malignant by comparison with normal reference cells.

Tumor epithelial cells were then further stratified into TRIM2⁺ and TRIM2− populations using a cluster-based approach, whereby tumor epithelial subclusters exhibiting consistently higher average TRIM2 expression across cells compared with other tumor epithelial clusters were classified as TRIM2+, while the remaining tumor epithelial clusters were designated as TRIM2−.

Cell–Cell interaction analysis

CellPhoneDB (version 4.0.0) was employed in a Python environment to evaluate cell–cell interactions. The significance of ligand-receptor pairs was assessed through 1000 random permutations of cell type labels. During each permutation, the average expression levels of ligands and receptors in interacting cells were calculated, forming a null distribution for each pair. To account for cluster size–related bias, we normalized significant ligand–receptor interactions by the product of the corresponding cell numbers. The heatmap and chord figures for ligand-receptor pairs were visualised by the ktplots R package. Differential receptor–ligand interactions between TRIM2+ and TRIM2− tumor cells were identified by comparing their significant interaction profiles with the same partner cell types. A receptor–ligand pair was defined as differential if it showed a statistically significant interaction in one subgroup (e.g., TRIM2+) but not in the other (e.g., TRIM2−). These subgroup-specific pairs were then subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses to reveal the functional pathways associated with TRIM2-dependent tumor–microenvironment communication.

Cell culture and Western blot

Normal human mammary epithelial cells (MCF-10A) and breast cancer cell lines (T47D, Hs578T, MCF-7, MDA-MB-231) were purchased from Wuhan Procell Life Science and Technology Ltd, China between 2019 and 2024. All cell lines were identified by short tandem repeat (STR), and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis was negative for mycoplasma contamination. Cells were routinely cultured in DMEM/F12, MEM, RPMI-1640, and DMEM (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) with or without hydrocortisone/insulin/NEAA with 10% FBS (ExCell Bio, Taicang, China) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin at 37 ℃ and 5% CO2, as suggested by the manufacturer.

Cell lysates of breast cancer cells were harvested with RIPA lysis buffer (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) or 2 × loading buffer. After BCA quantitation (Glpbio, Shanghai, China), equal amounts of protein (10 μg/sample) were separated on 10% SDS–polyacrylamide gel and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Bedford, MD, USA) for immunoblotting analysis. Then, the membranes were incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 ℃, followed by secondary antibody incubation for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the protein bands can be visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection reagent (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA). Primary antibodies used in this study included anti-TRIM2 (20,356-1-AP), anti-GAPDH (60,004-1-Ig), and anti-β-actin (66,009-1-Ig), which were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China).

TRIM2 knockdown and overexpression

Recombinant lentivirus was packaged in HEK-293 T cells using calcium phosphate-mediated transfection with an optimized plasmid ratio (psPAX2:pMD2G:pLVX-IRES-ZsGreen = 2:1:2), where TRIM2 overexpression and knockdown plasmids were constructed basing on the pLVX-IRES-ZsGreen vector. The culture medium was replaced with fresh complete medium 24 h post-transfection. Viruses containing supernatants were harvested 36–48 h after transfection, centrifuged at 400 g for 5 min, filtered through 0.22 μm membrane, aliquoted, and stored at −80 ℃ for long-term preservation. To evaluate infection efficiency, target cells were plated in 12-well plates and infected at 30–40% confluence. The medium was replaced with fresh complete medium 12–16 h post-infection, followed by puromycin selection (2 μg/mL) at 48 h after infection. TRIM2 protein expression levels were analyzed by Western blot to confirm the efficiency of knockdown and overexpression.

Cell proliferation assay and soft agar assay

A comprehensive assessment of cellular proliferative capacity was conducted through multiple complementary approaches. The metabolic activity was first determined using the CCK-8 colorimetric assay, with cell suspensions plated at 5 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates. Following a 24 h incubation, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and the plates were maintained at 37 °C for 60 min prior to absorbance measurement at 450 nm. Subsequently, proliferation capacity under nutrient-restricted conditions was analyzed through low-serum culture experiments. Cells were plated in 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well, which were cultured overnight in complete medium with 10% FBS and then maintained in 1% FBS medium with medium changes every other day, followed by cell counting at days 2, 4, and 6. Clonogenic potential was assessed by plating 1000 cells per well in 6-well plates and culturing in 10% FBS-containing medium for 12–14 days. The colonies were stained with 2% crystal violet solution and only well-defined colonies exceeding 50 μm in diameter were counted. In addition, we evaluated the anchorage-independent growth capacity of cells using a soft agar colony formation assay. Lentivirus-infected stable cells were seeded in 6-well plates (1 × 104 cells per well) with an upper layer of 0.7% agar growth medium containing cell suspension and a lower layer of 1.2% agar growth medium. The culture medium was replenished every 3 days, and the cells were cultured for about 10–20 days. Colonies ≥ 50 μm in diameter were imaged and counted.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

For Co-IP experiments, T47D or Hs578T cells were treated with MG132 for 4 h and then lysed in 0.5% Lubrol-Px lysis buffer (50 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 20% glycerol and 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4). The supernatant was mixed with 1 μL of antibody and incubated at 4 °C for 4 h. After that, 120 μL of protein A/G beads (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) were added to each tube, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with gentle rotation (10 rpm/min). The following day, thesupernatant was discarded after centrifugation at 1000 × g, and the beads were washed five times. The bound proteins were analyzed by immunoblotting.

Immunofluorescence

To detect the colocalization of TRIM2 and its substrate proteins ANXA9 or NCAPG (BASP1 or QPRT), T47D or Hs578T cells were seeded on glass slides one day before IF analysis. When the cell density reached 20% to 30%, the cells were fixed in 4% cold PFA for 15 min and incubated with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min to make the cells transparent. After blocking with 5% Goat Serum, cells were incubated with TRIM2 primary antibody (1:400, Proteintech) and ANXA9 primary antibody (1:300, Proteintech) or NCAPG (1:300, Proteintech) or BASP1 (1:300, Proteintech) or QPRT (1:300, Proteintech) for 12 h at 4 °C. The following day, Alexa Fluor Cy3/488 secondary antibodies were used to bind for 2 h at room temperature. The nuclei were then stained with DAPI (Solarbio, Beijing, China), and images were captured using a Nikon A1R confocal microscope.

Immunocyte infiltration analysis

Differential expression analysis of immune-related genes was performed on TCGA RNA-seq data using DESeq2, with samples stratified by mean TRIM2 expression. Genes with an adjusted p < 0.05 and |fold change|> 1.5 were considered significant. Immune activity across seven cancer-immunity cycle steps (covering 23 immune processes) was assessed using the Tracking Tumor Immunophenotype (TIP, http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/TIP/). Immune cell infiltration was estimated using TIMER2, integrating the TIMER, CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT-ABS, QUANTISEQ, XCELL, and EPIC algorithms.

Results

Reduced TRIM2 expression correlates with tumor progression and poor survival

Differential expression analyses of the TRIM family genes comparing BRCA and adjacent non-tumorous tissue revealed that TRIM2 is abnormally expressed at the methylation, mRNA, and protein levels (Fig. S1). To further investigate the transcriptional profile of TRIM2 across cancers, we analyzed data from the TIMER2.0 database. TRIM2 expression was significantly decreased in multiple malignancies, including BRCA, bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), glioblastoma (GBM), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), and clear cell renal carcinoma (KIRC), relative to their corresponding normal tissues (Fig. 1A). The downregulation of TRIM2 mRNA in BRCA was consistently validated in independent datasets, including the TCGA, METABRIC, and 4 GEO databases (Fig. 1B). At the protein level, TRIM2 expression was markedly reduced in BRCA tissues compared with adjacent normal controls (p = 2.10 × 10⁻1⁰) (Fig. 1C). TRIM2 expression may correlate with immunotherapy responsiveness, drug sensitivity, and therapeutic interaction

To further evaluate the association between TRIM2 expression and immunotherapy outcomes, we analyzed two independent immunotherapy breast cancer cohorts, CTR_Microarray_126_l and CTR_Microarray_170_l. TRIM2 expression levels were significantly higher in the immunotherapy-responsive group both in CTR_Microarray_170_l (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p < 0.01) and CTR_Microarray_126_l (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p < 0.02) datasets (Fig. 2A–B). To quantitatively assess the ability of TRIM2 expression to distinguish responders from non-responders, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed. We further evaluated drug sensitivity between the TRIM2-high and TRIM2-low groups to 198 compounds in the GDSC2. Notably, 64 drugs were identified as significantly sensitive to TRIM2, exhibiting lower estimated IC50 scores in patients with high TRIM2 expression (Fig. 2C–D). This indicates that these TRIM2-high patients have increased sensitivity to targeted therapeutic drugs, making them more likely to benefit from these treatments. To further explore potential therapeutic interactions, we performed molecular docking analyses between TRIM2 and these 64 sensitive drugs (Fig. 2E). Among the 6 top sensitive drug, Alisertib demonstrated the strongest binding affinity to TRIM2, with a binding energy of − 10.52 kcal/mol and an inhibition constant (Ki) of 19.28 nM. Tozasertib also exhibited favorable binding (binding energy = − 10.08 kcal/mol, Ki = 40.88 nM). In contrast, Daporinad and Dinaciclib showed weaker interactions, with a binding energy of − 8.98 and −8.27 kcal/mol, respectively. Notably, five compounds, including Alisertib and Tozasertib, demonstrated binding free energies ≤ − 10 kcal/mol, indicating a strong potential interaction with TRIM2 (Table S1).

Genetic alterations and promoter methylation of TRIM2

The genetic landscape of TRIM2 alterations was analyzed using the cBioPortal database (Fig. 3A). Across cancer types, mutation was the most prevalent genetic alteration, followed by deep deletion. Notably, amplification was the most frequent alteration in BRCA, with deep deletion as the second most common. Mapping of mutations revealed that TRIM2 alterations were concentrated within zf-C3HC4_2, zf-B_box, Filamin, and NHL domains, which are critical for ubiquitin ligase activity and substrate recognition (Fig. 3B). To further explore copy number variations (CNVs), we compared the CNV events between TRIM2 high and low expression groups (Fig. 3C). Promoter methylation analysis using the UALCAN platform illustrated that TRIM2 promoter methylation level was significantly higher in BRCA tumor tissues than adjacent normal tissues (p = 1.62 × 10−12). Additionally, hypermethylation of the TRIM2 promoter was correlated with an increased frequency of TP53 mutation (Fig. 3D). These findings suggest that both genomic and epigenomic alterations may contribute to the dysregulated expression of TRIM2 in BRCA, highlighting a potential role for these mechanisms in driving tumorigenesis.

Most peptide lengths were distributed over 6 ~ 18 amino acids, meeting the quality control requirements (Fig. S3A). In total, we detected 1782 ubiquitinated proteins, 3732 ubiquitinated peptides, and 3903 ubiquitination sites, of which those with site confidence ≥ 0.7 were taken for downstream analyses. The 98.4% proteins (1607 proteins) harbored less than 10 ubiquitination modifications, while 1.6% (26 proteins) harbored 10 or more modifications. Among the highly ubiquitinated proteins (n ≥ 10), PRKDC, CLTC, and ATP1A1 were the top three modified proteins, which had a total of 35, 32, and 23 ubiquitination sites, respectively (Fig. S3B). TRIM2 inhibits breast cancer cell proliferation and anchorage-independent growth in vitro

To investigate the effect of TRIM2 on the cell proliferation of breast cancer cells, we first detected TRIM2 protein levels in 4 different cancer cell lines. Western blot analysis revealed that TRIM2 protein was elevated in the breast cell lines MDA-MB-231 and T47D compared to the non-tumorigenic MCF10A, whereas relatively lower TRIM2 expression was observed in MCF-7 and Hs578T cells (Fig. 7A). Therefore, we overexpressed TRIM2 in MCF-7 and Hs578T cells which naturally exhibit low TRIM2 expression (Fig. 7B). Results of cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assays revealed that TRIM2 overexpression significantly reduced cell viability in both cell lines (Fig. 7C). Similarly, cell counting assays demonstrated a marked decrease in cell number, and colony formation assays showed fewer colonies in TRIM2 overexpressing cells than controls (Fig. 7D–E). Additionally, soft agar assays confirmed that TRIM2 overexpression significantly impaired anchorage-independent growth, as evidenced by 48.62–76.67% reduction in colony numbers (Fig. 7F). TRIM2 knockdown led to increased cell viability, cell number, and colony formation, consistent with the effects observed in TRIM2 overexpression assays (Fig. 8). TRIM2 may regulate the immune microenvironment in BRCA

Based on the previous enrichment analysis, the role of TRIM2 in the TIME was further investigated. The association between TRIM2 and immune components in BRCA was further validated using TCGA RNA-seq data. Discussions

BRCA remains one of the most common malignancies with high aggressiveness and mortality, necessitating the identification of effective biomarkers and therapeutic targets. TRIM2 has been widely demonstrated to play important roles in neurogenesis and tumor development, and is also implicated in non-malignant diseases such as Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease and glaucoma [7]. In recent years, multiple studies have reported that TRIM2 is significantly dysregulated in multiple cancer types, including ovarian cancer, follicular thyroid carcinoma, and clear cell renal cell carcinoma, and its roles may depend on tissue type and signaling context [7]. In addition, TRIM2 directly regulates the degradation of Bim, which plays an important role in tumor progression and therapeutic resistance [13]. Given the substantial heterogeneity in clinical outcomes and therapeutic responses among patients, the identification of reliable biomarkers for patient stratification and precision treatment is of critical importance. However, the role of TRIM2 in BRCA, including its underlying mechanisms, prognostic value, and potential association with immunotherapy response, remains insufficiently investigated. In this study, we provide the first comprehensive evaluation of the role of the TRIM2 gene in BRCA by integrating bioinformatics analyses with experimental validation.

Integrative multi-omics analyses revealed that TRIM2 is dysregulated across multiple cancer types, with particularly pronounced downregulation observed in BRCA. This alteration was consistently detected at both the transcriptomic and proteomic levels and may be associated with genetic mechanisms such as promoter methylation and somatic mutations, highlighting the critical role of TRIM2 in BRCA pathogenesis. Notably, high TRIM2 expression correlated with better prognosis and a greater likelihood of response to anti–PD-1 immunotherapy, suggesting its potential as a prognostic biomarker and a therapeutic target. Furthermore, although previous studies suggested that TRIM2 contributes to tamoxifen resistance in BRCA cells [13, 22], our drug sensitivity analysis revealed a significant positive association between TRIM2 expression and the efficacy of 64 anticancer agents, implying a complex functional role of TRIM2 in BRCA biology and providing new insights into overcoming drug resistance and developing novel targeted therapies.

Recent studies have revealed that dysregulated TRIM2 expression plays significant roles in tumorigenesis and cancer progression [7, 23]. To further investigate the role of TRIM2 in BRCA, we conducted a series of cell experiments. The results showed that TRIM2 overexpression significantly reduced the proliferation and non-anchored growth of MCF-7 and Hs578T cells (p < 0.05). Conversely, TRIM2 silencing promoted the proliferation and non-anchored growth of T47D cells (p < 0.05). Previous studies have shown that TRIM2 may exert complex biological effects through pathways such as PI3K/AKT, GPER–MAPK/ERK, and YAP, with its biological functions being dependent on tissue type and signaling context, thereby influencing tumor proliferation and migration and ultimately impacting prognosis [7]. In KIRC, for example, TRIM2 has been reported to suppress tumor progression by modulating hypoxia signaling pathways and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-mediated tumor cell migration/invasion [12]. In line with these findings, we observed that TRIM2 upregulation significantly inhibited cell proliferation in BRCA models, suggesting that TRIM2 may also play a tumor-suppressive role in BRCA.

Given that TRIM2 contains a canonical RING finger domain and possesses E3 ubiquitin ligase activity [24], its tumor-suppressive function is likely achieved by mediating ubiquitination and degradation of substrate proteins. Ubiquitin-proteomic analysis further supports this notion: in TRIM2-overexpressing cells, we observed significant ubiquitination abnormalities in multiple cancer-related proteins, including ITGB4, a gene with strong prognostic impact in BRCA and reported in several cancers to be closely associated with the TGF-β signaling pathway [25]. In line with this finding, we further observed that TRIM2 may exert its function in BRCA through the TGF-β pathway. Regarding receptor-ligand interactions, we observed significant differences between TRIM2+ and TRIM2− tumor cell clusters, with particular emphasis on the TGFB1/TGFB2-TGFBR3 axis. TGF-β, as an immunosuppressive cytokine, plays a crucial role in tumor progression [26]. Type III TGF-β receptor, the auxiliary receptor plays a crucial role in TGF-β signaling by sequestering TGF-β superfamily ligands. In BRCA, the downregulation of TGFBR3 leads to enhanced TGF-β signaling, which in turn promotes tumor progression and immune evasion [27]. In contrast, our study shows that TRIM2+ tumor cells maintain higher TGFBR3 expression levels. By promoting the interaction between TGFB1/TGFB2 and TGFBR3, we speculate that downstream TGF-β signaling in TRIM2+ cells may be suppressed, potentially reducing tumor invasiveness and metastasis, while sustaining a more active anti-tumor immune response. In addition, we found that TRIM2 in BRCA also exerts effects through regulation of the TNF and Hippo pathways. TRIM2 overexpression is associated with enhanced TNF signaling and activation of the Hippo pathway, suggesting that TRIM2 may contribute to its anti-tumor effects by simultaneously engaging multiple tumor-suppressive pathways.

Given that the efficacy of immunotherapy largely depends on the tumor immune microenvironment, particularly CD8⁺ T cell dysfunction and immune cell infiltration [28, 29], we next investigated whether TRIM2 influences these features. Using various algorithms, we extensively analyzed the role of TRIM2 in shaping the immune microenvironment. We observed a significant increase in CD8⁺ T cell recruitment and infiltration in the TRIM2-high group, which may be related to the involvement of TRIM2 in modulating the BRCA immune microenvironment through TGFBR3 regulation, as loss of TGFBR3 has been associated with reduced CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and an immunosuppressive tumor milieu that promotes cancer progression [27, 30]. Interestingly, we also observed a significant decrease in the infiltration of Tregs and M2 macrophages, which are widely recognized as strong suppressors of CD8⁺ T cell function. This finding suggests that TRIM2 may exert a more profound role in reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment toward a state that is favorable for immunotherapy. Patients with high TRIM2 expression exhibit higher levels of immune checkpoint expression. Elevated expression of immune checkpoints, robust infiltration of CD8+ T cells, and low infiltration of Tregs and M2 macrophages have all been strongly associated with successful immunotherapy [31–33]. This may explain why patients with high TRIM2 expression are more likely to respond to immune treatments.

Despite these advances, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although multiple cohorts were analyzed to minimize bias, some of the results in this study have not yet been fully validated by wet-lab experiments. In particular, future studies should define the specific ubiquitin linkage types and binding interfaces underlying TRIM2–substrate interactions and incorporate in vivo models, such as breast cancer xenografts, to elucidate the mechanisms underlying its tumor-suppressive role in breast cancer. Second, the immunotherapy analyses relied on small retrospective transcriptomic cohorts, limiting causal interpretation; thus, the association between TRIM2 expression and immunotherapy response should be interpreted cautiously and warrants prospective clinical and in vivo validation.

Taken together, this study comprehensively evaluate the role of TRIM2 in BRCA through multi-omics analyses and functional validation. We demonstrate that TRIM2 is significantly downregulated at both the transcriptomic and proteomic levels, and its reduced expression is associated with unfavorable prognosis. Elevated TRIM2 expression correlates with enhanced responsiveness to immunotherapy, highlighting its potential clinical utility. Drug sensitivity and molecular docking analyses further identify multiple TRIM2-sensitive compounds, underscoring its promise as a therapeutic target. Multi-layered functional enrichment analyses revealed that TRIM2 modulates key cancer- and immune-related pathways. Mechanistically, TRIM2 may exert tumor-suppressive effects through ubiquitination of cancer-related proteins such as ITGB4, thereby modulating TGF-β signaling and maintaining TGFBR3 expression to inhibit tumor progression and immune evasion. Additionally, high TRIM2 expression is also linked to a more active immune microenvironment characterized by increased CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and immune checkpoint activity. Therefore, TRIM2 holds considerable potential as a prognostic biomarker and a potential therapeutic target in BRCA. It may not only facilitate patient stratification and outcome prediction but also provide important clinical implications, particularly in the context of immunotherapy and combined targeted treatment strategies.
