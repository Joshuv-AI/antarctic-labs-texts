Aberrant degree centrality profiles during rumination in major depressive disorder
Authors: Feng‐Nan Jia, Xiao Chen, Xiang‐Dong Du, Zhen Tang, Xiao‐Yun Ma, Tian‐Tian Ning, Si‐Yun Zou, Shang‐Fu Zuo, Hui‐Xian Li, Shi‐Xian Cui, Zhao‐Yu Deng, Jia‐Lin Fu, Xiao‐Qian Fu, Yue‐Xiang Huang, Xue‐Ying Li, Tao Lian, Yi‐Fan Liao, Li‐Li Liu, Bin Lu, Yan Wang, Yu‐Wei Wang, Zi‐Han Wang, Gang Ye, Xin‐Zhu Zhang, Hong‐Liang Zhu, Chuan‐Sheng Quan, Hong‐Yan Sun, Chao‐Gan Yan, Yan‐Song Liu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractRumination is closely linked to the onset and maintenance of major depressive disorder (MDD). Prior neuroimaging studies have identified the association between self‐reported rumination trait and the functional coupling among a network of brain regions using resting‐state functional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). However, little is known about the underlying neural circuitry mechanism during active rumination in MDD. Degree centrality (DC) is a simple metric to denote network integration, which is critical for higher‐order psychological processes such as rumination. During an MRI scan, individuals with MDD (N = 45) and healthy controls (HC, N = 46) completed a rumination state task. We examined the interaction effect between the group (MDD vs. HC) and condition (rumination vs. distraction) on vertex‐wise DC. We further characterized the identified brain region's functional involvement with Neurosynth and BrainMap. Network‐wise seed‐based functional connectivity (FC) analysis was also conducted for the identified region of interest. Finally, exploratory correlation analysis was conducted between the identified region of interest's network FCs and self‐reported in‐scanner affect levels. We found that a left superior frontal gyrus (SFG) region, generally overlapped with the frontal eye field, showed a significant interaction effect. Further analysis revealed its involvement with executive functions. FCs between this region, the frontoparietal, and the dorsal attention network (DAN) also showed significant interaction effects. Furthermore, its FC to DAN during distraction showed a marginally significant negative association with in‐scanner affect level at the baseline. Our results implicated an essential role of the left SFG in the rumination's underlying neural circuitry mechanism in MDD and provided novel evidence for the conceptualization of rumination in terms of impaired executive control.Keywords: degree centrality, dorsal attention network, executive function, fMRI, major depressive disorder, rumination

INTRODUCTION

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is one of the most burdensome psychiatric disorders worldwide, with high prevalence and recurrence rates (World Health Organization, 2017). As one of the core pathological processes of MDD, rumination is characterized by an elevated repetitive, self‐reflective, and passive focus on the depressive mood and its possible causes and consequences (Marchetti et al., 2016). Rumination has been implicated in the onset, maintenance, and recurrence of depressive episodes (Shimony et al., 2021; E. R. Watkins, 2008; Zhang et al., 2020). Despite its role in the development and maintenance of MDD, the neural underpinning of rumination remains elusive.

A growing number of resting‐state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rsfMRI) studies reported that questionnaire‐measured (e.g., ruminative response scale [RRS]) rumination trait was associated with functional connectivity (FC) among a set of brain regions. For instance, the rumination trait is related to FCs between brain nodes involved in self‐referential thinking, including the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and the posterior cingulated cortex (PCC), as well as brain regions implicated in the executive function, including the dorsal lateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) and regions within the superior frontal gyrus (SFG), such as the frontal eye field (FEF) (Bessette et al., 2018; Feurer et al., 2021; Jacob et al., 2019; Philippi et al., 2018; Philippi et al., 2020; Provenzano et al., 2021; Satyshur et al., 2018). Of note, these brain regions are critical nodes from three large‐scale brain networks: the default mode network (DMN), including mPFC and PCC; the frontoparietal network (FPN), including dlPFC; and the dorsal attention network (DAN), including SFG regions such as FEF. The DMN has been frequently implicated in self‐related cognition, spontaneous thought, and autobiographical memory (Andrews‐Hanna, 2012; Andrews‐Hanna et al., 2014). Moreover, recent evidence indicated that DMN could be further divided into three different subsystems, corresponding to different nodes and psychological processes (Andrews‐Hanna et al., 2010; Andrews‐Hanna et al., 2014).

Substantial evidence has linked rumination with deficits in executive control over negative information (Gotlib & Joormann, 2010; E. R. Watkins & Roberts, 2020). Executive control is conceptualized as high‐level cognitive processes exerting control over lower‐level ones, which is necessary for goal‐directed behavior (Yang et al., 2017). It is proposed to consist of three separable but interrelated processes: set shifting, inhibition, and updating in working memory (Miyake et al., 2000). Studies have shown that experimentally inducing state rumination impairs performance on executive function tasks (E. Watkins & Brown, 2002; Whitmer & Gotlib, 2013). Meanwhile, difficulties with attentional switching and cognitive inflexibility have been consistently linked to trait rumination, not only in community samples of adults (X. Chen et al., 2016; Mennies et al., 2021; Zetsche et al., 2018), but also in adolescents (Wagner et al., 2015). Although specific cognitive control functions have been ascribed to localized brain regions or individual networks (Egner, 2017), recent evidence has highlighted the notion that cognitive control is implemented by dynamic interactions among networks embedded in a global brain architecture (Menon & D'Esposito, 2022). Both FPN and DAN play an essential role in the top‐down cognitive control processes (Christoff et al., 2016), and their interaction with DMN is one of the critical features of brain intrinsic functional architecture (M. D. Fox & Raichle, 2007). Thus, the impaired cognitive control FPN and DAN exert on DMN may lead to elevated rumination in MDD (Christoff et al., 2016; Marchetti et al., 2016).

However, the neural substrates of thinking during “rest” in previous rsfMRI studies could bear substantial differences from thinking during an active rumination (Berman et al., 2014). To allow for a direct assessment of the neural processes related to depressive rumination, researchers have developed a rumination induction task (Cooney et al., 2010), including statements eliciting abstract self‐focus (rumination) and concrete objective images (distraction) as a control condition. Using this task, prior task‐based fMRI studies have found elevated activation during rumination as compared to distraction in mPFC, PCC, subgenual anterior cingulate cortex, parahippocampus, and amygdala in adult (Cooney et al., 2010; Johnson et al., 2009) and adolescent (Burkhouse et al., 2016) MDD patients. Recent meta‐analysis evidence has demonstrated enhanced activation during rumination in the DMN (H. X. Zhou et al., 2020). Nevertheless, the FC patterns among these brain regions during active rumination are still unknown. To address this issue, we modified the rumination induction task to induce participants into a continuous rumination state, which allows for calculating the FC of BOLD time series from different brain regions. Using this paradigm, we found a reduced FC within the DMN and distinct FCs among DMN subsystems in a group of healthy adults during rumination. Next, we applied a novel metric, stability (L. Li et al., 2020), to characterize the similarity of FC profiles over time during rumination and found a reduced stability in DMN and an enhanced stability in the FPN during rumination (X. Chen et al., 2020; X. Chen & Yan, 2021).

A natural and robust framework to understand the network characteristics of the brain is the graph‐theoretic approach, which formalizes the brain as a mathematical object consisting of nodes (brain regions) and edges (FCs among nodes) (Rubinov & Sporns, 2010). Degree centrality (DC) is a graph‐theoretic measure of a node's importance or influence based on the number of connections it has with other nodes (Buckner et al., 2009). Existing rsfMRI studies have found abnormal DCs in dlPFC, pre and post‐central gyrus, parahippocampus, insular, and inferior parietal lobe in MDD patients (J. Li et al., 2019; Sheng et al., 2018; C. G. Yan et al., 2019; Zheng et al., 2022; W. Zhou et al., 2021). Of note, Chen, Wang, and Ding (2022) found that MDD patients were characterized by reduced DC in the left insula, which was negatively associated with self‐reported rumination.

Here, we sought to apply the rumination state paradigm to a group of MDD patients and directly examine the DC profiles throughout the entire surface. We hypothesized that MDD patients would show abnormal DC profiles during rumination compared to HCs and dissociated FCs among DMN, DAN, and FPN.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants

We recruited outpatient and inpatient adults with MDD from Guangji Hospital in Suzhou, Jiangsu, China. A group of HCs were recruited through advertisements from the local community. All MDD patients underwent a 17‐item HAMD evaluation. Shared exclusion criteria for MDD patients and HCs included: (1) acute physical illness; (2) contraindications to MRI scanning; (3) history of alcohol or substance abuse; (4) pregnancy or breast‐feeding; (5) chronic neurological disorders; (6) head trauma; and (7) left‐handedness. MDD patients were included if they met the following criteria: (1) aged between 18 and 45 years; (2) met the criteria of DSM‐5 according to a structured clinical interview evaluated by a trained rater; and (3) 17‐item HAMD score ≥17. The exclusion criteria for MDD patients also include bipolar disorder, any psychotic disorder or current psychotic symptoms, intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, delirium, and neurocognitive disorder. The exclusion criteria for HCs also include any lifetime psychiatric disorder. Informed consent was signed before any behavioral or neuroimaging data was collected. The local institutional review board approved the study protocol.

We initially screened a total of 139 subjects. Thirteen patients and six HCs were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria. Sixty‐three MDD patients and fifty‐seven HCs participated in the study. We cleaned the data before performing statistical analysis. One patient was excluded for failing to complete the scan. One patient and two HCs were discarded due to missing demographic data. Thirteen patients and nine HCs showed excessive head motion (mean framewise displacement > .2 mm) and were excluded. We excluded one patient who reported to be right‐handed in the screening phase but was shown to be left‐handed according to the Chinese version of the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (Oldfield, 1971). Two patients were discarded because their MRI images' phase coding direction differed from the rest of the sample (Figure S1). The final sample consisted of 45 MDD patients (33 females, mean age = 26.09) and 46 HCs (35 females, mean age = 29.22, see Table 1). No significant difference regarding sex ratio (𝝌2 = .091, p = .763), age (t = −1.596, p = .114), and head motion (rumination: t = 1.322, p = .190; distraction: t = .785, p = .435) was found between MDD patients and HCs (Table 1).

Rumination state task

We administrated a rumination state task using E‐Prime 2.0 software (Psychology Software Tools, Pittsburgh, PA) (Schneider et al., 2002). This task was previously validated in healthy adults (X. Chen et al., 2020; X. Chen & Yan, 2021). Briefly speaking, subjects underwent four sessions: one resting state, one negative autobiographical memory state, one rumination state, and one distraction state (the order of rumination and distraction states were counterbalanced across subjects).

Before scanning, subjects were briefed on the protocols of the paradigm. They were then prompted to recall four adverse autobiographical events and generate keywords for each. During the resting state session, a “+” was presented on the screen, and the participants were simply asked to “fixate on the cross and keep awake.” The pre‐generated keywords were presented in the negative autobiographical memory state. The subjects were prompted to generally recall the event corresponding to the keywords on the screen. In the rumination state, prompts (e.g., “Think: Why can't I handle things better in these events I just remembered?”) adapted from the rumination inducement task (Cooney et al., 2010; Sonja Lyubomirsky & Nolen‐Hoeksema, 1995) and RRS (S. Nolen‐Hoeksema, 1991) were presented. These prompts were designed to help subjects reflect on themselves, like spontaneous responses when distressed. The distraction state served as the control condition, in which prompts like “Think about: A train stopped at a station” were presented. Subjects were asked to imagine and focus on external topics according to the current prompt as vividly as possible. Each session lasted 8 min, containing four prompts (each lasted 2 min, Figure 1a, Table S1).

Measures

We evaluated participants' tendency to ruminate with the 22‐item RRS (Susan Nolen‐Hoeksema & Morrow, 1991). Previous research has identified two factors for this scale: “brooding” and “reflection.” Brooding is the maladaptive rumination factor, while reflection is a general interest in oneself and might be less harmful to one's mental health (Treynor, 2003).

Subjects were asked to report their mood levels before the resting state and after each session using a 9‐point Likert scale (1 = very unhappy, 9 = very happy). Subjects' self‐reported mood levels after each session were analyzed using R (R Core Team, 2013) with rstatix (Kassambara, 2020). We performed a two‐way ANOVA to examine the effect of Group (random effect, MDD patients vs. HCs) and Condition (fixed effect, before and after the resting state, negative memory, rumination, and distraction). Post hoc pairwise comparisons among conditions were performed using paired t tests with the Bonferroni correction.

fMRI and preprocessing

Data were acquired in a 3 T scanner (MAGNETOM Skyra, Siemens). The preprocessing of fMRI data was conducted using the toolbox for Data Processing & Analysis for Brain Imaging on Surface (DPABISurf) (C.‐G. Yan et al., 2021). For details on the imaging data acquisition and preprocessing pipeline, please refer to SI Methods.

Degree centrality

We defined DC as the sum of weights of significant connections for a given vertex (Buckner et al., 2009). Here, we conducted a vertex‐to‐vertex correlation analysis to calculate the weighted sum of positive FC (i.e., Pearson correlation of vertexes' time series of BOLD signals) that exceeded a threshold of r > .25 as the DC metric using DPABISurf (SI Methods) (Buckner et al., 2009). We further Z‐standardized (subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation of the entire surface for each vertex) and smoothed (fullwidth at halfmaximum of 6 mm) the DC metric maps.

A general linear model (GLM) was used to decompose a two‐way mixed effect ANOVA of a two‐level fixed within‐subject factor (rumination vs. distraction) and two‐level random between‐subject factor (MDD patients vs. HCs) for each vertex (Figure 1b). The head motion (mean FDJenkinson) was included as a covariate. The multiple comparison correction was performed using a permutation test with threshold‐free cluster enhancement (TFCE) (Smith & Nichols, 2009; Winkler et al., 2014), as this strategy has been shown to achieve a balance between controlling false positive rates and improving the results' reproducibility (X. Chen et al., 2018). For details, please refer to SI Methods.

Association analysis between the significant cluster and the meta‐analytic cognition dictionary

We interpreted the cognitive functions of the significant DC cluster using Neurosynth (https://neurosynth.org) (Yarkoni et al., 2011) and BrainMap (https://www.brainmap.org) (P. T. Fox & Lancaster, 2002; Lu et al., 2022). Neurosynth and BrainMap are two online databases with a massive collection of previous coordinate‐based brain imaging studies (e.g., task‐based fMRI studies) (Lu et al., 2022). Cognitive functions and task paradigms previously found to be related to the regions of interest (ROI) could be easily obtained for interpretation with these two databases. For details, please refer to SI Methods.

FC analysis

To quantify the whole‐brain network connections to the region showing a significant interaction effect, we defined brain networks using the 400‐parcellation functional atlas of Schaefer et al. (2018). We first defined the brain area showing a significant effect (the left SFG cluster, cluster size: 41 vertices) as the ROI. Then, the mean time series of BOLD signals in the ROI was calculated. The Pearson correlations between all other vertices and ROI's mean time series were then calculated as FC maps and underwent Fisher's r‐to‐z‐transformation. Finally, the average FC of each brain network was extracted (for details, see SI Methods). Similarly, we constructed a GLM to examine the interaction effect regarding the FCs between the significant area and brain networks. The multiple comparison correction was performed using the Bonferroni correction (p < .05/9).

Relationship between network‐wise FC of SFG and self‐reported affect levels as well as questionnaire‐measured rumination trait

We calculated the Pearson correlations between the left SFG's network‐wised FCs that showed significant interaction effects and self‐reported mood levels at the baseline and after rumination/distraction. The multiple comparison correction was conducted via Bonferroni correction (p < .05/12). We also explored correlations between left SFG's network‐wised FCs and questionnaire‐measured rumination trait.

Validation analyses

Given the level of affect could be a confounding factor of the potential interaction effect between the rumination and distraction conditions, we further conducted a validation analysis on the adverse autobiographical memory condition and the rumination condition. Specifically, we performed a two‐way mixed effect ANOVA of a two‐level fixed within‐subject factor (rumination vs. negative autobiographical memory) and a two‐level random between‐subject factor (MDD patients vs. HCs), like the primary analysis. We selected drug naïve patients and patients with medication and conducted mixed effect analysis to validate the potential medication effect on our main findings. Furthermore, we explored the temporal dynamic features (coefficients of variation, CV, of the dynamic FC) of the region showing significant interaction effects to demonstrate that neuroimaging data from the present research may bear intriguing time‐varying brain properties (for details, please refer to the supplementary materials).

RESULTS

Self‐reported mood levels

A two‐way ANOVA revealed that both group (F = 28.613, p < .001, 𝜼2 = .135) and condition (F = 26.970, p < .001, 𝜼2 = .135) had a significant main effect on subjects' self‐reported mood levels. No significant interaction effect between group and condition was found (F = .364, p = .768, 𝜼2 = .001). Post hoc comparisons showed that participants' affect levels after the negative memory session were significantly lower than levels after the resting state in both groups (MDD: t = −4.41, p adj < .001; HC: t = −4.13, p adj = .001). Participants' affect levels after the rumination state were also significantly lower than levels after the resting state in MDD patients (t = −3.03, p adj = .037) but not in HCs (t = −2.81, p adj > .05). Both MDD and HC groups showed no significant differences between the resting state and distraction sessions (all p adj s > .05) (Figure 2a) (Tables S2–S4).

Mixed effects of DC

The mixed effect analysis yielded a significant interaction effect of DC between the group (MDD vs. HC) and the condition (rumination vs. distraction) in the left SFG (t = −4.895, p < .001, Cohen's f 2 = .194, MNI coordinates of the peak: x = −23, y = 4, z = 47, Figure 2b). Of note, the cluster was largely overlapped with the FEF. The post hoc comparisons showed that MDD patients' DC during the rumination state was significantly reduced compared to the distraction state (t = −2.084, p = .043, Cohen's f 2 = .028), whereas the HCs showed significantly enhanced DC during rumination compared to the distraction state (t = 4.951, p < .001, Cohen's f 2 = .101). MDD patients' DC during the distraction state was significantly higher than the HCs (t = 3.127, p = .002, Cohen's f 2 = .106) (Figure 2c). This analysis also yielded a significant condition effect (rumination vs. distraction) in the right middle frontal gyrus (t = 4.783, p < .001, Cohen's f 2 = .260, MNI coordinates of the peak: x = 39, y = 44, z = 13, Figure S2). No significant group difference in DC was revealed.

Meta‐analytic functional profiles of the region showing significant interaction effects

We conducted a meta‐analysis using Neurosynth (https://neurosynth.org) to explore the psychological functions of the brain region revealed in the previous mixed effect analysis. This analysis suggested that the left SFG region primarily engaged in executive processes (e.g., selection, planning, working memory, Figure 2d). Furthermore, the BrainMap analysis showed that the region showing significant interaction effect was associated with experiment paradigms from the cognition domain, including reasoning, working memory, attention, and spatial. It was also related to perception (motion vision) and action (unspecified execution) (Figure S3).

Network‐level FC

Next, we performed a mixed‐effect analysis on the network‐wise FCs of the left SFG region. We found significant group × condition interaction effects for the FC between the left SFG and the DAN (t = −2.862, p adj = .024, Cohen's f 2 = .093), and the FPN (t = −3.258, p adj = .014, Cohen's f 2 = .121) (Figure 3). Post hoc comparisons showed that MDD patients' FC between the left SFG and the DAN during the rumination state was lower than the distraction state (t = −2.248, p = .030), whereas no difference was revealed for HC (t = 1.681, p = .100). HCs' FC between the left SFG and the FPN was significantly increased during rumination as compared to the distraction state (t = 4.491, p < .001). In contrast, no such effect was revealed in the MDD patients (t = .481, p = .633). No significant group difference was found. A similar mixed‐effect analysis was also conducted on the network‐wise FC to the brain region showing a significant condition effect. As expected, we found no significant interaction effect (all p adj s > .05). Significant condition effects were revealed in FCs of FPN (t = 3.697, p adj = .003, Cohen's f 2 = .155), VAN (t = 3.044, p adj = .028, Cohen's f 2 = .105), core subsystem of DMN (t = 3.211, p adj = .017, Cohen's f 2 = .117), and MTL subsystem of DMN (t = 3.981, p adj = .001, Cohen's f 2 = .180) (Figure S7).

Analysis of the relationship between network‐wise FC and self‐reported affect levels, as well as questionnaire‐measured rumination trait

Self‐reported affect levels of MDD patients before the initial resting state and the SFG‐DAN FC during distraction session showed a marginally significant negative correlation (r = −.431, p adj = .058). No significant correlation was revealed in HCs (Figure 4). We found no significant correlation between FC and self‐reported rumination measures after correction for multiple comparisons. Validation analysis results

We found a significant condition effect in the left dorsal mPFC (dmPFC) (t = −4.270, p < .001, Cohen's f 2 = .178, MNI coordinates of the peak: x = 16, y = 53, z = 53, Figure S4) during rumination as compared to the adverse autobiographical memory condition. Post hoc comparisons showed that both MDD patients' (t = −2.041, p = .047) and HCs' (t = −2.475, p = .017) DCs were reduced (Figure S4). No significant interaction or group effect was revealed. We found significant interaction effects of the left SFG in patients with and without medication usage (drug naïve patients: t = −3.505, p = .001, Cohen's f 2 = .219; patients with medication: t = −5.048; p < .001, Cohen's f 2 = .369; Figure S5). Analyses of the dynamic features of the SFG region's FC profiles showed various CV patterns, but no significant interaction effect was found (SI Results, Figure S8).

DISCUSSION

Rumination is a common symptom and a substantial potential recurrence risk of MDD. To understand its neuropsychological mechanisms, we characterized the DC across the entire surface during an active, continuous rumination state in MDD patients and HCs. We found a significant interaction effect regarding DC in the left SFG. Further pairwise comparisons showed that MDD patients were characterized by reduced DC in the left SFG during rumination compared with distraction state, while HC showed an opposite pattern. Meta‐analyses showed that this region is primarily involved with executive processes such as selection, planning, and working memory. Subsequently, we found significant interaction effects regarding the FCs between the left SFG cluster and the DAN and FPN, implicating an impaired executive function during rumination in MDD patients. Finally, we found a marginally significant negative association between SFG‐DAN FCs and the baseline in‐scanner affect levels in patients with MDD but not in HCs when a stringent strategy for multiple comparison correction was conducted.

Our findings add to a growing body of literature highlighting executive processes' critical role in rumination, especially in MDD patients (Christoff et al., 2016; Koster et al., 2011; Marchetti et al., 2016; Song et al., 2022). Previous task‐based fMRI studies using the rumination induction task have repetitively reported increased activity in the left SFG in the rumination condition as compared to the distraction condition (Burkhouse et al., 2016; Cooney et al., 2010; Johnson et al., 2009). The self‐reported rumination trait is related to FCs between the SFG and mPFC (Philippi et al., 2018) and amygdala (Feurer et al., 2021) in MDD patients. In the current study, the left SFG region showing significant interaction effects is mainly located in the so‐called FEF area, which is involved in the selection of visual stimuli and the control of attention (Vernet et al., 2014). It is also a “hub” region bearing dense anatomical connections with various brain regions. Quantitative analyses have revealed that this region, together with other nodes in FPN, is involved in the response inhibition (Cai et al., 2014). As a result, it may be crucial in the top‐down modulation of human visual attention and perception processes. In line with this notion, we found significant interaction effects regarding its FCs with two critical large‐scale brain networks: the DAN and FPN. DAN is mainly associated with attention processes towards the external world (Corbetta et al., 2008), while FPN is posited to be generally involved with higher cognitive control over the working memory (Niendam et al., 2012). We found that patients with MDD were characterized by reduced DC in the SFG during rumination compared with distraction, while HCs showed the opposite pattern. Notably, FCs between the SFG region and DAN, as well as FPN, were also reduced during rumination in MDD patients. Our findings indicated that the integration of brain networks involved in executive function (i.e., DAN and FPN) might be dampened in patients with MDD when they engage in an active rumination state. Therefore, the rigid and inflexible control from this abnormal cognitive control system to the self‐generated thought processes may lead to a narrowed theme of the contents of the self‐generated thought, which could be deemed as a rumination process (Christoff et al., 2016). Our findings also supported the attentional scope model of rumination. This theory denotes that a narrowed attentional scope may lead to enhanced emotional response and dampened problem‐solving, which is related to rumination (Whitmer & Gotlib, 2013). Recent behavioral findings have confirmed that people with higher rumination traits showed reduced visual attentional scope (Fang et al., 2017).

Our findings may also implicate the difference in the neural underpinnings of constructive and unconstructive repetitive thought (E. R. Watkins, 2008). As a form of unconstructive repetitive thought, rumination is unhelpful to individuals' mental health, probably due to its negative valence and an abstract level of construal. On the contrary, constructive repetitive thought is characterized by a more concrete process and thinking content oriented toward problem‐solving. Even though our healthy participants were prompted to ruminate after a negative autobiographical memory phase, they may reflect on themselves more constructively, leading to a distinct neural underpinning. A variant of cognitive‐behavioral therapy (rumination‐focused CBT, RFCBT) (E. Watkins et al., 2007) has been designed to help patients adopt a more adaptive thinking style. Studies, including randomized clinical trials, have demonstrated its superiority in reducing depressive symptoms over medication alone (E. R. Watkins et al., 2011). Moreover, the present study indicates a possibility to reverse the unconstructive repetitive thought (i.e., rumination) by targeting the key node of its brain mechanism using non‐invasive neuromodulation techniques such as transcranial magnetic stimulation and transcranial direct current stimulation (Chou et al., 2020).

We failed to find any significant case–control difference or interaction effect regarding the DMN, which seems inconsistent with previous findings. One recent meta‐analysis (H. X. Zhou et al., 2020) found that the activation of DMN was elevated during rumination, but this study only summarized studies conducted in healthy controls. Earlier small sample‐sized fMRI studies (Greicius et al., 2007; Zhu et al., 2012) showed that MDD patients were characterized with abnormally increased FC within DMN, which could be related to questionnaire‐measured rumination trait (Kaiser et al., 2015). However, recent large‐scale, multi‐sited studies (C. G. Yan et al., 2019) found a decreased DMN FC in MDD patients. Furthermore, another recent study failed to replicate the association between DMN FC and rumination with an independent larger group (N MDD = 115) of medication‐free MDD patients (Tozzi et al., 2020). Together with these results, our present findings showed that the involvement of DMN in rumination in healthy adults could not be readily generated in the clinical sample and implicated a complex association between DMN activity and rumination.

Subjects' self‐reported mood levels largely replicated our previous research in healthy adults (X. Chen et al., 2020) and partially supported the validation of the paradigm. Not surprisingly, MDD patients' mood levels were significantly lower than HCs. After the negative autobiographical memory condition, both groups showed significantly lower self‐reported emotional levels than the resting state. This lower affect level was sustained after the rumination session. These results proved the well‐established relationship between rumination and a depressed mood (S. Nolen‐Hoeksema, 1991). The depressed mood leads to rumination, which sustains the depressed mood. Our findings also revealed that subjective mood levels were associated with network‐wised FCs between the SFG cluster and DAN. The mood depends on a complex network encompassing interception, emotional state, and memory (Kragel et al., 2018) and is thus speculated to arise from a complex set of network interactions. Researchers have demonstrated that it is possible to track an individual's mood fluctuations and classify different mood categories using the whole brain FC pattern (Mirchi et al., 2019; Saarimäki et al., 2022). In MDD, the pathophysiological mechanism between the FC profiles and the depressive symptoms could be the emotion dysregulation (Rive et al., 2013). Thus, the enhanced FCs between the SFG cluster and DAN/FPN may represent compensation for the dysfunctional emotional regulation processes (Rive et al., 2013), which led to lower affect levels.

The continuous, self‐administrated state paradigm applied in the present research has been previously applied in healthy adults (X. Chen et al., 2020; X. Chen & Yan, 2021; Milazzo et al., 2016), and current and remitted MDD patients (Berman et al., 2014; Zamoscik et al., 2014). This paradigm is similar to the typical resting‐state paradigm (Biswal et al., 1995). Accordingly, various analytical frameworks from rsfMRI can be readily brought to the analyses of fMRI data obtained with our “rumination state” paradigm. Furthermore, such an integrated paradigm has been proposed to characterize more considerable variance compared to the typical resting state paradigm and might generate more reliable findings, which has been partially proved previously with a design of repeated measures (X. Chen et al., 2020). Multiple large‐scale studies with thousands of MDD patients have found modest effect sizes regarding the neuroimaging abnormalities in MDD compared with HCs (Chen, Lu, et al., 2022; Chen, Wang, & Ding, 2022; Schmaal et al., 2020; Thompson et al., 2020; C. G. Yan et al., 2019). In line with this notion, we failed to find any significant group effect in the present study, probably due to the stringent multiple comparison correction strategy (permutation test with TFCE) used in the current study (X. Chen et al., 2018). The statistical power of the present study might not be able to detect the nuanced potential case–control difference. A more constrained mental state and a carefully designed control condition might better highlight the potential group differences and boost the effect size.

One concern of the rumination‐distraction contrast is the confounding emotion effect. Accordingly, we conducted a validation analysis using the negative autobiographical memory session as a control. We found reduced DC in dmPFC in the rumination condition compared to the negative autobiographical memory condition, but no significant interaction effect was revealed. Some previous studies applied the negative autobiographical memory condition as a rumination induction (Berman et al., 2014; Milazzo et al., 2016). Our results highlighted the different neural underpinnings between these two conditions. Nevertheless, rumination is closely related to autobiographical memory and has been posited to reduce the specificity of autobiographical memory (i.e., the over‐general autobiographical memory) by adopting an abstract, analytical, and evaluative processing style (Sumner, 2012). As a result, even though the negative autobiographical memory is like rumination in terms of affect valence, it may not be the ideal control condition. Future research may be required to develop a novel control condition that bears a negative affect but is unrelated to rumination. Our validation analysis of the potential medication effects showed that our main findings might not be the effects of antidepressant medications. Indeed, rumination is one of the residual symptoms after antidepressant medication treatments (Riso et al., 2003). Our results further highlighted the necessity of developing psychotherapy targeting the rumination (Roberts et al., 2021). Only MDD patients in the acute phase were included in the current study, so we cannot rule out the possibility that the revealed aberrant DC profiles associated with rumination were solely a symptom alongside depression. Rumination is usually conceptualized as a response style to distress (S. Lyubomirsky et al., 2015). Nevertheless, studies utilizing the RRS have provided evidence that the tendency to ruminate is sufficiently stable to be considered a trait‐like characteristic, remaining consistent even in the face of changes in levels of negative affect (S. Nolen‐Hoeksema et al., 2008). For example, higher levels of rumination tendency have been found to predict the onset of depressive episodes in non‐depressed people (S. Nolen‐Hoeksema, 2000). Furthermore, several studies reported associations between the levels of rumination trait in remitted MDD patients and brain activity during tasks and rest (Bessette et al., 2018; Delaveau et al., 2017; Nejad et al., 2019). Bessette et al. (2018) found elevated FC between DMN and FPN in remitted MDD patients and an association between DMN‐FPN FC and rumination trait. Therefore, rumination might not merely be a symptom of depressed mood but also a relatively stable tendency in healthy populations and patients in a euthymic phase.

The findings of our study must be considered with respect to several limitations. Although the present study's sample size is relatively large, further validation with a larger sample size and various ethnic groups is needed. Although the rumination state paradigm might generate a larger effect size and thus require fewer subjects to produce a reliable result, a thorough validation is necessary before this paradigm can be put into clinical usage. DC is a simple and commonly used graph theoretical metric. However, it only considers positive FCs in a brain network and may ignore some critical information encoded by the sign. A positive correlation may suggest cooperation and integration, whereas a negative correlation may indicate competition and segregation (Fornito et al., 2013). Future studies may leverage novel graph theoretical metrics that consider both positive and negative FCs. We only prompted subjects to report their emotions after each session to indirectly ensure their involvement in our experimental inducements. Recently, a so‐called “think‐aloud” paradigm has been developed to characterize ongoing spontaneous thoughts and their corresponding neural underpinnings (H. X. Li et al., 2022; Raffaelli et al., 2021). Applying this paradigm to the rumination state task in MDD patients could shed new light on the neural mechanisms of rumination. The current findings were based on the “static” method for characterizing brain network properties. Studies have shown the importance of examining the fluctuations of network FCs, which has been proposed as essential for adaptation to the changing environment and ongoing human cognition (Kaiser et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2020). The continuous, state‐like design of the current study provided a suitable repertoire of brain activities for such dynamic analyses. We have previously shown that the stability of FC profiles was altered during an active rumination state (X. Chen & Yan, 2021). Future studies could leverage such analytical frameworks to uncover the time‐varying properties during active rumination.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, we applied a rumination state paradigm in a group of clinical MDD patients and corresponding HCs to characterize the whole‐surface DC profiles during rumination. Results showed significant interaction effects in the left SFG, a key node of DAN. Meta‐analyses associated it with several executive processes, including selection, planning, working memory, and so forth. Furthermore, we found significant interaction effects regarding its FCs with the DAN and FPN. Correlation analysis implicated the relationship between the baseline in‐scanner affect levels and the SFG region's FCs with DAN during the distraction state. These findings highlight the essential role of cognitive control in the neuropathological mechanisms underlying rumination in clinical MDD patients.
