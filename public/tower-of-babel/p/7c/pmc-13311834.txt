Chicken-derived Lactobacillus strains alleviate Mycoplasma gallisepticum infection and improve growth performance in broilers
Authors: Qiao Guo, Zhenghao Che, Fuli Hu, Jiahui Wu, Shan Lv, Wenqing Zhao, Shiying Li, Yingjie Wang, Xiuli Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Restrictions on antibiotic use in poultry production have increased interest in probiotic alternatives for controlling infectious diseases. This study isolated chicken-derived Lactobacillus strains and evaluated their probiotic properties and protective effects against Mycoplasma gallisepticum (MG) infection in broilers. A total of 81 isolates were recovered from different intestinal segments of healthy chickens and assigned to four genera and six species. After screening for acid tolerance and broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, four representative strains were selected for further study: Lactobacillus crispatus Z2, L. crispatus Z18, Ligilactobacillus agilis Z22, and Ligilactobacillus salivarius M12. In vitro, cell-free supernatants (CFS) from these strains reduced MG colony formation and significantly decreased MG adhesion to chicken alveolar epithelial type II cells (P < 0.05). At non-cytotoxic concentrations, CFS treatment also reduced mgc2 copy number and gapA expression in MG-infected cells (P < 0.05). The selected strains further showed favorable probiotic characteristics, including rapid growth, acidification capacity, and adhesion to intestinal epithelial cells. For in vivo evaluation, broilers were experimentally challenged with MG and assigned to different probiotic intervention groups, with tiamulin used as an antimicrobial control. Probiotic administration alleviated MG-associated growth impairment in broilers. Strains Z2 and Z22 significantly increased final body weight, whereas Z2, Z22, and M12 improved body weight gain during the late growth phase (P < 0.05). All four strains reduced feed conversion ratio during early growth, and Z22 also improved overall feed efficiency (P < 0.05). Histopathological analysis showed that probiotic treatment reduced inflammatory infiltration in the lung and preserved cecal epithelial integrity. TUNEL staining further demonstrated reduced pulmonary apoptosis after probiotic intervention (P < 0.05). Consistent with these effects, probiotic treatment decreased pulmonary MG burden, as reflected by lower mgc2 copy number, and downregulated the adhesion-related gene gapA in lung tissue (P < 0.05). These results support chicken-derived Lactobacillus strains as candidate antibiotic alternatives for controlling MG infection and improving broiler performance.

Introduction

Mycoplasma gallisepticum (MG) is an important respiratory pathogen in chickens and a major cause of chronic respiratory disease in commercial poultry production (Wang et al., 2021, 2024b). MG infection is associated with impaired growth performance, reduced feed efficiency, decreased egg production, and increased mortality under severe or complicated field conditions (Li, et al., 2025). In addition to direct production losses, MG can predispose birds to secondary infections and aggravate respiratory pathology, thereby further compromising flock health and productivity (Wang et al., 2021, 2024b). Consequently, MG remains a priority target for disease control in poultry production.

Control of MG infection has traditionally relied on antimicrobials, particularly macrolides and related agents (Kleven, 2008). Although these treatments can reduce clinical signs and production losses, repeated or prolonged use increases the risk of antimicrobial resistance, reduced treatment efficacy, and antibiotic residues in poultry products. Restrictions on antimicrobial use in food-producing animals, including the ban on antibiotic growth promoters in the European Union, have accelerated the search for non-antibiotic strategies to maintain poultry health and productivity (Noohi, et al., 2021). Therefore, alternative approaches that can mitigate MG infection while reducing reliance on antibiotics are urgently needed.

Modulation of the intestinal microbiota has emerged as a promising strategy for improving poultry health. The avian gut microbiota contributes to nutrient utilization, epithelial barrier function, and immune regulation, and may also influence host resistance to respiratory infections through gut–lung communication(İncili, et al., 2022; Yang, et al., 2017). Probiotics, particularly lactic acid bacteria (LAB), are therefore of increasing interest in antibiotic-reduction programs. LAB are common commensals of the chicken gastrointestinal tract and may benefit the host through competitive exclusion of pathogens, production of organic acids and antimicrobial compounds, and modulation of mucosal immune responses(Noohi et al., 2021; Patterson and Burkholder, 2003). Previous studies have shown that selected lactobacilli can improve growth performance and reduce pathogen colonization in chickens, including Salmonella and respiratory disease-associated pathogens(Brown and Clarke, 2017; Darbandi, et al., 2022).

Despite these benefits, probiotic activity is highly strain-specific and cannot be inferred solely from taxonomic identity. Host-derived isolates may be better adapted to the physicochemical and ecological conditions of the target host, potentially improving their colonization, persistence, and functional compatibility (Johnson, et al., 2023). In chickens, homologous Lactobacillus strains have been reported to colonize more efficiently and exert more favorable growth-promoting effects than strains from non-avian hosts (Johnson et al., 2023; Shi, et al., 2022). However, host origin alone does not guarantee efficacy; candidate strains must be systematically evaluated for gastrointestinal tolerance, antimicrobial activity, epithelial adhesion, and in vivo benefits.

In the present study, we aimed to identify chicken-derived lactobacilli with inhibitory activity against MG and to evaluate their probiotic potential. We isolated LAB from healthy chickens, screened them for acid tolerance and antimicrobial activity, selected representative strains for functional characterization, and assessed their effects in an MG challenge model in broilers. This study provides a basis for developing host-adapted probiotic tools for MG control and performance improvement in poultry production.

Materials and methods

Isolation and screening of bacterial strains

Intestinal contents were collected aseptically from the duodenum, jejunum, ileum, cecum, and rectum of clinically healthy chickens as previously described (Reuben, et al., 2019). Samples were suspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), serially diluted, and plated on de Man–Rogosa–Sharpe (MRS) agar supplemented with calcium carbonate. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 48 h under aerobic conditions. Colonies surrounded by clear halos, indicative of acid production and calcium carbonate dissolution, were selected and purified by repeated streaking to obtain single-colony isolates (Kang, et al., 2020).

Purified isolates were examined by Gram staining and light microscopy. Gram-positive rods were selected for molecular identification. Genomic DNA was extracted using standard procedures, and the nearly full-length 16S rRNA gene was amplified with universal primers 27F and 1492R. PCR products were sequenced by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Taxonomic assignment was performed by BLAST analysis against the NCBI 16S rRNA gene database (Ding, et al., 2017).

Confirmed isolates were cultured in MRS broth at 37°C for 24 h. Glycerol stocks were prepared in MRS broth containing 20% (v/v) glycerol and stored at −80°C. Before phenotypic testing, strains were subcultured three consecutive times to ensure stable growth and viability.

Acid tolerance assay

To assess tolerance to acidic conditions, overnight cultures were inoculated (2%, v/v) into fresh MRS broth adjusted to pH 6.5 (control), pH 4.0, or pH 3.0. Cultures were incubated at 37°C for 3 h. Viable counts were determined by serial dilution and plating. For the control group, colony-forming units (CFU) were measured at 0 h; for acid-treated groups, CFU were determined after 3 h of exposure. Survival rates at pH 3.0 and pH 4.0 were calculated relative to the initial viable counts under control conditions(Jose, et al., 2015).

Antimicrobial activity assays

Preparation of cell-free supernatants (CFS)

Each isolate was inoculated (2%, v/v) into MRS broth and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Cultures were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 5 min to remove bacterial cells. Supernatants were collected and passed through 0.22 μm pore-size membrane filters to obtain sterile cell-free supernatants (CFS). Unless otherwise specified, freshly prepared CFS was used for antimicrobial and cell-based assays.

Mycoplasma gallisepticum (MG), Mycoplasma synoviae (MS), Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Salmonella spp. were obtained from the Key Laboratory of Preventive Veterinary Microbiology, Huazhong Agricultural University.

Anti-mycoplasma activity against MG and MS

MG and MS were cultured in FM-4 broth and modified Frey broth, respectively, and adjusted to 1 × 10⁵ color-changing units (CCU)/mL. For each assay, 0.8 mL of fresh broth was mixed with 0.1 mL of pathogen suspension and 0.1 mL of CFS. In control groups, an equal volume of sterile MRS broth replaced the CFS(Wang, et al., 2023).

Baseline CCU values were determined for positive control cultures at 0 h. After incubation at 37°C for 24 h, CCU values were reassessed. Cultures were then maintained for up to two weeks, and final CCU values were estimated based on the characteristic color change of the medium. Antimicrobial activity was interpreted as follows: CCU = 0, bactericidal effect; 0 < CCU < 4, marked inhibition; CCU ≥ 4, weak or no inhibition.

Agar well diffusion assay

To evaluate activity against S. aureus, E. coli, and Salmonella spp., indicator bacteria (1 × 10⁶ CFU/mL) were incorporated into LB agar maintained at approximately 45°C and poured into plates. After solidification, wells (∼8 mm diameter) were punched into the agar and filled with 100 μL of CFS. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 48 h, and inhibition zone diameters were measured in millimeters(Guan, et al., 2024).

Solid medium MG inhibition assay

MG suspensions were evenly spread onto solid FM-4 agar. After absorption of the inoculum, 0.1 μL of CFS was applied onto the agar surface. Plates were incubated at 37°C for up to two weeks. MG growth in the vicinity of the application site was examined microscopically to assess inhibitory effects.

Primary AEC II cell culture and treatments

Primary chicken alveolar epithelial type II (AEC II) cells were isolated and maintained as previously described (Wang, et al., 2022). For in vitro infection assays, cells were assigned to three groups: (i) untreated control (B); (ii) MG infection group (M), in which cells were exposed to MG (100 μL, 1 × 10⁹ CCU/mL); and (iii) CFS treatment group, in which MG-infected cells were subsequently treated with cell-free supernatants (CFS) from selected lactobacilli.

To quantify the effect of CFS on MG adhesion, AEC II cells were seeded in 96-well plates and infected with fluorescently labeled MG. After 6 h or 18 h of incubation, culture supernatants were removed and monolayers were washed three times with PBS to eliminate non-adherent bacteria. Fluorescence intensity associated with adherent MG was measured at 490 nm (A). The initial fluorescence of the inoculum was recorded separately (B). Adhesion was expressed as A/B × 100%, providing a relative measure of MG attachment.

Cell proliferation and cytotoxicity assay

Chicken type II alveolar epithelial (AEC II) cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 2 × 10⁴ cells per well and allowed to adhere for 12 h. To assess potential cytotoxicity, cells were exposed to CFS at final concentrations ranging from 0.188% to 1.0% (v/v) for 24 h.

Cell viability was measured using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance at 450 nm was recorded with a microplate reader. Viability was expressed as a percentage relative to untreated controls. Concentrations that did not significantly reduce viability were used in subsequent infection assays.

Fluorescent labeling of bacterial strains

For adhesion and interaction studies, MG cultures were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min to collect bacterial pellets. Pellets were resuspended in PKH67 labeling solution and incubated at 37°C for 2 h in the dark. Labeled bacteria were washed three times with sterile PBS to remove unbound dye and resuspended in FM-4 medium. Preparations were stored at −80°C until use.

For in vivo adhesion experiments, candidate probiotic strains were labeled with carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (cFDA-SE) using a comparable protocol(Zhao, et al., 2022).

DNA/RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Genomic DNA was extracted from infected or control AEC II cells and from tissue samples using a commercial genomic DNA extraction kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol Universal Reagent (TIANGEN, Beijing, China).

RNA concentration and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically (A260/A280 ratio). Equal amounts of RNA were reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a first-strand cDNA synthesis kit (TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table 1). Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH or β-actin, and relative expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. For bacterial burden analysis, MG-specific gene copy numbers were determined by qPCR using genomic DNA as template.

Growth curve analysis

After three successive subcultures, candidate strains were inoculated (2%, v/v) into MRS broth and incubated at 37°C for 36 h. At defined time points, samples were collected to determine viable counts (CFU), optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀), and culture pH. Growth curves were constructed from these parameters to characterize growth kinetics and acidification profiles(Shao, et al., 2023).

Adhesion assay to intestinal epithelial cells

Primary chicken intestinal epithelial cells were prepared as previously described (Kaiser, et al., 2016). Cells were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured to approximately 90% confluence. Prior to infection, monolayers were washed three times with sterile PBS and maintained in antibiotic-free medium.

Log-phase cultures of candidate strains were added at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10:1 (bacteria: cells) and incubated for 3 h at 37°C. Non-adherent bacteria were removed by washing with PBS. Cells were then lysed with 0.1% Triton X-100 (0.5 mL per well) for 10 min at room temperature. Lysates were serially diluted and plated on MRS agar for CFU enumeration after 48 h of incubation.

Wells containing bacteria but no epithelial cells served as input controls. Adhesion was calculated as: Adhesion rate (%) = (recovered CFU from epithelial cell wells / CFU from control wells) × 100%(Cao, et al., 2019).

In vivo adhesion assay

Selected strains were labeled with carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (cFDA-SE) and adjusted to 1 × 10¹⁰ CFU/mL. Chickens received 1 mL of labeled bacteria by oral gavage. Twelve hours later, intestinal samples were collected to assess bacterial localization and colonization as previously described (Zhao et al., 2022).

Experimental animals and treatments

A total of 450 clinically healthy Cobb broiler chickens were housed at the Experimental Animal Center of Huazhong Agricultural University. Birds were maintained under standard broiler husbandry conditions with free access to feed and water throughout the trial. The basal diet was formulated to meet the nutritional requirements of broilers and contained no antibiotics except for the tiamulin treatment specified in the experimental design. After a 5-day acclimation period, birds were randomly assigned to 15 treatment groups, with 30 birds per group and 6 replicate pens per group (5 birds per replicate). The experimental design, including probiotic administration, MG challenge, and tiamulin treatment schedules, is shown in Fig. 1. Body weight and feed intake were recorded at the indicated time points. Experimental design and treatment schedule for the feeding and MG challenge study.

Birds were reared in environmentally controlled rooms under routine broiler management conditions. Temperature, ventilation, lighting, and stocking density were managed according to standard husbandry practices. All birds were fed a basal diet formulated to meet or exceed the nutrient requirements of broilers, and no antibiotics were included in the diet unless specified in the treatment design.

Body weight and feed intake were recorded at the indicated time points. Body weight gain was calculated as the difference in body weight between two consecutive measurement points. Feed conversion ratio was calculated as feed intake divided by body weight gain. Mortality was recorded daily, and feed conversion ratio was adjusted when necessary. At the end of the trial, lung and intestinal tissues were collected for microbiological, molecular, and histological analyses.

Histopathological analysis

Lung and intestinal tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C overnight, dehydrated in graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4–5 μm. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined under light microscopy for evaluation of tissue architecture and inflammatory changes.

TUNEL assay

Apoptosis in AEC II cells was assessed using a commercial TUNEL kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and processed according to the manufacturer's protocol. Fluorescent images were captured with a Nikon Eclipse Ti fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). The apoptotic index was calculated as the percentage of TUNEL-positive nuclei relative to total nuclei.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software. Differences among groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Isolation and Identification of lactic acid bacteria

Intestinal contents from five anatomical segments (duodenum, jejunum, ileum, cecum, and rectum) of healthy chickens were plated on MRS agar supplemented with calcium carbonate. Colonies surrounded by clear halos, indicative of acid production, were selected and purified by repeated streaking. Gram staining and light microscopy confirmed that the selected isolates were Gram-positive rods.

Species identification was performed by amplification and sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene, followed by BLAST analysis. In total, 81 lactic acid–producing isolates were recovered. Based on 16S rRNA gene sequences, these isolates were assigned to four genera and six species: LatiLactobacillus sakei (n = 4), Limosilactobacillus reuteri (n = 7), Lactobacillus johnsonii (n = 2), Lactobacillus crispatus (n = 10), Ligilactobacillus agilis (n = 11), and Ligilactobacillus salivarius (n = 47) (Supplementary Table 2). L. salivarius was the most abundant species among the isolates. The distribution of species according to intestinal origin is summarized in Supplementary Table 2. Representative colony morphology on MRS agar and Gram-stained cellular morphology are shown in Fig. 2. Screening of antimicrobial activity

We then evaluated antimicrobial activity using cell-free supernatants (CFS) against five poultry-associated pathogens: Mycoplasma gallisepticum (MG), Mycoplasma synoviae (MS), Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Salmonella spp. Among the four Latilactobacillus sakei isolates, none demonstrated bactericidal activity against MG or MS. Inhibition zones against S. aureus were <12 mm for all strains. However, strains S6, S7, and S18 produced inhibition zones larger than 12 mm against both E. coli and Salmonella, whereas strain S2 showed inhibitory activity only against Salmonella (Supplementary Table 4). Within the Limosilactobacillus reuteri group, strain H15 exhibited bactericidal activity against MS but not MG. Strains H15, M7, and K28 generated inhibition zones >12 mm against E. coli and Salmonella, while strain M13 inhibited only Salmonella (Supplementary Table 4). The two Lactobacillus johnsonii isolates showed no bactericidal activity against MG or MS and produced inhibition zones <12 mm against S. aureus, but both inhibited E. coli and Salmonella with zones >12 mm (Supplementary Table 4).

In contrast, several Lactobacillus crispatus isolates displayed broader activity. Seven strains, including Z2 and Z18, demonstrated bactericidal effects against both MG and MS and produced inhibition zones >12 mm against S. aureus, E. coli, and Salmonella (Supplementary Table 4). Similarly, in the Ligilactobacillus agilis group, strain Z22 showed bactericidal activity against MG and MS and generated inhibition zones >12 mm against all three indicator bacteria (Supplementary Table 4). Characterization of selected candidate strains

On the basis of acid tolerance and antimicrobial profiles, four strains were selected for further analysis: Lactobacillus crispatus Z2, L. crispatus Z18, Ligilactobacillus agilis Z22, and Ligilactobacillus salivarius M12. These strains consistently demonstrated bactericidal activity against MG and MS and broad inhibition of the indicator bacteria tested.

In solid medium assays, CFS from all four strains inhibited MG colony formation (Fig. 3A). To exclude cytotoxic effects on host cells, CFS concentrations were titrated using a CCK-8 assay in chicken alveolar epithelial type II (AEC II) cells. Treatment with 0.75% (v/v) CFS from L. crispatus Z2 and L. salivarius M12, and 0.375% CFS from L. crispatus Z18 and L. agilis Z22, did not significantly affect cell viability (Supplementary Fig. 1). Effects of candidate probiotics on growth performance in MG-challenged broilers

The in vivo efficacy of the four selected strains was then evaluated in broilers experimentally challenged with MG. Growth performance, including body weight, body weight gain, feed intake, and feed conversion ratio, was recorded and compared among treatment groups. At 39 days of age, MG infection reduced body weight relative to uninfected controls. Discussion

Restrictions on antimicrobial use in livestock production have increased the need for alternative strategies that can maintain animal health while reducing selection pressure for antimicrobial resistance (Lekshmi, et al., 2017). Probiotics, particularly lactic acid bacteria (LAB), have therefore received considerable attention in poultry production (Melara, et al., 2023). LAB are well established components of the avian intestinal microbiota and have been associated with maintenance of microbial homeostasis, competitive exclusion of pathogens, and modulation of mucosal immunity (Li, et al., 2024; Tellez-Isaias, et al., 2021). However, probiotic efficacy is highly strain-specific and cannot be predicted solely from taxonomic identity (García-Hernández, et al., 2016; Isolauri, et al., 2004). This is especially relevant when probiotics are intended for disease control, because a candidate strain must not only survive gastrointestinal conditions but also exert measurable effects on pathogen burden, host tissue responses, or production outcomes. In the present study, we isolated LAB from healthy chickens and applied a stepwise screening approach to identify strains with probiotic-relevant traits, followed by functional validation in a Mycoplasma gallisepticum (MG) challenge model. This design allowed us to connect in vitro probiotic characteristics with in vivo outcomes in the target host.

Host-derived isolates are often considered suitable candidates for probiotic development because they may be better adapted to the physicochemical and ecological conditions of the host gastrointestinal tract. LAB are established members of the avian intestinal microbiota and have been associated with microbial homeostasis, competitive exclusion of pathogens, and modulation of mucosal immunity (Li et al., 2024; Tellez-Isaias et al., 2021). Nevertheless, host origin alone does not guarantee functional efficacy; each strain must be evaluated for traits relevant to survival, colonization, and pathogen inhibition (Collins, et al., 1998; Xu, et al., 2025). In this study, 81 chicken-derived LAB isolates were obtained from different intestinal segments, providing a strain resource for subsequent functional screening. The predominance of Ligilactobacillus salivarius among the isolates is consistent with the known abundance of lactobacilli in the chicken gastrointestinal tract, although the value of individual isolates depends on their functional performance rather than their frequency of recovery.

Acid tolerance was used as an initial screening criterion because orally administered probiotics must withstand gastric acidity before reaching the intestine. Most isolates remained viable after short-term exposure to pH 3.0–4.0, indicating that a substantial proportion of the recovered strains could tolerate acidic stress. Similar acid resistance has been reported for chicken-derived Limosilactobacillus reuteri and other host-adapted strains(Li et al., 2024). Although acid tolerance in vitro does not fully replicate the complex conditions of the upper gastrointestinal tract, it provides a practical and widely used indicator for selecting strains with potential oral applicability(Menconi, et al., 2013; Nguyen, et al., 2015). The high proportion of acid-tolerant isolates in this study supports the value of healthy chickens as a source of LAB candidates adapted to the avian gastrointestinal environment.

Antimicrobial activity was the second major selection criterion. A subset of isolates exhibited inhibitory activity against common poultry-associated pathogens, including Salmonella, Escherichia coli, and MG. The antagonistic activity of LAB is commonly associated with the production of organic acids, bacteriocins, hydrogen peroxide, and other metabolites that lower environmental pH or directly inhibit pathogen growth (Negi, et al., 2018). LAB may also interfere with pathogen establishment by competing for nutrients and epithelial binding sites (García-Hernández et al., 2016; Negi et al., 2018). In the present study, the observation that only selected isolates showed bactericidal activity against MG highlights the importance of strain-level screening. This is particularly relevant for MG control, as the pathogen lacks a cell wall and differs biologically from many conventional bacterial pathogens. Therefore, broad inhibitory activity against enteric bacteria cannot be assumed to predict anti-MG activity. The identification of strains capable of inhibiting MG, together with other poultry pathogens, provides a functional basis for their further evaluation.

Among the screened isolates, Lactobacillus crispatus Z2, L. crispatus Z18, Ligilactobacillus agilis Z22, and Ligilactobacillus salivarius M12 were selected for further characterization based on their combined acid tolerance and antimicrobial profiles. Their cell-free supernatants reduced MG-associated readouts in chicken alveolar epithelial cells, including mgc2 copy number, gapA expression, and bacterial adhesion. The reduction in mgc2 copy number suggests a lower MG burden in the cell model, whereas decreased gapA expression is relevant because GapA is an important MG adhesion-associated factor involved in host cell interaction. The reduced fluorescence signal of MG attached to AEC II cells further supports an inhibitory effect on MG adhesion. These results indicate that the selected strains or their secreted products may interfere with early host–pathogen interactions, which are critical for MG colonization and infection progression.

Adhesion to epithelial surfaces is another important functional property of probiotics because it may support persistence in the intestinal environment and prolong host–microbe interactions (Collins et al., 1998; Xu et al., 2025). The selected strains adhered to intestinal epithelial cells in vitro and were detectable in intestinal samples after oral administration. Previous studies have linked LAB adhesion to surface hydrophobicity, aggregation capacity, and other cell surface features (Li et al., 2024). Although these physicochemical properties were not directly examined in the present study, the observed adhesion rates indicate that the selected strains have the capacity to interact with intestinal epithelial surfaces. This property may contribute to their functional activity in vivo by supporting intestinal retention and sustained metabolic or immunological effects. However, the present data reflect short-term adhesion rather than long-term colonization, and this distinction should be considered when interpreting the results.

The biological relevance of the selected strains was further evaluated using an MG challenge model in broilers. MG is a major cause of chronic respiratory disease in poultry and is commonly associated with impaired growth performance, reduced feed efficiency, respiratory lesions, and economic losses (Kleven, 2008; Wang, et al., 2025). Antimicrobials such as macrolides and tetracyclines are often used for MG control, but repeated use may contribute to antimicrobial resistance and disrupt commensal microbiota (Kleven, 2008). In the present study, selected LAB interventions improved performance outcomes in MG-challenged birds. In particular, probiotic treatment alleviated MG-associated reductions in body weight gain and improved feed conversion ratio. These performance benefits are important because they show that the selected strains did not merely alter laboratory infection markers but also improved production-relevant outcomes in the target animal.

The improvement in growth performance may be linked to the reduction in MG burden and tissue damage observed in probiotic-treated birds. MG infection can impose a physiological cost on broilers by inducing respiratory inflammation, impairing epithelial integrity, and increasing energy expenditure for immune responses and tissue repair. In this study, probiotic administration reduced pulmonary MG colonization, decreased expression of the MG adhesion-associated gene gapA, and alleviated lung inflammatory infiltration and apoptosis. These changes provide a plausible biological explanation for the improved growth performance and feed conversion. Reduced pathogen burden may decrease respiratory tissue injury and systemic inflammatory pressure, allowing more nutrients and energy to be allocated toward growth rather than immune activation. In addition, preservation of intestinal epithelial structure in probiotic-treated birds may have supported nutrient utilization and barrier function, further contributing to improved performance. Thus, the production benefits observed in this study are likely related to combined effects on pathogen control, tissue protection, and host physiological status.

The reduction in pulmonary inflammation and apoptosis is also consistent with previous reports showing that selected LAB strains can mitigate MG-induced inflammatory injury. For example, Ligilactobacillus salivarius has been reported to reduce MG colonization and modulate host inflammatory responses (Wang, et al., 2024a). The present results extend these observations by showing that multiple chicken-derived LAB strains with distinct taxonomic identities can exert beneficial effects in an MG challenge model. This finding supports the concept that anti-MG activity is not restricted to a single LAB species, but instead may be found through systematic screening of host-derived strain collections. At the same time, the magnitude of protection differed among strains, reinforcing the strain-specific nature of probiotic function.

One possible framework linking orally administered LAB to respiratory outcomes is the gut–lung axis, in which intestinal microbial signals influence immune responses at distal mucosal sites (Brown and Clarke, 2017; Ichinohe, et al., 2011; King, et al., 2014). Previous studies have shown that short-chain fatty acids and vitamin A–dependent pathways can enhance mucosal immunity and promote pathogen clearance (Wang et al., 2021). Wang et al. (Wang et al., 2021) further demonstrated that intestinal dysbiosis increases susceptibility to MG, whereas restoration of microbial balance improves host defense. Our findings are compatible with this concept, as orally administered LAB reduced lung MG colonization and respiratory pathology. However, because immune mediators, microbial metabolites, and microbiota composition were not directly measured in the present study, the pathways linking LAB administration to reduced MG burden remain to be defined. Therefore, the gut–lung axis provides a useful interpretive framework, but the present study does not establish the specific mediators responsible for the observed effects.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the CFS of selected strains inhibited MG in vitro, the antimicrobial substances responsible for this activity were not identified. Further characterization of organic acids, bacteriocin-like compounds, or other secreted metabolites would help clarify the direct anti-MG mechanisms. Second, the animal experiment was conducted under controlled conditions, and probiotic efficacy may vary in commercial production systems with different management practices, environmental stressors, infection pressures, and resident microbiota (Reuben et al., 2019). Third, the present study mainly evaluated single-strain interventions. Multi-strain formulations or synbiotic combinations may provide additive or synergistic effects, but this requires systematic testing. Finally, although the study demonstrated reduced MG burden, alleviated tissue injury, and improved growth performance, more detailed analyses of immune regulation, microbiota composition, and microbial metabolites would strengthen mechanistic interpretation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified chicken-derived Lactobacillus strains with favorable probiotic properties, including tolerance to gastrointestinal stress, antimicrobial activity, epithelial adhesion capacity, and protective effects in an MG challenge model. These strains reduced MG colonization and MG-associated tissue injury while improving growth performance in broilers, supporting their potential as non-antibiotic tools for MG control in poultry production. These findings also provide a foundation for the further development of chicken-derived LAB as probiotic interventions against other infectious diseases in poultry.
