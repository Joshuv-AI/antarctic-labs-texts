Microglial Prrc2a regulates microglia-neuron interaction in cerebellum and motor functions in mice
Authors: Xiaoheng Li, Pin Yang, Binbin Dong, Lingling Jiang, Ruilian Xiu, Jia Guo, Shuoshuo Li, Ping Zhang, Runze Gu, Ao Li, Jinyu Zhang, Rong Wu, Fengchao Wang, Ying Shen, Ningsheng Shao, Jinbo Cheng, Zengqiang Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe crosstalk between microglia and neuron has been recognized as a crucial process in the development and disorders of the central nervous system. The present study probes into the role of Prrc2a—a post-transcriptional regulator that binds and stabilizes transcripts pivotal for microglial development—specifically within cerebellar microglia using a microglia-targeted conditional knockout model, identifying it as a crucial regulator of microglial function. Single-cell RNA sequencing underscored a transcriptomic shift in Prrc2a-ablated microglia, revealing an abnormally activated state. Prrc2a deficiency and the consequent alteration of cerebellar microglia were associated with reduced mutual interactions, altered Purkinje cell morphology and electrophysiological activity, decreased molecular layer width, and subsequently defects in motor balance and coordination. Collectively, this study provides insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying microglia-neuron interaction within the cerebellum.

Introduction

Microglia, the immune cells residing in the central nervous system, have been recognized as multi-functional contributors to brain development, the maintenance of brain function, and the etiology of diverse diseases.1,2 During development, microglia perform various functions such as shaping neuronal circuits,3,4,5 eliminating unnecessary synapses,4,5,6 influencing the destiny of neuron progenitors,7,8 and regulating the growth and maturation of glial cells.9,10 These abilities are dependent on the motility and structural plasticity of microglia, which enable them to perceive the local cues they encounter, process various inputs, and provide appropriate responses while acquiring multiple signatures simultaneously.11 Meanwhile, interactions between neurons and microglia across different brain regions drive microglia to develop region-specific transcriptomic profiles and functions.12,13 However, most research on neuron-microglia interactions has focused on the forebrain, leaving a knowledge gap in understanding how microglia shape and are shaped by the local environment of the cerebellum. Therefore, it is crucial to conduct a thorough investigation of microglia within specific regional contexts, particularly in the cerebellum.

Cumulative evidence suggests that the cerebellum is not only involved in motor control but also regulates non-motor functions.14,15,16,17 Cerebellar abnormalities are closely associated with neurological disorders, owing largely to its prolonged postnatal development, which renders it more vulnerable to various injuries and can lead to long-term detrimental effects in adulthood.18,19 During development, the cerebellar circuits are fine-tuned by neuron-neuron interactions.20,21 Although microglia are known to critically regulate neuronal development and circuit refinement in the forebrain,5,22 their roles in cerebellar development and crosstalk with cerebellar neurons are still not fully understood. The cerebellum’s prolonged postnatal development extends the temporal window for microglia-mediated neuroregulation, making the investigation of cerebellar microglia-neuron interactions particularly compelling. The cytoarchitecture of cerebellum is precisely organized and uniform.23,24 Different types of neurons, such as the excitatory glutamatergic and inhibitory GABAergic neurons and glial cells consist the three distinct layers of cerebellar cortex: the molecular layer (ML), the Purkinje cell (PC) layer, and the internal granular layer.25 Together, these neurons form the neural circuits of the cerebellum.25,26 Among these elaborately patterned circuits, the PCs, with their elaborately arborized dendritic trees, serve as the sole output neurons, underscoring their essential role in maintaining cerebellar function.

Post-transcriptional regulation plays a role in multiple aspects of brain development and function.27,28 N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is the most prevalent posttranscriptional modification of mRNA in eukaryotes and is abundant in the brain.29 A significant portion of our knowledge regarding the role of m6A modification in the brain comes from studies on neurons and their progenitors.27 However, its role in microglia remains unclear. Our previous study demonstrates that Prrc2a (proline-rich coiled-coil 2A) is a novel m6A reader.30 Prrc2a-bound transcripts are involved in brain development and glial cell differentiation.30 Moreover, Prrc2a is highly expressed in microglia, indicating its potential role in this cell type.31 Notably, Prrc2a expression peaks at embryonic day 17.5 (E17.5) and maintains relatively high levels throughout postnatal development30—a critical period when microglia undergo population expansion and actively orchestrate neural circuit refinement.32 This spatiotemporal correlation underscores the necessity to investigate Prrc2a’s functions in microglial biology.

In this study, we demonstrate that Prrc2a plays a critical role in cerebellar microglia. Using microglia-targeted Prrc2a knockout mice combined with single-cell RNA sequencing, we found that Prrc2a deletion disrupts microglial homeostasis, reduces communication between cerebellar microglia and PCs, and causes profound alterations in PC morphology and function, ultimately leading to impaired motor function in mice.

To assess alterations in microglia following Prrc2a depletion, we performed immunofluorescence staining for Iba1 (ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1), which is a well-established marker for microglia. Strikingly, Prrc2a cKO mice showed normal microglia density in forebrain regions, including hippocampus (CA1, DG) and cerebral cortex, at 2 months of age (Figures 1C–1E) and at 4 months (Figure S1A). However, they showed a significant increase in microglia density in the cerebellar nuclei (CN) (Figures 1F, S1A and S1B). These results were further confirmed by immunohistochemical staining (Figures 1G and 1H).

Prrc2a-ablated microglia exhibit aberrant morphology during development

Having selectively ablated the Prrc2a gene in microglia from P1 in mice, we sought to determine the temporal onset of alterations in microglia. To characterize morphological changes in Prrc2a-deficient microglia during development, we performed a skeleton analysis (Figures 2A–2E). We found that the soma area of Prrc2a-deficient microglia was significantly increased during postnatal development (Figure 2B). The average branch length, branch number, and junction number of Prrc2a-ablated microglia were markedly reduced at P7 and P14, but showed no significant differences at P28 when compared to their controls (Figures 2C-2E). These data demonstrate that Prrc2a ablation leads to significant abnormalities in microglial morphology, particularly during early developmental stages, and we also observed a gradual normalization of these features over time. Prrc2a is required for microglial proliferation during postnatal development

Having characterized the aberrant morphology of microglia in Prrc2a cKO mice, we next examined how their density in the cerebellum changes during postnatal development. We observed evident regional variations in microglial density between the CN and the cerebellar cortex (Figure 2F). Therefore, microglia were quantified separately according to the anatomical organization of the cerebellum. Microglial density was significantly reduced in both the CN and the cerebellar cortex at P7 following Prrc2a deletion (Figure 2G). Interestingly, this reduction gradually lessened over time, and density became nearly identical between genotypes by P21 (Figures 2G and 2H). By P28, microglial density in the CN of Prrc2a cKO mice was remarkably higher than that of controls (Figure 2I), consistent with our earlier observations in 2-month- and 4-month-old mice (Figures 1F–1H). These results indicate that deficiency in Prrc2a leads to perturbed microglial maturation and proliferation.

Prrc2a-ablated microglia exhibit an altered spatial distribution

Imaging of the cerebellum of Prrc2a cKO and their control mice also revealed that Prrc2a-ablated microglia showed a region-specific alteration in spatial distribution (Figure 2I). Quantification across cerebellar subregions indicated a particularly severe deficit in the ML, a region mainly consisting of PC dendrites. The density of microglia associated with PC dendrites (PC dendrite-associated microglia) was dramatically reduced in Prrc2a cKO mice at P7. Although this density increased slightly at later developmental stages, it remained significantly lower than that in controls at P28 (Figure 2J), suggesting inadequate communication between Prrc2a-deleted microglia and cerebellar neurons, particularly PCs, during early postnatal development.

scRNA-seq reveals transcriptional changes in Prrc2a-ablated microglia

In order to gain a more comprehensive understanding of Prrc2a deficiency on microglial transcriptome characteristics and subsequent neuronal changes in cerebellum, we performed single-cell RNA-sequencing of 18,658 murine cerebellar cells from Prrc2a cKO and Prrc2a f/f (control) mice at P7. Unsupervised clustering identified 11 distinct cell clusters, visualized using UMAP (uniform manifold approximation projection) (Figure 3A). These clusters were annotated based on known marker genes for different cell types (Figures 3B and S2A). We then analyzed the abundance of cells in each cluster (Figure S2B). The number of cerebellar microglia in Prrc2a cKO mice (193) was nearly half of that in controls (390), consistent with the observed reduction in microglial density at P7. Additionally, many other cerebellar cell types showed altered abundances in Prrc2a cKO mice. Furthermore, we analyzed the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in microglia from Prrc2a cKO mice compared to the control group. Gene ontology (GO) analysis revealed that most of the upregulated genes in Prrc2a-ablated microglia were associated with the interferon response and immune processes, while many repressed genes were related to RNA processing and microglial function, such as migration and endocytosis (Figure 3C). More specifically, upon Prrc2a deletion, we observed a marked upregulation of IFN signaling genes (i.e., Ifitm3, Bst2, Irf7, and Rtp4) and a downregulation of Mafb, an antagonist of antiviral responses.33 Additionally, there was a notable decrease in the expression level of Csf1r, Sall1, and genes involved in TGFβ signaling. These genes are considered key factors for microglial proliferation and homeostatic maintenance during microglia development.34,35,36 Moreover, the genes encoding functional receptors (i.e., P2ry12, Mertk, and Gpr34) were also significantly decreased in Prrc2a-deleted microglia (Figure 3D). These results were further validated by performing RT-qPCR on sorted cerebellar microglia (Figure S3A) and by western blot (Figure S3B) on cerebellar tissue from mice at P7. Together, these results demonstrate that upon Prrc2a deletion, the expression patterns of cerebellar microglia shifted to an abnormal immune-activated state.

Prrc2a stabilizes the key developmental transcripts of microglia

Given that Prrc2a is an RNA-binding protein, we next sought to elucidate how Prrc2a deficiency affects microglia function. We compared the binding targets of Prrc2a (GEO: GSE100490) with the DEGs between Prrc2a-deficient microglia and controls (Figure 3E). 987 DEGs were found to be potential targets of Prrc2a that regulate microglial function (Table S2). Among these genes, we observed the key transcripts necessary for microglial development, such as Csf1r and Mafb. Using RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)-qPCR, we further confirmed that Prrc2a binds to the key transcripts of microglia both in vitro (Figure 3F) and in vivo (Figure 3G). Additionally, the transcripts displayed a significantly decreased half-life in Prrc2a knockdown cells (Figures 3H and 3I). These results indicate that Prrc2a regulates microglial function by stabilizing the essential developmental and functional transcripts of microglia.

Prrc2a knockout disrupts functional microglial states

Extensive studies have revealed distinct microglial identities through the mouse life span, and the most heterogeneous phase of microglia occurs in the developing brain during the postnatal stage.37,38,39 Likewise, we observed two distinct subclusters on the UMAP of cerebellar microglia at postnatal day 7 (Figure 3A). We consequently re-clustered only microglia, resulting in two groups (Figures 4A and 4B). Intriguingly, Prrc2a deletion mainly reduced the number of cells in subcluster 1 (Figures 4A and 4C), which was characterized by highly expressed genes involved in cellular response to cytokine stimulus, cytokine production, and cell migration, among other processes, according to GO analysis (Figure 4D). In contrast, subcluster 2 was primarily associated with translation, peptide biosynthetic process, and gene expression, among other processes, leading us to hypothesize that microglia in subcluster 2 were in a proliferative state (Figure 4D). We therefore performed cell cycle analysis. Indeed, while approximately 27% of microglia were at M/G2-phase and S-phase in subcluster 1, the proportion in subcluster 2 was about 59% (Figure 4E). Gene expression analysis showed that M/G2-phase genes (Hist1h2bc, Cks1b, Top2a, H2afx, and Ube2c) and S phase genes (Tipin, Pcna, Gins2, Rfc2, and Tyms) were primarily expressed by microglia in subcluster 2, confirming that subcluster 2 was a proliferative group (Figure 4F). Since the number of microglia in subcluster 1 was dramatically reduced upon Prrc2a deletion, the ratio of two clusters became imbalanced. The subcluster 2 to subcluster 1 ratio changed from 1.2 to 2.4 after Prrc2a deletion (Figure 4C). Therefore, the cells exhibiting a proliferative state were much more abundant in Prrc2a-ablated microglia than in control. Colabeling of BrdU and microglial marker Iba1 in cerebellum at P7 confirmed the increased proportion of proliferative microglia (Figures 4G and 4H). Collectively, these findings suggest that the deletion of Prrc2a predominantly diminishes the microglia in a functional state while enhancing the proliferative state of microglia. Consequently, this leads to a dynamic change in microglial density in the cerebellum of Prrc2a cKO mice, characterized by an initial decrease followed by an increase with age. Prrc2a deletion in microglia impairs cerebellar cell communication

After characterizing the alterations in the microglial profile, we proceeded to investigate how Prrc2a-deleted microglia affect cerebellar neurons. We employed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data to elucidate the interactions among various cell types. The cell interaction network revealed that the deletion of Prrc2a in microglia led to a significant decrease in communication between various types of cerebellar cells. Notably, microglia and PCs emerge as central nodes in the downregulation of communication within the cerebellar network in Prrc2a cKO mice (Figures 5A–5C), suggesting that the dysfunction of Prrc2a-deleted microglia primarily impacts PCs, which aligns with the observed alterations in microglial spatial distribution (Figure 2J). Furthermore, analysis of specific receptor-ligand modules and their corresponding signaling pathways identified six pathways—SPP1, NCAM, IGF, GRN, JAM, and CCL—that are significantly downregulated in the communication from microglia to PCs (Figures 5D, S4A and S4B). Transcriptional profile elucidates postnatal morphological alterations in PCs of Prrc2a cKO mice

To investigate the effect of Prrc2a-deficient microglia on the transcriptome of PCs, we examined the DEGs of PCs between Prrc2a cKO mice and control littermates and observed that majority of DEGs in PCs of Prrc2a cKO mice were downregulated. GO analysis also showed significant downregulation of genes involved in neuron migration, developmental cell growth, microtubule cytoskeleton organization, and dendritic development in PCs of Prrc2a cKO mice (Figure 5E). Specifically, the genes involved in the regulation of PCs dendritic morphology (i.e., Mef2c, Adgrb3, Celsr2, Sema3a, and Sort1), cell tiling and self-avoidance (i.e., Robo2 and Dscam), migration (i.e., Calb1 and Ncam1), as well as branching and survival (i.e., Igf1 and Rorα) were markedly downregulated in Prrc2a cKO mice (Figure 5F). Using immunofluorescence staining, we next determined how PCs change in Prrc2a cKO mice during early developmental stages. Consistent with the transcriptional alterations, we observed that the width of the ML was reduced before P7 in mice with microglial Prrc2a deletion, and as the mice aged, the difference between Prrc2a f/f and cKO mice increased (Figures 5G and 5H). Collectively, these results indicate that deletion of Prrc2a in microglia affects the dendritic branching and maturation of PCs during early postnatal development.

PCs morphological and functional impairments in Prrc2a cKO mice

To confirm the morphological alterations in PCs, we examined these cells in 2-month- and 4-month-old mice. Compared to control littermates, both immunofluorescence staining (Figures 6A and S5) and Nissl staining (Figures 6B and 6C) showed a significant reduction in ML thickness in Prrc2a cKO mice. To further characterize the morphological changes in PCs of Prrc2a cKO mice, we conducted Golgi staining and observed that the dendrites of PCs in Prrc2a cKO mice exhibited atrophic changes (Figure 6D), along with reduced complexity of dendrite branching and shortened distance from the soma to the longest dendritic terminal (Figure 6E). These findings were consistent with the reduction in ML width. Collectively, these results demonstrate that ablation of microglial Prrc2a induces dendritic abnormalities in PCs. Having characterized the morphological alterations in PCs induced by Prrc2a-ablated microglia, we next examined the functional changes of PCs in Prrc2a cKO mice. Whole-cell voltage-clamp recordings of miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs) in PCs from 8-week-old mice revealed impaired excitatory synaptic transmission in Prrc2a cKO PCs (Figure 6F). Both the frequency and amplitude of mEPSCs were significantly reduced in PCs of Prrc2a cKO mice compared to control PCs (Figures 6G and 6H). Microglial Prrc2a deficient mice show motor function defects

Intriguingly, Prrc2a cKO mice showed distinguishable motor deficits. We therefore performed behavioral tests and gait analysis to evaluate their motor function. Compared to control littermates, Prrc2a cKO mice showed more foot slips and took longer to cross the balance beam, suggesting a mild motor balance and coordination abnormalities (Figures 7A–7C). Similar deficits were observed in the rotarod test, Prrc2a cKO mice were more likely to fall from the accelerating rod (Figures 7D and 7E). These results indicate that Prrc2a ablation in microglia causes motor coordination impairment in mice. In the CatWalk analysis, the lower regularity index in Prrc2a cKO mice indicated a tendency toward poor inter-limb coordination (Figure 7G). Discussion

Mounting evidence shows that microglia display regional diversity. A typical subpopulation is cerebellar microglia, characterized by unique morphology, transcriptional profile, and functional dynamics.12,40 Cerebellar microglia have been found to promote PC apoptosis,41 permit climbing fiber elimination,42 and sculpt GC circuits.43 However, little is known regarding interactions between cerebellar microglia and PCs during postnatal development. Here, we identified Prrc2a as an essential post-transcriptional modulator of cerebellar microglia by targeting crucial developmental and functional transcripts. Prrc2a knockout results in alterations in the phenotype of cerebellar microglia. Notably, a significant decrease in microglial density was observed at postnatal day 7, along with morphological changes, an immune-activated gene expression profile, and decreased communication between microglia and PCs. These functional alterations of cerebellar microglia at the postnatal stage thus lead to the developmental abnormalities in PCs and ultimately contribute to the motor defects of Prrc2a cKO mice. Our findings demonstrate that reciprocal interactions between functional microglia and PCs are critical for the precise regulation of PC development, thereby advancing our understanding of cerebellar microglial function.

Microglial development has been shown to be precisely regulated by a series of key factors.35,36 Among these factors, Csf1r is essential for microglial development and maintenance. In the cerebellum, Kana et al. demonstrated that Csf1-Csf1r signaling is required to maintain the microglial population and their signatures. Csf1 depletion from nestin-positive cells results in a severe reduction in microglial density in the cerebellum and functional changes in PCs.44 Another study, which utilized microglial Csf1r-specific deletion, also observed a significant loss of cerebellar microglia.42 In the present research, we found that Prrc2a binds to and stabilizes the transcripts of Csf1r, leading to a notable decrease in Csf1r expression following the deletion of Prrc2a in microglia. In line with previous studies, the population of microglia was dramatically reduced at P7, especially in the microglia that were associated with the dendrites of PCs. Accordingly, downregulation of Csf1r likely represents a key contributor to the microglial reduction in Prrc2a cKO mice. Additionally, we also found a significant decrease in genes involved in TGFβ signaling, which is crucial for microglial specification and population maintenance. This suggests an additional mechanism that may contribute to microglial loss. Although the changes in microglial density in Prrc2a cKO mice resemble those in Csf1/Csf1r deletion models, the transcriptional signature of Prrc2a-ablated microglia was distinct. Upon Prrc2a ablation, we observed aberrant upregulation of genes involved in IFN signaling. Given that either Sall1 or Mafb deletion can cause microglia to adopt an antiviral response state,45,46 this upregulation of IFN signaling may be due to reduced Sall1 and Mafb expression. Consequently, this immune-activated state of microglia may lead to neurotoxicity and neuronal dysfunction.47,48 Physiological expansion of interferon-responsive microglia (IRM) at P5–P7 facilitates targeted clearance of DNA-damaged neurons for circuit refinement. However, dysregulated type I interferon (IFN-I) signaling—as seen in Prrc2a cKO mice—induces pathological IRM hyperactivation, shifting from selective neuroprotection to nonselective neuronal threats.49 Recent research has further demonstrated that hyperactivity in the microglial IFN-I response reduces neuronal Mef2c transactivation and impairs cognitive resilience.50 These findings provide mechanistic insight into how Prrc2a-deficient microglia contribute to PC dysfunction.

The spatiotemporal orchestration of microglial density is pivotal for phase-specific functions. During early postnatal stages (P5–P7), microglia proliferate predominantly in white matter, adopting ameboid morphology to phagocytose apoptotic cells (e.g., granule cell precursors and PCs), thereby facilitating neural circuit assembly.41,51 By P8–P9, a transient density peak at the PC layer-internal granular layer (PCL-IGL) interface supports morphological transition to ramified states and climbing fiber synapse elimination via enhanced GABAergic inhibition onto PCs.42 From P10–P17, microglial density peaks in white matter and granular layers, enabling targeted clearance of migrating granule cells and apoptotic debris.43,51 Subsequently, density declines by ∼40% in GL by P30, synchronizing with reduced phagocytic demand and a shift toward surveillance states.43,52 In our study, Prrc2a ablation at P1 disrupts cerebellar microglial dynamics during critical developmental windows. At P7, coinciding with peak phagocytic activity, we observed pan-cerebellar microglial depletion, directly impairing apoptotic cell clearance as evidenced by elevated cleaved-caspase 3. While microglial density was recovered in CN by P14 and normalized globally by P21, functional deficits persisted: CN developed pathological hyper-density by P28 with aberrant morphology, and PC-associated microglia remain significantly reduced, indicating persistent migration impairment to neuronal microenvironments. Our findings demonstrate that microglia-PC communication during postnatal development provide essential support for PC maturation.

The bidirectional interactions between microglia and neurons allow microglia to be informed of the status of neurons, make corresponding reactions, and modulate neuron activities.32,53,54 This process takes place on multiple levels, consisting of indirect interactions via soluble factors and intermediate cells, as well as direct contact between cell membranes.54 Neuronal alterations upon microglial IFN response, as previously described, could be categorized as indirect interactions. In this study, we also found a remarkable decrease in the mutual interaction between microglia and the dendrites of PCs. Using CellChat analysis, we identified six downregulated signaling axes from microglia to PCs, Spp1-(Itga5+Itgb1), Ncam1-Ncam2, Igf1-Igf1r, Grn-Sort1, F11r-11r, and Ccl2-Ackr1, providing mechanistic clues linking microglial dysfunction to PC pathology. Notably, NCAM is crucial for PC migration,55 and attenuated NCAM signaling may perturb PC migration, potentially contributing to PC mispositioning within cerebellar circuits. Furthermore, microglial IGF1 downregulation represents a key trophic deficit.56 In the forebrain, microglia-derived IGF1 acts as a trophic factor that maintains neuronal survival during postnatal development.8 Similarly, in the cerebellum, IGF1 is essential for PC survival57; as established in ataxia models, postnatal IGF1 deficit profoundly compromises PC survival, while exogenous IGF1 supplementation rescues PC loss.58 Additionally, impaired GRN-Sort1 signaling reflects microglial dysfunction in neuroprotection: microglia serve as the primary CNS source of PGRN (progranulin), and GRN deficiency reduces PGRN secretion, disrupting extracellular GRN-Sort1 signaling.59 This loss induces pathological complement overexpression, driving aberrant synaptic pruning and microglia-mediated neurotoxicity.59 In the developing cerebellum, attenuated microglial GRN-Sort1 signaling may thus compromise PC dendritic maturation. However, the detailed mechanism of how microglia shape dendrites of PCs need further investigation.

Microglia-microglia communication is crucial for establishing functional networks. Microglia critically employ autocrine signaling for self-regulation, with multiple interconnected pathways contributing to this process. As demonstrated by Bedolla et al., microglial homeostasis depends intrinsically on TGFβ-Smad signaling; conditional knockout of TGFβ in microglia drastically reduces brain TGFβ levels, triggering microglial hyperactivation and downregulation of homeostatic genes.60 This aligns with Butovsky et al.’s work establishing TGFβ as a master regulator of microglial developmental programming.61 Notably, as microglia are the primary CNS TGFβ source, this represents a fundamental autocrine loop. Concurrently, chemokine pathways operate through autocrine loops: microglia-derived CCL ligands engage CCR receptors on neighboring microglia, triggering NF-κB-mediated inflammatory activation.62 Furthermore, IGF1 autocrine signaling directly regulates microglial population dynamics, evidenced by cell number defects upon microglia-specific IGF1 deletion.56 Our cerebellar data reveal parallel disturbances in these specific autocrine pathways (TGFβ, IGF1, and CCL), suggesting that disrupted microglia-microglia communication contributes to the aberrant transcriptomic state and functional impairment in Prrc2a-ablated microglia. While these represent only a subset of autocrine mechanisms, their dysregulation exemplifies how impaired self-regulatory circuits may compromise cerebellar microglial development.

Single-cell RNA sequencing is an effective high-throughput approach to examine cellular transcriptome changes in detail. Emerging evidence reveals microglial heterogeneity in development, homeostasis, and disease.38,39,63,64 During development, early postnatal microglia are the most diverse, consisting of states associated with cell metabolism, growth, motility, and proliferation, as well as transient states that have not fully stabilized yet.38 In our study, we identified two microglial states in the cerebellum at postnatal day 7: a functional state (state I), and a proliferative state (state II). Upon Prrc2a deletion, microglia underwent a pronounced shift in state distribution, losing a large proportion of cells in the functional state, while the proportion of microglia in the proliferative state increased substantially, contributing to approximately 71% of the total population. This dramatic shift toward a proliferative state illustrates the density variation of cerebellar microglia during the development of Prrc2a cKO mice. Additionally, given that microglial function is essential for neuronal development, this state imbalance is associated with and offers insight into the dysfunction of PCs in Prrc2a cKO mice.

Limitations of the study

While our study establishes a crucial role for microglial Prrc2a in cerebellum development, some limitations remain. First, the microglia-specific deletion of Prrc2a, while validated by qPCR on isolated cells, was not confirmed histologically due to the unavailability of a specific and reliable Prrc2a antibody. Another limitation is that our conclusion—that Prrc2a deficiency leads to perturbed microglial maturation and proliferation—is primarily based on density and morphological analyses. Future studies employing direct proliferation assays and maturation-specific markers could more definitively characterize these dynamic processes. Additionally, while our single-cell transcriptomic data reveal correlative alterations in microglia-to-PC signaling pathways, the precise molecular cascades linking Prrc2a-deficient microglia to PC impairment remain undefined. Our data cannot establish causality or resolve the spatiotemporal dynamics of these interactions, and we are unable to determine whether the neuronal defects stem directly from loss of microglial trophic support, aberrant cytokine signaling, or disrupted physical contacts. This underscores the complexity of microglia-neuron crosstalk and necessitates deeper mechanistic investigation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mice

Prrc2aflox/flox mice on a C57BL/6 background were originally generated by Dr. Fengchao Wang (National Institute of Biological Science, Beijing) as described in our previous study.30 Cx3cr1CreER mice (Stock No: 021160) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Sacramento, CA, USA). Prrc2aflox/flox mice and Cx3cr1CreER mice intercrossed to yield Prrc2aflox/flox (Prrc2a f/f) and Prrc2aflox/flox; Cx3cr1CreER (Prrc2a cKO) littermates and a mixture of male and female mice were employed in all experiments. To induce microglial Prrc2a knockout, tamoxifen (S1238, Selleck), dissolved in corn oil at a concentration of 20 mg/mL, was given to mice from postnatal day1 to day3 by gavage at 45 mg/kg each day (∼200 μg per mouse). Prrc2a-C-HA mice, in which the HA fusion gene is transcribed following the Prrc2a gene, were originally generated in the Genome Tagging Project at the Center for Excellence in Molecular Cell Science of the Chinese Academy of Sciences.

All animal procedures were performed on age-matched littermates of both sexes, with ages detailed in the respective results sections and figure legends. Where applicable, both sexes were included, and no differences were attributed to gender; therefore, data were pooled for analysis. Mice were housed under a 12 h light–dark cycle at 23 °C with free access to food and water in the Animal Care Facility at the Institute of Basic Medical Sciences. All experimental animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Beijing Institute of Basic Medical Sciences (IACUC-DWZX-2019-504).

Isolation of microglia

Anesthetized mice were perfused transcardially with prechilled saline, cerebella were dissected from brain and rinsed with ice-cold Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (D-PBS). The preparation of single-cell suspensions was conducted using the Adult Brain Dissociation Kit (130-107-677, Miltenyi Biotec). After removal of debris and red blood cells according to the manufacture’s instruction, microglia were isolated using magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS; Miltenyi Biotec). Briefly, microglia were labeled with anti-CD11b magnetic MicroBeads (130-093-634, Miltenyi Biotec), and subsequently isolated using a magnetic separator.

Cell culture

HEK293T and BV2 cell lines were obtained from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (Invitrogen). All cells were maintained in 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere at 37 °C.

Method details

Rotarod test

Mice were tested using the accelerating rotarod paradigm (Ugo Basile, Comerio, Italy).65 Prior to the final test, mice were pretrained on the Rota-Rod at 10 rpm three times per day for two days. During test, mice were placed on the rod that was accelerated smoothly from 4 to 40 rpm over a 5-min period with three trials at 1 h intervals each, and the average duration mice spent on the rod was recorded.

Balance beam test

The balance beam device consists of a horizontal round beam (outer diameter 12 mm, length 1 m and placed 50 cm above a padded surface), a strong light on the start side and a dark safety platform on the end side. Mice were pre-trained on the beam three times per day (at 1 h intervals) for 2 days before testing. During test, mice crossed over the beam, paw slips and traverse time were recorded.

Gait analysis

Gait analysis was conducted using the CatWalk quantitative gait analysis system (CatWalk XT 10.6; Noldus Information Technology, Wageningen, The Netherlands), which was designed for assessment of motor impairment and gait abnormalities.66 Briefly, mice walked through a 1.3-meter-long glass walkway when paws and footprints were captured by a high-speed video camera that is positioned underneath the walkway. Footprints were recorded and analyzed by CatWalk XT software. Mice were pretrained before testing to traverse the glass walkway. Three complete runs were recorded and analyzed for each mouse during test. To estimate motor coordination changes the following gait parameters were used: regularity index, footfall patterns, print length in four limbs (left front (LF), right front (RF), left hind (LH), and right hind (RH) paw), duty cycle and paw support.

Immunohistochemistry

Brains were removed, dissected, and fixed in 4% PFA for 48 h. After fixation, brains were dehydrated with gradient sucrose (15%, 20%, and 30%) in PBS and embedded into optimal cutting temperature compound. Sagittal sections (30 μm in thickness) were sliced with cryostat (CM3050S, Leica) with sampling specifically targeted to the vermal region 0.36–0.48 mm lateral to midline. Sections were mounted on adhesive slides and stored at −20°C until staining. Genotype-blinded procedures encompassed tissue handling, imaging, and analysis.

Immunofluorescence staining

Brain slices were washed in PBS for three times and then blocked with blocking buffer (PBS containing 0.4% Triton X-100, 5% horse serum, and 1% BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. Slices were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with fluorescent secondary antibodies (1:400, Jackson Immunoresearch, West Grove, PA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclear staining was visualized using DAPI (D9542; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The images were acquired using a Nikon confocal microscope (Nikon A1, Melville, NY, USA). Primary antibodies used include anti-Calbindin-D-28K (1:500, C9848, Sigma-Aldrich), anti-Iba1 (1:500, 019–19741, Wako, Richmond, VA, USA), anti-Gfap (1:800, MAB360, Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany), anti-BrdU (1:1000, ab6326, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA). Purkinje cell and BrdU+ analyses were conducted in lobules IV-V.

Immunohistochemical staining

After rinsed with PBS, brain slices were incubated with endogenous peroxidase blocking solution for 15 min at room temperature, immunohistochemical staining was performed using the avidin/biotin-based peroxidase system according to the manufacture’s instruction (Vectastain ABC kitVector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA). Briefly, slices were treated with anti-Iba1 (1:500, 019–19741, Wako) primary antibody overnight at 4 °C, and subsequently incubated with biotinylated goat anti-rabbit IgG and avidin-conjugated HRP and finally visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) followed by a reaction with hydrogen peroxide (DAB kit, Vector Laboratories). The Iba1-positive cells were counted blindly using ImageJ Pro Plus software.

Nissl staining

Brain slices were washed in PBS for three times and were then incubated in 1% toluidine blue staining solution for 5 min at 50 °C. slices were immediately washed twice in double distilled water and differentiated in 95% ethanol for several minutes. After dehydrated in 100% ethanol and cleared in xylene, slices were sealed with neutral resin medium.

Golgi staining

Golgi staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocols (FD Rapid GolgiStain Kit, FD NeuroTechnologies, Columbia, MD, USA). We cut 100 μm sections using cryostat (CM3050S, Leica). All sections were visualized on an Olympus confocal microscope (Olympus IX81, Tokyo, Japan).

BrdU labeling

To determine microglia proliferation at P7, mice were subjected to two intraperitoneal injections of BrdU (50 mg/kg, HY-15910, MedChemExpress) 2 h apart. Two hours after the last injection, mice were anesthetized and perfused with saline. Brain slices were prepared as described above. Before standard immunochemistry staining procedure, brain slices were incubated with 1M HCL for 10 min on ice, 2M HCl for 10 min at room temperature and for 20 min at 37 °C.

Sholl analysis

Sholl analysis of purkinje cells was performed for each cell in both group (17 purkinje cells were included). Concentric circles were spaced 20 pixels (∼3.45 μm) apart originating from the soma, and intersections of each cell with concentric circles were measured using the software ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Microglial skeleton analysis

Skeleton analysis of microglia was performed as described in our previous study.67 Briefly, we obtained 16 μm z stack images on Nikon A1 Confocal Microscope with 20× objective at 5× zoom at 2 μm intervals. Skeletonize [two-dimensional (2D)/3D] plugin and Analyze Skeleton plugin of the software ImageJ were used for the skeleton analysis.

Cell transfection

For stably knocking down genes in BV2 cells, shRNA against Prrc2a (targeting sequence was: TCGAAGAGCTGGGCCTATAAA) was annealed and ligated into pLKO.1 lentiviral vector (Addgene, Cambridge, MA, USA) and then co-transfected with viral packaging plasmids into HEK293T cells. After transfection, viral supernatant was collected (once a day for two days), centrifuged and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter and infected into BV2 cells with polybrene (#sc-134220, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, 1:1000). Three days after infection, cells were subjected to antibiotic selection.

mRNA half-life measurement

To measure the half-life of indicated mRNAs, actinomycin D (5 μg/mL, HY-17559, Medchemexpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) was added into the BV2 cell culture medium. After 0, 1, 3 and 6 h of incubation, cells were collected, and total RNAs were extracted and subjected to RT-qPCR analysis. Decay kinetics were modeled via log-linear regression of first-order degradation: normalized mRNA levels (RNAt) were transformed to ln (RNAt) = ln (RNA0) − k⋅t to derive the decay constant (k), with half-life calculated as T1/2 = ln2/k.68

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA from tissues or sorted cells was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). complementary DNA production was using One-step Firststrand cDNA synthesis kit (Transgen Biotechnology, Beijing, China). The mRNA expression was measured by SYBR-Green-based qPCR using the Delta-Delta CT method. β-actin was used as a housekeeping gene for input normalization. Primers for quantitative PCR were are listed in Table S1.

Immunoblotting

Tissues or cells were lysed with lysis buffer containing a cocktail of protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was measured using BCA assays. Proteins were denaturated, and separated by SDS-PAGE and then transferred to NC membrane. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. The immunoreactive proteins were then detected using an enhanced chemiluminescent (ECL) substrate and visualized with autoradiography film. Primary antibodies used were: anti-Iba1 (019–19741, Wako), anti-Calbindin-D-28K (C9848, Sigma-Aldrich), anti-cleaved caspase-3 (9664, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-β-actin (66009-1-lg, Proteintech), anti-Psd95 (ab18258, Abcam), anti-Csf1R (3152, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-HA (3724, Cell Signaling Technology).

RIP-qPCR

The RNA immunoprecipitation procedure was conducted using an established protocol with minor modifications.30 Nine dishes (15 cm) of BV2 cells were collected and lysed with 10 mL of lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.6), 2 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40, 0.5 mM DTT, 1% protease inhibitor cocktail, 0.4 U mL−1 RNasin). The lysates were collected. The protein concentration was determined, and the lysate was diluted to a final concentration of 5.0 mg/mL 250 μL of cell lysate was saved as input. After being precleared, the cell lysate was divided into two equal parts. The control group was incubated overnight with magnetic beads coupled with normal mouse IgG, while the experimental group was immunoprecipitated overnight with magnetic beads conjugated with anti-Prrc2a antibody (Mouse monoclonal anti-Prrc2a, sc373747, Santa Cruz). The beads were then collected, rinsed, and digested. RNAs from the input and IP samples were extracted by phenol-chloroform method, followed by qPCR assay.

As for brain tissue, cerebella were dissected from 7-day-old wild-type (WT) mice and Prrc2a-C-HA mice, with 18 mice in each group. In the immunoprecipitation reactions, the tissue lysates were incubated overnight with magnetic beads conjugated with anti-HA antibody (anti-HA, 3724S, Cell Signaling Technology).

Electrophysiology

Sagittal slices of cerebellar vermis (250 μm) were prepared from anesthetized mice using a vibrating tissue slicer (Leica VT1000S, Germany) and ice-cold aCSF containing (in mM): 125 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 1 MgCl2, 2 CaCl2, 26 NaHCO3 and 25 D-glucose, bubbled with 95% O2/5% CO2. After recovery for 30 min at 32 °C, slices were placed in a submerged chamber that was perfused at 2 mL/min with aCSF. Patch clamp electrodes (3–5 MΩ) were filled with an intracellular solution composed of either (in mM) 135 Cs-methanesulfonate, 10 CsCl, 10 HEPES, 0.2 EGTA, 4 Na2ATP, and 0.4 Na3GTP (pH 7.3, OSM 290) for voltage-clamp recording. PCs were visualized under an upright microscope (BX51, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) with a 40× water-immersion objective and equipped with infrared differential interference contrast enhancement. Whole-cell recordings were obtained with an Axon MultiClamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices, Foster City, CA). Currents were digitized at 10 kHz and low-pass filtered at 3 kHz. Recordings were excluded from analysis if the series or input resistance varied by > 15% over the course of an experiment. mEPSCs were recorded in whole-cell configuration in the presence of 0.5 μM tetrodotoxin (TTX). The offline analysis of mEPSCs was conducted using a sliding template algorithm (ClampFit 10, Molecular Device). The criteria for inclusion were an amplitude larger than 6 pA and a rise time (10–90%) longer than 1 ms. Overlapping events were rejected.

Single-cell RNA-seq and data analysis

7-day-old Prrc2a f/f and Prrc2a cKO mice (6 mice in each group) were euthanized, after perfusion with cold saline, cerebella were dissected for sample preparation. Single-cell suspensions were attained using the Neural Tissue Dissociation Kit-Postnatal Neurons (130-094-802, Miltenyi Biotec). Following the removal of red blood cells and dead cells, cell libraries were prepared using Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3ʹ Reagent Kits v3.1 (10× Genomics), and subsequently sequenced on the Novaseq 6000 (Illumina).

Single-cell RNA-seq data processing

Single-cell sequencing datasets were analyzed by package CellRanger (v6.0.0, 10× Genomics) and Seurat (v4.1.0).69 The 10× Genomics pre-built mouse genome for mm10–3.0.0 was used as reference. For quality control, genes expressed in fewer than 3 cells and gene count of less than 200 per cell were excluded from datasets. Furthermore, cells, excluding Purkinje cells, exhibiting more than 10% mitochondrial-associated genes and more than 0.2% red blood cell genes among their expressed genes were also removed. Putative doublets were filtered by DoubletFinder (v2.0.3)70 Clustering and sub-clustering was conducted using the FindNeighbors (dims = 1:50) and the FindClusters (resolution = 1) functions followed by PCA dimension reduction on the most variable genes selected by FindVariableGenes. RunUMAP (dims = 1:50) was used for dimensionality reduction and plotting UMAP maps. The FindAllMarkers function was utilized to identify marker gene. Each cell cluster was annotated manually based on known marker gene. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted utilizing the FindMarkers function, employing the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with a significance threshold of p < 0.001. The gene ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis was carried out using the clusterProfiler (v4.0.5).71 The CellCycleScoring function in Seurat was utilized to forecast the cell cycle phases of distinct microglia subpopulations.

Cell-cell communication analysis

Cell-cell interactions were analyzed by the CellChat package (v1.1.0).72 The CellChat objects were created and preprocessed using the default parameters in the mouse CellChatDB database, following the official CellChat workflow. Cell-cell communication networks were subsequently established by the computeCommunProb, computeCommunProbPathway, and aggregateNet functions. Circular plots were employed to visually depict differences in cellular communication between Prrc2a f/f and Prrc2a cKO mice using the netVisual_diffInteraction. Additionally, the netVisual_bubble was used to compare the probability of communication facilitated by ligand-receptor modules in various cell populations of Prrc2a f/f and Prrc2a cKO mice.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (v9.0) and the significance of differences was evaluated by two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test, multiple unpaired t-tests with Benjamini-Krieger-Yekutieli false discovery rate (FDR) correction (q < 0.01) or one-way/two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, as indicated in figure legends. To account for non-independent measurements within subjects, linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were fitted with subject-specific random intercepts using SPSS v27 (IBM, Armonk, NY). The data were expressed as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM) unless otherwise described. Statistical significance was denoted by ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
