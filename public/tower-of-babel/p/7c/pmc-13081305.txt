Serum and urinary mindin as a potential biomarker for diabetic retinopathy: a prospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Ibrahim Dogan, Cagatay Caglar, Ahmet Gursoy, Huseyin Kayadibi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo analyze the potential role of serum and urinary mindin as biomarkers for diabetic retinopathy (DR) severity in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM).MethodsThis prospective, cross-sectional study included 101 participants allocated into four groups: DM without DR (n = 26), DM with non-proliferative DR (n = 25), DM with proliferative DR (n = 25), and non-diabetic healthy controls (n = 25). Serum and 24-hour urinary mindin levels were compared among the groups.ResultsSerum mindin levels were significantly lower in Group 3 than in Group 1 and Group 2 (p < 0.001 and p = 0.001, respectively). Twenty-four-hour urinary mindin levels were significantly higher in the diabetic groups (Groups 1, 2, and 3) compared to the non-diabetic control group (p = 0.046 for Group 1, p < 0.001 for Groups 2 and 3). Correlation analysis revealed positive correlations between 24-hour urinary mindin levels and DR severity, HbA1c, proteinuria, and insulin use. Multivariate logistic regression analysis established HbA1c, urinary mindin level, and proteinuria as independent determinants of DR. Conversely, the serum mindin level was not a significant predictor of DR.ConclusionsTwenty-four-hour urinary mindin levels were significantly higher in patients with type 2 DM than in non-diabetic healthy controls. Furthermore, urinary mindin levels increased in correlation with DR severity. In contrast, serum mindin levels were not associated with DR according to multivariate analysis.Keywords: Biomarker, Diabetic retinopathy, Serum mindin, Urinary mindin

Background

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a leading cause of preventable and treatable blindness worldwide [1]. The increasing global prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM), coupled with increased life expectancy among diabetic patients, has led to a steady increase in the number of individuals with DR [2]. Histopathological studies of DR reveal early loss of pericytes and endothelial cells in retinal capillaries [3]. Subsequent pathological hallmarks include pericyte dropout, acellular capillaries, microaneurysms, and capillary basement membrane thickening [4]. Notably, similar microvascular damage is observed in diabetic nephropathy (DN), in which injury to glomerular podocytes is a central pathogenic event [5]. These findings suggest that DR and DN may share common pathogenic pathways. Consistently, numerous clinical studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between the incidence of DR and DN [6–7]. Local inflammation is a well-established risk factor driving the development and progression of both DR and DN [8].

Mindin (Spondin-2), an extracellular matrix protein, has emerged as a molecule of interest. Mindin is recognized for its pivotal role in initiating innate immune responses, facilitating inflammatory cell migration, and acting as an integrin ligand in vitro [9]. In relation to microvascular complications, mindin is directly associated with podocyte damage and has been proposed as a potential early marker for DN progression [10–11]. For example, one study revealed significantly elevated mindin expression in kidney biopsies from patients with DN compared with those from patients with other glomerulopathies or healthy controls, demonstrating high specificity for DN (p < 0.0001) [10]. Interestingly, however, serum levels of mindin—an extracellular matrix protein upregulated upon podocyte damage—were reported to be lower in patients with type 1 DM [12].

The convergence of capillary cell injury and inflammatory processes in both DN and DR suggests that shared molecular mediators may contribute to their pathogenesis. In DN, mindin has been shown to participate in glomerular capillary inflammation and podocyte injury, key events in the development of microvascular damage. Given that DR is likewise characterized by retinal capillary endothelial dysfunction, basement membrane alterations, and inflammation-driven microvascular injury, it is biologically plausible that mindin may exert similar effects in the retinal microvasculature. Therefore, beyond its established role in DN, mindin may represent a common inflammatory mediator linking renal and retinal microvascular complications in diabetes. Identifying such a molecule prior to clinically evident retinal changes could be invaluable for early detection and prevention of end-organ damage. Based on this mechanistic and pathophysiological overlap, the present study aimed to investigate the potential role of mindin as an early indicator of DR and to analyze its association with DR severity. To this end, we examined the correlation between DR stages and serum and 24-hour urinary mindin levels in patients with type 2 DM.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This study prospectively enrolled participants and conducted a cross-sectional analysis of baseline clinical, ophthalmic, and laboratory measurements. It was conducted at the University Education and Research Hospital. The study protocol received approval from the University Medicine Faculty Ethics Committee (06.01.2017/2016.52). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The study was performed in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population and eligibility criteria

The study enrolled adult patients who were diagnosed with type 2 DM for a minimum of six months. The exclusion criteria were as follows:

End-stage renal disease (glomerular filtration rate (GFR) < 15 mL/min/1.73 m²) and/or ongoing renal dialysis therapy.

Acute coronary syndrome within the preceding six weeks.

New York Heart Association Class III or IV heart failure.

Decompensated chronic liver disease.

Decompensated chronic liver disease.

Morbid obesity (Body mass index (BMI) ≥ 40 kg/m²).

Active infection.

Active malignancy.

Major surgical intervention within the last month.

Major surgical intervention within the last month.

History of retinal or vitreous surgery, uveitis, ocular trauma, or concomitant retinal disease.

History of retinal or vitreous surgery, uveitis, ocular trauma, or concomitant retinal disease.

Data collection

For all participants, comprehensive data, including comorbid conditions, current medications, and smoking status, were recorded. Body mass index was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²). Arterial pressure (AP) was measured in both arms, and the mean of three consecutive readings was used for analysis. All DR grading was performed by a single experienced retinal specialist based on dilated fundus examination using ETDRS criteria. Fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) and optical coherence tomography (OCT) were conducted when clinically indicated. Patients diagnosed with DR were classified according to the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) grading system [13] into NPDR or PDR stages.

Study groups

A total of 101 participants were divided into four groups:

Group 1 (n = 26): Patients with type 2 DM without DR.

Group 1 (n = 26): Patients with type 2 DM without DR.

Group 2 (n = 25): Patients with DM and NPDR.

Group 2 (n = 25): Patients with DM and NPDR.

Group 3 (n = 25): Patients with DM and PDR.

Group 3 (n = 25): Patients with DM and PDR.

Group 4 (n = 25): Age- and sex-matched healthy individuals without a diagnosis of DM.

Group 4 (n = 25): Age- and sex-matched healthy individuals without a diagnosis of DM.

Laboratory procedures and biochemical analyses

Venous blood samples were collected into clot activator tubes from all the subjects between 08:00 and 11:59 A.M. following a 12-hour overnight fast. After waiting for 30 minutes at room temperature, the blood samples were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 5 min to obtain the serum. A 24-hour urine sample was collected for each participant. Participants received standardized verbal and written instructions for 24-hour urine collection. Urine sample collections with implausible total volumes were verified and excluded if incomplete. Comprehensive laboratory profiling was conducted, including measurement of serum mindin, complete blood count (CBC), glucose, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, sodium (Na⁺), potassium (K⁺), calcium (Ca²⁺), phosphorus (P), uric acid, total protein, albumin, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), triglycerides (TG), C-reactive protein (CRP), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), 24-hour urinary protein excretion and routine biochemical parameters (serum and urine) were analyzed photometrically via standardized available kits on an autoanalyzer. CBC was performed via an automated CBC analyzer. HbA1c was quantified via a standardized high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) method. Serum and urinary mindin concentrations were determined via a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Cat. No: YLA0860HU). The assay has a reported measurement range of 0.05–38 ng/mL and a sensitivity of 0.031 ng/mL. The intra-assay and interassay coefficients of variation (CVs) were < 8% and < 10%, respectively. Absorbance readings were obtained via an EZ Read 400 ELISA microplate reader (Biochrom Ltd., Cambridge, UK). The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated via the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) formula. As noted in the exclusion criteria, individuals with an eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73 m² or who were receiving renal dialysis therapy were not enrolled.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were conducted via SPSS Statistics software, version 23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The sample size for each group was determined a priori using one-way ANOVA (G*Power 3.1) to ensure adequate statistical power. Data distribution was assessed via the Shapiro‒Wilk test. Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Nonnormally distributed variables are expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs; 25th–75th percentiles).

Intergroup comparisons were performed as follows:

For normally distributed continuous variables across the four groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by post hoc multiple comparisons via Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) test.

For normally distributed continuous variables across the four groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by post hoc multiple comparisons via Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) test.

For nonnormally distributed continuous variables, the Kruskal‒Wallis H test was used, with pairwise comparisons conducted via the Mann‒Whitney U test and appropriate Bonferroni correction for multiple testing.

For nonnormally distributed continuous variables, the Kruskal‒Wallis H test was used, with pairwise comparisons conducted via the Mann‒Whitney U test and appropriate Bonferroni correction for multiple testing.

Categorical variables were compared via the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Categorical variables were compared via the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Correlations between continuous variables were evaluated via Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ). To identify independent predictors associated with the presence of DR, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed. Variables showing a significance level of p < 0.10 in univariate analyses or those deemed clinically relevant were entered into the model. The results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A P value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance for all analyses, except where adjusted for multiple comparisons.

No statistically significant differences were observed among the groups regarding age (except for a specific difference between Group 2 and the control group), sex distribution, BMI, smoking habits, systolic blood pressure, or diastolic blood pressure (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

The prevalence of hypertension and coronary artery disease (CAD) was significantly greater in Groups 2 and 3 (patients with DR) than in Group 1 (DM patients without DR). Notably, the frequency of CAD was significantly greater in Group 2 (NPDR) than in Group 3 (PDR).

Regarding glucose-lowering therapies, while the use of oral antidiabetic agents did not differ significantly between Group 1 and Groups 2–3, insulin use was markedly greater in the latter groups (i.e., patients with DR).

Concerning concomitant cardiovascular medications, the use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEis) or angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) and diuretics was significantly greater in Groups 2 and 3 than in Group 1. Interestingly, statin use was significantly more common in Group 2 (NPDR) than in Group 3 (PDR).

Serum and urinary mindin levels

Analysis of mindin levels revealed distinct patterns. The serum mindin concentration was significantly lower in Group 3 (PDR) than in both Group 1 and Group 2 (NPDR) (p < 0.001 for Groups 1–3; p = 0.001 for Groups 2–3; Table 2).

Although serum mindin levels were also lower in Group 2 (NPDR) than in Group 1 (DM without DR), this difference did not reach statistical significance. No significant difference in serum mindin was found between the control group and groups 1–2 (Fig. 1).

Serum mindin levels in all groups

In contrast, 24-hour urinary mindin levels were significantly higher in Groups 1, 2, and 3 compared to the non-diabetic control group (p = 0.046 for Group 1, p < 0.001 for Groups 2 and 3, Table 2). While urinary mindin levels were numerically highest in group 3, the differences between groups 3 and 1–2 were not statistically significant (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that 24-hour urinary mindin excretion is significantly elevated in patients with type 2 DM compared with non-diabetic controls and, more importantly, is positively associated with the severity of diabetic retinopathy (DR). These findings suggest that urinary mindin may serve as an additional biomarker, independent of HbA1c and proteinuria, that reflects both the presence and severity of DR. According to multivariate logistic regression analysis, 24-hour urinary mindin emerged as the second strongest independent predictor of retinopathy, following HbA1c. In contrast, serum mindin levels were negatively correlated with DR severity.

The etiopathogenesis of DR is multifactorial, with oxidative stress [14–15] and chronic low-grade inflammation [16–20] recognized as central mechanisms. These pathways are closely linked, with inflammation representing a tissue response to oxidative stress-induced homeostatic imbalance. Despite the well-characterized pathophysiology and treatments targeting glycemic control, blood pressure, and lipid levels, the global prevalence of DR continues to rise. The progression of retinopathy in some patients despite adequate management of conventional risk factors suggests that additional, less well-defined molecular mediators are involved.

Mindin has been identified as a key regulator of inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-6 [20]. Leukocyte recruitment to sites of inflammation requires intricate interactions between leukocyte integrins and endothelial ligands. Mindin serves as a novel ligand for integrins and is critical for the migration of inflammatory cells [9], positioning it as a potential therapeutic target across various diseases. Similarly, in the retinal microvasculature, in a dextran sulfate sodium–induced rat model of acute colitis, Mindin aggravated colonic inflammation and promoted NF-κB promoter activation via Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR-9). Conversely, mindin-deficient rats exhibit severely impaired immune responses [21], underscoring the fundamental role of mindin in both innate and adaptive immunity.

A number of studies have established a correlation between mindin and DN. Murakoshi et al. [10] reported in a type 2 DM rat model that podocyte injury was more severe in a high-calorie group, and urinary mindin levels served as an early marker of this injury, detectable prior to changes in the albumin-to-creatinine ratio. In human studies, Kahvecioglu et al. [22] reported that serum mindin levels increased in parallel with proteinuria and decreased renal function in DN patients and were positively correlated with blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, and urinary albumin excretion. However, they concluded that serum mindin was inferior to microalbuminuria in diagnosing nephropathy and that urinary mindin was a more sensitive indicator of kidney damage than its serum counterpart. The elevated serum mindin levels in patients with reduced renal function may be attributable to decreased renal clearance.

In the present study, we found that alterations in urinary mindin levels were more pronounced and clinically significant than changes in serum levels in relation to DR. All diabetic subgroups had an estimated glomerular filtration rate within the normal range, whereas proteinuria exceeded the reference limit (> 150 mg/day) in patients with retinopathy (Groups 2 and 3). Serum creatinine, a late marker of renal impairment, was normal in these patients, indicating the presence of early, potentially hemodynamic alterations at the glomerular filtration barrier. The significant correlation between urinary mindin and proteinuria in our cohort suggests that urinary mindin reflects the degree of podocyte stress or injury, in line with prior recommendations to prioritize urinary rather than serum mindin measurement [10].

The absence of a significant correlation between serum and urinary mindin levels in our study, coupled with their opposing relationships with DR severity (positive for urine, negative for serum), implies distinct regulatory mechanisms or compartmentalization of mindin in diabetes. We also identified a significant positive correlation between urinary mindin and HbA1c, reinforcing the link between chronic hyperglycemia and this novel biomarker. In our multivariate model, HbA1c, 24-hour urinary mindin, and proteinuria emerged as independent determinants of retinopathy.

The close association between microalbuminuria and DR in patients with DM has been well documented; Rani et al. [23] reported twofold and sixfold higher prevalences of DR in patients with microalbuminuria and macroalbuminuria, respectively. Consistent with these observations, we detected significantly higher proteinuria in patients with DR and confirmed that proteinuria was an independent risk factor for retinopathy in our cohort. Mindin has been proposed as an early marker of glomerular basement membrane injury [24]. Through its roles in inflammation and cell‒matrix adhesion within the glomerulus, mindin may contribute to filtration barrier dysfunction [10], ultimately leading to podocyte loss, glomerulosclerosis, and proteinuria. The correlation between proteinuria and urinary mindin in our study suggests that elevated urinary mindin, which is detectable early in the pathogenic cascade leading to DN, may also serve as a stimulating or parallel early biomarker for retinopathy risk. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the relationships between DR and both serum and 24-hour urinary mindin levels.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted as a single-center investigation with a relatively modest sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the mean age of the control group did not match that of the diabetic groups perfectly, introducing the possibility of residual confounding. Another important limitation is the relatively small sample size per group (n = 25), which may have limited the statistical power to detect intergroup differences, especially between diabetic subgroups (Group 2 vs. Group 3). Future multicenter studies with larger, age-matched cohorts are warranted to confirm and extend our results. Finally, the higher ACE inhibitor/ARB use in Groups 2 and 3 may have influenced urinary mindin excretion; therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

In conclusion, 24-hour urinary mindin excretion increases in parallel with the severity of DR and acts as an independent predictor of retinopathy, second only to HbA1c. Its correlation with proteinuria underscores the shared pathophysiological pathways underlying diabetic microvascular complications. The dissociation between serum and urinary mindin levels highlights the importance of the urinary compartment for biomarker assessment. Our findings reaffirm the central roles of chronic hyperglycemia (HbA1c) and proteinuria in DR pathogenesis while adding urinary mindin as a potential complementary marker of microvascular damage.

As the first study to assess mindin in both serum and urine in the context of DR, our work provides a foundation for future research. Experimental and longitudinal clinical studies are needed to elucidate the precise molecular pathways involved in the development and progression of diabetic retinopathy and to determine whether targeting these pathways may have therapeutic implications.
