Scar revision for persistent pain and activity limitation after exploratory laparotomy in infancy: A case series
Authors: Shruthi Srinivas, Katherine C Bergus, Ethan A Mezoff, Peter C Minneci
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractExploratory laparotomy in neonates is typically performed via a transverse laparotomy incision. However, this incision may be complicated by poor cosmesis and scar contracture. In three patients, primary gastroenterologists identified significant scar contractures that resulted in pain and limitations with physical activity, necessitating surgical referrals. All patients required subsequent surgical revision of their scar, which involved creation of skin flaps, repair of abdominal wall hernias if present, and reapproximation of the subcutaneous tissue. We describe this phenomenon and the resultant need for surgical management to raise awareness of these late complications and suggest subcutaneous tissue reapproximation should be performed when possible during abdominal wall closure.Keywords: exercise intolerance, neonatal laparotomy

INTRODUCTION

Exploratory laparotomy may be indicated in neonates for a variety of pathologies, including necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), spontaneous intestinal perforation, and more. 1 Short‐term mortality is well‐studied, with higher rates in patients with laparotomy‐confirmed NEC, very low birth weight, and in those undergoing intervention in the neonatal intensive care unit. 2 , 3 However, few studies examine long‐term outcomes in neonates undergoing exploratory laparotomy.

Typically, neonatal exploratory laparotomy is performed by a transverse abdominal incision, allowing for maximal exposure, reasonable operative time, and low wound complication rates. 4 However, transverse incisions may be complicated by poor cosmesis and abdominal wall hernia given the significant abdominal muscular disruption. 5 In children undergoing transverse laparotomy, complications related to the abdominal wall scar may only become apparent as children grow into healthy teenagers. Scar contracture has the potential to limit activities and cause pain and discomfort during periods of growth.

We present a series of patients who underwent neonatal laparotomy via a transverse abdominal incision and developed abdominal scar contracture as adolescents, subsequently requiring revision.

METHODS

We performed a retrospective review of patients presenting for transverse laparotomy scar revision between 2013 and 2023. Patients were identified per review of the operating surgeon's records. Chart review was performed to determine the clinical course for each patient, including diagnosis and operative course at the initial operation, symptoms at the time of diagnosed scar contracture, and surgical management of scar contracture. We also collected data when available on outcomes following scar revision. The study was approved by the Nationwide Children's Hospital Institutional Review Board (IRB STUDY00004262).

RESULTS

Case 1

The patient is an ex‐premature infant with bowel obstruction secondary to ileal duplication cyst. She underwent exploratory laparotomy, small bowel resection, ileostomy, and mucous fistula via transverse laparotomy incision at 10 days of life. Stoma takedown was uneventful. At age 14, she was referred to pediatric surgery, at which time her scar had contracted down to the muscular level, limiting her range of motion, and causing pain.

She underwent scar revision. After scar excision, we visualized the muscles appropriately contracting, but the skin was misaligned such that the upper midline was off to one side. Skin flaps were created superiorly and inferiorly. Then, the midline was realigned with absorbable suture. The abdominal wall was closed in four layers (Figure 1A). Postoperatively, she did well. As of most recent follow‐up, her scar has been well‐healing (Figure 1B). She has subsequently returned to activity without need for continued surgical follow‐up.

Abdominal wall scars in Case 1, immediately post‐revision (A) and at postoperative follow‐up (B). This patient has not required further operative intervention and has otherwise returned to activity.

Case 2

The second patient is an ex‐29‐week neonate that underwent transverse laparotomy and small bowel resection for NEC leading to short bowel syndrome. At age 12, after achieving enteral autonomy, she presented to pediatric surgery clinic for evaluation of her abdominal wall scar. The scar had been causing her soreness and pain, worse with exercise, for approximately 6 months, which correlated with a period of recent growth. On exam, she had a healed transverse supra‐umbilical scar adherent to the muscular layer (Figure 2A). The dermis was fixed at the fascial level, raising concern for a possible incisional hernia.

Abdominal wall scars in Case 2, before revision (A) and immediately post‐revision (B). In this patient, the abdominal incision was associated with a small ventral hernia requiring repair during revision.

She underwent scar revision and incisional hernia repair. After excising the scar, a small fascial defect was encountered. Several adhesions were lysed to free the fascial edges and allow for closure with permanent suture. Skin flaps were created, and the subcutaneous tissues, dermis, and skin were all closed separately. Postoperatively, the wound healed well, and she has had no further scar‐related limitations to activity or pain (Figure 2B).

Case 3

The third patient is an ex‐25‐week premature female with malrotation and midgut volvulus and underwent neonatal exploratory laparotomy, ileocecectomy, end ileostomy, and mucous fistula via a transverse laparotomy incision. At age 12, she developed pain with activity at the site of her prior supraumbilical transverse scar. Physical exam demonstrated a transverse scar fused to the muscle layer without obvious hernia. Images are not available.

She underwent revision due to symptoms of persistent pain and exercise intolerance. Her scar was approximately 30 cm in total length, and she was noted to have two small incisional hernias. Each was separately repaired. Skin flaps were created, and the wound closed in multiple layers. Postoperatively, she developed small seroma that required no further intervention; her pain resolved. She has had vague abdominal pain not centered at her scar or associated with exercise since and is being managed conservatively as she grows.

DISCUSSION

We describe a series of patients in whom the transverse abdominal scar developed contracture with adherence to the muscle layer, leading to pain and exercise limitations. Each required excision of the scar with skin flap creation and layered closure, resulting in improvement. We advocate for approximation of the subcutaneous tissue when able to ensure adequate healing in patients undergoing transverse laparotomy.

Transverse laparotomy has been the standard operative approach in children undergoing laparotomy due to the familiarity of the incision and the rapid exposure. In children who survive past infancy, transverse laparotomy is associated with poor cosmesis, but is also associated with higher rates of ventral hernia requiring reoperation compared to less invasive methodology. 4 However, despite data on higher rates of ventral hernias in transverse laparotomies, there is limited description of functional scar after transverse laparotomy. Given the size of the patient and the relative acuity of the operations, it can be difficult to approximate each layer of tissue in a transverse laparotomy. Additionally, as with all our patients, many infants undergo repeat laparotomy through the same incision for complications or for stoma takedown. Repetitive tissue trauma can make identifying the plane between layers of the abdominal wall difficult, which further increases the risk of fixing the scar to the fascial layer. At the molecular level, scar formation may result in increased fibroblastic activity and collagen formation, resulting in fibrosis and subsequent adhesions. 6 This may worsen with each repeat laparotomy, eventually contributing to fixed scars in pediatric patients given limited abdominal size. Scar contractures in other fields, such as burn surgery, are known to limit function, especially in the extremities, so it is logical that abdominal wall scars can contribute to pain and exercise intolerance as well. 7

In patients with fixed abdominal wall scars such as from a transverse laparotomy, we demonstrate that a period of growth may result in functional limitations, including pain, exercise intolerance, and, subsequently, a need for revision. In addition to the development of hernia, symptoms of abdominal pain with exercise, skin “puckering” at the incision, and difficulty with movements such as torso extension should raise concern for scar contracture. In adults undergoing similar operations, pain at the scar has required fascial scar release. 8 In addition to functional limitations, a transverse laparotomy carries cosmetic concerns for a growing child. In patients with similar abdominal incisions, only half report satisfaction with their abdominal wall later in life. 9 Therefore, it is important to recognize the limitations that may arise from transverse laparotomy, especially as children grow, and to consider referral to a surgical provider for consideration scar revision if necessary. While surgical evaluation and management ultimately is the ideal solution for a scar requiring revision, patients awaiting surgical management may benefit from silicone‐based creams to limit hypertrophic growth, steroid injection, and laser therapy to improve scar appearance and contracture. 10 This also reinforces the need to reapproximate the subcutaneous tissue to ensure optimal healing. Alternative incisions such as Pfannenstiel or circumbilical laparotomy may have a different incidence of scar complications; however, these should be balanced with surgeon comfort, need for rapid intervention, and additional complications.

CONCLUSION

In neonates undergoing transverse laparotomy, there is a risk of future functional limitation secondary to scar contracture. We describe this phenomenon and the resultant need for surgical management to raise awareness of these late complications and suggest subcutaneous tissue reapproximation should be performed when possible during abdominal wall closure.
