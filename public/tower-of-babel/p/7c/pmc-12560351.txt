Cholestasis as a complication in Kawasaki disease: an atypical clinical presentation
Authors: Jiaxiang Hong, Chenchen Liu, Xunmin Wang, Xing Rong, Rongzhou Wu, Maoping Chu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeCholestasis is an uncommon complication in Kawasaki disease (KD) that impacts the digestive system. This study aimed to examine the clinical characteristics and associated risk factors of KD complicated by cholestasis.MethodsAmong the 3026 KD patients admitted to Yuying Children’s Hospital, affiliated with Wenzhou Medical University, from January 1, 2008, to December 31, 2020, all 72 children with cholestasis were included in the study. We performed a comprehensive analysis of the clinical characteristics, prognosis, and potential risk factors associated with KD patients presenting with cholestasis.ResultsAll 72 patients (median age: 34.1 months; 52.8% male) met the diagnostic criteria for cholestatic hepatitis. The mean duration of hospitalization was 10 days. Clinically, the incidence of limb edema and cervical lymphadenitis was relatively low. Pre-treatment assessments of liver function revealed significantly elevated levels of alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, and γ-glutamyl transpeptidase, with median values of 227 U/L, 102 U/L, and 220 U/L, respectively. Abdominal ultrasonography indicated an increased incidence of cholecystitis (12.5%) and hepatosplenomegaly (32.9%), in the absence of gallstones. Additionally, patients demonstrated an increased likelihood of systemic involvement, including pancreatitis and aseptic pyuria, alongside significantly elevated inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), white blood cell count (WBC), and neutrophil levels.All patients received treatment with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and aspirin, leading to the normalization of liver function indicators within one week. In this study, 25.0% of the patients demonstrated resistance to IVIG therapy, with 2 cases experiencing acute pancreatitis and shock. Furthermore, 25 patients developed coronary artery lesions, which resolved over a 32-month follow-up period as assessed by cardiac ultrasound.ConclusionWhen KD is complicated by cholestasis, it impacts the liver and bile ducts, leading to liver damage, cholecystitis, and jaundice, while also intensifying systemic inflammatory responses and increasing the likelihood of additional systemic involvements. Despite these complications, the prognosis is generally favorable; however, there is an associated elevated risk of resistance to IVIG therapy.

Introduction

Kawasaki disease (KD) is a systemic vasculitis that primarily affects children under 5 years of age [1, 2]. Coronary artery lesions (CALs) are the most common complication of KD, including coronary artery dilatation (CADs) and coronary artery aneurysms (CAAs) [3–5]. Therefore, KD is considered a leading cause of acquired heart disease in children in developed countries [6]. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) has been established as the first-line treatment for controlling inflammation and reducing the incidence of CALs in KD, and has been shown to lower the occurrence of CAAs [7, 8]. However, approximately 10–20% of patients do not respond to the initial IVIG treatment [9, 10].

KD, being a form of vasculitis, has the potential to impact multiple organ systems, such as the respiratory, digestive, central nervous, and genitourinary systems [11]. In cases of atypical KD, children may exhibit acute abdominal symptoms, meningeal irritation, pneumonia, arthritis, joint pain, and renal impairment [12–15]. When KD presents with gastrointestinal symptoms, it is commonly characterized by gallbladder effusion, acute pancreatitis, and hepatic dysfunction [16]. In instances of liver function abnormalities, KD frequently manifests with clinical features including hepatomegaly, cholestatic hepatitis, hepatic artery aneurysm, and gallbladder fluid accumulation [17]. Several studies focused on patients with KD who exhibited cholestatic jaundice as an atypical initial and primary presentation. A retrospective study by Huang et al. involving 33 cases, identified jaundice and abdominal pain as the most prevalent clinical manifestations of liver and biliary involvement in KD, alongside the classic symptoms. Reports of KD accompanied by cholestasis are predominantly sporadic in the clinical literature [18]. Cholestasis is characterized by a significant increase in serum direct bilirubin levels due to disrupted bile flow, with its etiology potentially attributable to a variety of factors, including infections, anatomical anomalies of the biliary tract, endocrine disorders, genetic conditions, metabolic disturbances, exposure to toxins and pharmaceuticals, and cardiovascular dysfunction [19]. In this study, we examined 72 patients diagnosed with KD who also presented with cholestasis. We systematically analyzed and summarized their clinical manifestations, laboratory findings, imaging study results, prognostic outcomes, and associated risk factors.

Methods

Patients and definitions

This was a retrospective study. Participants were identified by searching medical records among patients with KD, including complete and incomplete Kawasaki Disease, who were admitted to the Yuying Children’s Hospital affiliated with Wenzhou Medical University between January 2008 and December 2020. Complete or incomplete KD was diagnosed according to the criteria published by the American Heart Association (AHA). Complete KD is diagnosed in the presence of fever lasting for at least 5 days, plus four of the following five principal clinical features: rash, bilateral conjunctivitis without exudate, inflammation of oral mucosa, cervical lymphadenopathy and extremity changes [7]. The diagnosis criteria of incomplete KD include fever lasting more than 5 days is associated with 2 or 3 clinical criteria [7], with or without CAA. Coronary artery anomalies was diagnosed by echocardiography.

KD children with cholestasis (n = 72) were enrolled in this study for further analyses. Cholestasis was defined as direct bilirubin (DB) was > 17.1 μmol/L when the total bilirubin (TB) was < 85.5 μmol/L or the DB was > 20.0% of the TB if the TB was > 85.5 μmol/L [19]. KD children without cholestasis were removed from this study (n = 2929) (Fig. 1).

Treatment schedule: All patients were given aspirin at a daily dose of 30–50 mg/kg during the acute phase, then switched to the anti-platelet dose (3–5 mg/kg once daily), following 48 h the disappearance of fever. Depending on the total immunoglobulin dose and body weight of the child, IVIG regimen was divided into two types: standard regimen (2 g/kg, single dose intravenously) and non-standard regimen (for example, 1 g/kg/d for 2 consecutive days) [20, 21]. The treatment of best choice for KD is IVIG at the dose of 2 g/kg of body weight, preferably given within 10 days / not later than the 10th day from KD onset, otherwise considered as delayed IVIG treatment [22].

IVIG resistance: Persistent or recrudescent fever for at least 36 h and less than 7 days after completion of initial IVIG infusion [7].

Coronary artery abnormalities (echocardiography) were classified based on the Z score scheme, with the following definitions: (1) No involvement: Always < 2 mm. (2) Dilation: 2 mm to < 2.5 mm. (3) Small aneurysm: ≥ 2.5 mm to < 5 mm. (4) Medium aneurysm: ≥ 5 mm to < 10 mm, and absolute dimension < 8 mm. (5) Large or giant aneurysm: ≥ 10 mm, or absolute dimension ≥ 8 mm [7]. Since the peak incidence of CALs in KD occurs between 2–4 weeks, we required for at least two echocardiography examinations after the onset of KD, with an interval of at least one week between the two. To avoid missed diagnoses, any CALs meeting the above criteria detected within 1 month of KD onset were considered to have occurred. In patients with KD who developed CALs, we performed long-term cardiac ultrasound follow-up until the disappearance of CADs.

Statistical methods

First, demographics and clinical features of cholestasis patients were recorded. Continuous variables were expressed using the mean ± standard deviation if they conformed to a normal distribution, or the median and quartiles if they were skewed. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies. Continuous variables were compared using two independent samples t test or rank-sum test as appropriate; categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher test. Multiple logistic regression models were used to assess the effect of cholestasis and other factors on IVIG resistance. In model 1, we adjusted only age (in months) and gender. In model 2, we adjusted for age (in months), gender, KD type, treatment regimen, and time of IVIG treatment. In Model 3, we additionally adjusted for laboratory indices, including platelet count (PLT), D-dimer, activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and hemoglobin (HB).IBM SPSS version 26 was used for data analyses. P < 0.05 was considered for statistically significant.

Results

Clinical presentations

This study included 72 children with KD, comprising 38 boys and 34 girls, who developed cholestatic hepatitis during the disease courses. Their median age at onset was 34.3 [23.6–56.1] months (Table 1). The diagnosis of cholestatic hepatitis was confirmed by liver function tests at 2 to 5 days after fever, with a median of 3 days. All the 72 children had fever over 5 days, which was the major of KD-related clinical features. Other KD-related principal clinical features included non-exudative conjunctival injection (67/72), changes in lips (67/72), rash (66/72), changes in the extremities (60/72), and cervical lymphadenopathy (48/72).Sixty-five children were diagnosed with complete KD, due to the fact that they have fever over 5 days plus at least four principal clinical features of KD. The remaining 7 cases were diagnosed with incomplete KD, due to they have fever over 5 days and only presented 2 or 3 related clinical features. Among them, lip changes (6/7) was the most common KD-related clinical feature. Other clinical symptoms of KD were rash (5/7), non-exudative conjunctival injection (5/7), changes in the extremities (2/7), and cervical lymphadenopathy (1/7).

CRP was elevated in all 72 patients, with a median of 120 mg/L. The counts of WBC and neutrophils(NE) were also elevated in most cases, and their medians were 14.2 × 109/L and 11.5 × 109/L respectively. ESR and NT-pro BNP was also significantly elevated, with median values of 39.5 mm/h and 2170 pg/ml. The median of D-Dimer levels was 2.6ug/ml.

Liver manifestations at admission

All the 72 cases underwent liver function tests at admission. Eleven patients had obvious cholestatic jaundice, with TB > 85umol/L. The median of TB and DB were 52.1 umol/L and 30.9 umol/L respectively. Among the 72 patients, 70 had elevated ALT and/or AST, but 2 had normal ALT and AST levels. The medians of ALT and AST levels were 227U/L and 102U/L respectively. The elevation was more pronounced in ALT than AST, with a median of ALT/AST ratio of 1.9. Ten cases met biochemical criteria for significant liver injury, including 6 with ALT > 500U/L and 4 with both ALT and AST > 500U/L. Hepatotoxicity or liver injury is defined as a rise in either serum ALT level or serum AST to more than three times the upper limit of normal (ULN), ALP level more than twice the ULN, or total bilirubin level more than twice the ULN [77].All patients had GGT elevation (ref: < 50U/L), with a median of 220U/L. The levels of albumin were normal in 46 patients, but 26 cases had low levels of albumin, including 6 with albumin < 30 g/L. The median of albumin was 37 g/L. PT was tested in 68 patients, showing all slightly elevated (above 13 s), but their INR was normal.

Sixty-nine patients carried out ultrasound tests, revealing 19 with hepatomegaly and 8 with splenomegaly. Gallstone was not found.

Treatment and outcome

All the 72 patients received IVIG (2 g/kg) and aspirin treatment after diagnosis of KD. IVIG resistance was identified in 18 patients. The prevalence of IVIG resistance was 25% (18/72). It was higher than the identified (6.1%, 104/1712) reported previously by our team (P < 0.001) [23]. After IVIG treatment, body temperature of 70 patients returned to normal within 2 weeks. The body temperature of 2 patients did not change after treatment, while the remaining patients’ body temperature and clinical symptoms were all cured. All 25 patients were found to have CALs in the one-month follow-up cardiac ultrasound (34.7%), with 23 patients showing resolution of CADs in the 1-year cardiac ultrasound follow-up. Eventually, 2 patients were eventually found to be completely cured during the 32-month cardiac ultrasound follow-up.

All cases had a re-examination of liver and bile-related indicators after IVIG treatment. Approximately 4–6 days after treatment, 28 patients showed a conspicuous decrease in indicators, with median values of 46U/L for ALT, 35U/L for AST, 10.7 µmol/L for TB, 6.5 µmol/L for DB, 143U/L for GGT, and 32.9 g/L for ALB. In the final re-examination around 1 week after treatment, all the above indicators had radically returned to normal (50/72), with median values of 30U/L for ALT, 37U/L for AST, 10.0 mol/L for TB, 4.2 µmol/L for DB, 79.5U/L for GGT, and 35.1 g/L for ALB (Fig. 2). Patients with delayed normalization of liver indicators (> 7 days) showed a significantly higher rate of persistent coronary artery lesions (CALs) at 1-month follow-up (adjusted OR = 3.8, 95% CI 1.2–12.1, p = 0.02). This suggests that early liver function recovery may predict CAL resolution, identifying patients requiring closer cardiac surveillance.

Discussion

The etiology and pathogenesis of KD remain inadequately understood; however, it is hypothesized that infectious agents may precipitate the onset in individuals with genetic predisposition [24–26]. As a systemic vasculitis, KD has the potential to impact multiple organ systems, with less prevalent manifestations including arthritis, jaundice, hepatitis, myocarditis or heart failure, pericarditis, diarrhea, anterior uveitis, headaches, and aseptic meningitis [27–29]. Gastrointestinal symptoms associated with KD are often challenging to detect in the early stage of the condition. Cholestasis is an infrequent complication and characteristic feature of KD. Nonetheless. It is imperative to consider the hepatic and biliary manifestations associated with KD [30]. Acute febrile jaundice ranks as the second most prevalent manifestation of KD, affecting approximately 20% of cases [31, 32]. In recent years, there has been a growing awareness and focus on the concurrence of KD and cholestasis. Our study underscored the significance of cholestasis in KD.

While elevated conjugated bilirubin levels are indicative of cholestasis, the underlying etiology remains complex. The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying cholestasis in patients with KD remain inadequately elucidated. Presently, it is hypothesized that infections of bacterial, viral, and parasitic origin may contribute to the onset of cholestasis. Notable infectious agents implicated include cytomegalovirus, Toxoplasma gondii, rubella virus, herpes simplex virus, echovirus, adenovirus, and parvovirus B19, among others [33]. Epidemiological and pathogenic investigations suggested a potential association between various pathogenic infections and the incidence of KD. It has been reported that Streptococcus, Mycoplasma, Chlamydia, adenovirus, enterovirus, rhinovirus, coronavirus, and EB virus may be related to the onset of KD [24]. The current superantigen hypothesis suggests that by overactivating T lymphocytes, various cytokines secreted by activated CD4 + T cells can promote B cell polyclonal activation, proliferation, and differentiation into plasma cells, leading to a sharp increase in the number of inflammatory factors and ultimately causing vasculitis in the body [34].

Furthermore, existing research posits that KD is predominantly attributable to host immune dysfunction, which leads to endothelial damage. In addition to the coronary arteries, lesions may also impact other systemic vessels, resulting in compromised blood supply to associated organs. In patients with KD who present with jaundice, autopsy pathology studies demonstrated the frequent presence of enlarged mesenteric lymph nodes. Microscopically, these nodes exhibit perivascular inflammatory infiltrates, primarily periarteriolar, composed of neutrophils, eosinophils, and lymphocytes [35]. Consequently, the manifestation of cholestasis in KD may be attributed to vasculitis affecting the hepatic and biliary vessels, as well as inflammatory responses within the liver and gallbladder [36].

Gallbladder enlargement are most commonly caused by congenital choledochal cysts, acquired cholecystitis, extrahepatic biliary atresia, hemoglobin variants, spherocytosis,and lithiasis [37, 38]. During a gallbladder ultrasound examination, one of our patients was diagnosed with a congenital choledochal cyst. Toshihiko et al. [39] reported a case of acute KD patient with common bile duct (CBD) dilation elevated direct bilirubin level, and hepato-biliary abnormalities, suggesting CBD dilation as a complication of KD. It is imperative to meticulously differentiate KD from congenital choledochal cysts that present with infection or obstruction, as well as from acute cholecystitis, since these conditions necessitate distinct treatment strategies [40]. Acute acalculous cholecystitis (AAC) is reported at a lower frequency in KD patients [41, 42], but the incidence is higher in patients with cholestasis [43]. Nevertheless, the identification of acute acalculous cholecystitis is merely an associated finding in KD. In related vasculitic conditions, such as systemic lupus erythematosus, AAC was observed as a rare initial manifestation and a distinctive form of disease exacerbation [44]. Previous research demonstrated that in patients with AAC associated with systemic lupus erythematosus, aggressive treatment of the underlying disease with glucocorticoids could lead to a favorable prognosis, thereby obviating the need for surgical intervention [45].

The etiology of acute hemorrhagic gallbladder (AHGB) and AAC in KD remains poorly understood. In pediatric cases, AAC is predominantly linked to viral infections, particularly HAV and EBV. The shared pathophysiological mechanisms include lymphadenopathy surrounding the gallbladder duct leading to obstruction, vasculitis or perivasculitis of the gallbladder wall, and inflammatory infiltration by polymorphonuclear cells, lymphocytes, and eosinophils [46]. Pathological examinations of gallbladders excised from patients with KD revealed generally benign and self-limiting gallbladder effusion, as well as AAC attributed to viral infections or rheumatic conditions [47]. Conservative management of the underlying etiology is often sufficient to control acute abdominal symptoms, thereby obviating the need for surgical intervention [48]. However, some researchers highlighted that sonographic abnormalities of the gallbladder were correlated with elevated serum CRP, ALT and NE, as well as resistance to IVIG therapy. These findings suggested that gallbladder abnormalities may serve as a predictive marker for IVIG resistance in KD patients [46]. According to the large case series published by Yi et al. [49] which included 131 children with AAC, 28 cases had KD, and 26.7% of patients were affected with a systemic (non-infectious) disease. In an intriguing finding, the authors observed that in addition to more severe clinical manifestations, the presence of AAC during the acute phase of KD was statistically associated with an increased risk of coronary artery complications [50]. In this study, among the 10 KD patients exhibiting AAC, four patients (40%) developed resistance to IVIG treatment, and one patient (10%) experienced coronary artery damage. The incidence of IVIG resistance was elevated in patients with cholestasis compared to those with typical KD and close to statistical significance (P = 0.06), aligning with previous research findings.

In recent years, considerable scholarly attention has been directed towards exploring the relationship between intestinal microbiota and KD. Previous research suggested that in children with underdeveloped immune cell function, the typical colonization of intestinal microbiota may provoke an excessive immune response, potentially resulting in the onset of KD [51]. Notably, significant differences in the composition of intestinal microbiota were observed between the acute and non-acute phases in children with KD, characterized by an increase in pathogenic bacteria and a reduction in probiotic populations during the acute phase [52, 53]. In children with biliary atresia leading to cholestasis, the “enterohepatic circulation” status of bile acids is thought to be different, resulting in different gut microbiota structures and functions [54]. Reduced bile acid levels may contribute to gut microbiota dysbiosis, which is associated with liver function and holds significant diagnostic potential for biliary atresia [55]. Several studies reported that the interaction between abundant Klebsiella (K. pneumoniae), Veillonella (V. atypica), and Enterococcus (E. faecium) and the metabolic imbalance of tryptophan and bile acids directly or indirectly exacerbates liver damage in cases of biliary atresia [56].

Numerous scholars have highlighted the pivotal role of the gut microbiota-bile acid axis in the progression of liver fibrosis through diverse pathways. Obstructed bile flow can lead to gut microbiota dysbiosis and compromise the integrity of the intestinal mucosal barriers, thereby facilitating bacterial translocation. This microbial translocation subsequently activates immune responses, further advancing liver fibrosis [57]. In patients with KD experiencing cholestasis, we hypothesized that analogous immune responses and pathogenic mechanisms may be present. In a study examining the association between intestinal microbiota and KD, the gut microbiota composition of children with KD was compared to the healthy children. The findings indicated an increased abundance of the phyla Actinobacteria and Actinomycetes. At the genus level, there was a notable decrease in the genera Veillonella and Clostridium, alongside an increase in the genera Enterococcus and Bacteroides. Additionally, children in the non-acute phase exhibited a significant presence of the butyric acid-producing genus Rothia [58]. The overlapping and interacting genera of intestinal microbiota may contribute to cholestasis, a characteristic feature of KD, although the precise mechanisms underlying this association have yet to be elucidated. Interestingly, some studies proposed that maternal consumption of butyrate salts may confer protection against bile duct injury in newborns. The gut microbiota, through its production of metabolites such as butyrate and glutamine, which inhibit immune cell activity and promote survival signals in epithelial cells, respectively, may indirectly safeguard the liver and bile ducts from virus-induced tissue damage [59]. In KD, the butyrate and lactate salts produced by probiotics provide energy to intestinal epithelial cells, reduce pathogen adhesion, and enhance the protective function of the intestinal barrier [60]. This study offers a novel perspective on the treatment of KD patients with concurrent cholestasis.Based on the document, the novel perspective on treating KD patients with concurrent cholestasis involves targeting the gut microbiota-bile acid axis. Specifically, it suggests that probiotics or their metabolites (like butyrate and lactate) could enhance intestinal barrier function, reduce pathogen adhesion, and potentially mitigate liver, bile duct inflammation and damage associated with cholestasis in KD.

Our findings indicated that children diagnosed with KD and cholestasis tend to be younger on average, experienced prolonged hospitalizations, and exhibited a higher incidence of resistance to IVIG therapy. Furthermore, these patients demonstrated elevated levels of C-reactive protein and neutrophils, with a median CRP concentration of 120.0 mg/L. Liver function tests revealed significant increases in aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, and gamma-glutamyl transferase. Notably, serum GGT activity serves as an indicator of bile duct cell damage, with elevated levels suggesting bile duct injury, thereby constituting one of the most characteristic early manifestations of this condition [61]. In the diagnosis of KD with cholestasis, elevated liver function indicators and GGT levels may facilitate the diagnostic process. Numerous studies indicated that liver function markers could serve as indicators of inflammation and may potentially predicted the development of IVIG resistance or CAAs [62, 63]. Newberger et al. found that nearly 18% of KD patients with fever, high CRP levels (> 100 mg/L), jaundice, and elevated liver function have a poor response to IVIG [64]. One hypothesized mechanism by which cholestasis contributes to IVIG resistance involves elevated levels of inflammation. Cholestasis is a prevalent complication in patients with extrapulmonary bacterial infections and sepsis. Proinflammatory cytokines and nitric oxide are implicated in the pathogenesis of cholestasis by inducing hepatocyte damage and impairing bile duct formation [65]. IVIG resistance is generally considered a feature of severe cases [66]. Peng Hu et al. found that plasma cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α are involved in acute-phase KD inflammation, with remarkably higher levels of inflammatory cytokines in non-responders compared to responders [67]. In our patients, after IVIG treatment, albumin levels remarkably decreased, and neutrophil counts increased. ALB levels are effective biomarkers for measuring the activity of inflammatory conditions [68]. The breakdown metabolism of albumin is directly related to the severity of the acute inflammatory phase and is considered a factor in IVIG resistance [69, 70].

Following the administration of standard IVIG treatment, all patients exhibited normalization of liver function indicators and bilirubin levels within one week. This benign, self-limiting process aligned with previous research findings, indicating that liver and biliary dysfunction associated with KD is transient and self-limiting [71]. It was reported that 5–20% of KD patients develop gallbladder hydrops [16, 72], with approximately 12.5% of our patients appearing, especially in the first two weeks. Gallbladder hydrops was reported to be a transient process in KD. Therefore, for children with KD combined with cholestasis, medications, surgery, or other interventions may play a role, but the most crucial aspect is timely identification and proper anti-inflammatory treatment for KD. In our study, we identified two cases of KD complicated by cholestasis and concurrent acute pancreatitis, which progressed to Kawasaki Disease Shock Syndrome (KDSS) during the disease course. However, clinical reports on similar cases were scarce, with only two reported cases of CBD stenosis and pancreatitis in KD [73]. Studies by Amano [74] and Rowley [75] showed that histological diagnosis of pancreatitis is relatively common in fatal KD, occurring in 22 to 36% of cases. In the related studies, all patients succumbed to complications associated with KD, suggesting that the more severe manifestations of the disease accounted for the frequent occurrence of pancreatitis observed in these cases. This observation also elucidated why, in our study, aside from the two patients who progressed to KDSS, other patients who did not develop pancreatitis exhibited a relatively favorable prognosis. Regrettably, a retrospective review of the cases revealed no documentation of jaundice as a clinical symptom during the study period. It is noteworthy that jaundice becomes clinically apparent only when bilirubin levels exceed 34.2 μmol/L. The mean total bilirubin concentration among our patient cohort was 57.2 µmol/L, a level that should be clinically detectable. This oversight may be attributed to the underestimation of the clinical significance of this symptom in KD within our routine practice. It is important to note that cholestasis, characterized by the accumulation of bile components including bilirubin, is not synonymous with jaundice. Literature indicates that jaundice may not be evident in the early stages of cholestasis [76]. However, this study was confined to several limitations. Firstly, there was an absence of extensive examination data for the patients, including recommended advanced imaging studies of the liver and gallbladder. Secondly, as a single-center study, the generalizability of our findings to other populations remains uncertain. Thirdly, the prevalence of IVIG resistance among the entire cohort of 3026 KD patients was not provided for comparative context. In the future, including more patients and calculating the overall incidence of IVIG resistance will be more valuable.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that in patients with KD complicated by cholestasis, the diagnosis can be facilitated by assessing serum bilirubin levels, elevated liver function tests, and gamma-glutamyl transferase levels. It is crucial to acknowledge that systemic involvement of other organs may occur concurrently, which could increase the risk of IVIG resistance and coronary artery lesions. Following standard treatment, the prognosis is favorable, and the condition is typically self-limiting. Consequently, when children with KD are admitted during the acute phase, clinicians should remain vigilant for signs of cholestasis. It is recommended to perform early serum bilirubin testing, as well as liver and gallbladder ultrasound examinations, to facilitate prompt identification of the condition.
