Melatonin ameliorates autistic-like behaviors by restoring gut microbiota-derived tryptophan metabolites
Authors: Jinghua Shen, Jingjing Gao, Lu Gao, Dongying Yan, Ying Wang, Jia Meng, Hong Li, Dawei Chen, Jie Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by social deficits and repetitive stereotyped behavior. Disrupted microbiota‒gut‒brain axis (MGBA) signaling contributes to the pathology of ASD and cognitive disability. Melatonin (MT), a naturally occurring compound, has shown potential in ameliorating core symptoms of ASD and mitigating gut microbiota dysbiosis, yet the underlying mechanism is poorly understood. This study aimed to investigate whether exogenous melatonin improves behavioral deficits in valproic acid (VPA)-exposed male offspring rats, and the modulation of gut microbiota-derived tryptophan metabolites. In prenatal VPA-induced model rats, microbial diversity and construction was analyzed through metagenomic sequencing, targeted-metabolomics and transcriptomics were conducted to explore related metabolic pathways and molecular profiles. We identified 7 gut bacterial genus causally associated to ASD: Faecali-bacterium, Lachnospiraceae, Ruminococcaceae, Butyricimonas, and Bacteroides exhibited protection, whereas Erysipelotrichaceae and Clostridia enhanced risk. The exacerbation of Erysipelotrichaceae and Clostridia by VPA versus restoration of Faecalibacterium, Butyricimonas, Bacteroides and Bifidobacterium by melatonin, which are known to participate in tryptophan metabolism. Correspondingly, systemic metabolomics pointed to melatonin’s restoration of tryptophan metabolic disorders (IDO1-kynurenine, TPH1/2-serotonin-melatonin, and Indole-3-propionic acid (IPA)) induced by VPA, paralleled the rectification of microglial reactivity, synaptic proteins, dendritic morphology, and hippocampal neurogenesis. These molecular profiles were further integrated by transcriptomics, highlighted tryptophan-derived neurotransmitters and neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, contributing to enhanced social and cognitive behaviors under melatonin intervention. Based on multi-omic analysis, our findings underscore key bacteria and metabolites contributing to neurological and immune dysfunction in VPA-exposed rats, providing novel targets for possible therapeutics of melatonin.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06163-8.Keywords: Melatonin, Gut-brain axis, Tryptophan metabolism, Hippocampal neurogenesis, Microglia, Neurodevelopmental disorders

Introduction

Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), characterized by behavioral and cognitive impairments stemming from brain dysfunction, affect intellectual, motor, linguistic, and social capacities [1]. Autism spectrum disorder (ASD), an early-onset NDD marked by social communication deficits and repetitive stereotyped behavior, has a global prevalence of 1%~2%, with a higher percentage co-occurring intellectual disability of approximately 23.0% vs. 0.7% in typically developing children [2, 3]. With complex and heterogeneous etiology, ASD is speculated to derive from the interplay between genetic susceptibility and environmental factors [4, 5]. Prenatal exposure to the antiepileptic drug valproic acid (VPA) is a recognized environmental risk for ASD, and VPA disrupts neural stem cell (NSC) differentiation, neuronal migration, and network formation, which are linked to excitatory/inhibitory imbalance and histone deacetylase inhibition [6, 7]. More importantly, rodent models of gestational VPA exposure recapitulate ASD-like behaviors and gut microbial dysbiosis observed in humans [8].

Disruption of the microbiota‒gut‒brain axis (MGBA) has been implicated in ASD [9], indeed, gut microbes critically mediate bidirectional gut‒brain communication, thereby shaping neural development and modulating neurotransmission and host behavior [10, 11]. Metagenomic analysis revealed reduced species of Sutterella, Prevotella, and Bacteroides alongside diminished bacterial diversity in ASD children, with metabolomic perturbations in neurotransmitter networks (serotonin, dopamine, histidine, and GABA) [12]. In contrast, probiotic supplements (e.g., Lactobacillus spp., Bifidobacterium spp.) or fecal microbiota transplantation from healthy individuals alleviates ASD symptoms [13, 14]. Moreover, innate immune dysfunction and neuroinflammation are also closely involved, during which, microbiome regulate both microglial biology and autism pathogenesis majorly through microbial metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT), and expression of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, TNFα, and IFN-γ [15, 16]. Furthermore, gut microbiota regulates postnatal hippocampal neurogenesis and neurological function by modulating the maturation and activation of microglia, which contributes to immune defense and synaptic pruning [17]. However, the underlying molecular and microbial profiles for hippocampal plasticity impairment in ASD remain unclear.

Fecal microbiota transplantation from ASD children into germ-free mice induces ASD-like behaviors and microbiota-related changes in tryptophan metabolism [18], diverging into Serotonin, Kynurenine, and Indole pathways. More than 90% of endogenous 5-HT comes from intestinal enterochromaffin cells and functions peripherally; due to nonpermeation of blood‒brain barrier, 5-HT is produced from the sole precursor tryptophan (Trp) in the brain [19], whereas germ-free rats exhibit reduced peripheral tryptophan and hippocampal 5-HT levels [20], implicating the microbiota in regulation of tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH1/2)-dependent 5-HT synthesis. Paradoxically, 25.4% of ASD individuals display elevated serum 5-HT but central serotonergic deficits (reduced 5-HT, 5-HTP, 5-HIAA, and 5-HT1A receptor density) [21, 22]. The Kynurenine (Kyn) pathway is modulated by gut microbes via the immunoresponsive enzyme indoleamine-2, 3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1), generates neuroprotective kynurenic acid (KA) and neurotoxic quinolinic acid (QA) [23]. Elevated Kyn in 58.7% ASD patients suggests IDO1 hyperactivity and an imbalance of Kyn/Trp ratio [24]. Furthermore, reduced gut microbiota-specific metabolite indole 3-propionic acid (IPA) in the serum of both animal model and ASD children have been reported [25]. Emerging studies suggest that immune-linked IPA/AhR (aryl hydrocarbon receptor) signaling stimulates adult neurogenesis and mediates synaptic pruning of hippocampal microglia [25, 26], constituting a microbiota-IPA-brain axis for treating ASD.

Melatonin (N-acetyl-5-methoxytryptamine), a pleiotropic molecule chemically classified as an indoleamine, is synthesized from serotonin through two rate-limiting enzymes, AANAT (arylalkylamine N-acetyltransferase) and ASMT (acetylserotonin O-methyltransferase), and exerts anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective effects in NDDs, in addition to governing circadian rhythms [27]. ASD patients exhibit aberrant melatonin synthesis in both pineal gland and gastrointestinal tract, with reduced serum melatonin levels [28, 29]. Maternal melatonin supplementation ameliorates social dysfunction and microbial dysbiosis in VPA-exposed female offspring, mainly through upregulation of Akkermansia spp in activating dopaminergic neurons [30]. As most recently reported, melatonin improves tryptophan-serotonin metabolism via inhibiting gut microbe Alistipes Inops, which alleviates depressive-like behaviors [31]. Therefore, we investigated whether exogenous melatonin could rescue social and cognitive deficits in VPA-exposed ASD model rats primarily through modulation of gut microbiota-derived tryptophan metabolites, which reversed neurotransmitters, microglial activation, and subsequent synaptic pathology in the hippocampus. Based on multi-omic analysis, our findings underscore the key role of MGBA in developing therapeutics for ASD.

Methods

Chemicals and reagents

Sodium valproate [powder, 98%, CAS#1069-66-5] and melatonin [powder, ≥ 98% (TLC), CAS#73-31-4] were obtained from Sigma‒Aldrich Trading Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Details of the primary antibodies used for western blotting are providing in the Supplementary Information: Appendix S1.

Animals and treatments

Adult Sprague‒Dawley rats (20 females and 10 males, 12 weeks old), obtained from Changsheng Biotechnology (Liaoning, China; licence number: SCXK, 2020-0001), were housed in an SPF animal room at constant temperature (22 ± 2 °C) and relative humidity (50 ± 5%) under a 12 h light/dark cycle, with access to food and water ad libitum. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Jinzhou Medical University (Approval number: 240123) and complied with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Specific details are provided in the Supplementary Information: Appendix S1.

Behavioral assays

All experiments were conducted under blinded conditions in randomized trial order, adolescent male rats were subjected to behavioral tests beginning from PD40, with 2-day intervals between each test. Detail protocols for Open-field test (OFT), Novel object recognition (NOR), Three-chambered social interaction test, and Morris water maze (MWM) were provided in the Supplementary Information: Appendix S1.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

The rats were anaesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital (0.3 ml/100 g body weight) and transcardially perfused with normal saline, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Hippocampal and colonic tissues were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (24 ~ 48 h), immersed in 30% sucrose, and embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) for cryosection (-20 °C). The 10-µm-thick colon sections were stained with hematoxylin for 10–15 min, after differentiation, counterstained with eosin (Solarbio Technology, Beijing) for 3 min. Dehydration through an ethanol/xylene series preceded neutral resin mounting. Stained sections were imaged under Olympus BX51 microscope.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis

After peroxidase quenching and protein blocking, the colon sections were incubated with antibodies (ZO1/Occludin, 1:50, 4 °C/overnight). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (37 °C/1 h) enabled DAB (3, 3’-diaminobenzidine) chromogenic development (5 min), followed by ethanol dehydration and mounting with neutral resin. Brightfield imaging (Olympus BX53, Japan) coupled with ImageJ facilitated quantitative evaluation.

Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis

Hippocampal frozen Sect. (30-µm or 10-µm thick) were blocked with 5% goat serum-0.03% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with the following primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: rabbit anti-Sox2 (1:500, Abcam#ab92494) and mouse anti-Ki67 (1:500, Abcam#ab279653), rabbit anti-DCX (1:500, Abcam#ab207175) and mouse anti-BrdU (1:200, Santa Cruz#sc-32323), rabbit anti-Iba1 (1:500, Abcam#ab178846). Then sections were exposed to FITC- or Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit/mouse IgG (1:200, ABclonal) for 1 h under dark and mounted with DAPI-containing anti-quench medium. Fluorescence imaging was performed via Leica M165FC microscope, colocalization and counting analyses were conducted via ImageJ software.

Western blotting (WB)

Tissue lysates (colonic or hippocampal: radioimmunoprecipitation buffer, RIPA) underwent BCA quantification and denaturation (95 °C×5 min) before SDS‒PAGE separation, followed by PVDF membrane transfer. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat-dried milk then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies as following: synaptic scaffolding proteins (SHANK3, Neurexin-3, Neuroligin-3, and MECP2); neurotransmitter transporter and receptors (SERT/SLC6A4, 5-HTR1A and GABRA6); tryptophan metabolism (IDO1, TPH1, and TPH2); tight junction proteins (ZO1 and occludin). After washes, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (ABclonal, Wuhan, China) enabled Chemiluminescent detection on GE imaging systems (General Electric, USA). Bands’ intensity quantified via ImageJ were normalized to GAPDH.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

ELISA quantification was performed via sandwich ELISA (JYMbio, Wuhan, China) to assess: melatonin concentrations in serum, colonic and cerebral extracts; AANAT, ASMT levels in colon and pineal; plus IL-1β and TNFα in serum and hippocampus. Serum and tissue lysates were analysed with precoated antibody plates. Standards and samples were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min with HRP-conjugated antibodies, followed by TMB substrate development. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured and the values were normalized to corresponding standard curves (R > 0.99).

Morphometric analysis of dendritic spines and 3D quantification

Hippocampal CA1 dendritic spines were visualized using the Rapid Golgi Stain™ Kit (FD NeuroTechnologies, USA) and quantified by ImageJ combined Imaris. Detailed procedures were provided in the Supplementary Information: Appendix S1.

Metagenomic sequencing and assembly

Fecal samples were collected under sterile conditions and frozen at -80 °C before DNA extraction and analysis. DNA library construction and metagenomic sequencing were performed by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Brief procedures were provided in the Supplementary Information: Appendix S1.

Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis

Bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization was conducted using MR and MR-PRESSO to infer causal relationships between the gut microbiota and ASD. Genome-wide significant SNPs (P < 1 × 10− 5) from the MiBioGen consortium GWAS (n = 18,340) served as instrumental variables (IVs.), clumped for independence (r² <0.001; 10,000 kb window) via IEU (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/) Autism Spectrum Disorder data (GWAS ID: ieu-a-1185, n = 46,351) [32–34]. Harmonized datasets were analyzed with inverse-variance weighted (IVW) regression (fixed/random effects based on Cochran’s Q heterogeneity test, P < 0.05), supplemented by MR-Egger (intercept p < 0.05), weighted median, and leave-one-out. The results surviving FDR correction (q < 0.10) demonstrated robust instrument strength (F-statistics: 21.6–144.8) and variance explained (R²=0.57–10.11%). Methodological transparency was ensured through scatter plots, forest/funnel diagrams, and leave-one-out sensitivity profiles.

Targeted metabolomics analysis

Metabolomics analysis for amino acid, SCFA, and neurotransmitters in serum and hippocampi of rats were performed via ultraperformance liquid chromatography‒mass spectrometry (UPLC‒MS) by Iphenome Biotechnology Inc. (Dalian, China). Detail analytic approaches were provided in the Supplementary Information: Appendix S1.

Transcriptomic sequencing

To delineate molecular dynamics, mRNA sequencing of hippocampal tissues was performed by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Brief procedures were provided in the Supplementary Information: Appendix S1.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Hippocampal RNA underwent stringent quality control (OD260/OD280, RIN > 8) were validated via reverse transcription followed by SYBR Green qPCR (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher, USA) under standardized procedures: 95 °C×30 s initiation; 40 cycles of 95 °C×5 s denaturation, 60 °C×30 s annealing; and melt curve analysis. Target genes were quantified via the 2-ΔΔCt method and normalized to Gapdh, with the primer sequences detailed in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Results were represented as mean±standard deviation (SD), except for specific illustration. Repeated-measures two-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) addressed escape latency of the MWM navigation test. Differences among groups were assessed by one-way ANOVA following Kolmogorov-Smirnov test for normality. SNK-q or Tukey’s post hoc test was employed for multiple comparisons, otherwise, Tamhane’s T2 test was chosen when variance homogeneity not assumed by Levene test. All analyses were conducted in SPSS (v25.0; IBM) with graphical visualization via Graphpad Prism version 8.0, R (v4.4.1), SRplot (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/) and Chiplot (https://www.chiplot.online/) [35]. Statistical significance was defined when P-value < 0.05.

Results

Early melatonin treatment ameliorates social and cognitive deficits in VPA-induced ASD rats

To investigate whether maternal melatonin supplementation could improve ASD symptoms, combined behavioral tests, including three chamber sociability, open field, novel object recognition, and Morris water maze, were conducted to evaluate sociability, spontaneous activity and anxiety, recognition and spatial memory.

Prenatal VPA exposure caused social and cognitive dysfunctions in adolescent male rats: significantly reduced social investigation (S1-E index: 0.03 vs. CON = 0.77, P < 0.0001; Fig. 1B and C), and inverted novelty preference (S2-S1 index: VPA =-0.09, vs. CON = 0.76, P < 0.0001; Fig. 1D and E) in three-chamber test. Hippocampus-dependent spatial learning (Fig. 1M and N) showed reduction in Morris water maze escape latency (day4 latency: 30.51 ± 4.25 s vs. CON = 22.07 ± 3.5 s, P < 0.0001), and day6 target quadrant duration: 6.32 ± 3.23 s vs. CON = 11.15 ± 3.16 s, P < 0.0001; crossings: 3.25 ± 1.76 vs. CON = 7.17 ± 2.12). Anxiety-like hypoactivity (centre duration: 34.13 ± 10.25 s vs. CON = 81.47 ± 24.29 s, P = 0.0003; Fig. 1J and K), and disrupted recognition memory (NOR index: 23.04 ± 17.1% vs. CON = 72.68 ± 23.02%, P < 0.0001; Fig. 1G and H) were observed in VPA rats.

Moreover, we found that melatonin intervention restored social homeostasis (S1-E index: MTV = 0.49, P = 0.0054; S2-S1 index: MTV = 0.7, P = 0.0054), as well spatial memory (day4 latency 25.67 ± 3.82 s, P = 0.0143) and probe trial precision (day6 target quadrant duration: 11.06 ± 3.3 s, P < 0.0001). Moreover, melatonin inversed anxiety (total distance: 1,920 ± 391 cm, P = 0.0019; centre duration: 59.67 ± 15.78 s, P = 0.0029), and improved object recognition (NOR index: 50.35 ± 22.5%, P = 0.0079 vs. VPA).

Melatonin rescues gut microbiota dysbiosis in VPA-exposed male rats

Metagenomic sequencing was performed to analyze the gut microbial composition and metabolic information from fecal samples. A total of 1,583 microbiota species were shared among all three groups (Fig. 2A), VPA-exposure altered alpha diversity (Shannon index: 11.28 ± 0.08 vs. CON = 11.67 ± 0.12, Δ =-3.34%, P = 0.0101; Simpson index: 0.982 ± 0.004 vs. CON = 0.962 ± 0.005, Δ = 2.08%, P = 0.0056, Fig. 2B), which reflects the richness and diversity of the microbota, while MT partially restored (Shannon/Simpson: 11.45 ± 0.11/0.961 ± 0.006). Beta diversity shifts reflecting the degree of similarity between microbiota communities was observed (Bray-Curtis PCoA, PCo1 = 44.18% variance, P = 0.002, Fig. 2C), highlighted VPA-driven compositional divergence (CON-VPA distance = 0.62 ± 0.03) was attenuated 2.14-fold by melatonin (VPA-MTV distance = 0.29 ± 0.02).

LEfSe analysis revealed Bacteroidota (phylum Bacteroidetes), and Bacillota (phylum Firmicutes) were predominant microflora at the phylum level, Muribaculaceae, Lachnospiraceae, Prevotella, and Lactobacillus were discriminative taxa at the genus level (Fig. 2D). Taxon-resolved heatmaps (Fig. 2E) delineated VPA-associated blooms of Erysipelotrichia (log2FC = + 2.03, vs. CON, FDR = 0.032) and Clostridia (log2FC = + 2.01, vs. CON, FDR = 0.028), alongside depletion of Ruminococcaceae (log2FC = − 4.13, vs. CON, FDR < 0.001), Bacteroides (log2FC = − 1.50, vs. CON, FDR = 0.028), Lachnospiraceae (log2FC = − 1.53, vs. CON, FDR = 0.012), and Butyricimonas (log2FC = − 1.32, vs. CON, FDR = 0.011), all counteracted by melatonin (e.g., Clostridia log2FC = -1.39, Ruminococcaceae log2FC = + 1.14, Bacteroides log2FC = + 1.22).

The predicted metabolomic profiling (Fig. 2G) corresponding to the differential microbiome indicated VPA-driven disruption of tryptophan metabolism, characterized by Trp depletion (log2FC = − 0.68 vs. CON), with MT restoring 58% of dysregulated metabolites (FDR = 0.035). At the same time, glutamate reduction (log2FC = − 0.44 vs. CON) and lactate accumulation (log2FC = + 1.21 vs. CON) were inversed by MT.

Melatonin improves tryptophan-metabolizing gut microbiota causally correlated to ASD

By utilizing GWAS from ASD cohorts, two-sample Mendelian randomization analyses illustrated causal connections between the gut microbiota and ASD, with OR values (Odds Ratio) and P-values calculated through the Inverse Variance Weighted (IVW) method (Fig. 3A).

Faecalibacterium (P = 0.028, Fig. 3B), Lachnospiraceae (P = 0.015, Fig. 3D), Ruminococcaceae (P = 0.001, Fig. 3E), Butyricimonas (P = 0.014, Fig. 3G), and Bacteroides (P = 0.036, Fig. 3H) were identified as protective taxa, whereas Erysipelotrichaceae (P = 0.041, Fig. 3C) and Clostridia (P = 0.037, Fig. 3F) exhibited risk-enhancing effects. Based on above MR results (Fig. 3) and comparative cohort analyses for tryptophan-metabolizing bacteria (Fig. 2F), we speculate that microbiota-modulated indole pathway of tryptophan metabolism as an intervention target in alleviating ASD symptoms.

Melatonin mitigates VPA-induced serum tryptophan metabolic perturbations

Metabolites are responsible for communication between the gut and brain. Here, serum metabolomics (Fig. 4A) delineated VPA-specific depletion of melatonin (log2FC = -0.72, P = 0.0023), alongside marked accumulation of serotonin (log2FC = + 3.41, P < 0.0001) and kynurenine (log2FC = + 1.03, P = 0.0003). MT administration normalized 82% of VPA-disrupted metabolites, particularly in tryptophan metabolism (Fig. 4C, Kyn/Trp ratio: log2FC = + 1.28→-1.51, P = 0.0017; microbiota-derived IPA: log2FC =།1.17→ +1.36, P = 0.0002), SCFA (Fig. 4B, Acetate: log2FC = །1.07→ +1.36, P = 0.0006; Propionate: log2FC = །2.04→ +2.58, P = 0.0019; Butyrate: log2FC = །1.60→ +2.56, P = 0.0005), and lipid peroxidation markers (bilirubin: log2FC = །1.02→ +0.69, P = 0.0041, Fig. 4B). Specifically, melatonin orchestrated systemic metabolism through dual modulation of host neuroendocrine signaling (e.g., serotonin-melatonin production) and microbial co-metabolite networks (e.g., SCFA, indole derivatives), resulting in recovery of circulating melatonin levels (Fig. 4C: MTV 1.55 ± 0.13 nmol/L vs. VPA 1.43 ± 0.09 nmol/L, Δ = +1.1-fold, P = 0.0182). Since overexpression of the IL-17 A receptor on ASD monocytes has recently been identified [36], KEGG pathway enrichment (Fig. 4D) indicated IL-17 A/IL-17 receptor signaling along with Trp-metabolism, illustrated the capacity of MT in enhancing gut barrier and suppressing neuroinflammation.

Based on metagenomic and metabolomics results, we also found an interesting fact that distinct microbial communities were significantly associated with tryptophan-derived metabolites (Mantel test, P ≤ 0.05; r ≥ 0.25). Mantel networks (Fig. 4E) revealed Bifidobacterium, Bacteroides, and lactobacillus exhibited robust positive correlations with serotonin (Mantel’s r = 0.62, P = 0.01), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) (r = 0.55, P = 0.01), and indole-3-propionic acid (IPA) (r = 0.67, P = 0.01). Regarding associations with MT itself: positive correlation involved Bacteroides (r = 0.63, P = 0.01); negative correlations included Clostridia (r = -0.72, P = 0.01), Akkermansia (r = -0.47, P = 0.03), and Erysipelotrichia (r = -0.35, P = 0.02). Crucially, MT intervention decoupled VPA-enhanced Clostridium-Kyn correlations (Pearson’s r = 0.82→0.31, P = 0.019), while amplified Butyricimonas-serotonin (r = 0.41 → 0.68, P = 0.015) and Lactobacillus-IPA (r = 0.37 → 0.64, P = 0.015) linkages, indicating microbiota-mediated neurochemical crosstalk.

Melatonin alleviates serotonergic neurotransmitter perturbation in VPA rats

Hierarchical clustering of hippocampal metabolites (Fig. 5A) revealed VPA-specific depletion of synaptic transmission mediators, notably gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA: VPA = 3.73 ± 0.15 vs. MTV = 3.91 ± 0.18, P = 0.0009) and glutamine (Glu: VPA = 3.35 ± 0.09 vs. MTV = 3.55 ± 0.21, P = 0.003). The shift in hippocampal GABA/ Glu ratio (Fig. 5A, log2FC = -0.15​ → +0.37, P = 0.007) after melatonin administration demonstrated remediation of excitatory-inhibitory imbalance, concerning critical role of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus in generating GABA [10]. Furthermore, KEGG pathways showed that Glutamatergic synapse was correlated with above neurotransmitters and tryptophan metabolites, including Trp, serotonin, and KA/ Kynurenic Acid (Fig. 5B). Concomitantly, tryptophan-derived metabolites including IPA (CON: 4.35 ± 0.38 vs. VPA: 3.99 ± 0.21, P < 0.0001) and serotonin/5-HT (CON: 0.70 ± 0.34 vs. VPA: 0.42 ± 0.21, P = 0.0143), exhibited parallel restoration under melatonin intervention (Fig. 5C).

VPA induced dysregulation of Kyn- and serotonin-related metabolic enzymes, including IDO1 (1.2-fold upregulation vs. CON, P < 0.0001) and TPH1/TPH2 (4.1/5.3-fold suppression, P < 0.0001), which were attenuated under melatonin treatment (Fig. 5D-E). Accumulative evidence has revealed that gut microbiota regulates autism-like behaviors through several mechanisms: restoring the excitatory/inhibitory neurotransmitter balance; upregulating the GABA receptor gene expression in the brain; increasing oxytocin synthesis in the hypothalamus; and decreasing the proinflammatory cytokines [37]. Concomitant rescue of neurotransmitter-receptor dynamics was observed in melatonin-treated rats through normalization of SERT (P < 0.01) and 5-HTR1A (VPA 0.33 ± 0.07 vs. MTV 0.5 ± 0.04, P = 0.0004), GABA receptor subunit α6 (GABRA6: VPA 0.26 ± 0.16 pg/ml vs. MTV 0.48 ± 0.11, P < 0.0001; Fig. 5G-H). Moreover, hypothalamic oxytocin (OXT) -oxytocin receptor (OXTR) signaling was sequentially restored (Fig. 5H-I).

Melatonin restores colonic barrier integrity and microglial morphology in DG of VPA rats

The gut microbiota helps produce melatonin from tryptophan, in turn melatonin exhibits beneficial effect on gut barrier function and microbial population [38]. Similarly, colonic serotonin-melatonin metabolic reprogramming was confirmed by normalization of colonic melatonin synthesis enzymes (AANAT: P = 0.0187; ASMT: P = 0.0209), concomitant with restoration of endogenous melatonin levels (MTV = 29.75 ± 2.15, vs. VPA = 23.78 ± 2.07, P = 0.021, Fig. 6A). H&E staining (Fig. 6B) revealed that control rats maintained an intact mucosal architecture with polarized epithelium (goblet cell density = 12.8 ± 1.2 cells/100 µm) and organized crypt-villus units (crypt depth = 152 ± 8 μm). VPA induced epithelial discontinuity (goblet cells = 5.3 ± 0.9 cells/100 µm, P < 0.001) and crypt distortion (depth = 98 ± 11 μm, P = 0.0002), which were mitigated by MT intervention (goblet cells density: 10.1 ± 1.1; crypt depth = 138 ± 9 μm, P < 0.01 vs. VPA). Immunohistochemical analysis (Fig. 6C and D) revealed VPA-induced barrier tight junction protein occludin/OCLN reduction by 57% (P < 0.0001) and ZO1 by 54% (P < 0.0001), whereas MT partially restored (OCLN: +45%, P = 0.0002; ZO1: +50% vs. VPA, P < 0.0001). The results of western blotting (Fig. 6E) likewise showed corresponding changes. These molecular shifts are correlated with functional outcomes: melatonin enhances gut barrier integrity. Consequently, circulating and hippocampal pro-imflammatory cytokines IL-1β and TNFα were both elevated in VPA model rats (P < 0.001) and alleviated after melatonin treatment (Fig. 6F and K).

Gut microbiota disruption related to increased gut permeability and neuroimmune responses in the CNS [37]. As the resident immune cells of the brain, microglia regulate brain development and homeostasis via core functions including phagotrophy and pruning of synapse. Hence microglial number and morphology was further analysed using immunofluorescence and ImageJ (Fig. 6G). In the hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG), increased number of Iba1 + cells, decreased branches and total branches length, along with enlarged cell body area were observed in VPA model rats (P < 0.01 vs. CON, Fig. 6H-J). Noteworthy, microglial abnormality in this region was attenuated by melatonin, corresponding to the alteration of IDO1 and IAA/IPA in serum and hippocampus.

Melatonin reverses VPA-induced hippocampal synaptic and neurogenic pathological changes

Correlated to neuroplasticity, high confident ASD-linked synaptic genes expression were verified. Our results (Fig. 7B, C) revealed VPA-driven depletion of the synaptic scaffolding or transmembrane proteins SHANK3 (log2FC = − 1.62, P = 0.0078), NRXN3 (log2FC = − 1.23, P = 0.0002), NLGN3 (log2FC = − 1.43, P = 0.0312), and MECP2 (log2FC = − 1.12, P = 0.019), as well as restoration under MT treatment (P < 0.05). For dendritic morphology, Golgi-Imaris 3D reconstruction and quantification (Fig. 7D-F) indicated VPA-induced dendritic spine depletion (CON = 9.86 ± 1.2 vs. VPA = 6.97 ± 0.9 spines/10 µm, P = 0.004), characterized by a reduction in mushroom-type spines and filopodia (P < 0.001). MT administration reversed these anomalies, restoring total spine density to 82% of control levels (8.44 ± 1.1 spines/10 µm, P = 0.001 vs. VPA) and promoting morphotypes (mushroom spines: MTV 1.48 ± 0.33, P = 0.0014 vs. VPA; filopodia: MTV 2.82 ± 0.63, P = 0.0084 vs. VPA).

Considering IAA/IPA-AhR pathway and interactional microglia priming, hippocampal neurogenesis was further evaluated. Ki67+/Sox2 + colocalization revealed impaired neuronal progenitor maintenance in VPA-exposed rats (P < 0.001), which was reversed by melatonin treatment (10.2 ± 0.8%, P = 0.018); the quantification of BrdU+/DCX+ neuroblasts revealed that melatonin restored neuronotropic differentiation competence (57% vs. VPA 43%, P = 0.021), paralleled by Sox2 + progenitor pool expansion (1,895 ± 152 vs. 1,323 ± 174 cells/mm² in VPA, P = 0.008) (Fig. 7G-J). The above integrated molecular and morphological analyses delineated VPA-induced changes in synaptic pathology, with multimodal reversal from melatonin.

Melatonin improves transcriptome profile associated with neurotransmission and tryptophan metabolism in VPA rats’ hippocampus

Comprehensive transcriptomic analyses revealed profound hippocampal dysregulation induced by VPA, and the ability of melatonin to restore synaptic and neurochemical homeostasis. Volcano plots (Fig. 8A-B) identified 218 VPA-altered genes (|log2FC|>1, FDR < 0.05), including those encoding tryptophan hydroxylases (TPH1: log2FC =-1.09, P = 0.0003; TPH2: log2FC = − 1.16, P < 0.0001) and the synaptic scaffolding protein SHANK3 (log2FC = − 0.75, P = 0.002). MT supplementation reversed 142/218 (65%) of these perturbations (Fig. 8A-B), rescuing TPH1 (log2FC = + 0.39, P = 0.007), TPH2 (log2FC = + 0.72, P = 0.002), and SHANK3 (log2FC = + 0.42, P = 0.015), respectively.

KEGG pathway enrichment (Fig. 8C) highlighted neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction and Jak-STAT signaling pathway, which was directly involved in neurotransmission and neuroinflammation. Moreover, VPA-driven disruption of serotonergic synapse (P = 1.97 × 10⁻³, FDR = 0.012) and tryptophan metabolism (P = 1.87 × 10⁻³, FDR = 0.015) were partially normalized by MT (serotonergic synapse FDR = 0.024; tryptophan metabolism FDR = 0.018). GSEA (Fig. 8D) confirmed significant downregulation of the tryptophan metabolism gene set (NES =-1.01, P = 0.005), reflecting suppressed immune-neurotransmitter crosstalk, which was attenuated by melatonin through IDO1 inhibition and IAA/IPA promotion.

Discussion

As reported consistently that individuals with ASD have distinct gut microbiome compared with neurotypical children, characterized by reduced species diversity, altered bacterial composition, impaired synthesis pathways for neuroactive metabolites, and a lagged bacterial developmental trajectory [37]. Exogenous melatonin can improve behavioral symptoms in ASD patients and rodent models, mediated through gut microbes and their metabolites partially [38, 39]. Here, we found that melatonin supplementation during gestation and lactation enhanced sociability and improved recognition and spatial memory in VPA model adolescent rats. These behavioral changes paralleled the rectification of microglial reactivity, synaptic proteins, dendritic morphology, and hippocampal neurogenesis. Moreover, melatonin triggered the depletion of Escherichia and Clostridia alongside augmented Bacteroides, Butyricimonas, Faecalibacterium, and Bifidobacterium thus restored tryptophan-derived serotonin and indoles (IAA, (indole-3-acetic acid) and IPA (indole-3-propionic acid)), as well as GABA.

Divergent microbiota in ASD individuals primarily converge on reduced Prevotella, Corprococcus, Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, and Proteobacteria, along with elevated Desulfovibrio spp. (positively related to the severity of ASD) or Clostridium spp. (a neurotoxin-producing bacteria) [9, 10]. As concluded more recently, better cognitive performance corresponded to higher levels of Bacteroides [40]. Through bidirectional Mendelian randomization analysis utilizing GWAS, we identified significant causal relationship between 7 gut bacteria and ASD: Faecalibacterium, Lachnospiraceae, Ruminococcaceae, Butyricimonas, and Bacteroides exhibited protection, Erysipelotrichaceae and Clostridia enhanced risk. These bacteria are robustly correlated with indole-, kynurenine-, and serotonin- pathway of tryptophan metabolism, alongside SCFAs production [10, 41]. Concurrently, butyrate-producing Butyricimonas stimulate 5-HT production, affecting melatonin secretion via AANAT and ASMT [42]. In addition, Lactobacillus reuteri (L. R) and Escherichia coli (E. coli) recapitulated the effects of gut microbiota on host melatonin production [43], which were also enriched in melatonin-treated VPA rats.

The influence of microbiota on brain connectivity primarily depends on microbial metabolites. Our targeted metabolomic profiles for serum and hippocampus revealed findings matching previous reports, for serotonergic neurotransmission, tryptophan, 5-HT, 5-HTP, and 5-HIAA were consistently decreased in hippocampus, whereas only 5-HT elevated in serum of VPA-treated rodent offspring, together with enhanced SERT (serotonin transporter) binding and reduced 5-HTR (5-hydroxytryptamine receptor) [44]. Companied with reversion of metabolic rate-limiting enzymes TPH1/2, IDO1, ANNAT, and ASMT, melatonin treatment promoted tryptophan-serotonin-melatonin synthesis pathway, while suppressed IDO1-kynurenine [29, 31, 41]. Since neurotransmission activity of 5-HT in the synaptic cleft is terminated by SERT, which reuptakes 5-HT back to presynaptic terminals, SLC6A4/SERT transgenic mice exhibit ASD-like behaviors [45], whereas restoration of serotonin level alleviates phenotypes [46]. On the other hand, serotonergic neurons can project to hypothalamus and modulate oxytocin (OXT) release, which is essential on enhancing socially relevant recognition, cognition and memory [47]. Our results manifested improvements in 5-HT neurotransmission and 5-HTR1A expression, along with OXT-oxytocin receptor signaling, in melatonin-treated VPA rats. Considering the glutamatergic/GABAergic balance, downregulated GABA and GABA receptor was also observed in VPA rats, correlating with altered microbiota compositions, e.g. Bacteroides, Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Parabacteroides [48].

Focusing on IDO1/Kyn and IAA/IPA-AhR pathway, which are crucial for immune response and intestinal homeostasis [19], colon barrier integrity was further evaluated via tight junction proteins, and microglial reactivity through morphological parameters. In consistent with previous reports [41], melatonin enhanced tight junction assembly and mitigated intestinal pathology, along with alleviated circulating cytokines IL-1β and TNFα. Over-activation of IDO1 [49] and changed microglial phenotypes from resting to activated states (large cell body, less branches and total length) were observed in VPA model rats during infancy and adolescence [50], whereas gut microbiota-produced IPA can protect the microglia from inflammation thus promote neuronal function [51]; hence we speculate the reversion of microglial overactivation and inflammation after melatonin treatment partially attribute to microbial metabolites. Mechanistically, melatonin restructured gut microbial ecosystems by suppressing pro-inflammatory taxa while enriching neuroprotective genera, which redirected tryptophan metabolism: enhanced Bacteroides/lactobacillus-aromatic amino acid decarboxylase in boosting IPA production, while reduced Akkermansia/Clostridia-indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase activity in Kyn/KA synthesis.

Tryptophan-metabolizing gut microbes regulate dendritic branching and adult neurogenesis via serotonin and indole/IPA-AhR signaling [25, 26]; indeed, IPA supplementation notably mitigated ASD-related behavioral phenotypes [52]. In addition, abnormalities in synaptic proteins, reported as important signal transducers associated with ASD, including cell adhesion molecules (neuroligins (NLGNs) and neurexins (NRXNs)), postsynaptic scaffolding proteins (SH3 and multiple ankyrin repeat domains (SHANK), glutamate receptors (NMDAR subunits, GluN2B), and inhibitory GABAA receptors (GABRA)), can modulate the strength of synapses and ultimately alter the structure and functional connectivity of neuronal networks [53]. Coincidentally, an aetiology study prioritized OXT-related biomarkers associated ASD and defined 30 high positive-contributed oxytocin-related genes (OTRGs), including SHANK3, NLGN4X and NLGN3 [54, 55]. Our RNA sequencing analysis of hippocampus revealed VPA-altered genes implicated in tryptophan metabolism and serotonergic as well as GABAergic synapses; specifically, TPH1/2, IDO1, SHANK3, and GABRA6 were identified. These transcriptomic findings illustrated potential molecular profiles for melatonin-recovered ASD behaviors, highlighted neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction.

Currently, there are no officially approved effective medications for the core symptoms of ASD. Postnatal neurodevelopment parallels the establishment and maturation of the microbiome, therefore, gut microbiota dysbiosis potentially enhances the vulnerability of ASD [11]. Meanwhile, fetus depends on maternal melatonin, which easily penetrates the placenta and blood-brain barrier [56], more importantly, the rescue effects of maternal melatonin supplementation during early development stages (postnatal or parallel with VPA exposure) on social deficits of VPA mice could last to adulthood [30], thereby providing potential interventions for autism. Certainly, with limited litters and subjects, this study should include fecal microbiota-transplantation or indole metabolites (e.g., IPA) supplementation to illustrate direct role of microbiome-tryptophan axis in neurobehavioral effects of melatonin. Moreover, alleviation of social interaction deficits and enhancement in GABAergic synaptic function in Ctnnd2 KO mice model of ASD were blocked by melatonin receptors antagonists luzindole [57], therefore, receptor-mediated pathways should also be considered in future exploration.

Conclusions

In summary, melatonin supplementation during early development restores systemic tryptophan metabolism by depletion of risk bacteria Clostridia and augment of protective Butyricimonas and Faecalibacterium, causally identified through Mendelian randomization. Thus alleviates autism behaviors via modulating dendritic development and microglial pruning, as well as adult hippocampal neurogenesis and neurotransmitters homeostasis. Considering the pleiotropic effects of melatonin, the specific microbiota- IPA-brain axis need further exploration.
