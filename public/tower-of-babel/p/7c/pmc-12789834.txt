Late-Onset Embolization of a Mitral Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair Device
Authors: Naeem Tahirkheli, Salman Khalid, Sania Tahir, Syed Haider Ali, Subtain ul Hassan Abid, Ayesha Ahmad, Muhammad Awais, Pashmina Kumari, Yusuf Ali Shah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Transcatheter edge-edge repair (TEER) with the MitraClip device (Abbott) is a well-established treatment for severe symptomatic mitral regurgitation (MR) in high-risk surgical patients,1 especially when symptoms persist despite optimal medical therapy. The procedure has a superior safety profile compared with surgical repair.2 We encountered a rare and catastrophic complication—MitraClip embolization into the right coronary cusp of the aortic valve, occurring 30 days postprocedure.3 Most reported cases required surgical retrieval of an embolized MitraClip; however, we were able to successfully remove it percutaneously via the right femoral approach.

Take-Home Message

•Late-onset MitraClip embolization is rare, and although surgical retrieval is usually pursued, we present a percutaneous approach for its safe retrieval.

History of Present Illness

An 82-year-old Caucasian man presented with severe primary degenerative MR due to a prolapsing and flail posterior leaflet. After heart team discussion and prohibitive surgical risk, it was decided to proceed with TEER. The patient underwent MitraClip implantation (XTW G4) at the A2-P2 position. Postprocedure transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) showed MR reduction from 4+ to 1+-2+ and improved ejection fraction. The next day, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed normal ejection fraction (60%) with intact clip, medial orifice gradient 6.8 mm Hg, and lateral orifice gradient 6.5 mm Hg. He was then discharged in stable condition. At his 30-day follow-up, the patient reported worsening exertional dyspnea. He had just recovered from a 3-week episode of respiratory tract infection with severe coughing and retching, which could have been the cause of his exertional dyspnea. Moreover, severe coughing may generate abrupt intrathoracic and left ventricular pressure surges, exerting mechanical stress on the clip-leaflet interface, particularly when leaflet capture is borderline. This may have contributed to delayed clip detachment in our patient. There was no jugular venous distention noted on examination, and the cough had resolved. His cardiac enzymes and other laboratory markers were unremarkable apart from mild elevation of B-type natriuretic peptide levels. Chest x-ray was not suggestive of pneumonia.

Differential Diagnosis

Considerations included acute coronary syndrome, acute heart failure exacerbation, pneumonia, mitral stenosis, and pulmonary embolism.

Investigations

The patient's cardiac enzymes and other laboratory markers were unremarkable apart from mild elevation of B-type natriuretic peptide levels. Chest x-ray was not suggestive of pneumonia.

TTE showed severe primary MR due to prolapsing and flail posterior leaflet, moderate tricuspid regurgitation, and aortic regurgitation. Preserved ejection fraction >60% was indicated, with no evidence of MitraClip attached to the anterior or posterior mitral leaflet. The MitraClip appeared to be dislodged in the right coronary cusp (Figures 1A and 1C). On chest x-ray, the MitraClip appeared more centered over the heart, demonstrating a more horizontal appearance when compared with the previous examination. There were no signs of pneumonia. Electrocardiogram showed normal sinus rhythm, with no arrhythmias or acute ischemic changes. Laboratory tests indicated mild anemia and mild thrombocytopenia. Renal function was indicated as normal. TEE showed severe MR, with dislodgement of the MitraClip in the right coronary cusp (Figure 1B, Videos 1A and 1B).

The clip appears as a highly echogenic, mobile structure distinct from the native valvular apparatus. Imaging was critical in identifying the clip's location after suspected detachment. Management

After heart team discussion, it was decided to proceed with percutaneous retrieval as per patient consent given prohibitive surgical risk. Bilateral ultrasound-guided femoral artery access was obtained, and the depth for Manta closure was measured in the right common femoral artery. A 24 F Gore DrySeal sheath and a 60-cm 10-F sheath were introduced into the right common femoral artery over a J-tip 0.035-inch guidewire, while a 6-F sheath was placed in the left femoral artery (Video 2). An 8-F JR 4.0 guiding catheter was advanced through the 10-F sheath to the aortic root.

Aneri forceps (2.4-mm outer diameter, 9-mm grasp) were then introduced through the 8-F guide. The MitraClip was located and grasped under simultaneous TEE and fluoroscopy guidance using orthogonal views (Figure 2, Video 3). After making the grip a little taut, individual motion of the leaflets was carefully visualized on TEE to make sure that any part of the aortic valve apparatus was not grasped. Once good leaflet motion was confirmed, the forceps were tugged harder, and the clip was freed from the sinus. The clip, held within the forceps, was then retracted into the 10-F sheath, and the entire assembly was withdrawn toward the 24-F sheath (Figure 3).

Initial Attempt to Retrieve the Dislodged MitraClip by Aneri Forceps

Fluoroscopic anteroposterior image demonstrating the radio-opaque MitraClip device located within the right coronary cusp of the aortic root. The clip is visualized as a distinct metallic structure separate from the coronary catheters and guidewires. This imaging confirmed the location of the embolized device before the initial retrieval attempt with Aneri forceps.

Attempt to Retrieve Embolized MitraClip Into the Sheath

Fluoroscopic anteroposterior image demonstrating an attempt to retrieve the embolized MitraClip device into the 24-F vascular sheath with Aneri forceps. The clip is visualized engaged by the forceps within the ascending aorta during percutaneous retrieval efforts and moved toward the vascular sheath.

The clip failed to fold into the 24-F sheath. A slight tug caused it to dislodge again, freely floating in the descending aorta (Figure 4, Video 4). The Aneri forceps were quickly exchanged with an 18-to 30 mm EN Snare system (Merit Medical) (Figure 5A, Video 5). The snare successfully captured the clip, and it was retrieved in the 24-F sheath (Figure 5B, Video 6) and subsequently removed from the body.

Dislodgement of MitraClip Back Into the Descending Aorta

Fluoroscopic anteroposterior image showing migration of the MitraClip device back into the descending thoracic aorta after an unsuccessful retrieval attempt. The metallic clip is visualized proximal to the diaphragm, separate from the vascular sheath and retrieval device.

Retrieving the MitraClip With the Snare and Advancing It Into the Sheath

(A) Fluoroscopic anteroposterior image showing the final retrieval attempt of the embolized MitraClip using a snare device within the descending aorta. (B) Subsequent advancement of the captured MitraClip into the vascular sheath under continuous fluoroscopic guidance, confirming successful percutaneous retrieval.

The next task was to take out the 24-F sheath. The site was closed using an 18-F Manta device (Teleflex) and 2 ProGlide (Abbott) sutures. A J-tip guidewire was advanced from the left femoral access across the aortic bifurcation into the 24-F sheath on the right side and was successfully externalized. A peripheral balloon (8 × 40 mm) was inserted in the right common iliac artery through the J-tip wire and inflated at 6 atm (Figure 6, Video 7). The 24-F sheath was removed, and the site was tightened but not closed with 2 ProGlide sutures as part of the preclose method. The Manta was deployed, and the balloon was deflated and observed for bleeding. As there was no bleeding, the ProGlide sutures were tightened and locked.

Balloon Inflation of the Right Common Iliac Artery to Secure Hemostasis

Fluoroscopic anteroposterior image demonstrating balloon inflation within the right common iliac artery to achieve vascular hemostasis after percutaneous retrieval of the MitraClip device. The balloon is advanced on a J-tip wire introduced through left femoral access, positioned across the right femoral access site, and inflated under imaging guidance to control bleeding.

Final TEE images showed normal aortic valve apparatus, with no injury or regurgitation.

Follow-Up

Follow-up TEE showed no procedure-related complications, and there was no damage to the aorta and surrounding structures, with successful outcome. The patient was subsequently called back for a repeat MitraClip, which was carried out successfully. The clip has since remained stable (Figure 7, Video 8).

Successful Repeat MitraClip Placement

Transesophageal echocardiography midesophageal 2-chamber view demonstrating the newly deployed MitraClip securely grasping both anterior and posterior mitral leaflets. Color Doppler imaging confirmed minimal residual mitral regurgitation with stable clip position.

Discussion

Although the MitraClip procedure is generally safer than surgery, it is not without complications. In the EXPAND G4 study (n = >1,100), common 30-day adverse events with the fourth-generation clip included mitral valve reintervention in 1.9%, single-leaflet device attachment in 1.7%, myocardial infarction in 1.2%, stroke in 1.2%, mitral valve stenosis in 0.5%, and leaflet injury in 0.4%.3 Embolization occurred in only 0.3% of patients, and overall device-related complications requiring surgery were seen in 0.3% of patients.4 Device-related complications such as detachment occur in 1% to 5% of cases, typically during leaflet grasping or after deployment, owing to a number of factors.5 They are typically evident on immediate or follow-up TEE <48 hours postprocedure.6,7 However, our case showed an interesting late-onset embolization in an otherwise successful MitraClip procedure.

The risk factors for device embolization are difficult to determine. Some of them include suboptimal leaflet grasp, device malposition, anatomical factors, and operator experience. MitraClip devices have 2 polyester fabric-covered arms, which promote tissue growth. In a study by Ladich et al,8 the healing response included early pannus formation for a tissue bridge in the subacute interval of 31 to 90 days. Although surgical retrieval is typically recommended for embolized MitraClip given its larger size, percutaneous removal is a possible alternative for patients who are at high risk for surgery. Angiographic and TEE guidance and correlation was crucial in guiding the secure removal of the MitraClip from the right coronary cusp without injuring the aorta and aortic valve leaflets. Snare forceps were initially avoided in favor of Aneri forceps to reduce the risk of aortic valve apparatus injury. When the clip migrated into the descending aorta, a prompt switch to snare forceps allowed for successful retrieval. The challenge of removing the 24 F sheath was overcome by placing 2 ProGlide sutures and a Manta device.

Conclusions

Device-related complications such as detachment are typically evident on echocardiography at the conclusion of the index procedure or during the immediate postprocedure period. Our case demonstrates a rare late-onset embolization without any underlying trauma or endocarditis and its successful percutaneous retrieval.

(A and B). TEE revealing an embolized MitraClip device within the ascending aorta. This case underscores the importance of TEE in promptly diagnosing structural device embolization.1

Video 2

Introduction of Gore DrySeal Under Fluoroscopic Guidance

The video illustrates the fluoroscopy-guided introduction of a 24-F Gore DrySeal sheath via right transfemoral access. Under continuous fluoroscopy, the introducer sheath is advanced into the common femoral artery after successful ultrasound-guided arterial puncture and wire passage. The video highlights optimal sheath positioning and access technique, which is critical to minimizing vascular complications owing to the large sheath size in structural heart procedures.

Video 3

Endovascular Retrieval of Dislodged MitraClip Using Aneri Forceps

The video captures the maneuvering and grasping of a MitraClip dislodged in the ascending aorta with Aneri forceps, demonstrating secure engagement and extraction technique with specialized retrieval instruments, avoiding aortic valve injury.

Video 4

Failed Removal of MitraClip Using Aneri Forceps With Distal Dislodgment Into the Descending Aorta

This video demonstrates an attempted percutaneous retrieval of a MitraClip device that had embolized into the ascending aorta. Removal was attempted using Aneri forceps; however, during manipulation, the device could not be secured and subsequently dislodged further, migrating distally into the descending thoracic aorta. The case highlights the technical difficulty and potential complications associated with percutaneous retrieval of embolized structural heart devices.

Video 5

Percutaneous Retrieval of MitraClip From the Descending Aorta Toward the Sheath Using the EN Snare Device

This video illustrates successful percutaneous retrieval of an embolized MitraClip device from the descending thoracic aorta using a triple-loop EN Snare system. Under fluoroscopic guidance, the device was carefully engaged and extracted without vascular injury. The case highlights the feasibility of endovascular retrieval techniques for embolized structural heart devices in challenging anatomical locations.

Video 6

Successful Retrieval of Embolized MitraClip Into the Sheath Using the EN Snare Device

This video demonstrates the percutaneous retrieval of an embolized MitraClip into the sheath using a triple-loop EN Snare device. The clip was securely snared in the descending aorta and smoothly withdrawn into an appropriately sized vascular sheath under fluoroscopic guidance. The procedure was completed without complications, illustrating effective endovascular management of device embolization.

Video 7

Balloon Inflation at the Right Femoral Arterial Access Site for Hemostasis

This video demonstrates balloon inflation at the right femoral artery access site to achieve temporary hemostasis after large-bore sheath removal. Under fluoroscopic guidance, a peripheral balloon was inserted in the right common iliac artery through a J-tip wire that was introduced from the contralateral side and inflated at 6 atm to tamponade the arterial puncture site, minimizing bleeding and facilitating vascular closure or further intervention as a preclose method for deployment of 2 Proglides and a Manta device. This highlights the technical difficulty of closing a large-bore vascular access.8

Video 8

TEE Demonstration of Successful MitraClip Deployment With Mitral Regurgitation Reduction

This video demonstrates the final result of a repeat successful MitraClip procedure. The clip is seen securely attached to the mitral valve leaflets, with evidence of proper coaptation. Color Doppler imaging shows a significant reduction in mitral regurgitation, confirming effective valve repair and optimal device positioning.
