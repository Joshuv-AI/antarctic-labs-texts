The persistence of changes in insulin sensitivity and metabolic gene expression after acute exercise in male and female mice
Authors: Jack H. Thompson, Alexa M. Fooskas, Erica J. Locke, Renee N. Washart, Alexandria M. Chin, Paige A.P. Ponzo, Joshua Oduwole, Nicholas DiGiuseppe, Max Matalavage, Stephen C. Kolwicz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute exercise has been suggested to increase insulin sensitivity, although the duration of the improvement may last anywhere from 4 to 72 h. In addition, changes in gene expression in response to exercise is suggested to last at least 24 h. Despite this, exercise training studies assess metabolism anywhere from immediately after to 72 h after the last exercise session, which could confuse the acute versus chronic exercise effects. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to assess the duration of changes in insulin sensitivity after acute exercise and the impact of acute exercise on metabolic gene expression.Materials and methodsMale and female mice endured a single bout of aerobic exercise and underwent insulin sensitivity tests at 30 min, 4 h, and 24 h following acute exercise. Furthermore, skeletal muscle was harvested, and gene expression analysis was performed at 30 min and 24, 48, and 72 h after the exercise bout.ResultsFemales had increased insulin sensitivity compared to males independent of exercise. However, males showed increased sensitivity 30 min following exercise while females did not. Neither males nor females had improved insulin sensitivity 24 h following exercise. Both male and female mice had upregulation of several metabolic genes 24 h after acute exercise, which was comparable to sedentary controls after 48–72 h of recovery.ConclusionsThe results show that acute exercise does not significantly affect insulin sensitivity 24 h after exercise but the effects of acute exercise on metabolic gene expression persist after 24 h and return to baseline within 48–72 h.

Introduction

Participation in exercise is known to elicit many positive physiological effects including reduction of blood pressure (Cornelissen and Smart, 2013), improved blood lipid profiles (Lavie et al., 2015), and weight loss (Powell-Wiley et al., 2021). Moreover, exercise is recommended as a treatment plan for type 2 diabetics because of improved glycemic control (Syeda et al., 2023). However, the effects of exercise can manifest during both the acute and/or chronic setting. Acute exercise consists of a single bout of exercise while chronic exercise, or exercise training, consists of multiple training intervals over an extended period. Therefore, consideration of the acute versus chronic effects of exercise are critical in furthering the knowledge of the beneficial effects of exercise. Moreover, a clear understanding of the persistence of the metabolic effects of acute exercises are needed to accurately design and interpret data obtained from chronic exercise training studies.

Improvement in muscle glucose uptake and insulin sensitivity has been a long appreciated beneficial effect of exercise. However, enhanced glucose uptake and insulin sensitivity following acute exercise has been purported to last up to 24–48 h (Cartee, 2015), which can make dissecting the adaptations from chronic exercise more difficult. Skeletal muscle glucose uptake in rats was increased at 30 min but not 2 h following acute exercise (Goodyear et al., 1990), while classic studies evaluated enhanced insulin sensitivity 12–16 h after exercise in humans (Devlin et al., 1987) or 18 h after cessation of exercise in rats (Cartee et al., 1989). Other work suggested that insulin sensitivity can persist up to 72 h after acute exercise in middle-aged humans (King et al., 1995). While the methodological approaches of these studies require careful consideration, none define an appropriate time point at which to evaluate insulin sensitivity after exercise. Therefore, further research focusing on understanding the persistence of insulin sensitivity in the post exercise period remain important.

Exercise training studies can be difficult since differences in frequency, intensity, duration, and length of training are critical variables that may affect the interpretation of the results. However, the time after the last exercise session in which the analyses occur can be crucial. Acute exercise is known to induce numerous molecular responses, including changes in skeletal muscle gene expression that may last up to 24 h (Egan and Sharples, 2023). Of interest, some of these investigations have been limited to the 24-h post exercise period in trained humans (Louis et al., 2007; Yang et al., 2005). Analyses of tissue after exercise training in animal models has occurred at 24 h (Foryst-Ludwig et al., 2011; Gibb et al., 2017; Monleon et al., 2014) or 48 h (Burelle et al., 2004; Dobrzyn et al., 2013) after the last exercise session. Some studies have performed tissue analysis for training studies less than 24 h from the last exercise bout or failed to define the time of analysis/extraction (Broderick et al., 2005; Hafstad et al., 2011; Riehle et al., 2014). Given these inconsistencies, further investigations into the persistence of metabolic changes in tissue are required to establish an appropriate recovery time after exercise before analysis can be performed.

While defining changes in insulin sensitivity and metabolic gene expression after acute exercise are important, uncovering sex-specific differences in metabolic responses to exercise may be profound for health and performance. We recently published a study that evaluated sex differences in exercise endurance capacity in mice and found that females had higher run times which was related to greater capacity to use lipids (Holcomb et al., 2022). Likewise, we recently found notable sex-differences in mice fed a ketogenic diet, including protection from the development of glucose intolerance in females (Sprankle et al., 2024). These and future studies are important in addressing sex as a biological variable (Clayton, 2018) and help to identify and characterize potential differences in physiological and metabolic processes between males and females.

The present study was designed to clarify gaps in the literature surrounding the persistence of insulin sensitivity and gene expression changes following acute exercise. With the inclusion of both male and female mice, the study also explored sex-specific differences in physiology and metabolism. Findings from the study could be important in providing some methodological answers to translational issues related to rodent studies examining exercise metabolism (Fuller and Thyfault, 2021) and contribute to rigor and reproducibility efforts in animal models of exercise (Hastings et al., 2022). Importantly, the findings will be critical in providing researchers with the appropriate recovery window so that any measured exercise-induced changes in exercise metabolism can be attributed to chronic adaptations rather than acute responses.

Materials and methods

Research design

Male (n = 37) and female (n = 35) mice were subjected to a single 60-min bout of aerobic exercise (i.e., acute exercise). A cohort of male (n = 25) and female (n = 23) were subjected to insulin sensitivity tests (IST) conducted at three different time points post exercise (30 min, 4 h, 24 h). A group of non-exercised, sedentary male (n = 20) and female (n = 17) were used as controls. In a separate cohort of male (n = 12) and female (n = 12) mice, the gastrocnemius muscle was harvested immediately after exercise (i.e., 30 min) and after 24, 48, and 72 h of recovery from the acute exercise bout. Expression of genes involved in glucose, fatty acid, ketone body, and mitochondrial metabolism were analyzed to assess transcriptional changes in response to acute exercise.

Animal model

All experimental protocols used in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Ursinus College. For the experiments in the study, 57 male and 52 female C57BL/6NCrl mice at 12 weeks of age were used. All mice were obtained from in-house breeding colonies. Prior to the experiments, mice were randomly assigned to acute exercise (EX) or sedentary (SED) control groups. Mice were maintained in the animal vivarium on a 12-h light/dark cycle and fed a standard rodent chow (Lab Diet, #5001), which consisted of 13.4 % calories from fat, 57.9 % calories from carbohydrates, and 28.7 % calories from protein. Throughout the study, mice were housed at 3–5 animals per cage. Food and water were provided ad libitum except during the short-term fasting period prior to the insulin sensitivity tests and tissue harvests as indicated below.

Exercise protocol

Mice randomly assigned to the exercise groups participated in a 60-min bout of aerobic exercise on a motorized treadmill (EXER 3/6, Columbus Instruments, Columbus, OH). Exercise sessions were conducted at the same time of day for all mice between 1100 and 1300 h. The treadmill speed was set to 15 m/min at a 10 % incline for all mice, estimated to be a moderate intensity (i.e., 60–65 % of maximal intensity) based on previous literature (O'Neill et al., 2022; Massett and Berk, 2005; Billat et al., 2005). Throughout the exercise session, mice were motivated via gentle prodding of their hindquarters (Poole et al., 2020). All mice completed the 60-min exercise session with no adverse events. Prior to the exercise session, animals in the exercise and sedentary groups were placed into clean cages with access to water only and transported to the procedure room housing the mouse treadmill. After the 60-min exercise session, mice were returned to their cages. Exercise and sedentary mice assigned to the 30-min post exercise group had access to only water prior to the insulin sensitivity tests or tissue harvests. Exercise and sedentary mice assigned to the 4-h or 24-hr post exercise groups were returned to their housing cages with access to food and water until insulin sensitivity tests were performed or the gastrocnemius muscle was harvested.

Insulin sensitivity test

Prior to the insulin sensitivity test (IST), all mice were fasted for 90–120 min (Carper et al., 2020). To start the test, baseline blood glucose levels were measured in blood acquired from the tail tip using a handheld glucometer (Contour Next EZ, Ascensia Diabetes Care). An insulin stock solution was created by mixing powdered insulin (#16634, Sigma Aldrich, Lot #SLCJ4745) with water to yield a 56.6 U/ml solution concentration. Mice then received an intraperitoneal (IP) injection of an insulin solution at 1.0 U/kg per body weight. After injection, blood glucose was measured at 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min to monitor blood glucose levels. ISTs were performed in three separate cohorts of mice at three different intervals: 30 min, 4 h, and 24 h post exercise. After completion of the IST, mice were injected with ∼170 mg/kg of sodium pentobarbital. After confirming sedation with a toe-pinch test, blood was collected from the open chest cavity using a 1 ml syringe. Blood was incubated on ice for 15 min and centrifuged at 2000g for 20 min at 4 °C. The serum was removed and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Tissue harvest

At the end of acute exercise, mice were returned to their cages for 30 min, 24, 48, or 72 h of recovery. Mice in the 30-min post exercise group had access to water while mice in the longer recovery groups had access to food and water up until 90–120 min prior to the tissue harvest. After the fasting period of 90–120 min, mice received an IP injection of ∼170 mg/kg of sodium pentobarbital. After confirmation of sedation, the gastrocnemius was removed and frozen in liquid nitrogen. An identical procedure for the collection of tissue and blood was followed for mice in the sedentary groups. Muscle tissues were stored at −80 °C until additional analyses were performed.

Serum and glycogen analysis

Concentrations of insulin (#62100, Crystal Chem, Elk Grove Village, IL) and cortisol (#500360, Cayman Chemical, Chicago, IL) were measured in the serum collected from exercise and sedentary mice at the completion of the ISTs using commercially available ELISA kits following the manufacturers’ instructions. Glycogen content was measured in frozen gastrocnemius muscle harvested from exercise and sedentary mice using the alkaline extraction procedure (Passonneau and Lauderdale, 1974). The amount of glucose released from the glycogen extract was quantified using a commercial kit (#GAHK-20, Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO).

Gene expression analysis

For the gene expression analysis, approximately 25 mg was collected from the middle portion of the frozen gastrocnemius tissue. RNA was extracted from the gastrocnemius tissue using the RNeasy Mini Kit (#74104, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). cDNA synthesis was performed using the Omniscript reverse transcriptase (RT) and random hexamers according to the manufacturer's instructions (#205111, Qiagen). A real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) analysis was performed with iQ SYBR-Green Supermix (#1708880, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) using a PCR thermocycler (Realplex, Eppendorf North America, Inc). The mRNA levels of genes involved in glucose metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, ketone body metabolism and mitochondrial metabolism were analyzed. Statistical analysis

Data are reported as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). ISTs were analyzed using the area of the curve (AOC) analysis (Virtue and Vidal-Puig, 2021). Statistical differences for ISTs and gene expression were determined by a student's t-test or by a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by a Tukey post-hoc test. Statistical significance was evaluated at the P < 0.05 level. GraphPad Prism 10.0 was used to create all graphs and perform all statistical analyses.

Results

Sedentary female mice display higher sensitivity to insulin

Previous studies suggested that female mice have higher insulin sensitivity compared to males (Gao et al., 2021; Jo et al., 2023; Macotela et al., 2009). To determine if sex differences in insulin sensitivity independent of acute exercise were present, sedentary male and female mice were injected with insulin and blood glucose levels were monitored over 120 min. Blood glucose levels were lower in female mice at each time point after insulin injection (Fig. 1A). To evaluate whether the observed change was statistically significant, an area of the curve (AOC) analysis was performed. Insulin sensitivity is not affected 24 h after acute exercise

To evaluate whether one bout of moderate aerobic exercise affected insulin sensitivity and to assess the persistence of exercise-induced insulin sensitivity, we performed ISTs in mice at 30 min, 4 h, and 24 h after exercise. The results of these experiments are presented in Fig. 2. At 30 min following a single bout of exercise, insulin significantly lowered blood glucose in male exercise (EX) mice compared to sedentary (SED) controls (Fig. 2A and B). Interestingly, blood glucose levels were not lower, and the AOC was not increased further in female EX mice, although the AOC of female SED mice were significantly higher than male SED (Fig. 2A and B). ISTs were also conducted in mice 4 h following acute exercise. While females displayed mildly improved insulin sensitivity compared to males (main effects of sex, P = 0.018); surprisingly, neither male or female EX mice showed a significant reduction in blood glucose or increase in AOC compared to their SED counterparts (Fig. 2C and D). Similar to the findings of 4 h after exercise, insulin sensitivity was not significantly altered in male EX or female EX mice 24 h after acute exercise (Fig. 2E and F). In summary, the findings suggest that one single 60-min bout of aerobic exercise of moderate intensity does not improve insulin sensitivity in male or female mice at either 4 h or 24 h after termination of the exercise session. Relationship of insulin, skeletal muscle glycogen, and cortisol to insulin sensitivity

To evaluate potential reasons for the loss of insulin sensitivity within 4 h post exercise, we measured serum insulin and cortisol concentrations as well as skeletal muscle glycogen content. As shown in Fig. 3A, serum insulin concentrations at the conclusion of the ISTs were significantly lower in female mice while male and female exercise groups remained unchanged compared to their respective sedentary controls. For comparison, we measured serum insulin concentrations after a 90-min fasting period in sedentary male and female mice (n = 6 per group) not exposed to the ISTs. Insulin concentrations were higher in males compared to females (0.94 ± 0.13 ng/ml vs. 0.66 ± 0.10 ng/ml, P = 0.12). Skeletal muscle glycogen content was affected by acute exercise in both male and female mice (main effects of exercise, P = 0.038) with approximately 15% lower levels in the 30-min post exercise groups, which recovered to near or above baseline levels in the 4- and 24-h post exercise cohorts (Fig. 3B). Serum cortisol levels at the end of the ISTs were not significantly different in male and female post exercise groups relative to sedentary controls (Fig. 3C). Sex differences in glucose metabolism genes after acute exercise

To further evaluate the metabolic effects of acute exercise, we elected to analyze genes at an immediate time point (i.e., 30 min after exercise) and extended time points (i.e., 24, 48 and 72 h after exercise) during the recovery from acute exercise. For glucose metabolism, we selected a total of 4 genes involved in glucose transport, insulin signaling, and glycolysis. Glut1, the gene responsible for insulin independent glucose uptake, showed a nearly 2-fold upregulation immediately following exercise in both males and females, which remained significantly above baseline at 24 h for males and was reduced for females. (Fig. 4A). Glut4, the gene coding for insulin dependent glucose uptake, showed a similar but more robust pattern as Glut1 with a significantly higher upregulation in males (Fig. 4B). Interestingly, acute exercise did not induce a significant upregulation for Irs1, the gene coding for the insulin receptor (Fig. 4C). HkII, the predominant hexokinase isoform in skeletal muscle, was upregulated in both male and females at 30 min post exercise and remained significantly elevated in females at 24 h (Fig. 4D). Importantly, besides Irs1 which remained unchanged after acute exercise, Glut1, Glut4, and HkII, were significantly reduced and returned to near baseline levels after 72 h of recovery (Fig. 4A, B, 4C). Sex differences in fatty acid metabolism genes after acute exercise

Three specific genes were examined to determine the impact of acute exercise on transcriptional regulation of fatty acid metabolism. Pparα, the master transcriptional regulator of lipid metabolism, showed a 3-fold upregulation in both sexes 30 min post exercise, which rapidly declined at 24 h (Fig. 5A). Cd36, one of the primary transporters of fatty acid uptake in cells, was also upregulated in both sexes 30 min post exercise, as well as maintained upregulation in males 24 h following exercise (Fig. 5B). Like Cd36, Mcad, an important enzyme involved in beta-oxidation, showed upregulation in both sexes 30 min post exercise, as well as maintained upregulation in males 24 h following exercise (Fig. 5C). These findings suggest that genes involved in fatty metabolism are also upregulated 24 h after acute exercise, especially in male mice. Acute exercise does not upregulate ketone body metabolism genes

Given recent interest in ketone body metabolism and exercise performance (Harvey et al., 2019; Gouwens et al., 2025), skeletal muscle metabolic genes in the ketone body oxidation pathway were also analyzed. Bdh1, the enzyme that catalyzes the initial step in ketone body oxidation, was downregulated in males and females at 30 min and 24 h after acute exercise and returned to near sedentary levels with 48 h of recovery (Fig. 6A). Oxct1, the gene that codes for the rate limiting enzyme in ketone body oxidation was unaffected by acute exercise (Fig. 6B). Acat1, the final enzyme in ketone body oxidation that yields acetyl CoA, was significantly upregulated in male and female gastrocnemius muscles 30 min and 24 h after exercise (Fig. 6C). While Acat1 is linked to ketone body oxidation, the enzyme also plays an important role in beta-oxidation (Goudarzi, 2019). Thus, the observed upregulation in Acat1 may be more related to fatty acid oxidation, rather than ketone body oxidation, especially considering the downregulation and no change in Bdh1 and Oxct1, respectively. Overall, these results suggest than acute aerobic exercise does not increase the transcription of genes involved in ketone body oxidation in the skeletal muscle. Sex differences in mitochondrial gene expression after acute exercise

The expression of genes involved in mitochondria metabolism were also assessed in the gastrocnemius muscle of male and female mice after acute exercise. Pgc1α, the transcriptional regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, was upregulated nearly 4-fold at 30 min post exercise, which significantly lowered by 24 h after exercise, and returned to sedentary levels within 48 h of recovery in both sexes (Fig. 7A). An important regulator of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) transcription, Tfam, was also significantly upregulated at 30 min and 24 h after exercise in both sexes, with a higher response in males (Fig. 7B). Citrate synthase (Cs), the initial enzyme in the tricarboxylic acid cycle, was also significantly elevated nearly 2.5-fold in both sexes at 30 min and remained upregulated at 24 h after exercise, with significantly lower effect in females (Fig. 7C). Discussion

In this study, we characterized the duration of insulin sensitivity and changes in metabolic gene expression following one singular bout of aerobic exercise of moderate intensity in mice. A secondary interest was to determine whether sex differences in the acute responses of insulin sensitivity and metabolic gene expression existed in mice. These investigations led us to several important findings. First, female mice have greater insulin sensitivity, independent of exercise, compared to their male counterparts. Second, acute exercise provides significant improvements in insulin sensitivity 30 min following acute exercise in male, but not in female mice. Third, improvements in exercise-induced enhancements in insulin sensitivity are short-lived, subsiding within 4 h after acute exercise. Lastly, acute exercise can induce gene upregulation up to 24 h post-acute exercise in key metabolic genes in the glucose, mitochondrial, and fatty acid metabolic pathways, which return to baseline after 48–72 h of recovery. These studies provide important information regarding the regulation of glucose homeostasis following acute exercise. Furthermore, our findings highlight the importance of data analysis for exercise training studies, ensuring that exercise training studies are cognizant of the acute exercise effects on gene expression.

An initial finding of our study confirmed an increase in insulin sensitivity in female sedentary mice compared to male sedentary mice. This finding is consistent with other literature, in both humans and mice, which found that sedentary untrained females are more insulin sensitive compared to males (Macotela et al., 2009; Jo et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2021; Magkos et al., 2010; Nuutila et al., 1995). A reason for the improved insulin sensitivity could be correlated to higher estrogen levels in females, resulting in higher levels of sex hormone binding globulin which has a positive association with insulin sensitivity (Geer and Shen, 2009). Although we did not account for estrus cycle during the ISTs in this study, future work using ovariectomized or aged female mice could further test this hypothesis.

While previous work suggested that acute exercise can reduce blood glucose levels in insulin-resistant rodents (Henriksen, 2002), the duration of insulin sensitivity following acute exercise in apparently healthy animals has been debated. In our study, we hypothesized that improved insulin sensitivity would last greater than 4 h following a single bout of exercise with female mice demonstrating increased insulin sensitivity at that time point. Contrary to our hypothesis, insulin sensitivity was not improved at 4-h post exercise in males and females compared to sedentary controls. However, acute exercise improves short-term (i.e., 30 min post-exercise) whole body insulin sensitivity in male mice. This finding is likely an additive or synergistic effect of both insulin-stimulated and exercise-stimulated glucose uptake in the skeletal muscle as previously identified in both humans and rodents (DeFronzo et al., 1981; Wallberg-Henriksson et al., 1988). Interestingly, we did not observe enhanced insulin sensitivity in female mice 30-min after acute exercise. We believe this is, in part, due to enhanced sensitivity to insulin in females at rest, making the effects of acute exercise less significant. Our results differ from a similar study in female sheep where insulin sensitivity persisted up to 18 h following acute exercise (McConell et al., 2015) While differences in animal models are an obvious explanation for the conflicting result, the study used the hyperinsulinemic euglycemic clamp, which is considered the gold standard in assessing insulin sensitivity. Therefore, our method of measuring insulin sensitivity may not be the most accurate. However, we did find that serum insulin concentrations were significantly lower in female mice which could be indicative of improved insulin sensitivity. Additional investigations, specifically analysis of skeletal muscle glucose uptake, are needed to further dissect these observations.

Independent of sex, our study did not identify significant differences in insulin sensitivity at 4 or 24 h after acute exercise. In male mice, insulin sensitivity has been shown to be enhanced in the soleus muscle 85 min after exercise (Hamada et al., 2006). In rats, insulin sensitivity was improved at 4 h but not 24 h after 45 min of treadmill exercise (Richter et al., 1982). While short-term improvements in insulin sensitivity following exercise are common, prolonged effects appear to be related to carbohydrate consumption and the resynthesis of glycogen in the recovery period (Jensen et al., 1997). In carbohydrate fed rats, muscle insulin responsiveness was lost within 3 h post exercise but persisted until 48 h in carbohydrate restricted rats which was associated with a muscle glycogen content equal to above resting values (Cartee et al., 1989). In our study, muscle glycogen content was reduced by ∼ nearly 20 % in the male exercised mice with improved insulin sensitivity. Mice in the 4 h and 24-h post exercise groups had access to normal chow up until 90–120 min prior to the ISTs. This likely contributed to improved glycogen resynthesis during this time frame and potentially contributed to the loss of whole-body insulin sensitivity by 4 h post exercise. In addition to dietary composition several other factors could be critical in mediating the lasting effects including animal species, mouse strain, exercise protocol, and methodology of insulin sensitivity. Nevertheless, our findings highlight an additional area of need for exercise-based research, including the importance of intensity in exercise studies.

In addition to assessing insulin sensitivity post exercise, we also performed RT-PCR analysis for several genes in four main metabolic pathways. The primary reason for this investigation was based on discrepancies in the exercise training literature regarding the appropriate time after the last exercise session in which to perform metabolic assessments. We hypothesized that metabolic gene expression would remain elevated up to 24 h following acute exercise, so that analysis of these genes from chronic exercise training studies should occur later in the recovery period. As predicted, our analysis not only demonstrated that upregulation of metabolic genes was common 30 min following acute exercise but was often still present 24 h post exercise. Notably, all metabolic genes assessed in our study returned to sedentary levels within 48–72 h after exercise.

Our analyses of key genes in glucose, fatty acid, and mitochondrial metabolic pathways revealed some notable results. Within 30 min following the acute exercise bout, gene expression of both Glut1 and Glut4 were significantly upregulated 2-3-fold, particularly in males. Interestingly, the upregulation of the Glut4 gene was significantly less in female mice at both 30 min and 24 h of recovery. Despite the higher gene expression of Glut4, this did not appear to translate to improved insulin sensitivity after 24 h of recovery. Meanwhile, our findings of mild elevation of HkII expression 24 h after exercise is consistent with a previous report in humans (Yang et al., 2005). Regarding genes involved in fatty acid metabolism, we identified significant upregulation of Pparα, Cd36, and Mcad in the immediate (i.e., 30 min) post exercise period as well as the 24-h period, especially in male mice. In comparison, short term upregulation in Pparα was noted in human skeletal muscle after a single bout of high intensity exercise (Kuang et al., 2022) whereas Cd36 expression in muscle was unchanged after a 30 min run in humans (Yang et al., 2005). For mitochondrial genes, studies in mice found similar results surrounding genes such as Pgc1α and citrate synthase where mRNA levels were elevated up to 6 h after acute exercise (Safdar et al., 2009; Sun et al., 2016) with Pgc1α returning to baseline levels at 24–48 h (Sun et al., 2016). Several of the genes analyzed in our study showed an approximate 2-fold increase compared to sedentary controls after 24 h of one single bout of acute aerobic exercise with females demonstrating either an attenuated upregulation or faster return to baseline. While we did not directly assess the metabolic pathways that these genes regulate, our findings do raise concerns regarding the assessment of metabolism, particularly in metabolic gene expression in exercise studies. Studies that report changes in metabolism with exercise training but perform analyses 24 h after the last exercise session, could be highlighting the acute, rather than, the chronic exercise effects. Overall, the lasting effects of acute exercise on gene expression show the importance in the time of tissue analysis for exercise training studies.

One unanticipated finding from our study was the downregulation of Bdh1 mRNA during the first 24 h of recovery and the non-responsiveness of Oxct1 in the skeletal muscle after acute exercise. The failure of Bdh1 and Oxct1 to upregulate after one bout of acute exercise is intriguing since previous work showed that mRNA levels of Bdh1 were upregulated in skeletal muscle from mice after chronic exercise training (O'Neill et al., 2022; Svensson et al., 2016). However, acute exercise has been shown to induce ketosis (i.e., higher serum beta-hydroxybutyrate) in the immediate recovery period, particularly in female mice (Fulghum et al., 2022; Holcomb et al., 2022). Since ketogenesis is a process relegated to the liver and ketone body oxidation is increased with elevated serum concentrations (Evans et al., 2017), perhaps the need to upregulate genes involved in ketone body oxidation in the skeletal muscle during acute exercise is not necessary. Based on a recent report demonstrating improved exercise performance in mice treated with ketone monoester (Gouwens et al., 2025), the role of ketone body metabolism in acute exercise and with chronic exercise training are of high research interest in the field.

There are several limitations in our study design that should be considered. First, we assessed whole body insulin sensitivity and did not directly assess insulin-stimulated glucose uptake by tissues, namely skeletal muscle. For the ISTs, we elected to utilize a shorter fasting period, rather than the typical 4–6 h fasting based on a recent report that identified 2 h as an optimal fasting period in mice (Carper et al., 2020). Second, our metabolic analysis only focused on changes in gene expression and not protein content, enzymatic activity, or substrate utilization. Therefore, our observations of changes in gene expression may not be reflective of systemic or tissue specific metabolism. Third, while we included both male and female mice in our analysis, we did not control for estrus cycle in females. Likewise, we did not assess concentrations of sex hormones. Nevertheless, our findings are important in establishing time dependent changes in insulin sensitivity and metabolic gene expression in the post recovery period following one bout of aerobic exercise.

In summary, the goal of this study was to determine the persistence of insulin sensitivity and changes in metabolic gene expression following acute exercise in both male and female mice. We found insulin sensitivity persists 30 min after acute exercise, but not at 4 or 24 h. Additionally, we confirmed that females were more sensitive to insulin compared to males, independent of exercise. Upregulation of key genes in glucose, lipid, and mitochondrial metabolic pathways was observed at both 30 min and 24 h following acute exercise but resolved after 48–72 h of recovery. This finding is especially important for exercise training studies, emphasizing the lasting impact that one bout of acute exercise can have on gene expression, so that investigations can accurately dissect the acute responses from the chronic adaptations. Future research in this area should focus on the assessment of the lasting effects of exercise to standardize the appropriate time intervals in which to analyze tissues after exercise. These efforts are critical in improving the research literature so that exercise can be properly considered as an important strategy for the primary and secondary prevention of chronic diseases.

In conclusion, we believe the observations made in our study are crucial for future studies examining metabolic adaptations to chronic exercise training. For studies evaluating changes in glucose tolerance, insulin resistance, and/or insulin sensitivity, the acute effects of exercise appear to subside within 24 h, particularly if mice consume normal chow diets during the recovery window. Therefore, any changes in glucose homeostasis measured at the 24-h window should be reflective of the chronic exercise effects, especially in healthy animals. However, additional examination of acute effects of exercise on insulin sensitivity may be needed for high-fat, ketogenic, or other carbohydrate-restricted diets. For studies examining molecular adaptations to exercise training, a recovery window of at least 48 h, preferably 72 h, would be required before analyzing gene expression, and other cellular markers in tissues.
