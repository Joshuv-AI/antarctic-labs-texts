The Brain‐Age Gap in Pediatric Dystonia: Neuroanatomical Deviations Inform Deep Brain Stimulation Outcomes
Authors: Timur H Latypov, Alexandre Berger, Manuela Mohareb, Karim Mithani, Sara Breitbart, Lindsey M Vogt, Thiemo Dinger, Inge Meijer, Alexander G Weil, Alfonso Fasano, Carolina Gorodetsky, George M Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDystonia in children is a heterogeneous condition with variable response to deep brain stimulation (DBS). Brain‐age gap, a machine learning‐derived metric of structural deviation from norm, may capture signatures that differentiate underlying biotypes and predict outcomes.MethodsA brain age model was trained on several thousand normative developmental trajectories (n = 2623). Brain‐age gap (the difference between predicted and chronological age) was computed from pre‐DBS T1‐weighted magnetic resonance imaging in 37 children with dystonia and compared to matched healthy controls. Associations with etiology, Burke–Fahn–Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale (BFMDRS) and Pediatric Quality‐of‐Life (PedsQL) scores were examined.ResultsChildren with dystonia showed a greater brain‐age gap (structural deviation) compared to controls (P < 0.001). Greater gap was linked to worse baseline motor scores and poorer 1‐year quality‐of‐life improvement. Patterns differed by etiology, with distinct regional deviations in genetic and acquired dystonia.ConclusionBrain‐age modeling reveals biologically distinct subtypes of pediatric dystonia and may offer a biomarker for stratification and outcome prediction. © 2026 The Author(s). Movement Disorders published by Wiley Periodicals LLC on behalf of International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society.Keywords: Brain‐age Gap, Deep Brain Stimulation, Machine Learning, Neuromodulation, Neuroimaging, Pediatric Dystonia, Predictive Biomarker, Structural MRI, Surgical Outcomes

Childhood dystonia results from diverse etiologies with varying severity, and variable responses to deep brain stimulation (DBS). 1 , 2 , 3 Etiologies are broadly divided into genetic and acquired forms, typically resulting from perinatal injury, structural brain lesions, or metabolic insults. 1 , 2 This distinction is clinically important: genetic dystonia subtypes (particularly those that are not metabolic/neurodegenerative) generally retain more intact motor circuitry and tend to respond better to DBS, whereas acquired forms often reflect diffuse or irreversible damage with less predictable benefit. 4 , 5 , 6 This underscores the need for imaging biomarkers that offer objective, biologically grounded, and generalizable measures of disease burden and treatment potential. 7

One promising candidate is brain‐age modeling, a machine learning approach that indexes structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) features against chronological age to quantify deviations from normative development. 8 , 9 Although the “brain‐age” terminology reflects these methodological origins, the resulting gap serves as a sensitive structural marker rather than just a measure of senescence. 8 , 10 , 11 Brain‐age modeling has been widely used to study neurodegenerative and psychiatric conditions, however, its role in pediatric movement disorders remains unexplored. Normative childhood brain development modelling exist, however, their utility as biomarkers for predicting disease burden or treatment response in pediatric movement disorders has not been established. 12 , 13

We apply brain‐age modeling to a cohort of children with dystonia undergoing globus pallidus internus (GPi) DBS and show that deviations in brain‐age correlate with motor impairment and distinguish postoperative trajectories across dystonia subtypes. Structural brain aging emerged as a signature of clinical response, with potential applications as a single‐measure data‐driven biomarker for patient stratification, prognostication, and etiological insight in pediatric dystonia.

Methods

This retrospective study was approved by the research ethics board of the Hospital for Sick Children (protocol number: 1000068019).

Brain‐Age and Local Deviation Analysis

We developed a pediatric brain‐age model using a tree‐based algorithm (XGBoost Regression), trained on 2623 healthy individuals (age, 1.9–90 years) from publicly available datasets, to estimate brain age. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 The model integrates 338 regional structural metrics into a single estimate by weighting the importance of each feature based on the normative datasets (Fig. S1). Brain‐age gap was calculated as the difference between predicted and chronological age. Local deviations were assessed using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), which quantified the regional contributions to each subject's brain‐age. 18 Deviations from age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls' mean structural size (cortical thickness/cortical surface area/subcortical volume) were used to compute subject‐specific and group‐level local deviation maps.

Gray and white matter features from T1‐weighted MRI scans were used as the input for the brain‐age model. Imaging data was processed using FreeSurfer's recon‐all pipeline (v7.2.0), 19 extracting cortical thickness and surface area for 148 Destrieux atlas regions 20 (296 features), along with subcortical volumes via the “aseg” module. 21 This yielded 338 structural features per subject. To preserve biologically meaningful individual morphological differences associated with age, no normalization or head‐size corrections were applied. Previously, it was demonstrated that this data extraction approach is not susceptible to introduce site‐specific bias, therefore, we did not perform correction for the possible dataset or scanner differences. 22 , 23 , 24 The Supplementary Methods list the full methodological details.

Participants and Assessments

The brain‐age model was tested on preoperative T1‐weighted MR data from 37 children with dystonia. Children underwent GPi‐DBS implantation at The Hospital for Sick Children (n = 34) and Centre hospitalier de l'Université de Montréal (n = 3) between 2014 and 2024 and were enrolled in the CHILD‐DBS registry. 25 Etiology was classified as genetic (n = 26) or acquired (n = 11) based on clinical, genetic, and neuroimaging data (Table S1). Inclusion required availability of a high‐resolution preoperative T1‐weighted MRI and clinical follow‐up at 12 months. Visual inspection was used for quality control, and scans with major segmentation errors or gross abnormalities (large focal lesions and ventriculomegaly) were excluded (n = 2).

Dystonia severity was assessed using the motor component of the Burke‐Fahn‐Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale (BFMDRS), and quality of life was measured using the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL), collected at baseline and 12 months post‐DBS. PedsQL questionnaire was completed by parents, and BFMDRS scoring was done by trained movement disorder specialists.

Statistical Analysis

Independent t tests assessed group differences in brain‐age gap between dystonia patients and age‐ and sex‐matched controls (1:5 matching ratio), and between genetic and acquired subtypes. Pearson correlation tested associations with baseline BFMDRS and PedsQL percentage change. Shapiro–Wilk test confirmed that brain‐age gap and BFMDRS values were following normal distribution (P ~ ns). A general linear model (brain‐age gap ~ age + outcome × etiology) assessed predictors of outcome (PedsQL, BFMDRS), including the effect modification of outcome by etiology. False discovery rate (FDR) multiple comparisons correction was applied to all P‐values.

Results

Children with Dystonia Show Elevated Brain‐Age Gap Linked to Motor Impairment

Brain‐age predictions were first validated in the normative reference cohort, showing strong agreement between predicted and chronological age (Fig. S2). When applied to children with dystonia, the model revealed that the brain‐age gap was significantly elevated compared to age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls (mean difference: 14.87 ± 12.16 years; P < 0.001) (Fig. 1B,C). This indicates widespread deviations from normative brain structure in children with dystonia. Higher brain‐age gap was significantly associated with more severe pre‐surgical BFMDRS score (Pearson r = 0.43, P = 0.01) (Fig. 1D).

Brain‐Age Gap Patterns Differentiate Genetic and Acquired Dystonia Outcomes

Children with acquired dystonia exhibited markedly higher brain‐age gaps (22.85 ± 14.21 years), indicating more severe structural deviation compared to those with genetic forms (11.5 ± 9.91 years) (Fig. 2A) (P = 0.01). These group‐level differences point to distinct structural signatures across etiological subtypes.

Differential expression of brain‐age gap and local deviation in genetic and acquired dystonia. (A) Brain‐age gap was significantly greater in acquired compared with genetic dystonia. (B) Relationship between brain‐age gap and quality‐of‐life (PedsQL) improvement after DBS differed by etiology: higher brain‐age gap predicted poorer outcomes in genetic dystonia, which is reflected through significant interaction between etiology and outcome (brain‐age gap ~ age + etiology × outcome). (C) Brain‐age gap showed an age‐related decline in acquired but not genetic dystonia, suggesting differing developmental trajectories. (D) Local deviation maps for acquired dystonia revealed widespread positive deviations (accelerated aging), particularly in subcortical regions and posterior cortical areas. (E) Genetic dystonia showed mixed profiles, with subcortical acceleration but cortical regions of negative deviation, including superior frontal, cuneus, and posterior cingulate cortices. PedsQL, Pediatric Quality‐of‐Life; DBS, deep brain stimulation.

We examined how deviations in brain‐age relate to postoperative quality‐of‐life improvements following DBS. First, we tested the association between brain‐age gap and PedsQL change pre‐ DBS versus 1‐year post‐DBS using a general linear model, adjusting for chronological age on a subset of the patients. Higher brain‐age gap was significantly associated with less improvement in quality of life (P = 0.02). Next, we evaluated whether this relationship differed by dystonia etiology. A significant interaction between outcome and etiology was observed (P = 0.03) (Fig. 2B). Individuals with genetic dystonia showed an inverse association between brain‐age gap and postoperative PedsQL improvement, indicating that less structural deviation portends better quality of life outcomes. Importantly, we did not observe any significant relationships when assessing the brain‐age gap and improvement in the BFMDRS score 1 year after DBS implantation. No significant relationship was found between brain‐age gap and disease duration (r = 0.004, P = ns).

Local Deviation Pattern Similarities and Differences by Dystonia Etiology

To examine whether the observed group differences reflect distinct structural neurodevelopmental profiles, we compared local deviation patterns derived from SHAP‐based regional contribution maps (Fig. 2D,E). Both genetic and acquired dystonia showed signs of higher local deviation in subcortical structures, including the putamen, thalamus, nucleus accumbens, as well as the lateral ventricles. In contrast, their cortical profiles diverged, genetic dystonia exhibited widespread regions of negative local deviation, particularly within the superior frontal gyrus, cuneus, and posterior cingulate cortex, indicative of preserved cortical thickness relative to age‐matched norms.

Discussion

Normative brain modelling provides a foundation for examining how injury and development interact in childhood. 26 In pediatric dystonia, where the offending pathology occurs against a background of ongoing maturation, 2 such models uncover patterns that conventional severity scales often miss. Positioning each scan within age‐referenced data enables structural abnormalities to be interpreted along a neurodevelopmental or neurodegenerative axis rather than isolated findings without context. 27 Our results place brain‐age as a tool linking neuroimaging to patient biotypes and outcomes pediatric dystonia. Importantly, no significant association between BFMDRS improvement at the 1‐year follow‐up and brain‐age gap was observed. This discrepancy likely reflects the limitations of the BFMDRS in capturing the broader functional impact of DBS in pediatric patients, particularly in domains beyond motor symptoms such as cognition, mood, and participation. 5 , 28

We uncovered differences in brain‐age gap profiles by etiology. Children with acquired dystonia showed markedly larger brain‐age gaps and weaker DBS benefit (Fig. 2), likely suggesting a phenotype related to diffuse or irreversible injury. 4 , 29 , 30 Genetic cases displayed lower gaps and more pronounced improvement, consistent with evidence that patients with genetic or idiopathic dystonia typically respond better to neuromodulation. 2 , 4

Local deviation analysis revealed shared and distinct spatial patterns across etiological subtypes. Both genetic and acquired dystonia showed larger gaps within subcortical structures, including the putamen, thalamus, and striatum, consistent with their common involvement in motor circuit pathology. 30 , 31 Cortical profiles, however, diverged, genetic dystonia exhibited widespread regions of “negative local deviation.” The regions displaying this profile include superior frontal, cuneus, and posterior cingulate cortex. In contrast, acquired dystonia presented diffuse positive local deviations across the cortex, consistent with more extensive structural alteration.

Negative local deviation typically reflects preserved or thicker cortical gray matter relative to age norms. 32 In genetic dystonia, this pattern may indicate delayed or atypical pruning of associative cortices, supporting the view that genetic forms represent a circuit disorder amenable to modulation. 30 , 33 This interpretation is in line with prior reports implicating abnormal synaptic development and pruning processes in genetic dystonia, which could result in preserved gray matter. 34 , 35 Although the cross‐sectional design precludes defining the extent of primary developmental deviation or compensatory remodeling, we suggest that this signature is a summation of both processes. Retained cortical preservation could provide a structural substrate for compensatory plasticity, potentially explaining stronger DBS response observed in this group. 2 , 4 By contrast, the widespread positive deviations seen in acquired dystonia likely reflect cortical tissue loss or gliosis, in keeping with structural injury described in pediatric diffusion and volumetric studies. 5 , 31 This likely limits the capacity for network reorganization and reduces neuromodulatory efficacy.

Although our analyses focused on pediatric dystonia, the framework is applicable to other neurological conditions such as epilepsy (Latypov et al, under review), traumatic brain injury, or cognitive and neurodevelopmental disorders. 8 , 9 Brain‐age gap and local deviation metrics could be used as a reference for interpreting structural changes across diverse pathologies.

This study has limitations. First, the cohort size was modest. Second, given the retrospective cross‐sectional design, the brain‐age gap serves here as a static index of cumulative structural deviation at the time of surgery, rather than a longitudinal measure of biological aging. Future multicenter prospective studies will be important to validate these findings and assess the longitudinal utility of this biomarker in pediatric movement disorders.

In summary, our findings support the brain‐age gap as a biologically meaningful imaging marker that reflects etiology, and clinical outcomes. Brain‐age modeling quantifies where each child lies along normative development, providing a lens to interpret pathology in context, stratify patients, and inform clinical decisions.
