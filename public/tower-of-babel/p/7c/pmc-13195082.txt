Nanocomposite of iron-junk derived goethite and α-MnO2 nanorod for efficient sequestration of total arsenic from aqueous medium
Authors: Amulya Prasad Panda, Priyanka Kumari, Basit Ismail Gilani, Subhashree Mishra, Seikh Jiyaur Rahaman, Dharitri Rath, Gautam Sarkhel, S K Swain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn view of the demand for synthesizing newer and sustainable materials for efficient and single step sequestration of total arsenic from aqueous medium, a novel nanocomposite of waste iron junk derived goethite with uniform shaped α-MnO2 particles has been synthesized in the present work. The synchronization of goethite and α-MnO2 provides a single platform for addressing the issue. The performance of the material is investigated by systematic batch experimentation techniques. Alongside, interaction mechanism of arsenic species at solid-liquid interface was supported by mathematical modelling study and analytical instrumentation study. The material shows excellent affinity for both the arsenic species (As(III): 66.19 mg g− 1 and As(V): 121.20 mg g− 1) under circum-neutral pH range (0.2 g L− 1 of material is enough to remove > 80% of 0.25 mg L− 1 arsenic within 20 min). The study further shows a new direction of waste management by utilizing iron junk for sustainable remediation of groundwater contaminants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46992-y.Keywords: Total arsenic, Oxidation, α-MnO2, Goethite, Nanocomposite, AdsorptionSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Arsenic contamination of groundwater is a significant environmental issue across the globe. Chronic exposure to water tainted with arsenic has been linked to serious health problems1,2. Arsenic exists in the aqueous medium as arsenite (As(III)) and arsenate (As(V)). Since arsenite is a neutral species at pH values lower than 9.2, it is far more mobile and hazardous than arsenate, which is ionic across the pH range3–5. Many treatment techniques have been investigated to lower the amount of arsenic in drinking water, with adsorption showing great promise and effectiveness6,7.

It is a common practice to remove oxy-anionic As(V) by adsorption using nanomaterials (mono-, bi- and tri- metal/metal oxides), however As(III) is practically challenging to remove due to its non-ionic form in aqueous solution. As a result, pre-oxidation of As(III) is always preferred for improved elimination in order to change it into its ionic and less dangerous form, i.e., As(V). Several oxidants have been used in the past for this purpose, including ozone, potassium permanganate, chlorine, Fenton’s reagent8,9. However, as some reactions are excessively slow or result in toxic byproducts, these chemical treatments are unable to address the issue completely and more importantly it makes the entire process more complex and expensive. Considering the above fact, major focus has been given to synthesize an efficient and single-step approach for removal of both arsenic species.

The polymorphs of manganese dioxide have been well proven for the oxidation of environmental pollutants because of their distinct physicochemical characteristics and structural flexibility10,11. Different polymorphs of manganese dioxides (α, β, γ, δ, and ε type MnO2) exist based on the staking of the fundamental octahedral units [MnO6]11–13. With changing the experimental circumstances, distinct MnO2 polymorphs of varied oxidation reactivity and different crystal phase can be synthesized. The presence of Mn(III) content, crystal phase, reducibility, crystallinity, average oxidation state, textural characteristics, morphology and tunnel structure etc., are the parameters which play important role in deciding the oxidative reactivity of MnO2 polymers. However, some of the properties play preliminary role whether some plays secondary role.

In past, researchers have investigated the reactivity of MnO2 for oxidation of As(III). Wang et al., synthesized sea urchin-like α-MnO2 using KIO4 for arsenite oxidation14. Wei et al., studied the oxidation ability and mechanism of β-MnO215. and Rathi et al., identified Mn(III) edge sites in Mn oxides as key to the oxidation process16. Wang et al., also used δ-MnO2 modified activated carbon for As(III) oxidation and adsorption17. Based on the literature reports, α-MnO2 could be effective as well as stable in the oxidation of As(III).

However, its low pHPZC prevents it from being used as an efficient material to remove As(V) species; instead, it can only help speed up the removal of arsenic by oxidizing poisonous and non-ionic As(III) to ionic As(V). For effective adsorption of As(V), iron-based adsorbents such as goethite, hematite, magnetite, ferrihydrite, zero-valent iron etc., are proven to be the preferred materials because of its environmental compatibility, affordability, abundance, availability of ample surface hydroxyl groups and high pHPZC18–20. Efforts have also been given on valorisation of different waste iron-based materials to convert into nano-based materials for improved porosity, surface area and purity21–24.

Therefore, the present study focuses on synthesizing new goethite (GNP) nanoparticles by chemical valorization (acid-alkali treatment) of waste iron junk materials with high surface area and porosity for enhanced removal of As(V). On the basis of above discussion, it can be anticipated that combining the manganese and iron-based materials can provide a platform for effective removal of total arsenic from aqueous solution in single step.

In this context, a nanocomposite of α-MnO2 and iron waste junk derived goethite nanoparticles was synthesized in the present study and investigated for the removal of total arsenic from aqueous medium. Some studies earlier successfully demonstrated the application of goethite-MnO2 composite for removal of As(V)25–27. However, the present study introduces a novel nanocomposite obtained from waste (iron junk) derived hydroxyl-rich goethite nanoparticles and defect-rich 1D α-MnO2 nanorods. Coupling of these two materials provides a synergetic platform for simultaneous and single-step sequestration of total arsenic from aqueous solution. The current study primarily aims to analyze the arsenic removal performance of the synthesized material through systematic batch experimentation and to determine the responsible factors that aid in arsenic uptake, as well as it’s arsenic capturing mechanism using analytical techniques and mathematical modelling.

Experimental section

Chemicals and materials

In this study, chemicals of analytical-grade were used as received. In preparation of α-MnO2, potassium permanganate (KMnO4) and acetic acid (CH3COOH) were used. The iron junk utilized in this research was collected from the waste dump site of institute campus (Birla Institute of Technology, Mesra, Ranchi (India)). Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and hydrochloric acid (HCl) were used for chemical treatment of iron junk. Sodium salts of arsenate (Na2HAsO4·7H2O) and arsenite (Na2AsO2) were used for preparation of stock As(III) and As(V) solutions. All the materials synthesized and aqueous solutions used were done using double distilled water (DW).

Materials synthesis

Synthesis of α-MnO2 nanorods

The α-MnO2 was synthesized in a controlled condition at an optimized temperature. Briefly, 80 mL of 0.05 M KMnO4 solution was prepared in a glass beaker, followed by dropwise addition of 4 mL of acetic acid (CH3COOH). The solution was stirred well for at least 30 min and then transferred to an autoclave vessel for hydrothermal reaction at 110 °C for 10 h. Upon completion of reaction, the autoclave was cooled down to room temperature and resulting suspension was collected in a beaker. The resulting brown precipitate was repeatedly washed thoroughly with distilled water up to neutralization of pH. Thereafter, the solid precipitate was filtered out, dried and finely grinded for further use. The prepared material is named as α-MNP. The synthesis procedure is presented in Fig. 1a.

Graphical presentation of synthesis of (a) α-MNP, (b) GNP and (c) α-MGNP.

Synthesis of iron-junk derived goethite nanoparticles (GNP)

5 g of collected waste iron junk was finely grinded and dispersed in a 100 mL HCl (6 N) solution and heated at 70–80 °C for 3–4 h, following which it was allowed to stay at room temperature until dissolution of junk solids. The solution was decanted and 4 M NaOH was added drop-wise to the filtrate until complete precipitation (pH ~ 10.5). The precipitates were filtered out, followed by dried overnight at 80 °C and then grinded. The synthesis procedure of the goethite NP (named hereafter as GNP) is graphically presented in Fig. 1b.

Synthesis of nanocomposite adsorbent

The nanocomposite adsorbent was prepared by physical mixing method. The synthesized MNP and GNP powder was mixed in a ratio of 2:1 (w/w) and grinded thoroughly to ensure a homogeneous sample. The resulting solid sample (named as α-MGNP) of dark brown color was kept dry and subsequently used as arsenic removal adsorbent in batch experimentation study. Figure 1(c) provides a graphical illustration of the synthesis procedure of nanocomposite.

Further, the nanocomposite exhibits characteristic vibrational features of α-MnO2 and goethite confirming successful integration of the materials and existence of both Fe-O and Mn-O oxide framework which is much necessary from the point-of-view of total arsenic removal from aqueous solution. The nanocomposite shows a complex thermal profile showing the characteristic thermal transitions of both the individual components.

Total arsenic removal performance of α-MGNP composite

Dose variation study

To determine the optimal dose for removal of arsenic species from aqueous solution, batch experiments with variable adsorbent dose were carried out. As presented in Fig. 10, optimum removal observed with 0.2 g L− 1 of α-MGNP nanocomposite for both As(III) and As(V) with more than 95% of removal from 0.2 mg L− 1 initial concentration. Thus, the remaining batch experiments has been carried out with 0.2 g L− 1 of material.

Time-variable arsenic removal and kinetic modeling

The time-variation kinetic experiments were performed using different arsenic solutions (As(III), As(V) and As(mix)) of specific concentrations. The kinetic experiments data are depicted in Fig. 11(a–c). Preliminary analysis shows that a similar trend has been followed in all the cases. The removal trend can be categorized into three different phases, i.e., (i) rapid adsorption phase (2–10 min): in this phase the adsorption rate was very fast and majority of the ions (50–60%) get adsorbed, which could be due to the quick diffusion of arsenic ions from bulk to material interface and availability of ample sites on the adsorbent for arsenic uptake. (ii) Thereafter, in the second phase the rate of adsorption slowed down with time because of gradual occupancy of adsorption sites. (iii) finally, in the third phase the arsenic adsorption experiment reached equilibrium because of some rate-limiting processes and less availability of adsorption sites.

Kinetic data for adsorption of (a) As(III), (b) As(V) and (c) As(mix) by α-MGNP nanocomposite (Experimental Condition: w: 0.2 g L− 1, T: 298 K, C0: 0.25 mg L− 1, pH: 6.5 ± 0.5).

Furthermore, on comparing the kinetic experiments, it is observed that it takes 50 min to reach equilibrium in case of As(V), whereas it takes 10 min longer time in case of As(III) and As(mix). This shows a slightly higher affinity of the synthesized α-MGNP towards As(V) than As(III).

On analyzing the As(III) kinetic experiment data, it is observed that in two minutes of reaction time almost 60% of As(III) gets adsorbed; however, interestingly 0.052 mg L− 1 of As(V) gets appeared at the same time in the solution, confirming the instantaneous oxidation of As(III) to As(V) by the adsorbent in the removal process of As(III). After which, the concentration of both As(III) and As(V) gets lower with time and reach equilibrium. This confirms the simultaneous oxidation and adsorption of arsenic species over the adsorbent.

In case of As(mix) kinetic experiment, the kinetic experiment follows the similar trend and reached equilibrium at 60 min. Furthermore, the rate of As(V) was higher than that of As(III) in case of mix environment which could be due to the difference in their chemical properties and removal mechanism. Overall, it can be concluded that the developed material is well efficient in removal of both the arsenic species both in individual and competitive mix environment.

Kinetic data modeling: The kinetic experimental data were further analyzed using kinetic mathematical models through non-linear regression. The equations along with the notations are mentioned in the Sect. S2 (SI-I)37,38. The obtained parameters and kinetic model fitted plots are presented in Table S1 and Fig. 12.

Non-linear fitted (a) PSO, (b) PSO and (c) IPD kinetic model plots for adsorption of As(III), As(V) and As(mix) by α-MGNP nanocomposite. Symbols denote experimental data; solid lines denote model fits.

In all cases, PSO kinetic model has a comparatively higher correlation coefficient (R2) and lower χ2red value than the PFO kinetic model. Additionally, qe values calculated by PSO models and the experimentally determined qe values also well matched. This demonstrates the best fitting of PSO kinetic model to the experimental results, indicating the chemisorption nature of arsenic adsorption process by α-MGNP37,38.

Furthermore, the rate-limiting step in the arsenic adsorption process was determined using the IPD kinetic model. As seen by the intra-particle plots, the intercepts of these plots, qt vs. t1/2, deviate from the origin, which suggest that the pore diffusion process is not the only rate-limiting step. Therefore, it can be stated that the sorption process is influenced by both surface adsorption and intra-particle diffusion processes.

Effect of solution pH

The pH variation experimental results are shown in Fig. 13, and it clearly show the influence of pH on performance of the material. The adsorbent material adsorbs As(V) with more than 95% effectiveness in the pH range of 3–8. After that, when the pH was increased, the As(V) removal steadily decreased. The As(V) sorption capacity decreased to 80.53% when the pH was raised to 10.5. For As(III), negligible impact of pH was observed up to pH 8.5; beyond that, a slight decrease in removal was observed. This could be attributed to the chemical behavior of As(III) and As(V) under different pH3,39.

Result of pH variation study (Experimental Condition: w: 0.2 g L− 1, T: 298 K, C0: 0.25 mg L− 1).

The iso-electric point (IEP) of α-MGNP can be used to demonstrate the performance of α-MGNP at varying pH values. As the IEP of the material was found to be 8.25, high As(V) adsorption occurred at lower pH values (< 8.25) due to the significant electrostatic interaction between positively charged material and negatively charged As(V) species.

However, the removal effectiveness decreases at higher pH values (> 8.25) due to the repulsion between negatively charged As(V) species and the negatively charged surface. Therefore, it can be predicted that ion-exchange process is principally responsible for arsenic adsorption in higher or alkaline pH range. Above all, the synthesized α-MGNP is capable of reducing arsenic levels in drinking water to meet the WHO maximum contaminant limit (MCL), even within the circum-neutral pH range (6.5–8.5).

Effect of initial arsenic concentration and isotherm modelling

The performance of the α-MGNP was further analyzed with a range of initial arsenic concentrations. As presented in Fig. 14, the arsenic uptake efficiency increases with increase in concentration and eventually reaches a saturation level. The material exhibits better uptake efficiency towards As(V) compared to As(III), likely because of their differences in chemical speciation and adsorption behavior in the experimental pH condition.

The arsenic removal performance of α-MGNP with different initial concertation (Experimental Condition: w: 0.2 g L− 1, T: 298 K, pH: 6.5 ± 0.5).

Further, various adsorption isotherm models were fitted with the experimental data through non-linear regression to understand the adsorption mechanism and the interaction of arsenic species at the solid-liquid interface in a better way. The Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, and Dubinin-Radushkevich (D-R) isotherm models were considered for the isotherm modelling26,40–42. The isotherm modelling graphs, derived parameters along with the mathematical equations of the models are presented in Fig. 15 and Table S2 (SI-I).

The non-linear fitted isotherm plots of Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin and D-R models for adsorption of (a) As(III) and (b) As(V). Symbols denote experimental data; solid lines denote model fits.

The experimental data was best fitted with Freundlich isotherm, as indicated by higher correlation coefficient values (R2) (0.985 for As(III) and 0.998 for As(V)) and lower χ²red. This shows the maximum possibility of multi-layer adsorption of arsenic species on the adsorbent material. The ‘n’ value was further found to be greater than 1, implying favourable adsorption. The maximum monolayer loading capacity of arsenic on α-MGNP was calculated to be 66.19 mg g− 1 for As(III) and 121.20 mg g− 1 for As(V) using the Langmuir isotherm model. The efficiency of the material was found to be superior compared to many materials reported in the literature (Table S3).

Temkin and D-R isotherm models were used to understand further details on nature of the arsenic adsorption process. Both the arsenic species exhibit exothermic adsorption, as evidenced by the positive heat of adsorption values (As(III): 6.37 kJ mol− 1 and As(V): 10.70 kJ mol− 1) determined using the Temkin isotherm. Additionally, the D-R isotherm was used to determine the estimated mean free energy values, which were found to be between 8 and 16 kJ mol− 1. This suggests the chemisorption of arsenic species onto the adsorbent surface40,42,43.

Arsenic removal mechanism

XPS analysis was carried out to elucidate the arsenic uptake mechanism and to assess the chemical states and purity of materials. As displayed in Fig. 16a, the survey spectrum of α-MGNP shows the presence of peaks of Fe, Mn, O element confirming the purity of the synthesized composite. For arsenic reacted materials, an additional peak of As was observed as displayed in Fig. 16b. This confirms the stable binding of arsenic over the composite. The Fe 2p deconvoluted spectra of unreacted composite (Fig. 16c) shows the presence of dominant peaks around 710.9 eV, 712.58 eV corresponding to Fe 2p3/2 and 724.2 eV, 725.9 eV corresponding to Fe 2p1/2 orbital, showing characteristic Fe-Fe bond and surface Fe(III) of goethite material (Fe3+)44,45. Also, satellite peaks were observed around 719 eV and 733 eV due to the diagnostic feature for the presence of Fe3+ in the oxide-hydroxide environment. Similarly, Mn 2p spectra (Figure S2) shows two strong peaks around 641 eV and 653 eV corresponding to Mn 2p3/2 and Mn 2p1/2 spectra with a spin-orbit splitting of ~ 11.8 eV confirms the formation of MnO2 in the composite46,47. The Mn 2p3/2 deconvoluted spectra (Fig. 16d) further show sub-peaks correspond to Mn(II) and Mn(III) and Mn(IV), indicating the presence of minor amount of Mn2+ and Mn3+ along with Mn4+46,48. In fact, as reported earlier, the presence of lower-valence manganese species in MnO2 sample found to play an advantageous role in the oxidation process by taking part in the redox reaction through surface electron transfer11–13. The material stability after reaction with As(III/V) can further be confirmed from the strong appearance of Fe 2p and Mn 2p peaks as like in the composite.

XPS spectra of unreacted α-MGNP composite and arsenic (As(III) and As(V)) adsorbed composite: (a) survey spectrum, (b) its zoomed view (35–54 eV), (c) deconvoluted Fe 2p, (e) deconvoluted Mn 2p3/2.

Further, the O 1s deconvoluted spectra of the unreacted composite shows the characteristic peaks of lattice oxygen (M–O–M), adsorbed oxygen (M–OH) and surface adsorbed water molecules (H–O–H) around 529.70 eV, 531.5 eV and 533.6 eV respectively (Fig. 17a)12,49. However, for As(III/V) reacted composite, the peak associated with M-OH shows a distinct decrease in peak area, which confirms the participation of surface hydroxyl group in the arsenic adsorption reaction.

(a) Deconvoluted O1s XPS spectra of unreacted α-MGNP composite and arsenic (As(III) and As(V)) adsorbed composite and (b) deconvoluted As 3d spectra of As(III) and As(V) adsorbed composite.

To further validate the arsenic adsorption mechanism, the As 3d spectra of arsenic adsorbed composite were analyzed (Fig. 17b). The deconvoluted spectra for both the arsenic (As(III/V)) reacted materials show multiple peaks corresponding to As(V)-O, confirming the adsorption of arsenic in the form of As(V) only44,50. This confirms the complete oxidation of As(III) to As(V) during adsorption due to the presence of α-MnO2 in the composite material. Furthermore, the observed As 3d peaks are slightly red-shifted compared to standard As(V) peak (44.8 eV), which suggest the chemisorption nature of adsorption44,50.

To summarize, both As(III) and As(V) ion gets chemisorbed on α-MGNP composite soley as As(V) via exchanging surface hydroxyl groups, as strongly evident from FTIR and XPS analysis. However, As(III) gets completely oxidized to As(V) prior to its adsorption by MNP material, owing to its strong redox property. To visualize the arsenic capturing mechanism of α-MGNP composite, a graphical picture is presented in Fig. 18.

Performance of α-MGNP in presence of interfering ions

The preliminary analysis of impact of interfering ions revealed the negligible interference of chloride, sulfate and nitrate even at higher concentration. Thus, As(III/V) removal performance of α-MGNP in presence of phosphate and bicarbonate was determined at different initial concentrations. As depicted in Figure S3, for both As(III/V), removal efficiency of α-MGNP was still maintained above 70% in presence of high concentration of phosphate (5 mg L− 1) and bicarbonate (500 mg L− 1). In both the cases, impact of interfering ions is higher in removal of As(V) than As(III), which could be due to its repulsion with the phosphate and bicarbonate because of negatively charged nature. Further, the decrease in removal efficiency in presence of phosphate was higher compared to bicarbonate, which is obvious due to the similar chemical structure of As(V) and phosphate (PO43−) in aqueous medium. The results highlight the selectivity and practicability of α-MGNP for arsenic remediation, even under challenging water condition.

Performance of α-MGNP under groundwater conditions

As displayed in Fig. 19, α-MGNP demonstrated remarkable performance in removing arsenic from all the groundwater samples (A to E) containing arsenic in the range of 0.05–0.5 mg L− 1. The final arsenic concentration in all the samples was found to be below the WHO permissible drinking water level, regardless of the initial arsenic concentration, indicating the efficiency, selectivity and reliability of the synthesized α-MGNP material.

Reusability

Effective management of arsenic-bearing material is crucial from environmental safety perspective and designing a cost-effective process, making it important to develop highly reusable material. Thus, to investigate the reusability of α-MGNP, consecutive adsorption experiments have been conducted up to five adsorption cycles with both As(III) and As(V). Following each cycle, α-MGNP was regenerated using basic solution (0.5 M NaOH) before being reused. As depicted in Fig. 20, α-MGNP retained a noticeable removal capacity of 80.65% for As(III) and 85.11% for As(V) even after five cycles. Furthermore, the efficiency dropped just 16.4% for As(III) and 14.35% for As(V) from its initial performance. These findings demonstrate high reusable capacity of α-MGNP nanocomposite and its potential for long-term use in the cleanup of arsenic from aqueous solution.

Conclusions

In the present work, effort has been to develop an efficient nanocomposite of goethite and α-MnO2 for sequestration of total arsenic in aqueous medium. Nanorod shaped synthesized α-MnO2 is utilized as As(III) oxidizing agent; whereas, goethite derived by chemical valorization of waste iron junk utilized for As(V) capture. Analytical results demonstrate that the excellent oxidative capacity of MnO2 attributes to its reducibility property, and presence lower-valence manganese content in it. Based on the findings of batch experiments, mathematical modelling (kinetic and isotherm) and analytical data interpretation (FTIR & XPS), the arsenic removal mechanism has been confirmed which is found to be of chemisorption nature. Overall, the synergetic action of GNP and MNP resulted in simultaneous removal of both arsenic species in effective manner. The synthesis of high surface area (~ 142 m2 g− 1) α-MGNP adsorbent in a controlled and sustainable manner with outstanding arsenic capturing efficiency and high reusability demands further work for utilizing the material in pilot-scale experiments.
