Innovative biomaterials in promoting intraoral wound healing: Mechanisms, applications, and challenges
Authors: Tianyi Zhang, Mujie Yuan, Xuejiang Hao, Hui Gao, Minhua Teng, Fang Hu, Yan Liang, Jing Deng, Fan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Intraoral wounds face unique challenges, including a humid microenvironment, mechanical stress, and microbial colonization, complicating healing. Local and systemic factors further impair regeneration. Biomaterials with tailored mechanical properties, antibacterial activity, and bioactivity show promise in promoting mucosal repair and bone regeneration. However, dynamic microenvironment adaptation and multifunctional integration remain unmet needs. This review provides an integrative overview of the latest advancements in biomaterials for intraoral wound healing. We discuss the physiological mechanisms of intraoral wound healing, key influencing factors. Additionally, we explore the idea properties of biomaterials and their applications in various intraoral wound scenarios and highlight future directions in biomaterial development to guide clinical practice and research.

. Introduction

Intraoral wounds, stemming from diverse causes such as trauma, surgical interventions (including tooth extraction, dental implant placement, and periodontal surgeries), and pathological conditions (like oral ulcers), are prevalent in clinical practice [1,2]. The oral cavity's unique physiological environment, characterized by a moist milieu, constant mechanical stress from mastication and phonation, and a complex microbial ecosystem, poses significant challenges to the wound-healing process [3]. Unlike wounds in other body regions, intraoral wounds demand rapid and efficient healing to restore oral function, prevent infection, and minimize patient discomfort [4].

Wound healing in the oral cavity is a complex, multi-stage process involving hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and maturation [5]. However, the distinct tissue structures of the oral mucosa and alveolar bone confer unique healing characteristics. For instance, oral mucosal wounds generally heal faster with minimal scar formation, attributed to factors such as a high cell turnover rate, rich blood supply, and the presence of growth factors in saliva [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. In contrast, alveolar bone healing, especially after tooth extraction or implant placement, is a slower process that requires precise coordination of osteoblast and osteoclast activities for proper bone remodeling [10].

Local oral factors, including the moist environment, rapid epithelialization, microbial interference, and mechanical stress, significantly influence intraoral wound healing. The moist environment, while beneficial for cell migration, can also dilute coagulation factors and increase the risk of infection [9,11]. Rapid epithelialization may mask the slow progress of deep-tissue repair in oral mucosal wounds [9,12]. Microorganisms in the oral cavity, both symbiotic and pathogenic, can either promote or hinder wound healing, depending on various factors [13,14]. Mechanical stress from mastication and phonation can disrupt the newly formed tissues at the wound site, delaying the healing process [15]. Importantly, the interplay between local and systemic factors may exacerbate healing barriers. Systemic factors such as diabetes mellitus and immunosuppression also have a profound impact on intraoral wound healing. Diabetic patients often experience impaired wound healing due to oxidative stress, inflammation, and an increased susceptibility to bacterial infection [16]. Immunosuppressed patients, such as those undergoing radiotherapy or chemotherapy, are more prone to opportunistic infections, which can severely impede the wound-healing process [17].

To actively counteract these multidimensional barriers, biomaterials are emerging as a promising approach through the rational design of properties that target site-specific challenges. Rational design of biomaterials with specific properties, such as appropriate mechanical strength, adhesion, hemostasis, antimicrobial property, anti-inflammation, osteogenic property and so on, is essential for promoting intraoral wound healing [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. Critically, this rational design paradigm necessitates context-aware adaptation. Different types of wounds (ranging from small oral ulcers to complex postoperative wounds) inherently impose distinct spatiotemporal demands on material systems [[22], [23], [24]]. Consequently, biomaterial formulations must demonstrate precision adaptability to both wound pathophysiology and environmental stressors, thereby enabling synergistic healing acceleration. This demand for innovative biomaterials has driven the further development of relevant research, aiming to develop higher-quality biomaterials for intraoral wound healing to meet the urgent clinical treatment needs.

This review aims to comprehensively summarize the current knowledge on intraoral wound-healing mechanisms, the influence of local and systemic factors on intraoral wound healing, the designs and applications of biomaterials for specific intraoral wound healing, and the challenges and future directions in this field (Fig. 1). By bridging mechanistic insights and material innovation, this review aims to accelerate the development of next-generation biomaterials that improve healing outcomes, reduce complications, and enhance patients’ quality of life.Fig. 1A schematic summary of biomaterials for intraoral wound healing.Fig. 1

. Intraoral wound healing process

The wound healing process in the oral cavity follows the classic four-phase paradigm (hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, maturation) [25], but exhibits unique adaptations due to its distinct physiological environment. This section focuses on the essential contrasts between oral mucosal and alveolar bone healing compared to skin and long bone repair, emphasizing characteristics critical to biomaterial design.

Key features of oral mucosal healing

Unlike skin wounds, oral mucosal injuries heal remarkably faster with minimal scarring, owing to saliva-enhanced hemostasis, a balanced inflammatory response, rapid re-epithelialization, and optimal collagen remodeling.

Hemostasis: Contrast to skin healing, oral mucosal wound repair demonstrates markedly accelerated hemostasis and a uniquely favorable microenvironment, primarily due to saliva-derived factors. Upon injury, oral mucosa rapidly initiates hemostasis: endothelial damage and exposed extracellular matrix (ECM) activate platelets and trigger the coagulation cascade [[26], [27], [28]], leading to vasoconstriction, platelet plug formation, and fibrin-based clotting [26,29]. Platelets release mediators, chemokines, cytokines, and growth factors that promote cell recruitment and healing [26,29,30]. The fibrin–fibronectin clot acts as a provisional matrix for epithelial and fibroblast migration [5,26], while thrombin activation and subsequent fibrinolysis fine-tune coagulation and enhance permeability [29,31,32]. Crucially, hemostasis is significantly faster in oral mucosa than in skin, due to high tissue factor in both clot and saliva. Saliva further provides growth factors and antimicrobial peptides that enhance platelet aggregation, accelerate repair, and inhibit infection, establishing a distinctive regenerative setting not seen in skin [9,11,33].

Inflammation: Unlike the prolonged and intense inflammatory phase characteristic of skin healing, oral mucosal wound repair exhibits a markedly faster-resolving and less inflammatory response, significantly reducing fibrosis risk. After hemostasis, the inflammatory phase begins, wherein chemokine-driven immune cells peak at 24–48 h and persist for about a week [27]. Neutrophils arrive first, clearing necrotic tissue and activating monocytes via cytokines to initiate re-epithelialization [5,34,35]. Monocytes then differentiate into macrophages, which remove pathogens and debris while releasing both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory factors; macrophage polarization into pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype or repair-oriented M2 phenotype is critical to healing outcomes [36,37]. A key distinction lies in the intensity and duration of inflammation: skin healing often shows strong, persistent infiltration of neutrophils and macrophages releasing IL-6, TNF-α, and pro-fibrotic TGF-β1, increasing scarring risk. In contrast, oral mucosa demonstrates reduced neutrophil and T cell infiltration, lower pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β), and higher levels of anti-inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-10) and anti-fibrotic TGF-β3. Additionally, Foxp3+ regulatory T cells help suppress excessive immunity, further minimizing fibrosis [8,9,11,[38], [39], [40]].

Proliferation: The proliferative phase in oral mucosa initiates earlier and exhibits accelerated re-epithelialization, reduced contraction, and more balanced tissue regeneration, markedly decreasing scarring risk, different with skin healing. This phase involves angiogenesis, granulation tissue formation, collagen deposition, re-epithelialization, and wound contraction [30,41]. Angiogenesis is driven by growth factors from platelets, fibroblast growth factor (FGF)-2, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), stimulating endothelial cells to form new vessels and restore blood supply [6,42,43]. Simultaneously, fibroblasts migrate into the wound and secrete ECM components such as collagen and fibronectin.Critically, oral mucosal healing begins within 24 h, with keratinocytes demonstrating higher migratory capacity—closing 5 mm wounds in 2–3 days versus ∼7 days in skin—aided by saliva-derived histatins that directly stimulate epithelial movement. Moreover, oral fibroblasts resist TGF-β1-induced myofibroblast differentiation, limiting wound contraction. While skin healing often produces excessive granulation tissue, persistent collagen I/III, and prolonged angiogenesis linked to hypertrophic scarring, oral mucosa achieves rapid and balanced ECM deposition and earlier vascular normalization [8,9,11,38,39]. Epithelial cells migrate from wound edges to seal the surface, and fibroblasts differentiate into myofibroblasts to facilitate closure [44].

Maturation: Unlike skin healing which commonly results in scar formation, the maturation phase in oral mucosal wounds typically achieves scarless or minimal-scar regeneration through superior ECM remodeling. This final healing phase aims to rebuild tissue structure and restore function by clearing excess vessels, fibroblasts, and inflammatory cells while extensively reorganizing the ECM [40,45]. Fibroblast-secreted matrix metalloproteinases play a key role, regulating the degradation of old collagen and synthesis of new collagen [46]. The initial loose fibronectin network is gradually replaced by dense, organized collagen bundles, with type III collagen being substituted by type I collagen [47]. However, oral mucosa exhibits strikingly different remodeling outcomes: it maintains faster collagen turnover, a higher Type III/I collagen ratio, richer deposits of hyaluronic acid and fibronectin, and elevated MMP-to-inhibitor ratios (e.g., MMP-2, MMP-3). These features enable efficient degradation of provisional matrix and highly organized collagen architecture, contrasting with skin's disorganized scar tissue that regains only ∼80 % tensile strength. Although remodeling occurs under functional stress like mastication, the oral ECM environment facilitates near-scarless regeneration with superior structural restoration [6,8,9,11,38,39,42,43,48].

Unique aspects of alveolar bone healing

Unlike long bone fracture repair, alveolar bone regeneration occurs via stable clot-guided intramembranous ossification, accelerated M2 macrophage-driven osteogenesis, and direct woven-to-lamellar bone remodeling.

Hemostasis and Inflammation: The socket's contained geometry stabilizes the initial blood clot, rich in cytokines/growth factors, enabling direct intramembranous ossification without cartilage intermediate [[49], [50], [51]]. Crucially, the socket's environment, surrounded by intact alveolar bone walls, provides inherent stability and optimal retention of the initial blood clot. This differs from endochondral ossification in semi-stabilized long bones, which requires a cartilage template [20,52,53]. M2 macrophages dominate early, secreting VEGF/FGF/bone morphogenetic protein to drive osteoblast differentiation [20]. Macrophages undergo functional differentiation: pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages actively eliminate pathogens and necrotic tissue; anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory mediators such as IL-1 receptor antagonists and IL-10, as well as growth factors like VEGF and FGF. Crucially, factors such as bone morphogenetic protein released by M2 macrophages drive the directional migration and differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells into osteoblasts [20].

Proliferation: Fibroplasia and woven bone deposition begin within 2 weeks, bypassing chondrogenesis [[54], [55], [56], [57]]. Fibroblast migration and proliferation are central, accompanied by an increase in collagen and extracellular matrix proteins. Granulation tissue is replaced by a provisional matrix rich in mesenchymal stem cells, dense fibers, and blood vessels. This matrix supports cell migration and new tissue formation, with over half of the cells having osteogenic potential [55,56]. Fibrous proliferation entails the rapid deposition of a temporary matrix, which is then penetrated by newly formed blood vessels and osteoblasts. In contrast, femoral fractures form cartilaginous callus under similar timelines [20,52].

Remodeling: Woven bone is replaced by lamellar bone/marrow via coupled osteoblast-osteoclast activity (regulated by RANKL/RANK/M-CSF), optimizing bone quality over months-years [10,20]. Early bone modeling is mainly characterized by resorption of the alveolar wall and bone bundles. Bone remodeling is initiated from the apical region after about four weeks, as immature woven bone is gradually replaced by mineralized lamellar bone and bone marrow. Endochondral healing involves calcified cartilage replacement, differing in cellular dynamics [20,52].

. Factors affecting intraoral wound healing

The management of intraoral wounds depends on a thorough understanding of the characteristics of intraoral wounds and the environment in which they exist. The following sections will introduce the factors influencing intraoral wound healing. This information aims to provide support and theoretical foundations for the development and improvement of specific biomaterials for intraoral wounds (Fig. 3).

Local factors

During the process of intraoral wound healing, a variety of local factors play crucial roles, interacting with each other and collectively influencing the repair process. These factors include both positive aspects that promote healing and negative aspects that may impede it. The following sections will delve into how these local factors, including the moist environment, rapid epithelialization, microbial interference, and mechanical stress, affect the healing of intraoral wounds.

Moist environment

The moist environment formed and maintained by saliva in the oral cavity facilitates cell migration and is one of the key factors in wound healing; for example, it promotes the migration of fibroblasts to the wound site to synthesize collagen and extracellular matrix components [5,11]. In addition, saliva is rich in bioactive peptides and proteins such as epidermal growth factor (EGF), VEGF, and FGF, which directly promote the healing process. Among them, EGF can enhance the migration ability of oral keratinocytes and fibroblasts. Cathepsin inhibitors and antimicrobial histatins help regulate proteolytic activity and prevent infection [38,[58], [59], [60]].

However, the continuous moistening of the wound by saliva can also have adverse effects. On the one hand, the constant flushing of saliva can dilute clotting factors at the wound site and affect the hemostatic process—a critical initial step in wound healing. For example, saliva dilution may reduce the concentration of coagulation factor VIII, thereby inhibiting the coagulation cascade, delaying thrombin formation and fibrin clot production, and impairing early wound closure [6]. On the other hand, the moist oral environment also provides suitable conditions for microbial growth. The oral cavity itself harbors a large number of microorganisms, and the continuous moistening by saliva makes it easier for microbes to adhere to and proliferate on the wound surface, increasing the risk of infection. Once infection occurs, the inflammatory response is exacerbated, and the released cytokines and inflammatory mediators can damage newly formed tissues and cells, interfere with the normal healing process, and even lead to delayed or non-healing [9,61,62].

Rapid epithelialization

Oral mucosal wounds re-epithelialize significantly faster than skin [9]; epithelial cells begin to migrate and proliferate within 24 h after injury, and wounds ≤5 mm are usually fully re-covered within 2–3 days, whereas equivalent skin defects require up to 7 days [8,38,39]. This rapid epithelial closure promptly seals the surface, limits microbial penetration, and provides a relatively clean micro-environment for healing.

Nevertheless, deep-tissue repair proceeds much more slowly. Re-epithelialization is only the earliest phase; subjacent restoration still demands collagen synthesis by fibroblasts and neovascularization [12]. Swift surface closure can mask lagging deep repair: the epithelium may appear intact while underlying connective tissue remains deficient, with disordered collagen alignment and inadequate vascularization [63]. If care is discontinued prematurely or excessive force is applied, the wound readily dehisces [64,65]. Moreover, rapidly proliferated epithelial cells often form loose intercellular junctions and an immature basement membrane, conferring limited resistance to mechanical stress and microbial challenge and predisposing to secondary injury, thereby compromising the stability and durability of healing [66,67].

Microbial interference

The oral cavity is a complex ecosystem in which commensal and pathogenic microorganisms coexist. Under physiologic conditions a dynamic equilibrium prevails: commensals protect oral health by competing for nutrients and occupying adhesion sites, thereby restraining pathogen outgrowth [13,68]. Wounding disrupts this balance. Tissue damage and wound exudate provide abundant nutrients, enabling low-abundance pathogens to proliferate rapidly. For example, in the wound after tooth extraction, pathogenic microorganisms such as Staphylococcus aureus, which were originally at a low level, may increase rapidly in number due to the suitable environment provided by the wound [69].

Moreover, oral microbes readily form biofilms on wound surfaces—structured communities embedded in self-secreted extracellular polysaccharide matrices [14]. Biofilms dramatically enhance microbial resistance to host immunity and antimicrobial agents. Within the biofilm, bacteria continuously release toxins and inflammatory mediators that irritate adjacent tissue and perpetuate chronic inflammation. In patients with periodontitis, for example, Porphyromonas gingivalis-rich biofilms steadily liberate lipopolysaccharide and other virulence factors, activating immune cells to produce high levels of interleukin-1 and tumour necrosis factor. These cytokines degrade ECM, inhibit fibroblast proliferation and impair collagen synthesis, ultimately delaying wound healing [70].

Mechanical stress

Mastication is one of the important daily functions of the oral cavity. During the wound-healing process, mastication activity exerts continuous mechanical stimulation on the wound. When masticating, the mastication force of the teeth on food is transmitted to the oral mucosa and the tissues around the wound through the periodontal tissues [15]. Excessive mastication force may damage the stability of the newly formed tissues at the wound site. For example, in the early stage of wound healing after tooth extraction, the newly formed blood clot and granulation tissue are relatively fragile. The greater pressure generated when masticating hard food may cause the blood clot to fall off, making the wound bleed again and delaying wound healing [20,71].

Phonation activity also exerts mechanical stimulation on oral wounds. The movement of oral muscles and the friction between the tongue and the oral mucosa during phonating all act on the wound. Especially for patients with oral mucosal wounds in frequently active areas such as the tongue and lips, the impact of phonation activity on wound healing is more obvious. Moreover, frequent phonation activity makes the wound continuously exposed to the oral environment, increasing the chance of microbial infection and further interfering with wound healing [72].

Systemic factors

Systemic health conditions can profoundly influence intraoral wound healing, with multiple factors such as age, nutritional status, hormone levels, psychological stress, and mental health playing important roles [[73], [74], [75]]. In this study, we focus primarily on the effects of diabetes mellitus and immunosuppression.

Diabetes mellitus

In patients with systemic diseases, particularly those diagnosed with diabetes, the healing of intraoral wound is significantly more challenging. Various factors, including the excessive accumulation of ROS, elevated levels of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), bacterial infections, and persistent inflammation, contribute to prolonged healing times [76]. Specifically, AGEs bind to receptors expressed on the cell surface, activating inflammatory signaling pathways (e.g., NF-κB) and promoting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Additionally, hyperglycemia-induced mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) production stimulates the activation of protein kinase C and the formation of AGEs, ultimately leading to the expression of inflammatory genes, including TNF-α and TGF-β [16]. These factors collectively impede the migration of oral and dermal keratinocytes, inhibit the phenotypic shift of macrophages from the M1 to M2 subtype, and perpetuate inflammation. Moreover, the hyperglycemic state in diabetic patients is conducive to bacterial infections, further complicating the healing process of intraoral wound [77].

Immunosuppression

Patients undergoing immunosuppression, such as those receiving radiotherapy or chemotherapy for cancer, experience varying degrees of immune system dysfunction. This suppression makes them highly susceptible to opportunistic infections, which are among the key factors impeding intraoral wound healing. Radiotherapy and chemotherapy, while effective in killing tumor cells, also cause significant damage to the immune system [78]. Radiation therapy directly injures immune cells such as lymphocytes and macrophages, leading to a reduction in their numbers and a decline in their function [79]. Chemotherapeutic agents interfere with the DNA synthesis and metabolism of cells, thereby affecting the proliferation and differentiation of immune cells and weakening the overall immune function [80]. Due to impaired immune defense, opportunistic infections frequently occur in the oral cavity of immunosuppressed patients. Microorganisms that are normally present at low levels or in a commensal state, can proliferate extensively and cause infections [81]. These pathogens grow rapidly in the local tissues of the wound, releasing toxins that destroy tissue cells and exacerbate the inflammatory response. The inflammatory mediators released by inflammatory cells further damage surrounding normal tissues and disrupt the critical steps of wound healing, including cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis. For instance, Candida infection in oral wounds can form a white pseudomembrane, with hyphae invading the tissue and causing local inflammation. This leads to symptoms such as pain, redness, and delayed healing of the wound [17].

. Designs and applications of biomaterials for specific intraoral wound healing

Intraoral wound healing is a complex process involving the coordination of various regulatory factors to restore the damaged mucosal barrier function [6]. Intraoral wounds are diverse, ranging from small oral ulcers to complex postoperative wounds. During the healing process, these wounds face common challenges, such as the moist environment, abundant bacterial presence, and mechanical forces from mastication [26]. However, each wound type also has specific healing requirements. This necessitates the use of materials with strong adhesion, antibacterial property, and anti-inflammation etc., combined with targeted treatment strategies to promote healing. In this section, the healing characteristics of different types of intraoral wounds and the properties of ideal wound healing materials are first discussed, with a focus on designs of innovative biomaterials and their research progress for four major wounds: oral ulcer, tooth extraction, implant and periodontal wounds (Table 1). Oral ulcer wounds

Oral ulcers are a common mucosal disease characterized by persistent erosion or disruption of the oral epithelium, typically occurring on the tongue, inner lips, and buccal mucosa. These superficial lesions are confined to the epithelial and superficial connective tissue layers, sparing the underlying muscle. They are often associated with infections, trauma, and immune dysregulation [82]. Given the unclear etiology and pathogenic mechanisms, treatment typically focuses on symptom relief, including reducing inflammation and pain, preventing infection, and promoting healing [83]. However, the effectiveness of most topical therapies, such as mouthwashes, gels, films, and spray, is often limited when used alone, primarily due to the constant activity of oral muscles and continuous saliva secretion, which reduce adhesion and shorten the duration of drug activity within the oral cavity, thereby diminishing therapeutic efficacy [84]. Furthermore, ulcer healing efficiency is influenced by several interacting factors: the fibrinous membrane covering the ulcer surface impedes drug penetration, and the concomitant loss of mucosal barrier function heightens the risk of bacterial infection [85]. Local environmental factors at the wound site, including reduced levels of growth factors, may directly contribute to ischemia and impaired tissue regeneration, while systemic conditions such as hyperglycemia associated with diabetes, can exacerbate these pathophysiological processes, worsening ulcer severity [86].

To address these interconnected challenges, the development of innovative intraoral wound dressings has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy. An ideal biomaterial must: (1) integrate multifunctional synergies, encompassing anti-inflammatory properties to modulate excessive immune responses and pro-healing capabilities to accelerate tissue regeneration, (2) exhibit robust wet adhesion to withstand salivary flushing and mechanical forces from mastication, ensuring persistent coverage at the wound site, and (3) deliver drug efficiently for prolonged action and deep penetration, while ensuring superior patient comfort to enhance therapeutic efficacy and compliance.

Effective anti-inflammatory action remains a core challenge. Inflammation is a hallmark feature of oral ulcers, with current treatments primarily targeting inflammation via pharmacological approaches [87]. Basic anti-inflammatory corticosteroids like betamethasone and dexamethasone (DXMS) show significant anti-inflammatory effects, but their efficacy is limited by rapid metabolism, inability to penetrate the overlying fibrinous pseudomembrane (restricting action to superficial layers), and shortened retention time due to salivary flow and oral movement. These limitations highlight the need for innovative strategies to address penetration barriers, prolong retention, and optimize anti-inflammatory efficacy. The research response has evolved along two main pathways: first, renovating drug delivery, via microneedles (MNs) that pierce the mucosal barrier or biomimetic adhesives that extend residence, thereby overcoming the short dwell-time and poor penetration of conventional gels and films; and second, introducing novel therapeutic agents or mechanisms that offer new modes of action.

MN systems , for instance, can penetrate the fibrinous pseudomembrane, enabling deep drug deposition into ulcer beds. Guo et al. [88] developed a Betamethasone 21-phosphate sodium (BSP)-Betamethasone 17,21-dipropionate (BDP)@ Hyaluronic acid (HA) MNs for treating oral ulcers (Fig. 3A), which demonstrated sufficient mechanical strength to penetrate the mucosa of rats’ ventral tongues (∼207 ± 3 μm depth). Upon insertion, the rapidly dissolving HA MN delivered BSP and BDP to the ulcer base within 3 min. Compared to traditional topical injections and creams, BSP-BDP@HAMNs enabled painless penetration and provided a prolonged therapeutic effect deep within the ulcer.

Meanwhile, materials engineered with strong wet adhesion can resist salivary flushing and mechanical stress, significantly extending drug residence time at the target site. For example, Ji et al. [89] utilized the chemical principles of mussel adhesion in moist environment to develop a polysaccharide oral patch called “Chitoral”. The Chitoral instantly dissolves upon contact with saliva and then forms inter-molecular complexes with oral mucins, rapidly converting into an insoluble, adhesive hydrogel-like material by the cooperative actions of covalent crosslinking and physical entanglement. Xing et al. [90] designed an ideal Janus patch for accelerating the treatment and healing of oral ulcers (Fig. 3B). The Janus patch has a dual-functional surface, consisting of an adhesive layer and a smooth layer. Inspired by the composition and mixed adhesion mechanism of barnacles, Xing et al. modified glycine and combined it with hydroxyapatite NPs (HAp) to form an adhesive layer capable of forming rich covalent bonds with tissues. This bio-inspired approach addresses a key clinical need: maintaining robust adhesion in the wet, dynamic oral environment to protect the ulcer and prolong therapeutic contact. However, long-term adhesion strength, tissue compatibility, and patient-perceived comfort remain factors that must be carefully balanced before clinical adoption.

Beyond refining the delivery of conventional anti-inflammatory drugs, research is also exploring next-generation materials with intrinsic anti-inflammatory properties. Nanozymes, for example, are novel anti-inflammatory agents whose intrinsic enzyme-mimetic activities (e.g., ROS scavenging) can precisely modulate inflammatory cascades while avoiding the rapid metabolism of traditional hormones. Gu et al. [91] developed vitamin B2-modified iron oxide NPs that improved oral ulcer wound healing through anti-inflammatory and antibacterial activities, aligning with the clinical understanding that managing oxidative stress and inflammation is central to ulcer treatment. In the study by Zhang et al., the anti-inflammatory agent astragalus polysaccharide (APS) was encapsulated in MNs [92]. The sustained release of this small-molecule drug robustly accelerated oral-mucosal repair and remodeling by scavenging excess ROS, driving M2 macrophage polarization, and blocking ferroptosis through the Nrf2/HO-1/SLC7A11 axis. Compared to antibiotics or corticosteroids, APS carries no risk of microbial resistance, mucosal atrophy, or hormonal imbalance, and avoids the potential cytotoxicity of metal ions, offering a simpler, more comprehensive route to ulcer healing.

However, the therapeutic effect of dressings with a single function is ultimately limited. Hence, developing multifunctional oral ulcer wound dressings is a priority, as different functionalities can act synergistically for better healing outcomes. Building on their previous work that loaded the common anti-inflammatory agent DXMS into microneedles, Zeng et al. further incorporated basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), a potent angiogenic cytokine, into the same microneedle array to accelerate ulcer healing. In vitro and in vivo, the DXMS&bFGF MNs cut inflammation and boosted capillary density, outperforming single-agent or blank controls [93]. Similarly, Wang et al. [94]developed a soluble HA-based MN patch loaded with DXMS acetate, vitamin C, and lidocaine hydrochloride for treating recurrent oral ulcers, leveraging vitamin C's role in pro-proliferation and its common deficiency in some patients. The patch shortened total healing time to 5 days, significantly outperforming the control group. Although topical agents (corticosteroids, wound-healing accelerants and herbal preparation) reliably damp symptoms of oral ulcers, their prolonged use is accompanied by appreciable adverse effects. Inspired by the robust regenerative capacity of mesenchymal stem cell, Dong et al. fabricated an injectable fibrinogen/thrombin hydrogel loaded with stem-cell-derived lysate from human exfoliated deciduous teeth. The construct efficiently polarised macrophages toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype while suppressing M1-marker secretion, and simultaneously enhanced epithelial proliferation and vascular remodeling adjacent to the basement membrane [95]. Multifunctional integration strategies markedly accelerate healing through synergistic therapy and are widely regarded as the next frontier. Nevertheless, the quality control, long-term stability, and intricate component–component interactions inherent in multi-ingredient systems introduce fresh hurdles for clinical translation.

Beyond common aphthous ulcers, these innovative strategies also show great promise for treating oral mucositis (OM) and diabetes-related ulcers. OM is a common complication in head and neck cancer patients undergoing radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy. Oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and impaired wound healing are key mechanistic targets [[96], [97], [98], [99]]. While several moist-adhesive bioadhesives with anti-inflammatory and mucosal repair properties have been developed [100,101], their clinical translation has been hampered by poor comfort and compliance. Therefore, an ideal bioadhesive must balance effective treatment with users' comfort [102]. Zhang et al. [103] designed a salivary amylase (SA)-responsive lozenge composed of porous manganese-substituted Prussian blue (PMPB) nanocubes (NCs), anti-inflammatory apremilast (Apr), and a starch controller. The SAs-responsive release manner and the high tissue affinity property of starch not only prolonged the resistance time of active components in the oral cavity compared to direct deglutition but also improved the comfort of use that bioadhesives failed to achieve. Fu et al. [104] employed dynamic CS-poly (ethylene glycol) Schiff base linkages to convert liquid Kangfuxin into a shape-adaptive gel for spatiotemporally dynamic treatment (SDT) of OM (Fig. 3C). This shift towards patient-centric designs like lozenges is critical for improving adherence in vulnerable OM patients.

Systemic factors, particularly diabetes, can significantly impair oral ulcer wound healing. The sustained hyperglycemic environment disrupts the healing process, leading to loss of physiological cascade and synchrony [[105], [106], [107]], resulting in immunosuppression, excessive inflammation, impaired angiogenesis, and increased infection susceptibility, which significantly slow healing [76]. A mainstream research direction is pairing strongly adhesive hydrogels with immunomodulatory nanoparticles that combine potent ROS scavenging and near-infrared photothermal bactericidal activity. Xiang et al. [108] proposed a novel hydrogel patch (SCE2) composed of a UV-adhesive biopolymer matrix (SFMA and CHS-NB) and healing-promoting squid ink NPs. Cai et al. developed a light-adhesive hydrogel loaded with platinum-decorated melanin nanoparticles [109]. Chen et al. designed a photoresponsive, adhesive hydrogel based on silk-fibroin/hyaluronic-acid matrix embedded with Fe3+/shikonin nanoparticles [110]. These nanoparticles (squid-ink, platinum-melanin, Fe3+/shikonin) all combine outstanding ROS-scavenging with potent NIR photothermal bactericidal activity, effectively eradicating bacteria, rebalancing oxidative stress, and promoting transition from inflammation to active tissue regeneration, thereby addressing the challenges of diabetic oral ulcers. Yet their clinical utility, especially the durability of this efficacy under fluctuating glycaemic conditions, remains to be rigorously established.

Tooth extraction wounds

Tooth extraction, a common dental procedure necessitated by irreparable caries, severe periodontitis, trauma, or impaction, creates a complex healing environment within the oral cavity [111]. While routine, the subsequent healing is highly vulnerable to oral contaminants, mechanical disruption from tongue movement and mastication, and bacterial infiltration, often leading to complications such as pain, swelling, infection, and the dreaded dry socket, which significantly impair patient quality of life [112,113].

Conventional management relies on absorbable gelatin sponges and platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) to promote hemostasis and soft tissue healing [114,115]. However, a critical appraisal reveals these materials are fraught with limitations:their propensity to swell compromises physical barrier integrity, poor wet adhesion leads to premature displacement, lack of inherent antimicrobial activity increases infection risk, and rapid degradation fails to provide sustained protection, ultimately resulting in inadequate socket sealing and delayed healing [116,117]. This underscores the pressing clinical need for a new generation of biomaterials that can transcend mere passive filling and actively orchestrate the healing process. An ideal socket dressing must therefore be multifunctional, integrating: (1) environmental resistance via minimal swelling and robust wet adhesion; (2) pathogen defense through broad-spectrum antimicrobial and anti-biofilm activity; (3) jemostatic and immunomodulatory competence for rapid coagulation and inflammation control; (4) osteogenic induction to preserve alveolar architecture and promote bone regeneration; and (5) soft tissue regenerative coordination to facilitate angiogenesis and epithelialization.

Hydrogels have emerged as a premier platform to address these challenges, owing to their tunable physicochemical properties, excellent biocompatibility, and ability to conform to irregular socket geometries. A primary hurdle for intraoral hydrogels is swelling-induced mechanical degradation and loss of adhesion. Wu et al. [71] devised a composite hydrogel with a rigid macromolecular network constraining a stretchable long-chain network, achieving controllable swelling and maintained mechanical strength in vitro and in a canine model. However, while promising, the long-term clinical applicability of this system remains unverified, particularly in the context of infected sockets or in patients with compromised healing capacities. Beyond anti-swelling, strong mucosal adhesion is paramount to withstand oral forces. Li et al. [118] engineered a reversible adhesive hydrogel leveraging cationic, hydrophobic, and hydrogen-bonding interactions for high adhesion strength (18.67 kPa) and rapid sealing (<10 s). Its thermoresponsive removal at 40 °C, however, presents a potential clinical drawback, as oral temperature fluctuations could prematurely destabilize the adhesive interface, and its biodegradation profile in vivo is not comprehensively evaluated. Injectable hydrogels offer a distinct advantage by enabling minimally invasive delivery and perfect adaptation to complex socket morphologies. He et al. [22] developed a Tetra-PEG hydrogel that provided effective sealing, enhanced hemostasis, and promoted bone regeneration in an anticoagulant rat model, outperforming traditional gelatin sponges (Fig. 4A). A pivotal advancement involves endowing hydrogels with potent antimicrobial properties to combat oral biofilms and prevent infection. Guo et al. [120] mimicked periodontal tissue using a dual-crosslinked hydrogel of quaternized chitosan (QMC) and dopamine-grafted alginate (OSA-DA), combining antimicrobial quaternary ammonium groups with mussel-inspired wet adhesion. While the design is biomimetic and innovative, the complex synthesis could hinder reproducibility and scale-up, and the antimicrobial efficacy was demonstrated against planktonic bacteria rather than mature, resilient biofilms more relevant to clinical infections. For a more aggressive approach, Chen et al. [121] constructed a photothermal cascade nanoreactor (CPNC@GOx-Fe2+) that generates hydroxyl radicals for synergistic photothermal/chemodynamic therapy, subsequently initiating hydrogel formation. This sophisticated system represents a significant leap in antimicrobial strategy, but the clinical practicality of applying precise photothermal stimulation intraorally is questionable, and the potential cytotoxicity of released metal ions and radicals demands meticulous long-term safety assessment. In addition to boosting hydrogel antibacterial activity via chemical functionalization, physically engineering spatially separated compartments is emerging as a promising alternative strategy like Janus membranes. Hu et al. [19] fabricated a PCL-CS Janus membrane (PCJM) with a dense, antibacterial hydrophobic PCL layer and a hydrophilic, hemostatic CS sponge layer (Fig. 4B). It demonstrated superior hemostasis and promoted healing in beagle models. The dual-layer structure is compelling for addressing multiple healing phases, but the long-term stability of the interface between the two layers under dynamic oral conditions is not addressed, and the cost-effectiveness of combining 3D printing with freeze-drying for manufacturing may be prohibitive.

Perhaps the most significant long-term challenge post-extraction is alveolar ridge resorption, which jeopardizes future implant placement [[122], [123], [124], [125], [126]]. Socket preservation aims to mitigate this, but current graft materials and spacers like gelatin sponges often suffer from poor osteogenicity and mechanical mismatch [127,128]. Shape-memory polymers offer an elegant solution for minimally invasive insertion and precise defect fitting. Zhang et al. [129] developed a porous PLGA-g-PCL-based scaffold coated with a PDA/Ag/HA antibacterial layer. It can be compressed for insertion and regains its shape and stiffness in situ. While the shape-memory feature is ingenious, the reliance on silver (Ag) for antimicrobial activity raises concerns about potential cytotoxicity and argyria with long-term exposure, and the osteogenic efficacy in large bone defects remains to be proven. Alternatively, Pan et al. [130] designed an injectable, self-healing polysaccharide hydrogel/hydroxyapatite (GH) composite that significantly enhanced new bone formation in rat mandibles (Fig. 4C). The simplicity of an injectable osteogenic paste is attractive, but its mechanical strength is likely insufficient to resist physiological masticatory forces in a clinical setting, potentially leading to material collapse and compromised space maintenance. Beyond tuning material physicochemistry to counter ridge resorption, harnessing endogenous bioelectric cues now offers a fresh direction for alveolar ridge preservation. Recognizing bone as an inherently electroactive tissue, Wang et al. developed a multifunctional membrane incorporating piezoelectric poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-trifluoroethylene), BaTiO3 nanoparticles, and conductive polypyrrole to simultaneously promote osteogenesis and angiogenesis [131]. This innovative device aligns with the natural healing timeline: its intrinsic surface potential first recruits endogenous stem cells, while subsequent cell-derived traction forces generate on-demand piezoelectric signals that enhance osteogenic differentiation via the Ca2+/CaN/NFAT pathway. By spatiotemporally coupling mechanical energy with bioelectric cues, this self-powered system represents a novel in situ strategy for alveolar ridge preservation.

Implant wounds

Dental implant restoration is currently the primary method for patients addressing tooth loss. However, insufficient remaining alveolar bone volume due to prolonged tooth loss, periodontitis, or trauma can hinder the desired outcomes of direct implantation [132,133]. GBR is one of the most commonly used techniques in alveolar ridge augmentation surgery. The principle behind GBR relies on the differing migration rates of various tissue cells; for instance, epithelial cells and fibroblasts migrate faster than osteoblasts. By placing a barrier membrane between soft tissue and the bone defect, GBR creates an environment that favors the growth of bone tissue. This membrane prevents the faster-migrating connective tissue cells and epithelial cells from entering the bone defect area, allowing slower-migrating precursor osteoblasts to occupy the defect site preferentially, thereby promoting bone regeneration while protecting the blood clot and alleviating tissue pressure [[134], [135], [136]].

GBR membranes are categorized as non-degradable (e.g., polytetrafluoroethylene, titanium mesh) or biodegradable (e.g., collagen-based). Non-degradable membranes provide good biocompatibility and strong space-maintaining ability but require a second surgery for removal [134,137]. Biodegradable membranes avoid removal surgery and integrate with host tissue, yet often exhibit poor mechanical strength and lack antibacterial properties, limiting their use in complex clinical conditions [138,139].

Therefore, an ideal GBR membrane should integrate precise physical barrier function with active biological regulation to synergistically address the core challenges in bone defect repair: selectively preventing soft tissue invasion while creating an optimal microenvironment for bone tissue regeneration. This next generation of intelligent barriers should encompass four key performance dimensions: (1) excellent mechanical stability and controllable degradation characteristics to ensure long-term space maintenance; (2) asymmetric (Janus) functionality, enabling efficient soft tissue barrier on one side and active osteogenic/angiogenic induction on the bone-facing side; (3) synergistic bioactive functions, including antibacterial and anti-inflammatory properties, microenvironmental homeostasis regulation (such as ROS scavenging), and promotion of soft tissue integration; (4) compatibility with the oral commensal microbiota to maintain host-microbe balance.

Research has progressed significantly to overcome these limitations, particularly focusing on enhancing the mechanical and biological properties of biodegradable membranes. Zhang et al. [140] augmented the bilayer structure of collagen Bio-Gide membranes by incorporating nanostructures (Fig. 5A). They utilized ice templating to integrate nanowires composed of chitosan (CS), graphene oxide (GO), and calcium silicate (CaSi). This approach preserved the membrane's inherent structure, a compact side to prevent cell interference and a porous side for cell adhesion, while significantly improving its mechanical properties, biodegradability, antibacterial activity, and osteogenic potential. Similarly, Wang's group fabricated a Mg-reinforced sandwich-membrane: an inner Mg scaffold that furnishes robust mechanical support and void maintenance before degrading, then steadily releases Mg2+ to drive osteogenesis, all sheathed above and below by a PLGA/collagen hybrid that slows magnesium corrosion and boosts surface biocompatibility [141]. Beyond synthetic polymers, ECM materials like small intestinal submucosa (SIS) have gained attention for their site-specific regenerative capabilities, low immunogenicity, and favorable mechanical properties. However, their inherent lack of antibacterial and potent osteogenic activity remains a drawback. To address this, Mu et al. [142] developed a novel modification strategy for SIS membranes using derived peptides from histone 5 (JH8194) and histone 1 (Hst1). JH8194 offers antibacterial and bone-forming properties, while Hst1 enhances the migration of fibroblasts and endothelial cells, promoting re-epithelialization and angiogenesis. This multifunctional modification presents a promising direction for enhancing GBR membranes.

A critical and frequently encountered clinical challenge in GBR is the compromise of the overlying soft tissue. When the barrier membrane becomes enveloped by soft tissue under tension, it can lead to wound dehiscence and membrane exposure. Reported incidence rates for these complications reach up to 22.7 %, which can result in bacterial colonization, impaired bone regeneration, and ultimate failure of the bone augmentation procedure. This highlights the dual challenge of achieving simultaneous gingival healing and alveolar bone regeneration in severe defects [[143], [144], [145], [146]]. The key to addressing this issue lies in utilizing barrier membranes with a dual-sided design to reconstruct the microenvironment conducive to both soft tissue repair and bone regeneration [147].

Janus membranes, which possess asymmetrical properties on each side, have emerged as a focused research area in GBR to meet these conflicting requirements [[148], [149], [150]]. Lai et al. [147] pioneered a barrier membrane featuring a Janus electric microenvironment (JEM) using polarized polyvinylidene fluoride ferroelectric films (Fig. 5B). The negatively polarized side (JEM-) promotes osteogenic differentiation, while the positively polarized side (JEM+) enhances soft tissue regeneration. This innovative approach provides a new method for selectively regulating tissue regeneration at interfaces. However, while Janus materials address the basic need for differential physical structures, they often fall short of eliminating adverse microenvironmental factors that hinder bone repair, such as accumulated ROS, bioelectric imbalances, and pathogenic bacteria. This limitation stems from a lack of integrated multiple bioactive functions, which can be particularly detrimental for patients with compromised healing capacities. The common strategy of simply combining functional components (e.g., antibiotics, Ag NPs) often struggles to effectively balance various bioactivities and can lead to unintended consequences like antibiotic resistance, material toxicity, or localized alkalization that negatively impacts osteogenesis [151,152].

A more advanced design philosophy involves drawing inspiration from nature to create structures that intrinsically possess multifaceted synergistic functions. He et al. [24] developed a superstructural porous membrane (SPM) inspired by leaves, utilizing a combination of breath figure and phase separation (BP) techniques. This SPM features asymmetric surface porosity, with a dense bacterial cellulose (BC) layer for mechanical robustness and soft tissue exclusion, and a loose porous surface for osteoblast adhesion and nutrient transport. Furthermore, the incorporation of porous carbon nanospheres (CNSs) as core functional components enables the membrane to improve the healing microenvironment by scavenging ROS, restoring bioelectric balance, and killing bacteria. This biomimetic strategy paves the way for next-generation barrier membranes with integrated bioactive functions.

Perhaps the most paradigm-shifting advancement in GBR membrane design is the growing recognition of the oral microbiome's role in regenerative outcomes. Moving beyond merely blocking cells, the frontier now involves symbiosis-promoting strategies. Microbial symbiosis homeostasis represents a dynamic equilibrium between host tissues and commensal microbiota in a healthy oral environment [153]. While traditional membranes focus on cellular competition (e.g., osteoblasts vs. fibroblasts), they largely overlook the dual role of oral microbiota: commensals support regeneration through immunomodulation and nutrient cycling, while pathobionts (e.g., Porphyromonas gingivalis) can trigger dysbiosis and inflammation in compromised conditions [154]. Therefore, successful symbiosis engineering requires a delicate balance: selectively suppressing pathogens while preserving or even leveraging beneficial commensals (e.g., Streptococcus salivarius). This necessitates a move beyond broad-spectrum biocides towards precision interventions.

In a groundbreaking approach, Woojin Choi et al. [155] developed a symbiotically tntegrating occlusive membrane (SIOM) via biomimetic enamel mineralization (Fig. 5C). The SIOM employs a zwitterion-mediated mineralized interface that mimics natural processes. Its design achieves pathogen-commensal balance through two mechanisms: (1) Pathogen suppression via localized pH elevation and calcium flux, disrupting acidogenic pathogens while sparing pH-neutral commensals; (2) Commensal support through nanotopography-guided adhesion of health-associated bacteria. Human saliva co-cultures and in vivo canine studies confirmed SIOM's efficacy: it significantly reduced periodontal pathobionts while enriching symbiotic genera, accelerated gingival wound closure, attenuated inflammation, and enhanced osteogenesis. This establishes SIOM as a paradigm for next-generation GBR therapies that concurrently sustain symbiotic microbiota, prevent inflammation, and promote bone regeneration.

Periodontal wounds

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease affecting the periodontal supporting tissues, including the alveolar bone, periodontal ligament, and cementum. This condition leads to progressive alveolar bone resorption, ultimately resulting in tooth mobility, displacement, or loss [156]. Traditional treatment methods for periodontitis primarily include scaling and root planning, aiming to remove dental calculus and subgingival biofilms to prevent and control the disease. For mild to moderate periodontitis, basic treatments may be supplemented with pharmacotherapy, such as systemic or local antibiotics, especially in areas difficult to access with instruments, like narrow and deep periodontal pockets or root furcation lesions. In cases of severe periodontitis, surgical interventions may be necessary, including flap surgery, membrane surgery, and gingivoplasty, to reduce the depth of periodontal pockets [2,156]. However, both basic and surgical treatments can cause damage to the gingival tissues and increase the risk of infection in the oral environment, negatively impacting healing outcomes. To overcome these limitations, next-generation periodontal therapies demand biomaterials that transcend passive barrier functions to actively orchestrate the healing microenvironment. An ideal periodontal wound healing material should function as a multifunctional and synergistic platform. Its core design must simultaneously achieve four integrated performance dimensions: (1) targeted antibacterial activity (precisely eliminating pathogens such as Porphyromonas gingivalis and disrupting biofilms); (2) intelligent inflammation modulation (efficiently scavenging excessive ROS and blocking pro-inflammatory factor cascades); (3) multi-tissue regenerative induction (coordinating the repair of alveolar bone, periodontal ligament, and soft tissue) and (4) clinical adaptability (strong wet-state adhesion to resist saliva flushing).

The research response to these challenges has been multifaceted, exploring strategies from targeted antimicrobials to immunomodulation. Timely removal of pathogens in the early stages is crucial. Fang et al. [157] developed a peroxide enzyme mimic constructed from nanodiamonds (O-NDs) that effectively controls infection with low-concentration H2O2, minimizing tissue damage (Fig. 6A). This approach aligns with the need for precise antimicrobial strategies that avoid broad-spectrum agents disrupting the commensal microbiome. Similarly, Zhang et al. [158] developed electroactive nanocomposite membranes (EMs) that exhibit broad-spectrum antibacterial activity through ROS generation within bacterial cells (Fig. 6B). Intriguingly, Gao's team fused four natural building blocks (xylitol, succinic acid, caffeic acid and citric acid) into a single hydrogel whose backbone inherently antagonizes the TLR4–MD2 complex [159]. Without loading any extra drugs, the material both damps inflammation and goads bacteria into over-producing antibiotics while simultaneously punching holes in their cell walls, curbing periodontopathogens and preserving sub-gingival microbial homeostasis. The recipe is green, cheap and clinic-ready. Beyond antimicrobials, regulating the host immune response represents a paradigm shift in periodontitis management. Substantial evidence indicates that macrophages play a central role in both the destructive and repair phases [160,161]. The polarization of macrophage phenotypes has been shown to be a prerequisite for successful periodontal regeneration [[162], [163], [164]]. In this context, He et al. [23] developed a molybdenum-containing bioactive glass-ceramic (Mo-BGC) scaffold that enhances mitochondrial function in macrophages, inducing a pro-regenerative M2 polarization and promoting multi-tissue regeneration in dogs (Fig. 6C). This immunomodulatory approach directly addresses the core inflammatory pathogenesis of periodontitis, offering a more holistic strategy than mere antibacterial action.

Post-surgical wound management remains a critical challenge. Periodontal surgery, while effective for debridement, causes significant trauma. The subsequent wound is exposed to a complex environment where bacterial accumulation, salivary flow, and food debris can impede healing. Traditional periodontal dressings are often rigid, uncomfortable, and require removal, potentially causing secondary injury. [165,166]. To address the poor biocompatibility and inadequate functionality of existing dressings, Feng et al. [72] synthesized a CS-gallate graft copolymer (CS-GA) hydrogel. It exhibits excellent ROS scavenging, antibacterial effects, hemostatic capability, and promotes wound healing. However, the clinical translation of such advanced hydrogels must overcome challenges related to ease of application, patient comfort, and cost-effectiveness.

The ultimate goal of periodontitis treatment is the regeneration of lost periodontal tissues [167]. Guided tissue regeneration (GTR) employs a barrier membrane to exclude epithelial cells and fibroblasts from the defect, allowing periodontal ligament cells and osteoblasts to repopulate the area. Despite being a cornerstone of regenerative dentistry, conventional GTR membranes (e.g., collagen, PTFE) have significant limitations: they lack bioactivity, offer no defense against infection, and often require removal. Consequently, infection due to membrane contamination and limited regeneration in complex defects remain common complications [138,168].

Electrospun membranes functionalized with bioactive agents represent a promising advancement. Liu et al. [169] incorporated magnesium oxide nanoparticles (nMgO) into a PLA/gelatin membrane, achieving antibacterial effects and neutralizing acidic degradation products to improve the pH microenvironment. Other studies have developed core-shell structured membranes for sustained drug release (e.g., ornidazole for antibacterial, bFGF for regeneration) [170] and coaxial fibers combining antibiotic release with a bone-guiding core [171]. While these designs offer superior functionality, their mechanical properties, degradation kinetics, and ability to achieve simultaneous regeneration of bone, ligament, and cementum, the true hallmark of periodontal regeneration, often fall short of the ideal. Addressing these specific hurdles is paramount for advancing periodontal wound treatment.

Other intraoral wounds

In addition to the previously mentioned causes of intraoral wounds, such as ulcers, tooth extractions, periodontal procedures, and implants, there are also oral mucosal defects resulting from trauma or other surgeries, including acute wounds and cleft palate surgeries. These defects typically involve full-thickness loss of the oral mucosa, including the loss of epithelial cells and damage to the underlying connective tissue [172]. Consequently, several issues arise: severe bleeding due to injury to the vascular system within the connective tissue, and a high risk of infection, leading to prolonged healing times [[173], [174], [175]]. Currently, autologous tissue grafting and acellular dermal matrix repair are standard therapies for full-thickness oral mucosal defects. However, these methods lack hemostatic, antibacterial, and anti-inflammatory properties, necessitating additional measures such as gauze compression and antibiotic use to control bleeding and prevent infection. This approach can slow the healing process and increase the risk of systemic drug exposure, potentially leading to the development of drug resistance [176].

To transcend current limitations, the field is pivoting toward integrated, multi-functional biomaterial systems that actively orchestrate the healing cascade, an approach that is rapidly revealing unique design philosophies and therapeutic edges in the management of recalcitrant full-thickness wounds. Rather than treating hemostasis and inflammation as isolated steps, these next-generation platforms weave the two into a single, self-coordinating network tailored to the multi-layered pathology of full-depth injury. Zhu et al., for instance, engineered a low-swelling, ultra-adhesive hydrogel through a dual-cross-linking strategy that marries gelatin methacrylate (GelMA), nanoclay and tannic acid [12]. Conceived as a direct answer to the clinical triad of profuse bleeding, high infection risk and runaway inflammation, the formulation instantly films and tamponades the wound bed while its rationally tuned chemistry simultaneously damps TNF and IL-17 signaling. In a full-thickness oral-defect model this single patch delivered rapid hemostasis, sustained antibacterial action and multi-target anti-inflammation, compressing the healing trajectory from epidermis to connective tissue into a dramatically shortened timeline.

On the other hand, Zhou et al. revisited the largely overlooked bioelectric blackout that accompanies full-thickness injury [177]. Their conductive PAA-SA hydrogel threaded with reduced graphene oxide (rGO) re-establishes a 3-D electrical conduit across the lesion, effectively turning a short-circuited wound bed back into an intact bioelectrical loop. The restored micro-currents act as an endogenous cue that galvanizes fibroblast recruitment and differentiation, amplifies growthfactor secretion and boosts anti-oxidative resilience, culminating in full-thickness mucosal regeneration in rats within seven days. Yet for all their elegance, these integrated systems also invite critical reflection. Long-term biocompatibility of electroactive nanocomponents, the precision required to tune multiple signaling axes without off-target crosstalk, and the scalability of multi-step fabrication remain open questions that must be rigorously addressed before “active coordination” can leap from bench to bedside. The ongoing shift, from merely replacing lost tissue to proactively guiding regeneration, signals not just technological maturation but also a deeper epistemic transition: future biomaterials must be conceived less as inert scaffolds and more as active regulators that continuously interact with cells and tissues, and we still have to determine how their structure and surface chemistry should synchronize with biological timing and mechanical demands.

As the most prevalent congenital cranio-maxillofacial anomaly, occurring in approximately 1 in 700 live births, cleft palate represents a singularly intricate challenge within craniofacial-tissue-regeneration medicine [[178], [179], [180]]. The current surgical mainstream, exemplified by the double-opposing Z-plasty, is designed to close the oronasal communication and re-establish anatomical separation [181]. However, chronic insufficiency of autologous soft tissue frequently imposes excessive tension on the suture line, precipitating a cascade of complications including fistulation, wound contracture, hypertrophic scarring, and subsequent maxillary growth restriction [182,183]. Autologous skin grafts introduced to offset soft-tissue deficits may, in turn, introduce fungal super-infection, ectopic hair growth, and graft contraction, thereby merely substituting one pathological spectrum for another [[184], [185], [186], [187]]. Against this backdrop, Li and co-workers recently advanced an off-the-shelf, fully resorbable composite construct (DAM-POC) that integrates a decellularised amnion matrix (DAM) with a biodegradable poly(octamethylene citrate) elastomer (POC) [188]. The DAM preserves the ultrastructural integrity and biological barrier function of native extracellular matrix, whereas the POC phase provides a tuneable mechanical backbone that resists the repetitive shear and tensile loads inherent to the infant palate. In a validated rodent cleft model, the scaffold promoted near-complete hard-tissue and soft-tissue restitution within eight weeks, offering a minimally invasive therapeutic alternative for paediatric patients. Nevertheless, a critical appraisal reveals that the translational trajectory of such bioengineered grafts remains fraught with unresolved bottlenecks. Paramount among these is the imperative to synchronize material degradation kinetics with the rapidly evolving dimensions of the growing infant palate, while simultaneously ensuring long-term biomechanical and biochemical concordance between graft and host. Additional translational checkpoints include stringent immunogenicity control, scalable and standardised manufacturing pipelines, and intraoperative manoeuvrability commensurate with the confined surgical field of the neonatal oral cavity. Rigorous question of these parameters will ultimately dictate whether DAM-POC evolves from an elegant pre-clinical prototype to a clinically transformative intervention.

. Conclusion and outlook

Intraoral wounds, due to their structural, locational, and environmental characteristics, present unique challenges requiring meticulous care and management for fast, complete, and painless recovery. In recent years, significant advances have been made in the development of biomaterials for promoting intraoral wound healing. As clinical demands keep deepening and refining, the functions of oral biomaterials have expanded from simple physical coverage to multifunctional composites, with increasing emphasis on intelligent design. Given the characteristics of intraoral wounds and the developmental trends of oral biomaterials, this review focuses on summarizing the multiple idea functions (such as hemostasis, anti-inflammation and antioxidation, antibacterial, tissue regeneration, and adhesion, etc.) of biomaterials suitable for intraoral wounds in existing research, and also summarizes the strategies for realizing these functions in specific intraoral wounds. Despite their potential, biomaterials for intraoral wound healing still face several key challenges that need to be addressed.

1.Precise Regulation of the Oral Microbiome The oral microbiome plays a central role in wound healing, yet exhibits a striking duality: commensal bacteria such as Streptococcus salivarius contribute to healing by secreting anti-inflammatory metabolites, while pathogenic bacteria like Porphyromonas gingivalis hinder the repair process through biofilm formation and release of pro-inflammatory signals [194]. This characteristic has placed current biomaterials relying on broad-spectrum antimicrobials in a predicament, while eliminating pathogenic bacteria, they also disrupt the homeostasis of commensal bacteria and may even induce drug resistance. Therefore, the regulation of the microbiome should not stop at “indiscriminate inhibition” but needs to move toward more refined “precision regulation,” ultimately achieving the active shaping of a microbiological environment conducive to healing.

Recent studies have revealed the possibility of such a transformation. For instance, aptamer-functionalized hydrogels can specifically recognize pathogenic biomarkers and then release bacteriocins to precisely target the pathogens [195]. Modified materials supplemented with prebiotics provide growth support for commensal bacteria, demonstrating the potential to cultivate beneficial microbial communities [196,197]. However, these explorations still have obvious limitations: the dynamic interactions between biomaterials, the microbiome, and host immune cells have not been fully elucidated, and existing experimental models struggle to simulate the temporal changes in microbial composition during healing, such as the natural process from the dominance of pathogenic bacteria in the early inflammatory phase to the re-establishment of commensal balance in the proliferation phase.

Future research needs to achieve breakthroughs on the basis of deepening cognition. First, further exploration should be conducted on the integration methods of probiotics/prebiotics, targeted antibacterial strategies (eliminating pathogenic bacteria while preserving commensals), and the design of microbiome-friendly biofilms, so as to transform biomaterials from passively adapting to the environment to actively guiding microbial balance, opening up new directions for wound management. Meanwhile, with the help of multi-omics technologies such as metagenomics and transcriptomics, map the interaction profiles of the microbiome, host, and materials at different healing stages, and screen out microbial characteristics that can predict healing outcomes to provide a more accurate basis for material design. At the technical level, the adhesion of commensal bacteria such as streptococci can be selectively promoted by regulating the surface topography and chemical properties of materials. For example, by mimicking the structure of their natural adhesins, while inhibiting the colonization of pathogenic bacteria. This is a key path for actively shaping the microbial environment. In addition, it is necessary to clarify the relationship between microbial metabolites and immune cytokines. On this basis, develop “microbiome-immune” dual-responsive materials, enabling the materials to dynamically regulate microbial balance and host responses according to environmental signals.

2. Personalized Therapy The outcomes of intraoral wound healing are significantly influenced by individual variations, including systemic conditions (such as diabetes), lifestyle factors (such as smoking), and wound-specific characteristics (such as location and etiology). Currently, the “one-size-fits-all” biomaterials struggle to address such heterogeneity. For instance, the efficacy of standard hydrogels is notably reduced in diabetic patients with impaired angiogenesis.

The focus of future work lies in driving the development of truly personalized biomaterials and treatment protocols. This requires going beyond the focus on physical form. Although flowable hydrogels have solved the problem of morphological adaptation, the core of personalization lies in the customization of biological functions. From the perspective of wound types, oral ulcers require materials with super-strong wet adhesion, the ability to continuously release anti-inflammatory and analgesic drugs, and protective barrier functions. For extraction sockets, emphasis is placed on osteoconductive and osteoinductive scaffolds, whose controlled absorption rate needs to match bone formation kinetics, and they can be loaded with precise antimicrobial agents according to risks to prevent dry socket. Periodontal defects require materials that can effectively resist biofilms and synergistically guide the regeneration of multiple tissues (cementum, periodontal ligament, and bone).

From the perspective of patient specificity, the composition, drug load, degradation rate, and mechanical properties of materials should be customized based on wound etiology (trauma or disease), location characteristics (such as highly mobile tongue or stable gingival area), size and depth, patient comorbidities (such as diabetes), and the core variable—individualized oral microbiome profiles. Enabling technologies play an important role. Artificial intelligence and big data analysis can predict healing conditions and optimize material parameters by integrating patient medical history, 3D wound imaging, microbiome data, etc. In-situ forming technology supports the on-site deployment of polymer concentration, cross-linking degree, and bioactive load at the point of care, thereby achieving personalized adaptation in clinical scenarios.

3. Environmental Adaptability The dynamic nature of the oral cavity, characterized by pH fluctuations, mechanical shear forces from mastication, and enzymatic activity, poses unique challenges to the retention and functional exertion of biomaterials. Current materials often suffer from issues such as poor wet adhesion (e.g., rapid detachment from gingival tissues) or unstable drug release (e.g., burst release caused by pH changes) [198].

Recent innovations include bioinspired adhesives (such as dopamine-modified hydrogels mimicking mussel adhesion) and stimuli-responsive polymers (such as pH-sensitive polyacrylic acid derivatives) [120,199]. MN patches also show promise in enhancing drug penetration into periodontal pockets while resisting mechanical displacement [88]. However, challenges remain in balancing adhesiveness with biocompatibility (e.g., avoiding mucosal irritation caused by strong adhesives) and achieving multi-stimuli responsiveness (e.g., responding simultaneously to pH and shear forces).

Future directions should focus on designing materials with “adaptive mechanical properties”, where stiffness and adhesive strength can be dynamically adjusted in response to mechanical stress,for example, through shear-thinning hydrogels that soften during mastication to reduce tissue damage and then re-solidify to maintain their position. Additionally, integrating multi-responsive crosslinking (such as dual pH-enzyme sensitive linkers) is crucial to ensure controlled drug release across the full range of fluctuations in the oral microenvironment.
