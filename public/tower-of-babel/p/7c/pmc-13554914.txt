Atrioventricular Block Induced by Right Coronary Artery Spasm in Vasospastic Angina
Authors: Antonin Trimaille, Loic Faucher, Baudouin Koenig, Alexandre Schatz, Laurence Jesel, Halim Marzak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVasospastic angina (VSA) is a reversible cause of myocardial ischemia due to transient coronary spasm and may rarely be associated with conduction disturbances.Case SummaryA 64-year-old man presented with nocturnal rest angina. The initial angiography results were normal. Holter monitoring during symptoms showed transient ST-segment elevation with Mobitz type I second-degree atrioventricular (AV) block progressing to 2:1 AV block, causing presyncope. He later experienced syncope. Repeat angiography revealed diffuse coronary vasospasm involving the right coronary artery, fully reversible with nitrates, confirming VSA. Verapamil therapy resulted in symptom resolution and no further conduction abnormalities during follow-up.DiscussionRight coronary artery spasm may induce transient AV block due to ischemia of the AV node.Take-Home MessagesVSA should be considered in unexplained AV block or syncope with ischemic electrocardiographic changes. Calcium-channel blockers are effective even in ischemia-induced conduction disorders.

History of Presentation

A 64-year-old man was referred to our institution on December 20, 2020, for evaluation of new-onset angina occurring at rest, predominantly at night. Physical examination was unremarkable. Past Medical History

His cardiovascular risk factors included a history of smoking (former smoker) and a family history of coronary artery disease (CAD). He had no significant comorbidities and was not taking any medications.

Differential Diagnosis

CAD, vasospastic angina (VSA), intrinsic atrioventricular (AV) conduction disease, arrhythmic syncope, and reflex syncope were considered.

Investigations

Given the high clinical probability of CAD, invasive coronary angiography was performed on December 21, 2020, via radial access, with intra-arterial administration of vasodilators (1 mg of isosorbide dinitrate and 1 mg of nicardipine) to prevent radial artery spasm. The left coronary artery showed no significant stenosis (Figure 1A), while the proximal right coronary artery exhibited a nonsignificant stenosis (Figure 1B). Before the Holter results could be reviewed, the patient presented to the emergency department after a syncopal episode on January 27, 2021. Clinical examination, ECG, and laboratory findings were unchanged. Repeat coronary angiography was performed on February 1, 2021, without prior vasodilator administration and revealed spontaneous diffuse vasospasm of both the left and right coronary arteries, with near-occlusion of the proximal right coronary artery (Figures 3A and 3B). The vasospasm resolved promptly after intracoronary nitrate administration (Figures 3C and 3D). During this examination, he was asymptomatic, with no chest pain or presyncope. The baseline ECG remained unchanged, showing no ST-segment deviations or AV conduction disturbances. Management

Treatment with long-acting nitrates (nicorandil 10 mg twice daily) and a calcium-channel blocker (amlodipine 10 mg daily) was initiated. However, the patient developed headaches and nausea, leading to discontinuation of nitrates. Exercise stress testing revealed frequent premature ventricular complexes (PVCs). Amlodipine was therefore replaced with verapamil (120 mg daily) to address both PVCs and coronary vasospasm.

An insertable cardiac monitor was implanted on February 2, 2021, to investigate potential alternative causes of syncope.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Since implantation of the device, the patient reported only occasional mild angina episodes, occurring approximately once per month and consistently relieved by a single dose of Natispray 0.15 mg/dose (Teofarma S.r.l.), with no recurrence of presyncope or syncope while on verapamil (120 mg daily).

Accordingly, the verapamil dose was increased to 240 mg once daily in a prolonged-release formulation in September 2021, with the last angina episode reported in January 2022. Serial interrogations of the cardiac monitor during follow-up revealed no significant conduction abnormalities. The device was explanted on October 6, 2023, because of battery depletion.

Discussion

VSA, first described by Prinzmetal et al,2 is caused by transient epicardial coronary artery spasm leading to myocardial ischemia, typically associated with transient ST-segment elevation. Although chest pain is the most common manifestation, VSA may also be complicated by arrhythmias and conduction disturbances.3 We report a case of transient AV block associated with right coronary artery vasospasm.

VSA is characterized by transient coronary artery spasm leading to myocardial ischemia. The underlying mechanisms are multifactorial and include endothelial dysfunction, hyper-reactivity of vascular smooth muscle cells, inflammation, oxidative stress, autonomic imbalance, and exposure to risk factors such as smoking.3

The current diagnostic criteria for VSA include nitrate-responsive angina, transient ischemic ECG changes, and the documentation of coronary artery spasm, either spontaneously or during provocative testing.1 Our patient fulfilled all these criteria, with typical rest angina, transient ST-segment elevation on Holter monitoring, and angiographically documented coronary vasospasm reversible with nitrates.

Although VSA is most commonly associated with chest pain, it may also result in severe complications, including myocardial infarction, ventricular arrhythmias, and sudden cardiac death.1,4 In a cohort study including more than 300 patients with VSA, the incidences of ventricular arrhythmias and high-degree AV block were similar, both reaching approximately 14% during 3-year follow-up.5 These findings highlight that conduction disturbances may represent an under-recognized but clinically significant manifestation of VSA.

Several case reports have also described the association between coronary vasospasm and severe bradyarrhythmias. For instance, Unverdorben et al6 reported a patient presenting with myocardial infarction without obstructive CAD, followed weeks later by recurrent syncope episodes. Holter monitoring demonstrated transient ST-segment elevation associated with AV block progressing to asystole. In our case, although no syncope was documented during Holter monitoring, the patient experienced presyncope at the time of Mobitz type I second-degree AV block, followed by a paroxysmal 2:1 AV block. The subsequent syncopal episode may have corresponded to a higher-degree AV block or even transient asystole, which was not captured.

AV block during myocardial ischemia has been well described, particularly in the setting of inferior myocardial infarction.7 This association is largely explained by the vascular supply of the AV node, which is provided by the AV nodal artery, most commonly arising from the right coronary artery.8 Therefore, ischemia related to right coronary artery spasm may directly impair AV nodal conduction.

Importantly, AV block occurring during ischemia is typically transient. This relative reversibility is attributed to the intrinsic resistance of the conduction system to ischemic injury, which is supported by a rich collateral blood supply and a high intracellular glycogen content.7 Nevertheless, even transient ischemia may induce functional impairment of conduction tissue. Although the exact mechanisms remain incompletely understood, several hypotheses have been proposed, including increased parasympathetic activity and local metabolic changes such as potassium release during ischemia.7

In addition, conduction disturbances have been reported in the context of reperfusion, particularly after thrombolytic therapy. In such cases, enhanced vagal tone has been suggested as a contributing mechanism.9 However, in our patient, the temporal relationship between ST-segment elevation and AV block strongly supports a direct ischemic mechanism rather than a reperfusion-related or vagally mediated phenomenon.

The management of VSA is primarily based on calcium-channel blockers and long-acting nitrates, which are recommended as first-line therapy.10 In the present case, nitrate intolerance due to adverse effects necessitated treatment adjustment. A nondihydropyridine calcium-channel blocker (verapamil) was selected, given its efficacy in both suppressing PVCs and preventing coronary vasospasm.

However, the use of nondihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers may raise concerns in patients with conduction disturbances because of their negative chronotropic and dromotropic effects. By inhibiting L-type calcium channels, these agents reduce myocardial contractility and heart rate while promoting coronary vasodilation. In patients with underlying conduction system disease, this pharmacological profile may theoretically exacerbate bradyarrhythmias.

In our patient, the absence of recurrent conduction abnormalities during follow-up supports the hypothesis that AV block was secondary to transient ischemia rather than intrinsic conduction system disease. This observation suggests that, with careful monitoring, nondihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers may be safely used in selected patients with VSA-related conduction disturbances.

Overall, this case underscores the importance of considering VSA in the differential diagnosis of unexplained AV block or syncope, particularly when associated with transient ischemic ECG changes.

Conclusions

This case highlights an uncommon presentation of VSA complicated by transient AV block and syncope. VSA should be considered in patients presenting with unexplained conduction disturbances or syncope, particularly when associated with transient ischemic ECG changes.
