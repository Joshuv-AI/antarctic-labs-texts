Development and characterization of a topical ferrochelatase inhibitor nanoemulsion for choroidal neovascularization therapy
Authors: Debadatta Mohapatra, Seung-Yong Seo, Timothy W. Corson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Choroidal neovascularization (CNV) is a hallmark of neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD). We previously identified the heme synthesis enzyme ferrochelatase (FECH) as a promising therapeutic target. This study aimed to develop and characterize topical ophthalmic nanoemulsions (NEs) of a novel FECH inhibitor, SH-17023, for CNV therapy, to avoid the intravitreal injections needed for standard-of-care anti–vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) biologics. SH-17023-loaded NEs were prepared by spontaneous emulsification and optimized using D-optimal mixture design-based Quality-by-Design to obtain nanometric globule size (Zavg), low polydispersity index (PDI), and highest drug loading capacity (% LC). Therapeutic efficacy was assessed in the laser-induced CNV (L-CNV) mouse model with fundus imaging, optical coherence tomography (OCT), fluorescein angiography, and ex vivo vasculature staining. The optimized formulation was transparent with a globule size of 32.832 ± 2.125 nm, PDI 0.201 ± 0.003, spherical morphology, and LC of 7.436 ± 0.035%. It showed zeta potential of −29.2 ± 0.45 mV, sustained drug release, and robust accelerated and kinetic stability. Attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction revealed excellent drug–excipient compatibility. Ex vivo goat cornea permeation of NEs showed significantly higher drug transport (permeability coefficient (Kp) = 0.050 ± 0.012 h−1.cm−2, steady-state flux (Jss) = 37.51 ± 9.29 μg.h−1.cm−2) than the drug in solution (Kp = 0.002 ± 0.0004 h−1.cm−2 and Jss = 1.575 ± 0.488 μg.h−1.cm−2) with an enhancement ratio of 23.815. Optimized NEs significantly reduced L-CNV assessed by OCT and ex vivo staining by >45% compared to blank NEs, without ocular irritation or toxicity, indicating their promise for nAMD therapy.

Introduction

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a complex degenerative disease causing irreversible central vision loss, mainly in persons 60 years or above (Wong et al., 2014). Worldwide, about 200 million people suffer from some form of AMD with an anticipated incidence of 288 million by 2040 (Fleckenstein et al., 2024; Wong et al., 2014). AMD mainly affects the macula of the eye, causing progressive damage to visual ability. There are two major types of AMD: dry AMD (degenerative) and wet AMD (exudative AMD or neovascular AMD/nAMD). nAMD is less common (10% of cases) compared to dry AMD (90%), but is responsible for 90% of blindness caused by AMD (Holekamp, 2019). The nAMD involves macular neovascularization (MNV), with new blood vessels developing from the choroid (choroidal neovascularization, CNV) or the deep retinal vasculature to the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) or through the RPE to the subretinal region. The development and progression of this abnormal vasculature results in hemorrhage, exudations, scarring, and RPE detachment, leading to rapid vision loss (Hobbs et al., 2024).

Intravitreal injections of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) drugs, such as faricimab, bevacizumab, brolucizumab, ranibizumab, conbercept, and aflibercept, are the current standard of care for the treatment of nAMD (Fei et al., 2024; Muniyandi et al., 2025; Sarkar and Dyawanapelly, 2021). These anti-VEGF therapies have a high cost, show limited efficacy in many patients, can lead to tachyphylaxis, have a risk of ocular and systemic side effects, and require frequent intravitreal injections with significant patient and health system burden and access challenges (Fei et al., 2024; Muniyandi et al., 2025). Our survey study revealed that 80% of patients with ocular neovascular disease would prefer topical or oral therapies if available (Jacobs et al., 2021). Hence, identification of alternative molecular targets and therapeutics with suitable delivery systems is needed for effective treatment of nAMD.

We previously identified ferrochelatase (FECH), a key enzyme in heme biosynthesis, as a promising alternative antiangiogenic target (Babu et al., 2020; Basavarajappa et al., 2017; Sishtla et al., 2022). FECH is a mediator of neovascularization in the eyes and is upregulated in murine and human eyes undergoing CNV (Basavarajappa et al., 2017). FECH catalyzes the insertion of ferrous ion into protoporphyrin IX (PPIX) in the final step of heme biosynthesis (Hamza and Dailey, 2012). The protoheme serves as a cofactor for proteins important for angiogenesis, including mitochondrial complex IV and endothelial nitric oxide synthase, thereby promoting angiogenesis (Basavarajappa et al., 2017; Shetty et al., 2020). Mutation or knockdown of FECH blocks angiogenesis in vitro and in vivo (Basavarajappa et al., 2017). N-methylprotoporphyrin (NMPP) is a potent inhibitor of FECH (Dailey and Fleming, 1983) and the antifungal drug griseofulvin produces NMPP in vivo as an off-target effect via alkylation of the heme group of cytochrome P450 (Liu et al., 2015), hence both have been used in preclinical studies for the inhibition of FECH. Both NMPP and griseofulvin selectively block the growth of endothelial cells without causing death in vitro. The intravitreal and oral administration of griseofulvin reduces CNV (Basavarajappa et al., 2017; Chobisa et al., 2024). However, NMPP does not have properties required for drug development due to its bulkiness and poor solubility. Further, the unusual mechanism of griseofulvin hinders the development of novel griseofulvin analogues for FECH inhibition (Sishtla et al., 2022). We previously conducted a high-throughput screen followed by structure-activity relationship studies to identify small-molecule inhibitors of FECH. The study identified triazolopyrimidinone derivatives having strong activity for FECH inhibition, notably a potent compound SH-17023 (Sishtla et al., 2022). SH-17023 inhibits FECH and causes precursor (PPIX) accumulation in human retinal endothelial cells (HRECs) (Sishtla et al., 2022). It demonstrated in vitro anti-angiogenic activity, inhibiting HREC proliferation, tube formation, and migration and showed in vivo therapeutic activity against ocular neovascularization in the laser-induced choroidal neovascularization (L-CNV) model when delivered intravitreally as a proof of concept (Sishtla et al., 2022). Given the shortcomings of this delivery route, topical administration is appealing. However, the topical administration of SH-17023 is greatly limited due to its poor aqueous solubility (log P = 5.28). Hence, a suitable formulation is needed for effective delivery to treat CNV.

Topical administration offers pain-free administration, improved patient adherence, low cost, site-specific drug delivery, and ultimately reduces side effects (Mehrandish and Mirzaeei, 2021; Yang et al., 2022). However, topical drug delivery through traditional dosage forms (eye drop, drug solution, ointments, gels, and suspensions) faces several challenges, such as limited drug permeation across complex ocular barriers (pre-corneal, corneal, and conjunctival barriers) and physiological events (induced lacrimation, tear turnover, conjunctival absorption, drug metabolism, drainage, efflux etc.) leading to poor ocular bioavailability (Henostroza et al., 2020; Mohapatra et al., 2025). Further, they offer pulsed delivery (an initial high concentration during instillation followed by rapid decline due to drainage), failing to provide sustained drug release or targeted delivery, leading to less than ideal therapeutic outcomes (Dhaval et al., 2020; Mohapatra et al., 2025).

Advanced therapeutic platforms use novel drug-delivery systems (NDDS) for ocular disease therapy. Among various NDDS, nanoemulsions (NEs) are identified as a promising formulation for effective ocular permeation (Mohapatra et al., 2025). NEs, also called miniemulsions, are isotropic colloidal dispersions having two immiscible phases (oil and water) emulsified by surfactants/co-surfactants or co-solvents having a globule size of 20–500 nm (Fernandes et al., 2021; Singh et al., 2020; Smail et al., 2021). Due to their nanometric globule size, they appear as a transparent or translucent dispersion and offer long-term stability (Smail et al., 2021). Based on the dispersion/external medium and dispersion/internal phase, they are classified into w/o, o/w, and bi-continuous types (Choradiya and Patil, 2021; Singh et al., 2020). Among them, the o/w type can be used for ocular drug delivery due to the aqueous external phase, which can interact with aqueous tear fluid, and the internal oil phase solubilizes and loads hydrophobic drugs, thereby allowing prolonged ocular retention and improved ocular permeation (Fernandes et al., 2021; Singh et al., 2020). The oily excipients of NEs interact with the lipid layer of the tear film, offering prolonged ocular retention and serving as drug depots (Singh et al., 2020). NEs enhance the aqueous solubility of hydrophobic drugs, reduce contact angles, provide improved contact with ocular tissue, prolong ocular retention, reduce dosing frequency, offer sustained/controlled drug release, improve ocular permeation, and enhance therapeutic outcomes in pre-clinical and clinical contexts (Attia et al., 2024; Choradiya and Patil, 2021; Fernandes et al., 2021; Kishore et al., 2024; Singh et al., 2020).

In this work, ophthalmic NEs of the novel FECH inhibitor, SH-17023, were developed for CNV therapy. The NEs were formulated by spontaneous emulsification and optimized using a D-optimal mixture design-based statistical Quality by Design (QbD) approach to obtain a nanometric globule size, low polydispersity index, and the highest drug loading capacity. Further, the optimized NEs were characterized for physicochemical and pharmaceutical properties as well as ex vivo ocular permeability, and in vivo irritancy. Therapeutic efficacy was shown in the L-CNV murine model.

Liquid chromatography-Mass spectrometry (LC-MS) and validation

The LC-MS analysis was performed on an Agilent LC system (Agilent 1260 Infinity, Agilent, USA) furnished with an autosampler (1260 HIP ALS), thermostatically controlled column chamber (1260 TCC), binary pump (1260 Bin pump), degasser (1260 HiP degasser), reverse phase column (Poroshell 120, EC-C18, 2.1 × 50 mm, 2.7 μm particle size), with triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific™ TSQ Endura™), and Thermo Scientific Xcalibur software (4.4). The column was kept in a thermostatically controlled chamber with a temperature of 40 °C. The gradient elution was carried out with Optima™ LC/MS Grade water (Fisher Scientific) containing water as mobile phase A (A) and Optima™ LC-MS grade acetonitrile (Fisher Scientific) as mobile phase B (B) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. For the gradient elution, the mobile phase was 90% A and 10% B (0.00–1.30 min), followed by a linear gradient of 80% A and 20% B (1.30–2.0 min), and switch to 10% A and 90% B (2–6 min), then returned to 80% A and 20% B (7.0–7.5 min), followed by the equilibrium gradient of 90% A and 10% B (7.5–10.0 min) (Supplementary file Section 1.1, Table S1, and Fig. S1. A triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific™ TSQ Endura™) equipped with Heated-electrospray ionization (H-ESI) was used for the MS analysis. Then, the data were acquired in positive ionization mode with selected reaction monitoring (SRM) mode at the precursor-to-product ion transitions (Q1/Q3) for SH-17023 and midazolam (as internal standard) at m/z of 425.2/281.1 and 326.1/290.5, respectively.

Working standards (1–1000 ng/mL) of SH-17023 containing 2 ng/mL of midazolam (as internal standard) were prepared in LC-MS grade methanol. The sample injector speed was maintained at a drawing and ejecting speed of 200 μL/min. Five microliters of each working standard were injected into the LC-MS system using an autosampler, and the data were analyzed using Thermo Xcalibur Qual Browser software. The absence of any carryover effect was ensured by injecting blank samples (LC-MS grade methanol, Optima™, Fisher Scientific) intermittently after every two injections. The LC-MS analysis was carried out in triplicate, and the calibration curve was made using the area of the analyte normalized to the area of the internal standard vs. the concentrations (ng/mL). The optimized LC-MS method was validated as per International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) guidelines for linearity and range, accuracy, precision, limit of detection (LOD), limit of quantification (LOQ), and robustness. The detailed LC-MS methodology, including estimation of mass of precursor to product ion, preparation of calibration standards, and validation as per ICH guidelines are presented in the supplementary file (Section 1.1.1). The validated LC-MS method was used for the solubility study, estimation of drug content, loading capacity, stability, release study and ocular permeation studies.

Screening of oil, surfactant, co-surfactant, and co-solvent by solubility study

The solubility of SH-17023 in various excipients (oils, co-surfactants, surfactants, and co-solvents) was evaluated by saturation solubility study with the use of a validated LC-MS method. Various oils (almond oil, anise oil, avocado oil, bergamot oil, castor oil, cinnamon oil, citronella oil, clove oil, corn oil, emu oil, eucalyptus oil, green tea oil, jojoba oil, lavender oil, lemon oil, orange oil, peppermint oil, pine oil, rose oil, safflower oil, sesame oil, sunflower oil), surfactants & co-surfactants (Caproyl®90, Caproyl® PGMC, Labrafac™ MC60, Labrafil® M 1944 CS, Labrasol®, Span® 80, Tween® 40, Tween® 20, Tween® 80), and co-solvents (glycerol, propylene glycol, PEG 400, PEG 300, Transcutol® P) were used for solubility determination. Briefly, an excess amount (5 mg) of SH-17023 was added to 50 μL of various oils, surfactants, co-surfactants, and co-solvents, vortexed (BV 1000 vortex mixer, Benchmark Scientific Inc., USA), bath sonicated for 10 min (Ultrasonic bath, Fisher Scientific, USA), and placed in a thermal mixer (Thermo Scientific, USA) at 37 ± 1 °C with 250 rpm for 24 h. Then the samples were centrifuged (Eppendorf 5417R, USA) at 10,621 ×g for 10 min, and 10 μL of the supernatant was collected. The collected supernatants were extracted and suitably diluted with LC-MS grade methanol, centrifuged at 10,621 ×g, filtered via a 0.22 μm syringe filter (Milex®-GV, Sigma Aldrich, USA), and kept in screw capped amber coloured vials for the drug quantification by the validated LC-MS method.

Construction of pseudo-ternary phase diagrams

The pseudo-ternary phase diagrams were constructed with the selected oils, surfactants, co-surfactants, and cosolvents having maximum drug solubility using the water titration method (Cardoso et al., 2022). Briefly, various ratios of surfactants or surfactant with cosurfactants (1:1) or surfactant with cosurfactants & co-solvents (1:1:1, Smix) were mixed with selected oil phase at 1:9, 2:8, 3:7, 4:6, 5:5, 6:4, 7:3, 8:2, and 9:1 (v/v) into transparent glass vials (Attia et al., 2024). Millipore water was mixed dropwise, vortexed until the formation of a homogeneous phase, and visualized for optical clarity after each water addition. The volume of water required to change the clarity/attainment of turbidity was considered the point of water titration, and the samples were kept tightly sealed with Parafilm at 37 °C for 48 h to observe clarity changes. Then the percentage of each phase (%v/v) was calculated at the point of critical change in clarity/turbidity/phase separation. The pseudo-ternary phase diagrams were plotted using Origin Pro 2025 software (Origin Lab Corporation, USA) and the monophasic zones were identified for the development of nanoemulsions.

Formulation and development of SH-17023-loaded nanoemulsion by D-optimal mixture design

Response surface analysis

The pseudoternary diagrams were used to formulate nanoemulsions with the use of appropriate oil, surfactant/co-surfactant, and water. The impact of each formulation component on the formulation and the optimization was carried out by a quality-by-design approach. The influence of critical material attributes (CMAs) on critical quality attributes (CQAs) of the NE was investigated by response surface methodology (RSM). The % v/v of rose oil (X1), surfactant-cosurfactant-cosolvent mixture or Smix (Labrasol® + Tween® 80 + Transcutol® P) (X2), and water (X3) were considered as CMAs, and their impact on CQAs, such as hydrodynamic globule size (Y1 = Zavg), polydispersity index (Y2 = PDI), and loading capacity (Y3 = LC) was investigated (Table 1). The response surface analysis and formulation optimization were carried out using a D-optimal mixture design using Design-Expert® software (Stat-Ease Inc., USA). The lower and higher levels of X1, X2, and X3 were chosen from the pseudo-ternary phase plots and pilot formulations.

Using the D-optimal mixture design, 16 batches of nanoemulsion were prepared. Briefly, the oil phase was mixed with the surfactant and vortexed for complete solubilization. Then the exact amount of water, as per the design of experiments (DOE) batch (Table 2, D-optimal mixture design), was added and vortexed at room temperature to develop an NE.

The dependent variables (Zavg, PDI, and LC) of each batch were measured in triplicate, and the mean outcomes were studied by the DOE software using ANOVA to investigate model significance (p < 0.005) and adequacy. The model parameters (model p-value, model F-value, lack of fit F-value, lack of fit p-value, the difference between adjusted and predicted R2 value, correlation coefficient (R2), and adequate precision) were investigated for adequacy of the used DOE model. The goodness of fit of the experimental design was investigated by diagnostic plots. To predict the best NEs, the statistical model was fitted to various models (linear, cubic, quadratic, special quadratic, and special cubic). The impact of each factor on the selected responses was analyzed using 2D and 3D response surface plots.

Optimization and checkpoint analysis

Through numerical and graphical optimization methods, the best CMAs required for the formulation of the desired nanoemulsion with low Zavg, PDI, and high LC were obtained. Characterization of NE

The optimized NE was further characterized for the following parameters.

Organoleptic properties

The optimized NE was observed with the naked eye for the signs of transparency, clarity, precipitation, phase separation, and colour.

pH

The pH of the optimized NE was evaluated using a calibrated pH meter (AB15 pH meter, Fisher Scientific, USA) at room temperature to ensure the ocular compatibility (Ismail et al., 2020).

Conductivity

Conductivity of the NEs was measured at room temperature using a calibrated (with a standard KCl solution having the conductivity of 1413 μS/cm) digital conductivity meter (PC 700, Eutech Instrument, Singapore).

Viscosity

The viscosity of NEs was measured at 25 °C by a parallel plate Rheometer (MCR 72, EDU Edition, Anton Paar, Austria) furnished with RheoCompass™ software using a 25 mm spindle. The viscosity was measured in triplicate. After each viscosity measurement, the plate was cleaned and dried.

Percentage transmittance

The %T was measured by UV–visible spectroscopy using a Multi-Mode Reader (BioTek Synergy H1 Hybrid, Agilent, USA) at 600 nm using water as a blank. The undiluted NE was taken in a 96-well plate, and the %T was measured in triplicate.

Refractive index and isotropy

The RI of the optimized NE was estimated using an Abbe digital refractometer at 25 ± 1 °C (Ismail et al., 2020). Briefly, 3 to 4 drops of undiluted NE were spread uniformly on a prism, and the RI was noted. The isotropy of the NE was verified by measuring and comparing the RI values of 3 samples collected from different locations of the NE.

Dilutability

The dilutability of the optimized NE was estimated by diluting with water, PBS (pH = 7.4), and simulated tear fluid (0.22 g NaHCO3, 0.68 g NaCl, 0.14 g KCl, 0.008 g CaCl2·2H2O, and water to 100 mL) at multiple dilutions (1:10, 1:50, 1:100, and 1:1000 times v/v). The samples were stored at room temperature for 24 h and examined for the signs of precipitation, clarity, and phase separation (Kassem et al., 2022).

Globule size, polydispersity, and zeta potential

The globule size (Zavg), polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential (ζ) of the optimized NE were evaluated by a dynamic light scattering (DLS) instrument (Zetasizer Nano series, Malvern Panalytical Ltd., UK) at 25 °C with backscattering of 173° and equilibrium time of 120 s (Ismail et al., 2020). Polystyrene disposable cuvette (DTS0012, Malvern) was used for Zavg and PDI, and disposable folded capillary cells (DTS1070, Malvern) were used for ζ measurement. The NEs were diluted ∼670 times with HPLC-grade water, filtered through a 0.22 μm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) syringe filter (Milex®-GV, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and analyzed by the instrument in triplicate.

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and selected area electron diffraction (SAED)

HRTEM analysis was performed to examine the morphology of the optimized NEs, and SAED was used to evaluate their crystallinity. Briefly, the optimized NEs were diluted 100 times with HPLC-grade water, and TEM samples were prepared by the drop-casting method. Briefly, 20 μL of the diluted NEs was dropped onto a 400-mesh carbon-coated copper grid (Ted Pella, USA), and the excess sample was absorbed with filter paper and dried overnight at room temperature to remove traces of water. Then the sample was subjected to the HRTEM instrument (TECNAI G220, FEI, USA) having a high-angle annular dark field detector for morphology and crystallinity analysis at 200 kV. Then the average globule size and size distribution were analyzed from the HRTEM photomicrographs using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health (NIH), USA).

Drug content

The drug content of the optimized NEs was evaluated using the validated LC-MS method. Briefly, the optimized NEs were diluted with LC-MS-grade methanol for the extraction of the contained drugs, followed by centrifugation at 10,621 ×g for 10 min, filtration of the supernatant (0.22 μm syringe filter), and estimation of the drugs using the LC-MS calibration curve. The free drug/unencapsulated drug in the supernatant was separated from drug-loaded NE droplets by the ultracentrifugation. In vitro drug release and release kinetics

The in vitro drug release of SH-17023 from the optimized NEs and solution was studied using a dialysis bag method. Cellulose dialysis tubing (MWCO 12–14 kDa, Bartovation, USA) was soaked overnight in deionized water to swell the bag and remove the preservatives. Based on preliminary experiments, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH = 7.4) containing 5% sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) was used as the release medium for the in vitro release study. Accurately, 100 μL of optimized NEs was added to 500 μL of release media, placed in a dialysis bag, hermetically sealed at both ends with threads, and placed vertically in a beaker containing 50 mL of release media. An equivalent quantity of SH-17023 solution in Transcutol P was used as a control for comparison. The receptor medium was maintained at ocular surface temperature of 34 ± 0.5 °C (Kassaee et al., 2023; Mahboobian et al., 2020; Mahran et al., 2021) with continuous stirring at 150 rpm by a temperature-controlled magnetic stirrer (Fisher Scientific, USA). At predetermined time intervals, a 1 mL aliquot was collected from the beaker and immediately replenished with the same volume of fresh blank medium to preserve sink conditions. The release study was continued up to 72 h. The quantity of drug released was quantified by the validated LC-MS method. Results are presented as the cumulative percentage of SH-17023 released over time. Drug-excipient compatibility

The drug-excipient compatibility was studied by attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) analysis (Porto et al., 2024) and X-ray diffraction studies.

ATR-FTIR study

Briefly, in the ATR-FTIR analysis, the vibrational spectra of SH-17023, rose oil, Labrasol®, Tween® 80, Transcutol® P, physical mixture, blank/placebo NE, and optimized NE were compared to predict the possible interaction among SH-17023 and excipients of NEs using an ATR-FTIR spectrophotometer (Nicolet™ iS50, Thermo Scientific, USA) equipped with an ATR accessory. Data acquisition was made using OMNIC Paradigm software (Version 2.8 HF2) within the wavenumber range of 4000 to 400 cm−1 with 40 scans, and 4 cm−1 spectral resolution.

X-Ray diffraction study

Similarly, the diffraction peaks of SH-17023, rose oil, Labrasol®, Tween® 80, Transcutol® P, physical mixture, blank/placebo NE, and optimized NE were compared to predict the possible interaction among SH-17023 and excipients of NEs using a diffractometer (Rigaku Miniflex 600 Japan) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 A°) at 15 mA current and 40 kV voltage. The diffraction was acquired using MiniFlex Guidance software (Version 2.0.2.1) at 25 °C from 2 Theta of 10 to 100° with a step size of 0.02°/step. Since most of the formulation components are viscous liquids, the samples were prepared by drop casting on a glass plate and drying at room temperature.

Ex vivo ocular permeation study

An ex vivo trans-corneal permeation study was conducted using goat eyes. Freshly excised goat eyes were collected in ice-cold PBS, transported at 4 °C, and dissected to obtain the cornea. The transcorneal diffusion study was carried out under non-occlusive conditions using a six-station vertical jacketed Franz diffusion apparatus (PermeGear Inc., USA) with a 3 mL receptor volume with a spherical joint (5 mm orifice diameter, 12 mm spherical diameter, and an effective diffusional area of 0.2 cm2). Bubbles between the cornea and receptor media were eliminated. The cornea was sandwiched between the donor and receptor chambers with the corneal epithelium towards the donor compartment and endothelium towards the receptor compartment containing fresh phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4 with 5% SLS) as diffusion media. Sodium azide (0.025% w/v) was added to the medium to avoid microbial growth (Attia et al., 2024). The Franz-diffusion cells were placed in black anodized aluminum cell holders, and the medium was stirred at 600 rpm using a temperature-controlled Franz cell magnetic stirrer (PermeGear V-series, V6-CB-01 Stirrer). The receptor compartment was maintained at ocular surface temperature (34 ± 0.5 °C) (Mahboobian et al., 2020) using a heater circulator (Julabo CORIO CD-BC4, 4.5 L). Before the sample application, the system was equilibrated for 30 min at 34 ± 0.5 °C to achieve corneal hydration and ocular surface temperature. Optimized NE and SH-17023 solution (in Transcutol) (15 μL) in triplicate were uniformly applied onto the corneal surface, which was in contact with receptor medium. Aliquots of 0.2 mL were collected at various time intervals (0, 0.083, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 14, 16, 19, 24, 26, 28, 30, 36, 48, 52, 56, 63, and 72 h) from receptor compartment and replenished with the same volume of fresh diffusion medium to maintain the sink condition. Stability studies

The stability studies were carried out under accelerated conditions (heating-cooling, freeze-thaw, and centrifugal) and at room temperature (25 °C) up to 6 months.

Heating-cooling cycle

In the heating-cooling stability studies, the optimized NE was exposed to three cycles of heating and cooling within 40 and 4 °C each for a period of 48 h followed by examination for the signs of instability (cracking, creaming, and phase separation), colloidal properties (globule size, zeta potential), drug content, and chemical integrity etc. (Kassaee and Mahboobian, 2022; Mahboobian et al., 2020; Mehrandish and Mirzaeei, 2021; Mohapatra et al., 2025). The colloidal properties were evaluated using DLS, whereas the drug content was evaluated using the validated LC-MS method. Chemical integrity was evaluated by assessing shifts or disappearance of functional group peaks in the optimized NEs using ATR-FTIR spectroscopy.

Freeze-thaw cycle

This involved exposure of optimized NE to three cycles of freeze-thaw (−20 °C and 25 °C) each for a period of 48 h followed by evaluation of the signs of instability (cracking, creaming, and phase separation), colloidal properties (globule size, zeta potential), drug content, and chemical integrity (Kassaee and Mahboobian, 2022; Mahboobian et al., 2020; Mehrandish and Mirzaeei, 2021; Mohapatra et al., 2025).

Centrifugation study

The centrifugal stability was evaluated by subjecting the optimized NEs to centrifugation (Eppendorf, 5417 R) for 30 mins at 17950 ×g, followed by evaluation of signs of instability, colloidal properties, and chemical integrity (Kassaee and Mahboobian, 2022; Mahboobian et al., 2020; Mehrandish and Mirzaeei, 2021; Mohapatra et al., 2025).

Kinetic stability/ long-term stability study

Kinetic stability was assessed by storing the optimized NEs for up to 6 months at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) and evaluating signs of instability, chemical integrity, and colloidal properties (Mohapatra et al., 2025).

Each animal was thoroughly examined for pre-existing corneal injury, signs of eye irritation, or ocular defects 24 h before starting the ocular irritation study. Draize technique

Each animal was thoroughly observed for any signs of toxicity, survival, or behavioural alteration, or change in body weight. The Draize irritation scores were assigned by visual evaluation of changes in ocular structures immediately and at 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 24 h after first administration, followed by assessments performed twice daily up to 7 days (before the first morning dose and in the evening). The degree of ocular irritation was investigated by scoring lesions of the cornea and conjunctiva, and chemosis at specific intervals as per Draize's scoring. Individual eyes of each experimental group were scored for each irritation parameter, and the average score for each parameter was calculated. Then, the total ocular irritation scores (OIS) for all irritation parameters were calculated by summing the average results for each parameter across treatments. The ocular irritation is evaluated from the average total ocular irritation score from 0 to 16, where an average score of 0–3 represents non-irritant, 4–8 represents slightly irritant, 9–12 represents moderately irritant, and 13–16 represents severely irritating or corrosive nature of the applied product (Fardous et al., 2022; Mohapatra et al., 2025). The irritation score of 2 or 3 in any evaluation or more than 4 for the total ocular irritation index (sum of all clinical irritation scores) was considered a meaningful irritant (Mahboobian et al., 2020). Since the eyes are very small and black in the case of C57BL/6J mice, the iris was not evaluated. The average ocular irritation score for cornea, conjunctiva, and chemosis was calculated for each group and interpreted for possible ocular irritation (Mohapatra et al., 2025). Further, the eyes were observed for any signs of tearing/allergic reaction, or inflammation.

Slit-lamp biomicroscopy, fundoscopy, SD-OCT, and FA

Slit-lamp biomicroscopy, SD-OCT, and FA were carried out on the initial day (Day 0) and at the end of the study (Day 7). Slit-lamp biomicroscopy was carried out to study the anterior segment of the eye using both narrow and wide beam exams. The narrow beam slit lamp exam was carried out to investigate complications in the cornea or in the anterior chamber, such as corneal swelling, corneal inflammation, corneal roughness or a change in the clarity of aqueous humor, whereas the wide beam biomicroscopy was carried out to investigate the entire eye surface and identify any changes or inflammation in cornea, conjunctiva, or the eyelid (Ibrahim et al., 2020). Further, the eyes were topically dosed with 0.5% (2 μL/eye) sodium fluorescein and investigated under fluorescent light on the slit lamp for identifying corneal surface abnormalities/ smoothness (Fardous et al., 2022).

Spectral domain OCT with guidance of bright-field live fundoscopy was performed using the Micron® 5 intraocular imager. The SD-OCT of the cornea and retina was carried out to study the abnormalities in the cornea and retina. Both horizontal and vertical cross-sectional OCT were done for a detailed observation of various ocular structures. Several OCT images of the cornea and retina were captured across the entire eye. Fundoscopy was performed to examine the retina, optic disc, macula, fovea, and retinal vessels.

Fundus FA was carried out to observe the ocular vasculature by i.p. administration of sodium fluorescein (2.5% prepared in sterile PBS) to anesthetized mice at a dose of 2.5 mL/kg. The FA photomicrographs were captured after 3 min of dye administration using a fluorescent imaging filter (Barrier: GFP/FA-B, and Excite: GFP/FA-E) of the Micron® 5 intraocular imager.

Laser-induced choroidal neovascularization (L-CNV) model

CNV was developed by laser-induced rupture of Bruch's membrane (Chobisa et al., 2024; Gong et al., 2015; Muniyandi et al., 2025; Sishtla et al., 2022). L-CNV was performed on both eyes using the laser injector of the Micron® 5 intraocular imager at four locations/eye (12, 3, 6, and 9, o’clock or 1, 4, 7, and 10 o’clock to avoid hitting major blood vessels) with a 50 μm spot size, 270 mW pulse power, for 70 ms duration via an ophthalmic argon green laser (wavelength 532 nm) to rupture Bruch’s membrane. The fundus orientation was made by adjusting the mouse stage (up-down or left-right), focus, and aperture in such a way that the optic nerve was positioned at the centre of the view field, and the nerve fibers were uniform in all quadrants, as even focus is necessary to produce consistent CNV lesions (Gong et al., 2015). Before the laser was used, the beam intensity was measured several times using a laser power meter, with a 50 mW power setting for a 2500 ms pulse, producing a power level of 13–25 mW (24.5 in our case) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Only intact eyes without noticeable ocular abnormalities (cataract, visible defects, and anomalous structures) were used for the L-CNV model (Gong et al., 2015). The uniformity and the diameter of the aiming beam on the fundus were observed before laser shooting. After the laser application, the eyes were lubricated with lubricating gel and placed on a pre-warmed heating pad, and atipamezole (1 mg/kg) was administered i.p. for reversal of anesthesia.

The in vivo CNV progression was evaluated after 7 days of laser treatment using the Micron® 5 intraocular imager equipped with Micron® 5 camera head, interchangeable objective lenses (LT 2 lens), temperature-controlled animal stand, and Micron Software Suite (Version: MSS 2.0.1). Fundoscopy and SD-OCT

SD-OCT with bright-field live fundoscopy was performed using the Micron® 5 intraocular imager. Vertical and horizontal cross-sectional OCT imaging was carried out through the center of each laser-induced lesion. Several OCT images were captured across the lesion, and the largest cross-sectional lesion was used to calculate the lesion volume. The OCT analysis was performed by treating the lesions as ellipsoids and measuring their maximum dimensions in all three planes from OCT photomicrographs. Where a, b, and c are semi-axes (half the lengths) along the x-axis, y-axis, and z-axis, respectively. The values of “a” and “c” are taken from the larger diameter of horizontal OCT and vertical OCT, respectively; whereas “b” is taken from whichever OCT image has the larger value.

Fundus fluorescein angiography

The FA was carried out to study vascular leakage after fundoscopy and OCT imaging by i.p. administration of sodium fluorescein (2.5% prepared in water for injection) to anesthetized mice at a dose of 2.5 mL/kg. The FA photomicrographs were captured after 3 min of dye administration using a fluorescent imaging filter (Barrier: GFP/FA-B, and Excite: GFP/FA-E) of the Micron® 5 intraocular imager. The FA images of the CNV lesions were used as a semi-quantitative measure of the leakage of lesions. Then, lesion severity was graded on a discrete scale from 0 (no fluorescent lesion) to 3 (saturated fluorescent lesion). Since lesion grading is a subjective evaluation, inter-personnel grading was performed by three independent observers, with masking of the experimental groups (Chobisa et al., 2024).

Ex vivo immunostaining and lesion volume quantification

The ex vivo immunostaining was carried out to visualize microvasculature in the choroidal flat mount as per our previous protocol with slight modifications (Chobisa et al., 2024). After the FA imaging, mice were euthanized using CO2 (3–4 L/min for 7 min), and the eyes were enucleated. The collected eyes were pierced with a needle and were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight. After fixation, the eyes were dissected to remove the cornea from the posterior cup (retina/choroid). The posterior cups were further dissected to collect retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)-choroid. Then, the RPE-choroid was washed thrice (10 min/wash) at room temperature and incubated with 2.5% of bovine serum albumin (BSA) containing 0.3% of Triton X-100 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 1.5 h to avoid nonspecific staining. The blocking solution was removed and further incubated with lectin (biotin-conjugated GS-IB4) at a dilution of 1:250 in a solution (0.5% BSA in 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 48 h at 4 °C in an orbital shaker. After incubation, the RPE-choroid was washed thrice with PBS (20 min/wash) and incubated with detection reagent (DyLight 488 Streptavidin, 1:400 dilution in 0.5% BSA in 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS solution) in a dark environment overnight at 4 °C using an orbital shaker. The non-specific staining was evaluated by considering no lectin control with the addition of the detection reagent. Afterwards, RPE-choroids were washed thrice with PBS (20 min/wash), stained with DAPI (diluted 1:10000 in PBS) for 10 min, and then washed twice in PBS (10 min/wash). The choroidal tissues were flat-mounted, covered with Fluoromount-G mounting medium along with glass coverslips, and stored in the dark at 4 °C.

The choroidal flatmounts were observed at 5× (all lesions) and 20× (individual lesions) magnifications. The z-stack photomicrographs of choroidal lesions were captured (3 stacks per lesion, a gap of 3 μm, 20× magnification) using an inverted LED fluorescence phase contrast motorized microscope (AXIO observer, Carl Zeiss, GmbH, Germany). The L-CNV lesion volumes were calculated from the lesion area of choroidal flat mounts measured by ImageJ software (1.52a, NIH, USA) by multiplying the total depth of the image (6 μm) by the average of the stained area of each lesion (Sishtla et al., 2022). Lesions are included in the analysis considering exclusion criteria (Gong et al., 2015; Poor et al., 2014), such as (i) inability to locate or identify lesion, (ii) presence of dissection damage, (iii) presence of inadequate dissection such as intact portions of the retina that was stained on top of choroid, (iv) linear lesion instead of circular, (v) lesion had fused with another lesion (both lesions excluded), (vi) the lesion had a size indicating it was an outlier lesion (“too big,” is more than 5 times larger than the next biggest lesion in the eye; “too small,” that is, it was less than 1/5 the area of the next smallest lesion in the eye; “too different,” that is, after all of the lesions in a specific treatment group were measured, the lesion’s area was 5-fold greater than the mean for that group), and (vii) the lesion was the only usable lesion in an eye. Additional exclusion criteria for CNV and FA (no FA leakage) were also considered.

Statistical analysis

The mean and standard deviation (SD) for all experiments was calculated and expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed with Student’s t-test at P < 0.05 using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, Inc., USA).

Results and discussion

LC-MS analysis and validation

The detailed validation results of the developed LC-MS method are provided in the supplementary file (Section 3.1, Fig.S2-S4, and Table S2-S5). Very sharp and distinct peaks of SH-17023 and midazolam as internal standards were obtained at run time (Rt) of 4.22 and 4.53, respectively (Fig.S2). SH-17023 showed the linearity within 5–1000 ng/mL with the regression equation of y = 1.179 x + 19.741 (n = 3) with an R2 value of 0.998 (supplementary file, section 3.1.1). The percentage recovery was found to be within 98.303–100.091% at 50, 100, and 150% of standard addition (supplementary file, section 3.1.2, Table S2). High recovery values (>98% and low percent relative standard deviation (% RSD <2%) for all selected levels of SH-17023 demonstrated excellent accuracy of the developed LC-MS method. The precision of the developed LC-MS method, evaluated at 5, 50, and 500 ng/mL of working standard solution via repeatability, intra-day, and inter-day studies, showed a low % RSD (< 2%) in all cases, reflecting the attainment of a high degree of precision for the developed LC-MS method (supplementary file, section 3.1.3, Table S3). The LOD and LOQ for SH-17023 were found to be 0.531 ng/mL and 1.611 ng/mL, respectively (supplementary file, section 3.1.4, Table S4). Very low values of LOD and LOQ of the developed LC-MS method represent its excellent sensitivity for qualitative and quantitative analysis of SH-17023. Robustness is a measure of the ability of the developed LC-MS analytical method to remain unaffected by minor changes in analytical variables. The robustness of the developed LC-MS method examined by deliberate variations in chromatographic conditions reflected a lower % RSD value (<2%) of the area ratio or analyte to internal standard and retention time (supplementary file, section 3.1.5, Table S5), reflecting robustness of the developed method. This provides the reliability of the developed LC-MS method for qualitative and quantitative analysis of SH-17023 under slight variations in analytical conditions. A summary of the complete validation outcomes is provided in Supplementary Table S6. The overall validation results (linearity and range, accuracy, precision, LOD, LOQ, and robustness) as per the ICH guideline reflected the suitability of the developed LC-MS method for accurate quantification of SH-17023.

Screening of oil, surfactant, co-surfactant, and co-solvent by solubility study

Screening of suitable excipients is necessary for the development of stable NEs with high drug loading capacity. Oil, surfactants, and co-surfactants are major components of NEs. The oil solubilizes the hydrophobic drugs. The surfactants and co-surfactants adsorbed at the oil-water interface decrease the interfacial tension, leading to globule size reduction and formation of NEs (Smail et al., 2021). Sometimes, surfactant alone is not sufficient to reduce interfacial tension and produce stable NEs. Hence, a combination of surfactants and co-surfactants is needed to produce NEs with ultra-low dimensions and prolonged stability (Smail et al., 2021). A saturation solubility study was conducted to find suitable excipients for the development of NEs. The results of the solubility study of SH-17023 in various oils, surfactants, co-surfactants, and co-solvents are shown in Fig. 1 and Table S7 (supplementary file, section 3.2). Formulation and development of SH-17023-loaded NEs

NEs were prepared by a low-energy spontaneous emulsification method due to the advantage of spontaneous nanoglobule formation with gentle stirring without the need for specialized instruments (Dehghani et al., 2024; Ismail et al., 2020). Such a method utilizes the internal energy of the system to form the NEs spontaneously. Furthermore, they produce smaller globules compared to high-energy methods, offer ease of scale-up, and avoid the degradation of thermostable drugs that may occur with high-energy methods (Ismail et al., 2020).

The choice of excipient is important for the development of stable NEs. Non-ionic surfactants and co-surfactants, such as Labrasol® (Da Silva Gonçalves et al., 2024), Tween® 80 (Attia et al., 2024), and Transcutol® P (Kassaee and Mahboobian, 2022; Mahaling et al., 2024; Mahboobian et al., 2017) were chosen for the development of NEs due to their reported high safety profile in ocular formulations. Further, the surfactants with high HLB values (>10) are required to produce o/w type NEs with good physical stability (Attia et al., 2024). The requirement is fulfilled by the use of Tween® 80 and Labrasol®, having the HLB values of 15 and 12, respectively (Attia et al., 2024). The Transcutol® P was used as a cosolvent in the formulation to further enhance the solubility and stability of the formulation.

Labrasol® is a medium-chain triglyceride used mostly as an amphipathic non-ionic surfactant/co-surfactant/emulsifier and permeation enhancer. It improves the solubilization of hydrophobic drugs and enhances stability and homogeneity of an API in dosage form (Da Silva Gonçalves et al., 2024). It has been utilized as a surfactant/co-surfactant/edge activator/penetration enhancer in multiple ophthalmic formulations, such as nanostructured lipid carrier (Liu et al., 2012), microemulsion (Maulvi et al., 2021; Raval et al., 2018), microemulgel/microemulsion-based gel (Dhaval et al., 2020), penetration enhancer vesicles, transfersomes (Ashraf et al., 2018), spanlastic nanovesicles-loaded gel (Yasser et al., 2024), and NEs (Mahboobian et al., 2017) without causing any tissue damage.

Tween® 80/polysorbate 80 is a non-ionic surfactant widely used in various ocular formulations as a surfactant, emulsifier, edge activator/flexibility enhancer without any irritation or toxicity (Said et al., 2024). It has been utilized in many ocular drug delivery systems, such as NLC (Liu et al., 2012), solution, suspension, emulsion, gel (Ubhe et al., 2024), NEs, ocular inserts (Said et al., 2024), Ocusert (Shaikh et al., 2024), nano crystal (Kiran and Niharika, 2024), microemulsion (Zafar et al., 2024), self-nanoemulsifying drug delivery systems (SNEDDS) (Zingale et al., 2024), and solid lipid nanoparticle, without any sign of eye irritation. In the case of NEs, the Tween® 80 allows better stabilization of NEs, forming a protective layer around the oil globule, causing the emulsification, reducing the surface energy, and preventing their aggregation (Zingale et al., 2024).

Transcutol®P has been reported as cosurfactant for many NDDS, including NEs (Kassaee and Mahboobian, 2022; Mahaling et al., 2024; Mahboobian et al., 2017), microemulsions (Gohil et al., 2020; Salimi and Behrouzifar, 2020; Zhang et al., 2023), microemulgels (Dhaval et al., 2020), SNEDDS (Vikash et al., 2023; Zingale et al., 2024), in situ gelling systems (Ahmed et al., 2024), in situ gelling NEs (Kassaee et al., 2023), polymeric nanoparticles (Beirampour et al., 2024), leciplex (Bagul et al., 2024), and cubosomes (Nemr et al., 2024), for improved permeability in ophthalmic applications without any ocular irritation.

To our knowledge, there are no scientific reports on the ocular use of rose oil; therefore, for the first time, the ophthalmic NE of rose oil was developed and characterized for ocular irritation to identify its suitability for topical ocular application.

The pseudoternary phase diagrams with larger monophasic area (Fig. 2a, d, and e) were considered for development of NEs. Preliminary batches were formulated with the use of pseudoternary phase diagrams due to the maximum NE region, which will offer the maximum region of operability/flexibility for the development of an optimum NE. Initially, three preliminary formulations (200 μL oil, 600 μL surfactant or surfactant-cosurfactant or surfactant-cosurfactant-co-solvent mix, and 200 μL water) were prepared with the use of phase diagrams (Fig. 2a, d, and e), and the outcomes (Zavg, PDI, zeta potential, and drug loading capacity) were analyzed. The formulation with the phase diagram Fig. 2a, having rose oil, Labrasol®, and water demonstrated very low PDI (0.081 ± 0.001), high zeta potential (−35.166 ± 0.903 mV), and drug loading (6.28 ± 1.356%) compared to other formulations with phase diagrams Fig. 2d and e. However, the globule size was found to be relatively larger (101.2 ± 0.512 nm) compared to the other two formulations (13.573 ± 0.532 nm and 45.6 ± 0.236 nm). After loading the drug in the formulation having rose oil, Labrasol®, and water, it was found to develop precipitates after 40 days. Due to the physical instability of the formulation, the remaining phase diagrams (Fig. 2d and e) were considered for the development of NEs. The formulation with phase diagram Fig. 2d, rose oil, Labrasol®, and Tween® 80 demonstrated a globule size of 13.573 ± 0.532 nm, PDI of 0.314 ± 0.012, zeta potential of −7.03 ± 0.884 mV, and loading capacity of 3.43 ± 2.137%. Due to the low loading capacity, this formulation was not considered for further optimization. Then the formulation with phase diagram Fig. 2e having rose oil, Labrasol®: Tween® 80: Transcutol® P (1:1:1), and water was considered for the QbD-based development of NEs due to the smaller globule size (45.6 ± 0.236 nm), relatively high loading capacity (5.44 ± 1.873%), low PDI (0.290 ± 0.003), excellent zeta potential (−29.16 ± 1.161), and prolonged physical stability (absence of phase separation, precipitation, sedimentation, flocculation) after loading of drugs.

Response surface analysis

Response surface analysis allows for understanding the effects of various factors on responses during formulation optimization. In the QbD approach, the desired critical quality attributes (CQAs) are defined, and the influence of each independent variable is investigated. The lower and upper levels of the independent variables cannot be selected at random when the sum of the formulation components is essential to develop a stable formulation (Barot et al., 2012). Among various experimental designs (central composite design, Box–Behnken designs), the D-optimal mixture design is widely used for formulation optimization, specifically when the proportions of the formulation components are responsible for experimental outcomes (Gohil et al., 2020; Shah et al., 2018). In a D-optimal mixture design, the proportion of each formulation component is more essential than its actual amount in the combination, as the sum of components in each mixture must equal 100% v/v. In contrast, other statistical designs rely on the amounts of formulation components to achieve the desired formulation with the required quality attributes (Shah et al., 2018). The experimental runs in a three-component mixture design are represented in an equilateral triangle in which the components’ boundaries are limited in the design space. Hence, the D-optimal mixture is used for improved prediction power with a minimum number of experimental runs (Barot et al., 2012; Fouad et al., 2013).

In our nanoemulsion formulation, the three-component system includes the % of rose oil (X1), Smix (Labrasol® + Tween® 80 + Transcutol® P) (X2), and water (X3). In a recent study, travoprost-loaded NEs demonstrated a significant decrease in the globule size with an increase in the surfactant (Tween® 80) concentration and a significant increase in the globule size with increasing the oil (Lipophile®) concentration (Ismail et al., 2020). The smallest globule size was reported with the highest surfactant and lowest oil concentration (Ismail et al., 2020). A high oil concentration causes resistance to oil disruption during stirring, leading to increased globule size (Ismail et al., 2020). As the oil phase forms the internal dispersed phase of NEs, an increase in its percentage forms bigger globules, leading to an increase in the Zavg. Surfactants and cosurfactants act as emulsifiers in the NEs, reducing interfacial tension at the oil-water interface and globule size (Ismail et al., 2020). The increase in surfactant concentration causes increased coverage on the NE globule surface, leading to the formation of smaller globules (Ismail et al., 2020). The co-solvents in the Smix further reduce the interfacial tension. With an increase in the proportion of Smix, the decrease in globule size is due to the reduction of the interfacial tension and the lowering of free energy between oil and water. At low concentrations of Smix, the interfacial tension and free energy are very high, leading to an increase in globule size of the NEs. In a study regarding clotrimazole-loaded ophthalmic microemulsions, the globule size was reported to decrease with increasing the concentration of Smix from 60% to 70%, which is ascribed to the decrease in the surface tension upon increasing the Smix concentration (Al-mahallawi et al., 2021). In another study, the effect of percentage of oil (isopropyl myristate), surfactant (Tween® 80), and cationic agent (cetyl trimethyl ammonium bromide) was studied on the globule size, zeta potential, and PDI of dorzolamide hydrochloride-loaded NEs using Box–Behnken design. The size of NEs was found to be increased with increasing the volume of oil phase and decreasing with increasing the surfactant concentration (Kassem et al., 2022). The reduced globule size at higher surfactant concentration was also reported for the reduction of the interfacial tension between the oil and aqueous phase (Kassem et al., 2022). The higher surfactant concentration stabilized the small oil globules by forming a steric barrier on their surface and preventing the coalescence of globules (Kassem et al., 2022).

Here, the globule size of the NE was found to be decreased with an increase in the proportion of dispersion medium/continuous phase (water), which can be ascribed to the very good dispersion of oil globules at high water content. However, after a very high concentration (70%) of Smix, the size of the NEs was found to be increased. Such an outcome is ascribed to possible accumulation of surfactants, which further attract the surfactants from the oil-water interface and allow the flocculation of oil droplets in the NE, resulting in an increase in globule size. For this reason, the globule size was found to be very high even at low percentage of oil (<15%) due to the very high surfactant concentration.

The PDI value represents the homogeneity of the NEs (Kassem et al., 2022). A PDI value below 0.5 indicates a narrow size distribution and a homogeneous nature of NEs. Uniform globules facilitate the effective corneal delivery of low-water-soluble drugs (Al-mahallawi et al., 2021). In all trial runs (16 batches), the PDI values ranged from 0.19 to 0.412, i.e., <0.5, indicating the monodisperse and homogeneous nature of the developed nanoemulsions. The obtained PDI values were observed to be decreased with a decrease in the proportion of rose oil and an increased proportion of Smix and water (Fig. 3c and d). The results are in accordance with the PDI results of dorzolamide hydrochloride-loaded NEs (Kassem et al., 2022), and a clotrimazole-loaded microemulsion (Al-mahallawi et al., 2021) where the PDI values are reported to be decreased with increasing the Tween® 80 and Smix concentration, respectively. It was reported that the greater concentration of surfactant would cause the formation of homogeneous globules (Kassem et al., 2022). In contrast, at elevated oil levels, very large and more heterogeneous globules were generated, resulting in increased PDI value. An increase in the proportion of aqueous phase promoted the fine dispersion of globules, resulting in a lowering of PDI values.

Loading capacity is the actual quantity of loaded drug per unit weight of formulation. The LC for all the NEs is found to be within 4.358% to 8.360%. A higher LC is required to load the maximum quantity of drug within a minimum quantity of formulation to achieve maximum therapeutic effect. From the response surface plots, it was observed that LC increases with increasing proportions of oil and Smix. Since SH-17023 is more soluble in Smix than in rose oil, the loading capacity increased with an increased proportion of Smix, followed by rose oil (Fig. 3e and f). In a recent study, regarding a poorly water-soluble drug (luliconazole) loaded NEs, the effect of concentration of oil phase and Smix was studied on the drug content and globule size (Yang et al., 2022). The drug loading was reported to increase with increasing the percentage of the oil phase, due to improved drug solubility with increasing oil concentration (Yang et al., 2022).

Organoleptic properties

The developed NE was observed as clear, transparent, slightly pale yellow in colour, without any sign of precipitation, phase separation, or flocculation. The transparent nature is characteristic of NEs (De Oca-Ávalos et al., 2017).

pH

The pH of the optimized NE was found to be 5.58 ± 0.216 (Table 3). The NEs showed slightly acidic pH, which can be tolerated on the eye without potential irritation. The acceptable pH range of ocular formulations is within 3.5–8.5 (Dhahir et al., 2021; Kassem et al., 2022) or more precisely within 6.5–8.5 (Choradiya and Patil, 2021; Mohapatra et al., 2025). The pH of the optimized NEs is within the acceptable limits of pH for ocular drug delivery, representing their topical applicability potential. Similar pH values (pH 5.5–5.9) were reported for travoprost-loaded ophthalmic NEs (Ismail et al., 2020). In another report, very low pH values (pH 3.80–4.20) were reported for β-caryophyllene-loaded NEs for the treatment of Acanthamoeba keratitis and reported to be close to the acceptable limits (pH 4–8) for ocular application (Porto et al., 2024).

Conductivity

The conductivity of the optimized NE was found to be 53.166 ± 0.124 S/cm (Table 3). The results of conductivity testing represent the type of NEs. Despite the use of a non-ionic surfactant in the optimized NE, they demonstrated electroconductive behavior. A very high electrical conductivity indicates that the optimized NEs are oil-in-water (o/w) and that they dilute readily in water. The conductivity of the diluted sample remained unchanged at 0, 12, and 24 h, indicating the stability of the NEs after dilution. In case of any instability, phase separation occurs, and the conductivity gradually decreases.

Viscosity

The viscosity of the optimized NE was measured to check their consistency and suitability for topical ocular administration (Mohapatra et al., 2025). Very low viscosity leads to spillage and poor ocular retention, whereas overly high viscosity can make topical ocular administration difficult; therefore, NEs should have an optimum viscosity to ensure both adequate retention and ease of installation (Mohapatra et al., 2025). The obtained viscosity of the optimized NEs was found to be 124.486 ± 0.669 mPa·s at a shear rate of 10 s−1 (Table 3), which is within the permissible viscosity limits (15–150 mPa·s) for topical ophthalmic application and maximum corneal retention (Mohapatra et al., 2025). The developed NE demonstrated low viscosity, which is expected for the consistency of NEs.

Percentage transmittance

The optimized ophthalmic NE was found to be very transparent with a % T value of 96.531. A very high % transparency (>95%) represents the potential of the ophthalmic NEs for topical ocular application without any blur effects. Such a formulation would offer improved patient compliance. The blank NE showed a % T value of 98.552%. The relatively lower %T in the case of optimized NE compared to blank NE is due to the successful entrapment of the drug.

Refractive index

The refractive index (RI) represents the transparency of the ocular formulation (Mohapatra et al., 2025). A transparent ocular formulation offers clear visualization after topical application. An RI value of <1.476 is optimal for topical ocular application without causing blurring or vision discomfort (Dhahir et al., 2021; Ismail et al., 2020; Kassem et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2020). The RI value of the developed NE (1.347 ± 0.0001; Table 3) is close to the RI of water (1.333) and tear fluid (1.340–1.360) (Kassem et al., 2022), representing the optical clarity of NEs and their suitability for topical ocular application (Mohapatra et al., 2025). Further, the RI measurements of three independent samples from optimized NEs were found to be very close to each other, indicating the isotropic nature of the novel ophthalmic formulation.

Dilutability

After dilution with water, PBS (pH = 7.4), and simulated tear fluid, the NE did not show any sign of cracking, precipitation, or phase separation, demonstrating dilution stability (Kassem et al., 2022) and water as the external phase, i.e., o/w type. The o/w type NEs have been frequently employed and are ideal for topical ocular administration, as the external phase is water; this makes them easier to dilute with aqueous tear fluid and offers the potential to deliver hydrophobic drugs (Attia et al., 2024; Kassem et al., 2022).

Globule size, polydispersity, and zeta potential

The globule size, polydispersity index, and zeta potential values of the optimized NE were found to be 32.832 ± 2.125 nm, 0.201 ± 0.003, and –− 29.2 ± 0.45 mV, respectively (Table 3, Fig. 5). A very low globule size will offer improved ocular penetration (Fig. 5a). A globule size of <200 nm is reported to be optimal for ophthalmic drug delivery (Ismail et al., 2020; Mohapatra et al., 2025), since they offer improved effective surface area, increased solubility, and improved ocular permeation via receptor-mediated endocytosis (Mohapatra et al., 2025).Fig. 5Vesicle size, morphology, size distribution, and crystallinity of optimized NEs. PDI value varies from 0 to 1, where 0 represents a completely uniform/homogeneous size distribution with monomodal distribution, and 1 represents polydispersity/heterogeneous globule size with multimodal distribution (Durgapal et al., 2022). A higher PDI value represents that the system possesses a high surface free energy, which leads to phase separation and fusion of globules to achieve a low surface free energy state through aggregation (Durgapal et al., 2022). The PDI value <0.3 represents the homogeneous nature of the formulation and narrow size distribution, whereas a value >0.3 indicates broad size distribution and heterogeneity (Mohapatra et al., 2025). The correlogram is shown in Fig. 5b. Smaller globules move faster, and the correlation decays quickly, producing a steep curve. Smooth single-exponential decay represented monodisperse globule size with narrow size distribution. A very low PDI value avoids Ostwald ripening, thereby improving the stability (Mohapatra et al., 2025).

The negative zeta potential at the surface of NEs will provide repulsion among the nanoglobules, thereby improving colloidal stability (Al-mahallawi et al., 2021). The zeta potential value within ±0–10 mV signifies highly unstable, a value between ±10–20 mV signifies relatively stable, a value between ±20–30 mV signifies moderately stable, and values greater than ±30 mV signify high stability of colloidal dispersion (Mohapatra et al., 2021). The developed NEs showed a zeta potential of −29.2 ± 0.45 mV, reflecting a moderately stable nature (Fig. 5c). An inverse phase plot represents the negative zeta potential on the NE globules (Fig. 5d). A higher zeta potential will offer repulsive forces among the emulsion globules, thereby preventing the aggregation and coalescence, and offering prolonged stability (Mohapatra et al., 2025).

HRTEM, Globule size distribution, and SAED

The HRTEM photomicrographs of the optimized NE showed spherical smooth globules (Fig. 5e and f). The morphology of the globules is consistent with previous reports (Zhang et al., 2022). However, the aggregation of globules is due to the evaporation of the external aqueous medium from the TEM grid during drying. The size distribution curve represented a narrow size distribution with average globule size of 32.798 ± 3.012 nm, which is in accordance with the DLS results (Fig. 5g). However, some of the globules were found to be relatively large in size due to the fusion of the droplets on the TEM grid during sample processing. The SAED results revealed a diffused pattern, reflecting the amorphous nature of the NE (Fig. 5h).

Drug content

The percentage drug content of the optimized NE was found to be 99.585 ± 7.719% by the validated LC-MS technique. A drug content of 85–115% (± 15%) is accepted and considered as a good product, whereas a content of 75–125% (± 25%) is not accepted as a good product (Mohapatra et al., 2025). The evaluated drug content of the developed NE is within the accepted limit.

Entrapment efficiency

EE is a critical quality attribute as it reflects the fraction of the drug associated with the dispersed internal phase. The entrapment efficiency of the optimized NE was 97.091 ± 1.294%. A high entrapment efficiency (>90%) is required for ophthalmic NEs to maximize ocular bioavailability, thereby reducing dosing volume and frequency (Mohapatra et al., 2025). A high % EE was also indicated by the absence of SH-17023's characteristic ATR-FTIR peaks in the optimized formulation (see section 13.5.14), indicating effective drug loading in the internal oil phase and the absence of free drug in the external medium of the ophthalmic NEs (Yang et al., 2022; Youssef et al., 2021).

In-vitro drug release and release kinetics

The dialysis bag method has been widely used for the release study of ophthalmic NEs (Ismail et al., 2020; Mahboobian et al., 2020). The release profile of SH-17023 from solution and optimized NE is shown in Fig. 6a as the cumulative percentage of drug release as a function of time (h). The release of SH-17023 from the solution was found to be faster than the optimized NE. The release of the drug from the solution plateaued after 6 h, while the optimized NE continuously released the drug up to 72 h. At 6 h, the cumulative percentage drug release from solution and NE were found to be 98.274 ± 1.104 and 51.798 ± 3.743%, respectively. The optimized NE demonstrated a biphasic release pattern, i.e., initial rapid release followed by sustained release. The initial rapid release might be due to the release of a small quantity of drug localized at the o/w interface. The sustained release profile of SH-17023 from the optimized NE is attributed to the release of the drug from the oil core, which behaves as a drug reservoir to sustain release (Attia et al., 2024; Ismail et al., 2020). The release of hydrophobic drugs from o/w emulsion involves the partitioning/diffusion of drugs from the oil core to the intermediate surfactant layer, followed by release into the aqueous phase, hence shows sustained release behaviour (Youssef et al., 2021; Youssef et al., 2022). A similar sustained release profile was reported for the travoprost-loaded ophthalmic NEs (Ismail et al., 2020), ciprofloxacin NEs (Youssef et al., 2021), moxifloxacin NEs (Youssef et al., 2022), and prednisolone NEs (Attia et al., 2024). Since SH-17023 is highly lipophilic (log P = 5.28), the maximum concentration of the drug is available at the oil core of NEs. Among various kinetic models, the Korsmeyer-Peppas model showed the highest regression coefficient (R2 = 0.808) compared to the other kinetic models. The release exponent or “n” obtained from the Korsemeyer-Peppas model represents the mechanism of drug release. A value of n < 0.43 represents Fickian diffusion, within 0.43–0.85 represents anomalous diffusion, 0.89 represents Case II transport, whereas a value >0.85 represents super Case II transport (Kassem et al., 2022). The value of n for the optimized NEs was found to be 0.770, representing anomalous diffusion, which is mainly mediated by more than one mechanism (Kassem et al., 2022). Anomalous diffusion was previously reported for ophthalmic NEs (Kassem et al., 2022). The anomalous diffusion of the NEs is influenced by globule size, size distribution, surfactants, and viscosity.

Ex vivo ocular permeation

Corneal permeability impacts on the ocular bioavailability of topically administered ophthalmic drugs. The ex vivo corneal permeation of optimized NE across the goat cornea was compared with the drug solution to investigate permeation enhancement due to the topical NE formulation. The LC-MS chromatograms of SH-17023 from solution and optimized NE at 4, 24, 48, and 72 h are shown in Fig. S8–11. An increase in the area of SH-17023 was observed in the case of optimized NEs compared to the solution, reflecting the enhanced permeation of NEs across the goat cornea. Fig. 6b depicts the cumulative amount of drug permeated per unit area of cornea from the drug solution and optimized NEs. The NEs showed significantly higher (p < 0.05) amount of drug permeation across the cornea compared to the drug solution. Optimum hydrophilicity and lipophilicity are required to achieve ocular permeability and bioavailability (Ismail et al., 2020). In the case of NEs, due to the presence of oil and surfactants/co-surfactants and maintenance of optimum hydrophilic-lipophilic balance, the permeation was improved. A recent study reported improved ocular bioavailability with a highly lipophilic drug, travoprost (log P = 4.6), via NE formulation (Ismail et al., 2020).

X-Ray diffraction

The XRD study of NEs allows evaluation of the physical state/crystallinity of the drug, the presence of surface-adsorbed drugs, drug-excipient interactions, and the effective loading of the drug into the oil globules of NEs. A halo XRD diffraction pattern represents an amorphous nature, whereas sharp diffractions indicate crystallinity. Any shifting of the diffraction peaks represents a possible drug-excipient interaction. The XRD results of NEs and their excipients are shown in Fig. 6d. The XRD pattern of rose oil, Labrasol®, Tween® 80, and Transcutol® P showed halo diffraction patterns with very small notches at 2θ of 16.62° and 13.05°, representing an amorphous nature. SH-17023 also displayed a halo diffraction pattern, with very small diffractions at 2θ of 31.85° and 45.41° representing the amorphous state. The physical mixture of all formulation components showed superimposed peaks of all the diffractions, representing compatibility among the drug and excipients. The optimized NE demonstrated similar diffraction to that of blank NE without any small diffractions of SH-17023 (31.85° and 45.41°), representing successful loading of drugs into the core of the emulsion globules, absence of surface adsorbed drugs, and retention of the amorphous state of the drug in the optimized NEs.

Stability studies

The impact of temperature on the stability/thermodynamic stability of NEs was evaluated by heating-cooling and freeze-thaw stability studies, whereas the resistance of NEs towards sedimentation and phase separation was observed by the centrifugation study (Mohapatra et al., 2024). The optimized NE showed no sign of change in clarity, drug precipitation, cracking, creaming, or phase separation at the end of the heating-cooling and centrifugal stability study. However, it showed temporary turbidity during exposure to the freezing cycle and recovered after exposure to room temperature during the thaw cycle. Due to the change in solubility at low temperature (−20 °C) and ice formation of the external aqueous phase, it showed the signs of instability after the freeze-thaw cycle. Similar stability outcomes for NEs loaded with acyclovir were reported (Mahboobian et al., 2020). The NE showed stability under the centrifugation cycle. However, it demonstrated turbidity when stored at −21 °C and 4 °C but recovered clarity when kept at room temperature (25 °C) and 40 °C. The temporary appearance of turbidity was reported to be due to the lower drug solubility at lower temperature and coagulation of oil droplets or interfacial surface crystallization (Mahboobian et al., 2020). Further, no significant change in the Zavg, PDI, and zeta potential was observed at the end of heating-cooling (Zavg = 32.036 ± 0.13 nm, PDI = 0.251 ± 0.005, and zeta potential = −28.566 ± 1.167 mV) and centrifugation studies (Zavg = 31.93 ± 0.024 nm, PDI = 0.25 ± 0.005, and zeta potential = −30.033 ± 0.518 mV). In contrast, the globule size (749.3 ± 2.174) and PDI (0.259 ± 0.019) were increased after the freeze-thaw cycle with a substantial decrease in zeta potential (−12.133 ± 0.659 mV), reflecting possible instability under such storage conditions. The percentage of drug content was 87.865 ± 3.759% and 92.859 ± 10.025% after heating-cooling cycle and freeze-thaw cycle, respectively. The retention of the higher drug content reflects the maintenance of drug integrity under such storage conditions. The irritation potential of the developed NEs may be further evaluated in rabbit or other large animal experimental models.

Slit-lamp biomicroscopy, fundoscopy, SD-OCT, and FA

The slit-lamp biomicroscopy results (Fig. S12) of all experimental groups before starting the ocular irritation studies reflected healthy eyes without any sign of ocular abnormalities. The wide beam slit lamp biomicroscopy of the blank NEs and optimized NE-treated mouse eyes after 7 days of twice-daily administration (b.i.d) reflected no signs of inflammation of conjunctiva, cornea, and eyelid, whereas the narrow beam examination revealed the absence of corneal swelling, corneal inflammation, changes in corneal surface smoothness, and clarity of aqueous humor (Fig. S13). The anterior chamber was found to be normal, with unaltered cornea, healthy epithelia, free from surface abrasion and swelling, clear aqueous humor (absence of flare or cells), and intact lens. The biomicroscopy of the NE-treated eye (right eye) was similar to that of the untreated left eye of the same experimental subject and to the negative control/isotonic saline-treated eyes (0.9% NaCl). Further, the results are similar to the slit-lamp biomicroscopy results of day 0 (before starting the irritation studies) (Fig. S12). In contrast, the wide beam slit lamp biomicroscopy of the positive control (1 N NaOH) treated eyes showed a high degree of inflammation of the cornea, conjunctiva, and eyelid, and the narrow beam reflected corneal swelling, inflammation, surface roughness of the cornea, and clouding of aqueous humour (Fig. S13).

Topical application of sodium fluorescein solution evaluates the corneal surface defect using slit-lamp biomicroscopy (Yang et al., 2022). The anterior segments of the mouse eyes under blue light (fluorescein-stained eyes) photomicrographs at the end of the study revealed no sign of edema, congestion, discharge, or abnormalities in the cornea or conjunctiva, reflecting excellent tolerability of the optimized NE (Yang et al., 2022). The anterior segment of NE-treated mice showed a similar structure to that of the untreated left eye of the same experimental mouse, and the negative control (0.9% NaCl) treated mouse eyes. However, the standard irritant NaOH showed higher fluorescein uptake (compared to other treatments) due to corneal surface epithelial erosion and ocular abnormalities, and disruption of corneal barrier function, which reflected severe ocular irritation (Fig. S13).

The fundoscopy, corneal OCT, and retinal OCT results of the experimental mice eyes before starting the ocular irritation studies represented an intact ocular structure without a visible sign of abnormalities (Fig. S14). The fundoscopy results of mice eyes following repeated topical application of optimized NE and blank NE up to 7 days revealed a healthy retina with uniform retinal fibers, normal vasculature (uniformly organized and with expected branching pattern) and healthy optic nerve. It also demonstrated no signs of exudates, retinal hemorrhage, inflammatory lesions, or alterations in pigmentation. The fundoscopy results are comparable to the untreated control eyes of the same experimental mice and isotonic saline-treated eyes, demonstrating the retention of retinal integrity and non-toxic nature of the ophthalmic NEs. In contrast, the standard irritant (1 N NaOH) treated eyes demonstrated clouding of the fundus image, ocular inflammation, corneal redness, and narrowing of the eyes/pupil (Fig. 8). The NE-treated eyes showed no signs of corneal swelling, surface erosion, central or limbal neovascularization, hyphema, or cataract. In contrast, the standard irritant (1 N NaOH) induced significant pathology (Fig. 9). It caused corneal epithelial erosion and coagulative necrosis involving the squamous, polygonal, and basal cell layers, along with destruction of Bowman’s layer. The corneal stroma showed infiltration of polymorphonuclear leukocytes and exhibited both central and limbal neovascularization. Further, the detachment of Descemet’s membrane, endothelial hyperplasia, and associated neovascularization were also observed. The iris exhibited fragmentation, blending with endothelial cells, and damage to deeper structures of the anterior segment. The anterior chamber (aqueous humor) showed hyphema, profuse central neovascularization, and infiltration of acute inflammatory cells. The lens exhibited capsular thickening with subcapsular anterior epithelial hyperplasia, and some lens fiber cells lost their elongated architecture and became enlarged. In addition, focal lens dislocation and capsular rupture were observed, with the lens being displaced towards the cornea. However, the posterior chamber (vitreous humour), sclera, choroid, and retina were unremarkable in all cases. The overall histopathological outcomes of 1 N NaOH-treated mice eyes demonstrated noticeable ocular irritation and toxicity, in contrast to the NE formulations.

ATR-FTIR study for NE-cornea interaction evaluation and systemic effects

ATR-FTIR spectroscopy allows the investigation of the interaction of various treatments with the corneal tissue (Zhang and Aksan, 2010). Since the major components of the cornea are collagen and water, a change in the IR intensity or shifting of peaks represents protein denaturation and potential ocular interaction (Porto et al., 2024; Zhang and Aksan, 2010). The results of FTIR of the treated cornea of C57BL/6J mice eyes are shown in Fig. 10. A broad peak between 3600 and 3000 cm−1 is ascribed to -OH stretching of water (Porto et al., 2024; Zhang and Aksan, 2010). The peaks at 2925.23 cm−1 and 2855.67 cm−1 are ascribed to the –CH2 or –CH3 stretching bands of the lipid membrane. The peaks at 1642.27 cm−1 and 1546.68 cm−1 are ascribed to the CO stretching of collagen amide I and the NH2 bending of amide II, respectively (Porto et al., 2024). A very weak vibrational peak at 1248.36 cm−1 is ascribed to collagen amide III C—N stretching coupled with N—H bending (Zhang and Aksan, 2010). A significant decrease in the intensity of the amide II peak at 1546.68 cm−1 was noticed in the cornea of 1 N NaOH-treated eyes without any shifting of peaks, reflecting protein denaturation and potential ocular interaction. The NaOH causes alkali burn, leading to denaturation of protein, disruption of collagen, and saponification of lipid, which was noticed by decreased intensity of the FTIR spectrum. In contrast, no significant frequency shift or intensity change of the FTIR spectra was observed in the cornea that received various other treatments (0.9% NaCl, blank NE, and optimized NE), and the spectra are similar to that of the untreated control eye (left eye), reflecting the retention of corneal integrity without any degradation of corneal protein or potential ocular interaction. Therapeutic effect of SH-17023 NE on L-CNV

The murine L-CNV model is widely used to mimic neovascularization in nAMD (Chobisa et al., 2024; Gong et al., 2015). This model uses an argon laser that ruptures a specific point in the interface of Bruch’s membrane-choroid complex, which induces angiogenesis from the choroid region into the subretinal space, mimicking clinical features of human nAMD (Chobisa et al., 2024; Gong et al., 2015). The therapeutic effect of treatments was evaluated on the L-CNV model up to 7 days via fundoscopy, OCT, FA, and ex vivo vasculature staining (Fig. 11 and Fig. 12) (Chobisa et al., 2024; Gong et al., 2015). C57BL/6J mice of both sexes were used in the L-CNV experiment, as pigmented mouse eyes absorb laser energy and respond consistently to laser photocoagulation in the eyes (Gong et al., 2015). Fundoscopy and OCT

Fundoscopy includes visualization of the interior ocular surface, including retina, its vasculature, optic disc, and posterior pole, whereas OCT is a non-invasive ocular imaging technique that captures high-resolution cross-sectional images of retina and L-CNV lesions, which demonstrate the extent of choroidal neovascularization (Chobisa et al., 2024). The lesions of various treatment groups were monitored to study the in vivo CNV progression by fundoscopy and OCT after 7 days of laser challenge. After 7 days of treatment, the optimized NE showed a significant reduction of OCT-derived lesion volume (p < 0.05) compared to the blank NE group (Fig. 11b).

Fluorescein angiography

Fundus FA revealed vascular leakage of the CNV lesions. The representative FA photomicrographs of various groups are shown in Fig. 11a. Qualitatively, the FA leakage of the optimized NE-treated group is smaller (low fluorescence or almost no fluorescence) compared to the blank NEs-treated group (saturated fluorescence). In contrast, the sham group showed no FA leakage due to the absence of a laser burn. In the semi-quantitative score of FA lesion severity, in terms of lesion grades (grade 0–3) the optimized NE-treated group showed a relatively low percentage of grade 2 and grade 3 lesions and a high percentage of grade 0 and grade 1 lesions compared to the blank NE group (Fig. 11c).

Ex vivo immunostaining and lesion volume quantification

The extent of neovascularization was further investigated in ex vivo choroidal flat mounts of CNV lesions using GS-IB4 as a vasculature marker (Fig. 12). This provides a complementary evaluation technique for quantifying choroidal lesion volumes from z-stacked images. Representative photomicrographs of choroidal flat mounts stained with DAPI and isolectin at 5× magnification and at 20× magnification are shown in Fig. 12a and Fig. 12b, respectively. The quantitative lesion volumes for various treatments, calculated from Z-stacked images of lesions stained with a vasculature marker, are shown in Fig. 12c. The lesions in the blank NE groups showed typical L-CNV morphology (Fig. 12a and Fig. 12b). The mean lesion volume of the optimized NE-treated group showed significantly lower lesion volume (p < 0.05) compared to the blank NE group, which is consistent with the results of OCT quantification. Considering the lesion volume quantification via OCT, ex vivo vasculature staining, and semi-quantitative lesion grading using FA, the optimized NE demonstrated a significant reduction in CNV volume of >45%, without ocular irritation or toxicity.

Earlier reports from our lab showed that SH-17023 inhibits FECH and causes PPIX accumulation in Human Retinal Endothelial Cells (HRECs). The compou also demonstrated in vitro anti-angiogenic activity through inhibition of HREC proliferation, migration, and tube formation. Further, it is verified for in vivo therapeutic activity in the L-CNV model via intravitreal injection (Sishtla et al., 2022). However, the intravitreal injections cause significant patient and health system burden and access challenges. By avoiding the challenges of posterior ocular drug delivery, the NE formulation developed here demonstrated a successful therapeutic option that reduces dependence on intravitreal injections, thereby having potential for improving patient compliance and quality of life plus enhancing care in resource-limited settings where intravitreal injection access may be difficult. Compared to the simple eye drops (drug solution), the optimized NEs represented enhanced trans corneal permeation and sustained drug release, representing their suitability for ophthalmic drug delivery for the effective treatment of CNV.

Conclusion

Ophthalmic NEs loaded with a novel FECH inhibitor were developed for the treatment of CNV. The NEs were prepared by the spontaneous emulsification method and optimized using QbD-based D-optimal mixture design for globule size, polydispersity, and loading capacity. The formulation demonstrated nanometric globule size, with narrow size distribution, and excellent loading capacity. Further, it demonstrated excellent pharmaceutical properties, sustained drug release, drug-excipient compatibility, and stability. It demonstrated enhanced ex vivo ocular permeability compared to the drug solution. Finally, it demonstrated excellent in vivo ocular tolerability and therapeutic activity in the L-CNV murine model without significant toxicity or irritation. The topical NEs of novel FECH inhibitor (SH-17023) have promise for further development as an adjunct or alternative to anti-VEGF therapies for CNV.
