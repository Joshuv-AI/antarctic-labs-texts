Blocking interleukin 33 reduces joint, systemic, and lung inflammatory responses in the combined collagen-induced arthritis-inhalant endotoxin exposure model
Authors: Jill A Poole, Jacob L Larsen, Geoffrey M Thiele, Todd A Wyatt, Amy J Nelson, Michael J Duryee, Angela M Gleason, Oliver W Schanze, Aaron D Schwab, John D Dickinson, Deanna Mosley, Michael Lush, Han-Jun Wang, E Suzanne Cohen, Bryant R England, Ted R Mikuls
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTherapies capable of resolving rheumatoid arthritis (RA)-associated lung disease, particularly in the setting of environmental exposures, are lacking. This study sought to determine whether blocking interleukin (IL)-33 in a murine model of RA-associated lung disease induced by combined collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) and repeated inhalant lipopolysaccharide (LPS) exposures could be advantageous. Arthritis prone male DBA/1J mice received CIA (vs saline) injections (days 1 and 21) plus intranasal LPS (100 ng) or saline daily for 4 weeks and were subsequently treated with 5 doses of anti-IL-33 (10 mg/kg) or isotype over 11 days. Anti-IL-33 therapy significantly (P < .05) mitigated several CIA+LPS-induced effects. Anti-IL-33 reduced arthritis inflammatory score by 76% and serum acute phase reactant pentraxin-2 by 70% and reversed weight loss induced by CIA+LPS coexposure. Anti-IL-33 reduced CIA+LPS-induced antimalondialdehyde acetaldehyde but not anticitrullinated autoantibodies in serum and reduced serum levels of inflammatory mediators. Lung cell infiltrates induced by coexposure, including neutrophils, activated lung macrophages, transitional monocytes-macrophages, monocytic-like cells, and dendritic cells, were reduced with anti-IL-33. Anti-IL-33 also reduced CIA+LPS-induced lung levels of chemokines involved in monocyte-macrophage recruitment and fibrotic/repair mediators collagen deposition, IL-33 expression, inflammatory CC motif chemokine receptor 2+ monocytes/macrophages, and airway Muc5b immunostaining induced by CIA+LPS were reduced with anti-IL-33. CIA+LPS-induced lung expression of post-translational modified autoantigens including citrullinated and malondialdehyde acetaldehyde and its common substrate, vimentin, was reduced with anti-IL-33 treatment. Blocking IL-33 was beneficial in hastening lung recovery after establishment of disease by coexposure to CIA+LPS and potentially represents a novel therapeutic approach in RA-associated lung disease.Significance StatementBlocking the interleukin (IL)-33 signaling pathway after the establishment of, but not during the induction of, rheumatoid arthritis (RA)-associated lung disease, as modeled by coexposure of collagen-induced arthritis with repetitive inhalant endotoxin, demonstrated beneficial consequences in reducing inflammatory joint, systemic, and lung disease. Treatment with anti-IL-33 agents, particularly in men with a history of environmental exposures, may represent a novel approach to reduce disease burden in humans with established RA-lung disease, underscoring an important role for the IL-33 pathway.Key words: Lung, Autoimmunity, Arthritis, Interleukin 33, Repair, Inflammation

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a devastating autoimmune disease frequently associated with a number of extra-articular manifestations, including respiratory diseases such as interstitial lung disease (ILD).1 Although subclinical lung disease affects up to 40% of individuals with RA, clinically relevant ILD affects up to 10% of patients, and respiratory-related diseases are among the leading causes of mortality in men and women with RA.2, 3, 4 Whereas RA is more common in women, ILD is substantially more common among men with RA. Treatment options for RA-ILD are limited. Although newer therapeutic agents, including nintedanib, pirfenidone, and nerandomilast, have demonstrated value in slowing lung function decline in progressive forms of ILD, including RA-ILD, there remains concern about modest efficacy for pulmonary manifestations, lack of efficacy for extrapulmonary manifestations, and tolerability.5, 6, 7 Thus, there is a need for more efficacious and better-tolerated agents to treat, slow, or even reverse the progression of RA-associated lung disease.

The pathogenesis of RA-ILD is multifactorial with roles for autoimmunity, dysregulated inflammatory and fibrotic pathways, exposures to various environmental and occupational agents, and genetic factors.2,8,9 These environmental and occupational agents include tobacco smoke, air pollutants, military-related exposures, agriculture, textile, silica, construction, and more.9 The importance of these exposures in driving RA/RA-ILD has been best described among men, but not among women.10 Because endotoxin is a key inflammatory agent common among many of these exposures,11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 it is often used to model environmental/occupational exposures. Indeed, modeling strategies combining repetitive inhalant exposures, including lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and organic dust extracts in the setting of arthritis (collagen-induced arthritis, CIA), have demonstrated several features resembling human RA-ILD.17, 18, 19, 20 Features include potentiation of arthritis, increased lung cell infiltrates (particularly activated monocytes/macrophages), accumulation of inflammatory and repair/profibrosis mediators with enhanced expression of modified lung autoantigens (ie, citrullinated and malondialdehyde acetaldehyde [MAA]-modified proteins), and systemic autoantibody responses. This animal modeling strategy has also demonstrated that male mice are profoundly more susceptible to most effects of coexposure as compared with female mice,18,20 which was subsequently shown to be testosterone dependent.17 This animal model provides an important preclinical platform to explore potential therapeutic strategies.

The alarmin interleukin (IL)-33 pathway represents a potential therapeutic target, as it plays a key role in the pathogenesis of lung diseases by promoting tissue remodeling. Anti-IL-33 approaches in humans have shown efficacy in reducing exacerbations in asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.21 Its role in pulmonary fibrotic diseases and autoimmunity is increasingly recognized as complex based upon it dual role in inflammation and tissue repair. Lung tissue IL-33 expression is increased in patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis22,23 and those with RA-associated lung disease.18 Correspondingly, the combined coexposure (eg, CIA+LPS) animal studies demonstrated increased lung IL-33.17, 18, 19 Although lung IL-33 is also increased in bleomycin (BLM)-induced fibrosis, the benefits of IL-33 blockade are context-, dose-, and timing-dependent, as inhibition is not advantageous when administered 14–28 days after a single bleomycin injury, but is beneficial when given early (0–14 days) after a single exposure or during the fibrotic phase of repeated bleomycin administration.24, 25, 26 Likewise, a recent systematic review underscores the complexity of IL-33 in RA (joint) disease pathogenesis that are dependent on stage, immune milieu, and cellular context.27 Its potential therapeutic role in RA-lung disease remains unknown. The objectives of this study were to leverage the CIA+LPS coexposure model to investigate whether inhibition of the IL-33 pathway would attenuate severity of joint, systemic, and lung disease, thus providing support for future clinical translation.

Materials and methods

Animal exposure model

Modifications of the established combined CIA with repeated intranasal LPS exposure model17,18 were used to test the effects of anti-IL-33 treatment. Mice were randomized to 1 of 3 experimental groups, including (1) Sham (saline injection/saline inhalation), (2) CIA+LPS (CIA injection/LPS inhalation) with isotype treatment, and (3) CIA+LPS with anti-IL-33 treatment (experimental schematic, Fig. 1A). The arthritis prone DBA/1J male mice (age 6–8 weeks) were purchased from Inotiv and allowed to acclimate for 1 week before initiation of experiments. Male mice were used for all studies based upon established sex-based differences in lung disease susceptibility in this arthritis-lung disease model with female mice profoundly less susceptible to development of lung disease in the setting of arthritis induction17; implications for sex-based generalizability are addressed in the Discussion. CIA was induced as per the Chondrex protocol (Chondrex, Inc).20 Airway inflammation was induced with administration of repetitive intranasal LPS inhalation.17 Under light sedation with isoflurane, mice received 100 ng of LPS from gram-negative Escherichia coli (O55:B5; Sigma) in 50 μL of sterile saline or saline alone daily for 4 weeks (weekends excluded).

After the 4-week induction of arthritis-lung disease, animals were subsequently treated with 5 doses of anti-IL-33 (ST2-IL1RAP-Fc, 10 mg/kg, i.p.) or isotype control (NIP228 hIgG1, 10 mg/kg) over 11 days (both reagents gifted from AstraZeneca). NIP228 hIgG1 is a human IgG1 kappa monoclonal antibody against 4-hydroxy-3-iodo-5-nitrophenylacetic acid, which is used as a negative control for protein fusion studies.28 Anti-IL-33 is a fusion protein that combines the extracellular domains of the IL-33 receptor (ST2), its coreceptor IL-1 receptor accessory protein and a human IgG1 Fc domain into a single fusion protein based upon work by Ali et al,29 a reagent generated by AstraZeneca. This fusion protein binds IL-33 (acting as a decoy) to inhibit IL-33 signaling. The 10 mg/kg dose at 3 times a week was selected to provide maximal IL-33 suppression. Dose response studies were performed in acute Alternaria challenge studies, and the 10 mg/kg dose was found to provide maximal inhibition (data not shown). Limited pharmacokinetic analysis showed that the anti-IL-33 reached the lung (bronchoalveolar lavage fluid [BALF]) rapidly and levels were sustained over 48 hours (data not shown). Moreover, the 10 mg/kg, 3× weekly regimen, of similar tool reagents (ST2-Fc fusion protein) has been used successfully in several chronic models.25,30,31 Thus, although dose response analysis was not possible in the CIA+LPS model here because of study size limitations, the dosing schedule used in this study was selected to achieve maximal inhibition based on previous work in a range of acute and chronic models. Mice were euthanized 1 day after the final treatment with anti-IL-33 or isotype control by isoflurane, followed by exsanguination (right axillary blood collection). In separate studies, mice were pretreated with anti-IL-33 versus isotype control × 2 doses before initiation of the CIA+LPS exposure model with anti-IL-33 or isotype control treatment continued 3 times weekly during the CIA+LPS exposure modeling, followed by 2 doses upon completion (“pre-, during, and post”-CIA+LPS modeling). In all studies, no respiratory distress, signs of stress, or significant weight loss (defined as >20%) were observed throughout the exposure period.

All animal studies are detailed in accordance with the Animal Research Reporting In Vivo Experiments guidelines (https:arriveguidelines.org/). Animal procedures were approved by the University of Nebraska Medical Center Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol #19-043-05-FC) and were in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines for the use of rodents.

Arthritis evaluation and collagen deposition

A nonblinded semiquantitative arthritis scoring system (Chondrex; www.chondrex.com) was applied based on average foot examination per mouse by 2 trained technicians, with a score ranging from 0 (no inflammation) to 4 (erythema and severe swelling encompassing ankle, foot, and digits).

In a subset of animals, formalin-fixed ankles were decalcified using 15% EDTA/0.5% paraformaldehyde in PBS at pH 8.0 for 4 weeks, changing buffer 3 days a week.32 After decalcification, joints were paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with modified Masson’s trichrome to quantify collagen deposition. Slides were scanned with an iScan Coreo Au (Ventana) slide scanner by the institution’s tissue sciences facility and converted into digital format. Quantification of collagen was done using ImageJ software (NIH) after deconvolution and thresholding using methods previously described.33

Systemic markers including serum pentraxin-2, CIT and MAA autoantibodies, and targeted serum proteomics

Serum was processed as previously described.20 Serum pentraxin-2 (MPTX20) levels were assessed using an ELISA kit (R&D Systems), according to manufacturer instructions (minimal detection difference of 0.159 ng/mL). Anti-citrullinated (CIT) and anti-MAA (IgG) antibodies were quantified using ELISA, with data presented as relative units of the specific isotype detected.18 Human serum albumin was used as the substrate antigen and modified with MAA or CIT for antibody measurement. Antibodies to native human serum albumin were subtracted from antibodies recognizing modified antigens to eliminate background reactivity to native protein. Targeted proteomic serum analysis was performed based on Proximity Extension Assay technology, where oligonucleotide-labeled antibody probe pairs bind to their respective target protein present in the sample using the Olink Target 48 Mouse Inflammation panel (Olink Proteomics AB). Samples were run and analyzed by an Olink service provider, Washington University St. Louis Genome and Technology Access Center. The Olink Target 48 panel provides absolute quantification (pg/mL) of protein concentration of 43 biomarkers using a high-resolution calibrator curve with a single-run-time calibrator to adjust the pre-established calibration curve for the specific run.

Lavage fluid cells and lung homogenate analyses

BALF was collected using 3 × 1 mL of PBS (pH, 7.2). Total BALF cell counts from pooled lavages were enumerated using a BioRad TC 20 cell counter. Differential cell counts were determined from cytospin-prepared slides (Wescor Cytopro 7620 Cytocentrifuge) with Diff-Quick (Siemens). Cell-free BALF from the first lavage fraction was evaluated for mediators. After BALF isolation and removal of blood from the pulmonary vasculature, right lobe homogenates were prepared.17 BALF and lung homogenates were analyzed for proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory, profibrotic, and wound repair mediators by ELISA using an admixture of custom multiplex assays by Luminex and single assay ELISA kits (R&D Systems and Abcam). Analytes quantified (and thresholds for detection in pg/mL) included IL-6 (1.6), IL-10 (1.97), IL-33 (6.85), CCL2 (0.304), CCL4 (476.0), CCL7 (1.53), CCL8 (5.21), CCL22 (16.5), CXCL2 (1.5), CXCL10 (65.8), fibronectin (0.48 ng/mL), matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-3 (125), MMP-8 (53), MMP-9 (78.1), tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase (TIMP)-1 (31.3), endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS/NOS3) (5.58), and inducible NOS (iNOS/NOS2) (18.1).

Flow cytometry analysis

Total lung cells were enumerated using BioRad TC 20 cell counter after tissue dissociation of right lung lobes.17 Single-cell suspensions were stained with LIVE/DEAD Fixable Dead Cell stain (Invitrogen) and incubated with CD16/32 (Fc Block, Biolegend) to minimize nonspecific antibody staining. Cells were simultaneously stained with antibodies (BD Biosciences, unless otherwise stated) against mouse cell surface markers including rat anti-mouse CD45 (clone:30-F11), CD11b (M1/70), Ly6G (1A8), Ly6C (HK1.4, BioLegend), F4/80 (T45-2342), CD103 (2E7, Invitrogen), CD4 (RM4-5), C8a (53-6.7), CD19 (1D3, Invitrogen), major histocompatibility complex (MHC) Class II (I-A/I-E) (M5/114.15.2, Cell Signaling Technology), hamster anti-mouse CD3e (145-2C11), or Armenian Hamster anti-Mouse CD11c (N418, Invitrogen). Cells were acquired on a BD LSRII Yellow/Green cytometer configured with 355-, 405-, 488-, 561-, and 633-nm lasers. Postacquisition, data were exported and stored using the flow cytometry standard 3.1 format and analyzed using FlowJo software version 10.10 (FlowJo).

The gating strategy for identification of nondebris, singlets, and live CD45+ cells is depicted in Supplemental Fig. 1. Lung immune cells were gated and defined as neutrophils (CD11c−Ly6G+Ly6C+), CD3+CD4+ T cells, CD3+CD8+ T cells, and CD19+ B cells, as previously reported.17,34,35 Monocyte, macrophage (Mϕ), and dendritic cell (DC) populations were first categorized as resting alveolar macrophages (CD11c+F4/80hiCD11bloMHC Class IIlo), activated lung Mϕ (CD11chiF4/80hiCD11bhiMHC Class IIhi), transitional lung monocyte-Mϕ (CD11c+CD11bhiF480loCD103−), monocytic-like cells (CD11c−CD11bhiCD103−Ly6Chi), and DCs (CD11cvarCD11b+F480loCD103+MHC Class IIhi) as previously reported (see also Supplemental Fig. 2).36, 37, 38 Transitional monocyte-Mϕ populations were further analyzed by MHC Class II expression, and DCs were further analyzed by CD11c expression, as reported by us and others.36, 37, 38 Lung cell populations were quantified by absolute counts by normalizing gated percentages to total cell numbers (multiplying percent gated of CD45+ cells by total lung cells). Total CD45+ cell recovery was comparable across groups, indicating that reductions in specific subsets reflect absolute decreases rather than shifts in relative proportions.

Lung histopathology and autoantigens

Left lung lobes were excised and inflated to 15 cm H2O pressure with 10% formalin (Fisher Scientific) for 24 hours to preserve pulmonary architecture.17 Lungs were processed and cut (4–5 μM) at midpoint sections to include regions of both large and small airways as well as blood vessels. Tissues were stained for H&E, modified Masson’s Trichrome and Sirius Red (for collagen accumulation assessment), or preserved for subsequent immunohistochemistry by the Tissue Science Core Facility in the Department of Pathology and Microbiology as previously described.17 H&E-stained slides of the entire lung section (1-section) from each animal were reviewed and semiquantitatively scored by an expert pathologist blinded to experimental conditions.17,39 This scoring system (scored 0–4) evaluates the spectrum of inflammatory changes for alveolar and bronchiolar compartments with higher scores indicating greater inflammation.17,39

The CC motif chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2) is a critical facilitator of monocyte recruitment and activation that, upon reaching inflamed tissue, can differentiate into macrophages.40,41 To quantify CCR2+ inflammatory monocytes/macrophages, lung sections were stained with anti-CCR2 (1:100, ab273050 Abcam) and crossabsorbed with donkey anti-rabbit AlexaFluor488 (A21206, Lot#2156521, Invitrogen) at 1:100 dilution as secondary antibodies. To quantify IL-33 expression, mouse anti-IL-33 (AF3626, 2.5 μg/100 μL; R&D Systems), and donkey anti-goat (AlexFluorPlus555, A32816) at 1:100 dilutions were used as secondary antibodies. Antibody- and lectin-specific staining for mucins was performed as previously described.42,43 Anti-Lectin-UEA-1 (#RL 1061, Vector Labs) conjugated with a fluorophore that reacts at 495 nm (1:500 dilution) was used for staining Muc5ac.43,44 A mouse anti-Muc5b (3E1, courtesy of Dr Burton F. Dickey at MD Anderson Cancer Center) antibody (1:500 dilution) was used to stain for Muc5b. Fluorophore-labeled donkey secondary antibody (1:1000 dilution) (#A31570, Invitrogen) was applied. Slides were mounted with VECTASHIELD Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole; to identify nuclei) (Cat#H-1200, Vector Laboratories) and visualized under a Zeiss fluorescent microscope (Zeiss Observer.Z1 Zeiss). Immunofluorescent images were then captured using an Axio Observer Z.1 microscope and analyzed with Zen software (Zeiss). Expression of molecules from mouse airways was quantified with a common signal threshold with multiple fields at 40× magnification, using ImageJ (v2.0) software (NIH,) as previously described.18,42,43,45 Intracellular Muc5b mucin was quantified with common signal threshold of all microscopic fields by volume density calculations as previously described.42,46

Vimentin is an extracellular matrix protein that is also targeted by post-translational modification generated during the process of inflammation and previously demonstrated to be increased with CIA+LPS coexposure.17,18 CIT and MAA-modified proteins are post-translationally modified proteins and lung autoantigens that have been increased with CIA+LPS coexposure.17,18 Increased expression and robust colocalization of MAA and CIT with vimentin occurs in the lungs of humans with inflammatory arthritis and lung disease, including individuals with RA-ILD.19,47 Lung sections were stained with Cy5 rabbit anti-vimentin (Bioss), mouse monoclonal anti-peptidyl-citrulline antibody (clone F95 IgMκ, Millipore Sigma), or Zenon AF594 label (Invitrogen) rabbit polyclonal IgG antibody to MAA.19 Detection of the F95 antibody was done using an AF488 AffiniPure donkey anti-mouse IgM, μ chain-specific antibody (Jackson Immunoresearch). DAPI was added, and samples sealed with Fluoromount-G (Southern Biotech). Fluorochromes were detected using a Revolve fluorescence microscope (ECHO). Multiple images of the lung parenchyma per mouse were captured at 20× magnification, and antigen expression was quantified by integrated density using ImageJ FIJI plugin.18,19

Noninvasive functional respiratory parameters

In separate subset studies, animals were acclimated to whole body plethysmography (WBP) chambers for 1 hour over 3 days before data collection. Animals were weighed and placed in the WBP chamber for a 30-minute acclimation period, followed by a 1-hour recording session. Data were recorded using DSI FinePointe WBP software (Data Sciences International). Parameters included respiratory rate, tidal volume, minute volume, expiratory flow at 50% TV, peak inspiratory flow, peak expiratory flow, relative time to peak expiratory flow, and enhanced pause (Penh).

Statistical analysis

Sample sizes were extrapolated from previous work assessing combined CIA+LPS models.17 The mean (± SD) number of total lung cells was 20.2 × 105 (3.54 × 105) with CIA+LPS coexposure17 and a sample size of n = 5 in each group would achieve 80% power at the 0.05 level of significance to 30% difference between groups. For studies here, there were a total of 10 mice in each of the 3 experimental groups. Sample numbers below the maximum number reflect limitations in available sample quantity.

All data are depicted as mean with SD. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to test for normality among treatment groups. If the assumption of normality was satisfied, a one-way ANOVAwas used or a lognormal ordinary ANOVA used for log-transformed data. For nonparametric data, a Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. The 2-stage Benjamini, Krieger, and Yekutieli procedure was applied using a false discovery rate threshold of 5%.48 All statistical analyses were performed and data figures generated (color-blind safe setting) using GraphPad Prism (version: 10.2.2) software, and statistical significance was accepted at a P value < .05.

Results

Anti-IL-33 treatment improved weight parameter, reduced inflammatory arthritis outcomes, and increased joint collagen deposition following combined modeling of CIA+LPS coexposure

Mice underwent arthritis induction (CIA) with 4 weeks of daily LPS inhalation (CIA+LPS) and then were subsequently treated (i.p.) with 5 doses of anti-IL-33 or isotype control over 11 days with comparison to a sham-treated group (saline injection/saline inhalation) (schematic, Fig. 1A). Weight changes are a critical marker of systemic disease. There was significantly less weight gained with CIA+LPS versus sham upon completion of the 4-week exposure model, but during the postexposure treatment phase, there was increased weight gain with anti-IL-33 treatment but not isotype control (Fig. 1B). At time of necropsy, arthritis inflammatory scores were increased with CIA+LPS versus sham, and there was a 76% reduction in CIA+LPS-induced arthritis inflammatory scores with anti-IL-33 treatment versus isotype (P = .0025) (Fig. 1C). Correspondingly, there was increased collagen deposition in the ankle joint of mice treated with anti-IL-33 (vs isotype) (Fig. 1, D and E) with decreased inflammation, suggesting an increase in tissue repair processes.

Anti-IL-33 treatment reduced serum levels of pentraxin-2 and anti-MAA, but not anti-CIT autoantibody levels, and serum biomarkers of IL-1ß, IL-16, and CCL22 in CIA+LPS coexposed mice

CIA+LPS-induced serum levels of acute phase reactant pentraxin-2 were also significantly reduced with anti-IL-33 treatment versus isotype with mean ± SD levels of 34.5 ± 15.9 versus 114.9 ± 52.2 (P = .02), respectively (Fig. 2). Serum anti-CIT autoantibodies induced by CIA+LPS coexposure were not reduced by anti-IL-33 treatment, but anti-MAA autoantibodies were significantly reduced with anti-IL-33 treatment (P = .007) (Fig. 2). The targeted serum proteomics analysis demonstrated that CIA+LPS coexposure -induced IL-1ß, IL-16, and CCL22 were significantly reduced with anti-IL-33 treatment (vs isotype) (Fig. 2). There were 17 serum proteins significantly induced by CIA+LPS coexposure (vs sham) that were not significantly modulated by anti-IL-33 treatment (ie, CCL2, CCL4, CCL5, CCL12, CXCL1, CXCL9, CXCL11, IL-1⍺, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, IL-27, fibroblast growth factor 21, PDCD1G2, CSF3, CD274, and tumor necrosis factor) (Supplemental Table 1). However, there were trends for decreased serum levels of IL-6 (P = .053) and increased fibroblast growth factor 21 (Fgf21, a hormone that regulates metabolic pathways) (P = .088) with anti-IL-33 (vs isotype) treatment in CIA+LPS coexposed mice. The remainder of the 23 biomarkers were either not detected or did not significantly differ across the 3 treatment groups.

CIA+LPS coexposure-induced lung cell influx was reduced by treatment with anti-IL-33

CIA+LPS-induced influx of BALF total cells, neutrophils, macrophages, and lymphocytes was reduced with anti-IL-33 treatment as compared with isotype control (Fig. 3A). Correspondingly, total cell infiltrates in lung tissues were increased with CIA+LPS coexposure, and anti-IL-33 treatment significantly reduced the numbers of total lung cell infiltrates by 41% as compared with isotype control (P = .0076) (Fig. 3B). Similar patterns were demonstrated for specific cell subpopulations, including activated lung Mϕ (CD11chiF4/80hiCD11bhiMHC Class IIhi), transitional monocyte-Mϕ (CD11c+CD11bhiF480loCD103−), monocytic-like cells (CD11c−CD11bhiCD103−Ly6Chi), DCs (CD11cvarCD11b+F480loCD103+MHC Class IIhi), neutrophils (CD11c−Ly6G+Ly6C+), and CD19+ B cells. In contrast, resting alveolar macrophages (CD11c+F4/80hiCD11bloMHC Class IIlo) were reduced with CIA+LPS coexposure versus sham with no difference between anti-IL-33 and isotype treatment. CIA+LPS also induced CD3+CD4+ T cells, but there was no difference between treatment groups. There was no increase in CD3+CD8+ T cells with CIA+LPS versus sham. As an indicator of activation status of monocytes and macrophages, MHC Class II expression by transitional monocyte-Mϕ and monocytic-like cell subpopulations was analyzed. CIA+LPS coexposure increased the number of MHC Class II-expressing transitional monocyte-Mϕ and monocytic-like cells versus sham, and this MHC Class II high expressing response was reduced with anti-IL-33 treatment versus isotype control for both cellular subpopulations (Table 1). There were no differences in MHC Class II low expressing transitional monocyte-Mϕ and monocytic-like cells across the 3 experimental groups. Moreover, DCs were delineated by CD11c expression, and both CD11c-high expressing “conventional” DCs and CD11c-low expressing “plasmacytoid” DCs were strikingly increased with CIA+LPS coexposure (3.8-fold and 9.6-fold increase, respectively) versus sham. This increase was also significantly reduced with anti-IL-33 treatment (Table 1).

Anti-IL-33 treatment reduced lung mediator levels of fibronectin, extracellular matrix proteins, IL-33, leukocyte chemoattractants, and oxidative stress markers induced following CIA+LPS coexposure

CIA+LPS coexposure increased mediators implicated in lung tissue repair/fibrosis processes, including lung levels of fibronectin, MMP-3, MMP-8, and MMP-9, and TIMP-1 as compared with sham (Fig. 4; Supplemental Fig. 3). Of these mediators, anti-IL-33 treatment reduced levels of BALF/lung tissue fibronectin, and lung tissue MMP-8 and MMP-9, but not MMP-3 or TIMP-1. Consistent with past reports,17, 18, 19 CIA+LPS exposure induced increases in lung levels of IL-33 in these current studies, and importantly, treatment with anti-IL-33 reduced lung levels of IL-33 (Fig. 4). Several leukocyte chemoattractants were also investigated, with results demonstrating that anti-IL-33 treatment reduced CIA+LPS-induced CCL2, CCL8, and CCL22 (chemoattractants important in monocyte/macrophage recruitment), but not CXCL2 and CCL7 (Fig. 4; Supplemental Fig. 3). CIA+LPS exposure also induced increased in lung levels of oxidative stress markers including eNOS/NOS3 and iNOS/NOS2, and these oxidative stress markers were reduced with anti-IL-33 treatment (Fig. 4). CIA+LPS-induced IL-10 levels were not affected by anti-IL-33 treatment (Supplemental Fig. 3).

CIA+LPS coexposure-induced increases in semiquantitative lung inflammatory score that were significantly reduced with anti-IL-33 treatment (P = .021) (Fig. 5B). Anti-IL-33 treatment reduced CIA+LPS coexposure-induced CCR2+ inflammatory cells and IL-33 expression, and Muc5b mucin expression

Lung tissues were also stained for CCR2+ inflammatory monocytes/macrophages to complement the aforementioned flow cytometry studies demonstrating increases in monocyte/macrophage subpopulations with CIA+LPS coexposure that were reduced with anti-IL-33 treatment. Correspondingly, anti-IL-33 treatment strikingly reduced CIA+LPS-induced CCR2+ inflammatory lung monocytes/macrophages by approximately 75% as compared with isotype control-treated mice (Fig. 6, A and B). Next, the reduction in CIA+LPS-induced lung tissue IL-33 protein levels with anti-IL-33 treatment, as demonstrated by ELISA (Fig. 4), was confirmed by immunohistochemistry staining and quantification of IL-33 tissue expression (Fig. 6, A and B). Lung tissues were also stained with lectin UEA-1 to detect Muc5ac or with anti-Muc5b to evaluate airway mucin levels (Fig. 6, A and B). There was low expression of Muc5ac staining with no difference across treatment groups. In contrast, there was a striking increase (10.4-fold increase) in Muc5b staining after CIA+LPS coexposure (isotype control treatment versus sham, P < .0001), and this response was reduced by 51% with anti-IL-33 treatment.

Noninvasive respiratory mechanics across treatment groups demonstrated minimal effects

WBP was performed in separate studies to assess respiratory mechanics. At this time point, there were no significant effects among treatment groups for respiratory rate, tidal volume, minute ventilation, mid-tidal expiratory low, relative time to peak expiratory flow, peak expiratory flow, and peak inspiratory flow (Supplemental Fig. 4). Penh, a marker of altered breathing patterns,49,50 was significantly decreased (P = .0118) in the CIA+LPS coexposure group versus sham, and this response was not significantly (P = .084) reversed with anti-IL-33 treatment.

Anti-IL-33 treatment administered before, during, and after CIA+LPS coexposure modeling demonstrated little to no effect on lung consequences but improved arthritis

In separate experimental studies, mice were treated twice over 4 days with anti-IL-33 (or isotype control) before initiation of CIA+LPS coexposure with continual anti-IL-33 treatments 3 times a week during CIA+LPS exposure × 5 weeks, followed by 2 final anti-IL-33 treatments over 3 days after last LPS exposure, with results summarized in Supplemental Table 2. In these studies, arthritis inflammatory scores were significantly reduced with anti-IL-33 treatment (vs isotype) by 72% (P = .002). Whereas there were also significant reductions in CIA+LPS-induced lung tissue IL-33 and fibronectin levels with anti-IL-33 treatment as compared with isotype control, there were otherwise no significant effects between anti-IL-33 and isotype control for CIA+LPS-induced cellular infiltrates, tissue repair mediators, or lung inflammation.

Discussion

There is a critical need for more effective and better-tolerated agents to treat, slow, or reverse progression of RA-associated lung disease, as current therapeutic options remain limited in efficacy and safety. We demonstrate for the first time that blocking the IL-33 signaling pathway in an established preclinical model of RA-ILD resulted in several beneficial effects for joint, systemic, and lung inflammatory outcomes. Blocking IL-33 in this setting reduced both articular and systemic inflammation (ie, pentraxin-2, IL-1ß, IL-16, and CCL22) and reversed weight loss that accompanies CIA+LPS modeling. IL-33 inhibition led to simultaneous reductions in lung inflammation and fibrosis, characterized by marked reductions in expression of chemoattractant mediators, RA-related autoantigens (CIT and MAA), and circulating anti-MAA autoantibodies. CIA+LPS coexposure also increased intracellular Muc5b (but not Muc5ac) mucin expression, and importantly, IL-33 inhibition reduced coexposure-induced Muc5b expression. This is notable given the central role that Muc5b has been proposed to play in RA-ILD51, 52, 53, 54 and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis pathogenesis.55 Anti-IL-33 treatment did not affect all CIA+LPS-induced responses; there was no difference (or compensatory effect) for the majority of the serum proteins interrogated. There was also no effect when anti-IL-33 was administered “pre, during, and post”-co exposures. These findings suggest that treatment with anti-IL-33 inhibitory agents, particularly in males with a history of environmental exposures, may represent a novel approach to reduce disease burden in humans with established RA-ILD.

IL-33 is a versatile and complex cytokine; it is recognized as an alarmin that senses damage/activation of epithelial or endothelial cells to respond immediately to stimulate type 2 (T2) immune responses. In addition to promoting inflammatory responses, IL-33 simultaneously mediates tissue repair and wound healing with non-T2 and neutrophilic features.56 As both beneficial and detrimental effects of targeting IL-33 have been described across various diseases, careful considerations of the context of specific disease pathology are critical to inform potential therapeutic translation. For example, IL-33-deficient mice develop greater emphysematous changes in the lung with cigarette smoke extract,57 suggesting a protective role for IL-33 in early disease development. Lack of lung IL-33 expression was associated with mortality with COVID-19, whereas high lung IL-33 expression was associated with COVID-19 fibrosis.22 Lung IL-33 expression is consistently elevated in several murine models of fibrosis, including bleomycin (BLM)-, silica-, and CIA+LPS (or CIA+ organic dust extract).18,24,58,59 Moreover, adoptive transfer of IL-33-stimulated macrophages directly to alveoli aggravates fibrosis with BLM,60 and IL-33 potentiates T2-independent BLM fibrosis.61 There are conflicting reports about blocking IL-33 in BLM fibrosis. Initiating anti-IL-33 treatment during the fibrotic stage of acute BLM fibrosis (ie, 2 weeks after a one-time BLM injection) did not affect lung consequences,25 but initiating it between 0–14 days after single BLM injection was beneficial.26 Our findings support targeting IL-33 after establishment of disease and removal of the inhalant exposure as opposed to targeting IL-33 early in disease progression. Our findings are also consistent with an experimental allergic asthma study whereby blocking IL-33/ST2 signaling pathway demonstrated beneficial response during the resolution stage, after the allergen was removed.62 These collective findings suggest that the biological role of IL-33 is highly context- and stage-dependent. Early in disease induction and ongoing inhalant exposure, IL-33 inhibition alone may not be sufficient to counteract the combined effects of multiple proinflammatory mediators. In contrast, once disease is established and the inciting exposure is removed, sustained IL-33 signaling may contribute to persistent pathogenic inflammation and tissue remodeling, rendering IL-33 inhibition therapeutically advantageous.

A key mechanism by which IL-33 mediates lung fibrosis is through recruitment and activation of immune cells, particularly neutrophils and alternatively activated monocytes-macrophages polarized toward tissue remodeling and fibrosis.26,63 Consistent with these effects, anti-IL-33 reduced neutrophils and transitional infiltrating monocytes-macrophages and DCs characterized by high MHC Class II, CD11b, and Ly6C expression, all induced with coexposure. In addition to being overrepresented in human disease,64, 65, 66, 67 recruited and activated lung monocytes-macrophages play a critical role in the development of murine RA-ILD, as depletion of the circulating monocyte reservoir leads to marked attenuation of lung endpoints in the CIA+LPS model.45 Our findings also demonstrated reductions in key monocyte chemoattractants at the lung level with IL-33 blockade, including decreases in CCL2, CCL8, and CCL22. Correspondingly, CCR2, a critical facilitator of monocyte recruitment and activation leading to differentiation of macrophages in settings of inflammation, was strikingly reduced with anti-IL-33 treatment in coexposed mice. These data suggest that IL-33 may act both upstream of monocyte recruitment and as an amplifier of established inflammation in this model. The coordinated reductions in monocyte chemoattractants and CCR2+ myeloid populations with IL-33 blockade are also consistent with a role for IL-33 in regulating epithelial–immune cross-talk that sustains inflammatory cell recruitment during established disease. Thus, anti-IL-33 may represent a viable strategy to reduce inflammatory monocytes-macrophages in RA-ILD to modify disease severity.

Coexposure of CIA+LPS has been consistently associated with the generation of post-translationally modified lung autoantigens, CIT and MAA, colocalizing with vimentin.17,18,45 Anti-IL-33 treatment strikingly reduced their expression, which may be partly explained by corresponding reductions in infiltrating monocytes-macrophages, as these cells are key drivers of lung CIT and MAA expression.45 Notably, CIA+LPS-induced increases in oxidative stress markers eNOS and iNOS were also reduced with anti-IL-33 treatment, suggesting that dampened oxidative stress by anti-IL-33 may also contribute to reduced CIT and MAA generation. Whereas serum autoantibodies were also increased in CIA+LPS coexposure, only anti-MAA (not anti-CIT) autoantibodies were decreased with anti-IL-33 treatment, which may be explained by findings suggesting that anti-CIT antibody (in contrast to nonspecific autoantibody such as anti-MAA) concentrations increase during early stages of RA development but are less sensitive to change once disease is established.68,69 For the first time herein, we show that Muc5b expression is increased with CIA+LPS coexposure modeling, which is highly relevant as the MUC5B promoter variant leads to its increased expression in the lung and represents the strongest genetic risk factor in RA-ILD.52,70,71 Importantly, anti-IL-33 treatment reduced Muc5b mucin expression induced by CIA+LPS. This shared feature of lung Muc5b expression with human disease should be exploited in future studies to further elucidate the mechanistic role of this mucin in disease pathogenesis.

Whereas IL-33 is increased in the synovial fluid of patients with RA (without lung disease),72 this alarmin has been associated with anti-inflammatory properties in the context of arthritis. In CIA-induced arthritis, IL-33 injections during early but not late time points suppressed arthritis.73 There are also reports of no difference in articular inflammation in antigen- or CIA-induced arthritis in IL-33-deficient mice.74 In contrast, others found that mice lacking ST2 demonstrated attenuated CIA-induced arthritis, and IL-33 exacerbated arthritis when IL-33 was administered later (day 21).75 Correspondingly, initiation of anti-IL-33 blocking antibody after onset of CIA-induced arthritis (days 25-27) significantly reduced the severity of CIA and joint damage through downregulation of inflammatory cytokines.76 Our studies support these later studies whereby blocking IL-33 signaling after disease onset is beneficial against arthritis in addition to attenuating detrimental lung findings characteristic of the CIA+LPS model.

Further evidence underpinning the potential importance of IL-33 in RA-ILD comes from a study of ∼3000 US Veterans with RA.77 In this cohort study, there were no associations between serum IL-33 and prevalent ILD at baseline. In contrast, RA patients with serum IL-33 concentrations falling in the highest baseline quartile were significantly less likely to develop incident ILD over follow-up. These observations also highlight a potential role for anti-IL-33 treatment after establishment of lung disease, not as a preventive therapy. The finding that RA in the absence of lung disease is more common among women, but RA-ILD is substantially increased in men, has implicated a possible role of sex hormones. In previous animal studies that recapitulated this observation, castrated (low testosterone state) versus intact male mice demonstrate reduced CIA+LPS-induced lung disease associated with reduced lung IL-33 levels, suggesting that a testosterone dependent pathway may affect IL-33 in the lung.17 Thus, additional studies are warranted to understand these potential specific hormone-signaling and gene-regulating effects related to IL-33. In addition to support provided by its efficacy in this preclinical assessment, the feasibility of clinical translation with anti-IL-33 approaches in RA-ILD is further supported by trials in non-RA populations demonstrating a favorable safety profile.78

There are several limitations to this study. Experiments were conducted only in male mice given increased disease susceptibility in this model, which limits generalizability to females. Although CIA+LPS recapitulates key features of RA-associated lung disease, it does not fully reflect the clinical and biological heterogeneity of human RA-ILD; thus, findings should be viewed for possible mechanistic insights provided. Although we were unable to detect differences in respiratory mechanics, this likely relates to assessments occurring during recovery. Earlier time points are needed to capture peak dysfunction and treatment effects. Morever, to precisely determine effects on lung function, more robust invasive measurements of airway resistance and compliance, as opposed to the noninvasive measurements conducted here, should be investigated. Hydroxyproline-based collagen quantification was not performed, precluding correlations between fibronectin and total collagen content despite histologic evidence of increased deposition. Finally, although the selected dose was based on prior pharmacologic validation,79 the lack of a formal-concentration-response analysis prevents direct linkage between inflammatory reduction and graded target inhibition.

Conclusion

Blocking the IL-33 pathway after the establishment of RA-associated lung disease, particularly in the setting of occupational/environmental airborne hazard exposures, may be a beneficial approach to reduce and potentially reverse adverse lung and arthritis consequences.
