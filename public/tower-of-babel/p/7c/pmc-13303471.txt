Minimally invasive stabilisation of anterior pelvic ring injuries through anterior infix versus percutaneous anterior retrograde pubic screw: a prospective comparative cohort study
Authors: Ahmed Goda El-Hamalawy, Mohamed Abdelmoneim, Mohamed Al-Sayed, Mohamed Youness, Fouad Sadek, Elsayed Kassem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundManagement of unstable anterior pelvic ring fractures has shifted toward minimally invasive fixation techniques. This prospective randomized cohort study compares anterior subcutaneous internal fixator with percutaneous retrograde pubic ramus screw fixation, evaluating radiological outcomes, functional scores, complications, and peri-operative parameters.MethodsFifty adult patients with unstable anterior pelvic ring injuries (Tile B & C; Young–Burgess lateral compression and vertical shear mechanisms) were enrolled between 2023 and 2025 and randomized into two equal groups: group 1 (internal fixator) and group 2 (retrograde pubic screw). Both groups underwent individualized posterior ring fixation as required. Outcomes assessed included operative time, blood loss, fluoroscopy time, union rate, Matta radiological score at 6 months, Majeed and Pelvic Outcome Scores at 12 months, and post-operative complications.ResultsManagement of anterior pelvic injury showed that retrograde screw fixation demonstrated significantly shorter operative time (32.0 ± 9.2 vs. 50.3 ± 8.4 min, p < 0.001) and less intraoperative blood loss (68.3 ± 16.2 vs. 122.3 ± 31.8 ml, p < 0.001). Radiological outcomes were comparable: excellent Matta score in 68% vs. 64% (p = 0.834). Union time showed no significant difference (14.1 ± 2.4 vs. 15.1 ± 1.5 weeks, p = 0.076). Functional outcomes at 12 months showed no significant difference in Majeed score (p = 0.517), while Pelvic Outcome Score favored the retrograde screw group (32.3 ± 3.5 vs. 30.0 ± 3.3, p = 0.023). Internal fixator had a higher rate of lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury (28%), while retrograde screw fixation showed fewer reoperations and lower implant removal rate.ConclusionUpon reviewing study cases and relevant literature, both minimally invasive techniques can provide reliable fixation and comparable radiological and functional outcomes for unstable anterior pelvic ring fractures. Retrograde pubic ramus screw fixation offers advantages of reduced blood loss and lower neurovascular complications. Internal fixator offers slightly reduced fluoroscopy exposure but carries a higher risk of lateral femoral cutaneous nerve irritation.Level of evidenceLevel 4.

Introduction

Pelvic ring fractures account for approximately 3% of adult fractures and may lead to significant morbidity and mortality. In unstable anterior pelvic ring injuries, achieving a stable fixation and an adequate reduction is essential to reduce these risks [1].

In lateral compression (LC) injuries—according to the Young and Burgess classification [2]—the anterior ring is commonly stabilized using an external fixator (Ex-fix), a subcutaneous internal fixator (Infix), plate fixation, or a percutaneous retrograde pubic ramus screw [3]. The Nakatani classification system [4] categorizes superior pubic ramus fractures into three zones based on their relationship to the obturator foramen: Zone I (medial), Zone II (transforaminal), and Zone III (lateral to obturator foramen).

The Infix technique is a minimally invasive and an effective method for managing complex anterior pelvic ring injuries, providing satisfactory reduction, favorable radiological and functional outcomes, and a reliable fracture healing. Reported complications include lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN) and femoral nerve palsy, vascular injury, wound problems, and surgical site infection. Meticulous dissection and sound anatomical knowledge are crucial for optimizing outcomes and patient satisfaction [3].

Conversely, percutaneous fluoroscopy-guided retrograde pubic ramus screw fixation has emerged as an alternative for stabilizing anterior pelvic ring injuries in Nakatani Zone II and III fractures [4]. This technique offers minimal blood loss and low rates of urological, neurological, and vascular injury, in addition to reduced infection rates and fewer loss-of-reduction events; however, it is technically demanding. Recently, this technique was improved to overcome difficult anatomical variations [5].

This study aimed to compare the outcomes of infix versus percutaneous anterior retrograde pubic ramus screw fixation in the management of unilateral zone II and III pubic ramus fractures in unstable pelvic injuries. Primary evaluation was based on the Pelvic Outcome Score [6], with secondary assessments including intra-operative parameters, radiological outcomes using the Matta score [7], and functional outcomes using the Majeed score [8].

Patients and methods

From August 2023 to August 2025, a prospective randomized cohort study was conducted at a university hospital, a tertiary referral center for complex pelvic injuries, to compare the outcomes of minimally invasive fixation techniques for anterior pelvic ring fractures—specifically anterior infix versus percutaneous retrograde pubic ramus screw fixation. Fifty patients with anterior ring injuries were enrolled and randomly allocated into two equal groups using a concealed sealed-envelope technique: group 1 underwent anterior infix fixation, while group 2 received percutaneous retrograde pubic ramus screw fixation. All cases were operated upon by one of the authors who are qualified pelvic and acetabular trauma surgeons.

Sample size estimation was performed using a clinical sample size calculator for interventional studies, with an alpha error of 0.05, study power of 0.80, and a 95% confidence interval. Based on previously published data, the mean time to union was 13.3 weeks for anterior infix fixation and 8 weeks for retrograde screw fixation. The calculated sample size required for comparing both techniques in lateral compression anterior pelvic ring injuries was 54 patients, including a 10% increase to compensate for potential loss of follow-up (25 patients per arm).

A convenience sampling technique was used to recruit eligible patients, who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Randomization was then performed using the concealed allocation method described above. All patients were followed for a minimum of 12 months. Informed consent for both the surgical procedure and the participation in the study was obtained from all patients at admission. According to Helsinki declaration, all ethical standards were applied carefully in the current study. The study received ethical committee approval from Cairo University Hospital Ethical Committee with IRB number “MD-476–2023”.

Upon admission, all patients were assessed according to the ATLS protocol, as all injuries resulted from high-energy mechanisms. A detailed history, comprehensive general examination, and focused local examination of the affected limb—including assessment for shortening, external rotation deformity, and neurovascular status—were performed. All patients were catheterized to evaluate for hematuria and to detect any associated urological injuries.

Radiological assessment included standard pelvic radiographs in anteroposterior (AP), inlet, outlet, and Judet oblique views. Computed tomography (CT) was performed for all patients to provide a more accurate evaluation of the pelvic ring injury and to aid in the surgical planning. Pelvic fractures were classified according to the Young and Burgess system [2].

All patients received prophylactic antibiotics before anesthesia induction, using a first-generation Cephalosporin (Cefazolin). Management of the posterior pelvic ring injury was individualized based on the fracture pattern and included various fixation techniques such as ilio-sacral screws or plate fixation.

Surgical technique

General anesthesia was administered to all patients, and all procedures for anterior ring management were performed in the supine position. In group 1, infix was utilized. The construct consisted of two large-diameter (range 6–7 mm), length (range 75–100 mm) pedicular screws inserted just above the anterior inferior iliac spine (AIIS), connected by a 5–6 mm curved and pre-contoured titanium rod placed subcutaneously. The spine system utilized belongs to a local manufacturer (Egifix).

A 3-cm longitudinal incision was made over the AIIS on both sides. Although the AIIS may not be easily palpable in obese patients, it is generally located approximately 2 cm distal and 2 cm medial to the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS). Careful dissection was carried down to the bone within the interval between the sartorius and tensor fasciae latae to expose the pelvic surface while preserving the surrounding neurovascular structures and specifically the LFCN. Fracture configuration and entry point were confirmed intra-operatively using fluoroscopic views (iliac oblique, obturator outlet, and obturator inlet), adjusted to achieve a teardrop appearance before instrumentation (Fig. 2).

The entry point was prepared using a sharp awl, and a pedicle finder was then introduced to create the screw trajectory, directed toward the posterior superior iliac spine. A ball-tipped probe was used to ensure the integrity of the bony corridors without breaching the cortex. Each pedicle screw was inserted into the supraacetabular region through the dense bone corridor between the inner and outer iliac tables, taking care to remain superior to the hip joint and the greater sciatic notch. Importantly, the screw heads were intentionally left 4–6 threads proud to maintain an epi-fascial rod position and minimize the risk of compression of the femoral nerve or adjacent vascular structures.

The rod is inserted subcutaneously under the skin at the level of the bikini line after being over-contoured on the abdomen (Fig. 3).

Manipulative multiplanar reduction of the displaced hemipelvis can be achieved by compression or distraction over rods as appropriate before securing the rod to the screw heads. Reduction of the pelvis can be achieved using C-rings, compression or distraction using pins in the ASIS, or injured leg traction was applied to achieve reduction according to the fracture morphology.

Retrograde pubic ramus screw insertion was performed as follows: the pubic tubercle was first identified, and the anterior column corridor was marked on the skin using the AP view. This pathway is marked on the skin, with a small skin incision made along the marked line at the level of the ipsilateral pubic tubercle, adjacent to the base of the penis or the mons pubis, followed by a blunt subcutaneous dissection toward the injured pubic tubercle while protecting the soft tissues with a sleeve. Adequate exposure of the bony landmarks at the entry point was essential. The entry point varied according to the fracture pattern and the Nakatani classification (zones II and III). In zone II fractures, the entry was made at the sub-pubic angle near the symphysis, allowing optimal purchase in the medial fragment. In zone III fractures, a more inferolateral entry point was selected to accommodate a longer screw and improve fixation strength.

A 1.6-mm guidewire was inserted at the chosen entry point and used when necessary for indirect fracture manipulation and alignment. The drill trajectory was initiated in the obturator outlet view with a cephalocaudal inclination, and under continuous fluoroscopic guidance, the drill position was adjusted to align with the intra-medullary corridor of the superior pubic ramus. After perforating the first cortex, the drill was advanced gently using oscillating or continuous motion while maintaining extra-articular screw placement relative to the acetabulum, confirmed on obturator outlet and inlet views. Care was taken to avoid medial or lateral deviation to prevent injury to the obturator nerve and vessels. A decompressed bladder was ensured via catheterization to minimize the risk of iatrogenic injury (Fig. 4).

Indirect reduction was performed in displaced fractures using either the screw itself or the pedicle canal finder of the spinal system inserted along the planned trajectory to facilitate the fracture realignment and fixation. Drilling was continued until the drill tip reached the lateral cortex of the ilium above the acetabulum. A cannulated 3.2-mm drill bit was then used, followed by the insertion of a 6.5-mm cannulated screw. A washer was not required, as the screw head is seated within the thick tendinous attachments of the adductor musculature at the pubic bone. If the drill could not safely traverse the acetabulum without breaching the joint, a shorter screw was selected to terminate at the anterior acetabular lip in Nakatani zone II fractures. In obese patients or those with altered pelvic anatomy, modifications to the entry point and the screw trajectory were made accordingly. A small incision was created over the pubic tubercle or adjacent to the symphysis pubis as required for exposure.

All intra-operative parameters—including the operative time, the fluoroscopy time, estimated blood loss, and the intra-operative complications—were documented. Post-operatively, all patients underwent neurovascular assessment and standard radiographic evaluation (AP, obturator oblique, iliac oblique, inlet, and outlet views) to confirm the proper screw placement. The urinary catheter was inspected to exclude urological injury. Thromboprophylaxis with low-molecular-weight heparin was initiated on post-operative day two. Toe-touch weight bearing was permitted immediately post-operatively and progressed to partial weight bearing according to the radiographic evidence of healing. Full weight bearing was allowed upon confirmation of complete union, defined by radiographic disappearance of fracture lines, absence of local tenderness, and a painless straight leg raise.

All patients were instructed for follow up after two weeks post-operatively to check the wound condition and the suture removal. Then, patients were advised to have monthly visits for radiological follow-up until complete union was achieved. Majeed score and pelvic outcome scores were assessed at 12 months for all patients for functional evaluation [6, 8].

Statistical analysis

Data was coded and entered using the statistical package for social science SPSS version 26. Data was checked for normality using Shapiro-Wilk test. Data was summarized using number and percent for qualitative variables, mean and standard deviation for quantitative normally distributed variables, and range for quantitative not normally distributed variables. Comparison between groups was done using the Chi-square test or fisher’s exact test where appropriate, for qualitative variables, independent samples T-test for independent comparisons of quantitative normally distributed variables. For comparing quantitative variables, which were not normally distributed; nonparametric Mann-Whitney test was used for independent comparisons. Spearman correlation was used for testing linear relations between variables. ANCOVA multivariate analysis was used. P value less than or equal to 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

From August 2023 to August 2025, a prospective randomized comparative cohort study involving 50 patients with unilateral pubic ramus fractures was performed (Figs. 5 and 6). All cases were followed up for at least 12 months (range 12–20 months) with a mean of 13.5 months (S.D) ± 1.1. The mean age in group 1 was 26.1 years (range 18–42 years) (S.D.± 6.7 years), while the mean age for group 2 was 25.4 years (range 16–41 years) (S.D 7.8 years), with no significant statistical distinction between both groups (P value = 0.625).

Group 2 showed shorter mean time to union (14.08 ± 2.4 weeks/range 11–19 weeks) than group 1 (15.12 ± 1.51 weeks/range 12–18 weeks) with no statistically significant distinction between both groups (P value = 0.076). The mean follow-up period for group 1 was 13.2 months ± 1.5 (range 12–18 months), while for group 2, it was 14.2 months ± 1.6 (range 12–20 months), with no statistically significant distinction (P value 0.571).

Quality of reduction was assessed post-operatively using Matta radiological score [7] at 6 months with no statistically significant difference (P value = 0.834), however this finding will be affected by the difference in initial displacement, which was higher in group 1, that it may affect the final Matta score post-operatively. In group 1; 16 patients (64%) showed excellent score, with a mean residual displacement of 3.5 mm (S.D ± 1.1), while in group 2, it was achieved in 17 patients (68%), with a mean of 1.9 mm (S.D ± 0.8). Comparing initial displacement pre-operatively and Matta radiological outcome at 6 months, there was a negative correlation between them (r=−0.588, p-value = 0.001). The correlations between Matta score and age, sex, mode of trauma, displacement degree, fracture classification (Young or Tile classifications) and operative delay till fixation in both groups showed no statistical relation.

Functional outcome was assessed using the Majeed score and Pelvic outcome score at one-year of follow-up [6, 8]. Majeed score showed no statistical distinction (P value 0.517) with the mean scores of groups 1 and 2 were (84.3 ± 4.1/range 60–90), and (86.8 ± 5.1/range 65–90) respectively. On the other hand, Pelvic outcome score at 1 year was significantly higher among group 2 (P value = 0.023), with the mean scores for groups 1 and 2 were 30.04 ± 3.26 (range 21–35) and 32, 28 ± 3.47 (range 25–38) respectively, however the presence of some variation in type of posterior ring fracture and its method of fixation may influence the mean time to union and functional outcomes when comparing both groups, trying to avoid this bias statistically by performing comparison between both groups using the P value as a parameter that revealed no statistical significance between both groups regarding these outcomes. Comparing the age of series’ cases to Majeed score and Pelvic outcome score at 1 year showed that younger patients had more favorable outcomes, where there was a statistically significant distinction with P value of 0.041 and 0.011, respectively.

Patients with associated fractures and polytrauma patients showed lower Majeed score [8] and Pelvic outcome score [6] at 1 year of follow-up with no statistically significant distinction (P value = 0.831 and 0.191 respectively). Pre-operative displacement relation to Majeed and Pelvic outcome score were not statistically significant with p-value 0.308 and 0.089 respectively. The correlations between total Majeed score and age, sex, mode of trauma, other skeletal trauma, displacement degree, fracture classification (Young and Burgess and Tile classifications), operative delay till fixation and LFCN affection in both groups were performed. No statistical relation was found except the negativity of LFCN affection and pin tract infection in group 1 (the lower lateral femoral cutaneous nerve affection and pin tract infection, the more the total Majeed score [8]).

Post-operative wound infection showed statistically significant difference between both groups (P value: 0.04). Group 1 showed three cases with wound infection, the first showed superficial infection encountered at the first follow up visit, improved by repeated dressing and antibiotics according to culture and sensitivity (C&S). The other two cases showed late infection (at 9 months) with a full union and a good functional outcome achieved, so debridement and removal of the implant were performed while group 2 showed one case with superficial infection in the first follow-up visit, improved by repeated dressing and antibiotics according to (C&S), however the low number of cases that encountered infection in each group didn’t allow for strong evidence and needs larger sample to able to be confirmed.

Iatrogenic LFCN Injury was encountered among seven cases in group 1. Five cases (71. 4%) had bilateral LFCN injury that had relieved after implant removal; the other two cases, LFCN injury wasn’t relieved, one of them was bilateral, while the other was unilateral, with a statistically significant distinction between both groups (p-value 0.010). This evidence was supported by literature reports suggesting strong correlation between Infix and LFCN injury [3].

Regarding implant removal, in group 1, 22 cases (88%) underwent implant removal, while the other three cases (12%) refused re-operation. On the other hand, in group 2, two cases needed removal, the 1 st because of screw backing out and the 2nd case because of unspecified implant irritation that had improved after removal, with a statistically significant distinction between both groups (p-value = 0.001).

Group 1 showed one case (4%) of loosening and nonunion, while group 2 showed one case (4%) of screw backing out, which required removal. When discussing incidence of complications (including infection, LFCN injury, loosening, non-union and backing out), there was a statistically significant difference (P value: 0.012) with group 2 having a lower incidence of complications, however larger sample size are needed to confirm these results (Table 3).

Discussion

Early restoration of the structural integrity and the stability of the pelvic ring is imperative to normalize the patient’s acute physiology and to allow for early restoration of mobility and function. While posterior pelvic ring stability is typically restored by open or percutaneous methods, concurrent anterior stabilization significantly improves biomechanical stability and can be performed as a definitive procedure [9]. Fixation techniques remain a topic of controversy.

In the current study, anterior ring fixation only operative time for group 1 was significantly longer than group 2, with a mean of 50.32 min (range 39–80 min). This finding is similar to the operative durations reported by Sharma et al. [10] (45–90 min) and Hoskins et al. [11] (51 min). However, it exceeded the times reported by Xiao-Tian et al. [12] (26.6 min) and Shetty et al. [13] (29 min).

In contrast, the operative time in group 2 was longer than that reported by Zou et al. [14] (15.0 ± 4.1 min), yet shorter than Wang et al. [15] (46.3 ± 7.6 min), and comparable to Routt et al. [5], who documented 22–54 min (mean 33 min). The significantly shorter operative time in group 2 is attributed to the minimally invasive nature of the percutaneous screw placement and was improving with training curve, whereas infix requires larger incisions, greater soft-tissue dissection, rod contouring, and pedicle screw insertion, all of which are time-consuming.

More blood loss encountered during anterior ring fixation only was encountered in group 1 (mean 122. 28 ml) than group 2 (mean 68. 36 ml), which was similar to what was reported by Ansari et, al [16], that ranged from (40–350 ml), xu et al. [17], who reported a range of 20–150 ml And Wuxet. al [18], which was 100 ml (range 60–200 ml), and Yin et al. [19], which ranged from 79.1 ± 15.6 ml. Conversely, blood loss encountered during anterior ting fixation only in group 2 exceeded that reported by Routt et al. [5] (1–12 ml) but was comparable to Wang et al. [15] (72.5 ± 10.8 ml). Blood loss was statistically significant more in group 1. This was attributed to percutaneous screw insertion technique while infix needed larger incision and more soft tissue exposure.

Fluoroscopy time in the current series was more than Zou et al. [14], who reported a mean of 21.2 ± 3.9, but less than Routt et al. [5], who reported (range 1 min 38 s (sec)- 4 min 50 s) with a mean of 3 min 20 s.

On the other hand, group 1 showed a lower intra-operative fluoroscopy time 35.8 s (± 7.9), which was similar to Zou et al. [14] with 32. 6 ± 7.7 s.

Radiological time didn’t show statistical distinction between both groups. This was attributed to complex narrow curves of rami that needed multiple views to ensure accurate screw placement avoiding screw mal-positioning, cortical breaching and neurovascular injury in comparison to imaging to both screws in the infix application. Intra-operative fluoroscopy was needed in both groups to check position of screws, and its need decreased with progression of the series and improvement of the learning curve.

Initial fracture displacement was significantly greater in group 1. Post-operative reduction quality, evaluated using Matta’s radiological criteria, showed excellent results in 16 patients (64%) in group 1 and 17 patients (68%) in group 2, with no significant distinction.

Patients with associated injuries and polytrauma demonstrated lower functional outcomes, particularly at 6 months, though the differences were not statistically distinct. This may reflect prolonged immobilization, muscle atrophy, and prolonged rehabilitation demands commonly seen in the polytrauma patients.

As there were different methods of posterior pelvic ring fixation methods in the current study, which could implicate the functional and radiological outcome in both groups, a multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA analysis) was performed while controlling for potential confounding variables. The dependent variables were Majeed Score at 1-year, Pelvic Outcome Score at 1-year, Matta Score at 6 months [6–8].

Independent Variable (Fixed Factor) was type of the posterior fixation. The following variables were included as covariates because they differed between both groups or were shown to correlate with outcomes, anterior fixation type (Infix vs. retrograde screw), time to surgery (significantly longer in group 1, P = 0.001), Initial fracture displacement (significantly higher in group 1, P = 0.007), patient age (correlated with time to union and functional outcomes).

Posterior fixation type, overall MANCOVA multivariate analysis (All three scores combined), P-value was 0.891. There was no statistically significant overall distinction of the posterior fixation method on the combined functional and radiological outcomes, with no significant distinction of posterior fixation type on both the 1-year Majeed score (P-value 0.765), the 1-year Pelvic Outcome score (P-value 0.842), and the 6-month radiological Matta score (P-value 0.923).

On the other hand, using the anterior fixation Type, overall MANCOVA utilisation, there was a statistically significant overall effect of the anterior fixation method (infix vs. retrograde screw) on the combined outcomes (P-value 0.038). Individually, both the Majeed score at 12 months (P-value 0.517) and the radiological Matta score at 6-months (P-value 0.834) were not significantly distinct. Group 2 had a significantly better Pelvic Outcome Score, even after controlling for covariates (P-value 0.023).

Pelvic Outcome Score was statistically distinct between both groups, while the Majeed Score wasn’t, because the first is a more detailed score (describing the patient condition, with more pain analysis, discussing the need for narcotic use, objective examination of patient muscle power, range of motion, radiological assessment of patients X-rays and residual displacement in the anterior and posterior pelvic ring fractures). So, the pelvic outcome score was more comprehensive, which caused the statistical distinction between both groups.

Covariates, time to surgery and initial displacement did not have a significant independent effect on the set of outcomes (P-value 0.215, 0.104, respectively). Patient age had a significant independent effect on the combined outcomes, confirming that younger age is associated with better outcomes (P-value 0.028). However, the final 1-year outcomes showed no significant differences. Faster bone healing, improved rehabilitation compliance, and fewer comorbidities likely explain this trend.

Group 1 implant removal rate was more statistically significant, because of regular implant removal offered for the patients of this group due to discomfort caused by the infix, unlike the retropubic screw, which didn’t need regular removal procedures.

For group 1, the time of the infix removal was in line with Steer et al. and Wu X et al. [18] around (4 to 5 months), while Fang et al. [20] did not routinely remove the infix from the patients, and 27.5% of their patients elected to keep the implant in to avoid another operation.

Low rate of infection in the current study was attributed to the minimally invasive nature of both techniques, less soft tissue dissection compared to open techniques, and strict sterility measures and antibiotic prophylaxis.

In group 1, seven cases (28%) were reported with LFCN injury, that was more than what were reported in the following studies, Zou et al., Sharma et al., Wu x et al., Xu et al., Muller et al., Xiao- Tian et al., Gardner et al., Shetty et al., Vaidya et al. (6. 9%), (16. 6%), (9. 7%), (3.7%), (0%), (19. 4%), (13%, (8%), (3%), (8.4%), (8.4%) of their cases respectively [10, 12–14, 17, 18, 21–23].

On the other hand, it was less than the following studies, Steer et al., Dahill et al., Hoskins et al., Fang et al. who reported (45. 9%), (55%), (57. 1%), (48%) of their patients respectively [11, 20, 24, 25].

To reduce LFCN irritation, our technique emphasized minimizing rod length lateral to the screw, using poly-axial screws with smaller diameters, and avoiding deep placement near the nerve trajectory. Wide rod-to-bone spacing (20–25 mm) was used to reduce femoral nerve compression.

Time to surgery in group 1 was slightly longer compared with Zou et al. [14], Yin et al. [19], and Sharma et al. [10], and shorter than in Wu et al. [18], Müller et al. [21], and Steer et al. [24]. Group 2 time was longer than those reported by Zou et al. [14], but shorter than Wang et al. [15], and Rommens et al. [26], whose reported ranges were 4–22 days.

The longer operative delay in group 1 is mainly attributable to late referral and associated injuries requiring stabilization prior to pelvic surgery.

Strengths of the study

Strengths of this study include being (to our knowledge) one of the first prospective randomized investigations comparing infix and retrograde pubic screw minimally invasive techniques for anterior ring fractures, with stringent inclusion/exclusion criteria, random patient assignment, and standardized clinical and radiological follow-up protocols. This is also the first study to the best of our knowledge to provide clear recommendations for retrograde pubic screw insertion in multiple planes, reducing malposition risk and fluoroscopy use. Consistent functional outcome findings strengthen study conclusions.

Limitations of the study

Limitations included the small sample size (25 patients per group), relatively short follow-up (minimum 12 months) with larger sample size and longer follow-up required for better evidence evaluation, and heterogeneity in posterior ring fracture type according to both Tile and Young and burgess classifications, with more unstable fracture patterns in group 2, which result in different instability patterns facilitating error in functional outcome and results of the cases, also resulting in different methods of fixations used for management of such different subset of fractures, which may result in more bias in results developed. Also, the presence of associated fractures may affect the functional outcome and patients’ improvement because of time needed for rehabilitation of such injuries, which may affect the functional outcome scores detected early during follow-up. The heterogenicity in initial displacement that was significantly higher in group 1 may cause bias in results concerning time to union, functional outcomes and Matta score between both groups reflecting the fact that patients of group 1 may have been exposed to more severe high energy trauma causing more injury and more initial displacement in these patients that may reflect on patients’ outcomes. In light of the limitations of the present study, future research should aim to provide more robust evidence by narrowing the scope to a single type of posterior ring fracture treated with a standardized method of fixation. Such studies would benefit from larger sample sizes and extended follow-up periods to enhance statistical validity and capture long-term outcomes. Moreover, ensuring greater uniformity in preoperative criteria—particularly in pelvic fracture classification and initial displacement—would help minimize bias and allow for more accurate comparisons. These refinements would contribute to a clearer evaluation of treatment effectiveness and strengthen the reliability of conclusions drawn in this field.

Conclusion

This study suggests that both percutaneous retrograde ramus screw and infix through minimally invasive techniques represent valid methods for dealing with unstable anterior ring fractures, which was observed in different settings of posterior ring fracture type and its fixation methods. Evaluation of the study cases and relevant literature discussed within this manuscript found that retrograde pubic screw had the advantages of less risk of iatrogenic nerve injury and a lower rate of implant irritation, and need of implant removal, but with a steep learning curve, while Infix had the advantage of avoiding more radiological exposure intra-operatively.
