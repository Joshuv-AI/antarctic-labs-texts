Cluster analysis of global research hotspots and thematic evolution in breast cancer and general anesthesia a bibliometric study from 1985 to 2024
Authors: Qiangyan Wu, Chengqi Deng, Peng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResearch at the intersection of breast cancer and general anesthesia has advanced rapidly, yet its thematic structure and evolving hotspots remain unclear. This study provides the first comprehensive bibliometric analysis of the global research landscape from 1985 to 2024. Its objective is to systematically map the field, identify evolving research hotspots, and analyze thematic trajectories to reveal shifting research priorities.MethodsA bibliometric analysis was conducted using the Web of Science Core Collection. VOSviewer, CiteSpace, and the R package “bibliometrix” were used to construct co-occurrence networks and perform cluster and burst keyword analyses.ResultsA total of 428 studies were included. Cluster analysis of high-frequency keywords identified four major research clusters: (1) the impact of anesthetic technique on cancer recurrence and metastasis, (2) diagnostic and management strategies in breast cancer, (3) surgical approaches and pain outcomes, and (4) analgesic techniques and perioperative management. Keyword burst analysis revealed an early focus on diagnosis and surgical technique, with a clear transition in the past decade toward patient-centered outcomes such as recurrence prevention, persistent pain, survivorship, and quality of life.ConclusionThis cluster analysis-based bibliometric study reveals four primary research hotspots in breast cancer and general anesthesia, highlighting a thematic evolution from technical and procedural concerns to patient-centered outcomes and survivorship. These findings suggest future research will focus increasingly on optimizing anesthetic strategies to improve long-term outcomes and quality of life for breast cancer patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04778-8.

Background

Breast cancer remains the most prevalent malignancy among women worldwide, with surgical intervention playing a pivotal role in management and survival outcomes [1–3]. Advances in multimodal treatment—including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, hormone therapy, and targeted therapies—have substantially improved survival and quality of life for many patients [4, 5]. General anesthesia is the standard approach for facilitating breast cancer surgery, ensuring operative safety and patient comfort [6]. Over recent decades, research has increasingly turned to the potential influence of anesthetic technique on not only immediate perioperative outcomes but also long-term cancer recurrence, metastasis, and patient quality of life [7, 8]. The interplay between anesthetic agents, immune modulation, tumor biology, and chronic pain has become a central focus, yet the thematic structure and evolution of this interdisciplinary research field remain incompletely understood.

Within perioperative care, anesthetic management is a critical yet sometimes underappreciated component. General anesthesia is routinely used in breast cancer surgery to ensure patient comfort and safety [9]. Emerging evidence suggests anesthetic technique may influence not only immediate recovery (pain, cognitive function) [10, 11], but also long-term outcomes such as cancer recurrence and metastasis [9, 12]. Mechanistic studies indicate that inhalational agents (e.g., sevoflurane, isoflurane) may suppress immune function and facilitate tumor spread [12], while intravenous anesthetics like propofol might better preserve immunity, albeit with their own risks [13]. Opioid-based analgesia—common in breast surgery—has been linked to immune suppression and enhanced tumor growth in preclinical studies [14], leading to interest in opioid-sparing approaches [15]. Anesthetic decision-making is further complicated by patient comorbidities such as obesity, hypertension, or diabetes, which increase perioperative risks [16].

A key unresolved controversy concerns whether specific anesthetic techniques—particularly total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) with propofol versus volatile anesthetics—can meaningfully influence long-term oncologic outcomes such as recurrence or metastasis. Some clinical and mechanistic studies suggest that TIVA with propofol may preserve immune function and exert anti-tumor effects, whereas inhalational agents (e.g., sevoflurane, isoflurane) and opioid-based analgesia may suppress immune surveillance and potentially facilitate tumor spread [9–14]. However, findings across observational studies, randomized trials, and meta-analyses remain inconsistent and sometimes contradictory [9, 10, 12, 15]. Despite extensive efforts, no consensus has been reached regarding the impact of anesthetic management on long-term breast cancer outcomes, and it remains unclear how research priorities in this area have shifted over time—from early work focused on diagnosis and epidemiology to more recent interest in perioperative strategies, recurrence prevention, and survivorship.

Given the breadth and rapid growth of the literature, there is a growing need for a systematic, data-driven approach to map research hotspots, track thematic evolution, and identify emerging priorities. Cluster analysis of high-frequency keywords, combined with citation burst analysis, offers a powerful means to dissect the intellectual landscape and clarify how research foci have shifted over time [17]. While previous bibliometric studies have examined trends in breast cancer or anesthetic practice separately [18, 19], few have systematically mapped the knowledge structure, major research clusters, and evolving frontiers at their intersection.

Accordingly, this study employs advanced cluster analysis and bibliometric mapping to delineate the principal thematic clusters, collaboration networks, and evolving research priorities in breast cancer and general anesthesia from 1985 to 2024. By clarifying the intellectual structure and highlighting emerging trends, our findings aim to inform future research directions and support optimization of perioperative management for breast cancer patients.

Methods

Search strategies and data collection

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) to identify publications on breast cancer and general anesthesia. The search, performed on September 12, 2024, used precise Boolean logic and synonym expansion: TS=(“Breast Neoplasm*” OR “Breast Tumor*” OR “Breast Cancer*” OR “Breast Carcinoma*” OR “Mammary Neoplasm*” OR “Mammary Tumor*” OR “Mammary Cancer*” OR “Mammary Carcinoma*”) AND TS=(“general anesthesia” OR “general anaesthesia”). Only English-language primary research articles published from 1985 to 2024 were included; reviews, meeting abstracts, proceedings, editorials, corrections, letters, and non-English materials were excluded. Data were exported in “Full record and cited references” and “plain text” formats for bibliometric analysis. All screening was performed only within WoSCC, and all identified records were included for further analysis (Fig. 1). WoSCC’s internal data cleaning process automatically removes duplicate records, and no additional duplicate removal steps were performed. No formal assessment of “ambiguous records” was conducted. To confirm data quality, two authors independently checked a random sample of 50 records for accuracy, and no obvious deviations were identified.

Statistical analysis

Three advanced bibliometric tools—VOSviewer (v1.6.20), CiteSpace (v6.3.R1), and R-bibliometrix (v4.3.3)—were employed to systematically evaluate and visualize key bibliometric indicators. Cluster analysis was the central analytical approach of this study, enabling systematic identification and characterization of major research hotspots and thematic structures in the literature.

VOSviewer facilitated mapping of collaboration networks (authors, institutions, countries) and keyword co-occurrence, using a minimum node size of 5 and network visualization options such as fractional counting and LinLog/modularity clustering for clear community detection [20]. CiteSpace supported in-depth co-occurrence analysis, clustering, and burst detection of keywords and references, with data sliced annually (1985–2024). Clustering quality was assessed by modularity Q (> 0.3) and silhouette S (> 0.7). For burst detection, γ was set to 1.0 and the minimum burst duration to 2 years to highlight significant citation bursts and emerging trends [21]. R-bibliometrix was used to further analyze and visualize publication trends, rankings (by country, institution, author), and citation metrics. Across all software, node size reflected publication or citation frequency, link thickness indicated collaboration/co-occurrence strength, and colors represented clusters or temporal evolution.

A minimum keyword occurrence threshold of 5 was chosen for cluster analysis. This threshold helps maintain interpretability by reducing isolated or rarely used terms, is appropriate for a medium-sized corpus (hundreds to thousands of articles) to retain core concepts, and is consistent with empirical thresholds commonly used in published bibliometric studies, facilitating comparison with existing research [22].

To assess researcher influence and productivity, we calculated the H-index, G-index, and M-index [23]. The H-index, from the Web of Science Core Collection, measured both productivity and citation impact [24, 25]. The G-index emphasized highly cited works, providing a nuanced view of scholarly impact, while the M-index normalized the H-index for career duration, allowing fairer comparisons. Journals were categorized by Journal Citation Reports (JCR) impact factors (IF), contextualizing each publication’s influence within the field.

Results

Overview of publications on breast cancer and general anesthesia

A total of 532 studies were initially identified from WoSCC (Fig. 1). After applying exclusion criteria, 104 studies were removed (64 reviews, 6 meeting abstracts, 12 proceedings, 11 editorials, 4 retracted, 1 correction, 4 letters, 1 book chapter, 18 non-English papers), leaving 428 eligible studies. These publications, authored by 2,545 contributors and spanning 206 journals, collectively cited 9,436 references and included 867 unique keywords across 163 countries or regions (Fig. 2A). The time span was 1985–2024, with an average annual growth rate of 6.46%. Each document averaged 6.47 co-authors, and 17.29% involved international collaboration. There were 29.71 citations per document on average, and the mean document age was 9.9 years.

The most cited article, “Can anesthetic technique for primary breast cancer surgery affect recurrence or metastasis?” (Anesthesiology, IF = 9.1, 2006), received 645 citations [26]. The second, “Micrometastatic breast cancer cells in bone marrow at primary surgery: prognostic value in comparison with nodal status” (J Natl Cancer Inst, IF = 9.9, 1996), had 407 citations [27]. The third, “Fat injection to the breast: technique, results, and indications based on 880 procedures over 10 years” (Aesthetic Surgery Journal, IF = 3.0, 2009), received 370 citations [28].

Publication output in this field has increased markedly from 1985 to 2024 (Fig. 2B). After just 2 articles in 1985 and sporadic output into the mid-1990s, growth accelerated, reaching 7 articles in 1999 and 14 in 2008. From 2015 onward, there was a marked surge, with peaks of 26 publications in 2016, 34 in 2021, and a record 37 in 2024, reflecting a sustained annual growth rate of 6.46%.

The Annals of Surgical Oncology (IF = 3.4, H-index = 10) and Anesthesia and Analgesia (IF = 4.6, H-index = 8) also rank highly. Analysis of burst keywords: evolution of research focus over time

Keyword citation burst analysis from 1985 to 2024 reveals evolving research priorities (Fig. 8). “Carcinoma” showed the highest burst strength (8.01, 1999–2012), followed by “breast cancer” (4.09, 2000–2009), reflecting strong early interest in disease classification and diagnosis. Other prominent bursts include “experience” (3.51, 2004–2009) and “trial” (4.54, 2006–2018), highlighting the focus on clinical research and evidence-based practices. “Radiofrequency ablation” gained attention (3.02, 2009–2011), while “general anesthesia” (5.22, 2011–2016) marked ongoing debate about anesthesia practices. Recently, keywords like “postoperative pain” (4.33) and “persistent pain” (4.07, both 2019–2024) indicate a shift toward pain management, with “survival” (5.84, 2021–2022) and “quality” (2.83, 2021–2024) reflecting growing emphasis on patient outcomes and healthcare quality.

Discussion

This bibliometric analysis provides important insights into the evolving intersection of breast cancer and general anesthesia research. By identifying four major thematic clusters and tracking their prominence over time, the study demonstrates a dynamic shift in research priorities. Early work focused on diagnosis and epidemiology, but recent decades have emphasized perioperative care, particularly how anesthesia techniques affect cancer recurrence, pain management, and patient-centered outcomes. Keyword burst analysis highlights the field’s transition from technical and surgical issues to survivorship and quality of life, reflecting both scientific progress and changing clinical needs.

The cluster analysis of global research in breast cancer and general anesthesia highlights how influential studies, high-impact journals, and international collaboration collectively shape the field’s thematic evolution. Seminal works such as Buggy and Sessler’s (2006) investigation into anesthetic technique and cancer recurrence, Diel et al.’s (1996) exploration of micrometastatic disease [26], and Delay et al.’s (2009) research on reconstructive outcomes exemplify the integration of anesthetic management, surgical approach, and patient recovery as core research concerns [27, 28]. Collectively, these studies underscore the field’s ongoing interest in optimizing both surgical and anesthetic strategies to improve oncological and postoperative outcomes.

The prominence of multidisciplinary journals like the British Journal of Anaesthesia and Anesthesiology, reflected in their high impact factors and consistent publication of studies on perioperative strategies, immune modulation, and long-term oncologic outcomes [9, 29], further underscores the field’s commitment to evidence-based, patient-centered care [30]. Additionally, our cluster mapping demonstrates that high-impact research is often generated through international, multi-institutional networks, with leading centers such as the Cleveland Clinic, University College Dublin, and Fudan University serving as pivotal nodes for collaboration and knowledge dissemination [31–33]. Author network analysis reinforces that researchers deeply engaged in global partnerships, such as Buggy and Sessler, are especially influential in advancing research frontiers and translating innovation into clinical practice [34–36]. Together, these findings emphasize that the advancement of breast cancer anesthesia research is fundamentally driven by clustered thematic exploration, interdisciplinary publication, and robust international collaboration.

Cluster 1. anesthetic technique and cancer recurrence

The question of whether anesthetic technique influences cancer recurrence remains a central and unresolved controversy. Observational studies and meta-analyses have suggested that TIVA with propofol may lower recurrence rates compared to volatile agents, consistent with mechanistic evidence showing that volatile anesthetics and opioids can suppress natural killer cell function and promote tumor angiogenesis [9, 10, 12–14, 26, 34]. However, evidence remains inconclusive due to limitations in study design, population heterogeneity, and short follow-up. Recent randomized controlled trials have not consistently shown a survival benefit for any one technique [9]. The moderate level of evidence underscores the need for large, multicenter RCTs with standardized protocols and long-term outcomes.

Diagnosis, epidemiology, and cancer management

This cluster emerged earliest, highlighting the global burden of breast cancer and efforts to improve diagnosis and management [1–3]. Advances in imaging, biopsy, and systemic therapy facilitated earlier detection and more personalized treatments [6, 7, 37]. As clinical standards evolved, this cluster’s prominence declined but remains important for contextualizing perioperative and long-term outcome research. Increasing focus on survivorship, quality of life, and health disparities reflects the field’s shift from disease-centric to patient-centered approaches [8, 38, 39]. The decline of this cluster parallels the rise of research into perioperative and pain management.

Surgical approaches and pain outcomes

Initially centered on surgical techniques, this cluster has shifted towards addressing persistent pain—a major, often overlooked complication affecting many breast cancer survivors [39]. Research has moved from technical innovation to optimizing perioperative pain control, multimodal analgesia, and enhanced recovery [9, 36, 38]. Evidence now supports regional anesthesia techniques, such as pectoral nerve and paravertebral blocks, in reducing acute and chronic pain [9, 38]. Increasing attention to complications, depression, and quality of life demonstrates a broader focus on multidimensional recovery and patient-centered outcomes [38–40].

Analgesic techniques and perioperative management

This cluster has evolved from basic comparisons of local anesthetics to sophisticated multimodal analgesic regimens using non-opioid adjuncts, regional blocks, and patient education [38]. The rise of keywords like “prevention,” “efficacy,” and “double-blind” reflects growing reliance on prospective trials [11, 38]. Advances in analgesic strategies are associated with improved perioperative outcomes, fewer opioid-related complications, and greater emphasis on quality and patient satisfaction [36, 38, 41]. Integrating these innovations into surgical care represents a new frontier, aligning with the demands of modern oncology.

Time dimension and hotspot migration

Temporal analysis reveals a clear migration of research focus in this field. Early years were dominated by the “diagnosis and management” cluster, while the past decade has seen a shift toward “anesthetic technique and recurrence” and “pain management.” The recent emergence of survivorship and quality of life as burst keywords reflects a growing emphasis on patient-centered care. Importantly, these research transitions are gradual and overlapping, with new hotspots building on previous themes.

Burst keyword analysis provides further insight into shifting priorities. From 1999 to 2012, keywords like “carcinoma” and “breast cancer” were prominent, highlighting the foundational emphasis on disease epidemiology and clinical management [1–3]. The mid-2000s saw an increase in terms such as “experience” and “trial” (2004–2009), paralleling the rise of large clinical trials and long-term follow-up studies that shaped evidence-based surgical and perioperative strategies [7, 37].

Between 2009 and 2011, “radiofrequency ablation” became a burst keyword, reflecting growing interest in minimally invasive, image-guided techniques [42]. As questions about long-term efficacy and patient selection emerged, research focus shifted towards refining indications and integrating these methods with established surgical and anesthetic protocols [43].

From 2011 to 2016, “general anesthesia” dominated, underscoring intense interest in how anesthetic techniques may affect tumor biology, immune modulation, and recurrence risk [9, 26, 34]. Seminal studies, such as Exadaktylos et al. (2006), sparked ongoing comparative and mechanistic research [26], although meta-analyses and reviews continue to show inconclusive results [10, 12].

Since 2019, keywords like “postoperative pain,” “persistent pain,” “survival,” and “quality” have surged, marking a shift toward long-term, patient-centered outcomes and survivorship. Chronic pain is now recognized as a major determinant of quality of life after breast cancer surgery [38–40], and innovations in pain management—such as regional anesthesia and opioid-sparing regimens—are increasingly validated by evidence showing benefits in recovery and patient satisfaction [9, 38]. The ongoing burst of “survival” also highlights efforts to clarify the impact of perioperative anesthetic management on long-term outcomes [9, 33], while “quality” reflects a broader focus on healthcare standards and patient-reported outcomes [41]. Despite suggestive evidence, the effect of anesthetic technique on cancer recurrence is still unresolved, limited by retrospective study designs, heterogeneity, and lack of robust RCT data [9, 13, 14].

To address these gaps, several strategies are needed. First, the creation of a global breast cancer anesthesia outcomes database would enable large-scale, real-world analyses and facilitate robust adjustment for confounding factors. Second, the advancement of prospective, multicenter RCTs comparing TIVA and volatile anesthesia—incorporating molecular biomarkers, immune function, and patient-reported outcomes—remains essential for resolving ongoing controversies. Third, greater integration of translational research, combining laboratory and clinical approaches, will be critical for elucidating mechanisms linking anesthesia to cancer biology [9, 13, 14]. Fourth, expanding research into chronic pain prevention, rehabilitation, and survivorship will ensure perioperative care is aligned with patient priorities [38, 39, 41]. Finally, fostering global collaboration and open data sharing will accelerate knowledge translation and reduce geographic disparities.

Strengths and limitations

This bibliometric analysis systematically examines nearly four decades of research at the intersection of breast cancer and general anesthesia, identifying key research hotspots, thematic trends, and influential contributors. The use of advanced tools like VOSviewer and CiteSpace enabled detailed mapping of collaboration networks, keyword co-occurrence, and citation patterns, offering nuanced insights into evolving research priorities. However, several limitations exist. The analysis was limited to English-language publications in the WoSCC, introducing potential language and database bias and possibly overlooking relevant studies in other languages or databases. Additionally, the effectiveness of bibliometric methods—such as burst detection and cluster analysis—depends on data density and may be less robust for emerging or underdeveloped topics. The study’s reliance on publication and citation metrics may not fully capture the clinical quality or real-world impact of research. Furthermore, the composition of the keyword clusters is sensitive to parameter choices and may therefore vary. We selected a minimum keyword-occurrence of five based on the number of articles included and on precedent in the literature; nonetheless, potential bias arising from this threshold cannot be fully excluded. Finally, the analysis did not address clinical subgroups (e.g., cancer subtype, patient population, or specific anesthetic protocols), which are important for translating trends into clinical recommendations. Future research should integrate qualitative assessments and clinical data to provide a more comprehensive, practice-oriented perspective.

Conclusion

This bibliometric study employing cluster analysis identified four primary research hotspots at the intersection of breast cancer and general anesthesia: (1) the impact of anesthetic technique on cancer recurrence and metastasis, (2) diagnostic and management strategies, (3) surgical approaches and pain outcomes, and (4) analgesic techniques and perioperative management. Citation burst analysis revealed a clear thematic shift: while earlier research focused on diagnosis and surgical methods, the past decade has increasingly emphasized recurrence prevention, long-term pain management, survivorship, and quality of life. These findings highlight the evolving priorities in the field and suggest that future research will center on optimizing anesthetic protocols and perioperative care to improve both oncological and patient-centered outcomes for breast cancer patients.
