Volcanic scoria as a sustainable alternative to sand in structural lightweight concrete
Authors: Aklilu Shitu, Ermias Shitu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the potential of using scoria, a lightweight volcanic aggregate, as a sustainable replacement for natural sand in the production of structural lightweight concrete, addressing the challenges posed by sand scarcity in Ethiopia. The concrete mixtures were proportioned to achieve a minimum compressive strength of 25 MPa, with natural sand partially or fully replaced by scoria at volumetric substitution levels of 35%, 50%, 70%, and 100%. Key concrete properties were assessed at curing ages of 7, 28, and 56 days. The experimental program included slump, compressive strength, split tensile strength, flexural strength, water penetration, and ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) tests. The 35% replacement mix achieved the highest compressive strength at 43.6 MPa, surpassing the control sample (39.6 MPa), while the 50% replacement yielded optimal flexural (4.88 MPa) and tensile strengths (3.36 MPa). Workability decreased with increasing scoria content, and densities dropped from 2405 kg/m³ (control) to 2164 kg/m³ (100% scoria). Scoria’s microstructural properties, observed via SEM and XRD, confirmed its glassy, porous structure, contributing to enhanced water resistance, with penetration depths decreasing to 20 mm at 35% scoria replacement. These findings suggest that scoria, at optimal levels, can improve concrete’s mechanical and durability properties, offering a viable, eco-friendly sand alternative.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17494-0.

Introduction

Concrete is one of the most widely recognized and utilized human-made materials in construction worldwide. After water, it is the second most consumed material on Earth, with an estimated annual usage of 30 million tons1 and it plays a crucial role in construction, serving as a fundamental component in structural frameworks. It provides the foundational support, strength, and contributes significantly to the overall quality of a building. Concrete can be tailored into various forms to meet specific requirements based on the type of building or structure being constructed. Due to its extensive use, numerous researchers are studying the engineering properties of concrete across Nano, micro, and macro scales2. Concrete consists mainly of aggregates, water, and cement, and frequently incorporates supplementary cementitious materials along with chemical admixtures1.

Concrete is one of the most versatile construction materials, able to be shaped into nearly any structural form ranging from cylindrical water tanks to rectangular beams and columns used in high-rise structures. Despite its solid and durable nature, one of the practical challenges associated with concrete is its weight. Concrete is typically considered a heavy material due to its standard density range of approximately 2200–2400 kg/m33. This issue becomes particularly significant with the advent of modern concrete technologies, such as precast and prefabricated concrete and offsite construction, as they increase structural dead loads. Consequently, larger cranes and trucks with high lifting and transport capacities are needed. The need for larger cranes and high-capacity transport trucks not only escalates construction costs but also significantly amplifies the environmental footprint due to increased fuel consumption, emissions, and logistical demands4–6. This challenge can be mitigated by using concrete mixtures containing lighter materials as a result, lightweight concrete has been developed and is now widely used in construction projects where reducing the weight of structures is essential2. Rajaonarison7 examined the use of scoria in lightweight concrete, showing that mixes enhanced with scoria achieve favorable compressive strength while reducing overall weight an ideal feature for areas prone to seismic activity. Their research highlights scoria’s potential as a substitute for traditional sand, providing a balance between weight reduction and durability, which supports sustainable construction practices.

The choice of aggregates is crucial in concrete production, as they constitute about 60 to 75% of the concrete’s total volume. Aggregates not only contribute to concrete’s strength but also add bulk, aiding in the concrete’s placement. Warati et al.8 explored the suitability of scoria as a fine aggregate, revealing that it provides effective insulation properties while preserving the structural integrity of concrete. They concluded that scoria’s porous structure contributes to reducing concrete density, enhancing its insulating properties, and making it a viable option for load-bearing and thermally efficient structures. Globally, the construction industry continues to advance, with researchers striving to lower the cost of concrete materials and thus overall construction expenses by examining and validating the effectiveness of locally sourced materials as potential substitutes.

Researchers have suggested the use of various recycled and waste materials as sustainable alternatives in concrete production, including marble waste9,10, waste glass11,12 demolition waste, various types of ash12,13, coffee14, plastic14,15, ceramic waste16, sugarcane bagasse ash (SCBA)17, waste oyster shell18, microporous ethylene vinyl acetate19, crushed clay brick20, glass and polypropylene fibers21, treated bamboo fiber22, Peruvian sisal fiber23 among others.

In construction, utilizing alternative materials aims to reduce raw material consumption and costs, effectively addressing both waste management and resource scarcity. Additionally, these innovative materials can enhance certain properties of concrete, contributing to improved performance and sustainability in building practices.

Comprehensive studies conducted on lightweight expanded polystyrene (EPS) concrete that incorporates fly ash and silica fume, demonstrating notable improvements in both mechanical properties and thermal insulation24–26. Their findings emphasize the effectiveness of EPS in reducing the density of concrete and enhancing its insulating capabilities, making it well-suited for non-structural applications while contributing to improved energy efficiency in buildings. Babu et al.24 studied EPS aggregate concretes incorporating fly ash, finding that the mix exhibited increased compressive strength and better durability. Their research highlighted the effectiveness of EPS in creating a lightweight, cohesive concrete matrix that resists cracking, making it suitable for low-load applications, including partition walls and insulation layers.

Conventional concrete, while structurally reliable, is resource-intensive and contributes significantly to environmental degradation through the depletion of river sand and high carbon emissions. In contrast, scoria offers both technical and practical advantages it is lightweight, locally abundant in volcanic regions, and has favorable mechanical and thermal properties. It also contributes to reducing the density of concrete without compromising strength, making it ideal for lightweight structural applications. The incorporation of scoria addresses limitations in current concrete types by reducing dead loads, improving thermal insulation, and supporting more sustainable construction practices. These benefits formed the technical basis for selecting scoria as a replacement for fine aggregate in our investigation.

Fine aggregate is a crucial component of concrete that warrants significant focus in the development of environmentally friendly, or “green,” concrete. Aslani et al.27 investigate high-performance self-compacting concrete incorporating recycled concrete aggregates and rubber granules, with focus on enhancing workability, durability, and minimizing environmental impact. The findings suggest that substituting traditional aggregates with these lightweight materials provides strong load-bearing capacity while reducing density, making it ideal for eco-friendly, structurally robust construction applications. Yifru et al.28 explored replacing a portion of sand with marble waste and scoria in lightweight concrete, demonstrating favorable strength and thermal properties. Their findings indicated that these alternative materials not only minimized environmental waste but also produced concrete with lower density and thermal conductivity, providing sustainable solutions for non-structural applications. Morales-Conde et al.29 emphasized that utilizing scoria as a replacement for sand addresses the immediate shortage of materials while contributing to the production of green concrete. Scoria’s inherent porosity decreases concrete density, enhancing insulation properties and lowering the environmental footprint by reducing resource extraction needs. Furthermore, substituting river sand with scoria alleviates the environmental consequences of river sand mining, helping to preserve ecosystems and support sustainable construction practices. This approach aligns with green building principles by minimizing waste, conserving natural resources, and promoting the use of eco-friendly, durable materials in construction.

The use of alternative raw materials to enhance the properties of concrete in both its fresh and hardened states has evolved considerably over time. Recent studies have investigated the use of wood sawdust30, glass fiber reinforcement, recycled concrete, and rice husk ash31, as well as secondary aluminum chips32, residual wood ash33, and refractory brick waste34. While conventional concrete remains structurally reliable, it is highly resource-intensive and contributes significantly to environmental degradation, particularly through the depletion of natural river sand and high carbon emissions associated with material processing.

Research such as that by Aslani et al.27 demonstrates that gradual replacement of sand with alternative materials helps identify the optimal performance levels without significantly compromising the concrete’s structural integrity. In studies on lightweight concrete7,8, observed that gradual increases in lightweight aggregate content enable researchers to monitor how properties like compressive and tensile strength change with higher aggregate levels.

Scoria presents both technical and environmental advantages. It is a lightweight, porous volcanic material that is locally abundant in many regions, including Ethiopia as shown in Fig. 1. Its abundance in Ethiopia is significant according to Zeleke et al.35, Ethiopia is the fourth leading global producer of pumice and scoria aggregates, following Italy, Chile, and Ecuador. Its use in concrete can reduce the overall structural dead load without compromising strength, and its insulating properties contribute to better thermal performance. These characteristics make scoria an ideal candidate for lightweight and sustainable concrete applications. In this study, the incorporation of scoria as a partial or full replacement for fine aggregate was investigated, aiming to address the environmental and technical limitations of conventional concrete and support more sustainable construction practices.

From a practical standpoint, while scoria improves sustainability and reduces unit weight, it often demands higher water content or the use of superplasticizers to maintain adequate workability. These performance trade-offs between strength, workability, and eco-efficiency are emphasized in the review by Fořt et al.36, who highlight that optimization of aggregate packing and porosity control is essential for ensuring structural integrity in lightweight concrete applications.

The construction industry in Ethiopia is expanding rapidly, with concrete extensively used for residential, industrial, and multi-story building projects. This surge in construction activity has driven up the demand for concrete, which, in turn, strains the supply of natural concrete ingredients, raising concerns about resource depletion. Such challenges highlight the importance of sustainable material sourcing to support long-term economic growth, especially in developing countries. As a widely available natural resource in Ethiopia, scoria presents a promising alternative to sand in concrete production. Its abundance offers a viable, cost-effective local material, potentially decreasing dependency on conventional sand, which may be expensive or unsustainable to acquire. By incorporating scoria into concrete, the Ethiopian construction sector could achieve greater resource efficiency and support sustainable building practices, ultimately benefiting both the environment and the local economy. Therefore, the primary objective of this paper is to assess the potential of scoria as a replacement for sand.

The novelty of this study lies in its comprehensive assessment of volcanic scoria as a sustainable alternative to natural sand in structural lightweight concrete, specifically within the Ethiopian construction context. Unlike previous research that often limited scoria replacement to low or moderate levels, this study systematically evaluates high-volume fine aggregate replacement ratios (35%, 50%, 70%, and 100% ) offering a broader understanding of scoria’s feasibility. A key innovation is the integration of multiple performance assessments, including mechanical properties (compressive, flexural, and split tensile strengths), durability indicators (water permeability and ultrasonic pulse velocity), and advanced microstructural analyses using SEM and XRD techniques. These analyses revealed scoria’s glassy, porous morphology and pozzolanic activity, directly linking its microstructure to improved strength and durability. Moreover, the study incorporates a practical cost-benefit analysis based on real-time Ethiopian market prices, demonstrating significant economic savings alongside environmental advantages such as reduced carbon emissions and minimized river sand mining. This holistic approach bridging mechanical performance, durability, microstructural insight, and economic-environmental impacts positions the study as a novel and impactful contribution to the advancement of sustainable construction materials.

Methodology adopted

The testing procedures, equipment, and methodologies used to evaluate concrete properties in this study adhered to ASTM specifications. Laboratory investigations included tests on both fresh and hardened concrete. Fresh concrete was evaluated with a slump flow test as seen in Fig. 5 to ensure suitable workability42. For hardened concrete, assessments encompassed compressive strength tests, indirect tensile tests (such as split cylinder and flexural strength tests), and durability evaluations, including water penetration and ultrasonic pulse velocity tests. These mechanical properties are crucial for determining the overall quality and performance of concrete in construction applications43,44. The characteristics of different materials used in producing concrete with scoria aggregates are also explored, including moisture content, unit weight, specific gravity, and grain size distribution.

XRD analysis and findings

X-ray diffraction (XRD) is an essential analytical method widely used to identify chemical phases and measure the crystallite size in materials, including volcanic rocks like scoria45. Scoria’s complex mineral composition makes XRD an ideal tool for detailed characterization, offering insights into its structural properties and phase composition as shown in Table 3. In this study, XRD analysis was conducted to determine the chemical phases and crystallite size of scoria. This information is valuable for understanding scoria’s suitability as a construction material, as it provides details on its mineral structure and potential performance in concrete applications. To extract meaningful information from XRD output, several analytical methods can be employed, as outlined by Speakman46. These include basic XRD data analysis using HighScore Plus software version 5.0 (https://www.malvernpanalytical.com/en/products/category/software/x-ray-diffraction software/highscore-with-plus-option), which facilitates phase identification and comparison with standard diffraction patterns. Additionally, profile fitting techniques can be applied to determine crystallite size and other peak characteristics.

According to chemical tests conducted by the Ethiopian Geological Survey shown in Table 4, scoria contains 53.44% SiO₂, 9.81% Al₂O₃, and 11.16% Fe₂O₃, totaling 74.41%. This meets47 standards for classification as a pozzolanic material, which contributes to increased concrete strength. Moreover, elevated SO₃ content may result in reduced strength and greater drying shrinkage in mortars subjected to sodium sulfate exposure. The SO₃ content in scoria is only 0.07%, well below the 3% threshold, indicating that using scoria may reduce drying shrinkage in concrete mixes. The safe level of chloride in concrete is a somewhat debated topic, with a traditional view suggesting that chloride levels below 0.4% by cement mass pose a low corrosion risk, 0.4–1% a medium risk, and above 1% a high risk. Given scoria’s chloride content of less than 0.1%, it falls well within the safe level. Additionally, tests by the Ethiopian Geological Survey indicate a very low loss on ignition for scoria, suggesting minimal mass change during concrete production .

Microstructural analysis of scoria and sand samples

The microstructure of a solid is defined by the type, quantity, size, shape, and distribution of its phases. This microstructure in materials (like sand, scoria, metals, polymers, ceramics, or composites) significantly affects physical properties such as strength, toughness, ductility, hardness, resistance to corrosion, performance at high or low temperatures, and wear resistance45. Recent research by Nie48 indicates that the porous structure of scoria increases water resistance and decreases the density of concrete. SEM and XRD analyses support these microstructural benefits, highlighting scoria’s potential for use in lightweight, durable concrete applications. A microstructural analysis was conducted to compare the microstructures of sand and scoria using an optical microscope, aiming to assess porosity and morphological similarities as seen in Fig. 6. The structure of sand and scoria used in the research is shown in Figs. 7 and 8. In the figure it can be noticed that the shape of both sand and scoria is irregular.

Mix Preparation

The primary objective of this research is to conduct an experimental study exploring the feasibility of using scoria as a substitute for fine aggregate in concrete. All experimental procedures were conducted at the Construction Material Laboratory at Addis Ababa Institute of Technology as seen in Fig. 14. In this study, the mix proportions were prepared following51. Five concrete mixes with a target minimum compressive strength of 25 MPa were prepared. One mix served as the reference, containing only natural aggregate, while the other four mixes incorporated scoria aggregate, replacing natural aggregate by 35%, 50%, 70%, and 100% by volume with an equivalent volume of scoria

Experimental program

Mix design is the process of finding the right proportions of coarse aggregate, fine aggregate, water and cement for a pre-required concrete strength according to52. Although there are lots of papers and standards for determining mix design, the perfect mix design can only be mastered through repetition of trial mixes and following the correct path which results in a continuous error reduction, when compared with the anticipated result53.

For each concrete mix as seen in Table 7, three samples were prepared for compressive strength testing at 7, 28, and 56 days, using 15 cm x 15 cm x 15 cm concrete cubes. A total of 75 cubes were cast 45 for compressive strength testing across various scoria replacement percentages, and 30 for water penetration testing at different scoria levels. Additionally, three specimens for each replacement level were prepared for splitting tensile strength and flexural tests, and the test also conducted at 7, 28, and 56 days. The splitting tensile strength tests used 15 cm x 30 cm concrete cylinders, totaling 45 cylinders across scoria replacement levels. For flexural strength, 10 cm x 10 cm x 50 cm molds were used, with 45 beams in total Concrete mixes with scoria aggregate were tested to meet the same performance standards as concrete with natural aggregate (Fig. 15).

Results and discussion

Workability

In this study the slump was determined for each fresh concrete mix according to42. The experimental test results shown in Fig. 16 indicate that the workability of fresh concrete declines as the proportion of scoria used as fine aggregate increases. With higher scoria content, the blended fine aggregate becomes finer. This reduction in workability is likely due to the increased fineness modulus of scoria compared to river sand and the greater porosity of scoria, both of which affect the aggregate’s coarseness and interaction within the concrete mix.

Density of concrete

At the end of the required curing days, 3 concrete cubes specimen were taken out for each mix from curing tank to measure the density of concrete according to54 before conducting compressive strength. The density of the test sample at the age of the 28th day for the different levels or proportions of scoria replacement was determined, and the results are indicated in Fig. 17.

Fig. 17Density of 150 mm cubes concrete on 28 days.

Density of 150 mm cubes concrete on 28 days.

The figures indicate that concrete density decreases as the percentage of scoria increases, attributed to scoria’s lower unit weight. This reduction in density helps to lower the concrete’s self-weight, allowing for a reduced design dead load on structural members. Consequently, this enables smaller concrete cross-sectional dimensions to support structural elements, which can lead to savings in concrete volume.

Compressive strength of concrete

Tests for compressive strength of concrete were carried out in this research according to55 after the cube samples were fully immersed in water for 7, 28 and 56 days. The tests were performed on 150 mm cubes as shown in Fig. 19.

The improvements in concrete properties as shown in Fig. 18 were attributed to the unique physical characteristics of scoria. Scoria has lower silt and clay content than river sand, which supports proper cement hydration and strengthens the bond between aggregates, enhancing compressive strength. The blend of finer river sand with coarser scoria created a well-graded particle size distribution, increasing the solid volume and, consequently, the concrete’s strength. The highest compressive strength was observed at a 35% scoria replacement. Beyond this level, as scoria content increased, the volume of finer sand decreased, reducing the solid volume and the paste-aggregate interaction due to a diminished packing effect, leading to a decline in compressive strength. However, even at 50%, 70%, and 100% scoria replacement, compressive strength remained comparable to the control mix due to the well-graded particle distribution of the blended aggregate versus river sand alone .

The experimental findings indicate that the 35% scoria replacement mix achieves the highest compressive strength (43.6 MPa), exceeding that of the control sample (39.6 MPa). This enhancement aligns with findings from7, who noted improved compressive strength at optimal scoria contents due to the combined effect of scoria’s porous structure and pozzolanic reactivity. The pozzolanic reaction enhances the formation of secondary calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel, leading to a denser microstructure and improved aggregate-cement interface bonding. However, beyond the 35% replacement, compressive strength gradually decreases, a trend consistent with observations by8, who attributed similar outcomes to reduced aggregate packing density and weaker interfacial transition zones at higher replacement levels. The decreased strength at elevated scoria contents may also be linked to higher water demand and increased porosity, which negatively affect the cement paste’s cohesiveness and load-bearing capacity. Despite this decrease, the compressive strength remained adequate even at higher scoria levels, suggesting the practical feasibility of using scoria for non-structural applications.

Environmental benefits

The extraction and processing of natural sand are associated with significant environmental challenges, including riverbank erosion, biodiversity loss, and high carbon emissions from transportation and washing processes. For instance, sand mining in the Mekong Delta has led to serious riverbank erosion and destabilization due to a lack of awareness and regulatory control60. Similarly, extensive river mining has been linked to ecological degradation and loss of biodiversity across river systems61. In contrast, scoria is a naturally available volcanic material, often locally abundant in Ethiopia, which requires minimal processing. Its lightweight, porous nature contributes to reducing the structural dead load, potentially lowering material demand in foundations and superstructures. A study by62 confirmed that volcanic scoria can effectively replace natural sand in cement-based materials without compromising strength. Thus, replacing sand with scoria not only reduces the ecological footprint but also aligns with sustainable construction practices .

Conclusion

The findings of this study confirm the suitability of scoria as a partial replacement for fine aggregate in concrete production. Scoria exhibited superior quality, notably a lower silt content compared to river sand, and its lower specific gravity contributed to reduced concrete density. This density reduction led to a lighter concrete with an enhanced strength-to-weight ratio, improving structural efficiency. Replacing sand with scoria improved key concrete properties, including compressive strength and water resistance, particularly at 35% – 50% replacement levels. At these ratios, scoria-based concrete achieved optimal performance, demonstrating low water permeability and a strong correlation between ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) and compressive strength, supporting UPV as a reliable non-destructive quality indicator.

The chemical analysis revealed that scoria contains high levels of reactive oxides, including SiO₂ (53.44%), Al₂O₃ (9.81%), and Fe₂O₃ (11.16%), which together exceed 70%, satisfying the requirements of ASTM C618 for classification as a natural pozzolan. These oxides, particularly amorphous silica, engage in pozzolanic reactions with calcium hydroxide released during cement hydration, forming additional calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel.

This secondary C–S–H formation enhances the interfacial transition zone (ITZ) between the scoria particles and the cement paste, resulting in stronger particle bonding, reduced microcracking, and fewer interconnected voids. The SEM analysis further supports this by showing a dense matrix and improved microstructural integrity at optimal scoria replacement levels.

These chemical and microstructural improvements have a direct influence on the water permeability performance. The water penetration test results show that the lowest penetration depth (≈ 20 mm) occurred at 35% scoria replacement, indicating a denser and less permeable matrix. This finding aligns with existing literatures that emphasizes the role of pozzolanic reactions in refining pore structure and enhancing impermeability.

While lower replacement levels (e.g., 35%) offered balanced strength and durability, higher levels (up to 100%) may be suitable for lightweight, non-structural applications. Given its local availability in Ethiopia, scoria presents a sustainable alternative to river sand, reducing material transportation and aligning with green concrete and resource conservation initiatives.

Future scope

Future research on scoria-based concrete should focus on long-term durability aspects such as creep, shrinkage, and fatigue under extended loading. Incorporating supplementary materials like fly ash or silica fume may further enhance mechanical performance for high-performance use. Detailed SEM and XRD analyses at varied magnifications could offer deeper insights into microstructure, particularly porosity and bonding.

Expanding UPV testing across different concrete grades and curing conditions would improve its value for quality control. Optimizing scoria’s fineness modulus could enhance workability and broaden its application in sustainable construction. Extending curing periods beyond 56 days may also reveal additional strength gains, as some studies suggest continued development up to 90 days.

Finally, developing a regression model to correlate UPV with compressive strength would support reliable, non-destructive strength estimation in field applications, improving quality control and structural evaluation of lightweight scoria-based concrete.
