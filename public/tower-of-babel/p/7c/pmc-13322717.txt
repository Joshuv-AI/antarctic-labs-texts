Restoring stability at the craniovertebral junction using the Goel technique: Outcomes of posterior C1–C2 fixation in 216 patients
Authors: Jignesh Joshi, Srikant Balasubramaniam, Banumathy Srikant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Atlantoaxial instability arising from diverse craniovertebral junction (CVJ) pathologies poses substantial surgical challenges because of complex regional biomechanics and proximity to critical neurovascular structures. Posterior C1–C2 fixation using the Goel–Harms technique is widely adopted; however, large institutional outcome data remain limited.Objective:The objective of this study was to evaluate the clinical and radiological outcomes, perioperative parameters, and complications following posterior C1–C2 fixation in patients with CVJ anomalies and atlantoaxial instability.Materials and Methods:A retrospective cohort study was performed of patients who underwent posterior C1 lateral mass–C2 pedicle screw fixation between January 2008 and December 2023 at a single tertiary center. Clinical outcomes were assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), Nurick grading system, and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI). Radiological evaluation included the atlantodental interval (ADI), clivus–canal angle, and standard craniometric lines. Perioperative variables, complications, and length of hospital stay were analyzed.Results:A total of 216 patients were included (mean age: 34.6 ± 5.2 years; 62.0% male). Traumatic pathology was the most common etiology (45.4%), followed by congenital (24.1%) and degenerative causes (16.7%). Significant postoperative improvement was observed in VAS (5.39 ± 1.68–1.46 ± 1.21), Nurick grade (3.68 ± 1.26–2.06 ± 0.87), and ODI (49.4 ± 12.2–22.19 ± 9.3) (all P < 0.001). Radiological parameters demonstrated significant correction, including ADI and clivus–canal angle (P < 0.001). The overall mortality rate was 1.39%. The mean intensive care unit stay was 2.3 ± 1.4 days, and the mean hospital stay was 8.5 ± 3.3 days.Conclusion:Posterior C1–C2 fixation using the Goel–Harms technique provides safe, reproducible, and effective stabilization, with significant clinical and radiological improvement in patients with atlantoaxial instability associated with CVJ anomalies.

INTRODUCTION

The atlantoaxial (C1–C2) region is a highly mobile and biomechanically complex segment of the upper cervical spine and lies in close proximity to critical neurovascular structures, including the cervicomedullary junction, upper cervical spinal cord, and vertebral arteries, which makes surgical stabilization in this region technically demanding.[1] Disruption of the osseoligamentous stabilizing elements of this complex, resulting from trauma, congenital and developmental anomalies, inflammatory disorders, degenerative disease, and neoplastic involvement, may lead to atlantoaxial instability.[2] Congenital stigmata such as odontoid hypoplasia, atlas arch defects, and syndromic craniovertebral junction (CVJ) malformations are frequently associated with ligamentous insufficiency and predispose to pathological mobility at the C1–C2 segment.[3] Clinically, atlantoaxial instability commonly presents with neck pain, restriction of cervical motion, long-tract signs, and progressive cervical myelopathy.[4]

Diagnosis is established by correlating clinical findings with imaging investigations. Dynamic cervical radiographs are used to demonstrate pathological motion, computed tomography (CT) is employed to define osseous anatomy and facilitate instrumentation planning, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is essential for evaluation of neural compression and associated soft-tissue pathology. Intraoperative and perioperative imaging techniques have also been incorporated to support accurate instrumentation in this anatomically constrained region.[5] Surgical stabilization is indicated in patients with neurological compromise or mechanically unstable disease. Posterior fixation represents the principal method of management of atlantoaxial instability, and segmental fixation based on the Goel–Harms principle using C1 lateral mass and C2 pedicle or pars screws connected by rods has become a widely adopted posterior stabilization technique.[6]

Achievement of solid arthrodesis remains a fundamental objective of C1–C2 surgery. Posterior decortication of the C1 and C2 posterior elements or the atlantoaxial joint surfaces, followed by placement of bone graft, has traditionally been performed to promote fusion, and both autologous graft and alternative graft materials have been used to support posterior atlantoaxial fusion.[7] More recently, three-dimensional planning and patient-specific template-guided techniques have been introduced to further improve the accuracy of posterior C1–C2 screw placement in complex anatomy.[8]

This study aimed to evaluate the clinical and radiological outcomes of posterior C1–C2 fixation in patients with CVJ anomalies and atlantoaxial instability.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Department of Neurosurgery, TNMC and BYL Nair Hospital, Mumbai, by reviewing medical records of patients diagnosed with CVJ anomalies who underwent posterior C1 lateral mass–C2 pedicle screw fixation using the Goel–Harms technique between January 2008 and December 2023. CVJ anomalies were defined as congenital or acquired disorders involving the occiput–C1–C2 complex associated with atlantoaxial instability.

Clinical assessment and outcome measures

All patients underwent detailed preoperative clinical evaluation, including a comprehensive history, general physical examination, and focused neurological examination. Particular attention was given to the presence of external or clinical stigmata suggestive of underlying syndromic or congenital CVJ anomalies.

Neurological status, severity of myelopathy, and functional impairment were documented preoperatively and during follow-up. Pain severity was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS). Functional and neurological outcomes were evaluated using the Nurick grading system and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI). Postoperative clinical assessments were performed at each scheduled follow-up visit, and any neurological deterioration, new deficits, or complications were recorded.

Radiological parameters assessed included the atlantodental interval (ADI) and craniometric lines (McRae, McGregor, and Chamberlain lines). The clivus–canal angle was measured for assessment of basilar invagination and cervicomedullary alignment.

Surgical technique and intraoperative imaging adjunct

All procedures were performed using a posterior midline approach and segmental C1–C2 fixation based on the Goel–Harms principle. Following a sharp midline skin incision to the level of the nuchal fascia, the fascia was opened, and paraspinal muscles were dissected in a subperiosteal fashion using monopolar cautery. Care was taken to preserve the caudal cervical musculature attached to the C2 spinous process, along with the supraspinous and interspinous ligaments, wherever feasible.

The neurovascular bundle comprising the C2 nerve root and the venous plexus in the C1–C2 region was identified and dissected only as necessary and was preferably preserved. Facet or lateral mass joint decortication was not routinely performed during instrumentation. Intraoperative fluoroscopy using a C-arm was employed for level confirmation, trajectory guidance, and verification of implant positioning. Where available, intraoperative navigation was used to assist accurate screw placement.

Instrumentation was performed by inserting C1 lateral mass or pedicle screws, followed by C2 pedicle screws. Screw trajectories were prepared using a calibrated drill guide and power drilling, followed by guidewire-assisted insertion of self-tapping screws. Rods were then applied and secured to achieve segmental fixation. Intraoperative imaging was repeated after screw placement to confirm accuracy. Any malpositioned screws were revised before final fixation. Wounds were closed in a multilayer fashion over suction drainage.

Fusion strategy and assessment

Posterior arthrodesis was performed using a bone graft placed over the posterior elements of C1 and C2. Autologous bone graft material was used. Postoperative bony fusion was primarily assessed at 6 months using dynamic flexion–extension radiographs and CT imaging. In addition, postoperative cervical CT scans and two-plane radiographs were evaluated at various intervals by a senior neuroradiologist to assess for implant-related complications, including loosening, displacement, or fracture, and to determine the status of osseous atlantoaxial fusion. Fusion was defined as the presence of a bridging bony mass at any location between the C1 and C2 vertebrae in the absence of hardware failure.

Follow-up protocol

Patients were followed postoperatively at regular intervals of 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and annually thereafter. At each visit, clinical evaluation included a detailed neurological examination, pain assessment using the VAS, and functional outcome assessment using the Nurick grading system and ODI.

Radiological follow-up included dynamic cervical radiographs and CT imaging at 1 year to evaluate bony fusion, implant integrity, and alignment. MRI was performed selectively in patients with persistent or new neurological symptoms, suspected syringomyelia progression, or to evaluate neural decompression. All postoperative complications, reoperations, and episodes of neurological deterioration were documented throughout the follow-up period.

RESULTS

This was a retrospective single-center study conducted in 216 patients who underwent operative intervention for CVJ anomalies between January 2008 and December 2023 at the Department of Neurosurgery, TNMC and BYL Nair Charitable Hospital, Mumbai.

DISCUSSION

Atlantoaxial instability represents the common biomechanical consequence of a heterogeneous group of CVJ pathologies, including traumatic disruption of the osseoligamentous complex, congenital and developmental malformations, inflammatory ligamentous laxity, degenerative facet incompetence, and, less commonly, infective or neoplastic destruction. Progressive loss of segmental stability at C1–C2 leads to abnormal translational and rotational motion with resultant cervicomedullary compression, deformity of the craniovertebral alignment, and neurological compromise. Posterior segmental C1–C2 fixation using the Goel–Harms principle addresses the fundamental pathological substrate by providing immediate rigid stabilization, enabling reduction, restoring alignment, and facilitating arthrodesis and therefore remains the preferred surgical strategy in patients with symptomatic or mechanically unstable disease.

In the present cohort, a clear male predominance was observed in 134/216 (62.04%) patients, and the mean age at presentation was 34.6 ± 5.2 years, indicating that atlantoaxial instability requiring operative intervention most frequently affected young to middle-aged adults. Trauma was the most common underlying etiology in 98/216 (45.37%) patients, followed by congenital causes in 52/216 (24.07%) and degenerative pathology in 36/216 (16.67%), while inflammatory disorders were present in 17/216 (7.87%), infective causes in 8/216 (3.70%), and neoplastic pathology in 5/216 (2.31%). A similar etiological distribution has been reported in contemporary surgical series, in which trauma-related causes constitute the majority of indications for posterior C1–C2 fixation, with relatively fewer patients presenting with inflammatory or tumor-related disease.[910] The predominance of traumatic and degenerative etiologies in the present study reflects the evolving epidemiology of atlantoaxial instability in modern practice and underscores the declining relative contribution of inflammatory disorders with advances in disease-modifying medical therapy.

Neck pain or occipital headache was the most common presenting symptom, seen in 171/216 (79.17%) patients, followed by motor weakness in 146/216 (67.59%) and restriction of neck movements in 127/216 (58.79%). Cerebellar signs were present in 36/216 (16.67%), lower cranial nerve palsy in 21/216 (9.72%), and bladder or bowel involvement in 23/216 (10.64%) patients. Similar patterns of presentation, with predominance of pain and myelopathic symptoms, have been reported in contemporary atlantoaxial fixation series.[1112] The coexistence of pyramidal symptoms with lower cranial nerve and autonomic involvement in a subset of patients suggests multilevel cervicomedullary compression related to both atlantoaxial instability and associated CVJ deformities, highlighting the need for timely surgical stabilization to prevent further neurological deterioration.

Basilar invagination was the most frequent associated congenital anomaly in this cohort, observed in 59/216 (27.31%) patients, followed by atlas assimilation in 39/216 (18.05%) and platybasia in 41/216 (18.98%). Chiari malformation was present in 28/216 (12.96%) patients and syringomyelia in 21/216 (9.72%). Less frequent abnormalities included block vertebrae, scoliosis, odontoid anomalies, and Klippel–Feil anomaly. Previous studies by Goel and colleagues have characterized similar congenital craniovertebral abnormalities and their relationship with atlantoaxial instability, noting that basilar invagination and altered facet and joint morphology are common structural substrates in such patients.[13] A study conducted by Shah et al. reported that patients with central (axial) AAD frequently demonstrate associated congenital CVJ abnormalities, including atlas assimilation and Chiari malformation, thereby emphasizing the contributory role of underlying osseous and developmental anomalies in the pathogenesis of atlantoaxial instability.[14] The similarity between the patterns of congenital stigmata in these cases and the present cohort reinforces the importance of detailed preoperative anatomical assessment to guide surgical reduction and fixation strategies.

In the present series, intraoperative screw repositioning or revision was required in 16/216 patients (7.40%), intraoperative neuro-navigation was utilized in 29/216 patient (13.42%), and additional posterior decompression in the form of C1 or C2 laminectomy and/or foramen magnum decompression was performed in 31/216 patients (14.35%). The mean intraoperative blood loss was 142 ± 54 ml, and the mean operative time was 164 ± 48 min. A study conducted by Itoh et al. demonstrated that navigation-assisted posterior C1–C2 fixation improves screw placement accuracy and reduces the need for intraoperative revision, particularly in patients with complex CVJ anatomy.[15] Similarly, a study conducted by Rezvani et al. reported that the selective use of advanced image guidance during posterior atlantoaxial fixation is beneficial in anatomically high-risk cases and does not need to be routinely applied in all patients.[10] These findings, together with the low rate of screw revision observed in the present cohort, indicate that conventional fluoroscopic guidance supplemented by targeted use of navigation is an effective and pragmatic strategy for posterior C1–C2 instrumentation.

Dural tear (8/216, 3.70%) and excessive blood loss (11/216, 5.09%) were the most frequent intraoperative adverse events, while postoperative wound-related complications (13/216, 6.01%) and transient neurological worsening (9/216, 4.17%) were the most common early postoperative complications. Vertebral artery injury occurred in 4/216 patients (1.85%), and the overall mortality was 3/216 (1.39%). Comparable perioperative morbidity profiles following posterior C1–C2 fixation have been reported in the literature, with major neurovascular complications remaining infrequent.[16] Low rates of vertebral artery injury and hardware-related complications after Goel–Harms fixation have likewise been documented in other contemporary series.[10] Overall, the similarity in complication patterns across published studies and the present cohort supports the safety of posterior atlantoaxial fixation when meticulous surgical technique and appropriate intraoperative imaging are employed. Achievement of solid arthrodesis remains a critical determinant of long-term construct stability following posterior atlantoaxial fixation. In the present study, posterior fusion was performed using an autologous bone graft placed over the posterior elements of C1 and C2, and fusion assessment was based on dynamic radiographs and CT. Previous studies have demonstrated that posterior C1–C2 fusion using either autograft or allograft provides reliable fusion rates and durable clinical outcomes when combined with rigid segmental fixation using screw–rod constructs.[711] The use of autologous graft in the present cohort was therefore consistent with established fusion strategies and aimed to optimize biological fusion potential while avoiding graft-related morbidity associated with alternative graft substitutes.

Significant postoperative improvement was observed in pain, neurological function, and functional disability, together with consistent radiological correction of atlantoaxial alignment, reduction in the ADI, and improvement in the clivus–canal angle and other craniometric parameters. Similar gains in patient-reported outcomes and neurological recovery after C1–C2 fusion have been reported by Kleinstück et al. and Guo et al.[911] Reliable restoration of atlantoaxial alignment following posterior fixation in patients with associated CVJ anomalies, together with parallel improvement in functional and radiological parameters, suggests that adequate reduction and rigid segmental stabilization are key determinants of meaningful neurological recovery in atlantoaxial instability.

The mean intensive care unit stay of 2.3 days and the mean overall hospital stay of 8.5 days are consistent with contemporary perioperative pathways for posterior atlantoaxial fixation that emphasize early mobilization and standardized postoperative care.[15] These findings suggest that modern screw–rod constructs provide sufficient immediate stability to facilitate early rehabilitation and safe postoperative recovery in patients undergoing C1–C2 fixation.

The present study has certain limitations. It is a retrospective, single-center analysis, and the findings therefore reflect the surgical practice, patient population, and perioperative protocols of a single tertiary referral unit. Although the cohort is relatively large, the heterogeneity of underlying CVJ pathologies and the absence of subgroup analyses based on specific etiology or anatomical subtype limit more detailed interpretation of disease-specific outcomes. Nevertheless, the present study represents one of the larger contemporary institutional experiences of posterior C1–C2 fixation and provides clinically relevant evidence supporting the safety, effectiveness, and reproducibility of the Goel–Harms technique across a broad spectrum of CVJ disorders.

CONCLUSION

Posterior C1–C2 fixation using the Goel–Harms technique provides reliable clinical and radiological improvement in patients with atlantoaxial instability associated with a broad spectrum of CVJ anomalies. In this large single-center cohort, the procedure was associated with a significant reduction in pain and disability, meaningful neurological recovery, restoration of atlantoaxial alignment, and satisfactory perioperative safety. Rigid segmental fixation, combined with appropriate patient selection, meticulous surgical technique, and selective use of intraoperative imaging adjuncts, allows effective stabilization and facilitates successful arthrodesis even in complex craniovertebral anatomy. These findings support posterior C1–C2 screw–rod fixation as a safe, reproducible, and durable surgical strategy for the management of symptomatic and mechanically unstable CVJ disorders.
