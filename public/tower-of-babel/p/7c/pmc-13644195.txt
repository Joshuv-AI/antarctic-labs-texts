Evaluation of AI developed for rib fracture detection in identifying vertebral fractures on abdominal CT
Authors: Hyunjung Kim, Yongfeng Gao, Jin Woo Kim, Jhii-Hyun Ahn, Ga-Young Yoon, Yeon-Jun Kim, Sung Min Ko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the performance of an AI algorithm originally developed for rib fracture detection in identifying vertebral fractures on abdominal CT scans.MethodsA retrospective study was performed on 58 patients with both CT and MRI scans. An AI model and ER clinicians independently assessed CT images for fractures of the vertebral body, transverse processes, and spinous processes. Two spine radiologists reviewed both CT and MRI data and established the reference standard.ResultsAt the patient level, both the AI algorithm and ER clinicians achieved 100% sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and F1-score. At the vertebral level, the AI algorithm achieved higher sensitivity than ER clinicians (67% vs. 54%, p < 0.05), with similar accuracy (93% vs. 94%, p = 0.73). At the anatomical location level, the AI algorithm achieved a sensitivity of 62%, accuracy of 98%, and an F1-score of 0.69, compared with 46% sensitivity (p < 0.05), 97% accuracy (p = 0.92), and an F1-score of 0.62 (p = 0.50) for ER clinicians. Among 178 confirmed fracture locations, the AI algorithm detected 110 cases, whereas ER clinicians identified 81. Of the 97 fractures missed by ER clinicians, the AI algorithm correctly detected 51.ConclusionsThe AI software originally trained for rib fracture detection identified vertebral fractures with higher sensitivity compared to those detected by ER clinicians. Its ability to identify otherwise missed fractures highlights its value as a supplementary tool in urgent care.Keywords: Vertebral fracture, Thoracolumbar vertebrae, Computed tomography, Deep learning, AI, Thoracolumbar

Introduction

Thoracolumbar (TL) spinal injuries occur in approximately 7% of patients with blunt trauma and account for nearly half of all spinal injuries [1–3]. Trauma patients with TL fractures exhibit higher admission rates, longer hospital stays, and increased mortality compared to those without such fractures [4]. Delayed or missed diagnosis of TL fractures can lead to complications such as radiculopathy, spinal cord injury, and neurological deterioration [5]. In emergency departments, fractures account for a substantial portion of diagnostic errors, with nearly 80% of all missed diagnoses involving undetected fractures [6], with vertebral fractures posing a particular challenge due to their complexity and subtle presentation [7]. Computed tomography is a recommended imaging for the first assessment of vertebral trauma due to its easy access and wide availability [8, 9].

The advent of artificial intelligence (AI) in medical imaging, particularly through deep learning, offers a promising solution to these challenges. AI algorithms have demonstrated the ability to improve both the sensitivity and specificity of fracture detection across various anatomical regions, including the upper and lower extremity [10–13], hip, pelvis [14, 15], and spine [16] using radiographic images. For CT-based fracture detection, AI models have been developed for rib fractures, achieving detection sensitivities comparable to those of attending radiologists, ranging from 65.7% to 92.9% [17–20]. Similar progress has been made in detecting cervical spine fractures [21, 22]. Despite these advancements, most AI models for fracture detection are trained for specific anatomical regions, such as the ribs, rather than being designed for broader applications. However, AI models are inherently data-driven and may have the ability to generalize related tasks [23].

This study aims to evaluate the performance of an AI model originally developed for rib fracture detection in identifying vertebral fractures on abdominal CT scans. By assessing the model’s effectiveness in this new application, we seek to determine its potential for broader clinical use and identify opportunities for further optimization in AI-based fracture detection.

Materials and methods

Data sets

This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB), and informed consent was waived. This study was conducted from November 2023 to August 2024 and included trauma patients aged 18 years or older who presented it to the Emergency Department of a Level 1 trauma center with suspected spinal fractures. All patients underwent both CT and MRI scans to confirm the diagnosis. CT images were obtained using abdominal CT scans performed immediately upon visit with the uCT 960 + (United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai). The images were reconstructed with a matrix size of 512 × 512, a slice thickness of 3 mm, and a standard soft tissue reconstruction kernel. CT images were used for analysis by the AI algorithm and ER clinicians, while MRI scans served as supplementary data for radiologists to establish the ground truth. The exclusion criteria included patients under 18 years of age, patients with pathological fractures involving primary cancer or metastasis, patients with metal implants from previous spinal surgery, and those with significant artifacts on CT images.

AI Algorithm

This study evaluated a state-of-the-art vendor-neutral artificial intelligence algorithm, uAI Easy Triage-Rib (Shanghai United Imaging Intelligence), originally designed to detect acute rib fractures on chest CT scans.

The model consists of two cascaded convolutional neural networks: one for rib segmentation and another for fracture detection. Both networks are based on a customized 3D U-Net architecture [24], known as RB-Net, which follows an encoder-decoder structure. This U-shaped design allows the model to capture multi-scale image features while enabling precise localization of fractures. Further details on the AI algorithm can be found in reference [25].

Notably, the algorithm was not explicitly trained for vertebral fracture detection, as all training data consisted solely of rib fracture cases. The model was directly applied to abdominal CT examinations without additional retraining, fine-tuning, or threshold optimization for vertebral fracture detection. CT examinations were acquired using routine clinical multidetector CT protocols. However, as highlighted in the introduction, AI models are inherently data-driven and may possess the capability to generalize across related tasks [23]. Although fine-tuning the model with spine fracture data through transfer learning is expected to further enhance its performance, this study assesses the model’s inherent adaptability without additional training, providing a benchmark for its effectiveness in identifying vertebral fractures on abdominal CT. Both image normalization and preprocessing were automatically performed within the vendor’s standard inference pipeline prior to analysis.

Ground truth and evaluation model

Both CT and MRI images were independently analyzed by a musculoskeletal radiologist with 7 years of experience and a neuroradiologist with 11 years of experience. The readers were blinded to the ER clinicians and AI readings. To avoid confusion caused by transitional vertebrae, we assumed the most caudal vertebral body of the lumbar spine to be L5. Both readers evaluated the presence or absence of fractures in each vertebral body (VB), right transverse process (RT), left transverse process (LT), and spinous process (SP) at each spine level. On CT scans, a transverse or spinous process fracture was defined as the presence of cortical discontinuity or an identifiable fracture fragment. A vertebral body fracture was defined by cortical discontinuity of the vertebral wall or endplate, anterior wall buckling, or a height loss (collapse) of 25% or more. Additionally, on MRI, the presence of bone marrow edema within these structures was also considered a fracture, even in the absence of the aforementioned CT findings. In cases of interobserver disagreement, a consensus ranking was derived, and the agreed-upon results were used for further analysis. The data evaluated by the two radiologists served as the ground truth (GT).

The ER clinician's readings were retrieved from emergency clinical records, which were documented based on the clinical examination and initial CT scan interpretations performed by orthopedic or neurosurgeons prior to MRI acquisition. Fractures in each the VB, RT, LT, and SP at each spine level were documented individually, similar to how radiologists detect fractures. Fractures not documented in the medical record were regarded as non-existent. Fractures detected by AI were also evaluated individually at VB, RT, LT, and SP at each spine level separately.

The analysis was conducted at the anatomical location, vertebral, and patient levels using the following definitions:

Location level

Each anatomical site at each vertebral level (VB, RT, LT, and SP from C1 to L5) was treated as an individual evaluation unit. A true positive (TP) was defined as a fracture detected at the same vertebral level and specific anatomical location confirmed by the ground truth (GT). A false positive (FP) was defined as a fracture detected at an incorrect vertebral level or incorrect anatomical location not confirmed by the GT. A false negative (FN) was defined as a fracture confirmed by the GT but missed by the AI model or ER clinician. A true negative (TN) was defined as an anatomical location correctly identified as fracture-negative where no fracture was confirmed by the GT.

Vertebral level

Each vertebra from C1 to L5 was considered as one evaluation unit. A vertebra was classified as positive if fracture was identified at any anatomical location within that vertebral level. TP, FP, FN, and TN were defined according to the presence or absence of fracture at the vertebral level compared with the GT.

Patient level

Each patient was considered as one evaluation unit. A patient was classified as positive if at least one vertebral fracture was identified anywhere in the evaluated spine. TP, FP, FN, and TN were defined according to the presence or absence of any fracture at the patient level compared with the GT.

Statistical analysis

The diagnostic performance of the AI system and ER physicians in fracture detection was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and F1-score. Statistical comparisons were performed at the level of individual vertebrae and anatomical locations, partially accounting for within-patient clustering. Confidence intervals for sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were calculated using the Wilson method, while F1-score confidence intervals were estimated using bootstrap resampling. Interobserver agreement between the reference standard and the second radiologist was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient.

Differences in sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were assessed using z-tests for proportions. McNemar’s test was used for paired comparison analyses based on the same evaluation units. At the patient, vertebral, and anatomical location levels, pairing was defined according to the corresponding evaluation unit assessed by both methods against the same reference standard. The resulting p-values are reported in the corresponding performance tables. Because multiple anatomical observations originated from the same patient, the data included within-patient clustering. Therefore, bootstrap analyses for F1-score confidence intervals and hypothesis testing were performed using patient-level resampling to preserve the clustered data structure. All analyses adhered to a two-sided significance threshold of α = 0.05, enabling rigorous and statistically sound comparisons of diagnostic performance.

Results

Dataset characteristics

This study initially included 59 subjects, encompassing 4,012 anatomical locations. However, one subject was excluded due to severe motion artifacts, resulting in a final sample size of 58 participants and 3,944 analyzed locations. The participants' ages ranged from 20 to 87 years, with a mean age of 56 ± 15 years. The gender distribution was skewed, with a male-to-female ratio of 37:21.

The overall interobserver agreement with the ground truth was excellent, with coefficients of 0.98 at the location level, 0.99 at the vertebral level, and 1.0 at the patient level. A total of 19 compression fractures were identified in 13 patients, diagnosed based on the presence of bone marrow edema on MRI in the absence of vertebral body collapse or cortical discontinuity.

The data highlights that the majority of fractures occurred between the T11 and L5 vertebral levels, with the lumbar spine showing the highest incidence, accounting for 77% of all identified fractures. Each fracture was distributed as VB 42%, LT 31%, RT 24%, and SP 3%.

Vertebral-level results

As shown in Table 3, at the vertebral level, the reference standard identified 134 fracture-positive vertebrae and 852 fracture-negative vertebrae among a total of 986 evaluated vertebral levels. The ER clinicians identified 74 vertebral levels as positive, including 72 true-positive and 2 false-positive findings, while 62 fracture-positive vertebrae were missed. In contrast, the AI algorithm identified 114 vertebral levels as positive, including 90 true-positive and 24 false-positive findings, with 44 missed fracture-positive vertebrae.

Table 4 summarizes the diagnostic performance metrics. Location-level results

Tables 5 compares the performance of the ER clinician and the AI algorithm against the ground truth (GT). As shown, there are 178 positive cases in total. The AI algorithm identified 110 of these positive cases, whereas the ER clinician detected 81. Of the 97 fractures missed by the ER clinician, the AI correctly detected 51. Conversely, of the 68 fractures missed by AI, 22 were identified by the ER clinician. This comparison highlights the complementary nature of the AI and ER clinician performance, suggesting that integrating AI into clinical workflows could enhance diagnostic outcomes. While the ER clinician reported only 2 false positives, the AI algorithm identified 31.

Discussion

This study evaluated the performance of an AI algorithm originally designed for rib fracture detection in identifying vertebral fractures. The results demonstrate that the AI algorithm achieved performance comparable to the ER clinician, suggesting its potential as a valuable adjunct in fracture diagnosis. Despite not being specifically trained for vertebral fractures, the AI model identified fractures across multiple locations, underscoring its versatility and adaptability in fracture detection.

Regarding diagnostic performance for fracture detection, both the ER clinicians and the AI algorithm achieved 100% sensitivity and specificity at the patient level. However, at the vertebral level, the AI algorithm demonstrated a higher sensitivity compared to the ER clinicians (67% vs. 54%). Similarly, at the location level, the AI algorithm outperformed the ER clinicians with a sensitivity of 62% vs. 46%. While both the ER clinicians and the AI algorithm maintained high specificity exceeding 97%, the ER clinicians showed slightly higher values compared to the AI algorithm at both the vertebral level (≈100% vs. 97%) and the location level (≈100% vs. 99%). This aligns with its role as a screening tool, designed to prioritize sensitivity over specificity to minimize the risk of missed fractures. In contrast, the ER clinician maintained higher specificity, excelling at reducing false positives and ensuring accurate confirmation of true negatives.

From a clinical workflow perspective, the AI model may potentially function as a second-reader or triage support tool to assist clinicians in identifying vertebral fractures that might otherwise be overlooked during routine abdominal CT interpretation. The sensitivity advantage of the AI system may help reduce missed fractures, particularly in busy emergency or general radiology settings. However, the increased sensitivity was accompanied by additional false-positive findings, which may increase review workload and require confirmation by radiologists or clinicians. In contrast, false-negative findings remain clinically important because missed vertebral fractures may delay diagnosis and management. Therefore, the current results support the role of the AI model as a supplementary assistive tool rather than a replacement for expert clinical interpretation.

In this dataset, vertebral body, right transverse process, and left transverse process fractures were the most common, accounting for 42%, 24%, and 31% of cases, respectively, while spinous process fractures were relatively rare (3%). Historically, transverse process fractures have been regarded as minor injuries, as described by Denis et al. [26]. Consequently, ER clinicians may have misdiagnosed these fractures or omitted them from medical records. However, recent studies have established a significant association between transverse process fractures and abdominal injuries, as reported by Patten et al. [27], as well as substantial pain, functional limitations, and associated systemic pathologies, as highlighted by Günerhan et al. [28]. These findings underscore the clinical importance of accurately detecting transverse process fractures. In this study, AI consistently achieved higher sensitivity across all fracture regions on the location level. Although AI exhibited a slightly higher false-positive rate, it maintained high specificity (99%), demonstrating strong diagnostic reliability. Moreover, AI demonstrated a higher F1 score in detecting right and left transverse process fractures, indicating its balanced performance in an imbalanced dataset. This suggests its usefulness in identifying challenging or frequently overlooked fractures.

Despite its strengths, the AI algorithm also exhibited some misclassifications, particularly in borderline or subtle fracture cases. Several factors contributed to false positives, including subtle cortical discontinuities and minimal vertebral collapses, bone marrow edema without corresponding CT findings, and motion artifacts and normal anatomical variants such as limbus vertebrae and transitional vertebrae. These findings highlight two critical areas for improvement in AI-driven fracture detection: the integration of multi-modality imaging (CT and MRI) and the refinement of AI decision-making using clinical context. Some fractures were only visible on MRI but not CT, suggesting that future AI systems should incorporate multi-modality imaging for improved diagnostic accuracy. Additionally, AI models should be designed to distinguish true fractures from normal anatomical variations to reduce false positives without compromising sensitivity. One thing to note is that the use of MRI as part of the reference standard may introduce potential bias when evaluating CT-based detection methods. MRI can identify fractures that are occult or only subtly visible on CT, potentially disadvantaging both the AI algorithm and ER clinician interpretation. Therefore, some false-negative findings may reflect the intrinsic limitations of CT imaging rather than true detection failure. Future studies may benefit from separate analysis of CT-visible and MRI-only lesions to better characterize diagnostic performance. Moreover, in this study, all incorrect detections, including wrong vertebral level and wrong anatomical location assignments, were classified as false positives. Near-miss detections, such as adjacent-level or neighboring-structure errors, were not separately analyzed and may have different clinical implications compared with entirely spurious detections. Future studies with larger datasets may benefit from more detailed characterization of error severity and spatial proximity.

Our study has several limitations. It is a single-center retrospective study involving a limited sample size. Additionally, the spinal fractures included in this study were primarily distributed between T11 and L5, and the types of fractures were heterogeneous. Such variability in both anatomical location and fracture morphology may have introduced selection bias, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings to other clinical settings. Although the abdominal CT scans used in this analysis were acquired using a standardized imaging protocol, they were obtained from a single-vendor CT scanner. This reliance on a single manufacturer may pose a limitation to the generalizability and external validity of our findings across diverse scanning platforms.

The evaluated AI model is a proprietary commercial algorithm, and limited access to internal model architecture details, preprocessing parameters, optimization strategies, and inference thresholds may restrict reproducibility and independent validation. In addition, industry involvement may introduce potential sources of bias. To mitigate these concerns, the reference standard was independently established by experienced spine radiologists using combined CT and MRI review, and the AI model was evaluated without additional retraining or task-specific optimization for vertebral fracture detection. Nonetheless, external validation in larger independent cohorts remains necessary. Recent studies have demonstrated that AI systems may exhibit uneven or task-dependent performance when transferred beyond their original development objectives, emphasizing the importance of careful out-of-domain validation rather than assuming generalizability [29]. Similarly, the present study demonstrates preliminary cross-task adaptability of a rib fracture detection model for vertebral fracture assessment, while also underscoring the need for further external validation.

An additional limitation relates to the statistical methodology used for subgroup analyses. The use of z-tests for proportions assumes large sample sizes and independence of observations, which may not be held in subgroups with low event counts, such as spinous process fractures. Furthermore, location-level analyses involve multiple observations per patient, introducing within-patient correlation that is not explicitly modeled by these tests. Because statistical comparisons were performed at the vertebra and anatomical site levels, which only partially accounted for within-patient clustering, the comparison of diagnostic performance between clinicians and the AI model should be interpreted as exploratory. As a result, the reported p-values in these subgroup analyses may be less reliable and should be interpreted with caution. Future studies with larger cohorts should consider exact statistical methods or approaches that account for clustered data, such as patient-level resampling or mixed-effects modeling.

Our institution serves as a Level 1 Trauma Center, where it is a standard protocol to perform routine follow-up spine MRI whenever a fracture is identified on screening CT scans. However, this process may be omitted if external CT or MRI scans are already available. Since our study cohort was limited to patients who underwent both CT and MRI specifically at our center, there is a potential for selection bias. This may limit the generalizability of our findings to other institutions where concurrent CT and MRI imaging is not routinely performed. Evaluating fracture detection based solely on the CT interpretations of ER clinicians may introduce significant limitations, such as interpretational heterogeneity and ascertainment bias. Due to the acute nature of the clinical setting, ER clinicians may not exhaustively document every detected fracture in the medical records. Furthermore, there is a potential for selective reporting, where clinicians may prioritize the documentation of vertebral body fractures over transverse or spinous process fractures, reflecting their perceived clinical significance in emergency triage. The deep learning algorithm utilized in this study is limited by its inability to evaluate the posterior ligamentous complex, which plays a critical role in clinical decision-making for spinal stability. Consequently, our analysis was restricted to the detection of fractures involving the vertebral body, transverse processes, and spinous processes. Future studies should explore AI’s potential in detecting spinal instability and posterior ligamentous complex injuries to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of spinal trauma.

Despite these limitations, this study demonstrated the preliminary feasibility of applying an AI algorithm originally developed for rib fracture detection to thoracolumbar fracture assessment on abdominal CT examinations. The findings suggest that fracture detection models trained for one anatomical region may retain partial utility when applied to related fracture detection tasks in other body regions, although performance variability remains and dedicated validation is necessary. Additionally, this study was conducted using general abdominal CT images rather than dedicated thoracolumbar CT images, providing preliminary insights into the AI’s analytical capability with different data. This study supports the potential for developing an AI model capable of detecting fractures in various body parts using different types of CT images.

Conclusion

This preliminary, single-center, retrospective study explored the potential of an AI algorithm—originally developed for rib fracture detection—to serve as a supportive tool for enhancing the sensitivity of spinal fracture detection within emergency clinical settings. Furthermore, this study highlights that an AI algorithm trained for a specific purpose can yield unexpectedly favorable outcomes when applied to alternative imaging conditions.
