The relationship between spiritual care needs and religious tendencies of cancer patients
Authors: Hatice Öntürk Akyüz, Hakime Aslan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study was conducted to examine the relationship between the spiritual care needs and religious tendencies of cancer patients. The sample of the study consists of 245 patients treated in the oncology department of a state hospital in eastern Türkiye. Data were collected via face‒to-face meetings technique with inpatients between April and September 2024. The “Socio-demographic and Clinical Characteristics Form”, “Spiritual care needs Assessment Scale of Patients with Cancer” and “Religious Tendency Scale” were used for data collection. 51% of the participants were female, 76.7% were married, 38% were university graduates, 62.4% had a medium income level and 84.5% received help with their care. The correlation analysis revealed a positive and moderately significant relationship between patients’ religious tendencies and their spiritual care needs (r: 0.463, p < 0.001), meaning that as the patients’ spiritual care needs increased so did their religious tendencies. In this study, it was revealed as a result of the Wald statistical value that the spiritual care needs of the patients had a great impact on their religious tendencies.Keywords: Cancer, Nursing care, Patient, Religious, SpiritualitySubject terms: Cancer, Medical research

Introduction

Cancer is challenging to treat and affects individuals in many ways, including their quality of life. Beyond the complex course of the disease, the prolongation and side effects of treatment reduce patients’ physical performance and ability to perform activities of daily living, adversely affecting their overall well-being and psychosocial balance. Furthermore, a cancer diagnosis evokes worries of impending death and severe pain, posing existential concerns and questions. Cancer patients have a wide range of psychosocial and spiritual care needs that need to be met1,2.

Moreover, for cancer patients, prolonged treatment may lead to despair and diminished self-esteem, jeopardizing spiritual beliefs and personal relationships, increasing the need for social support, and evoking feelings of loneliness, especially in the event of hospitalization; as a result, patients may experience spiritual crisis, leading to significant increases in their spiritual care needs3,4. According to Astrow et al.5, 79% of cancer patients had at least one spiritual need, and 80% had at least one psychosocial need. In a study on spiritual care needs conducted in Turkey, cancer patients expected answers to their questions about the moment of death and thereafter (100%), hoped to feel peaceful and content (94.8%), desired to be accompanied by someone in this process (93.5%), felt the need to have other people pray for them (52.2%), and wanted to receive compassion and kindness (54.3%). Cancer patients often have significant care needs6.

Unmet spiritual care needs (e.g., finding meaning/purpose) have been shown to reduce patients’ quality of life and satisfaction with care5,7. Studies conducted in Turkey have shown that Muslim cancer patients frequently resort to religious resources to cope with this challenging process and maintain hope8–11. In Muslim Turkish society, “believing in accident and fate” is a common requirement of religion. Believing in fate, praying, and trusting and believing in God are important sources of hope in coping with situations that are beyond human control and are frequently used coping methods10,11. Especially in individuals with life-threatening diseases, religious orientation becomes more prominent when faced with fear of illness and death, existential anxiety or hopelessness12. Harbali and Koç10 noted that as Muslim oncology patients’ became more spiritually oriented, they used positive religious coping strategies, improving their perceived health quality and decreasing the level of distress. Patients often pray daily, make offerings and consult a clergyman to heal and cope.

Today, there is an important tendency that religion and spirituality are separate concepts. Religion is often seen as a subset of spirituality or culture. Koenig (2008) states that in the fourth and most recent version of spirituality, spirituality includes individuals who are not only religious and positive indicators of mental health, but also secular. In this model, everyone is spiritual, including atheists and agnostics13. Islam regards spirituality as an integral part and complement of piety, which derives its essence from religion. If spirituality is the ‘essence’, religion is the ‘form’. To what extent is it possible to preserve a formless essence? From the Islamic point of view, religion and spirituality are inseparable aspects of the human being, like soul and body, which constantly support, develop and enrich each other. Within the framework of Islam, spirituality is inextricably linked to the divine relationship with God and has a significant impact on the individual’s view of himself, his sense of purpose and his ties to the larger community14,15. In Islam, spirituality is promoted as the foundation of human development and the most important aspect of Muslim patients’ spiritual care needs in understanding the meaning of human nature and their relationship with God16. The perspectives of patients in the Muslim Turkish community on religion and spirituality are also important in terms of managing nursing care needs. This study focused on how the religious tendency of Muslim cancer patients change as their spiritual care needs increase, and on the relationships between these changes.

From the perspective of different religions, each belief system offers different forms of spiritual and spiritual support in coping with illness. For example, patients of Islamic faith find solace through prayer, patience, and belief in fate; Christian patients can understand their own suffering through the suffering of Jesus and receive spiritual support from the church community. Buddhist patients, on the other hand, may view suffering as a natural part of life and seek inner peace through meditation. Therefore, a patient’s religious beliefs are a significant factor in determining the content and intensity of their spiritual care needs, and how these needs should be addressed. Therefore, to provide effective spiritual support, the patient’s religious background and belief structure must be taken into account6.

The aim of the study was to examine the relationship between spiritual care needs and religious tendencies of cancer patients. In Turkey, studies addressing the relationship between the spiritual care needs of cancer patients and their religious tendencies have traditionally been limited to simple correlation analyses, t-tests, and classical linear regression models. However, these methods may be insufficient for analyzing complex socio-psychological data, as they fail to adequately account for the distribution characteristics and variance structure of dependent variables. In this study, Generalized Linear Models (GLM) were preferred, considering that the dependent variables do not meet the assumptions of continuous and normal distribution and that the effects of some sociodemographic variables may not be linear. This model enables more accurate predictions through various link functions by providing flexibility appropriate to the distribution characteristics of the dependent variable17,18. The use of this method in the Turkish context to examine the non-causal links between spiritual care needs and religious tendencies lends methodological originality to the study and makes an important contribution to the field.

Materials and methods

Research type

This research is descriptive and correlational.

Population sample

The study population consisted of patients hospitalized in the oncology ward of a state hospital in eastern Turkey. A total of 240 patients were needed to achieve a 95% significance level, 0.80 representativeness and 0.05 error in the power analysis. A convenience sampling method was used to select the sample from the population.

Data collection

The researcher collected the data using the face-to-face meetings technique from patients hospitalized in oncology clinics between April and September 2024. Patients hospitalized in the oncology clinics were visited in their rooms, and informed consent was obtained from all the patients who agreed to participate in the study. Patients who were conscious, had no communication problems, had been diagnosed with cancer at least 6 months prior and agreed to participate in the study were included. The “Socio-demographic and Clinical Characteristics Form”, the ‘Spiritual care needs Assessment Scale of Patients with Cancer’ and the ‘Religious Tendency Scale’ were used for data collection.

Spiritual care needs Assessment Scale of Patients with Cancer (SNASPC)

The aim of the scale developed by Hatamipour et al.19, is to measure the spiritual care needs of cancer patients. The validity and reliability of the scale for Turkish populations were assessed by Erci and Aslan16. The 7-point Likert scale consists of 38 items and 5 subdimensions: ‘Religious need’, ‘Finding meaning and purpose’, ‘Seeking peace’, ‘The need to communication’ and ‘Support and independence’. The points that can be obtained from the scale vary between 38 and 266. A high score indicates high spiritual care needs. The Cronbach’s α value of the scale was 0.85. In this study, the Cronbach’s α value of the scale was determined to be 0.90.

Religious Tendency Scale (RTS)

The religious disposition scale was developed by Kaya et al.20 and is 4-point Likert scale consisting of 48 items and 3 subdimensions: “belief”, “worship” and “acquiring knowledge”. The highest score is 192, and the lowest score is 48. The higher the score is, the greater the individual’s religious tendency. The Cronbach’s α reliability coefficient of the scale was calculated as 0.98. In this study, the Cronbach’s α value of the scale was determined to be 0.94. In order to evaluate the reliability of the measurement tools used in the study, the studies of Koenig and Al Zabe21 and Koenig and Carey22 were used.

Ethical approval

All procedures were performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Before the study, ethics committee approval (Decision no: 2024/03–32 and E.5281) was obtained from the Bitlis Eren University Ethical Principles and Ethics Committee and written permission was obtained from the institutions where the study would be conducted. Permission was obtained via e-mail from the authors who developed the scales used in the study and conducted the validity-reliability analysis. The purpose of the study was explained to the patients who participated in the study. Written and verbal consent was obtained from the participants.

Data analysis

In this study, the data analysis process was planned in multiple stages. First, descriptive statistics were calculated for participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, diagnoses, and treatment processes. Then, the normality of the variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, which indicated that the data did not follow a normal distribution (p < 0.05). Therefore, non-parametric tests were employed in subsequent analyses.

Afterwards, the relationships between the subdimensions of the Spiritual care needs Assessment Scale for Patients with Cancer (SNASPC) and the Religious Tendency Scale (RTS) were examined using Spearman’s rank-order correlation. Spearman correlation was chosen due to the non-normal distribution of the variables and used to explore the direction and strength of the relationships, providing initial insight into the selection of potential predictor variables for further analysis. In the current study, Spearman correlation served exploratory and descriptive purposes.

Subsequently, a Generalized Linear Model (GLM) was conducted to examine the predictive effect of spiritual care needs on religious tendency. Since the dependent variable, Prediction of Religious Tendency, consisted solely of positive and continuous values and exhibited a negatively skewed distribution, the model employed a Gamma distribution and a log link function23. This modeling approach helped stabilize increasing variance and ensured alignment with the distributional structure of the data, leading to more reliable and interpretable results.

Finally, the differences between participants’ sociodemographic characteristics and their SNASPC and RTS scale scores were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test and the Kruskal-Wallis test. All data were entered and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0 (Armonk, NY, USA). A significance level of 0.05 (95% confidence interval) was used as the criterion for interpreting the statistical significance of the results.

Generalized Linear Models (GLMs)

According to the results in Table 4, since the “Religious Tendency Scale” variable includes only positive and continuous values, and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test indicated that the distribution does not follow normality, the analysis was conducted using a Gamma distribution with a log link function. The Gamma distribution is appropriate for continuous and skewed data, while the log link function stabilizes variance and allows for the modeling of multiplicative effects.

The overall significance of the model was assessed using the Omnibus Test, and the model was found to be statistically significant (Likelihood Ratio Chi-Square = 100.973, df = 1, p < 0.001). This indicates that the variable Spiritual Need is a significant predictor of Religious Tendency.

According to the parameter estimates obtained from regression analysis, the coefficient (β) for Spiritual Need was 0.003 and the standard error was 0.0003, and this effect was statistically significant (Wald χ²=131.961, p < 0.001). The exponential coefficient (Exp(β) ≈ 1.003) indicates that, when other factors are held constant, a one-unit increase in spiritual need is associated with an approximate 0.3% increase in religious orientation scores. The Wald statistic was found to be quite high (χ²=131.961), indicating that the effect is statistically strong. However, the small regression coefficient suggests that the magnitude of this effect may be modest in practice. When interpreted on a logarithmic scale, this indicates that a one-unit increase in spiritual need leads to a significant and positive increase in religious orientation. Specifically, based on the exponential transformation of the regression coefficient (β = 0.003), when other factors are held constant, each additional point in spiritual need score predicts a 0.3% increase in religious tendency score. The 95% Wald confidence interval for the coefficient was narrow (0.003–0.004), indicating high precision in the estimate.

The model intercept was calculated as 4,414, which was also statistically significant (p < 0.001). This value represents the expected logarithmic value of religious tendency when the predictor is zero. Regarding model fit, the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was 2404,294, and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) was 2414,798, indicating a good fit of the model to the data.

According to the results in Table 5, the differences between the variables of marital status, educational level, perceived economic status, surgery status and receiving help with care and the mean SNASPC score were statistically significant (p < 0.05). Thus, divorced patients, literate patients, patients who perceived their economic level as bad, patients who had surgery and patients who received help with care had greater spiritual care needs. In addition, the differences between sex, employment status and occupation and the mean SNASPC score were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Discussion

In this study, in which we examined the relationship between the spiritual care needs and religious tendencies of cancer patients, we determined that there was a positive and significant relationship between the spiritual care needs and religious tendencies of patients. According to the Wald statistic, we found that spiritual care needs have a large impact on religious inclinations. According to published studies, patients often exhibit spiritual adaptation to cope with serious illnesses such as cancer and turn to religion and spiritual resources, which is good for psychological‒social adaptation in response to diseases16. Spiritual care needs may or may not be based on religious beliefs and refer to an individuals’ needs and expectations to discover the meaning, purpose and value of life24. Religion is seen as an important source for meeting these needs. Each religion has its own unique worldview and perspective on humanity and teaches its adherents to live a life in accordance with spiritual values25. Within the framework of Islam, spirituality is inextricably linked to a divine relationship with God and has a significant impact on an individual’s view of themselves, their sense of purpose and their connection to the larger community26. In Islam, the spiritual aspect is essential and related to religion. Therefore, in this study, religious tendencies increased with spiritual care needs. In support of this result, Nikrouz et al., reported that engaging in spiritual activities and meditations can provide solace and perspective to individuals, reducing existential pain associated with life-threatening illnesses27.

A qualitative study conducted by Köktürk Dalcalı and Kaya28 on the spiritual care needs of patients in oncology units and associated nursing care practices revealed that patients frequently resort to religious practices to cope. One of the subthemes identified is “Getting Closer to God-Religious Practices”. Furthermore, most patients reported that their disease came from God and that they believed in God and prayed to cope with difficulties. Patients also stated that they became closer to God and started praying more frequently after they were diagnosed with cancer. Most patients also stated that their views on religion did not change and that they started to worship and seek refuge in God more than before28. Rambod et al., reported that for Muslim hemophilia patients, spirituality is not just a source of solace but rather a dynamic force that enables patients to find strength in their religion, enabling them to fight their illness with courage and optimism. Moreover, prevalent among Muslims is the idea of testing and patience (Sabr), the idea that the problems in this world are designed to test people and encourage them to be patient when faced with problems29. Diseases are thought to come from Allah and will be cured or adapted by being patient, praying, and fulfilling religious responsibilities8. These thoughts and belief systems explain why cancer patients have increased religious inclinations to meet their growing spiritual care needs.

In this study, it was also determined that there were significant differences between some socio-demographic characteristics of the patients and their spiritual care needs. It was determined that divorced patients, literate patients, patients who perceived their economic level as bad, patients who underwent surgery and patients who received help in care had higher spiritual care needs. Kaplan et al.‘s study determined that the spiritual care needs of unmarried patients were higher than those of married caregivers30. It is thought that decreased social support, increased vulnerability and more existential questioning in the absence of a spouse may be the cause of increased spiritual care needs of individuals who are divorced or single. It was determined that the spiritual care needs of patients with low educational and economic level were higher. In the literature, it was observed that there were research results indicating that the spiritual care needs of patients with low economic level increased30,31. The decrease in education level and income can be explained by the fact that patients experience more economic and social problems. In addition, it was determined that the spiritual care needs of patients who underwent surgery and received support in care were higher. Alcan et al. determined that the spiritual care needs of individuals with additional chronic diseases in patients undergoing orthopedic surgery were higher, although not statistically significant32. The effects of surgical procedures on patients and receiving support in care suggest that the general condition of patients is aggravated. Physical, emotional and cognitive burden may have increased the need for spiritual care. In addition, the presence of symptoms in cancer patients trying to overcome a life-threatening disease, heavy treatment protocols, additional chronic diseases and the need for support in care may make individuals more spiritually sensitive. This may have led to an increase in spiritual care needs.

At such times, spirituality and religion often emerge as fundamental sources of strength, comfort, and coping. Research shows that spiritual and religious values play an important role in coping with cancer, improving quality of life, reducing psychological distress, and providing a more effective coping strategy17,22. In particular, it has been demonstrated that religious beliefs and rituals can positively influence individuals, especially coping mechanisms rooted in religious identity and spiritual belief systems, which support mental resilience5.

Patients’ spiritual care needs can vary greatly depending on their beliefs. For example, devout Christians may seek prayer or pastoral support; Muslim patients may find reading the Quran or performing zikr spiritually meaningful; Buddhist patients may prefer meditation and mindfulness practices; and secular or non-theistic individuals may continue to seek meaning through nature, relationships, or personal values33–35.

Understanding this diversity in spirituality and religious orientation (e.g., devout individuals who regularly worship, secular but spiritually oriented individuals, or individuals who do not hold religious or spiritual values) is critical to developing culturally competent and individualized care plans. Careful assessment of spiritual needs, particularly in cancer patients, is a critical step in supporting holistic health6–8.

Limitations

The limitation of this study is that it was conducted only on cancer patients in a hospital. This affects the generalizability of the results obtained. The results cannot be generalized to all Turkish cancer patients. In addition, the use of self-report-based measurement tools is another limitation. Self-reported responses reduce the level of evidence of the results.

Conclusion

In this study, the spiritual care needs of cancer patients were very high and their religious tendencies were high. There was a positive and significant relationship between patients’ spiritual care needs and their religious tendencies. Thus, patients’ spiritual care needs strongly affected their religious tendencies. Therefore, in this context, nurses are recommended to monitor the spiritual care needs of patients much more closely and undergo training to ensure their competence in meeting patients’ needs. Institution managers are recommended to cultivate an environment that allows patients to meet their religious needs and to provide spiritual counseling. It is also recommended to include spiritual care modules in nursing education, develop hospital-based spiritual care protocols, and use validated screening tools to assess the spiritual care needs of patients during admission. International literature similarly reveals that patients’ spiritual care needs should not be ignored. The World Health Organization (WHO) and many advanced healthcare systems emphasize the importance of spiritual support as an integral part of holistic patient care. In this study, a spiritual care approach that is sensitive to cultural differences, adopts universal ethical principles and patient-centered should be promoted on a global scale. Training health professionals in spiritual sensitivity and cultural competence in accordance with international standards both increases patient satisfaction and improves the quality of health services. Specific practice recommendations for oncology nurses including regularly assessing patients’ spiritual care needs, receiving targeted training in spiritual care, and collaborating with religious organizations to provide holistic and culturally sensitive support. In order to make stronger conclusions on the subject, it is recommended that randomized controlled experimental studies with a high level of evidence be conducted. Examining differences in spiritual needs across different cancer types and stages. Longitudinal studies on the effects of spiritual care on clinical outcomes such as patient quality of life, depression levels, and pain management.
