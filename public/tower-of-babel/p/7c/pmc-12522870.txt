Molecular cargo of small EVs from NSCLC patient BALF and plasma: unveiling their role in airway inflammation and immune regulation in a novel human 3D bronchial model
Authors: Magdalena Dlugolecka, Jacek Szymanski, Lukasz Zareba, Karolina Soroczynska, Zuzanna Homoncik, Malgorzata Polubiec-Kownacka, Ewa Frankiewicz, Diana Wierzbicka, Kannathasan Thetchinamoorthy, Joanna Domagala-Kulawik, Malgorzata Czystowska-Kuzmicz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTumor-derived small extracellular vesicles (EVs) play a crucial role in modulating immune responses and shaping the tumor microenvironment; however, their functional impact on airway immunity in NSCLC remains largely unexplored. This study represents the first attempt to investigate the immunomodulatory and tumor-promoting effects of NSCLC-derived EVs in a human 3D bronchial airway model, which closely mimics the human lung microenvironment.MethodsEVs were isolated from the plasma and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) of NSCLC patients and analyzed via nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) and high-resolution imaging flow cytometry. The lymphocyte compositions of the matched blood and BALF samples were profiled. To assess the functional effects of EVs, we employed a pioneering in vitro 3D airway coculture model that combines primary human airway epithelial cells and alveolar macrophages at the air‒liquid interface. Proteomic analysis of EV-treated cells and their secretome was performed to identify key molecular pathways underlying EV-driven immunomodulation.ResultsSurprisingly, no significant molecular differences were detected between EVs from cancerous (cBALF) and opposite (oBALF) lung compartments, despite a localized increase in regulatory T cells (Tregs) in the cBALF, suggesting regional immunosuppression. Plasma-derived EVs exhibited highly diverse, patient-specific molecular signatures but were not directly correlated with clinical or immune parameters. Functional studies of EVs with high and low surface molecular cargo in a 3D airway model revealed that both EV subgroups promoted monocyte/macrophage recruitment, angiogenesis, and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) via MCP-1 secretion and induced an immunosuppressive airway microenvironment, enhancing IL-10 production and shifting macrophages toward a tumor-promoting M2 phenotype. Proteomic analysis revealed distinct differentially expressed protein (DEP) profiles across epithelial and macrophage populations, ultimately resulting in protumorigenic and immunosuppressive outcomes. Notably, functional enrichment analysis of macrophages revealed that EV-driven M2 polarization occurred through the suppression of EGFR activity, a previously underrecognized mechanism that links EV-mediated immune suppression to lung cancer progression.ConclusionsThis study provides the first functional evidence that NSCLC-derived EVs drive immune suppression and tumor-supportive changes in a human 3D airway model, closely mimicking in vivo lung conditions. The identification of EGFR suppression as a driver of macrophage polarization underscores the need to consider macrophage-specific EGFR regulation in anti-EGFR therapies to prevent unintended protumorigenic effects. These findings pave the way for future studies exploring EV cargo, such as miRNAs, as potential therapeutic targets in NSCLC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02423-5.Keywords: NSCLC, Extracellular vesicles, In vitro 3D human airway model, BALF, Proteomic profiling, Immunoregulation, Macrophage polarization, EGFR, Tumor immune evasion

Introduction

Lung cancer is an aggressive malignancy with a highly unfavorable prognosis and a high death rate. Each year, 1.8 million people worldwide are diagnosed, and 1.6 million people die from the disease. The five-year survival rate ranges from 4 to 17% [1]. The most common forms of lung cancer are non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer (SCLC), occurring in approximately 80% and approximately 15% of cases, respectively. In contrast, the most common types of NSCLC are adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma [2]. Due to its prolonged development without visible symptoms, the disease is often diagnosed only at an advanced stage, when surgical treatment is no longer possible. Until recently, chemotherapy and radiation therapy were the only options used [1, 3].

Owing to the exploration of cancer biology and the development of modern research techniques, the causes and mechanisms of cancer development are increasingly understood; thus, more tailored and effective therapies can be designed. These advances make it possible to create personalized treatments such as targeted therapies and immunotherapy [4, 5]. Unfortunately, the efficacy of these therapies varies greatly from patient to patient and likely strongly depends on the degree of suppression of the immune system, especially in the tumor microenvironment. It is well established that antitumor immunity is impaired in cancer patients, particularly those with advanced disease, allowing tumors to evade immune attack. Accumulating evidence indicates that the host immune system is unable to be rejuvenated by immunotherapy and to effectively mount an antitumor response. Moreover, the use of immunotherapy is often associated with an increased risk of highly toxic effects and enormous costs [6]; therefore, careful selection of patients who might benefit from the therapy is essential.

In recent years, much attention has been given to the study of cell-to-cell information carriers, which are small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) that are 30–300 nm in size and are released from cells [7]. They are present in most body fluid types, including blood, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), joint fluid, urine, saliva, cerebrospinal fluid, fetal water and milk [8]. They are produced by both normal and pathological cells [9] and include mRNAs, miRNAs, proteins, and lipids from the cell of origin, among others. Interestingly, their number increases in pathological states, and in cancer patients, circulating sEVs include a subset of tumor-derived EVs that, due to their similarity to parental cells, contain specific cancer cell markers and can serve as a so-called liquid biopsy [10]. Recently, these tumor-derived sEVs have emerged as additional factors used by tumors to suppress immune responses and are therefore under extensive research as potential prognostic and predictive biomarkers of cancer progression and survival. The common presence of tumor-derived sEVs in body fluids allow their minimally invasive collection, for example, from peripheral blood [11, 12]. In the case of NSCLC, BALF appears to be a good source of tumor-derived sEVs [13, 14]. Owing to their proximity to the tumor, these sEVs may appear in BALF at an earlier stage of the disease and at higher concentrations than in peripheral blood and better represent the composition of the tumor microenvironment.

In this study, we conducted a detailed comparative analysis of EVs from the plasma and BALF of 82 patients suspected of having NSCLC via fluorescent nanoparticle tracking analysis (f-NTA) to determine their concentration, size, and tetraspanin (CD63, CD9, and CD81) content on the basis of previously established methods. Additionally, we performed a comprehensive assessment of the immune profile (activation/apoptosis markers and the presence of suppressive molecules) of plasma and BALF lymphocytes to correlate this profile with the EV molecular profile of the same patient. We characterized twenty-five potential markers of plasma EVs from NSCLC patients via single-EV analysis of the original plasma sample by imaging flow cytometry, identifying two patient subgroups with molecularly distinct plasma EV subpopulations: sEVs with low and high expression of potential immunosuppressive, proinflammatory, or tumor-promoting surface markers, respectively. To characterize the immunoregulatory potential of these two identified EV subsets, we established the MucilAir™-M Model, a highly innovative 3D in vitro model of the human airway epithelium. We investigated the impact of the selected plasma EV samples combined with BALF EV samples from both patient subgroups’ sEV subsets on cytokine secretion and the proteomic profile of airway epithelial cells and alveolar macrophages in this system.

Methods

Patients

The study group included 82 patients (41 men and 41 women) suspected of having NSCLC (Table 1). The inclusion criteria were patients who provided informed consent and were before anticancer treatment during the diagnostic procedure. The exclusion criteria were contraindication to bronchoscopy, lack of patient agreement, ongoing anticancer treatment, immunosuppressive therapy, and infection. Patients were aged 42–84 years, with a mean age of 67.5 years. The material collection, BAL, and EV separation procedures and f-NTA measurements were described in detail in our previous report [15].

Flow cytometric analysis of lymphocytes

Flow cytometry of lymphocytes was performed on a BD Lyric Flow Cytometer (BD) via BD FACSuite Software. Peripheral blood (50 µL) and isolated BALF cells from each lung, suspended in 300 µL of PBS (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland), were analyzed for each patient in three different staining panels as follows: apoptosis–phenotype, PD-1, and Treg. A different antibody mixture was prepared for each staining panel (Supp. Table 1). BALF cells and blood samples were incubated with the appropriate antibody mixture for 20 min in the dark at RT. Then, the red blood cells were lysed with 2 mL of lysis buffer (BD Pharm Lyse™ 10x diluted, cat. 555899, BD Biosciences) at RT for 5 min. Then, the cells were centrifuged at 200 × g for 5 min at RT. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were washed with 2 mL of FACS flow buffer and centrifuged again at 310 × g for 5 min at RT. The further procedure depended on the panel. For the apoptosis–phenotype panel cells were incubated with an ANX-FITC antibody and 7AAD in 100 µl ANX-binding buffer (FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit with 7-AAD, cat. 640922, Biolegend) for 15 min at RT in the dark. Then, 200 µl of ANX-binding buffer was added, and the samples were analyzed on a flow cytometer. For the PD-1 panel, the cells were dissolved in 150 µl of 4% cold PFA, 50 µl of CountBright Absolute Counting Beads (cat. C36950, Invitrogen) was added, and the samples were analyzed on a flow cytometer. The Treg panel cells were dissolved in 150 µl of 4% cold PFA and incubated for 15 min at RT in the dark. Then, 300 µl of FACS flow buffer was added, the cells were centrifuged at 420 × g for 5 min, and the cell pellet was dissolved in 100 µl of permeabilization buffer and incubated for 15 min at RT in the dark. Next, the antibodies against CTLA-4-FITC and CD3-zeta-PE were added, and the samples were incubated for 30 min at RT in the dark. Then, 300 µl of permeabilization buffer was added, and the samples were centrifuged at 420 × g for 5 min. The cells were then dissolved in 150 µl of 4% cold PFA, 50 µl of CountBright Absolute Counting Beads (cat. C36950, Invitrogen) was added, and the samples were analyzed via flow cytometry. FCS files were then analyzed via FlowJo Software (LLC).

Imaging flow cytometry

Plasma from 55 patients was analyzed at the Institute for Transfusion Medicine, University Hospital Essen, University of Duisburg-Essen, Essen, Germany. The samples were processed as described previously by Tertel et al. [16]. Briefly, plasma samples were thawed and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at RT, and 10 µl of the supernatant was stained for 1,5 h at RT with fluorochrome-conjugated anti-human Monoclonal antibodies for 25 different markers (Supp. Table 2). The samples were subsequently diluted (1:10) and analyzed on an AMNIS ImageStreamX Mark II Flow Cytometer (AMNIS/Luminex, Seattle, WA, USA) via a built-in autosampler for U-bottom 96-well plates (Corning Falcon, cat 353077). The acquisition times were 5 min per well at 60 × magnification and a low flow rate (0.3795 ± 0.0003 µl/min, directly determined by the system), with the removed beads being deactivated as described previously [17, 18]. The data were analyzed as described previously [19].

EV treatment of the 3D human airway epithelium in an in vitro model (MucilAir™-M-EV)

The MucilAir™ model is a fully humanized 3D in vitro model of a healthy fully differentiated and functional healthy upper airway epithelium containing ciliated, goblet, club and basal cells cultured at an air‒liquid interface. A coculture of this commercially available in vitro cell model with primary human airway epithelium-alveolar macrophages (MucilAir™-M) was established by seeding 20 thousand macrophages on the apical side of each epithelial tissue and incubating them (37 °C, 5% CO2, 100% humidity) for 24 h to recapitulate the in vivo conditions even more closely. EVs were administered to the culture in a manner that mimics the actual distribution in vivo: EVs from plasma were added to the basal side, which contained cell culture medium (recapitulating the blood vessel side), and EVs from BALF from the same patient were added to the apical side, which also contained alveolar macrophages (the air phase) (Fig. 1). There was also a negative control, where we added PBS instead of EVs, and a positive control with TNF-α (1.75 µg/mL, cat. 210-TA-020CF, R&D Systems, BIOTECHNE).

EV treatment of an in vitro cell model of the human airway epithelium cocultured with primary human airway epithelium-alveolar macrophages (MucilAir™-M) at the air–liquid interface. Created with BioRender.com

After 48 h of coculture (37 °C, 5% CO2, 100% humidity), the apical side was covered with 200 µl of MucilAir™ culture medium (cat. EP05MM, Geneva, Switzerland). After 20 min of incubation at 37 °C, the medium (apical medium) was collected and centrifuged at 400 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove the cells, which were subsequently stored at −80 °C for further cytokine and cytotoxicity analysis. The obtained cell pellet containing mostly alveolar macrophages was washed with 50µl of cold PBS, centrifuged at 400 × g for 10 min at 4 °C and stored at −80 °C for further proteomics analysis. The basal medium from each sample was collected in several aliquots and stored at −80 °C for analysis of the effects of EVs on inflammatory processes (secretion of inflammatory cytokines) and cytotoxic processes induced in epithelial cells (LDH test), as well as for proteomic analysis.

The epithelial tissue was washed with PBS and lysed with TrypLE™ Express (cat. 12604021; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 15 min at 37 °C. The dissociated cells were subsequently transferred to culture medium and centrifuged at 400 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellet (epithelial cells) was subsequently stored at −80 °C for further proteomics analysis.

Multiplex cytokine assay and cytotoxicity test

The basal and apical media were analyzed via the LegendPlex Human Inflammation Panel (cat. 740809, 13-plex; Biolegend, San Diego, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each sample was analyzed as three biological repetitions measured in two technical repetitions each. The apical medium was additionally analyzed in a similar way via the LegendPlex Human Macrophage/Microglia Panel (cat. 740503, 13-plex; Biolegend) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cytometric analysis of both panels was performed on a Cytek Aurora Cytometer at the Flow Cytometry Core Facility at the Nencki Institute of Experimental Biology PAS (Warsaw, Poland). The.fcs files were analyzed with the dedicated BioLegend’s LEGENDplex™ data analysis software.

Cell cytotoxicity was evaluated by measuring LDH activity in the basal medium via an LDH Cytotoxicity Detection Kit (cat. 04 744 926 001; Roche Diagnostics) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance of the background control (medium only) was subtracted from the measured absorbance. The low control was medium from the MucilAir™-M coculture treated with PBS instead of EVs. The positive control consisted of a sample containing an equal number of lysed airway epithelial cells. The level of specific cytotoxicity was calculated according to the following formula: ([abs 492 nm experimental - abs 492 nm low control])/[abs 492 nm positive control- abs 492 nm low control]) × 100. The samples and low control were run in three technical and biological repetitions. The positive control was run in three technical repetitions.

Proteomic analysis

Preparation of cell lysates

The cell pellets were lysed with RIPA buffer. Proteins were precipitated with ice-cold acetonitrile of LC‒MS grade (Merck Millipore, USA), centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 30 min at − 9 °C, and dried for 10 min at RT via an Eppendorf Concentrator plus (Eppendorf, Germany). The proteins were subsequently redissolved in 40 mM ammonium bicarbonate solution, reduced with 500 mM DTT solution, and alkylated with 1 M iodoacetamide solution (Sigma Aldrich, USA). The samples were subsequently transferred to the Laboratory of Regenerative Medicine MUW for further processing, where in-solution digestion was performed overnight (16 h) at 37 °C using Trypsin Gold (Promega, USA).

LC‒MS proteome analysis

The analysis was performed by the Laboratory of Regenerative Medicine MUW. LC‒MS analysis was conducted via a nano ultra high-performance liquid chromatography (nano-UHPLC) system (NanoElute, Bruker, USA) coupled to an ESI‒Q‒TOF mass spectrometer (Compact, Bruker, USA) via Captive Spray (Bruker, USA). Two-column separation was employed: a precolumn (300 μm × 5 mm, C18 PepMap 100, 5 μm, 100 Å, Thermo Scientific, USA) and a Bruker separation column (75 μm × 100 mm, C18 1.9 μm). The gradient consisted of 2% B to 55% B over 20 min at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The acquired spectra were calibrated via a lock mass calibrant and analyzed with Data Analysis software (Bruker). Protein identification was performed via ProteinScape (Bruker) with the MASCOT server, and the online SwissProt database was utilized for protein identification. For the generation of heatmaps in the proteomic analysis, R (version 4.3.3) was used. The analysis was performed with the support of the proteoDA package for data processing and normalization, and the heatmap library for visualization.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted via Excel, GraphPad Prism and Statistica software, and a p value < 0.05 was considered significant. For the quantitative analysis of the NTA data, all the samples were measured 3 times. They were measured in scatter mode before and after staining with dyes. The peak size (Mode) was chosen as the particle size for most samples. Where there was no mode, the median (X50) was used for calculations. The average concentration of the original sample was calculated. The results were converted to the concentration of particles in 1 ml of plasma or 1 ml of BALF. The distribution of the data was tested via the Shapiro‒Wilk test. For the Gaussian distribution, a two-tailed Student’s t test for the dependent variables was conducted. For nonnormally distributed data, the Wilcoxon test for dependent variables (EVs from one patient were compared) was used. The analysis of lymphocytes, surface markers from ImageStream, and cytokines was conducted via appropriate statistical tests on the basis of data distribution and group comparisons. The Shapiro‒Wilk test was applied to assess normality. For normally distributed variables, independent t tests with Levene’s test for variance homogeneity were used, along with one-way ANOVA accompanied by Levene’s test. In cases where normality was not met, the Mann‒Whitney U test with small sample correction and the Kruskal‒Wallis test were applied. Fisher’s NIR test was employed for post hoc comparisons. Correlations were assessed via Pearson’s correlation coefficient for normally distributed data and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient for nonnormally distributed data.

Results

Comparative f-NTA of oBALF, cBALF and plasma EVs from 82 patients

NTA measurements revealed that the concentration of plasma EVs in scatter (1.91 × 1011 ± 3.62 × 1011) and CMDR-labeled (5.48 × 1010 ± 6.73 × 1010) samples per mL of plasma or BALF, respectively, was significantly greater than that in samples obtained from both cBALF (9.30 × 108 ± 1.21 × 109) and oBALF (1.13 × 109 ± 1.44 × 109) (Fig. 2A, Supp. Figure 1B). The size of EVs in plasma (mean ± SD: scatter 96.5 ± 11.5 nm; CMDR + 115.0 ± 22.6 nm) was significantly smaller than that in both types of BALF EVs, which had mean sizes of 168.4 ± 22.5 nm (Scatter) and 180.2 ± 29.3 nm (CMDR+) in cBALF and 164.3 ± 19.8 nm (Scatter) and 177.8 ± 24.2 nm (CMDR+) in oBALF (Fig. 2B, Supp. Figure 1C). The mean size of EVs stained for tetraspanins was approximately 100 nm, with no significant differences between oBALF and cBALF. The protein concentrations of the plasma-derived EVs were two orders of magnitude greater (mean ± SD: 180.9 ± 139.7 µg/mL) than those of both BALF EV types, which showed no significant differences between them (mean ± SD: 1.25 ± 1.35 µg/mL in the cBALF and 1.22 ± 1.64 µg/mL in the oBALF) (Fig. 2C). BALF-derived EVs had a markedly greater percentage of CMDR + particles (57.3% in cBALF and 54.6% in oBALF) than did plasma-derived EVs (37.3%) (Fig. 2D). Among the tetraspanins, CD9 was the most abundant (75.3% in cBALF and 70.5% in oBALF), followed by CD63 (55.7% in cBALF and 63.3% in oBALF), with CD81 being the least abundant (13.2% in cBALF and 12.0% in oBALF). In contrast to BALF-derived EVs, where all tetraspanins were detected at varying percentages, none of the investigated tetraspanins were detected in plasma EV samples. To determine whether any measured parameters differ significantly between EVs from cBALF and oBALF, we assessed data normality using the Shapiro‒Wilk test, followed by Levene’s test or the Mann‒Whitney U test, as appropriate. No statistically significant differences were found between cBALF and oBALF for any of the analyzed parameters, confirming our previous results [15].

Next, we investigated the relationship between parameters measured in cBALF and/or oBALF and the same parameters measured in plasma. For most parameters, we did not find any statistically significant linear correlation between the values from BALF and plasma. However, there was a statistically significant positive linear correlation between the mode size of EVs in plasma and both cBALF (r = 0.33, p = 0.007) and oBALF (r = 0.57, p = 0.001), although the correlation was not strong. Additionally, we calculated a delta value (the difference in particle number between cBALF and oBALF). For this delta value, we observed a positive Spearman correlation for particle concentration in scatter, and respectively CMDR (r = 0.73, p < 0.001), CD63 (r = 0.76, p < 0.001), CD81 (r = 0.41, p < 0.001), and CD9 (r = 0.75, p < 0.001) (Supp. Figure 1). This finding indicates that as the number of total particles increases, the labeling efficiency with CMDR or fluorescent antibodies also increases. Next, we performed statistical correlation analysis between f-NTA results (oBALF, BALF, plasma) and clinical characteristics. Unfortunately, we did not find statistically relevant correlation with clinical statistics for any of them.

Phenotypic characterization of T lymphocytes in the peripheral blood and BALF of NSCLC patients

The cells obtained from the blood, oBALF and cBALF were phenotypically characterized via flow cytometry after being stained in three separate staining panels. The gating strategy for all panels is presented in Supp. Fig. 2-4. We observed some weak and moderate correlations between lymphocytes from the cBALF or oBALF and blood lymphocytes. Relatively strong positive correlations between the lymphocyte profile in peripheral blood and the profile in both lung compartments were found regarding the percentage of live, naive and effector CD8+ lymphocytes as well as the percentage of early and late apoptotic CD8+ and CD4+ lymphocytes, both in the whole patient population (Supp. Table 3) and in the patient subgroup with confirmed NSCLC (Supp. Table 4). Patients with lung cancer (LC) had a significantly lower percentage of total CD4+ lymphocytes and early activated CD4+ lymphocytes (CD69-positive) but a higher percentage of HLA-DR-positive CD4 lymphocytes in the cBALF compartment than patients without lung cancer (Non-LC) did (Fig. 3A and B). Similarly, lung cancer patients or patients with confirmed NSCLC had significantly fewer activated CD4+ lymphocytes (lower MFI of CD69) and CD8+ lymphocytes (lower percentage of CD8+CD69+) in the oBALF compartment than controls did (Supp. Figure 5 A-B). Furthermore, patients with diagnosed lung cancer as well as patients with specifically diagnosed NSCLC were characterized by a higher percentage of the CD4+CD25highCD127low population representing Tregs in both BALF compartments (cBALF and oBALF) than controls. ( Fig. 3C and Supp. Figure 5 C). This Treg population in the cBALF or oBALF compartment of NSCLC or lung cancer patients also presented higher expression of the immunosuppressive markers CTLA-4 and CD39 compared to corresponding population of controls ( Fig. 3E-F, Supp. Figure 5.D-E). Interestingly, a statistically significant increase in the expression of the apoptosis-promoting Fas receptor on CD8+ lymphocytes was observed between patients with stage II and stage III NSCLC (Fig. 3G).

Correlation of the lymphocyte profile with the EV profile

The number of EVs found in BALF and blood and their tetraspanin profile analyzed by NTA were compared with the phenotype and functionality of lymphocytes present in the corresponding biological fluids. In the case of plasma EVs, we found weak but potentially functionally relevant correlations (Supp. Tables 5–6). The total particle number and the number of CMDR+ vesicles in the blood were positively correlated with the percentage of central memory CD4+ lymphocytes and early activation of CD8+ lymphocytes (increases in the percentage of CD8CD69+ cells and the MFI of CD69+ CD8+ cells) and with the induction of the immunosuppressive potential of Tregs (increases in the expression of CTLA-4 and TGFβ on Tregs). At the same time, these EV parameters were negatively correlated with the percentage of effector CD4+ and late apoptotic CD8+ cells. Similarly, the total EV protein content in the blood was positively correlated with dead and early apoptotic CD4+ T cells (Supp. Table 6). In the case of the EV profile in the BALF compartment, similar to that in the plasma, the number of CD9+ and CD63+ EVs found in the cBALF correlated positively with CD8+ lymphocyte activation (the same was true for the total particle number in the oBALF), but the number of CD81+ vesicles correlated negatively with the early activation of CD4+ lymphocytes. In contrast to plasma, total particle and CMDR+ vesicle numbers were positively correlated with the percentage of CD4+ effector cells (Supp. Tables 7 & 9).

Molecular phenotyping of the plasma EVs of NSCLC patients by imaging flow cytometry

We performed phenotyping of EV-surface proteins by imaging flow cytometry directly in the collected and processed plasma of NSCLC patients. Specifically, we determined the concentrations of circulating EV subsets carrying one of the 25 studied markers of T cells, B cells, macrophages/monocytes, platelets, erythrocytes, or endothelial cells or markers connected to immunosuppression, inflammation or tumor development, respectively (Supp. Table 10).

The samples were divided into two groups based on the expression levels of these specific markers (Fig. 4A, Supp. Table 11) – EV high (with relatively high marker expression) and EV low (with relatively low marker expression) for further functional in vitro experiments.

Among other findings, we identified a strong positive correlation between the expression levels of the markers CD82 and HLA-DR (rs=0.85, p < 0.001, n = 55) and the markers CD9 and CD41 (rs=0.85, p < 0.001, n = 54) and a moderate correlation between the expression levels of CD24 and CD82 (rs=0.72, p < 0.001, n = 55) and CD13 and CD82 (rs=0.73, p < 0.001, n = 55) (Fig. 4B) on plasma EVs. A comparison of the molecular EV profile assessed by imaging flow cytometry with the EV phenotype determined by NTA revealed a moderate or weak negative correlation between the total number of particles and the concentration of CMDR+ and, in some cases, between the number of CD63+ EVs in the cBALF and the presence of the following surface markers: CD82, CD13, PD-L1, PS, CD81, CD227, HLA-DR, and CD66b (Supp. Figure 5.F). Immunomodulatory potential of phenotypically distinct NSCLC plasma- and BALF-derived EVs in an in vitro 3D model of human airways (MucilAir™-M-EV)

To elucidate the potential immunosuppressive and proinflammatory potency of the two distinct EV subgroups in a physiological and fully humanized context, we used the recently available 3D model MucilAir™. This highly innovative in vitro model consists of a fully differentiated and functional airway epithelium, containing ciliated, goblet, club and basal cells, isolated from a healthy control and cultured at an air‒liquid interface. We further modified this model by adding human alveolar macrophages from a healthy donor to the apical side to recapitulate the in vivo conditions even more closely (Fig. 1). The culture was incubated for 48 h with EV samples from the two distinct subgroups, whereby plasma-derived EVs were added to the basal side and the corresponding BALF-derived EVs from the same patients were added to the apical side of the culture. We measured the levels of diverse pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines in the cell culture supernatant from the basal side, which represented mostly the epithelial compartment, as well as in the buffer used for washing the air-exposed apical side, which represented mostly the macrophage compartment (Fig. 5A and B).

In general, the impact of the NSCLC-derived EVs on the cytokine profile measured in both the basal medium, representing, in our opinion, mostly the secretome of airway epithelial cells, and the apical wash, representing mostly the secretome of alveolar macrophages, was very weak (Fig. 5A and B). In contrast, the EVs from potentially immunosuppressive, proinflammatory EVs did not change the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines by airway epithelial cells, whereas the EVs from the EV-low group caused a minimal decrease in the secretion of some proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNFα (fold change 0.78), IL-1β (fold change 0.84) and IFNϒ (fold change 0.88) (Fig. 5A). The greatest impact of EVs on cytokine production was observed for the pleiotropic cytokine IL-6, where EVs from the high-EV group induced a nearly 4-fold increase in secretion. A less pronounced increase was also observed for the anti-inflammatory cytokines/chemokines MCP-1 (nearly 2-fold increase) and IL-10 (increase of one-third). The EVs from the low-EV group, in comparison, increased the secretion of these cytokines/chemokines to a much lesser extent (approximately one-third of the increase). In the case of cytokine secretion by macrophages (Fig. 5B), represented by the measurements of the apical wash, in contrast to the epithelial compartment, EVs from both EV groups had a much stronger effect on the cytokine profile. Depending on the EV type (“high” or “low”), this impact on proinflammatory cytokines was different and often opposite: some cytokines, such as TNFα and IL-12p40 (fold change of 1.5–1.9 and 1.2–1.8, respectively), are upregulated, whereas others, such as IL-1β (fold change of 0.39) and IFNγ (fold change of 0.74–0.86), are downregulated. In contrast to those from the epithelial compartment, especially the EVs from the EV high group, the secretion of IL-4, IL-6 and IL-10 was decreased, and the secretion of TARC was increased. Striking is the very strong induction of the immunosuppressive enzyme arginase (more than 1000-fold). Notably, in contrast to those from the epithelial compartment, the EVs from the EV high group were apparently more potent cytokine modulators. With respect to cytotoxicity, we did not detect any significant changes upon coincubation with EVs from either group in the LDH assay (Fig. 5C). Prompted by the relatively weak immunomodulatory potential regarding airway epithelial cells of EVs from both groups determined by multiplex cytokine assays, we performed an additional proteomic analysis of airway epithelial tissues (tissue samples), alveolar macrophages (macrophage samples) and basal medium (medium samples) collected after coincubation of the MucilAir™ Model with both EV subgroups. We identified 2648 and 2710 exclusive proteins in the EV high and EV low group, respectively, in the tissue samples. In the untreated control sample, 423 proteins were identified, and 265 proteins were present in all the groups (Fig. 6A). Discussion

EVs as potential immunoregulatory biomarkers affecting NSCLC progression and therapy

Recently, EVs have gained significant attention as potential biomarkers for the diagnosis and prognosis of a variety of diseases, including NSCLC [20]. BALF-derived EVs offer a minimally invasive window into the pulmonary microenvironment, potentially reflecting localized tumor dynamics more accurately than blood-based assays. These EVs may carry tumor-specific cargo such as mutated EGFR [21], modulate immune activity within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [22] and inform on tumor progression or therapeutic response [20, 23–25]. Genetic and proteomic profiles of BALF EVs can reflect tumor subtype and mutation status, offering utility for precision medicine [26, 27].

F-NTA EV profile in plasma and BALF samples from NSCLC patients

Our f-NTA analysis showed that plasma samples had markedly higher EV concentrations (~ 200-fold in scatter mode, ~ 50-fold with CMDR) and smaller EV size (~ 100 nm vs. ~170–180 nm) compared to BALF, in line with our prior findings as well as other reports [14, 15]. Tetraspanins were undetectable in plasma EVs due to lipoprotein interference that was explained in more detail in our earlier mentioned report [15]. No significant differences were found between cBALF and oBALF, suggesting a shared lung EV microenvironment or technical factors such as fluid dilution and sampling variability may mask localized differences. Indeed, a similar uniformity was observed regarding some aspects of microbial composition; however, localized changes in microbial richness and specific taxa were also observed [28]. Finally, since we are able to analyze by f-NTA only highly abundant tetraspanin EV markers that are not cancer specific, possible differences in EV cargo between cBALF and oBALF might remain undetected. Unexpectedly, we could not find any statistically significant correlation between the EV profile assessed by f-NTA and any of the clinical parameters in either of the analyzed fluids. Therefore, we could not make, as anticipated, any conclusions regarding the diagnostic or prognostic potential of the BALF EV profile in comparison to the plasma-EV profile.

T-cell profiles in plasma and BALF samples from NSCLC patients

Flow cytometry of matched samples revealed weak-to-moderate correlations between blood and BALF lymphocytes, with no consistent intra-patient differences between oBALF and cBALF (Supp. Table 3). Moreover, there were no significant differences between the assessed lymphocyte profiles from the cBALF and oBALF of the same patient, in contrast to other reports [29–32], which may imply that both lungs create the same microenvironment. However, comparison between NSCLC and non-cancer patients showed evidence of immunosuppression in the TME (Fig. 9A).

A Summary of significant changes in the immunological profile detected by flow cytometry in the cBALF compartment of NSCLC patients compared with non-NSCLC/noncancer patients, indicating T-cell dysfunction and immunosuppression in the TME. B Summary of the immunomodulatory effects of NSCLC-derived EVs (blood-derived EVs and cBALF-derived EVs with low/high-molecular cargo) on secreted cytokines evaluated in an in vitro 3D model of healthy human airways. The effects on the secretion of cytokines/chemokines by airway epithelial cells (AECs) and macrophages are shown

Specifically, cancer patients exhibited in BALF reduced early activated CD4⁺ T cells (CD4⁺CD69⁺) but simultaneous elevated late activation markers (CD4⁺HLA-DR⁺), suggesting a deficiency in local T-cell activation [33] and T-cell exhaustion due to chronic antigen exposure [34]. Some studies have shown increased HLA-DR expression on regulatory T cells (Tregs), which contributes to immune suppression in lung cancer [35].

Tregs (CD4⁺CD25highCD127low) were increased in cancer BALF, with elevated CTLA-4 expression indicating potent immunosuppressive activity [36]. This favors tumor progression, as high Treg levels are associated with a poor prognosis in patients with lung cancer. On the other hand, decreased CD39 expression on Tregs, an ectonucleotidase that degrades ATP to adenosine and suppresses immune responses, suggests a shift in immunosuppressive strategies.

In later-stage NSCLC, CD8⁺ T cells expressed higher levels of Fas, a death receptor that induces apoptosis in activated T cells, suggesting increased apoptosis and T-cell depletion. The increase of Fas MFI on CD8 cells along with staging imply that as the disease progresses, there may be changes in the expression and function of apoptosis-related receptors on CD8+ lymphocytes. Only weak correlations were found between EV profiles evaluated by NTA and lymphocyte phenotypes, likely due to NTA’s limitation to abundant, non-specific EV markers (Supp. Tables 5–9).

Single-vesicle analysis of plasma-derived NSCLC EVs by image flow cytometry

To overcome NTA’s resolution limits, we used high-resolution image flow cytometry to phenotype plasma EVs using 25 surface markers [17]. For this purpose, we used a previously established panel of 25 EV surface markers, including cell-specific markers (e.g., CD41, CD61, and CD90), tumor-related markers (e.g., MUC1 (CD227), and CD44) and immunosuppressive markers (PD-L1, CTLA-4 (CD152)) (Supp. Table 10) [16]. BALF EVs could not be profiled due to dilution, low yield or insufficient quality. Among plasma EVs, abundant CD9⁺, CD63⁺ (tetraspanin-positive), CD41⁺ (platelet marker), CD66b⁺ (granulocyte marker), and CD171⁺ (neuronal cell marker) subtypes were detected, while tumor and checkpoint markers (e.g., PD-L1, CTLA-4) were rare. The obtained molecular EV signatures did not significantly correlate with any parameters of the NTA EV analysis or with the lymphocyte profile of the patients assessed by flow cytometry.

However, we found some significant positive correlations between some identified surface EV markers. Notably, CD24⁺ EVs (a known immune checkpoint ligand and tumor marker) correlated with CD13⁺ EVs (angiogenesis and Treg markers). Since increased CD24 expression is evident across various human tumor cells, CD24-carrying plasma EVs might be considered tumor derived. CD13⁺ EVs in plasma could be of endothelial origin and be a sign of tumor-driven angiogenesis, or alternatively, be indicative of the Treg-mediated immunosuppression. CD82⁺ and HLA-DR⁺ EVs, potentially lymphocyte-derived, may reflect antitumor activity. CD82, a known TSG capable of inhibiting the metastatic process in NSCLC [37], was found together with CD81 to act as a costimulatory molecule expressed on T cells that can bolster TCR signaling cytotoxicity against target cancer cells in TILs within the TME of NSCLC [38]. Similarly, HLA-DR is also a known marker of T-cell activation and circulating CD3⁺HLA-DR⁺ EVs were found to be a marker for Th1/Tc1-type immune responses [39]. A positive correlation between the concentrations of supposedly tumor-derived CD24+ EVs and tumor-related CD13+ EVs and between CD82+ EVs and HLA-DR⁺ EVs of lymphocytic origin indicates functional dependence: a high tumor burden (= high CD24+ EV level) and tumor-related angiogenesis and immunosuppression (= high CD24+ EV level) correlate with an adequate antitumor response of T lymphocytes, which is reflected by high levels of CD82+ and HLA-DR+ EVs. Co-detection of CD9⁺ and CD41⁺ markers points to platelet-derived EVs. This co-occurrence of immunosuppressive and immune-activating EV signatures indicates complex immune dynamics in NSCLC, with diagnostic and prognostic potential.

Immunomodulatory impact of NSCLC-derived EVs in an in vitro 3D model of human airways

Cytokine/Chemokine modulation

We employed a human-relevant 3D MucilAir™ model combining airway epithelial cells (AECs) and alveolar macrophages (AMs) to study the effects of NSCLC-derived EVs, categorized into “EV low” and “EV high” groups based on immunosuppressive surface markers [40, 41]. This coculture system provides greater translational relevance than murine models, which often fail to replicate human lung conditions faithfully [42], and aligns with the ethical 3Rs principles. Recent studies have demonstrated the efficacy of similar coculture methods: coculture of fully differentiated primary human airway epithelium (AECs) and human monocyte-derived macrophages at the air‒liquid interface provided valuable insights into the role of alveolar macrophages during SARS-CoV-2 infection [43] and a triple coculture model (epithelial cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells) simulating the human airway barrier [44] revealed the immune modulation and nanoparticle interactions within the respiratory tract. Here, we used a similar model in which primary human AECs and alveolar macrophages from a healthy donor were combined and cocultured at an air‒liquid interface with NSCLC-derived EVs to decipher their immunomodulatory impact.

Multiplex analysis (summarized in Fig. 8B) revealed that EVs, particularly “EV high”, moderately yet clearly modified the airway microenvironment to create a supportive niche for tumor development and promote tumor-supportive and immunosuppressive signaling [22, 45–49]. EVs increased MCP-1 secretion by AECs, promoting monocyte/macrophage recruitment and TAM accumulation, which supports tumor progression. MCP-1 has been linked to increased lung cancer metastasis and progression by driving immune cell infiltration and promoting angiogenesis, as well as promoting the metastasis and EMT of NSCLC via the PI3K/Akt/mTOR axis and autophagy signaling pathways. Further changes were elevated IL-10, an anti-inflammatory cytokine that dampens dendritic and cytotoxic T-cell responses, facilitating immune escape. High IL-10 levels have been correlated with poor prognosis in lung cancer patients. Strong IL-6 induction, especially by “EV high”, which despite its inflammatory nature, may imply tumor progression via STAT3 activation and EMT via the PI3K/Akt/mTOR axis and autophagy signaling pathways. IL-6 has been linked to increased resistance to chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors in lung cancer as well as to promote the metastasis and EMT of NSCLC (reviewed in [50]).

Since tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), the largest fraction of the myeloid infiltrate in the majority of human solid malignancies including lung cancer, support tumor progression, immune evasion and resistance to treatment [51], we paid particular attention to cytokine changes in these cells upon coincubation with lung cancer-derived EVs and observed a shift from the anti-tumor M1 phenotype toward the pro-tumor M2 phenotype. Increased levels of TARC (CCL17), a chemokine that attracts Th2 cells (CD4+ T cells expressing CCR4), both in the basal and apical compartments, promote Treg recruitment and Th2-skewing. IL-1RA (IL-1 receptor antagonist) elevation and IL-1β/IL-6 reduction in AMs, indicates further M2 polarization and immune dampening.

The most striking impact of both types of NSCLC-derived EVs is the robust induction of arginase, particularly “EV high”, suggests a major immunosuppressive mechanism involving L-arginine depletion, which restricts T-cell proliferation—a strategy also observed by us in ovarian cancer [52] or others in lung cancer [53]. As shown in ovarian cancer, ARG1-containing EVs can inhibit the proliferation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells both in vitro and in vivo and are capable of reaching distant lymph nodes to modulate immune activity by targeting dendritic cells. In the context of NSCLC-derived EVs, increased arginase expression could reflect a similar mechanism of metabolic suppression, wherein the tumor leverages EV-mediated delivery of ARG1 to manipulate macrophages or other immune cells. The reduction in the secretion of the immunosuppressive cytokines IL-4 and IL-10 by alveolar macrophages, seemingly contradictory to arginase induction, may suggest that these cells do not fully switch to an anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotype but obviously still exhibit some immune-suppressive properties. The increase in TARC and arginase suggests that lung cancer-derived EVs push AMs toward a modified immunosuppressive (TAM-like) state, which favors tumor immune evasion. A reduction in IL-6, IFNγ and IL-1β could indicate that EVs fine-tune the inflammatory balance to suppress an excessive immune response while maintaining immune evasion. The increase in IL-1RA further supports the idea that EVs dampen macrophage-driven inflammation, preventing antitumor immune activation. Interestingly, “EV high” directly suppressed IL-1β, reducing the need for IL-1RA compensation. In contrast, “EV low” EVs required IL-1RA upregulation as an indirect means of suppressing inflammation. Overall, EVs reprogram AMs more strongly than AECs, steering them toward a TAM-like phenotype with increased arginase, TARC, and anti-inflammatory signaling.

Impact on the profile of secreted proteins

Proteomics confirmed significant EV-induced remodeling in epithelial cells, macrophages, and the extracellular medium. EV “low” samples altered more proteins in the medium, while both EV types affected tumor- and immunity-related proteins, including downregulation of some oncogenes and the upregulation of others but with an overall pro-tumorigenic effect. The identified differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were associated mainly with transcription regulation, chromatin remodeling, posttranslational protein modification, DNA repair, cytoskeleton remodeling and cation channel signaling.

EV “low” caused a marked reduction in oncogenes like PIG-S (component of the GPI transamidase complex implicated in cancer progression [54]), MYO5A (unconventional myosin-Va), and TRIO (guanine nucleotide exchange factor for RHOA and RAC1). Absence or weak expression of PIG-S was observed in normal lung tissue and SCLC, whereas moderate to high expression was detected in various forms of NSCLC, especially in large cell carcinoma [55]. Overexpression of MYO5A was linked to invasion, low lymphocyte infiltration and lower survival rates in HPV + HNSCC [56], and TRIO correlated with metastasis and poor survival in HCC [57]. Moreover, this obvious anticancer effect was counteracted by strong induction of the oncogenic proteins EFMT1, SMC2 and RAD50, resulting in enhanced proliferation, migration and resistance to DNA damage. EFMT1, a lysine methyltransferase 1, functions as an oncogene in multiple cancers, including PDAC and HCC [58]. Upregulation of SMC2, a central component of the condensin complex required for chromatin remodeling, correlated with poor prognosis in lung adenocarcinoma, and its silencing suppressed the proliferation, migration and invasion ability of LUAD cells and inhibited tumor growth in vivo [59]. High expression of RAD50, responsible for repair of DNA damage, correlated with poor prognosis in NSCLC and melanoma (reviewed in [60]) and may confer resistance to antitumor drugs by enhancing DNA damage response mechanisms. “High” EVs mediated therapy resistance by decreasing MED12, enhancing resistance to EGFR, ALK, and BRAF inhibitors [61, 62], as well as by up-regulating SHPRH and CGGBP1, inducing overwhelming amounts of DNA damage to cancer cells and promoting cell cycle progression [63, 64].

Impact on the proteome of AECs

EV “low” reduced IPO7, potentially exerting anticancer effects, but upregulated JAK1 and PLP8, implying enhanced proliferation, migration, and oxidative stress resistance as well as immunosuppression and immunotherapy resistance, e.g., against anti-PD-1 therapy, promoting CD8 T-cell plasticity and therapeutic responses of exhausted and effector memory–like T-cell clonotypes [65]. Notably, JAK1/2 inhibition led to the establishment of an antitumorigenic tumor microenvironment characterized by decreased levels of tumor-promoting chemokines and cytokines and reduced numbers of infiltrating myeloid-derived suppressor cells, thereby impairing tumor growth [66]. The activation of PLP8, a protein responsible for maintaining lipid homeostasis in mitochondria and providing cytoprotection against oxidative stress, may drive proliferation and migration via the PI3K/Akt and MAPK/Erk pathways [67].

Similarly, the downregulation of oncogenes such as ATD2B (a transcriptional coactivator of ER and AR), SYAC (alanine-tRNA ligase), TRCP7 (a transient receptor potential channel component), and NF1 (neurofibromin) by “EV high” may indicate suppressed oncogenic signaling, migration, and ER/Ras activity. However, these effects are offset by increased expression of FA13B, PKH4B, and NLRP3 and decreased levels of the tumor suppressor NF1. FA13B (coagulation factor XIII B chain) is linked to chemoresistance [68]. PLEKHG4, which activates RhoGTPases, promotes thyroid cancer proliferation, migration, EMT, and stemness, while its loss induces apoptosis [69]. NLRP3 upregulation, associated with poor prognosis, enhances inflammation-driven tumor progression via IL-1β and IL-18 (reviewed in [70]). The Canakinumab Anti-inflammatory Thrombosis Outcomes Study (CANTOS) showed that IL-1β inhibition led to a 77% reduction in lung cancer mortality and 67% in incidence [71]. High NLRP3 expression correlates with M2 macrophage infiltration and poor prognosis; its activation (via LPS + ATP) promotes A549 cell proliferation, reversed by IL-1RA and IL-18BP [72]. Reduced NF1 may also contribute to resistance against BRAF/EGFR inhibitors, tamoxifen, and retinoic acid in multiple cancers [73].

Impact on the proteome of AMs

Both EV types skew AMs toward a tumor-promoting M2 phenotype. Strong downregulation of tumor suppressor PIEZ2, a mechanosensitive Ca2+ channel linked to Th polarization, M2 switching, and Treg induction may promote NSCLC progression [74]. Reduced RNF213, an atypical E3 ubiquitin ligase and another suppressor, correlates with poor immune infiltration and survival in LUAD, though it may also promote carcinogenesis [75, 76]. Conversely, “EV low” downregulated oncogenes like MA1B1 (alpha-mannosidase) and the heparan sulfate proteoglycan SULF2, potentially limiting proliferation, migration, and M2 polarization [77, 78]. “EV high” EVs exhibited predominantly antitumor effects decreasing MYO3B, suggesting RhoA/ROCK1 inhibition and antitumor effects [79], while increased ICE1 could support chemoresistance [80].

Given the central role of alveolar macrophages in lung immunity and tumor progression, we performed functional enrichment STRING analysis with the identified DEPs in AMs. A protein‒protein interaction network emerged with EGFR as a central node, downregulated by both EV types, alongside with the interconnected proteins SOS2, HSPA4, and PDGFRB. In contrast, ANXA2, HUWE1, EEA1, and ITSN1—all regulators of EGFR degradation—were upregulated. While EGFR is widely studied in epithelial cancers, it also regulates macrophage plasticity. Its activation suppresses M2 polarization via SOCS1 upregulation; its deletion increases IL-10 and dampens inflammation [81]. EGFR downregulation in late-stage cancers may thus enhance TAM-mediated tumor support. This aligns with findings from the CAIRO2 trial, where cetuximab paradoxically worsened outcomes, likely by activating tumor-promoting macrophages instead of inducing ADCC [82].

We propose a similar mechanism: NSCLC-derived EVs reduce at several levels EGFR, promoting M2-like macrophage reprogramming (Fig. 10B). EV-induced upregulation of EEA1, HUWE1, and ITSN1 facilitates EGFR turnover, promoting its proteasomal degradation [83, 84]. SOS2 downregulation further suppresses Ras signaling. Meanwhile, upregulated ANXA2 may compensate EGFR decrease in by promoting EGFR endocytosis and MAPK/NF-κB activation. In macrophages, ANXA2 tetramers drive inflammatory mediator release High ANXA2 expression might also be responsible for the observed enormous ARG increase in macrophages, since ANXA2 was shown to induce ARG1 mRNA expression via TLR2/MYD88 in tumor-infiltrating neutrophils in a mouse model of NSCLC [85]. In sum, NSCLC-EVs modulate macrophage behavior through EGFR degradation, ANXA2 signaling, and arginase induction—shaping a TME conducive to tumor progression, immune evasion and therapy resistance.

Study limitations

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, while we systematically compared f-NTA parameters of EVs from oBALF, cBALF, and plasma across 82 patients, no statistically significant correlations with available clinical characteristics such as disease stage were observed. Larger, more stratified patient cohorts may be required to detect subtle associations between EV biophysical properties and clinical parameters. moreover, absence of healthy control BALF samples limits interpretation of disease-specific changes. For ethical reasons, BALF collection was only performed during clinically indicated diagnostic bronchoscopy, and such procedures are normally not carried out in healthy volunteers. Future studies including healthy control cohorts, where ethically and clinically feasible, would help establish baseline EV characteristics in plasma and BALF. Second, although flow cytometry is a well-established approach for assessing macrophage polarization, we were constrained by the limited yield of primary human alveolar macrophages obtained in our long-term ALI coculture model, which precluded parallel proteomic and cytometric analyses. Instead, we employed a multimodal strategy—cytokine/chemokine profiling, arginase expression, and deep proteomic characterization of cells and secretome—which consistently revealed hallmarks of M2 polarization. We believe this integrative approach provided robust functional and molecular evidence, although additional flow cytometry-based validation in larger cohorts would be valuable in future work. Third, our study relied on a human primary 3D ALI model, which we chose deliberately over murine models because of their limited translational relevance for NSCLC macrophage biology. While animal studies can provide complementary in vivo insights, interspecies differences in macrophage polarization, cytokine signaling, and EV uptake limit their utility for directly modeling human NSCLC. We therefore prioritized a reductionist but physiologically relevant human-based system. Nonetheless, future investigations in immune-humanized in vivo Models may further substantiate our findings. Finally, our conclusions are limited by the finite availability of both patient-derived EV samples and advanced 3D tissue cultures, which restricted the number of validation experiments that could be performed. Finally, EGFR-related pathways were inferred bioinformatically and require targeted functional confirmation. Despite these constraints, our results consistently highlight the capacity of NSCLC-derived EVs to reprogram alveolar macrophages toward an immunosuppressive, tumor-supportive phenotype in a human airway-like microenvironment.

Conclusions

Our study reveals that NSCLC-derived EVs shape a tumor-promoting lung microenvironment, largely by reprogramming alveolar macrophages toward an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype. While EVs from plasma showed diverse, patient-specific profiles, no molecular or immunological differences were observed between cancerous and non-cancerous BALF compartments, possibly due to technical limitations in BALF EV profiling. While f-NTA captured general EV patterns, image flow cytometry revealed immunosuppressive and tumor-associated EV markers that correlated with immune dysfunction in NSCLC. Supplementary flow cytometric analysis of immune cells revealed tumor-associated chronic T-cell activation, exhaustion, and Treg-mediated immunosuppression. Using a 3D in vitro airway model, both EV subtypes—despite differences in surface marker density—promoted immunosuppression via IL-10 and arginase induction, MCP-1 secretion, and suppression of proinflammatory cytokines. These findings suggest that NSCLC-derived EVs drive a tumor-supportive microenvironment, at least partially independent of their immunomodulatory surface marker composition. Instead, intravesical cargo, such as miRNAs, may exert a more significant influence, warranting further investigation. Proteomic analysis further highlighted EGFR downregulation as a potential driver of M2 polarization.

In summary, NSCLC-EVs actively modulate immune responses in the lung, with EGFR suppression in macrophages emerging as a key mechanism. These findings call for careful consideration of macrophage-specific EGFR signaling in anti-EGFR therapies to avoid unintended immunosuppressive effects.
