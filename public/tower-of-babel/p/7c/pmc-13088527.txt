Radiation-induced MYB reduction unleashes TIM-3 expression in NK cells to attenuate antitumor immunity in colorectal cancer
Authors: Gan Tao, Xiuli Guo, Jiaqiang Xiong, Sisi Yan, Xiaowan Guo, Kehua Hu, Wanyi Tai, Lin-lin Bu, Hui Qiu, Qiuji Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC), the third most common cancer globally, presents challenges like metastasis, recurrence, and therapy resistance. Natural Killer (NK) cells are vital for anti-tumor immunity but become dysfunctional in the tumor microenvironment (TME). This study explored radiation’s mechanism regulating NK cell function to support combined radiotherapy (RT) and T cell immunoglobulin domain and mucin domain-3 (TIM-3) targeting in CRC.MethodWe examined the effects of radiation on NK cell activation, cytokine secretion, and modulation of the TME. The expression of TIM-3 and MYB was analyzed in NK-92 cells, patient-derived peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), and clinical tumor specimens using flow cytometry, immunohistochemistry (IHC) and multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) staining. TIM-3 knockdown and MYB overexpression were performed to assess their regulatory roles in NK cell function, and the direct binding of MYB to the TIM-3 promoter was validated by dual-luciferase reporter and ChIP assays. In vivo efficacy was tested using a subcutaneous CRC mouse model treated with RT, anti-TIM-3 antibody, and/or NK cell depletion, followed by IHC and mIF analysis of mouse and human CRC tissues.ResultsRadiation augmented NK cell activity, enhancing cytokine secretion and modulating the TME. However, radiation paradoxically upregulated the inhibitory receptor TIM-3 while suppressing MYB expression in NK cells, as confirmed in NK-92 cells, primary human NK cells, and clinical tumor specimens. TIM-3 knockdown enhanced NK cell activation and cytokine production following radiation, whereas MYB overexpression potentiated radiation-induced NK cell activation by directly binding to the TIM-3 promoter and suppressing its expression. In vivo, combining RT with anti-TIM-3 antibody synergistically suppressed tumor growth in an NK cell-dependent manner and enhanced intratumoral infiltration of NK and CD8+ T cells. mIF of mouse and human tumor tissues confirmed decreased MYB, increased TIM-3, and enhanced NK cell infiltration following RT. Flow cytometry of irradiated human PBMCs further recapitulated the TIM-3 upregulation and MYB downregulation in primary NK cells.ConclusionsIrradiation activates NK cells but concurrently induces TIM-3 via MYB suppression. Blocking TIM-3 synergizes with RT to promote NK cell immune activation and inhibit CRC progression. These findings provide a mechanistic basis for combined RT and TIM-3 immunotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08097-y.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is the third most common malignant tumor worldwide, and its metastatic recurrence and acquired therapy resistance remain persistent clinical challenges [1]. Radiotherapy, as one of the main cornerstone modalities in oncology, its mechanistic role has evolved beyond localized tumor eradication to encompass systemic immunomodulation, particularly through radiation-induced abscopal effects and enhanced tumor immunogenicity [2–4]. However, traditionally, the low radiosensitivity of colon cancer is considered to limit its wide application. Therefore, there is an urgent need for combined targeted therapy to augment tumoricidal efficacy through synergistic biological pathway modulation.

Natural killer cells, as a central component of the innate immune system, hold significant value in RT synergy and immune microenvironment modulation. On one hand, RT can promote the release of tumor antigens by inducing DNA damage and immunogenic cell death, thereby enhancing the activation of NK cells. Moreover, surface receptors (e.g., NKG2D) are used to recognize stress ligands (e.g., MICA/B) exposed on tumor cells after RT to enhance the targeted killing efficiency of NK cells [5, 6]. On the other hand, RT may activate the local inflammatory response, promote the secretion of chemokines such as CXCL10, and increase the infiltration of NK cells into the tumor site [7]. Meanwhile, during the RT for colon cancer, NK cells may reduce complications such as intestinal mucosal injury resulting from RT by regulating the immune balance. However, within the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, NK cells exhibit upregulated expression of self-MHC class I-specific inhibitory receptors, which reduces their cytotoxic capacity. These inhibitory receptors include programmed death-1 (PD-1), T cell immune receptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT), lymphocyte activation gene 3 protein (LAG-3) and TIM-3 [8–10]. These receptors demonstrate upregulated expression on NK cells, thereby suppressing their antitumor effector functions, and thus emerge as novel therapeutic targets for checkpoint blockade-based immunotherapy [11].

TIM-3 is a cell surface receptor composed of extracellular, transmembrane, and intracellular domains, mediating pleiotropic immunomodulatory functions across diverse pathological contexts [12, 13]. As an inhibitory immune checkpoint, TIM-3 is widely expressed on immune cells and is recognized as a marker of NK cell maturation, being ubiquitously present on nearly all mature CD56dimCD16+ NK cells [14]. At the post-translational level, DHHC9-mediated palmitoylation of TIM-3 is essential for its membrane localization and stability; inhibition of DHHC9 promotes TIM-3 degradation, reverses T cell exhaustion, and enhances CAR-T/NK cell anti-tumor activity [15]. Beyond post-translational modifications, TIM-3 expression is transcriptionally regulated by multiple factors. Notably, TCF-1 (encoded by TCF7) represses TIM-3 transcription, maintaining T cell stemness and preventing exhaustion, whereas BLIMP1 (encoded by PRDM1) promotes TIM-3 expression and drives T cell exhaustion [16, 17]. These findings highlight the complex transcriptional control of TIM-3 in T cells and its functional implications. However, whether analogous regulatory networks govern TIM-3 expression in NK cells remains largely unexplored. Given its high expression on circulating and tumor-infiltrating NK cells in various malignancies and its association with poor prognosis [18–20], understanding the mechanisms underlying TIM-3 regulation in NK cells is of critical importance. Moreover, TIM-3 blockade reverses NK cell exhaustion, enhances IFN-γ production, and restores NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, positioning it as a promising therapeutic target [21]. Elucidating the transcriptional control of TIM-3 in NK cells, particularly in the context of cancer therapy, may therefore reveal novel strategies to enhance NK cell anti-tumor immunity and optimize combination immunotherapy.

V-myb avian myeloblastosis viral oncogene homolog (MYB) is an important member of the MYB transcription factor family. It regulates the expression of downstream genes by binding to specific sequences (e.g., PyAACG/TG) within the promoters of target genes. It is predominantly expressed in the hematopoietic system, intestinal stem cells, and immune cells [22]. Recent studies have found that MYB plays an important role in various cancers and is potentially associated with immune regulation [23, 24]. MYB exerts pro-tumorigenic effects in cancers by driving proliferation, inhibiting apoptosis, and promoting metastasis, while simultaneously contributing to anti-tumor immunity by maintaining T cell stemness and reversing exhaustion [25, 26]. Notably, MYB has also been shown to preserve effector function and prevent exhaustion in NK cells [27]. This dual functionality establishes it as a critical therapeutic target in precision oncology.

Multiple studies have demonstrated that radiation upregulates TIM-3 expression in T cells [28, 29], and this upregulation has been associated with diminished radiosensitivity. For example, radiation-induced TIM-3 expression mediates resistance to both RT and anti-PD-1 immunotherapy in lung cancer and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [4, 30]. However, the precise regulatory mechanisms underlying radiation-induced TIM-3 expression remain unclear. More importantly, existing studies have exclusively focused on T cells, leaving the question of how radiation regulates TIM-3 in NK cells and the underlying mechanisms largely unexplored. To address this gap, we employed an integrated bioinformatic approach coupled with transcriptomic analysis and identified MYB as a putative transcriptional regulator of TIM-3 in NK cells, with its expression significantly downregulated following radiation exposure. Molecular docking further predicted high-affinity binding between MYB and the TIM-3 promoter, leading us to hypothesize that MYB directly represses TIM-3 transcription in irradiated NK cells. Although MYB itself may not be readily druggable, delineating this regulatory axis could uncover upstream modulators amenable to therapeutic intervention, and MYB expression levels may serve as a predictive biomarker for response to combined RT and TIM-3 blockade.

Based on this hypothesis, we proceeded to investigate the functional role of MYB in NK cells following RT. Here, we demonstrate that during radiation-induced activation of NK cells in CRC, MYB expression is downregulated, thereby relieving its transcriptional repression of TIM-3 and impairing NK cell cytotoxicity through enhanced immune checkpoint signaling. Conversely, overexpression of MYB potentiated ionizing radiation (IR)-induced NK cell activation by directly suppressing TIM-3. Collectively, these findings uncover a mechanistic link between MYB and TIM-3 in NK cells post-IR and provide a rationale for combining RT with TIM-3 blockade to overcome NK cell dysfunction.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cell culture

The NK-92 cell line (RRID: CVCL_2142) was purchased from Pricella Biotechnology (Cat. No. CM-0530, Wuhan, China) and cultured in the NK-92-specific growth medium (Cat. No. CM-0530, Pricella Biotechnology). The human CRC cell line HCT116 (RRID: CVCL_0291) was purchased from the Cell Bank of the Committee on Type Culture Collection, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Cat. No. SCSP-5076, Wuhan, China). The SW480 cell line (RRID: CVCL_1724) was obtained from the Pricella Biotechnology (Cat. No. CL-0223). Both of the cell lines were cultured in high-glucose DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The HEK-293T cell line (RRID: CVCL_0063) was purchased from Pricella Biotechnology (Cat. No. CL-0005) and cultured in high-glucose DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Luciferase-expressing MC38 cells (RRID: CVCL_B288) were purchased from Hysigen Bioscience (Cat. No. TCM-C790L, Suzhou, China) and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 6.0 µg/mL puromycin. All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Genetic authentication and confirmation of the absence of cross-contamination were performed for all cell lines used in this study via STR (Short Tandem Repeat) profiling. Additionally, routine mycoplasma testing was performed to exclude mycoplasma contamination.

Cell transfection

siRNA Transfection: Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting TIM-3 were synthesized by AuGCT Biotechnology (Wuhan, China), with sequences provided in Supplementary Material Table S2. NK-92 cells were transfected with TIM-3 siRNA or negative control siRNA using the jetPRIME siRNA transfection reagent (Polyplus-transfection, Illkirch, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 2 × 10⁵ NK-92 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected with 50 nM siRNA mixed with jetPRIME reagent. Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for downstream analysis. Knockdown efficiency was validated by RT-qPCR and Western blot.

Plasmid Transfection: The MYB overexpression plasmid was constructed by MiaoLing Plasmid Platform (Wuhan, China). For transfection, NK-92 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and transfected with 2.5 µg of MYB overexpression plasmid or empty vector control using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were collected 48 h after transfection for RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis to confirm overexpression efficiency.

Mice and treatment

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks, SPF-grade) were subcutaneously inoculated with 5 × 106 MC38 CRC cells. Tumor growth was monitored daily, with tumor dimensions measured every 2 days using a digital caliper, and tumor volume was calculated as (length × width2)/2. When tumors reached approximately 500 mm3 (10 days post-inoculation), treatment with RT (RT) and monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) was initiated. Mice were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 7 per group initially): IgG isotype control, RT alone, anti-TIM-3 mAb alone, RT combined with anti-TIM-3 mAb, and RT combined with anti-TIM-3 mAb plus anti-NK1.1 mAb. To minimize variability, the smallest and largest tumors in each group were excluded from final analysis, resulting in five mice per group for data presentation. For anti-TIM-3 treatment, the antibody (murine-specific) was administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) at 200 µg per mouse every two days for a total of four doses. For NK cell depletion, anti-NK1.1 mAb (200 µg per mouse) was administered i.p. twice weekly throughout the study, as indicated in Fig. 2A. The antibody suspension consisted of 5% DMSO, 40% PEG300, 5% Tween-80, and 50% ddH₂O. Mice in the RT groups received radiation therapy (4 Gy per fraction) for three consecutive days. Mice were euthanized on Day 19 for tumor volume measurement and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining. All procedures complied with the ethical guidelines approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Wuhan University (Approval No. ZN2025091).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol Reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from RNA using RT-qPCR Fast Master Mix (Vazyme, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using a CFX384 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA), with β-actin mRNA serving as the internal control. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences used in this study are provided in Supplementary Material Table S1.

Radiation

Both NK-92 cells and C57BL/6 mice were exposed to X-ray radiation using a small animal radiation system (PXI X-RAD 225Cx, Precision X-ray, North Branford, CT, USA). For in vitro experiments, NK-92 cells were irradiated at the indicated doses and subsequently analyzed by RT-qPCR, western blotting, flow cytometry, and immunofluorescence assays. For in vivo studies, colorectal tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice received local tumor irradiation with 4 Gy per fraction administered daily for three consecutive days, beginning on the initial day of treatment. The radiation field was confined to the tumor area using lead shielding to minimize exposure to surrounding normal tissues.

RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq)

Total RNA was isolated from samples using TRIzol reagent, with RNA concentration quantified by NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometry. RNA sequencing was conducted on the Illumina HiSeq platform, followed by differential gene expression analysis using the “DESeq2” package with significance thresholds set at adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2 (fold change)| > 0.5. Functional annotation of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed through Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) implemented by the “clusterProfiler” package. The associated biological processes (BP) were visualized using bubble charts and GSEA enrichment plots, respectively. Significantly enriched GO terms were identified based on p < 0.05.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Commercially available ELISA kits were used to quantify the concentrations of TNF-α (RK00030, ABclonal), IFN-γ (RK00015, ABclonal), Granzyme B (GZMB; RK00089, ABclonal), and perforin (RK00135, ABclonal) in the designated samples following manufacturers’ protocols.

Western blotting

Total proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors, and equal protein amounts (20–30 µg) were separated on 10–12% SDS-polyacrylamide gels via electrophoresis and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies, washed, and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1 h, room temperature). Finally, protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) on a Bio-Rad imaging system. Antibodies used in this study are provided in Supplementary Material Table S3

Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

First, the slices are dewaxed and rehydrated through a series of graded immersions in xylene and ethanol. Then antigen retrieval using alkaline Tris/EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) is performed on the slices. For mIF, the slides were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide to inhibit the activity of endogenous peroxidase and blocked with 10% goat serum. Then, the slices were incubated with a series of primary antibodies and then with the appropriate secondary antibodies and TSA reagents. Nuclear re-staining was performed using 4’, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 10 min, and the sections were finally preserved with an anti-fading sealing agent. For IHC, after the recovery of the de-affinity antigen, the sections were incubated overnight with the primary antibody at 4 °C. Subsequently, slides were treated with a matched secondary antibody and streptavidin peroxidase (MXB Biotechnologies, China), DAB chromogenic development and hematoxylin reverse staining were performed to observe the target protein and cell nucleus. Finally, the slides were scanned using a digital slide scanner. All mIF and IHC images were acquired under identical exposure and gain settings to ensure unbiased comparison. The staining intensity was analysed and quantified by ImageJ software. The relevant antibodies used and their detailed information are listed in Supplementary Material Table S3.

Bioluminescence imaging

Following anesthesia with 1% pentobarbital sodium, C57BL/6 mice were intraperitoneally administered firefly luciferin (150 mg/kg; HY-12591B, MedChemExpress). After a 15-minute incubation period, bioluminescence imaging was performed using a Bruker In Vivo MS FX PRO Imager.

Isolation of PBMCs from healthy donors

Peripheral blood samples were collected from healthy volunteers at Wuhan University following informed consent, in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by density gradient centrifugation using Lymphoprep (STEMCELL Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, fresh heparinized blood was diluted 1:1 with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), carefully layered over Lymphoprep solution, and centrifuged at 800 × g for 30 min at room temperature with the brake off. The mononuclear cell layer at the interface was collected, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cell viability was assessed by trypan blue exclusion, and only samples with viability > 95% were used for subsequent experiments. Isolated PBMCs were either used immediately or cultured overnight before radiation treatment.

Flow cytometry

Cell apoptosis analysis: Cells were harvested, washed twice with ice-cold PBS, and resuspended in 1× annexin-binding buffer. The cells were then stained with Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) and propidium iodide (PI) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Apoptosis was analyzed immediately using a flow cytometer, with detection in the FITC (for annexin V) and PE (for PI) channels. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

NKG2D and CD69 expression analysis: NK-92 cells were harvested after the indicated treatments and washed with PBS containing 1% FBS. For surface staining, cells were incubated with the following antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark: PerCP-Cy5.5 (RB705)-conjugated anti-human NKG2D and PE-Cy7 (RY775)-conjugated anti-human CD69. Appropriate isotype controls were used to set gate boundaries. After staining, cells were washed twice and resuspended in PBS for acquisition. NKG2D and CD69 expression was detected in corresponding channels and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Analysis of PBMC subsets: Human PBMCs were stimulated with PMA for 5 h in the presence of brefeldin A before harvest. Cells were stained with Fixable Viability Stain 700 (FVS700) in the AF700 channel to exclude dead cells, followed by surface staining with antibodies against CD3 (BV510), CD56 (BV650), TIM-3 (BV421), and CD69 (PE-Cy7 [RY775]) for 30 min at 4 °C. After fixation and permeabilization, intracellular staining was performed with antibodies against MYB (APC) and IFN-γ (PE [RY586]). Fluorescence minus one (FMO) controls were used to define positive populations. Data were acquired on a flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software. NK cells were identified as CD3−CD56+ cells within the live lymphocyte gate, and expression of TIM-3, MYB, CD69, and IFN-γ was assessed in this population.

Trypan blue exclusion assay

Treated cells were collected using a trypan blue kit (C0017, Beyoti), centrifuged and washed twice with PBS. After mixing with trypan blue solution (1:1 ratio), samples were incubated at room temperature for 3 min in darkness. Cell viability was assessed by microscopic examination of 10 µL aliquots on counting plates: live cells remained colorless while dead cells stained blue. Survival rates were calculated as (live cells/total cells ×100%) based on counts from four randomly selected fields.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

HEK-293T Cells were seeded in 24-well plates (5 × 10⁴/well) and co-transfected with the MYB promoter-luciferase reporter (1 µg/well), Renilla control plasmid (0.1 µg/well), and TIM-3 overexpression/empty vector (0.5 µg/well) via Lipofectamine 3000. Dual-luciferase activities (firefly/Renilla) in cell lysates were sequentially measured 48 h post-transfection using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (KTA8010, Abbkine).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

MYB binding to the TIM-3 promoter was examined in NK-92 cells (1 × 10⁷) via chromatin immunoprecipitation (BeyoChIP™ Kit P2078). Formaldehyde-crosslinked chromatin was enzymatically fragmented (200–500 bp), immunoprecipitated with anti-MYB or IgG control at 4 °C overnight, then captured using magnetic beads. Purified DNA was quantified by qPCR with TIM-3 promoter-specific primers, normalized against 1% input DNA.

Co-culture system

An indirect co-culture system was established using 6-well Transwell plates with 0.4 μm pore polyester membrane, where 2 × 10⁵ colon cancer cells (HCT116 or SW480) in 1.5 mL complete DMEM medium with 10% FBS were seeded in the upper chamber while the lower chamber contained either 2 × 10⁶ NK-92 cells pretreated for 24 h under different conditions or DMEM medium alone as the control. The co-culture system was incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 48 h. Following incubation, cells from both compartments were collected separately for subsequent analyses.

Clinical sample collection

Paired CRC tumor tissues for immunohistochemistry (IHC) and multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) staining were collected from the Department of Radiation and Medical Oncology, Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University (Wuhan, Hubei, China). Each pair consisted of pre-treatment biopsy specimens from newly diagnosed patients who had not received any prior therapy and post-RT surgical specimens obtained from the same patients following neoadjuvant RT and tumor resection. All samples were collected with informed consent, and the study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University (Approval No. 2025115K).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graphical representations were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± SD. Two-group comparisons utilized two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test or two-tailed paired Student’s t test, while multi-group comparisons employed one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Irradiation increases the infiltration of NK cells in tumor tissues and activates NK cells in vitro

To investigate the effects of RT on NK cells in CRC, we collected biopsy specimens from CRC patients before and after RT. Immunofluorescence assay showed that the number of infiltrating NK cells in tumor tissues significantly increased after RT (Fig. 1A and B), suggesting that RT may promote the infiltration of NK cells into the tumor microenvironment. We established an in vitro model by subjecting human NK-92 cells to X-ray radiation at varying doses (0, 1, 2, and 4 Gy). Trypan blue exclusion assay demonstrated that X-ray radiation in the low dose group (1 Gy), unlike in the high dose group (2 Gy and 4 Gy), did not produce significant cytotoxic effects on NK-92 cells (Fig. 1C). Therefore, we selected NK-92 cells radiated with 1 Gy X-rays for subsequent analysis. At 24 h post-radiation, RT-qPCR was performed to assess the transcriptional levels of activation-related inflammatory cytokines, including IFN-γ, TNF-α, PRF, and GZMB. The results demonstrated that low-dose radiation significantly enhanced the expression of IFN-γ, TNF-α, PRF, and GZMB, thereby promoting NK-92 cell activation (Figs. 1D-G). Further flow cytometry analysis revealed that low-dose radiation (1 Gy) significantly upregulated the expression of the activation markers NKG2D and CD69 in NK-92 cells (Figs. 1H-K). To extend these findings to primary human NK cells, we isolated PBMCs from healthy donors and exposed them to 1 Gy X‑ray radiation. After 24 h, flow cytometric analysis of viable CD3−CD56+ NK cells demonstrated a marked increase in CD69 and IFN‑γ expression compared to non‑irradiated controls (Figs. 1L-O). These results collectively indicate that low‑dose radiation activates both NK‑92 and primary human NK cells. Overall, findings from both clinical specimens and in vitro experiments indicate that low-dose radiation induces NK cell activation without triggering cell death. However, the precise mechanisms underlying radiation-induced NK cell activation require further experimental investigation.

Radiation promotes the expression of TIM-3 in NK cells

To investigate the mechanisms underlying radiation-induced NK cell activation, NK-92 cells were irradiated in vitro with 1 Gy X-rays and harvested at 24 h post-irradiation for RNA sequencing. Differential expression analysis identified 268 DEGs, including 158 upregulated and 110 downregulated genes (Fig.2A). Among the DEGs, genes associated with NK cell immune function and cytotoxicity exhibited increased expression, such as TIM-3, CRTAM, CLNK, and CD160 (Fig. 2B). Enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were enriched in pathways related to NK cell activation, cytotoxicity, and immunomodulatory functions (Figs. 2D and E). Notably, TIM-3 was significantly upregulated in NK cells after radiation (Fig. 2C). Importantly, our results further demonstrated that both mRNA and protein levels of TIM-3 showed a consistent upward trend in NK-92 cells exposed to X-rays across varying radiation doses and time intervals (Figs. 2F-H). To further investigate the clinical relevance of radiation‑induced TIM‑3 upregulation, we analyzed two publicly available single‑cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‑seq) datasets. Due to the lack of CRC specimens with RT, we examined a dataset of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC; GSE221561, including 7 patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy and 2 undergoing surgery alone) and a dataset of renal cell carcinoma (RCC; GSE202374, comparing RT‑treated resected tumors with nephrectomy‑only controls) (Supplementary Material Figs. S1A and C). In both datasets, TIM‑3 expression was significantly elevated in NK cells from post‑RT samples compared to non‑irradiated controls (Supplementary Material Figs. S1B and D), corroborating our in vitro findings. Notably, TIM‑3 upregulation was not restricted to NK cells: in ESCC, increased TIM‑3 was also observed in B cells, dendritic cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, macrophages, mast cells, and T cells after RT; similarly, in RCC, TIM‑3 was upregulated in B cells, T cells, epithelial cells, macrophages, monocytes, and dendritic cells (Supplementary Material Figs. S1B and D). These results indicate that RT induces a broad upregulation of TIM‑3 across multiple cell types within the tumor microenvironment. Given that TIM-3 serves as a critical negative immune checkpoint in NK cells, we hypothesize that its upregulation during radiation-induced NK cells activation may counteract this process.

TIM-3 regulates radiation-induced NK cell activation

To determine whether radiation-induced upregulation of TIM-3 regulates NK cell activation, we established a TIM-3-knockdown NK-92 cell model using siRNA. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed at both mRNA and protein levels by RT-qPCR and Western blot (Figs. 3A-C). Subsequently, NK-92 cells were irradiated with a single dose of X-rays (1 Gy), and both cells and supernatants were collected after 24 h for qPCR and ELISA analyses. The results demonstrated that TIM-3 knockdown significantly enhanced the expression and secretion of inflammatory cytokines, including IFN-γ, TNF-α, PRF, and GZMB, thereby further promoting NK cell activation (Figs. 3D-K). Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis revealed that TIM‑3 knockdown significantly upregulated the expression of the activation markers NKG2D and CD69 in NK‑92 cells following radiation (Figs. 3L-O). These findings clarify that TIM-3 knockdown mitigates the radiation-induced upregulation of this inhibitory checkpoint, thereby potentiating NK cell activation and enhancing anti-tumor effector functions.

MYB transcriptionally represses TIM-3 by direct promoter binding

To identify transcription factors (TFs) regulating TIM-3 expression, we integrated bioinformatic predictions from JASPAR, hTFtarget, HumanTFDB, GTRD, and GeneCards databases, revealing 39 candidates TFs with potential binding sites in the promoter region of TIM-3 (Fig. 4A). The intersection of these candidates with DEGs from our previous RNA-seq data identified MYB as the overlapping transcription factor (Fig. 4B). RNA-seq differential expression analysis further demonstrated that MYB was significantly downregulated in irradiated NK cells (Fig. 4C). Subsequent validation via RT-qPCR and western blot confirmed that radiation suppressed both transcriptional and protein expression levels of MYB in NK-92 cells (Figs. 4D-F). Molecular docking analysis between MYB and the TIM-3 promoter revealed a high-affinity interaction, with an HDOCK docking score of -334.41 and a binding free energy of -50.86 kcal/mol (Fig. 4G). These values are consistent with strong protein-DNA interactions, as HDOCK scores below -200 typically indicate favorable binding [31], and the calculated binding free energy reflects stable complex formation across multiple contact points within the promoter region. The JSAPAR database showed the motif of MYB, which may be the binding site of MYB to TIM-3 (Fig. 4H). Finally, Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay and ChIP assays validated that MYB directly binds to the 2,100 bp upstream promoter sequence of TIM-3 (Figs. 4I and J).

MYB overexpression enhanced radiation-induced NK cell activation by inhibiting TIM-3

The MYB-overexpression NK cell model was constructed using the overexpression plasmid. First, RT-qPCR (Figs. 5A and B) and western blot (Figs. 5C-E) were performed to validate MYB overexpression efficiency and its effect on TIM-3 expression. The results demonstrated that MYB overexpression inhibited radiation-induced upregulation of TIM-3. ELISA assays further revealed that MYB overexpression enhanced the secretion of radiation-induced inflammatory cytokines, including IFN-γ, TNF-α, PRF, and GZMB, thereby promoting NK cell activation (Figs. 5F-I). Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis revealed that MYB overexpression significantly upregulated the expression of the activation markers NKG2D and CD69 in NK‑92 cells following radiation (Figs. 5J-M), consistent with the trends observed in cytokine secretion. These findings confirm that MYB mediates radiation-induced NK cell activation.

Finally, using an in vitro co-culture model of NK cells and CRC cells (Fig. 5N), flow cytometry analysis showed that radiation combined with MYB overexpression significantly enhanced NK cell-mediated tumor cell apoptosis compared to radiation alone (Figs. 5O-Q). Collectively, these results indicate that MYB overexpression amplifies radiation-induced NK cell activation by targeting and suppressing TIM-3 expression.

Radiation combined with anti-TIM-3 monoclonal antibody further suppresses tumor growth

In the in vivo experiments, the subcutaneous syngeneic tumor model was established in mice using MC38 CRC cells stably transfected with luciferase. We investigated the anti-tumor efficacy of NK cells in CRC by administering anti-TIM-3 monoclonal antibody (aTIM-3, to inhibit TIM-3 in mice), anti-NK1.1 antibody (aNK1.1, to deplete NK cells in mice), and radiotherapy (RT). Mice were divided into five groups: Control, RT, aTIM-3, RT+aTIM-3, and RT+aTIM-3+aNK1.1. The experimental timeline is illustrated in Fig. 6A. Comparisons of tumor volume changes across groups revealed that all treatment groups exhibited delayed tumor progression. Specifically, compared to the Control group, tumor volumes were reduced in both the RT and aTIM-3 groups. The RT+aTIM-3 group showed further tumor volume reduction compared to RT alone, demonstrating that the combined application of aTIM-3 further enhances the efficacy of RT. Notably, the RT+aTIM-3 + aNK1.1 group displayed larger tumor volumes compared to the RT+aTIM-3 group, indicating that TIM-3 in NK cells plays a crucial role in the anti-tumor process (Figs. 6B-D). These findings demonstrate that blocking TIM-3 in vivo enhances the anti-tumor effects of RT, and the therapeutic effects of the anti-TIM-3 monoclonal antibody may partially depend on NK cells in vivo.

IHC analyses of mouse tumor tissues revealed that the anti-NK1.1 antibody partially depleted NK cells in vivo. In the RT group, MYB expression was downregulated, while TIM-3 expression was markedly upregulated, correlating with suppressed tumor progression, in alignment with our in vitro findings. Furthermore, RT increased the infiltration of NK cells and CD8+ T cells into tumors, an effect that was more pronounced in the RT+aTIM-3 group (Figs. 7A-F). To further validate these findings, we performed multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) staining on tumor tissues from the Control and RT groups. Consistent with the IHC results, mIF analysis revealed that RT increased the infiltration of NK cells into tumors, accompanied by upregulated TIM-3 expression and downregulated MYB expression within the tumor microenvironment (Figs. 7G-J). Quantitative analysis of co-localization further demonstrated that the proportion of NKp46+TIM-3+ cells was significantly increased in the RT group compared to the Control group, whereas the proportion of NKp46+MYB+ cells was markedly decreased following RT (Figs. 7K and L). These in vivo observations are consistent with our in vitro findings that radiation downregulates MYB while upregulating TIM-3 in NK cells. The inverse correlation between MYB and TIM-3 expression within infiltrating NK cells further supports the regulatory relationship identified in our mechanistic studies. These results suggested that combining RT with anti-TIM-3 antibody further enhances anti-tumor immunity in vivo.

Radiation increases NK cell infiltration in CRC tissues by regulating the MYB-TIM-3 pathway

Additionally, IHC and mIF staining of pre- and post-RT biopsy specimens from CRC patients demonstrated that RT downregulated MYB and upregulated TIM-3 in tumor tissues, accompanied by increased NK cell infiltration, and mirroring the observations in mice (Figs. 8A-J). To further validate these findings in human peripheral blood, we isolated PBMCs from healthy donors and exposed them to 1 Gy IR. Flow cytometric analysis of viable CD3−CD56+ NK cells revealed that radiation significantly upregulated TIM-3 expression while downregulating MYB expression compared to non-irradiated controls (Figs. 8K-N), consistent with our observations in tumor tissues and in vitro NK-92 cells. In summary, our study reveals that RT induces NK cell infiltration into tumors in vivo while concurrently upregulating the immunosuppressive checkpoint TIM-3. Combined with the experimental results in mice, we confirmed that blocking TIM-3 through antibody therapy can enhance the anti-tumor effect of RT and further delay the progression of tumors.

Discussion

Radiotherapy plays a pivotal role in CRC treatment, with current research focusing on its combination with immunotherapy. TIM-3 has emerged as a critical immune checkpoint regulating T cell exhaustion, and its role in NK cells has attracted increasing attention [14]. In this study, we investigated the dual role of RT in anti-tumor immunity. We found that while RT activates NK cells, it simultaneously upregulates TIM-3 as a negative feedback mechanism. While TIM-3 regulation has been extensively studied in T cells (via TCF-1 and BLIMP1) [16, 17], its transcriptional control in NK cells remains largely unexplored. Our study identifies MYB as an NK cell-intrinsic transcriptional repressor of TIM-3, a distinct mechanism not previously described. This differs from radiation-induced PD-L1 upregulation, which occurs on tumor cells via cGAS-STING signaling and suppresses T cell activity [32]. TIM-3 upregulation in NK cells occurs through MYB downregulation, representing an intrinsic negative feedback loop that limits NK cell activation, supporting combination therapy with TIM-3 blockade to complement existing PD-1/PD-L1-targeted strategies.

RT triggers anti-tumor immune responses through multiple mechanisms. Among affected immune cells, NK cells exhibit significantly enhanced functionality, contributing to both local and systemic anti-tumor responses [33–35]. RT promotes NK cell activation by inducing immunogenic cell death and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) release [36–38], upregulating NKG2D ligands on tumor cells [39], and modulating the TME through cytokine regulation [40]. In our study, RT promoted NK cell activation, evidenced by increased effector cytokines (GZMB, PRF, IFN-γ, TNF-α) and activation markers (NKG2D, CD69).

Radiation effects on NK cells vary with dose and frequency. Low-dose RT may induce stronger immunogenic effects, while higher doses can trigger inhibitory checkpoints. TIM-3, initially identified on exhausted T cells, has emerged as a critical regulator of NK cell function [41]. Elevated TIM-3 expression is associated with NK cell dysfunction, characterized by reduced cytotoxicity and impaired cytokine production [42]. TIM-3 ligands such as galectin-9, overexpressed in tumors, suppress NK cell activity by inducing apoptosis and inhibiting IFN-γ secretion [19]. In this study, we confirmed that TIM-3 suppresses NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity and revealed its role in radiation-induced NK cell activation. In the subcutaneous syngeneic tumor model, combining RT with anti-TIM-3 antibody enhanced anti-tumor responses, increased NK and CD8+ T cell infiltration, and suppressed tumor progression. This effect was partially attenuated by NK cell depletion, suggesting that anti-TIM-3 efficacy partially depends on NK cell activity.

Prior studies have shown that RT upregulates multiple checkpoints, including PD-L1 on tumor cells and PD-1/TIGIT on T cells, contributing to immune resistance [32, 43–45]. These findings support combining RT with checkpoint blockade. However, radiation-induced checkpoint expression in NK cells remains less defined. Our study identifies TIM-3 as a key negative feedback regulator in NK cells following radiation, suggesting it may represent a dominant checkpoint mechanism in this lymphocyte subset. Whether TIM-3 acts independently or cooperates with other checkpoints warrants further investigation.

The mechanisms underlying radiation-induced TIM-3 upregulation in NK cells were unclear. Using integrated bioinformatic screening and RNA-seq, we identified MYB as a key regulator. Focusing on MYB carries two translational implications. First, MYB expression could serve as a predictive biomarker for response to RT plus TIM-3 blockade. Given that MYB downregulation leads to TIM-3 upregulation, patients with low MYB expression may benefit more from adding anti-TIM-3 therapy. Second, although MYB itself is not readily druggable, understanding how radiation downregulates MYB may reveal druggable upstream pathways. In cancer, MYB functions as an oncogene in hematological malignancies and solid tumors [46–48]. We validated that radiation downregulates MYB in NK cells at both transcriptional and protein levels. Dual-luciferase and ChIP assays confirmed that MYB directly binds the TIM-3 promoter and suppresses its transcription. MYB overexpression enhanced NK cell cytotoxicity and anti-tumor efficacy following radiation. These findings indicate that radiation reduces MYB expression, alleviating TIM-3 repression and leading to its upregulation. The upstream mechanisms of radiation-induced MYB downregulation remain to be elucidated. Based on known radiation biology, several pathways may be involved, including DNA damage response signaling, oxidative stress, and activation of inflammatory cascades such as NF-κB, which have all been implicated in regulating transcription factor expression and activity. Future studies using pathway-specific inhibitors or genetic approaches will be necessary to dissect these mechanisms. While the NK-92 cell line is a well-established model, it may not fully recapitulate the biology of primary human NK cells. To address this limitation, we confirmed our key findings in primary human NK cells (derived from PBMCs), where radiation concurrently activated NK cells while significantly upregulating TIM-3 and downregulating MYB. These results indicate that the MYB-TIM-3 regulatory axis is conserved in primary human NK cells and support the physiological relevance of our conclusions.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although we used paired pre- and post-RT clinical samples from the same patients, which minimizes inter-patient heterogeneity and controls for confounding variables related to individual patient characteristics, the sample size was relatively limited. Additionally, potential confounding factors such as differences in tumor stage, patient age, and sex still exist, and the limited sample size may not fully capture the diversity of these clinical features. Second, due to transfection efficiency constraints, we did not perform MYB overexpression combined with TIM-3 knockdown to observe NK cell activation; future studies employing more efficient transfection methods are warranted to definitively establish the causal hierarchy of the MYB-TIM-3 axis. Third, the specific mechanism by which radiation downregulates MYB requires further investigation, and elucidating this upstream regulatory network could identify druggable pathways. Fourth, the establishment of conditional gene knockout mice targeting TIM-3 specifically in NK cells would improve the reliability and translational relevance of these conclusions. Fifth, whether MYB regulates other immune checkpoints in NK cells remains to be determined; genome-wide studies such as ChIP-seq would help delineate the full spectrum of MYB target genes.

Conclusions

In summary, our study demonstrates that RT activates NK cells, as evidenced by upregulated activation markers and increased effector cytokine production. However, this activation is accompanied by concurrent upregulation of the inhibitory checkpoint TIM-3, which may limit the full magnitude of NK cell responses. Mechanistically, we identified the transcription factor MYB as a direct repressor of TIM-3 transcription. Radiation-induced downregulation of MYB relieves this repression, leading to TIM-3 upregulation and subsequent attenuation of NK cell effector functions. Overexpression of MYB counteracts this negative feedback by suppressing TIM-3, thereby enhancing NK cell cytotoxicity and potentiating the anti-tumor efficacy of RT (Fig. 9). These findings reveal a MYB-TIM-3 regulatory axis that modulates NK cell responses to radiation and provide a rationale for combining RT with TIM-3 blockade as a therapeutic strategy in CRC.
