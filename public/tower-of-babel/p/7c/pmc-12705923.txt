Sperm selection for assisted reproductive technology: do we have a winner?
Authors: Youjiang Li, Hao Wang, Xue Sun, Qiaodan Li, Jian Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Assisted reproductive technology (ART) has emerged as a pivotal solution for human infertility, with intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) being widely adopted. The quality of sperm is critical to the outcomes of ART, and the selection of optimal sperm is essential for enhancing embryo quality. In recent years, researchers have developed various advanced sperm selection methods that simulate the natural sperm selection process occurring in the female reproductive tract. Although many studies have been conducted to identify the selection technique, many doubts and disagreements still remain. In this review, we will examine the characteristics, advantages, and limitations of both traditional and advanced sperm selection methods, evaluate their impact on sperm quality and functional parameters, as well as ART outcomes for infertile couples, and provide evidence-based recommendations for embryologists to optimize sperm selection strategies.

Introduction

The global decline in fertility has emerged as a critical public health challenge. According to the World Health Organization, 8% to 12% of reproductive-aged couples are affected by infertility, with male factors contributing to 50% of these cases, including gonadal dysfunction, erectile disorders, and abnormal sperm quality [1–3]. Over the past 45 years (1973–2018), global sperm concentration has declined by 60.3%, decreasing at an annual rate of 0.87 million/mL [4]. Assisted reproductive technology (ART), which includes in vitro fertilization (IVF) and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), represents a key therapeutic strategy for the management of moderate to severe male infertility. While advancements in ART have significantly improved clinical success rates, recent data indicate a plateau in pregnancy and live birth outcomes, underscoring unresolved challenges [5].

Sperm quality defects, particularly impaired DNA integrity, are central to these limitations. Such defects not only reduce fertilization rates and compromise embryonic developmental potential but also increase the risk of epigenetic abnormalities in offspring [6, 7]. Mounting evidence underscores that sperm DNA fragmentation (SDF) directly correlates with diminished ART success [8]. In natural conception, rigorous physiological selection ensures that only one in 10,000 sperm reaches the oocyte [9]. However, in ART (especially ICSI), bypassing this natural selection increases the likelihood of using abnormal sperm, thereby elevating the risks of embryonic developmental anomalies [10]. Therefore, selecting sperm with optimal quality and fertilization capacity is paramount to improving ART outcomes.

Traditional sperm selection techniques, such as the swim-up method and density gradient centrifugation (DGC), rely on motility and sedimentation differences to enrich populations of motile and morphologically normal sperm. However, these methods exhibit limited efficacy in isolating immotile sperm or identifying those with intact DNA integrity [11]. In recent years, novel selection technologies based on hyaluronic acid binding, ultrafine structures, and microfluidic chips have developed rapidly. These technologies aim to precisely identify sperm with high fertilization potential and intact genetic material by simulating the selection mechanisms of the female reproductive tract, thus providing more efficient technical support for assisted reproduction [12, 13].

This review systematically evaluates the principles and clinical applications of current sperm selection strategies in assisted reproductive technology (ART). By comparing the strengths and limitations of both traditional and innovative approaches, we aim to clarify their applicability across diverse clinical scenarios. Furthermore, we provide evidence-based guidance for optimizing sperm selection protocols to ultimately enhance the success rates of assisted reproductive technologies.

Eligibility criteria

Articles that met the criteria specified in the PICOS table (Table 1) were included in this review. The primary inclusion criteria were (i) studies conducted on humans; (ii) articles investigating advanced sperm selection techniques; (iii) studies comparing advanced and conventional sperm selection methods; and (iv) articles assessing the potential impact of advanced sperm selection techniques on sperm function and assisted reproductive outcomes. The main exclusion criteria were (i) studies not conducted on humans and (ii) studies that did not compare advanced sperm selection methods with conventional techniques.

Study selection procedure

The study selection followed the flowchart illustrated in Fig. 1. Eligibility was evaluated against the inclusion criteria, with two researchers (H.W. and W.S.) independently reviewing all articles. Disagreements were resolved by a third researcher (Y.L.). At the initial screening stage, studies failing to meet the inclusion criteria or not published in English were excluded. Subsequently, titles and abstracts were screened, and studies that did not fulfill the inclusion criteria outlined in the PICOS table were removed. Full versions of the selected papers were accessed and subjected to an in-depth content review. Article quality screening

We evaluated the quality of the literature using the NHLBI-NIH guidelines. Specifically, the quality assessment tool for case–control studies was employed (https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health-topics/study-quality-assessment-tools, accessed on January 16, 2025). This tool consists of 12 yes/no questions designed to assess potential flaws that may affect study quality. A scoring system was applied based on the responses to these questions, with a total score ranging from 0 to 12. Studies scoring less than 5 were classified as “poor quality” and were therefore excluded from this review.

Discussion

Traditional sperm selection methods

Swim-up (SU)

The swim-up method is a classic sperm selection technique based on sperm motility. In this method, liquefied semen is placed in the lower layer of a culture medium. Motile sperm actively swim against gravity into the upper layer, thereby separating from immotile sperm, cellular debris, and other impurities. This method has several advantages: it is simple to perform, requires no complex equipment, and causes minimal damage to sperm. Since it relies on the sperm’s own motility for selection, it helps preserve their viability and functionality to the greatest extent [14].

Although the swim-up method can retrieve high-quality sperm, its recovery rate is generally low. It is not suitable for samples with a low total count of progressively motile sperm, highly viscous semen, or non-liquefying semen. Despite these limitations, for samples with normal semen quality, the swim-up method remains one of the primary choices for sperm selection.

Density gradient centrifugation (DGC)

The density gradient centrifugation (DGC) method is currently the most commonly used sperm selection technique in ART. Its principle is based on the differences in sedimentation properties of sperm with varying quality and motility in a specific density gradient medium. Density gradient media are typically solutions with discontinuous density variations, with common examples including Percoll and PureSperm [58]. These media exhibit excellent biocompatibility, ensuring that they do not cause toxic damage to sperm while providing a relatively stable physical environment [18]. During the DGC process, centrifugation generates a force that allows morphologically normal and highly motile sperm to overcome the resistance of the solution and reach the bottom of the centrifuge tube. In contrast, immotile sperm, morphologically abnormal sperm, non-germ cells, and cellular debris remain trapped within different layers of the discontinuous density gradient [19, 20].

Compared to the SU, DGC may have potential negative effects on normal sperm. Studies have shown that DGC-treated samples exhibit significantly higher levels of oxidative stress, increased sperm hyperactivation, and enhanced tyrosine phosphorylation, which may be associated with excessive activation [59–61]. In terms of DNA damage elimination, both techniques are effective in removing sperm with double-strand DNA breaks and severely damaged sperm [62].

Further research suggests that sperm obtained through DGC have a higher proportion of head vacuoles, indicating potential differences in membrane structure. Additionally, sperm selected via SU demonstrate better progressive motility, though their recovery rate is lower compared to DGC [59, 63]. Despite these differences in post-selection sperm parameters, neither method has demonstrated a significant advantage in key reproductive outcomes. Several studies have reported no statistically significant differences between the two methods in fertilization rate, high-quality embryo rate, blastocyst formation rate, and live birth rate in IVF/ICSI cycles [15–17].

A Cochrane review based on four randomized controlled trials (RCTs) further supports these findings. It concluded that in intrauterine insemination (IUI) cycles, SU and DGC resulted in comparable clinical pregnancy rates (22% vs. 24%), with insufficient evidence to recommend one method over the other [16].

In recent years, researchers have developed several new sperm selection techniques with a focus on preserving sperm DNA integrity and minimizing the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). These advancements aim to improve the effectiveness of ART and enhance treatment outcomes for male infertility.

Selection of immotile sperm

Hypo-osmotic swelling test (HOST)

The hypo-osmotic swelling test (HOST) is a technique used to select viable sperm based on the integrity of the sperm cell membrane. This method involves placing a semen sample in a hypo-osmotic solution. Viable sperm with intact membranes undergo cytoplasmic swelling due to osmotic pressure and exhibit characteristic tail curling under a microscope. In contrast, dead sperm do not show this phenomenon [21]. Initially, HOST was developed as a diagnostic test to distinguish whether immotile sperm were still viable. Compared to chemical staining techniques, HOST does not require staining and causes minimal damage to sperm. Therefore, it is commonly used for selecting viable sperm in ICSI procedures.

The functional integrity of the sperm membrane is crucial for reproduction, as sperm must withstand osmotic pressure changes from the epididymal tail (a hypo-osmotic environment) to the female reproductive tract (a nearly isotonic environment) [64, 65]. Casper [23] et al. reported that sperm selected using HOST resulted in an increase in fertilization rates from 26 to 43% and cleavage rates from 23 to 39%, compared to randomly selected sperm. Additionally, a study by Charehjooy [24] et al. indicated that the application of HOST improved embryo developmental potential, implantation rates, and pregnancy rates.

However, it is worth noting that the characteristic tail bending observed after HOST treatment may increase the difficulty of sperm aspiration into the microinjection pipette [22]. Despite this challenge, HOST remains widely used for selecting potentially fertilizable viable sperm, particularly in cases involving testicular or epididymal sperm extraction, testicular sperm aspiration, or severe asthenozoospermia.

Laser-assisted immotile sperm selection (LAISS)

Laser-assisted immotile sperm selection (LAISS) is a technique that identifies viable sperm based on the biomechanical properties of the sperm tail. Before ICSI, under high-magnification microscopy, morphologically normal immotile sperm are selected, and a precise laser pulse is applied to the tip of the sperm tail. If the sperm is viable, the tail responds immediately with characteristic curling or angular bending. This reaction is believed to be caused by laser-induced medium influx into the sperm tail, although the exact molecular mechanism remains unclear (e.g., changes in membrane permeability or cytoskeletal response) [15]. Since the laser is applied far from the sperm head, where DNA is stored, this method theoretically avoids genetic material damage.

Studies have shown that LAISS offers significant advantages in specific scenarios. For patients with complete immotile spermatozoa or severe asthenozoospermia, this technique effectively identifies viable sperm with fertilization potential. Compared to randomly selected sperm, LAISS-selected sperm achieve higher fertilization and high-quality embryo rates in ICSI [22, 26]. In 2017, a case report demonstrated successful pregnancy following ICSI using cryopreserved, immotile but viable sperm selected via LAISS, providing new insights into the handling of thawed sperm [27].

However, some studies suggest that while LAISS improves oocyte survival and fertilization rates in ICSI, it does not significantly enhance blastocyst utilization rates or sustained pregnancy rates compared to traditional methods [25]. Further large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled clinical trials are needed to accurately assess the clinical value and future potential of this technique in assisted reproduction.

Selection techniques based on microscopic sperm morphology

Motile sperm organelle morphology examination (MSOME)

Motile sperm organelle morphology examination (MSOME) was first introduced by Bartoov et al. in 2002. It is an innovative technique that combines optical microscopy with digital magnification, enabling sperm observation at magnifications exceeding 6000 × to assess sperm ultrastructure [66]. The evaluation criteria include the acrosome, post-acrosomal region, nucleus, neck, tail, and mitochondria. Among these, the sperm nucleus is the most critical ultrastructural component influencing ICSI outcomes [67]. The application of MSOME in ICSI led to the development of intracytoplasmic morphologically selected sperm injection (IMSI), which allows for the selection of the highest-quality sperm for microinjection [68].

Studies have shown that a higher proportion of morphologically normal sperm is positively correlated with fertilization rates in IVF and ICSI, while its relationship with clinical pregnancy rates remains inconsistent [69, 70]. The effectiveness of IMSI in specific patient populations has been investigated. Mangoli et al. compared pregnancy outcomes between patients with severe oligoasthenoteratozoospermia who underwent either ICSI or IMSI. The IMSI group exhibited a trend toward higher pregnancy and live birth rates, as well as improved biochemical pregnancy and implantation rates compared to the ICSI group [71]. Another study demonstrated that sperm selected via IMSI had lower levels of DNA fragmentation and reduced expression of apoptotic genes. Additionally, IMSI resulted in superior embryo kinetics, implantation rates, clinical pregnancy rates, and live birth rates compared to conventional ICSI [28].

However, IMSI requires expensive equipment with high maintenance costs and demands a higher level of technical expertise from embryologists. Although some studies have reported improved pregnancy rates with IMSI compared to standard ICSI, the supporting evidence remains insufficient. Additional research is required to define its therapeutic efficacy and safety profile. As a result, IMSI is not currently recommended for routine use in embryology laboratories. Instead, it may be beneficial for couples with multiple implantation failures, patients with severe male-factor infertility, and older couples [29].

Polarized light microscopy

The sperm selection method based on polarized light microscope relies on the intrinsic birefringence properties of the nucleus in cytoplasmically mature sperm [72]. In ICSI, polarized light microscope allows for the identification of sperm heads with highly condensed chromatin and intact acrosomes, which exhibit birefringence [73]. The birefringence observed in the post-acrosomal region is closely associated with acrosome reactivity and DNA integrity. Additionally, birefringence patterns correlate with sperm motility and normal morphology. Sperm with abnormal morphology or poor motility often exhibit atypical birefringence patterns, with morphology showing a stronger association with birefringence characteristics [74].

Studies have shown that during ICSI, selecting sperm with birefringence under a polarized light microscope leads to a significantly higher proportion of zygotes graded as Z1 and Z2 compared to the non-selected group. In patients with severe asthenozoospermia, sperm selected using a polarized light microscope results in better embryo development and improved clinical outcomes [30, 75]. The birefringence technique can be utilized to assess the acrosome reaction status of spermatozoa. In mature spermatozoa, the entire head typically exhibits birefringence, whereas in those that have undergone the acrosome reaction, the birefringence pattern is altered due to the release of enzymes from the acrosome, resulting in the retention of birefringence solely in the post-acrosomal region [73]. Gianaroli et al. used birefringence patterns under a polarized light microscope to select sperm that had undergone the acrosome reaction versus those that had not. Their results revealed no significant variation in fertilization rates or embryo developmental outcomes between the two groups. However, the implantation rate (39% vs. 8.6%, P = 0.006) and clinical pregnancy rate (54.5% vs. 14%, P = 0.002) were significantly higher when acrosome-reacted sperm were used for injection [31].

Despite these promising findings, further research is needed to validate this approach. Additionally, evaluating sperm head birefringence patterns is based on morphological assessment, requiring skilled embryologists to accurately perform this technique.

Sperm selection techniques based on functional properties

Hyaluronic acid binding test

Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a fatty acid commonly found in the extracellular matrix of cumulus cells. Mature sperm express HA-binding glycoproteins on their membranes, which allow them to bind and digest HA, making it a useful tool for selecting and isolating mature sperm for ART [16]. The expression of HA receptors serves as a biomarker of sperm maturity, and some studies have evaluated its application in ICSI.

Research has shown that sperm selected via the HA-binding test exhibit significantly higher rates of normal morphology, nuclear protein maturation, and lower DNA fragmentation rates [76]. In cases of severe teratozoospermia, HA-selected sperm have been associated with significantly improved fertilization rates and embryo quality in ICSI cycles [77]. However, other studies have found no significant differences between HA-selected sperm and conventional selection methods in terms of clinical pregnancy rate, live birth rate, miscarriage rate, or embryo quality. Further research is necessary to determine whether HA-based sperm selection can be recommended for routine clinical use [32–35].

Magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS)

Magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) is a technique that uses magnetic beads coated with annexin-V to distinguish between apoptotic and non-apoptotic sperm. Phosphatidylserine (PS), a negatively charged phospholipid normally located in the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane in somatic cells and sperm, becomes externalized to the outer membrane in the early stages of apoptosis, where it binds to annexin-V [78, 79]. Since the annexin-V method does not remove leukocytes or immature sperm, MACS is often used in combination with DGC.

The effectiveness of MACS in ART remains controversial. Some studies suggest that MACS enhances sperm viability and cryosurvival rates after freezing [39]. Additionally, in cases of unexplained infertility, MACS has been shown to significantly improve pregnancy and live birth rates in patients with high SDF (pregnancy rate, 60.7% vs. 51.5%; live birth rate, 47.4% vs. 31.2%) [40, 41]. In ICSI cycles for patients with high SDF, the combination of DGC and MACS (DGC-MACS) has been found to effectively select morphologically normal sperm with greater DNA integrity, leading to improved implantation rates and lower miscarriage rates [36–38].

However, other studies have reported no statistically significant differences in key clinical outcomes, such as pregnancy rate or live birth rate, between MACS and conventional sperm selection methods [42–44]. A recently published systematic review also concluded that the clinical utility of MACS remains uncertain, and further research is needed to identify the specific patient populations that may benefit from this technique [80].

Zeta potential

Mature sperm membranes possess a negative surface charge, with an electric potential ranging from −16 to −20 mV. This charge is generated by sialic acid glycoproteins during spermatogenesis and epididymal maturation, making it a potential marker for sperm maturity [81]. The zeta-potential technique relies on the electrostatic attraction between negatively charged sperm and positively charged plastic centrifuge tubes. This simple and cost-effective method allows for the rapid selection of mature sperm with intact DNA and normal morphology [45].

Studies have indicated that the zeta-potential method improves embryo quality and pregnancy outcomes compared to traditional DGC [48, 49]. A double-blind randomized controlled trial reported similar findings and also observed that sperm selected using the combination of DGC and the zeta-potential method had a higher XY/XX sex ratio compared to sperm selected using DGC alone [47].

However, the major drawback of this method is its low sperm recovery rate, making it unsuitable for patients with severe oligozoospermia [46]. Despite this limitation, the zeta-potential method is advantageous due to its simplicity and low cost. The selected sperm exhibit high DNA integrity, normal morphology, and enhanced motility. However, further research is required to determine whether this method influences the sex ratio of newborns.

Electrophoresis-based sperm selection

Electrophoretic sperm selection is a novel separation method based on the negative surface charge carried by the membranes of mature sperm. This technique applies an electric field to isolate functionally competent sperm from immature germ cells, dysfunctional sperm, and leukocytes. The separation mechanism is influenced by differences in sperm surface charge density and cell volume.

Studies have demonstrated that electrophoretic sperm selection offers significant advantages in clinical scenarios such as severe oligoasthenozoospermia and testicular sperm extraction. Compared to the DGC method, sperm isolated via electrophoresis exhibit superior motility parameters and normal morphology. Additionally, this technique effectively reduces reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and sperm DNA fragmentation index (DFI) [48], thereby improving the rates of fertilization, blastocyst formation, and embryo implantation in ART procedures [50, 51].

Regarding its impact on sex ratio, unlike the zeta-potential method, which may influence the X/Y sperm proportion, Ainsworth [82] et al. confirmed through molecular analysis that electrophoretic sorting maintains a normal X/Y sperm ratio without statistically significant deviations. However, some studies have raised concerns about potential ultrastructural damage to sperm, reduced mitochondrial membrane potential, and the long-term genetic safety of sperm exposed to electric fields and solution environments [21]. Further large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are required to validate their clinical safety and efficacy.

Microfluidic sperm sorting

Microfluidic sperm sorting is an emerging sperm selection technology that utilizes microfluidic devices to precisely control fluid dynamics within microchannels, mimicking the physiological environment of sperm migration in the female reproductive tract [83–85]. This technique enables highly selective sperm separation, efficiently removing dead sperm, abnormal sperm, and other impurities while enhancing sperm motility and DNA integrity [52, 53, 86].

In a clinical study comparing sperm obtained via microfluidic selection and direct swim-up for ICSI, sperm selected using the microfluidic method resulted in higher rates of high-quality embryos, implantation, and clinical pregnancy compared to the direct swim-up method [54]. Another study involving 181 infertile couples found that the microfluidic method yielded a higher clinical pregnancy rate than DGC only in a specific subgroup where the female partner was over 35 years old and the total motile sperm count ranged between 1 and 5 million, whereas in other populations, no significant differences were observed between the two methods in terms of high-quality embryo rates and clinical pregnancy rates [55].

Currently, most studies evaluating the impact of microfluidic technology on ART outcomes have reported no significant differences in fertilization rates, clinical pregnancy rates, or live birth rates compared to conventional sperm selection methods [55–57]. Additionally, the fabrication of microfluidic devices requires high-precision manufacturing techniques and specialized equipment, leading to increased costs. Given these considerations, further research is warranted to assess the potential benefits of this promising technology.

Limitations

This systematic review is subject to some limitations. The absence of manual reference searching may have led to the omission of relevant studies not indexed in the selected databases. Conference proceedings and gray literature were not included in the present review, which may represent a potential source of publication bias. Moreover, the possibility of publication bias should be acknowledged, as studies with negative outcomes may be underrepresented. To better elucidate the effects in specific subpopulations of infertile patients, future research with standardized inclusion criteria and larger sample sizes is warranted.

Conclusion

Sperm selection techniques play a crucial role in improving embryo quality and clinical outcomes in ART. Traditional methods such as DGC and swim-up remain the mainstream choices in clinical practice due to their stability and broad applicability. However, their efficacy in selecting sperm from patients with severe oligoasthenozoospermia or high DNA damage is limited. Emerging technologies, which select sperm based on functional, microscopic, and biomechanical properties, have demonstrated significant improvements in sperm DNA integrity and embryonic developmental potential. Nevertheless, most studies have focused on embryological outcomes rather than clinical endpoints, necessitating further high-quality evidence to validate their effectiveness in improving live birth rates. Future research should prioritize large-scale, multicenter RCTs to define the appropriate patient populations for each technology and evaluate their cost-effectiveness. Additionally, the integration of artificial intelligence and single-cell omics into sperm selection may pave the way for precision and personalized ART, offering renewed hope to infertile couples.
