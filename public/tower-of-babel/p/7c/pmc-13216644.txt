Retrospective analysis of clinical outcomes and risk factors in hematopoietic cell transplantation for relapsed or refractory mantle cell lymphoma in the post-ibrutinib era
Authors: Satoshi Yamasaki, Yutaka Shimazu, Yukiko Misaki, Makoto Onizuka, Masashi Sawa, Yoshinobu Kanda, Takahiro Fukuda, Chizuko Hashimoto, Makoto Yoshimitsu, Junya Kanda, Yoshiko Atsuta, Shinichi Kako
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIbrutinib, a Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor, has improved outcomes for patients with relapsed or refractory mantle cell lymphoma, yet prognosis remains inadequate for many. The choice between high-dose therapy with autologous hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT) and allogeneic HCT remains a topic of debate in this patient population. This Japanese retrospective study (2017–2023) analyzed 155 patients with relapsed or refractory mantle cell lymphoma receiving autologous (n = 105) or allogeneic HCT (n = 50). Autologous and allogeneic HCT showed comparable efficacy, with similar median OS (28 vs. 27 months). However, outcome predictors differed significantly. Autologous HCT: age > 60 years predicted worse OS (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.73) and allogeneic HCT: early progression surrogate based on diagnosis-to-HCT interval ≤ 24 months and relapsed or refractory status at HCT (HR = 4.10) and non-complete remission (HR = 3.53) correlated with poorer outcomes. Ibrutinib integration demonstrated critical benefits: post-autologous relapse patients receiving ibrutinib had superior 2-year OS (57% vs. 25%, P = 0.035), and no transplant-related mortality occurred in allogeneic HCT patients on ibrutinib (n = 10). This study confirms HCT use in the ibrutinib era, highlighting age, disease kinetics, and targeted therapy as key prognostic factors, and supports risk-adapted strategies combining HCT with novel agents like ibrutinib to optimize outcomes for high-risk mantle cell lymphoma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47347-3.Keywords: Mantle cell lymphoma, Autologous hematopoietic cell transplantation, Allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation, Relapsed or refractory, IbrutinibSubject terms: Cancer, Diseases, Medical research, Oncology

Introduction

Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is an aggressive B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma defined by the t(11;14) translocation causing cyclin D1 overexpression1. Over the past two decades, population-based analyses have shown that overall survival (OS) in MCL has improved, coinciding with the introduction of rituximab, novel therapeutic agents, and increased use of hematopoietic cell transplantation2,3. Despite therapeutic advances, including Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as ibrutinib (approved in Japan in 2016) and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T therapy, MCL remains characterized by high relapse rates and treatment resistance4. The clinical heterogeneity of MCL complicates standard treatment approaches, driving research toward genetic/epigenetic profiling and tumor microenvironment interactions5,6. Novel agents like ibrutinib, which irreversibly inhibits Bruton tyrosine kinase to block B-cell survival signals, have improved outcomes but intensified the need to define molecular responses and clinical benchmarks7.

Treatment selection relies on risk stratification, ranging from observation for indolent cases to intensive chemoimmunotherapy with hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT) for fit patients8. Although HCT remains the cornerstone of curative-intent therapy, its role must now be reevaluated in the context of ibrutinib and emerging modalities9. Autologous HCT (auto-HCT) offers lower treatment-related mortality (TRM) and lacks graft-versus-lymphoma effects, whereas allogeneic HCT (allo-HCT) provides durable remission potential at the cost of higher TRM and graft-versus-host disease risk8. Key risk factors include disease status, age, comorbidities, and molecular markers, such as TP53 mutations8.

Recent paradigm shifts have challenged traditional HCT sequencing. The TRIANGLE trial demonstrated that ibrutinib plus chemotherapy without auto-HCT achieved superior failure-free survival (88% at 3 years) versus transplant-containing regimens without ibrutinib10. This underscores the urgency to redefine the role of HCT, particularly for relapsed/refractory (R/R) disease post-ibrutinib, where real-world data show a median OS of just 5.6 months9.

This study addresses critical gaps in our current understanding by analyzing HCT outcomes in the post-ibrutinib era, identifying risk factors, and evaluating HCT efficacy amid an evolving therapeutic landscape.

Results

Patient characteristics

Patient- and disease-related variables in the R/R MCL patients are shown in Table 1. t(11;14) was detected in 98/106 fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)-tested cases (92.5%). TP53 aberrations were confirmed in 4/4 tested cases (100%), but the status was unknown for 151 patients (97.4%) because no testing was performed. The study analyzed 155 patients with R/R MCL, divided into three groups: (1) those receiving auto-HCT (n = 105), (2) those receiving allo-HCT without prior auto-HCT (n = 11), and (3) those receiving allo-HCT after relapse following auto-HCT (n = 39). Key differences between the groups included the time from diagnosis to HCT, which was longer in the allo-HCT group compared with that in the auto-HCT group, and the number of patients with early progression surrogate (EPS) was lower in the allogeneic group, especially for those receiving allo-HCT after a relapse post-auto-HCT.

Conditioning regimens for HCT

In the auto-HCT group, conditioning regimens included ranimustine + carboplatin + etoposide + cyclophosphamide (MCEC n = 9), ranimustine + etoposide + cytarabine + melphalan (MEAM n = 64), and melphalan + cyclophosphamide + etoposide + dexamethasone (LEED, n = 32). For the allo-HCT group, regimens included MAC (total body irradiation [TBI] + cyclophosphamide, n = 2; intravenous busulfan (ivBU) + fludarabine + melphalan, n = 2; ivBU + fludarabine, n = 1) and reduced-intensity conditioning (RIC) with fludarabine + melphalan ± TBI (n = 19/13), fludarabine + TBI (n = 7), fludarabine + melphalan + ivBU (n = 3), and fludarabine + cyclophosphamide ± TBI (n = 1/2), as defined by the Center for International Blood and Marrow Transplant Research.

All patients in the auto-HCT group received peripheral blood stem cells (PBSCs), whereas the allo-HCT group received bone marrow (n = 15), PBSCs (n = 24), or cord blood stem cells (n = 10). Transplant-related characteristics in the allo-HCT group are shown in Supplementary Table 1. Donors in the allo-HCT group included 9 human leukemia antigen (HLA)-identical siblings, 9 HLA-matched unrelated volunteers, 10 HLA-mismatched related families, and 22 HLA-mismatched unrelated volunteers. In this cohort, 40 patients (26%) received ibrutinib before HCT and 36 patients (23%) received ibrutinib as maintenance treatment. Among the auto-HCT recipients, 21 patients (20%) received post–auto-HCT rituximab maintenance, and a subset also received ibrutinib either as pre-transplant salvage or as post-transplant maintenance; the numbers and proportions for each category are detailed in Table 1. Ibrutinib was also used in a limited number of patients before or after allo-HCT, reflecting its role as a bridging or post-relapse therapy in this setting, although the small sample size precluded robust outcome analyses by line and timing of ibrutinib exposure.

Outcomes of autologous HCT

Engraftment and toxicity outcomes were as follows: neutrophil engraftment: median 11 days (range: 2–47), TRM: 6/105 (5.7%, 95% CI: 2.1%–11.9%), and disease-related deaths: 28/105 (26.7%). Survival outcomes were age impact: patients > 60 years had inferior 2-year OS vs. those aged ≤ 60 years (58.8% [95% CI: 44.0%–70.9%] vs. 78.8% [62.5%–88.6%], P = 0.017; Fig. 1A), relapse status: non-relapsed patients had superior 2-year OS vs. relapsed patients (90.5% [76.2%–96.4%] vs. 43.6% [28.8%–57.4%], P < 0.001; Fig. 1B), and post-relapse ibrutinib: improved 2-year OS in recipients (57.0% [35.9%–73.5%] vs. 25.0% for non-recipients [8.30%–46.1%], P = 0.035; Fig. 1C). Multivariate analysis indicated TRM risk was male sex (hazard ratio [HR] = 3.42, P < 0.001), disease-free survival (DFS) risk was age > 60 years (HR = 2.64, P = 0.001), and OS risk was age > 60 years (HR = 2.73, P = 0.009).

Outcomes of allogeneic HCT

Engraftment and toxicity outcomes were as follows. Neutrophil engraftment: median 17 days (range: 10–32), TRM: 6/50 (12.0%, 95% CI: 4.5%–24.3%), disease-related deaths: 9/50 (18.0%), and grade II–IV acute graft-versus-host disease (GVHD, 100-day): 66.7% (95% CI: 37.7–92.2) in the no prior auto-HCT group and 39.7% (95% CI: 26.2–57.1) in the prior auto-HCT group (Supplementary Table 2). Survival outcomes were EPS impact: inferior 2-year OS in EPS + vs. EPS − (26.7% [8.26%–49.6%] vs. 74.5% [54.9%–86.5%], P < 0.001; Fig. 2A) and ibrutinib post-HCT: zero TRM in recipients (n = 10, including 8 prior auto-HCT) vs. 15.0% in non-recipients (n = 40, P = 0.09; Fig. 2B). Multivariate analysis showed relapse risks were male sex (HR = 6.07, P = 0.050), EPS (HR = 6.01, P = 0.001), and non-complete remission (CR) (HR = 7.77, P = 0.007), and OS risks were EPS (HR = 4.10, P = 0.007) and non-CR (HR = 3.53, P = 0.034) (Tables 2, 3).

Comparative analysis

The detailed incidence and timing of major bacterial, fungal, and viral infections, including bacteremia, invasive aspergillosis, cytomegalovirus reactivation, and coronavirus disease 2019, are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. Notably, the rates of bacteremia were higher in the allo-HCT groups compared with those in the auto-HCT group. Proven invasive aspergillosis was observed exclusively in the allo-HCT cohorts. The full spectrum of infectious complications is detailed in Supplementary Table 3.

Given the significant baseline differences (longer diagnosis-to-HCT interval and lower frequency of EPS in the allo-HCT group), direct comparisons were limited. However, the following findings were obtained: median OS: 28 months (auto-HCT) vs. 27 months (allo-HCT), 2-year OS: 58.8%–90.5% (auto-HCT) vs. 26.7%–74.5% (allo-HCT), and TRM: 5.7% (auto-HCT) vs. 12.0% (allo-HCT).

Discussion

This nationwide retrospective analysis of 155 patients with R/R MCL who underwent HCT in Japan during the post-ibrutinib era provides several clinically relevant insights that directly inform contemporary practice.

First, age remains a decisive prognostic factor for auto-HCT: patients aged ≤ 60 years achieved a significantly higher 2-year OS compared with those > 60 years (78.8% vs. 58.8%, P = 0.017), and age > 60 years was independently associated with inferior OS (HR = 2.73, P = 0.009), even in the context of modern salvage regimens. This finding is consistent with a previous report11.

Second, the introduction of ibrutinib has fundamentally altered the post-transplant landscape. In this cohort, patients who received ibrutinib after relapse following auto-HCT had a markedly improved 2-year OS (57.0% vs. 25.0%, P = 0.035), and among allo-HCT recipients, those maintained on ibrutinib experienced no TRM, in contrast to historical TRM rates of 15%12. These results highlight the dual benefit of ibrutinib as a salvage and maintenance strategy and are aligned with recent real-world data showing a median OS of only 5.6 months after ibrutinib failure in Japanese MCL patients13, underscoring the importance of integrating HCT in the treatment sequence for high-risk or ibrutinib-refractory cases.

Third, disease kinetics at transplantation, specifically EPS and non-CR status, were the strongest predictors of poor outcomes after allo-HCT. Patients with EPS had a 2-year OS of only 26.7% compared with 74.5% for those without early progression (P < 0.001), and non-CR status further worsened the prognosis (OS HR = 3.53, P = 0.034). This finding is in line with recent international studies identifying EPS and refractory disease as critical adverse prognostic markers in the era of targeted therapies14,15.

Compared with our pre-ibrutinib-era results (median OS 48–65 months for auto-HCT or allo-HCT)11, the current median OS of 27–28 months reflects a shift toward the inclusion of more biologically aggressive, heavily pretreated, and ibrutinib-refractory cases, for whom the prognosis remains poor with non-transplant therapies alone12,13. Notably, HCT still provided durable disease control in a subset of these high-risk patients, as evidenced by improved OS among those receiving post-auto-HCT ibrutinib and by the absence of relapse among allo-HCT recipients transplanted in chemorefractory states16,17. In addition, the therapeutic landscape for MCL has rapidly evolved with the advent of CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy and bispecific antibodies, which are increasingly used in relapsed/refractory settings2–5. In this contemporary context, allo-HCT has become a rarely pursued option for MCL, which is now largely reserved for highly selected patients who either lack access to or fail to respond to novel cellular and immunotherapeutic approaches. Our findings should therefore be interpreted with the understanding that the role of allo-HCT in current practice is increasingly restricted, and that future treatment algorithms will likely position allo-HCT as a niche strategy alongside, rather than in competition with, CAR-T cell therapy and bispecific antibodies. Furthermore, recent data have shown that a shorter diagnosis-to-treatment interval is independently associated with adverse clinical features and inferior survival in patients with newly diagnosed MCL, underscoring the importance of incorporating the diagnosis-to-treatment interval into the baseline risk assessment18.

Limitations of this study include its retrospective design and incomplete molecular profiling. TP53 assessment was constrained by sparse FISH testing (n = 4), which precluded meaningful correlations with outcomes. Therefore, future studies should mandate systematic molecular profiling. The TRUMP database’s focus on transplant-specific variables also precluded a granular review of patient pathology, and future iterations should require structured immunohistochemistry/FISH reporting to improve subclassification accuracy. In addition, registry constraints limit the availability of detailed information on the timing and line of ibrutinib use. Ibrutinib was approved for frontline use in untreated MCL in Japan on February 23, 2023, and our study includes transplantation cases up to December 2023, so a small number of patients might have received ibrutinib as part of their initial therapy before HCT. However, because the TRUMP database does not capture the treatment line for ibrutinib, we were unable to identify such cases in this cohort. Because of limitations in the TRUMP database, we could not determine the precise date of disease progression or relapse prior to transplantation. In particular, the TRUMP database does not record the exact treatment line or dates of relapse in relation to ibrutinib, which prevented us from precisely quantifying post-ibrutinib relapses and from fully distinguishing second-line from third- or later-line ibrutinib use around allo-HCT. As such, our definition of EPS is based on the time from diagnosis to transplantation and disease status at transplantation, rather than the actual date of progression, which may have affected the accuracy of this surrogate endpoint. A further limitation of our surrogate definition is that patients who experience progression within 24 months of initial therapy, achieve CR with salvage treatment, and undergo transplantation after 24 months are not captured by the EPS definition, although such cases would be included under the original progression of disease within 24 months (POD24) criteria. This should be considered when interpreting our results.

Additionally, any direct comparison between auto-HCT and allo-HCT was confounded by substantial differences in patient characteristics (including a longer diagnosis-to-HCT interval, higher rates of chemorefractoriness, and more frequent use of allo-HCT after auto-HCT failure in this Japanese cohort than in Western series)12. Our study was not designed or powered to provide a definitive comparative analysis between auto-HCT and allo-HCT. Given the small number of allo-HCT recipients and the substantial baseline imbalances between the two transplant groups, propensity score–based methods (including matching or inverse probability of treatment weighting) could not adequately eliminate indication bias; therefore, we did not perform or report a formal adjusted comparison of these modalities. The study period also largely predated the widespread availability of CAR-T therapy in Japan, leaving the optimal integration of CAR-T and HCT unresolved19,20. Moreover, emerging minimal residual disease (MRD)-driven strategies are further reshaping the role of auto-HCT in contemporary MCL management. The Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG)-ACRIN EA4151 phase 3 trial used a highly sensitive next-generation sequencing-based MRD assay at first complete remission and demonstrated that autologous HCT did not improve OS or progression-free survival for patients who achieved undetectable MRD after modern rituximab-containing induction and were treated with maintenance rituximab, supporting the omission of auto-HCT in this large subgroup21. Conversely, exploratory analyses from EA4151 suggested that patients who remained MRD-positive after induction may have derived benefit from auto-HCT when this led to conversion to undetectable MRD, with markedly superior survival observed in those achieving post-transplant MRD negativity. Taken together with our data, these findings indicate that the role of auto-HCT in MCL is likely to become increasingly restricted to biologically high-risk, MRD-positive patients, whereas MRD-negative patients in first remission may increasingly be managed with MRD-adapted, non-transplant strategies.

Future directions should prioritize: (1) biomarker-driven bridging strategies, such as ibrutinib-venetoclax combinations, which have shown high response rates in TP53-mutated MCL, to optimize disease control pre-HCT22; (2) prospective trials of post-HCT maintenance with Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors (ibrutinib or newer agents) to reduce relapse and TRM, and to clarify their immunomodulatory effects on GVHD17; and (3) real-world comparative studies of CAR-T versus HCT in molecularly-defined high-risk subgroups, and system-level interventions to improve access to cellular therapies in Japan.

In summary, this study reaffirms the continued role of HCT in the management of R/R MCL in the targeted-therapy era, particularly for patients with high-risk features or those who have failed ibrutinib. The integration of novel agents into transplant strategies, guided by molecular and clinical risk factors, offers the most promising path toward improved and personalized outcomes for this challenging disease.

Patients and methods

Data source

Patient information was derived from the Transplant Registry Unified Management Program (TRUMP) database, collected by the Japanese Data Center for Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation and sponsored by the Japanese Society for Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation, which contains physician-reviewed data with informed consent and yearly follow-ups23,24. The study was approved by the Data Management Committee of the Japanese Society for Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation and the Ethics Review Board of St. Mary’s Hospital (approval number: 25-0509). This observational study was conducted using data from the TRUMP database, and all participants provided informed consent. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection and analysis methods

In this study, we carefully selected patients with definite R/R MCL by excluding those who underwent planned auto-HCT during their first CR as part of their initial therapy. In our cohort, first-line (1L) treatment predominantly consisted of rituximab-containing chemoimmunotherapy, and high-dose cytarabine-based induction was frequently used in transplant-eligible patients; overall, all patients received rituximab-based regimens, and 119 patients (77%) were treated with high-dose cytarabine-containing therapy as part of the 1L treatment (Table 1). Ibrutinib exposure was captured as a categorical variable according to timing and intent: (1) pre-HCT ibrutinib use (as salvage or bridging therapy before auto-HCT or allo-HCT), (2) post–auto-HCT ibrutinib maintenance, and (3) ibrutinib use before or after allo-HCT, and these variables are summarized in Table 1 to describe the treatment context of the cohort. In this cohort, 40 patients (26%) received ibrutinib before HCT, 36 patients (23%) received ibrutinib as maintenance therapy, and 21 patients (20%) received rituximab as maintenance therapy after auto-HCT (Table 1). In particular, the TRUMP database does not record the exact treatment line or dates of relapse in relation to ibrutinib, which prevented us from precisely quantifying post-ibrutinib relapses and from fully distinguishing second-line from third-—or later-line ibrutinib use around allo-HCT. Only patients whose disease status at transplantation was relapse, partial remission (PR) after salvage therapy, refractory to prior treatments, or CR achieved after salvage therapy for R/R disease were included in the study. This distinction ensured that CR status reflected a response to salvage therapy for R/R disease, not upfront consolidation. Consequently, cases with upfront auto-HCT, short intervals from diagnosis to HCT, or limited prior therapies were excluded to avoid non-R/R cases. We acknowledge that this strict selection may have excluded some R/R cases, and we evaluated the final sample size accordingly. Detailed statistical methods are described in the supplementary methods.

The presence or absence of TP53 aberrations was determined based on available documentation in the TRUMP database. TP53 status was assessed via fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) in only 4/155 cases (2.6%), all of which were positive. For t(11;14) FISH, testing was performed in 106/155 patients (68.4%), and 49/155 (31.6%) were untested because of non-examination. When TP53 status was not recorded, cases were classified as “unknown” rather than presumed to be wild-type. Among the cohort, TP53 aberration percentages were calculated exclusively among the tested cases (n = 4), with unknowns (n = 151) excluded from the frequency calculations but reported separately. Similarly, t(11;14) positivity rates were derived only from the 106 tested cases.

In this study, we defined the EPS as transplantation within 24 months from diagnosis combined with R/R disease status at HCT. We acknowledge that EPS cannot distinguish between true POD24 and primary induction failure (PIF) with delayed transplantation. Notably, PIF is generally included in the original definition of POD24 for follicular lymphoma, whereas definitions may vary across studies for MCL25.

Statistical analysis

We identified patients with MCL aged 15 years or older who received auto-HCT or allo-HCT for R/R MCL between January 2017 and December 2023. Diagnoses were based on institutional pathological assessments using the World Health Organization classification criteria applicable at the time of diagnosis26. Central pathological review was not performed. Diagnostic elements (CD5 +, CD20 +, cyclin D1 + by immunohistochemistry, and/or t(11;14) by FISH) were extracted only if explicitly documented in the TRUMP database fields. Notably, immunohistochemistry data were inconsistently recorded across institutions, and t(11;14) FISH was the sole universally captured molecular marker. This approach aligns with real-world registry studies, but limits standardized subclassification related to variable data granularity. Exclusion criteria included any history of solid cancers or hematological malignancies before HCT, or receiving HCT as a 1L consolidation procedure. Inclusion criteria included receiving rituximab-based combination chemotherapies and having sufficient follow-up data. A total of 155 R/R MCL patients were included in this analysis. Heterogeneity in treatment timing between the auto-HCT and allo-HCT groups was adjusted by considering lines of therapy and the time from diagnosis to HCT.

The study variables included: (1) patient-related variables: age at HCT or diagnosis, sex, ECOG performance status (PS) at HCT or diagnosis, number of regimens before HCT, and HCT-specific comorbidity index (HCT-CI)27; (2) disease-related variables: disease risk at HCT (CR, PR, PIF, relapse), Ann Arbor stage, international prognostic index, number of lymph nodes, and lactate dehydrogenase elevation at diagnosis; and (3) transplantation-related variables: conditioning regimen, stem-cell source, time to neutrophil engraftment, and relapse or refractory disease after HCT. Neutrophil engraftment was defined as the time required to reach an absolute neutrophil count of ≥ 500/µL for 3 consecutive days. Primary graft failure was defined as the failure of neutrophil engraftment by day + 35. Incidences of grade II–IV acute GVHD and the presence or absence of chronic GVHD were defined as reported previously28,29. Patients were considered evaluable for chronic GVHD if they had engraftment. The percentages of patients who relapsed or were refractory to allo-HCT were determined (relapse). The survival and OS rates were calculated. GVHD-free, relapse-free survival was defined as being alive without grade III–IV acute GVHD, moderate-to-severe chronic GVHD requiring systemic immunosuppressive therapy, relapse, or death30. Chronic GVHD-free, relapse-free survival was defined as the absence of chronic GVHD requiring systemic immunosuppressive therapy, relapse, or death31. All data were censored at the date of the last reported follow-up. Analyzed outcomes included TRM (defined as death occurring while a patient is in continuous remission after HCT, encompassing all causes of non-leukemia death following the transplant procedure), OS, and DFS. OS was rigorously defined as follows: (1) start point: date of HCT (auto-HCT or allogenic HCT); (2) endpoint: death from any cause; (3) censoring: patients alive at last follow-up were censored (median follow-up: 28 months for auto-HCT, 27 months for allogenic HCT); and (4) statistical handling: Kaplan–Meier estimates for survival curves, and landmark analyses at 6, 12, and 18 months post-HCT to mitigate immortal time bias. This aligns with established methodologies in HCT outcome research32.

We determined the frequencies and descriptive statistics for patient-, disease-, and transplantation-related variables in patients who underwent auto-HCT or allo-HCT. Categorical variables were compared between the two groups using Pearson’s χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate, whereas continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. All tests were two-sided, with 95% confidence intervals calculated, and P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. To accommodate competing risks, the cumulative incidence curves for TRM and relapse or progression in the two groups were estimated and compared using Gray’s test33. The competing risk events for TRM and relapse or progression were relapse and death, respectively. The impacts of patient-, disease-, and transplantation-related variables on TRM or relapses were evaluated using the Fine and Gray model. The DFS and OS were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and two-group comparisons were performed using the log-rank test. Multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted using the Cox proportional hazard model for multivariate analysis, including patient-, disease-, and transplantation-related variables. Baseline patient variables for analysis included age at HCT (age > 60 years), sex, EPS, ECOG PS (PS > 0) at HCT, disease status at HCT (non-CR vs. CR), history of auto-HCT (for allo-HCT), and HCT-specific comorbidity index (HCT-CI ≥ 2). Because ibrutinib exposure strongly correlated with adverse disease features and heavy pretreatment status, we did not include it as an independent covariate in the multivariable models to avoid collinearity and overfitting, given the limited sample size. Instead, we described the patterns of ibrutinib use (timing and line of therapy) and their associations with outcomes descriptively, as shown in Table 1 and the Results section. However, because the TRUMP registry does not capture the exact treatment line or precise relapse dates for ibrutinib, we were unable to determine the ordinal line of therapy (second vs. third or later) and the exact timing of relapse in all cases. All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Saitama, Japan; http://www.jichi.ac.jp/saitama-sct/SaitamaHP.files/statmedEN.html)34, a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing; www.r-project.org) that extends the functionality of R Commander by adding statistical functions. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1 and R Commander version 2.9-1.
