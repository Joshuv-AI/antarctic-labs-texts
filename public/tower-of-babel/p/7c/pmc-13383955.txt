Highly potent MyoAAV4A vector reverses GSD III pathology in aged mice and enables long-term muscle disease correction in young adult mice
Authors: Kuo-An Liao, Haiqing Yi, Haoyue Zhang, William Eisner, Sarah P Young, Baodong Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe recently reported a 6-week study in 3-month-old glycogen storage disorder (GSD) IIIa mice demonstrating that a myotropic AAV vector (MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull) expressing a bacterial glycogen debranching enzyme (pullulanase) under the control of an immunotolerizing dual promoter achieved markedly higher transduction efficiency and superior glycogen clearance efficacy in cardiac and skeletal muscle compared with the AAV9-Dual-Pull vector. In the present study, we evaluated the efficacy of MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull in aged GSD IIIa mice and assessed its therapeutic durability in young adult GSD IIIa mice. Intravenous injection of the MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull (5 × 1012 vg/kg) into 13-month-old male and female GSD IIIa mice significantly reduced glycogen accumulation in the liver, heart, and skeletal muscle and reversed hepatic and muscular dysfunctions 3 months after vector administration. Moreover, treatment of 3-month-old male and female GSD IIIa mice with MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull (5 × 1012 vg/kg) resulted in sustained pullulanase expression and significant glycogen reduction in the heart and quadriceps, accompanied by improved muscle function 9 months after vector administration. Our data demonstrate that MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull effectively reverses disease phenotypes in aged GSD IIIa mice and provides durable muscle correction in young adult mice, supporting the development of a persistent and effective AAV gene therapy for GSD III.Keywords: glycogen storage disease type III, GSD III, gene therapy, pullulanase, MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull, long-term efficacy, GSD IIIa mice

Introduction

Glycogen storage disorder type III (GSD III, OMIM 232400) is a rare inherited metabolic disorder due to mutations in the AGL gene. The AGL gene encodes the glycogen debranching enzyme (GDE), and its deficiency results in impaired glycogen breakdown, leading to the accumulation of abnormally structured glycogen (limit dextrin) in multiple tissues, primarily the liver and muscles.1 GSD III has an estimated prevalence of 1:100,000, and two major subtypes of this disease have been identified based on the extent of the tissue involvement. Patients with the most common subtype, GSD IIIa (∼85%), exhibit both liver and muscle involvement, while the remaining 15% have only liver symptoms (GSD IIIb).1,2 Typically, GSD III presents in infancy or early childhood with liver disease, characterized by hepatomegaly, fasting hypoglycemia, hyperlipidemia, and elevated liver enzymes. Although signs of liver disease seem to decrease with age, some patients may develop progressive liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma.3,4,5,6 Cardiac and skeletal muscle diseases appear during childhood and slowly progress in adulthood. Most GSD IIIa patients present variable degrees of muscle weakness and exercise intolerance, and some may become wheelchair dependent due to severe skeletal muscle impairments. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is the main heart involvement in GSD IIIa, in some severe cases, patients died of cardiac arrhythmias and congestive heart failure.3,6,7,8

With no curative treatment available for patients with GDE deficiency, current patient care is limited to dietary management, including uncooked starch supplementation and high-protein/high-fat diets.2,3,9 Although nutritional interventions have been shown to reduce the incidence and severity of hypoglycemia and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy,10,11,12,13 their beneficial effects on skeletal muscles remain less well established. Among the therapeutic approaches currently under investigation, gene replacement therapy using adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors represents the most promising strategy for GSD III, as AAV vectors, particularly AAV serotype 9 (AAV9), can reliably and efficiently transduce both liver and muscle tissues. However, the large size of human GDE cDNA (4.6 kb) hinders the development of this approach due to AAV’s limited packaging capacity (∼4.7 kb).

To overcome this limitation, we previously developed an innovative AAV vector carrying a bacterial GDE (pullulanase), whose cDNA size (2.2 kb) is sufficiently small to fit in a single AAV genome.14 Intravenous injection of an AAV9 vector (AAV9-Dual-Pull) containing a codon-optimized pullulanase cDNA driven by an immunotolerizing dual promoter, consisting of a liver-specific promoter (LSP) and a ubiquitous CMV enhancer/chicken β-actin (CB) promoter, resulted in decreased cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses, widespread pullulanase expression, and significant correction of liver and muscle diseases in GSD IIIa mice.15 More recently, we further demonstrated the long-term efficacy of the AAV9-Dual-Pull vector in GSD IIIa mice.16

To further enhance muscle transduction efficiency and reduce the required therapeutic dose, we packaged the AAV-Dual-Pull vector into two novel myotropic capsids, MyoAAV4A and MyoAAV4E, which exhibit superior muscle transduction efficiency in both mice and nonhuman primates.17 Administration of the MyoAAV vectors in 3-month-old GSD IIIa mice efficiently reduced glycogen content in all major affected tissues, corrected disease phenotypes, and restored liver and muscle function 6 weeks after vector administration. Notably, the MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull vector administered at a dose of 1 × 1012 vg/kg achieved better therapeutic outcomes in skeletal muscles than the AAV9-Dual-Pull vector at a dose 1 × 1013 vg/kg.18

In this study, we evaluated the efficacy of MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull in aged GSD IIIa mice and examined its therapeutic durability in young adult GSD IIIa mice.

Results

Treatment with MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull vector in aged GSD IIIa mice resulted in adequate pullulanase expression in major affected tissues

To evaluate the transduction efficiency of the MyoAAV4A vector in aged mice, male and female GSD IIIa mice were intravenously injected with MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull (5 × 1012 vg/kg) at the age of 13 months, and major affected tissues, including liver, heart, and skeletal muscles, were harvested 3 months later (Figures 1A and 1B).

We first assessed the abundance of vector genomes in AAV-transduced tissues. In male AAV-treated mice, AAV vector genome (vg) copy numbers were moderate in the liver (8.18 ± 7.46 vg/diploid genome) and heart (3.96 ± 1.30 vg/diploid genome) but low in the quadriceps (0.14 ± 0.05 vg/diploid genome). Similar AAV copy numbers were observed in female AAV-treated mice across these tissues (liver: 8.33 ± 5.50; heart: 3.62 ± 1.34; quadriceps: 0.12 ± 0.06 vg/diploid genome) (Figure 1C). We next measured the pullulanase activity in these tissues. In both male and female AAV-treated mice, the highest pullulanase activities were observed in the heart, followed by the quadriceps. Surprisingly, despite high AAV genome copy number in the liver, pullulanase activity was only slightly increased compared with the untreated (UT) mice (p < 0.05) (Figure 1D). Consistent with the pullulanase activity results, western blot analysis revealed low-level expression of pullulanase protein in the liver and robust expression in the heart and quadriceps of both male and female AAV-treated mice (Figure S1).

MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull treatment in aged GSD IIIa mice led to significant glycogen reduction in major affected tissues

To evaluate the efficacy of MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull in reducing tissue glycogen levels, glycogen content was measured in the liver, heart, and quadriceps at 3 months following AAV injection. In comparison with UT, AAV treatment significantly decreased glycogen levels in the liver (−47% in males and −58% in females), heart (−88% in males and −86% in females), and quadriceps (−87% in males and −88% in females). No glycogen accumulation was detected in any wild-type (WT) tissues (Figure 2A). To validate the glycogen content results, periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining of glycogen was performed on these tissue sections. As shown in Figure 2B, massive purple-stained glycogen was observed in the liver, heart, and skeletal muscles (quadriceps, gastrocnemius, and diaphragm) of UT mice, whereas no glycogen was detected in any WT tissues. Treatment with MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull greatly reduced the glycogen staining in the liver and almost completely removed glycogen accumulation in the heart, quadriceps, gastrocnemius, and diaphragm.

MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull vector slowed liver fibrosis progression, corrected GSD IIIa symptoms, and improved muscle function in aged GSD IIIa mice

Progressive liver fibrosis is commonly observed in GSD III patients.2 Here, we evaluated the hepatic fibrosis by trichrome staining on liver sections from aged GSD IIIa mice. Three months after gene therapy, significant amounts of Sirius red-stained fibrotic tissues were observed in the livers of UT male and female mice, whereas fibrosis was noticeably reduced in AAV-treated livers and absent in WT livers (Figure 3A). We then employed real-time reverse-transcription PCR (RT-PCR) to examine the mRNA expression levels of three fibrosis markers in liver tissues. Compared with WT control mice, the expression levels of collagen I and collagen III were significantly elevated in the livers of both male and female UT mice, whereas smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) expression was increased only in female UT mice (Figure 3B). Compared with UT mice, AAV-treated male and female mice exhibited reduced expression of collagen I (−51% in males and −64% in females) and collagen III (−42% in males and −50% in females). However, α-SMA expression in treated female mice remained at a level comparable to that observed in UT mice (Figure 3B).

(B) Relative mRNA expression levels of α-SMA, collagen I, and collagen III were measured in the livers of UT, MyoAAV4A-treated, and WT mice. To determine whether reduced tissue glycogen levels in aged mice treated with MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull translated into improved liver and muscle function, we measured plasma alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and creatine kinase (CK) activities, as well as GSD III disease biomarker urinary Glc4,19,20 3 months after AAV injection. In both male and female mice, AAV treatment markedly decreased plasma ALT (−57% in males and −88% in females) and CK (−68% in males and −71% in females) activities and significantly reduced the urinary Glc4 levels (−82% in males and −89% in females) (Figures 4A–4C).

Muscle functional tests demonstrated overall improvements in muscle strength and exercise tolerance by the AAV treatment. In the grip strength test, AAV treatment significantly enhanced grip strength in both male and female mice compared with UT (Figure 4D). In the wire-hang test, hanging time increased in both male and female AAV-treated mice, although the improvement reached statistical significance only in female mice (Figure 4E). In the treadmill test, AAV treatment significantly increased the running distance in both male and female mice (Figure 4F).

In summary, MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull treatment in aged GSD IIIa mice effectively reduced glycogen accumulation in major affected tissues, slowed the progression of hepatic fibrosis, normalized urinary Glc4 level, and improved both liver and muscle functions.

MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull treatment in young adult GSD IIIa mice resulted in persistent pullulanase expression and long-term glycogen reduction in muscles but not in the liver

To evaluate the long-term therapeutic efficacy in young adult mice, 3-month-old GSD IIIa mice were injected intravenously with the MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull vector at a dose of 5 × 1012 vg/kg, and major affected tissues, including liver, heart, and quadriceps, were harvested 5.5 and 9 months later (Figure 5A). We first assessed AAV genome contents in MyoAAV4A-transduced tissues. In both male and female AAV-treated mice, AAV genome copy numbers were extremely low but detectable in the liver (0.09–0.12 vg/diploid genome) and quadriceps (0.02–0.03 vg/diploid genome), whereas slightly higher levels were observed in the heart (0.11–0.13 vg/diploid genome) at 5.5 months following AAV administration (Figure S2). Similar AAV genome copy numbers were detected at the 9-month time point in the liver (0.05–0.07 vg/diploid genome), heart (0.13–0.24 vg/diploid genome), and quadriceps (0.02–0.03 vg/diploid genome) of both male and female treated mice (Figure S2).

We next measured pullulanase activity in these tissues. Compared with UT controls, male AAV-treated mice displayed significantly increased pullulanase activity in the heart and modestly increased activities in the quadriceps but not in the liver at 5.5 months following AAV administration (Figure 5B). Notably, elevated pullulanase activities in heart and quadriceps were also detected at 9 months after AAV administration (Figure 5B). Female treated mice showed similar pullulanase activity profiles at both time points (Figure S3A). Consistent with these pullulanase activity results, pullulanase proteins were detectable in the heart and quadriceps, but not in the liver, of both male and female AAV-treated mice 9 months post-administration via western blot analysis (Figure S4). As expected, glycogen content was significantly reduced in the heart (−49% and −56% at 5.5 months and 9 months post-injection, respectively) and quadriceps (−57% and −53% at 5.5 months and 9 months post-injection, respectively) of male AAV-treated mice, whereas no significant difference was observed between AAV-treated and UT livers at either time points (Figure 5C). Similar results were observed in female mice, with no significant difference detected in liver glycogen content between treated and UT groups, but significant reductions observed in the heart (−65% and −41% at 5.5 and 9 months post-injection, respectively) and quadriceps (−67% and −55% at 5.5 and 9 months post-injection, respectively) (Figure S3B).

MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull treatment in young adult GSD IIIa mice demonstrated long-lasting effect on decreasing plasma muscle enzyme activity and improving muscle function

To evaluate the restoration of muscle function, we first measured the plasma CK activity in male and female mice 5.5 and 9 months after AAV administration. Compared with UT controls, AAV treatment reduced plasma CK activity by 24% in male mice and 38% in female mice at 5.5 months post-injection, and by 41% in male mice and 62% in female mice at 9 months post-injection (Figures 6A and S5A). We next conducted a treadmill test to assess muscle function and exercise tolerance. AAV-treated male mice exhibited a modest increase in treadmill running distance at both time points (UT: 10.31 ± 3.75 m and MyoAAV4A: 41.58 ± 24.19 m at 5.5 months post-injection; UT: 8.30 ± 3.47 m and MyoAAV4A: 35.03 ± 22.51 m at 9 months post-injection). AAV-treated female mice showed a slight improvement in running distance at 5.5 months post-injection (UT: 15.31 ± 2.95 m; MyoAAV4A: 26.12 ± 21.75 m), while demonstrating a significant improvement compared with UT controls at the 9-month time point (UT: 13.80 ± 2.20 m; MyoAAV4A: 68.40 ± 36.71 m) (Figures 6B and S5B).

MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull treatment in young adult GSD IIIa mice prevented the progression of liver fibrosis

To assess the long-term effects of MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull treatment on liver function, plasma ALT activity was measured in UT and AAV-treated GSD IIIa mice 5.5 and 9 months after AAV administration. Compared with UT controls, plasma ALT activity decreased by 30% in treated male mice and 60% in treated female mice at 5.5 months post-injection. However, no difference in plasma ALT activity was observed between UT and treated male mice at 9 months post-injection, while treated female mice showed only a 28% decrease compared with UT controls at this time point (Figure S6).

Despite the absence of liver glycogen clearance and functional improvement, MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull treatment markedly reduced liver fibrosis in both male and female mice compared with UT controls at 9 months after AAV administration (Figure 7A). Quantitative RT-PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis of fibrosis marker expression showed that α-SMA, collagen I, and collagen III were highly expressed in male UT mice compared with WT controls. Significant increases in collagen I and III expression were also detected in the livers of female UT mice, albeit to a lesser extent than in male UT mice (Figure 7B). Unexpectedly, no reductions in the expression levels of these fibrosis markers were observed in treated mice (Figure 7B), despite Picrosirius red staining showing less fibrotic tissues in AAV-treated livers compared with UT controls (Figure 7A).

Taken together, these results demonstrated that MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull gene therapy in young adult GSD IIIa mice enables sustained expression of pullulanase and efficient reduction of glycogen accumulation in heart and skeletal muscle, accompanied by the long-term improvement of muscle function in both male and female mice.

Discussion

Among all natural AAV serotypes, AAV9 exhibits broad multi-organ tropism and is considered one of the most reliable vectors for transducing the liver, muscle, and central nervous system.21,22,23 This unique feature makes AAV9 a preferred capsid for the development of gene therapy for GSD III, which affects the liver, heart, and skeletal muscle. However, achieving sufficient muscle transduction often requires high-dose AAV9 administration, which can lead to unwanted adverse events such as hepatotoxicity.24,25,26 Capsid engineering represents an effective strategy to enhance tissue specificity while minimizing high-dose-associated toxicity. An excellent example of this approach is the newly developed MyoAAV series with enhanced muscle transduction.17 Given that AAV gene therapy is potentially a “one-and done” intervention, long-term efficacy is an essential component of successful gene therapy development for GSD III. To achieve sustained pullulanase expression in the liver and muscle, we minimized the CTL responses against pullulanase by using an immunotolerizing dual promoter and reduced the effective dose required for skeletal muscle correction by employing the myotropic MyoAAV4A capsid.

We recently reported a 6-week study in 3-month-old GSD IIIa mice, demonstrating that MyoAAV capsids (MyoAAV4A and MyoAAV4E) transduced the liver as efficiently as AAV9, while achieving significantly higher transduction efficiency in skeletal muscles and superior therapeutic outcomes compared with AAV9.18 These findings suggest that MyoAAV capsids, particularly MyoAAV4A, are suitable vectors for systemic delivery of therapeutic transgenes in GSD III. Encouraging data have also been reported in multiple gene therapy studies using the MyoAAV capsid for Duchenne muscular dystrophy.27,28,29 However, little is known about its transduction efficiency in liver and muscle at the advanced stage of disease. This is critical for GSD III gene therapy development, because cardiac and skeletal muscle involvement becomes more clinically evident with age. Moreover, some GSD III patients develop progressive liver fibrosis or cirrhosis later in life. Without effective treatments, these manifestations can progress to life-threatening complications.

In our previously published studies, we observed extensive liver fibrosis and severely disrupted muscle structure in aged GSD IIIa mice and dogs.16,30 In the current study, we first tested whether systemic administration of the MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull vector could achieve therapeutic efficacy in damaged liver and muscle in aged GSD IIIa mice. Remarkably, when administered at a dose of 5 × 1012 vg/kg, the MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull vector effectively transduced the liver, heart, and skeletal muscles in both aged male and female GSD IIIa mice (Figure 1). Glycogen accumulation was significantly reduced in the liver and restored to WT levels in the heart and skeletal muscles (Figure 2). Furthermore, MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull treatment significantly prevented hepatic fibrosis progression (Figure 3), reduced plasma ALT and CK activity, normalized disease marker urinary Glc4 level, enhanced the limb muscle strength, and improved the exercise tolerance in aged GSD IIIa mice (Figure 4). These findings demonstrate that the MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull treatment can successfully reverse GSD III disease pathology and restore liver and muscle function, even at advanced stages.

We next evaluated the long-term therapeutic efficacy in young adult GSD IIIa mice. Administration of MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull at a dose of 5 × 1012 vg/kg in 3-month-old GSD IIIa mice resulted in persistent pullulanase expression and activity in the heart and quadriceps, but not in the liver (Figures 5B and S4). Consistent with the pullulanase results, glycogen levels in the heart and quadriceps were significantly reduced at 5.5 months and remained reduced at 9 months post administration, whereas liver glycogen had already returned to UT level by 5.5 months post administration (Figures 5C and S3).

The complete loss of pullulanase expression in the liver and remarkably reduced pullulanase expression in the heart and quadriceps 5.5 months after AAV administration (Figure 5B) were likely due to the gradual loss of AAV vectors mediated by residual CTL responses in these tissues, as we have previously reported that the dual promoter substantially reduced but did not completely eliminate CTL responses to pullulanase in adult GSD IIIa mice.15 Furthermore, consistent with the typical time frame of CTL responses, AAV vector genome loss likely occurred prior to 5.5 months post-AAV administration because pullulanase expression subsequently stabilized throughout the remaining treatment period, as no further changes in pullulanase activity or glycogen content were observed in the heart and quadriceps at the 9-month time point (Figures 5 and S3). These findings strongly demonstrated the persistent therapeutic efficacy of MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull in muscles after a single intravenous injection.

Despite the complete loss of pullulanase expression in the liver by 5.5 months post-vector administration, MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull-treated mice exhibited markedly reduced hepatic fibrosis compared with UT mice at 9 months after AAV administration (Figure 7A). Interestingly, similarly high levels of collagen I and collagen III expression were observed in both AAV-treated and UT livers at this time point (Figure 7B), indicating ongoing hepatic fibrosis progression in both groups. This discrepancy is likely attributable to the longer duration of collagen deposition in the livers of UT mice and delayed fibrosis progression in treated mice, suggesting that early liver glycogen clearance by AAV treatment contributes to delayed hepatic fibrosis progression.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull treatment at a reduced dose effectively rescues GSD III disease phenotypes and alleviate disease progression in aged mice, and provides long-term muscle correction in young adult mice. Although this study provides valuable insights into the use of MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull for developing AAV gene therapy for GSD III, further studies are needed to achieve prolonged pullulanase expression in the liver, including testing novel liver-muscle dual promoter or exploring combinational treatment strategies to further reduce transgene-induced CTL responses and prevent gradual vector loss in AAV-transduced tissues.

Materials and methods

AAV vector construct and AAV viral packaging

The pAAV-Dual-Pull AAV vector plasmid,15 which contains an LSP-CB dual promoter and a 2.2-kb CpG-free pullulanase cDNA tagged with a hemagglutinin (HA) sequence, was packaged into MyoAAV4A capsid (MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull) in HEK293 cells using calcium phosphate transfection method.18 Harvested cells were spun down at 3,000 rpm for 10 min, pellets were resuspended in AAV lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-Cl pH 8.5, 150 mM NaCl) and went through 3 cycles of freeze-thaw, followed by clearance by centrifugation. The AAV vector was purified using iodixanol gradient ultracentrifugation method as previously described.31 Titers of AAV vector stocks were determined by quantitative real-time PCR using primers targeting the polyA tail.

Animals and viral vector administration

Animal care and experiment were conducted by following Duke University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approved guidelines. The Agl knockout mice (GSD IIIa mice) were generated by deleting exons 6–10 in the Agl gene as described.14 For AAV treatment in aged mice, 13-month-old male and female GSD IIIa mice received intravenous retro-orbital injection of MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull at a dose of 5 × 1012 vg/kg. Age- and gender-matched untreated GSD IIIa mice and WT mice were used as controls. Three months after vector administration, mice were sacrificed following overnight fasting, and tissues and blood were collected. For AAV treatment in young adult mice, 3-month-old male and female GSD IIIa mice were injected with MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull at a dose of 5 × 1012 vg/kg. Age- and gender-matched untreated GSD IIIa mice were included as UT controls. Behavioral tests and tissue and blood collection were done at 5.5 and 9 months, respectively, following vector administration. A group of 10-month-old WT mice served as normal controls. Fresh tissues were immediately frozen with dry ice and stored at −80°C until use or fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for histology.

AAV vector biodistribution

MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull genome copy number in tissues was determined by quantitative PCR (qPCR). Total DNA was extracted from frozen tissues using Wizard Genomic DNA Purification kit (Promega). qPCR was performed using iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) and following gene-specific primer pairs: forward 5′-GCCACTGGATGCCTACAACT-3′ and reverse 5′-CGTGCTGGTGCAGTGTATTG-3′ for pullulanase, and forward 5′-AGAGGGAAATCGTGCGTGAC-3′ and reverse 5′-CAATAGTGATGACCTGGCCGT-3′ for mouse β-actin. The linearized pAAV-Dual-Pull plasmid DNA was used to create a standard curve for calculating the viral vector genome copy numbers.

Western blot analysis

Tissues were homogenized on ice in RIPA buffer containing protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Cell Signaling Technology), followed by centrifugation at 18,000 × g at 4°C for 15 min. The protein concentration was measured using bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Equal amounts of protein samples were separated in NuPage 4–12% Bis-Tris polyacrylamide gels (Invitrogen) and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked in PBST containing 3% BSA and incubated overnight at 4°C with antibodies against HA-tag (Cell Signaling Technology, rabbit, 3724), β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich, mouse, A3854) and GADPH (Thermo Fisher Scientific, mouse, MA5-15738-HRP). After incubation, the membranes were washed three times with PBST and incubated with horseradish peroxide (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, A0545) for 1 h at room temperature. After washes, blots were visualized by ChemiDoc Image System (Bio-Rad) using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Pullulanase activity assay

Pullulanase activity was measured using the Pullulanase Assay Kit (Megazyme, K-PullG6) following manufacturer’s protocol as described.14 Frozen tissues were homogenized using tissue homogenizer (Caframo, RZR1) in deionized water on ice followed by 15 s sonication repeated 3 times (Qsonica Sonicators, Q500). After centrifugation at 18,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C, the supernatants were incubated with the substrate (4,6-O-benzylidene-4 nitrophenyl-63-α-D-maltotriosyl-maltotriose) for 10 min at 40°C. The reaction was terminated by adding the stop buffer (2% [w/v] Tris-Cl, pH 9.0), and the pullulanase activity was measured by reading the absorbance at 405 nm using a 96-well plate reader (PerkinElmer, Victor X3). The pullulanase enzyme standard provided in the kit was used to calculate the activity of pullulanase, and the data were normalized by the protein concentration of the homogenates measured by BCA assay.

Glycogen content assay

Glycogen content in tissues was measured indirectly as the glucose released from glycogen after total digestion with Aspergillus Niger amyloglucosidase (Sigma-Aldrich) as described previously.18 The tissue homogenates were boiled for 3 min and immediately cooled on ice for 10 min. The samples were then incubated with amyloglucosidase (final concentration 150 μg/mL in 25 mM potassium acetate pH 5.5) for 90 min at 37°C, and the reaction was stopped by boiling for 3 min. The glucose released from each reaction was determined by incubating the reaction mixtures with Pointe Scientific Glucose (Hexokinase) Liquid Reagents (Fisher Scientific) for 10 min at room temperature followed by measuring the resulting absorbance at 340 nm using a UV-vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1700, PharmaSpec). A glucose standard series was used for calculating glucose content in digested homogenates.

Histology

After primary immersion fixation in 10% NBF, tissues were washed with PBS, dehydrated with ascending grades of ethyl alcohol, cleared in xylene for 2 h, and infiltrated with paraffin. For PAS staining, sections of the paraffin-embedded tissue samples were stained with Schiff’s reagent (Sigma-Aldrich) as described previously.14 Briefly, slides were oxidized with 0.5% periodic acid for 5 min and rinsed with distilled water for 1 min followed by staining with Schiff’s reagent for 15 min. After 10 min of wash in distilled water, the slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, washed with tap water, incubated with bluing reagent for 1 min, and dehydrated with ethyl alcohol and xylene. Finally, all slides were mounted with Chemical Permount mounting medium (Fisher Scientific). For Picrosirius red staining, the paraffin-embedded liver sections were processed and stained using Picrosirius Red Stain kit (Polysciences) following the manufacturer’s protocol. All images were obtained with BZ-710 microscope (Keyence America).

RNA isolation and real-time qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from liver tissues with Trizol Reagent (Invitrogen). RNA was then reverse transcribed to cDNA using M-MLV reverse transcriptase and oligo(dT)12–18 primers (Invitrogen). qPCR was performed using iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) and gene-specific primer pairs described in Table S1. Target mRNA levels were normalized to β-actin reference gene. Levels of mRNA expression are presented as the fold change relative to a WT control.

Plasma enzyme activity assays

The activities of plasma ALT, AST, and CK were measured using the Liquid ALT (SGPT), Liquid AST (SGOT) and Liquid Creatine Kinase Reagent kits (Pointe Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, diluted plasma (1:5 with 0.9% [w/v] of NaCl) was incubated with the working reagent at 37°C, and the absorbance at 340 nm was recorded using a UV-vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1700) every minute for 5 min.

Measurement of Glc4 levels in urine

The concentration of urinary Glc4 was measured by stable isotope-dilution liquid chromatography-electrospray tandem mass spectrometry, as previously described.32

Behavioral tests

Grip strength was measured using a grip strength meter (Bioseb). Three independent measurements of four limbs’ strength were recorded in each trial, and two trials were performed for each mouse. Maximum values of grip strength were presented.

Wire hang test was performed as described previously.14 A wire mesh was positioned 30 cm above a soft bedding and carefully inverted. The time mouse fell from the mesh wire or the maximum time (5 min) was recorded. Three independent measurements were recorded and the maximum hanging time was used for comparison.

The treadmill exhaustion test was performed using a treadmill (LE8709, Panlab Harvard Apparatus) as previously reported.14 Mice warmed up by running at a lowest speed, 5 cm/s, and 25 degrees of slope for 3 min. Then, mice were allowed to run at 8 cm/s for 3 min. The speed was increased by 4 cm/s every 3 min until mice were exhausted or the maximal speed (32 cm/s) was reached.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Inc.). The Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to determine whether the data were normally distributed. For comparison between two groups with normally distributed data, an unpaired Student’s t test (for equal variance) or Welch t test (for unequal variance) was used. In cases where the data did not follow a normal distribution, an unpaired Mann-Whitney U test was performed. For comparisons among multiple groups with normally distributed data, ordinary one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test (equal variance) or Welch’s ANOVA with Dunnett’s T3 post-hoc test (unequal variance) was conducted. For non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post-doc test was performed. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
