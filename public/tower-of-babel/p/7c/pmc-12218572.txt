Development and validation of Intertemporal Decision-Making Ability Scale for early adolescents
Authors: Dawei Wang, Yang Liu, Tianqi Zong, Ziying Lu, Wenxu Mao, Linyan Miao, Yixin Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveIntertemporal decision-making ability refers to the ability of individuals to weigh costs and benefits at different time points and make choices, which is crucial for adolescents to resist temptation, improve self-control, and prospection. However, there are rarely studies that have been conducted to directly measure the intertemporal decision-making ability of adolescents.MethodIn this study, adolescents aged 9 to 15 years old were include to finish the measurement, and the intertemporal decision-making ability scale was developed based on three neural networks of intertemporal decision-making. After conducting the exploratory factor analysis, the final scale had 14 items in three dimensions.ResultsThe results showed that the intertemporal decision-making ability scale for early adolescents contains three dimensions, namely, cognitive control, reward valuation, and prospection, with a total of 14 items.ConclusionThe intertemporal decisionmaking ability scale developed in this study can effectively assess the intertemporal decisionmaking ability for early adolescents..KEYWORDS: Intertemporal decision-making ability, adolescents, cognitive control, value assessment, future anticipation

Introduction

Making decisions between outcomes available at different points in the future often require inhibiting decision-making impulsivity to maximise rewards, and then the degree of future orientation shown in the process could reflect the strength of individuals’ intertemporal decision-making ability (Frederick et al., 2002; He & Li, 2020; Liang & Liu, 2011; Liu et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2019). In the process of choice, delay discounting refers to the phenomenon in which the subjective value of future benefits diminishes as the time to delivery increases (Ainslie, 1975; He & Li, 2020; Samuelson, 1937), that is, individuals with higher levels of delay discounting tend to prefer immediate rewards over delayed ones. In other words, individuals with lower intertemporal decision-making ability exhibit higher levels of delay discounting and demonstrate a preference for smaller-sooner rewards. Thus, the study defines intertemporal decision-making ability as the ability of individuals to weigh the benefits between the present and the future, while also exhibiting a preference for future-oriented choices in intertemporal choice.

Numerous studies have found that the intertemporal decision-making ability is not innate, but develops continuously with the brain development (Anandakumar et al., 2018; Blakemore & Robbins, 2012; He & Li, 2020; Steinberg et al., 2010). Peters and Büchel (2011) proposed three brain networks related to intertemporal decision-making, the cognitive control network, the reward valuation network, and the prospection network, which are primarily located in the frontal cortex (Sowell et al., 2003). The prefrontal lobe undergoes rapid development during childhood and adolescence (Hill et al., 2010; Sowell et al., 2003), indicating that the adolescent stage plays a crucial role in the maturation of intertemporal decision-making ability over time.

Additionally, proficient intertemporal decision-making ability is crucial for adolescent development. The variation in individuals’ ability to make future-oriented decisions versus being constrained by immediate interests can be attributed to variations in their intertemporal decision-making ability (He & Li, 2020). During adolescence, the individual’s developmental task is to establish a stable ego identity. Ego Identity refers to a sense of self-awareness, knowing one’s future goals, and inner self-confidence to receive expected recognition from trusted individuals (Erikson, 1959). Previous studies have found that the healthy development of ego identity is associated with improved academic performance, social adjustment, and well-being of adolescents (Abu-Rayya, 2006; Pellerone et al., 2015; Perumal, 2020), while the poor development of ego identity hinders the development of mental health and even leads to problematic behaviours (Abu-Rayya, 2010; Saniye & Ayhan, 2023). One of the problems that must be solved before ego identity can be achieved is the temporary vision versus temporal confusion. Imagining future scenarios facilitates the perception of future goals (Baumeister et al., 2016; Kvavilashvili & Rummel, 2020), whereas acquiring intrinsic self-confidence requires a positive belief for the future (Jimenez & D’Argembeau, 2024), both of which are closely related to the future anticipation dimension of intertemporal decision-making ability. This means that having strong intertemporal decision-making ability will help individuals to view the future more positively and have a clearer plan for the future, which is conducive to achieving self-identity and plays an important role in individual development. At the same time, adolescence is described as a time of storms with tremendous psychological and physical changes, and adolescents are more likely to experience conflicts with their parents, emotional turmoil (Hall, 1904), and even exhibit risky behaviours (Arnett, 1999). Steinberg (2004, 2007) pointed out that adolescents engage in risky behaviours not because they are unable to recognise the consequences of such behaviours, but due to insufficient social and psychological maturity resulting in immature impulse inhibition abilities. Therefore, intertemporal decision-making ability may play a protective role. When confronted with the present temptations and future benefits, individuals with strong intertemporal decision-making ability are more likely to choose the future benefits. However, if the intertemporal decision-making ability is not well developed, it will hinder individual development and even have an impact on society. For example, adolescents who violate school rules by smoking, skipping classes, or getting into fights are not only detrimental to their own education, but also have a negative impact on the school atmosphere and school administration. Therefore, studying early adolescents’ intertemporal decision-making ability will be important for their personal development as well as the development of schools and society.

Existing studies on early adolescents’ intertemporal decision-making ability will be valuable in improving the ability of adolescents to resist temptation, such as, preventing academic procrastination. However, there still exist certain challenges in the domain of intertemporal decision-making ability, with the first factor to consider being the age range in which the measurement tool can be applied. In intertemporal choice, the commonly used paradigm for measuring delay discounting rate is the money choice task, which usually allows participants to choose between a smaller-sooner reward and a larger-later reward. For example, they may be asked to decide between receiving $5 today or $10 in 7 days. In addition, there are also scales that focus on the money domain, mainly the Monetary Choice scale compiled by Kirby (1997) (MCQ; 27 items; for example, “Would you rather have $54 Today Or, $55 in 117 Days?”), Wang and Dvorak’s (2010) scale (14 items; for example, “Would you prefer $120 tomorrow or $450 in 31 days?”). However, most of these tools are applicable to adults and relatively cumbersome to calculate. And Yang (2013) found that individuals in early adolescence are immature in their perceptions and weighing of various functions of money, so the existing measurement tools may not be applicable to children and adolescents. Secondly, in terms of the validity of measurement tools, current practices have found differences in the results between real and virtual intertemporal choice tasks, with a greater discounting rate observed for real rewards compared to virtual rewards (Kirby, 1997; Lane et al., 2003). Kirby (1997) suggested that it may be due to the fact that the real rewards used are typically smaller, thus resulting in greater delayed discounting. Therefore, the results obtained from these task paradigms are somewhat controversial. Finally, in terms of the structure of the measurement tools, there is no scale developed from the structure of intertemporal decision-making ability. Most of the existing measurement tools measure delay discounting rate, which can only describe the phenomenon of intertemporal choice and cannot indicate the process and mechanism of intertemporal choice. Based on the above-mentioned limitations, it is urgent to develop a scale on intertemporal decision-making ability for early adolescents. Specifically, among the theoretical models related to intertemporal decision-making ability, the delay discounting family model explains intertemporal choice based on the delay discounting rate, while the single-system valuation theory, dual processing system, self-control theory, and three brain networks of intertemporal decision-making focus on different aspects of intertemporal decision-making from brain regions. While existing measurement tools aim to assess intertemporal decision-making through delay discounting, the scale developed in this study aims to comprehensively describe the underlying processes and mechanisms of intertemporal choice, addressing its various dimensions with detailed precision. Based on the above considerations, among the related theoretical foundations, three brain networks are considered to be the most comprehensive. Ultimately, the study adopts three brain networks as the theoretical basis for developing tools.

The three brain networks of intertemporal decision-making proposed by Peters and Büchel (2011) is an important basis for measuring adolescents’ intertemporal decision-making ability. Firstly, the cognitive control network involves individuals’ top-down control of immediate options, and individuals with strong intertemporal decision-making ability perform better in cognitive control, which in turn promote them to choose more larger-later options. It has been found that the cognitive control network mainly includes brain regions such as the lateral prefrontal cortex (PFC) and the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC; Peng & Feng, 2014). When an individual’s cognitive control network is more involved in the decision-making process, they will prefer larger-later rewards (McClure et al., 2004; Volkow & Baler, 2015). For example, Peters and Büchel (2011) found that increased recruitment of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex enhances individuals’ cognitive control, resulting in less smaller-sooner selection. Secondly, the reward valuation network involves the evaluation of subjective values, and individuals with strong intertemporal decision-making abilities exhibit higher value evaluation abilities and are more inclined to assign greater subjective value to future rewards, which in turn makes individuals more inclined to larger-later choices. During the evaluation phase, the recruitment of the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), ventral striatum (VS) and other brain regions are positively correlated with the subjective value of delayed rewards (Christakou et al., 2011; Jimura et al., 2013; Oren et al., 2022; Smith et al., 2014). Finally, the prospection network involves the scenario imagination of decision outcomes (Chen et al., 2019). The stronger the intertemporal decision-making ability, the stronger the prospection ability, and the easier it is for individuals to imagine and evaluate future events and decision outcomes (Peters & Büchel, 2011). Episodic future thinking can impact intertemporal choice (O’Donnell et al., 2017; Ruhi-Williams et al., 2022), as evidenced by the fact that imagining future episodes can motivate participants to choose future rewards more frequently. This effect is modulated by the activity of brain regions such as the hippocampus (Mok et al., 2020). In summary, our study hypothesised that there may be three dimensions of intertemporal decision-making ability: cognitive control ability, reward valuation ability, and prospection ability.

Considering the correlation of intertemporal decision-making ability and adolescents’ impulsivity, intertemporal choice, as well as the positive effect of such ability on adolescents’ positive behaviours and perceptions, the impulsivity, intertemporal choice, prosocial behaviours, and general self-efficacy were selected as the criteria for the Intertemporal Decision-Making Ability Scale in this study. Intertemporal decision-making ability refers to an individual’s ability to assess present and future benefits, resulting in a preference for the delayed larger reward in intertemporal choice tasks. He and Li (2020) demonstrated that delay discounting serves as an effective indicator of intertemporal decision-making ability. Specifically, individuals with higher levels of delay discounting in the intertemporal choice tasks exhibit lower intertemporal decision-making ability. Therefore, this study hypothesised that intertemporal decision-making ability of early adolescents exhibits a positive relationship with intertemporal choice. In addition, impulsivity refers to an individual’s inclination towards spontaneous thoughts and reckless actions, often characterised by a lack of foresight or reflection, as well as insufficient consideration of the consequences of their own behaviours (Fineberg et al., 2014; VandenBos, 2015). Joireman et al. (2008) found a significantly positive correlation between high levels of impulsivity and scores on the immediate subscale of the Considering Future Consequences (CFC) scale, indicating a diminished tendency towards future-oriented thinking. Therefore, this study hypothesised a negative relationship between impulsivity and the intertemporal decision-making ability. Furthermore, prosocial behaviours encompass voluntary actions undertaken by individuals to proactively advance the well-being of others, foster social cohesion, mitigate conflicts, or address societal challenges with a forward-thinking perspective and genuine concern for sustainable development, harmony, and the future welfare of society. These behaviours not only consider immediate consequences but also prioritise long-term positive impacts on future generations (Eisenberg et al., 2006). It was found that future orientation was negatively related to delay discounting rates (Liu et al., 2022; Scott et al., 1995). Individuals with strong intertemporal decision-making ability tend to prefer larger-later rewards and have a stronger future orientation (Nurmi, 1992), resulting in a higher propensity for prosocial behaviours (Sjåstad, 2019), such as sharing more social resources (Kortenkamp & Moore, 2006). Therefore, the study hypothesised that individuals with high intertemporal decision-making ability will have more consideration for future outcomes and engage in more prosocial behaviours. Self-control is an important factor in intertemporal decision-making ability, as studies available have found that self-efficacy is closely related to self-control. Specifically, general self-efficiency (GSE) refers to the confidence of individuals in coping with the challenges in different environments or facing new situations (Schwarzer et al., 1997). Individuals with higher intertemporal decision-making ability exhibit stronger self-control, and are more likely to achieve their goals in daily life (Stavrova et al., 2019). The experiences of achieving these goals can affect individual self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997), which in turn has a positive effect on enhancing intertemporal decision-making ability (Bandura, 2012).

The main purpose of this study is to develop an intertemporal decision ability scale that is suitable for early adolescents, and further test its reliability, validity, and criterion-realted validity. The preparation of this scale would be of great value for future research on intertemporal decision-making ability, related interventions and promoting the development of intertemporal decision-making ability in early adolescence. Additionally, the study also helps to establish a foundation for cross-cultural comparisons of intertemporal decision-making ability for early adolescents, thereby elucidating the impact of cultural factors on individuals’ intertemporal decision-making ability, which seeks to offer novel perspectives and insights into the education and cultivation of adolescents within the context of globalisation.

Scale development

Pilot study – interviews

Study purpose

Through interviews with primary and secondary school students and teachers, this study aims to better understand the structure and content of intertemporal decision-making ability for early adolescents, and form the questions of the Intertemporal Decision-Making Ability Scale.

Participants

Primary and secondary school teachers and 12 primary and secondary school students (grades 4–9, 8 boys and 4 girls) from Shandong Province were selected as interview participants.

Interview syllabus

The semi-structured interview outline is formed and evaluated by experts based on the theory of three brain networks of intertemporal decision-making (cognitive control, reward valuation and prospection), including, (1)The connotation of intertemporal decision-making ability; (2)Types of intertemporal decision-making behaviour of primary and secondary school students; (3)The intertemporal decisions students have made in their lives and why they have made such decisions; (4)Influencing factors of intertemporal decision-making; (5)Characteristics of adolescents with high intertemporal decision-making ability. For example, “Think back to when you were in primary/secondary school, did you make some longer-term choices? Please specify one or two pieces. Why did you make those choices?”

Procedure and data analysis

The interview took place in a highly private setting, and interviewees were asked to sign informed consent before the interview to inform the purpose, content, sound recording and confidentiality of the interview. Interviews were conducted according to the interview outline, each interview lasted for 30 minutes with continuous recordings during the interview. The interview recordings were transcribed into a text consisting of over 56,000 Chinese characters and analysed by Nvivo 11.

Results

According to the three brain networks of intertemporal decision-making, three core codes were established: cognitive control ability, reward valuation ability and prospection ability. According to the interview text, we found out the text information related to each dimension, formed open coding, and then formed axial coding through summarising the text information. The selective codings were formed according to open coding and axial coding, and the question bank was constructed. We finally formed 463 open codings,11 axial codings and 3 selective codings. Axial codings “wait for reward” (51), “resist the temptation” (113), “focus” (19), “organize things” (4), “arrange time” (13), “make plans” (43), “set goals” (32) were classified as “cognitive control”; Axial codings “focus on income” (81) and “importance” (15) were classified as “reward valuation”. Axial codings “imagine future consequences” (35) and “think about the future” (56) were classified as “prospection”. Among them, reward valuation ability included “I think it is better if I can get more rewards” and so on; Cognitive control ability included “having children is so that I can control myself strictly”; prospection ability included “When I write my homework, I think about how to play after writing it, so that I can devote myself to writing my homework”.

We carried out a coding consistency test after coding by comparing the codes of two coders. The Kappa coefficients of each node were calculated, and the final results showed that all Kappa coefficients were greater than 0.4 (Table 1), that is, the coding consistency was good.

Four professionals with intertemporal decision-making ability were invited to discuss and conduct expert appraisals after the development of relevant items. Study 1a exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

Participants

The participants were 610 students from Shandong Province, China. A total of 30 participants were excluded from the study due to their exhibiting a high number of missed responses, regular response, or failure in the lie detector quizzes, such as “The sun rises in the west every day”. Thus, the final sample consisted of 580 students (295 males and 285 females), with an age range of 9 to 15 years (MAge = 12.74, SD = 1.57). Among these students, 36 (6.2%) were in the 4th grade, 50 (8.6%) were in the 5th grade, 188 (32.4%) were in the 6th grade, 95 (16.4%) were in the 7th grade, 114 (19.7%) were in the 8th grade, and 97 (16.7%) were in the 9th grade. The study was approved by the ethics standards of the Academic Board of Shandong Normal University and the declaration of Helsinki in 1964. Informed consent of the students and their primary caregivers was obtained.

Measure

Preliminary Intertemporal Decision-Making Ability Scale (IDMA)

The preliminary IDMA was a self-report scale consisting of 19 items. Participants responded on a 5-point Likert-type scale (ranging from 1 = Very inconsistent to 5 = Very consistent). Higher scores indicated greater intertemporal decision-making ability.

Procedure and data analysis

The paper scales were distributed and collected by professional psychological staff in the class after obtaining the informed consent of the schools, the class teachers, and the primary caregivers. The test was conducted by taking one class as a unit. All the analyses were performed with SPSS 26.0.

The items whose correlation coefficient between the item score and the total score was less than 0.40 were deleted. The results showed that none of them was deleted (Table 2). The Cronbach’s α of the 19-item scale was 0.94.

Study 1b confirmative factor analysis (CFA)

Participants

The 534 students in this study were selected from Shandong Province, China. A total of 25 participants were excluded from the study due to their exhibiting a high number of missed responses, regular response, or failure in the lie detector quizzes, such as “The sun rises in the west every day”. Thus, the final sample consisted of 509 students (272 males and 237 females), with an age range of 9 to 15 years (MAge = 11.85, SD = 1.58). Among these students, 116 (22.8%) were in the 4th grade, 157 (30.8%) were in the 5th grade, 84 (16.5%) were in the 6th grade, 52 (10.2%) were in the 7th grade, 59 (11.6%) were in the 8th grade, and 41 (8.1%) were in the 9th grade.The study was approved by the ethics standards of the Academic Board of Shandong Normal University and the declaration of Helsinki in 1964. Informed consent of the students and their primary caregivers was obtained.

Measure

Intertemporal Decision-Making Ability Scale-14 (IDMA-14)

The 14-items IDMA retained by EFA in study 1a was used (for example, “I can control myself from doing things that are not relevant to the task at hand”.). The participants responded on a 5-point Likert-type scale (ranging from 1 = Very inconsistent to 5 = Very consistent). The Cronbach’s α of this scale in the present study was 0.94.

Procedure and data analysis

The professional psychological staff conducted the corresponding training for the class teachers after obtaining the informed consent of the schools, the class teachers and the primary caregivers. The test was conducted on per-class basis. All the analyses were performed with SPSS 26.0 and Mplus 8.

Results

Three factors were assumed to contain early adolescent intertemporal decision-making ability according to the results of exploratory factor analysis, and Mplus 8.0 was used for confirmatory factor analysis. χ2 /df values, Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Standardised Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), and Root Mean Square Error Approximation (RMSEA) were employed to evaluate model fit, and χ2 /df < 5, TLI > 0.90 (Bentler & Bonett, 1980), CFI > 0.95 (Hu & Bentler, 1999), SRMR < 0.08 (L. T. Hu & Bentler, 1999), and RMSEA < 0.08 (Byrne et al., 1989) are considered as well-fitted critical values. All the indicators fitted by the model met the measurement requirements (χ2/ df = 3.230, TLI = 0.946, CFI = 0.959, SRMR = 0.039, RMSEA = 0.066), indicating that intertemporal decision-making ability scale for early adolescents had good construct validity.

Study 1c criterion-related validity

Participants

The 422 students in this study were selected from four middle and primary schools in Shandong Province, China. A total of 6 participants were excluded from the study due to their regular response or failure in the lie detector quizzes, such as “The sun rises in the west every day”. Thus, the final sample consisted of 416 students (227 males and 189 females), with an age range of 9 to 15 years (MAge = 12.18, SD = 1.73). Among these students, 70 (16.8%) were in the 4th grade, 55 (13.2%) were in the 5th grade, 60 (14.4%) were in the 6th grade, 56 (13.5%) were in the 7th grade, 86 (20.7%) were in the 8th grade, and 89 (21.4%) were in the 9th grade. The study was approved by the ethics standards of the Academic Board of Shandong Normal University and the declaration of Helsinki in 1964. Informed consent of the students and their primary caregivers was obtained.

Measures

Intertemporal Decision-Making Ability Scale (IDMA)

The IDMA developed in the current study was a 14-item self-report scale (for example, “I can control myself from doing things that are not relevant to the task at hand”.). This scale included three dimensions: cognitive control ability (7 items), reward valuation ability (3 items) and prospection ability (4 items). The participants responded on a 5-point Likert-type scale (ranging from 1 = Very inconsistent to 5 = Very consistent). The Cronbach’s α of this scale in the present study was 0.92.

Intertemporal choice tasks

The intertemporal choice tasks used in the study was adapted from the classic monetary intertemporal choice tasks (Hu et al., 2024), in which participants were presented with a choice between an immediately available smaller reward (Smaller-sooner, SS) and a delayed larger reward (Larger-later, LL) in each item. The immediately available smaller reward is a fixed option, set to “Receive 10 RMB today” (10 RMB equivalent to approximately 1.40 dollars), based on the spending patterns observed among early adolescents in China, preliminary interviews and tests. The delayed larger reward is characterised by variability in both timing and magnitude, with delay periods of 7, 15, 30, 60, and 120 days. Specifically, the delayed larger reward entails additional increments ranging from 10%, 20%, 40%, 80%, 120%, 160%, 240%, 320%, and up to a maximum of an extra fivefold (500%) on top of the base amount of 10 RMB. The task consists of a total of 50 items, consisting of a combination of 5 delay times and 10 increase ratios, accompanied by immediately available smaller rewards. For instance, participants were presented with the choice between receiving 10 RMB today or 26 RMB in 15 days. For each participant, the proportion of opting for delayed larger rewards in the task was used as an indicator of their intertemporal choice. The detailed information can be seen in Appendix 3.

Brief Barratt Impulsivity Scale (BBIS)

The BBIS was used to measure impulsivity, which was developed by Morean et al. (2014) and adapted from Luo et al. (2020). This scale had a total of 8 items (for example, “I don’t think about what I say”). The participants responded on a 4-point Likert-type scale (ranging from 1 = Never to 4 = Often). The Cronbach’ s α of this scale in the present study was 0.80.

Prosocial Tendencies Measure (PTM)

The altruistic scale of PTM was used to measure prosocial behaviours, which was developed by Carlo and Randall (2002) and adapted from Kou et al. (2007). This scale had a total of 4 items (for example, “The reason I devote my time and energy to volunteer service is not for more rewards”.). The participants responded on a 5-point Likert-type scale (ranging from 1 = Does not describe me at all to 5 = Describes me greatly). The Cronbach’s α of this scale in the present study was 0.87.

Generalised Self-Efficacy Scale (GSE)

Generalised Self-Efficacy Scale was developed by Schwarzer and Jerusalem (1995) and adapted from Wang et al. (2000). It consisted of 10 items (for example, “I can always manage to solve difficult problems if I try hard enough”.), The participants responded on a 4-point Likert-type scale (ranging from 1 = Not at all true to 4 = Exactly true). The Cronbach’s α of this scale in the present study was 0.90.

Procedure and data analysis

After obtaining the informed consent of the schools, the class teachers and the primary caregivers, online scales were distributed by professional psychological staff on SoJump platform. All analyses were performed with SPSS 26.0.

Discussion

It is very crucial for early adolescents to develop intertemporal decision-making ability. Intertemporal decision-making ability develops rapidly during childhood and adolescence, aiding individuals in establishing their self-identity. Therefore, if interventions can be made during this period to help individuals improve their intertemporal decision-making ability, it will not only be beneficial for individuals but also have a long-term impact on social development. However, there is a lack of measurement tools for early adolescents’ intertemporal decision-making ability. Therefore, it is necessary to develop a validated measurement tool.

In this study, based on the three brain networks of intertemporal decision-making (Chen et al., 2019; Peters & Büchel, 2011), the Intertemporal Decision-Making Ability Scale for early adolescents was developed, which had good reliability and validity, and confirmed the hypotheses of this study. The final scale consists of three dimensions, namely cognitive control ability, reward evaluation ability, and prospection ability. Currently, the main theories of intertemporal decision-making neural systems include dual-system valuation theory (McClure et al., 2004; Soman et al., 2005), single-system valuation theory (Kable & Glimcher, 2007, 2010), self-control theory (Figner et al., 2010), and the three brain networks summarised by Peters and Büchel (2011) based on previous studies. The results of the present study are consistent with those of Peters and Büchel (2011). When individuals are in the evaluation phase of intertemporal choice, they integrate information from both dimensions of delay time and reward size (Liu & Feng, 2012; Liu et al., 2012) to form the subjective value of immediate and future rewards, a process known as reward valuation. According to the definition of intertemporal decision-making ability, it is also involved in the process of weighing current versus future. After entering the choice phase, cognitive control may play a role. Previous studies have shown that cognitive control can help individuals suppress the impulse to choose immediate rewards (Peters & Büchel, 2011), enabling them to have smaller delay discount rates and be more inclined to choose future rewards, thus demonstrating strong intertemporal decision-making ability. In addition, prospection influences the process of valuing future outcomes, thereby prompting individuals to choose more future rewards (Peters & Büchel, 2010; Sharot et al., 2007). When the individuals are unable to accurately evaluate the value of immediate rewards and future rewards, suppress the impulse to choose immediate rewards through top-down control, or apply prospection to guide decision-making, it will lead to individuals prefer immediate rewards (Peters & Büchel, 2011). Therefore, the interaction among the three systems of cognitive control, reward valuation and prospection impacts on intertemporal decision-making ability.

In addition, during the exploratory factor analysis phase, three items with cross-loadings and unclear attributions were removed, as well as two items in which the dimension with the highest loadings did not correspond to the hypothesised dimension. The item “I enjoy waiting for a period of time to receive a bigger reward (for example, a larger prize)” was originally categorised under the dimension of “reward valuation”, but in the actual results, it was loaded on “prospection”. This may be because the item could facilitate individuals in associating themselves with envisioning future outcomes. The items “I tend to make the gains bigger for myself (for example, better academic performance)” and “I can imagine future achievements” are cross-loaded on the “reward valuation” and “prospection”. This may be due to assess one’s own gains is accompanied by imagining potential outcomes, and the word “achievement” carries a positive connotation (Dictionary Editorial Office, Institute of Linguistics, Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, 2016), which includes evaluating the results. The item “I have a wonderful vision of the future”. belongs to the dimension of “prospection”, but in the actual results it is loaded on “reward valuation”, probably because “good imagination” includes the evaluation of the future (Dictionary Editorial Office, Institute of Linguistics, Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, 2016). “I can imagine a successful scenario in the future and make more efforts”. is loaded on three dimensions, probably because it involves both future expectations and the evaluation of the value of the future success scenarios, as well as the cognitive control involved in putting in effort in achieving the goal.

The results of the criterion-related validity of this study showed that intertemporal decision-making ability was significantly and positively related to intertemporal choice, prosocial behaviours, and self-efficacy, while significantly and negatively related to impulsivity. Individuals with higher intertemporal decision-making ability tend to choose longer-larger options in intertemporal choice tasks, thereby highlighting the predictive role of stable traits such as cognitive abilities in shaping individuals’ behaviours. The finding of a significantly negative relationship between intertemporal decision-making ability and impulsivity is also aligned with the results of the previous study, which found that individuals with high levels of impulsivity exhibit impatience and shortsightedness in interpersonal relationships (Moeller et al., 2001). Additionally, they display inadequate emotional and behavioural regulation in daily life, as well as a limited ability to delay gratification (Smilkstein et al., 1982). Moreover, the results of the present study are consistent with previous research, indicating that individuals with higher intertemporal decision-making ability tend to exhibit more prosocial behaviours (Sjåstad, 2019). This result is in line with the future time perspective theory (De Volder & Lens, 1982). According to this theory, individuals with higher intertemporal decision-making ability are more likely to plan for their future and perceive good outcomes from prosocial behaviours, which in turn leads to increased prosocial behaviours. In addition, according to social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1997), direct experience can influence an individual’s self-efficacy. The higher an individual’s intertemporal decision-making ability, the stronger their cognitive control ability, and the easier it is for them to resist temptation and choose future rewards in the process of achieving goals in daily study and life, thus enhancing their self-efficacy.

Finally, IDMA has some contributions. First, IDMA focuses on the structure of intertemporal decision-making ability, which can help us better understand it. This is something that is currently lacking in research. Second, IDMA focuses on the early adolescent group as the research participants, which is targeted and suitable for this particular age group. Intertemporal decision-making ability affects various aspects of an individual’s life, including health, education, and financial management, and has important research implications. Thirdly, this study can provide a reliable measurement tool for future research on intertemporal decision-making ability and lay the foundation for future interventions to enhance intertemporal decision-making ability. The scale will enable educators to timely identify issues, ascertain specific factors that influence intertemporal decision-making ability, and devise more targeted interventions in clinical settings, for instance, future-oriented group training.

This study is still in its infancy in the related field and has some limitations. Firstly, this study only selected participants from a province in eastern China, which may introduce sampling bias. Future studies should collect data from multiple regions in order to further test the Intertemporal Decision-Making Ability Scale for Early Adolescents. Secondly, the data in this study were self-reported, which may have been influenced by social desirability bias. Therefore, future studies could incorporate objective measures to supplement the findings.

Conclusion

The Intertemporal Decision-Making Ability Scale for Early Adolescents contained three dimensions including cognitive control ability, reward valuation ability, and prospection ability. It was proved to be a reliable and valid tool for measuring intertemporal decision-making ability.
