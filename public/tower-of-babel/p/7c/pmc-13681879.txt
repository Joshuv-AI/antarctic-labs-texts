Risk factors for surgical site infection requiring reoperation after osteotomy around the knee: a multicenter retrospective study
Authors: Yuji Arai, Shuji Nakagawa, Yusei Katsuyama, Atsuo Inoue, Koichi Idei, Shohei Ichimaru, Keisuke Sugie, Kentaro Hayashi, Kenji Takahashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteotomy around the knee (OAK) is an established joint-preserving procedure for knee osteoarthritis; however, postoperative surgical site infection (SSI) remains a serious complication that may compromise bone healing and functional outcomes. Evidence regarding risk factors for SSI requiring reoperation after OAK is limited. This study aimed to identify perioperative factors associated with SSI requiring reoperation following OAK.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective study included 247 patients (302 knees) who underwent OAK at one institution and two affiliated hospitals between June 2015 and June 2024. SSI was defined as an infection requiring surgical debridement. Patient demographics, comorbidities, perioperative laboratory data, and surgical variables were evaluated. Factors associated with SSI were identified using Firth’s penalized multivariable logistic regression. Sensitivity analyses were performed after excluding knees that underwent osteotomy for valgus deformity and after restricting the dataset to one knee per patient.ResultsSSI requiring reoperation occurred in 12 of 302 knees (4.0%). Firth’s penalized multivariable logistic regression identified preoperative skin disease, preoperative liver disease, and postoperative hemoglobin decline as factors independently associated with SSI. These associations remained consistent in sensitivity analyses excluding knees that underwent osteotomy for valgus deformity and restricting the analysis to one knee per patient.ConclusionsPreoperative skin disease, preoperative liver disease, and postoperative hemoglobin decline were independently associated with an increased risk of SSI requiring reoperation after OAK. Awareness of these factors may aid perioperative risk assessment and postoperative surveillance. Further prospective studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate these findings and establish clinically applicable strategies for infection risk assessment and prevention.Keywords: Osteotomy around the knee, Surgical site infection, Risk factors, Retrospective study

Background

Osteotomy around the knee (OAK) is an established joint-preserving surgical option for patients with knee osteoarthritis, particularly in relatively young and active individuals. Through correcting lower limb alignment and redistributing mechanical load across the knee joint, OAK can relieve pain, improve function, and delay the need for total knee arthroplasty [1–7].

Despite these favorable outcomes, postoperative complications remain a clinical concern [8–10]. Surgical site infection (SSI) requiring reoperation is a serious complication as it can compromise bone healing, necessitate implant removal, prolong hospitalization, and adversely affect functional outcomes. Reported infection rates following OAK vary widely, reflecting differences in patient populations, surgical techniques, and definitions of infection [11].

Several risk factors for postoperative infection have been identified in other orthopedic procedures, including patient-related factors such as comorbidities and perioperative anemia, as well as surgery-related factors such as operative time and blood loss. However, evidence specifically addressing risk factors for SSI after OAK remains limited. Moreover, OAK has unique characteristics—including extensive soft-tissue dissection, implantation of fixation devices, and dependence on bone healing—that may influence susceptibility to postoperative infection differently from arthroplasty or fracture surgery.

Identifying patients at increased risk of SSI after OAK may contribute to improved perioperative management and postoperative surveillance. However, given the relatively low incidence of infection, robust data to guide risk stratification are lacking, and predictive models have not been well established.

Therefore, the purpose of this multicenter retrospective study was to identify perioperative factors associated with SSI requiring reoperation following OAK.

Methods

Study design and ethics approval

This retrospective multicenter study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine (ERB-C-2046).

Participants

Consecutive patients who underwent OAK for meniscal injury, knee osteoarthritis, idiopathic knee osteonecrosis, or varus deformity at one institution and two affiliated hospitals between June 2015 and June 2024 were retrospectively analyzed. Indications for OAK included failure of conservative treatment for more than three months, predominantly involving medialor lateral compartment pathology, with preserved range of motion. No restrictions were imposed regarding sex, body weight, age, osteoarthritis grade, degree of lower-limb deformity, or anterior cruciate ligament status.

Patients were excluded if they demonstrated advanced degeneration in non-target compartments, symptoms attributable to these compartments, flexion contracture ≥ 10°, or low activity levels. Knee osteoarthritis was classified as varus-type or valgus-type, and the surgical procedure was selected according to a predefined institutional algorithm (Fig. 1). In varus-type osteoarthritis cases with a postoperative medial proximal tibial angle ≥ 95°, double-level osteotomy was selected and excluded from the present analysis.

All procedures were performed by six orthopedic surgeons, each with more than 10 years of experience, using standardized surgical indications and techniques. Inclusion criteria were osteotomy of the proximal tibia or distal femur, fixation with a single Tris plate® (Olympus Terumo Biomaterials, Tokyo, Japan), and postoperative follow-up of at least three months. After exclusions, 247 patients (302 knees) were included.

The cohort comprised 93 male patients (114 knees) and 154 female patients (188 knees). Unilateral procedures were performed in 192 patients, with bilateral procedures performed in 55 patients. No patients required perioperative blood transfusion. Intraoperative blood loss was generally minimal, and no cases of massive bleeding or bleeding-related complications were observed. The mean postoperative follow-up period was 37 (range, 3–101) months. Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Surgical technique and postoperative management

For all target alignments, the correction for varus deformity was set at 62.5% of the tibial plateau width (Fujisawa point), whereas for valgus deformity, the target was the top of the medial intercondylar ridge [12–14]. All patients were placed in the supine position under general anesthesia. Arthroscopic examination was performed in all cases, and when meniscal injury was identified, partial meniscectomy or repair was conducted based on the type and severity of the lesion. Medial open-wedge high tibial osteotomy (MOWHTO) was performed using the technique described by Arai et al. [12], and medial open-wedge distal tuberosity osteotomy (MOWDTO) was conducted using the technique described by Akiyama et al. [5]. Skin incisions were made either straight or slightly curved, and all patients received artificial bone grafts. Hybrid HTO was conducted in accordance with Takeuchi et al.’s technique [15]. Medial closing wedge distal femoral osteotomy (MCWDFO) was performed based on Nakamura et al.’s established protocol [6], and tibial condylar valgus osteotomy (TCVO) was performed according to Chiba et al.’s method [16]. In medial closing wedge high tibial osteotomy (MCWHTO), as in MOWHTO, the hinge point was set on the lateral side of the tibia, and a wedge-shaped section on the medial side was removed and closed. Tris plates® (Olympus Terumo Biomaterials, Tokyo, Japan) were used for fixation in all procedures. Postoperative rehabilitation included range of motion exercises starting on the day following surgery, with no limitations based on the surgical technique used. Full weight-bearing was permitted at 3–4 weeks postoperatively for MOWHTO, and at 4–6 weeks for MOWDTO, hybrid HTO, MCWDFO, MCWHTO, and TCVO. Blood tests were conducted preoperatively and at one week postoperatively.

Definition of surgical site infection

SSI was defined as a postoperative infection requiring surgical reoperation (surgical debridement with or without implant removal), accompanied by clinical signs of infection and elevated inflammatory markers. Therefore, the present definition specifically reflects relatively severe SSI requiring surgical intervention and does not include superficial or conservatively managed infections.

Data collection

Demographic data, comorbidities, operative variables, and perioperative laboratory values were extracted from the patients’ medical records. Postoperative hemoglobin decline was defined as the difference between the preoperative and one-week postoperative hemoglobin value. Skin disease was defined as dermatologic conditions with active or chronic skin lesions identified preoperatively, including but not limited to asteatotic eczema, stasis dermatitis, nummular eczema, bullous pemphigoid, and neurofibromatosis. Liver disease was defined as preoperatively diagnosed hepatic disorders, including hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and fatty liver disease. Due to the limited number of infection events, these conditions were analyzed as aggregated categories rather than as individual disease entities.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Comparisons between knees with and without SSI were performed using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Variables with a p-value < 0.10 in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to identify factors associated with SSI requiring reoperation. Given the limited number of SSI events and the potential risk of sparse-data bias, the multivariable model was subsequently re-fitted using Firth’s penalized logistic regression [17–19], and these results were considered the primary analysis. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Sensitivity analyses were performed by (1) excluding osteotomy for valgus deformity and (2) restricting the dataset to the first operated knee in patients who underwent bilateral procedures to assess the robustness of the identified associations. Variables with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), which is a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Incidence of SSI

No patients required perioperative blood transfusion, and intraoperative blood loss was generally minimal, with no bleeding-related complications observed. SSI requiring reoperation following OAK occurred in 12 of 302 knees (males, 7 knees; females, 5 knees), corresponding to an overall infection rate of 4%. The median patient age at the time of surgery was 62 (range, 22–84) years, and the median BMI was 24.36 (range, 16.71–49.26) kg/m².

The knee joint diagnoses included 11 knees with osteoarthritis and 1 knee with a medial meniscus posterior root tear. The following patient background factors were identified among the cases with infection: a history of smoking (n = 6), alcohol consumption (n = 3), diabetes mellitus (n = 3), malignancies (n = 3), skin diseases (n = 2), and liver diseases (n = 2).

Preoperative blood test results were as follows (median [range]): albumin, 4.3 (3.3–5.1) g/dL; glomerular filtration rate (GFR), 91.2 (5.0–170.0) mL/min/1.73 m²; preoperative hemoglobin 13.8 (10.5–18) g/dL; in addition to postoperative hemoglobin, 12.1 (8.5–16.4) g/dL and change in hemoglobin (Δhemoglobin) 1.6 (-0.8–4.0) g/dL.

Surgical procedures included MOWHTO (9 cases), MOWDTO (2 cases), and hybrid HTO (1 case). The median operative time was 2 h 4 min (range, 1 h 23 min–3 h 46 min). The median time to infection onset was 107 days postoperatively (range, 16–363 days). The causative organisms identified were methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus (5 knees), methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (1 knee), methicillin-sensitive coagulase-negative Staphylococcus (1 knee), Group B Streptococcus (1 knee), and unknown (4 knees). Risk factor analysis

Following a comprehensive univariate analysis of each factor, conducted separately for 290 knees in the non-infected group and 12 knees in the infected group, no statistically significant differences were observed in patient background variables such as age, sex, body mass index, smoking history, and alcohol consumption. However, notable differences were observed in the prevalence of malignant disease, liver disease, and skin disease. Among the blood test parameters, significant differences were identified in the GFR and Δhemoglobin, whereas no significant differences were observed regarding the osteotomy site or operative time (Table 3).

A multivariate logistic regression analysis was subsequently performed, including variables with p-values < 0.1 in the univariate analysis—namely, malignant disease, liver disease, skin disease, GFR, and Δhemoglobin—as well as age and sex, which were included as essential patient background factors. The analysis findings showed statistically significant associations for skin disease, liver disease, and Δhemoglobin. To address concerns regarding sparse-data bias, the multivariable analysis was repeated using Firth’s penalized logistic regression. Skin disease, liver disease, and postoperative hemoglobin decline remained significantly associated with SSI (Table 4). A sensitivity analysis was then performed after excluding 12 knees that underwent osteotomy for valgus deformity. The results remained consistent, with skin disease, liver disease, and postoperative hemoglobin decline continuing to show significant associations with SSI (Table 5). To account for potential within-patient clustering, an additional sensitivity analysis was conducted by restricting the dataset to one knee per patient, retaining only the first-operated knee in patients who underwent bilateral procedures. Among the resulting 235 knees, SSI occurred in 9 knees (3.8%). Firth’s penalized logistic regression demonstrated findings that were consistent with the primary analysis, with skin disease, liver disease, and postoperative hemoglobin decline remaining significantly associated with SSI (Table 6).

Discussion

This multicenter retrospective study identified perioperative factors associated with SSI requiring reoperation after OAK. The principal findings were that preoperative skin disease, preoperative liver disease, and postoperative hemoglobin decline were significantly associated with the occurrence of SSI. These associations remained consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses, supporting the robustness of the findings. Nevertheless, given the limited number of infection events and the retrospective study design, the results should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

In this study, the incidence of SSI requiring reoperation was 4.0%, which is consistent with previously reported infection rates following OAK [10, 11, 20–29]. Furthermore, the reported infection rate of 4% should be interpreted as the incidence of severe SSI requiring reoperation, rather than overall SSI.

Large-scale registry-based and systematic studies have shown that although deep or reoperation-requiring infections post-OAK are relatively uncommon, they remain clinically important owing to their association with prolonged treatment, delayed bone union, implant-related complications, and compromised functional outcomes. The infection rate observed in this cohort therefore aligns with existing literature and supports the clinical relevance of identifying OAK-specific risk factors.

Among the identified factors, preoperative liver disease was shown to be significantly associated with SSI. Chronic liver disease is known to impair host immune function through mechanisms such as monocyte–macrophage dysfunction, altered complement activity, and increased bacterial translocation. In addition, coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, and hypoalbuminemia commonly associated with liver disease may adversely affect wound healing and increase susceptibility to postoperative infection [30–32]. In the context of OAK, which involves extensive bone cutting, implant fixation, and soft-tissue manipulation, these systemic vulnerabilities may further increase infection risk. These findings underscore the importance of careful preoperative evaluation and optimization of hepatic function when planning OAK.

Preoperative skin disease was also strongly associated with SSI in this cohort. Chronic inflammatory dermatologic conditions, including atopic dermatitis, eczema, and psoriasis, are characterized by impaired skin barrier function and increased colonization by Staphylococcus aureus [33]. Previous studies involving other orthopedic procedures, such as anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction and total knee arthroplasty, have reported an increased risk of postoperative infection in patients with these conditions [34, 35]. Given that OAK typically requires relatively long skin incisions and implant placement near the skin surface, compromised skin integrity may play a particularly important role. These findings suggest that preoperative dermatologic optimization and meticulous perioperative skin management may be particularly relevant for patients undergoing OAK.

Postoperative hemoglobin decline was identified as an associated factor for SSI. In the present cohort, no patients required perioperative blood transfusion, indicating that hemoglobin decline primarily reflected perioperative blood loss rather than transfusion-related effects. Rather than representing a direct causal factor, postoperative hemoglobin decline may function as a surrogate marker for perioperative bleeding, hematoma formation, or local tissue hypoxia, all of which may predispose patients to infection. Accordingly, this factor should be interpreted as an indicator for closer postoperative monitoring rather than as a modifiable preoperative risk factor. Tranexamic acid has been shown to reduce perioperative blood loss in other orthopedic procedures; however, its use was not standardized in this study, and the potential effect of antifibrinolytic therapy on SSI risk after OAK warrants further investigation [36]. In addition, although postoperative hemoglobin decline was associated with SSI, this variable was measured after surgery and does not represent a true preoperative risk factor. In the present cohort, hemoglobin values were obtained at one week postoperatively, which preceded the diagnosis of infection in all cases; however, this factor should be interpreted as a postoperative indicator rather than a predictor usable for preoperative risk stratification.

To address concerns regarding sparse-data bias and the limited number of SSI events, additional analyses were performed using Firth’s penalized logistic regression. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding osteotomy for valgus deformity and by restricting the dataset to one knee per patient to account for potential within-patient clustering. Across these analyses, the associations of preoperative skin disease, preoperative liver disease, and postoperative hemoglobin decline with SSI remained largely unchanged. These findings support the robustness of the identified associations despite the relatively small number of infection events. Nevertheless, the wide confidence intervals observed for some variables indicate residual statistical uncertainty, and the present findings should be interpreted as exploratory and require validation in larger cohorts.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective multicenter study, and the number of SSI events requiring reoperation was limited. Although Firth’s penalized logistic regression and multiple sensitivity analyses were performed to reduce sparse-data bias and assess the robustness of the findings, the relatively small number of events resulted in wide confidence intervals for some variables. Therefore, the identified associations should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive causal risk factors. In addition, external validation was not performed, and future studies with larger sample sizes are required to confirm the present findings.

Second, the unit of analysis was the knee rather than the individual patient, and some patients contributed bilateral cases. To address potential within-patient clustering, a sensitivity analysis restricted to one knee per patient was performed, and the principal findings remained largely unchanged. Similarly, sensitivity analysis excluding osteotomy for valgus deformity yielded consistent results. Nevertheless, because the cohort predominantly consisted of osteotomies for varus cases, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to populations with a higher proportion of osteotomy for valgus deformity.

Third, the postoperative follow-up period varied among patients, and some patients were followed for as little as three months. Although all infections requiring reoperation in this cohort were identified during the early postoperative period, late-onset infections after osteotomy have been reported. Therefore, the true incidence of SSI may have been underestimated.

Fourth, perioperative management was not completely standardized across participating institutions. For example, tranexamic acid administration was determined according to institutional or surgeon preference. Such differences may have influenced perioperative blood loss and postoperative hemoglobin decline, and residual confounding cannot be excluded.

Finally, postoperative hemoglobin decline was evaluated as a surrogate marker of perioperative blood loss and physiological stress. However, this parameter may also be influenced by factors such as preoperative anemia, perioperative fluid management, and other unmeasured variables. Therefore, the observed association should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct causal relationship between hemoglobin decline and SSI.

Conclusions

In this retrospective multicenter study, preoperative skin disease, preoperative liver disease, and postoperative hemoglobin decline were associated with an increased risk of SSI requiring reoperation after OAK. These associations remained consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses and may contribute to perioperative risk awareness and postoperative surveillance in patients undergoing OAK. However, given the limited number of infection events and the retrospective study design, the findings should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. Larger prospective studies with adequate event numbers and external validation are required to confirm these associations and to establish clinically applicable strategies for infection prevention and perioperative risk assessment.
