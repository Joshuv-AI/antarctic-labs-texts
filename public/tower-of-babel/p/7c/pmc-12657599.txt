Sexual health assessments, testing, and pre-exposure prophylaxis referrals among veterans with a sexually transmitted infection
Authors: Morgan Johnson, Amy Ratcliff, Linda Chia, Carol Callaway-Lane, Mary S. Dietrich, Deonni P. Stolldorf, Todd Hulgan, Christianne L. Roumie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDescribe sexual history completion among U.S. Veterans with a sexually transmitted infection (STI) and its association with number of ordered STI laboratory tests and offer of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP).MethodsWe assembled a retrospective cohort of Veterans with a STI seen at five sites in the southern United States and excluded those with diagnosis of HIV. We conducted a systematic chart review of sexual history using Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's five ‘Ps.’ The outcome was STI testing and PrEP offer. Charts were reviewed between December 2021 and May 2022.ResultsNo sexual history was documented in 43 % of the 278 encounters with differences across sites (p < 0.001). Missing documentation occurred for: partners (67 %); practices (93 %); protection (75 %); pregnancies (24 %, 14/58 females) and prior STIs (78 %). No association was observed between sexual history and complete STI testing (p = 0.42) but a positive association between sexual history components and individual STI tests, except HIV, was present (p ≤ 0.001). PrEP was offered to 1.4 % (4/278) of Veterans.ConclusionsLow sexual history documentation were associated with less STI tests ordered. PrEP referrals were low. Improvements in sexual history taking and PrEP offerings are essential.

Introduction

In the United States (U.S.), 27 million new sexually transmitted infections (STIs) are estimated annually and STI incidence continues to rise. (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2022; Weinstock et al., 2021) A recent publication by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reported that the Southeastern U.S. has the highest number of chlamydia, gonorrhea, and new human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) cases compared to the rest of the U.S (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2023; U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2020). Further, the Southeastern U.S. has the lowest pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP)-to-need ratio, indicating fewer PrEP users relative to the number of new HIV cases and need for PrEP (AIDSVu, 2020; Garner et al., 2018). Assessment of a sexual health history remains a key tool to identify likelihood of STIs and the need for PrEP discussion and utilization (NM et al., 2019; Douglas et al., 2021). The Veteran population, both male and female biological sex, have a greater likelihood of STIs than non-Veterans (Evans et al., 2017; Lehavot et al., 2002; Forkus et al., 2021). Previously the Veterans Health Administration (VHA) reported better PrEP usage among groups with higher likelihood of HIV acquisition compared to private retail pharamcies (Chartier et al., 2018). In 2021, the average PrEP usage was 74.9 per 100,000 nationally in VHA; however within five VHA medical centers in the southern U.S. the average was 45.9 per 100,000 Veterans, consistent with a systematic review and metanalysis' reporting low use of PrEP in the South (Kamitani et al., 1999).

The CDC recommends that each sexual health history assessment contain the five Ps (partners, practices, protection, pregnancy, and past history of STIs) as a clinical tool to navigate sexual health discussions (Reno et al., 2023). One VHA national study between 2015 and 2018 reported low rates of sexual history assessment for Veterans with a STI (Kobayashi et al., 2022a). To the authors' knowledge no previous publications have assessed associations between sexual history documentation and number of STI laboratory tests within the adult population.

Our aim was to assess the completeness of sexual health history assessment and its association with both number of STI testing ordered and PrEP discussions for individuals within five VHA sites located in the southern U.S.

Methods

Study design and population

The VHA Tennessee Valley Healthcare System institutional review board deemed this protocol a quality improvement project and ethical approval was exempt. We conducted a retrospective evaluation using data from a national dashboard for STI care within VHA. This systematic sample included individuals who had a positive STI result within the previous year. This patient group represents a population at elevated HIV acquisition risk and discussions with providers regarding PrEP should be reflected in the medical record.

Each positive STI case was defined as a positive laboratory test for gonorrhea, chlamydia, or syphilis from December 6, 2020, to December 6, 2021, at five Southeastern VHA sites across Tennessee and Kentucky. The date of the positive STI test was the index date. For each case we reviewed the electronic health record of the outpatient encounter. There were no instances where a Veteran had more than one STI during the study period.

We excluded Veteran encounters within Infectious Diseases clinic, any inpatient care, known HIV positive status, or Veterans with a positive rapid plasma regain syphilis titer with prior treatment and not a new infection.

Data collection

Chart review and abstraction occurred between December 2021 and May 2022. Data elements included: patient demographics, index STI organism and site, provider type seen for encounter (nurse, physician, or advanced practice provider [nurse practitioner or physician assistant]). Two reviewers (AR and MJ) reviewed cases to systematically evaluate documented sexual health histories at the index case diagnosis encounter or up to seven days after the index encounter.

Each reviewer evaluated if the CDC's five categories of sexual health (five Ps) were documented at the encounter (Reno et al., 2023). The five Ps are defined as partners, practices, protection, pregnancy, and past history of STIs. The project defined meeting each P based on a grading criterion (Table 1) where there was documentation of each individual element. Reviewers categorized sexual history taking as none (zero Ps), minimal (one or two Ps), moderate (three Ps), or full (four or five Ps). The primary outcome was a complete STI laboratory testing on or within 30 days of the index date. Complete STI testing was defined as completion of HIV, syphilis, gonorrhea and chlamydia testing. Additional data extracted from the electronic health record included the date and time of STI testing. The secondary outcome was documentation of PrEP discussion and offer of prophylaxis. To ensure data accuracy and consistency, each reviewer independently reviewed a sample of 18 cases. The inter-rater agreement of documented sexual health history was 94.4 %.

Statistical analysis

Frequency distributions summarized Veteran characteristics, laboratory data, and the extent of the sexual history completion by each of the five clinical sites. Differences among sites and documented sexual history were tested using Chi-square tests of independence (categorical variables) and Kruskal-Wallis test (continuous for age variable).

Separate logistic regression models assessed the association of the documented sexual history categories (none, minimal, moderate, full) with the complete STI panel and each of the four individual tests. Each of the logistic regression analyses included the following covariates: VHA clinical site, demographics, and provider type. We report the Wald Chi-Square statistics for the additive effect of the sexual history taken after considering the covariates, along with adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and 95 % confidence intervals (95 % CI). Exploratory analysis evaluated sexual health history documentation and the location of care for each Veteran. An alpha level of p ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 28) and STATA (version 18).

Documentation of the five Ps of sexual health history

At least one of the five Ps were documented for 57.6 % (n = 160) of the 278 positive STI encounters and 42.4 % (n = 118) of encounters did not have any sexual history documented. Minimal documentation of sexual history (one or two Ps) was present in 37.4 % (n = 104), moderate documentation (three Ps) in 15.1 % (n = 42), and full documentation of sexual history (four or five Ps) in only 5.0 % (n = 14). Missing documentation occurred for: partners (67.0 %, n = 186); sexual practices (93.2 %, n = 259); protection (74.1 %, n = 206); past STIs (73.0 %, n = 203) and pregnancies 24.6 % (n = 14 of the 57 females). As summarized in Table 2, females more frequently had some level of sexual history assessed than males (84.2 %, 48/57 vs. 50.7 %, 112/221, p < 0.001), Black/African Americans more frequently than White or Other (63.0 %, 85/135 vs. White: 56.8 %, 63/111, Other: 37.5 %, 12/32, p = 0.002), and if the encounter was with an advanced practice provider versus physician versus a nurse (Advanced Practice Provider 60.5 %, 52/86, physician 62.2 %, 92/148 vs. nurse 36.4 %, 16/44, p = 0.02). Of the 278 index STI cases, 48.5 % were chlamydia only (n = 135), 30.6 % (n = 85) gonorrhea only, 15.1 % (n = 42) syphilis, 5.8 % co-infection with chlamydia and gonorrhea (n = 16).

Primary outcome: complete STI evaluation and individual tests

Laboratory tests ordered and completed included gonorrhea (89.9 %, n = 250), chlamydia (89.6 %, n = 249), HIV (57.2 %, n = 159), and syphilis (39.6 %, n = 110). The complete panel with all four tests occurred for 72 (25.9 %) of the encounters. The combination of chlamydia and gonorrhea occurred for 89.6 % (n = 249) of encounters; chlamydia, gonorrhea, and syphilis 29.5 % (n = 82); HIV and syphilis only 32.0 % (n = 89).

We conducted the same analysis for each of the individual STI tests with differences noted among each individual test. There were significant statistical associations with extent of sexual history taken and each STI test (p ≤ 0.001) except for HIV (p = 0.95). Results demonstrated a reduced association with testing for syphilis and an increased odds of testing for chlamydia and gonorrhea. Compared to taking no history, Veterans with minimal and moderate/ full sexual history had an aOR 0.36 (95 % CI 0.19, 0.67) and aOR 0.32 (95 % CI 0.14, 0.70), respectively for syphilis testing. More sexual history completion increased the odds of chlamydia or gonorrhea testing (p ≤ 0.001). Compared to taking no history, Veterans with minimal and moderate/ full sexual history had an aOR 7.4 (95 % CI 2.07, 26.24) and aOR 8.0 (95 % CI 1.97, 32.28) for chlamydia testing. Veterans with minimal sexual history had increased odds of gonorrhea testing (aOR 8.5 (95 % CI 2.28, 31.35)). With moderate or full level of sexual history, compared to taking no history, the adjusted odds of gonorrhea testing was 12.6 (95 % CI 2.64, 60.11).

Location of care and PrEP offers

Among those who had complete STI panel laboratory testing (N = 72), the proportion who had no sexual history was 22.2 % in Emergency vs 54.0 % in clinic. Results evaluating minimal sexual history (44.4 % emergency vs 33.3 % clinic) and moderate/full history (33.4 % emergency vs 12.7 % clinic) consistently demonstrated higher proportion of complete sexual history obtained and complete testing for those Veterans evaluated in the emergency department/urgent care. The secondary outcome was PrEP offer. PrEP was offered in 1.4 % (4/278) of eligible Veterans and for 4/159 who were assessed for HIV (2.5 %).

Discussion

We report two main findings. First, the completion of a sexual health history was not associated with complete STI testing and this was primarily due to less testing for syphilis and HIV. While it is possible a comprehensive sexual health history was taken, but not documented, we postulate that these gaps are likely an accurate picture of practice. With the assumption that documentation reflects practice and that a positive STI in the previous six months is an indication to offer PrEP, there are missed opportunities to assess and inform individuals of the availability of PrEP services during a sexual health history discussion, prompting future improvement efforts. Second, we report substantial variability in both sexual health history and STI/ HIV testing by site within the Southeastern U.S.

Low adherence to national guidance in sexual health history taking has previously been reported in pediatrics, urgent care, and in U.S. Veterans (Douglas et al., 2021; Kobayashi et al., 2022a; Robertson et al., 2022; Liddon et al., 2022). Similar to our findings, one study found pediatric patients with a sexual history were seven times more likely to have STI testing ordered (Robertson et al., 2022). One national VHA report identified providers who were PrEP initiators had higher rates (80 % vs. 51 %) of obtaining a sexual history vs. non-PrEP initiators (Kobayashi et al., 2022b). Our study corroborates these findings by Kobayashi et al. and advances what is known in that we found this relationship holds true for gonorrhea and chlamydia STI testing not just HIV. We also report an inverse association and less syphilis testing. These results have implications not only for PrEP, but importantly for syphilis screening. The CDC recommends routine syphilis screening among sexually active individuals residing in counties where the syphilis rate is above 4.6 per 100,000. In areas of Tennessee, Kentucky, Alabama, South Carolina and Georgia the 2023 rates of syphilis average over 30 per 100,000 persons (with the highest rates for congenital syphilis). Therefore, increasing syphilis screening along with PrEP discussions should be an improvement goal as the odds of syphilis testing were lower than for other STIs such as chlamydia and gonorrhea.

Improving the sexual health history acquisition of patients is a critical need and multiple studies suggest patients are open to discussing sexual health with providers (Ryan et al., 2018; Fuzzell et al., 2016; Macdowall et al., 2010; Shepardson et al., 2021; Agochukwu-Mmonu et al., 2021). One VHA study reported that Veterans 45–64 years old were more likely to discuss sexual health only if they as the patient raise the topic; however, this is contrary to Veterans 18–44 and ≥ 65 years who noted that providers should initiate sexual health discussions (Shepardson et al., 2021).

Our second main finding noted differences in sexual history evaluation completeness at the five VHA sites. Clinicians perceived risk of STI acquisition in the Southern U.S. as low, which we postulate may impact acquisition of sexual history and utilization of STI co-testing (Ryan et al., 2018; Drumhiller et al., 2020). In a national case control evaluation across all VHA facilities of PrEP vs. non-PrEP initiators, only 5 % of Veterans with an STI had a complete sexual history documented; however, no evaluation of sexual history completeness was determined by U.S. region or site (Kobayashi et al., 2022b). Our results may signal potential gaps in clinicians perceived risk, assumptions, or stereotypes within Southeastern VHA facilities. According to data from AIDSVu, the Southeast U.S. accounts for more than half (53 %) of all new HIV diagnoses, leading to a greater likelihood for acquisition among persons who reside in these areas (AIDSVu, 2020). Veterans are more likely to engage in activities that are linked to STIs, suggesting that sexual health history screening should be an essential component of the general medical encounter for Veterans (Evans et al., 2017; Lehavot et al., 2002; Forkus et al., 2021). Our findings regarding sexual health history taking and STI testing support a call to action within VHA particularly in the Southern U.S. to standardize interventions to reduce variation in sexual health history assessment. If sexual health history was a standardized process, then providers would better match those who meet criteria for PrEP to an offer to PrEP prior to the acquisition of a STI.

Our study has limitations. We categorized sexual health history completeness based on electronic health record documentation which may not reflect the care provided at the individual encounter. Further, we only reviewed cases of positive STIs. Those who had negative STI test results may reflect the generalizable approach to STI testing and sexual health history assessment. The review of the positive cases reflects those at greatest need for a sexual history assessment and likely represents the best possible cases for a sexual history assessment and evaluation of complete STI laboratory testing. It is possible that Veterans may have refused specific STI testing (such as blood draw refusal for syphilis and HIV) and is a limitation of our study since refusal of specific laboratory testing data was not collected from provider notes. Our population for review is reflective of the demographics of Veterans seen for care within VHA, typically middle-aged males and therefore applicability of findings to the broader population may be limited, but it should be noted sexual healthcare recommendations are not age specific. This work did not assess sexual orientation and gender identity as this data was not systematically available. Future work is needed to assess provider barriers to documenting a sexual health history along with implementation of interventions to improve sexual health discussions such as annual discussions, system-wide standardized templates, order sets, and increased knowledge of PrEP availability and access. Additionally, future research should assess and address stigma and systemic biases that may be impacting sexual history assessment and counseling for PrEP.

Conclusion

Sexual health history assessments, comprehensive STI testing, and PrEP discussion and prescription are suboptimal among Veterans with a STI. Targeted interventions in the Southeastern U.S. region to improve sexual history taking, STI laboratory testing, and removal of barriers to HIV PrEP should be considered high priority for implementation in Veterans Health Administration clinics.
