Distribution and prognostic impact of LymphGen subtypes in Korean diffuse large B-cell lymphoma using clinical NGS panel∗†
Authors: Jeemin Yim, Sungyoung Lee, Sehui Kim, Bogyeong Han, Sojung Lim, Young Keun Song, Jeonghwan Youk, Jeong Mo Bae, Jiwon Koh, Cheol Lee, Young A Kim, Jin Ho Paik, Tae Min Kim, Hongseok Yun, Yoon Kyung Jeon

ABSTRACT
Key Points•The molecular subtypes of Korean DLBCL differ from those observed in Western populations.•Using a clinically targeted 121-gene NGS panel, the LymphGen classification system effectively stratified patients by prognosis.

Introduction

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is a heterogeneous disease characterized by diverse genetic alterations, biological behaviors, and clinical outcomes. Traditional molecular classifications, particularly the germinal center B-cell–like (GCB) and activated B-cell–like (ABC)/non-GCB types, are well-established in clinical practice and trials due to their prognostic relevance and association with distinct genetic mutations.1, 2, 3 In recent years, several molecular genetic classification systems for DLBCL have been proposed, including LymphGen classification, C1-C5 clusters, and the Haematological Malignancy Research Network system.4, 5, 6, 7 Although developed independently and using distinct analytical strategies, these genetic classification frameworks for DLBCL show substantial concordance and converge on similar biological themes at both the case-assignment and pathway levels, capturing recurrent modules such as EZH2/BCL2-driven GCB biology, B-cell receptor/NF-κB activation (eg, MYD88/CD79B), NOTCH pathway lesions with BCL6 rearrangements, TP53 disruption with aneuploidy, and immune-stromal programs. Each system offers the potential to improve risk stratification and guide precision medicine approaches for DLBCL.5,8, 9, 10 Among them, the LymphGen system, based on the work of Schmitz et al, categorizes DLBCL into MCD, BN2, N1, and EZB subtypes, and has since expanded to include A53 and ST2, thereby enhancing its prognostic and therapeutic applicability.4,5

Although several studies have explored the clinical utility of these molecular genetic classifications, and algorithms have been developed to further refine or simplify DLBCL molecular subtyping,11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 real-world validation using clinical-grade sequencing data, particularly from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples, remains limited. Moreover, harmonization across these classification systems is needed to standardize their use in clinical and research settings.17 Importantly, the landscape of DLBCL genetic subtypes across different populations remains incompletely characterized.

The genetic subtypes of DLBCL are closely associated with the cell of origin (COO).6,18 According to the LymphGen algorithm, the MCD and N1 subtypes are primarily related to the ABC/non-GCB type, whereas the EZB and ST2 subtypes are predominantly linked to the GCB type.4,5 Notably, the distribution of COO subtypes differs between Western and Eastern populations. In Eastern populations, the ABC/non-GCB type comprises up to 60% of DLBCL cases, whereas in Western populations, the GCB type can account for as much as 63%.19, 20, 21 These differences were validated in a large real-world Japanese cohort study of DLBCL.22 The EZB subtype, predominantly GCB, is the most common genetic subtype identified in North American DLBCL cohorts.5

Although most cases of DLBCL arise de novo, a subset develops through high-grade transformation of indolent low-grade BCLs.23 Notably, each DLBCL genetic subtype often exhibits biological and genetic similarities to other (often low-grade) lymphoma entities. For example, the EZB subtype shares key genetic features with follicular lymphoma.5,8 Follicular lymphoma accounts for ∼30% of non-Hodgkin lymphomas in North America and Western Europe but <10% in East Asian populations, including Korea.24, 25, 26

These differences underscore the likelihood that the landscape of DLBCL genetic subtypes varies across populations. Consequently, there is a critical need for population-specific validation of existing molecular classification systems to ensure their clinical relevance and applicability in diverse demographic settings.

The LymphGen subtypes can be identified using a probabilistic classification algorithm on a publicly accessible server that uses targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) data.5 Our institution developed and implemented a custom clinical NGS panel targeting 121 lymphoma-related genes.27, 28, 29 Using this panel and the LymphGen classifier, we characterized Korean DLBCL genetic subtypes and evaluated their associations with clinicopathological features and prognosis.

Materials and methods

Patients

A total of 239 patients with LBCL diagnosed at Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH) between 2015 and 2023 were included in this study (supplemental Figure 1). All clinical and pathological features were thoroughly reviewed. Among these patients, 123 were diagnosed with DLBCL, not otherwise specified (DLBCL-NOS), 77 with primary DLBCL of the central nervous system (PCNS-DLBCL), 3 with primary mediastinal LBCL, 1 with T-cell/histiocyte-rich LBCL, 2 with intravascular LBCL, 7 with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)–positive DLBCL in immunocompetent individuals, and 6 with immunodeficiency-associated EBV-positive DLBCL. Among the latter, 4 cases occurred in a posttransplant setting and 2 were associated with therapy-related immunodeficiency. Additionally, 20 patients were classified as having secondary DLBCL, having developed the disease after, or concomitant with, a low-grade lymphoma.

Of 123 patients with DLBCL-NOS, 117 received R-CHOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, Oncovin [vincristine], and prednisone) therapy. Two patients were treated with R-dmCODOX-M/IVAC (rituximab plus dose-modified CODOX-M [cyclophosphamide, vincristine, doxorubicin, and methotrexate] alternating with IVAC [ifosfamide, etoposide, and cytarabine]), a regimen typically used for Burkitt-like lymphomas. Three patients (1 in each trial) participated in clinical trials involving tafasitamab, polatuzumab vedotin/mosunetuzumab, and polatuzumab vedotin/R-CHP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, prednisone). One patient was transferred to a nursing facility without treatment. Clinical data and treatment outcomes were obtained from the medical records by 2 oncologists (J. Youk and T.M.K.). This study was approved by the institutional review board of SNUH (approval no. 1506-080-681), and the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Immunohistochemistry and ISH for EBER

Immunohistochemistry was performed on FFPE tissue sections using the antibodies and staining methods outlined in supplemental Table 1. EBV in situ hybridization (ISH) was performed with either the Bond ready-to-use ISH EBV-encoded RNA (EBER) probe (Leica Biosystems, Newcastle, United Kingdom) or the Inform EBER probe (Ventana Medical Systems, Tucson, AZ). The procedure was conducted with the Bond-Max autostainer (Leica Microsystems) and the Ventana Benchmark XT automated system (Ventana Medical Systems), following the manufacturers’ instructions. The COO of DLBCL was determined using the Hans algorithm.30 MYC and BCL2 double expression (DE) was defined as the expression of both MYC in ≥40% of tumor cells and BCL2 in ≥50% of tumor cells.18,31

Targeted NGS panel design

The FiRST lymphoma panel (FLP) was developed at SNUH to capture all coding exons and untranslated regions of 121 lymphoma-related genes with ±50–base pair flanks and was subsequently used for clinical NGS testing as previously reported,27, 28, 29 and described in the supplemental Methods and Results. To enable detection of hallmark noncoding events without whole-gene intronic tiling, selected intronic hot spots were included at loci such as the BCL2 major breakpoint region/minor cluster region vicinity, BCL6 promoter and intron 1, MYC promoter and intron 1, the CD274 promoter-proximal intron, and a SYK hot spot (supplemental Data 3). Panel versions were as follows: version 1.0 (120 DNA genes), version 1.1 (with HAVCR2 added for 121 DNA genes), and version 1.2 (121 DNA genes plus 151 RNA fusion targets). The current version of the panel (FLP version 1.2) comprises 121 DNA genes and 151 RNA genes (supplemental Table 2). Sequencing data were obtained from 239 FFPE cases: 3 using version 1.0, 159 using version 1.1, and 77 using version 1.2. Among the DLBCL-NOS cases, 2 used version 1.0, 109 used version 1.1, and 12 used version 1.2.

Targeted NGS sequencing

All targeted NGS assays used FFPE tissue samples. At least 50 ng of genomic DNA was extracted from each FFPE block using the Maxwell FFPE purification kit (Promega, Madison, WI). Library preparation was performed with the SureSelect XT-HS target enrichment system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). DNA quality and quantity were assessed with the 4200 TapeStation System (Agilent Technologies). Paired-end sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NextSeq 550Dx Platform (Illumina Inc, San Diego, CA).

Sequencing QC

Sequencing quality control (QC) metrics were calculated using Picard (Broad Institute, Picard toolkit, 2019; GitHub repository: https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/), SAMtools,32 and CNVkit,33 following the framework described by Dreval et al.34 Metrics included total and aligned read counts, on-target rate, unique alignment rate, corrected mean on-target depth, percentage of targets ≥30× coverage, percentage of 0× targets, and Fold-80 base penalty as well as AT and GC dropout (supplemental Data 1 and 2).

Bioinformatics analysis

For bioinformatic processing, reads were trimmed and aligned to the GRCh37/hg19 reference using Burrows-Wheeler aligner–maximal exact matches35,36 followed by local realignment with Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) Lite as part of the GATK best practices workflow.37 Single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and INDELs were identified through a consensus pipeline (SNVer,38 LoFreq,39 and VarDict40) with internal recount harmonization and standardized filtering criteria. FFPE-related artifacts were controlled by a panel of normals, an empirically curated blacklist, and orientation-bias modeling. Copy number variations (CNVs) were inferred using CNVkit, ABSOLUTE41 and an in-house R-based module implementing GC-content/tags per million-based correction, pooled-normal baselines, and purity-scaled segmentation. Structural variants were detected with Manta and in-house postprocessing workflow and were validated through RNA fusion analysis (STAR-Fusion/Arriba). Variant annotation was performed with the aid of SnpEff42 and ANNOVAR.43 To reduce the contribution of potential germ line variants, variants with allele frequencies of ≥0.1% in the Genome Aggregation Consortium East Asian Database,44 Korean Reference Genome Database,45 or Korean Variant Archive46 were flagged as putative germ line and deprioritized during expert review; variants below this threshold were considered candidate somatic events for downstream analyses. All variant classes were visually confirmed by dual review in an expert curation system to ensure clinical traceability. Detailed algorithmic parameters are described in the supplemental Methods and Results and supplemental Figures 2 and 4-5.

LymphGen classification

The mutation and copy number profiles of each sample were processed using the LymphGen algorithm (https://llmpp.nih.gov/lymphgen/index.php), a machine-learning–based classifier designed to stratify samples into distinct genetic subtypes. This algorithm uses a predefined set of driver gene mutations, CNVs, and structural variants to classify samples into 6 subtypes: MCD, BN2, N1, EZB, ST2, and A53. For this study, mutation data and CNV profiles were analyzed using LymphGen version 2.0. The prediction of the A53 subtype was based on gene-level CNV data from the targeted sequencing panel, because chromosomal arm-level data were not included. To enhance classification precision, cases were further stratified by confidence scores. Cases with scores of ≥0.9 were designated as core group members, representing high-confidence classifications, whereas those with scores between 0.5 and 0.89 were designated as extended group members, indicating moderate-confidence classifications. Cases with scores of <0.5 or ambiguous classifications were categorized as “other.”

External benchmarking

To evaluate the robustness of LymphGen classification using the FLP, we benchmarked our panel-based subtype calls against the publicly available Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC) DLBCL data set.47 SNV, INDEL, and CNV data were downloaded from cBioPortal (study identifier: mbn_msk_2024), and cytogenetic translocation data (BCL2, BCL6, MYC) were obtained directly from the authors. We reran the LymphGen algorithm restricted to the genomic features covered by the FLP and compared the resulting subtype assignments with those derived from the full MSKCC-Integrated Mutation Profiling Actionable Cancer Targets (IMPACT) panel. Performance metrics including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score were calculated across all overlapping cases and within the high-confidence (“core”) subset (confidence of ≥0.9). The detailed workflow and evaluation criteria are described in the supplemental Methods and Results.

Automated quantification of tumor-infiltrating immune cell numbers via image analysis

To evaluate the status of tumor-infiltrating immune cells across the LymphGen subtypes, tissue microarrays were constructed using 2-mm cores from the intratumoral areas of 54 DLBCL-NOS samples and subjected to immunohistochemistry with the antibodies and staining methods listed in supplemental Table 1. To ensure objective and reproducible data during the analysis of tumor-infiltrating immune cells, quantification was automated using an image analyzer. Immunohistochemically stained slides were scanned at high magnification with the ScanScope CS2 eSlide system (Aperio Technologies, Vista, CA). Aperio ImageScope software (Aperio Technologies) automatically quantified the densities of CD3+, CD4+, CD8+, and FOXP3+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes per square millimeter, as well as the positive pixel counts for both CD68 and CD163 per square millimeter. Major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and II expression was visually assessed and scored on a scale from 0 to 3.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM, Armonk, NY). The LymphGen distributions among different DLBCL cohorts and various entities were statistically analyzed using the Pearson χ2 test or the Fisher exact test, with the strengths of associations quantified by calculating the ϕ-coefficient or Cramér V coefficient. The associations between clinicopathological features and LymphGen subtypes were compared using the Fisher exact test. Survival data analyses were performed using the Kaplan-Meier method with the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate survival analyses used Cox proportional hazards modeling with backward elimination. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and the significance level was set to .05. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. A result was considered statistically significant if the P value was <.05 or the false discovery rate (FDR) was <0.05 if applicable.

Results

Sequencing quality and performance of bioinformatics workflow

Sequencing of 239 FFPE-derived lymphoma samples using the FLP achieved consistently high performance across panel versions (version 1.0 to version 1.2). The median corrected coverage was 465× (interquartile range, 378-576×), the median percentage of target bases covered at ≥30× was 91.7%, and the median duplicate rate was 1.3%. Quality metrics were highly uniform across samples and versions, and no significant differences in QC distributions were observed between LymphGen-classified and unclassified (other) groups (all P ≥ .154), indicating that unclassified (other) status reflects intrinsic biological heterogeneity rather than sequencing artifacts (supplemental Data 1 and 2).

FFPE- and oxidation-related artifacts were effectively suppressed through a pooled panel of normals, an empirically curated recurrent-artifact blacklist, and GATK orientation-bias modeling. Independent reanalysis with FilterByOrientationBias, a GATK module that models and filters sequencing artifacts caused by oxidative DNA damage and strand-specific errors, confirmed the complete removal of strand-specific oxidation artifacts, leaving only a single residual false-positive C>T event, which was excluded from the final data set (supplemental Data 6). Comprehensive QC metrics and per-sample variant data sets are provided in supplemental Data 1-5.

External benchmarking of LymphGen classification

Benchmarking against the MSKCC DLBCL cohort demonstrated that panel-limited implementation of LymphGen classification using the FLP achieved high crossplatform reproducibility. Across 396 overlapping cases, overall concordance was high (accuracy, 0.96; sensitivity, 0.80; specificity, 0.97), with no systematic bias across the 6 major subtypes (MCD, EZB, BN2, ST2, N1, and A53). Discrepancies were largely confined to “composite” and “other” categories, which reflect biologically ambiguous or low-confidence cases rather than technical errors. Within the high-confidence subset (confidence ≥0.9; n = 145), accuracy further increased to 0.99 with perfect recall across all major subtypes (supplemental Figure 3).

The distribution of LymphGen subtypes varied across different DLBCL entities (Table 1). DLBCL-NOS samples (n = 123) were classified as MCD (25.2%), A53 (14.6%), ST2 (7.3%), BN2 (5.7%), EZB (3.3%), EZB/ST2 composite (1.6%), and unclassified (other) (42.3%). Patients with PCNS-DLBCL (n = 77) were classified as MCD (70.1%), composite MCD/A53 (2.6%), A53 (3.9%), BN2 (2.6%), ST2 (1.3%), EZB (1.3%), and other (18.2%). Among patients with EBV-positive DLBCL (n = 7), ST2 was the most common (42.9%). However, all patients with immunodeficiency-associated EBV-positive DLBCL (n = 6) were unclassified (other). In the patients with intravascular LBCL (n = 2), 1 was classified as MCD and the other as unclassified (other), whereas all patients with primary mediastinal LBCLs (n = 3) were unclassified (other). N1 subtype was not detected in our cohort.

The distribution of LymphGen subtypes significantly differed between DLBCL-NOS and PCNS-DLBCL cases on one hand and patients with EBV-positive DLBCL on the other (supplemental Figure 7; Table 2). The strengths of association were quantified using Cramér V coefficient and the ϕ-coefficient (Table 2). Analysis of the ϕ-coefficient revealed that MCD was strongly associated with PCNS-DLBCL, whereas A53 and ST2 were more associated with DLBCL-NOS (Table 2). The LymphGen status of secondary DLBCLs varied based on the underlying low-grade lymphoma (supplemental Table 3). Notably, 9 (60%) of 15 patients with DLBCL secondary to follicular lymphoma were classified as EZB (n = 7) or composite EZB (1 EZB/A53 and 1 EZB/ST2). Of 3 patients with DLBCL arising from marginal zone lymphomas, 1 was classified as BN2, and 1 with DLBCL developing from lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma was classified as MCD.

The patterns of LymphGen subtypes observed in systemic and PCNS-DLBCL entities, as well as the underlying low-grade lymphomas, align with previously reported results.5,8 These results demonstrate that our clinical NGS panel provides a crucial foundation for applying the LymphGen algorithm, highlighting its reliable classification performance even with limited genomic data.

Clinical features of DLBCL-NOS by the LymphGen subtypes

The clinical features of 123 patients with DLBCL-NOS, categorized by LymphGen subtypes, are summarized in supplemental Table 4 and supplemental Figure 8. Patients with the A53 and EZB subtypes tended to be younger than those with other subtypes. The revised International Prognostic Index (R-IPI) score differed among the LymphGen subtypes. The proportion of patients in the poor R-IPI risk group was higher in those with the MCD subtype. Patients with the EZB subtype were more likely to have DLBCL of GCB origin than those with other subtypes. No other significant differences in clinical features were observed between the LymphGen subtypes.

LymphGen distribution based on the COO and DE status of DLBCL-NOS

A Sankey diagram illustrates the distribution of LymphGen subtypes among 123 cases of DLBCL-NOS, classified by the COO and MYC/BCL2 DE status (Figure 2B). The LymphGen distribution by the COO is shown in Figure 2C. The EZB subtype was strongly associated with GCB-DLBCL (GCB vs non-GCB, 10.5% vs 2.4%; ϕ-coefficient, 0.175). In contrast, MCD was moderately associated with non-GCB DLBCL (GCB vs non-GCB, 15.8% vs 29.4%; ϕ-coefficient, −0.145; Figure 2C).

The LymphGen distribution varied significantly between DE DLBCL and non-DE DLBCL (Figure 2D). MCD showed a moderate association with DE DLBCL (DE vs non-DE, 34.3% vs 21.6%; ϕ-coefficient, 0.132). In contrast, EZB was moderately associated with non-DE DLBCL (DE vs non-DE, 0% vs 6.8%; ϕ-coefficient, −0.143), and ST2 showed a moderate association with non-DE DLBCL (DE vs non-DE, 2.9% vs 9.1%; ϕ-coefficient, −0.108; Figure 2D). When comparing the genetic alterations between the DE and non-DE groups, the DE group exhibited nominally higher rates of CD79B, MYD88, and TET3 mutations (P < .05), although none remained significant after FDR correction (FDR, 0.96; supplemental Figure 9). Conversely, BCL6 mutations were more common in the non-DE group (P = .065; FDR, 0.96). Additionally, copy number gains in BCL2 (P < .05; FDR, 0.58), MALT1 (P < .05; FDR, 0.57), PDCD1LG2 (P < .05; FDR, 0.57), CD274 (P < .05; FDR, 0.57), and CD79A (P < .05; FDR, 0.58) were more frequent in DE DLBCLs compared with non-DE DLBCLs (supplemental Figure 9).

The prognostic significance of individual gene mutations was also analyzed in patients treated with R-CHOP (supplemental Figure 13). Patients with mutations in TP53, HIST1H1E, and CD79A exhibited significantly poorer OS, whereas mutations in BTG1 and KMT2D showed a trend toward worse survival. In contrast, IRF8, TNFRSF14, and FOXO1 mutations were linked to better survival. Patients with copy number losses in INO80, XPO1, and SMARCA2 exhibited poorer OS. Conversely, gains in TRAF2, NOTCH1, and BCL6 were associated with worse OS.

The immune microenvironment of the LymphGen subtypes

Representative immunohistochemical images of key immune cell subsets and MHC expression, as well as their levels across LymphGen subtypes, are shown in supplemental Figures 14 and 15. A marginally significant difference in the density of CD4+ T cells was observed across the LymphGen subtypes (P = .077), with the MCD subtype showing lower CD4+ cell counts per square millimeter and with the EZB subtype exhibiting higher counts (supplemental Figure 15A). No notable differences were observed in the densities of tumor-infiltrating CD3+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, FOXP3+ regulatory T cells, CD68+ macrophages, CD163+ M2 macrophages, or in the CD163+:CD68+ macrophage ratio among the LymphGen subtypes. MHC class I expression showed a trend toward lower levels in patients with the EZB subtype than in those with other subtypes (P = .086; supplemental Figure 15B).

Discussion

This study classified Korean DLBCL cases using an institutionally developed 121-gene clinical NGS lymphoma panel in conjunction with the LymphGen classifier, enabling a comprehensive analysis of the distinct clinicopathological features and prognostic significance associated with each genetic subtype. The LymphGen algorithm successfully categorized ∼58% of systemic DLBCL cases and 82% of PCNS-DLBCL cases in our cohort. These classification rates are comparable with the 55% reported in a previous DLBCL cohort study conducted by the MSKCC, which used a broader 400-gene clinical NGS panel.47 Benchmarking against the MSKCC-IMPACT data set confirmed high reproducibility and absence of systematic bias, supporting the robustness and crossplatform transferability of the algorithm even when applied to a smaller clinical panel. Sequencing QC metrics demonstrated high data uniformity across all panel versions, and no QC differences were observed between LymphGen-classified and unclassified (other) groups, confirming that the unclassified status reflects biological heterogeneity rather than sequencing artifacts.

Our findings revealed notable differences in the distribution of DLBCL genetic subtypes between the Korean cohort and Western populations. Unlike Western cohorts, in which the EZB subtype is predominant, the MCD subtype was more prevalent in our Korean cohort. This overrepresentation of the MCD subtype and underrepresentation of EZB may reflect the higher proportions of non-GCB/ABC-type DLBCL observed in Asian populations.19, 20, 21 The EZB subtype is genetically linked to follicular lymphoma5,8 and, in line with this, most secondary DLBCLs in patients with follicular lymphoma in our study were classified as EZB. Follicular lymphoma has historically been less common in Korean populations compared with Western populations,26 which may, partly, account for the reduced frequency of the EZB subtype observed in our Korean DLBCL cohort. However, given the divergent evolution of IGH::BCL2–primed precursors into FL and EZB-DLBCL, the lower incidence of EZB-DLBCL observed in the Korean population may also be attributable to lower frequency of IGH::BCL2–primed precursors in East Asians.49

Recent genetic studies of DLBCL in Japanese and Chinese cohorts12,14 were compared with findings from our Korean cohort. Although direct comparisons are limited due to differences in disease entities, classification algorithms, and gene panels, several notable distinctions in the genetic subtypes were observed. In the Japanese cohort of 177 patients, including 20 with immunodeficiency-associated lymphoproliferative disorders, the analysis of 144 genes classified patients into MYD88 (31%), NOTCH2 (5%), BCL2 (corresponding to the EZB; 13%), SGK1 (corresponding to ST2; 9%), and composite (6%) subtypes.14 In contrast, analysis of the Chinese cohort of 1011 patients using a 38-gene panel identified TP53Mut (15%), BN2-like (11%), MCD-like (10%), ST2-like (7%), EZB-like (6%), and N1-like (5%) subgroups.12 The predominance of MYD88 subtype in the Japanese cohort and the TP53Mut subgroup in the Chinese cohort align with findings from our Korean cohort, in which MCD and A53 subtypes were most prevalent. However, other subtype distributions differed. These findings highlight the significant influence of ethnic and geographic factors on the molecular subtype distribution of DLBCL. A key strength of our study is the application of the LymphGen algorithm, which generated subtype-specific mutation profiles that are largely consistent with those reported in previous studies. This underscores the robustness and broad applicability of the LymphGen classification system across diverse populations and clinical settings.

The distributions of LymphGen subtypes varied among distinct DLBCL entities. Notably, most (70.1%) PCNS-DLBCL cases were classified as the MCD or composite MCD subtypes, consistent with previous reports.5,50 Although EBV-positive DLBCL harbors genetic mutations distinct from those of EBV-negative DLBCL,51, 52, 53, 54, 55, 56 the molecular classification of EBV-positive DLBCL remains incompletely defined.52 In this study, EBV-positive DLBCL was frequently classified as the ST2 subtype, at a significantly higher rate than in DLBCL-NOS. The ST2 subtype was characterized by deregulated JAK/STAT signaling.5 A previous study on EBV-positive HIV-associated DLBCL reported recurrent mutations in genes involved in the JAK/STAT3 pathway.53 In contrast, all 6 cases of immunodeficiency-associated EBV-positive DLBCL in our cohort were unclassified by LymphGen. These findings indicate the need for further investigations to define the molecular subtypes of EBV-positive DLBCL.

Our findings emphasize the prognostic significance of the genetic subtypes in DLBCL. In this study, the MCD and A53 subtypes (or MCD, A53, and BN2 subtypes) were associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes. This association was especially pronounced when LymphGen classification was integrated with MYC/BCL2 DE status. Patients classified under the MCD/A53/BN2 subtypes were more frequently categorized into higher-risk R-IPI groups and demonstrated poorer prognosis. Identifying these high-risk molecular subtypes has potential therapeutic implications, suggesting that patients harboring these molecular profiles may benefit from intensified or targeted treatment strategies.

Although the number of patients with the EZB subtype was limited, we observed reduced MHC class I expression in patients with DLBCL-NOS within this subtype. This finding is partially supported by a previous report indicating that EZH2 mutations, characteristic of the EZB subtype, can induce MHC deficiency in DLBCL.48 Additionally, lower CD4+ cell infiltration was noted in patients with the MCD subtype. These observations support the idea that immune evasion mechanisms may vary among DLBCL subtypes,57,58 highlighting the need to integrate immune profiling into the molecular classification of DLBCL to better understand the interactions between lymphoma cells and the tumor microenvironment.

This study has several limitations. First, although the cohort size was not small, it was not large enough to allow us to draw definitive conclusions. None of the 4 EZB-type and 2 EZB/ST2-type DLBCL-NOS cases harbored MYC rearrangements, precluding any comparison between EZB-MYC–positive and EZB-MYC–negative tumors, which are known to have distinct genetic and prognostic characteristics.5 Second, our analysis focused primarily on systemic DLBCL-NOS, and the findings may not be generalizable to other LBCL entities, such as PCNS-DLBCL. Third, although we used the LymphGen classifier for molecular subtyping, additional validation using other molecular classification algorithms could provide further insight. Fourth, the targeted panel lacked complete arm-level CNV and noncoding region coverage, possibly underestimating A53- or aberrant somatic hypermutation (aSHM)-related lesions. Moreover, the “other” category remains biologically heterogeneous and will require multiomic approaches to define potential novel subclasses. Lastly, we did not conduct an in-depth analysis of the unclassified group, which may be necessary to identify novel subtypes or therapeutic targets.

In summary, the molecular classification of Korean patients with DLBCL revealed distinct genetic distributions compared with those observed in Western populations. The LymphGen subtype classification was successfully implemented using a clinically targeted NGS panel, and patients with MCD/A53 or MCD/A53/BN2 subtypes were identified as having a poor prognosis. These findings support the clinical utility of LymphGen for guiding personalized treatment strategies in DLBCL, highlighting the need for continued refinement of molecular classification approaches to better capture the heterogeneity of DLBCL across diverse populations.
