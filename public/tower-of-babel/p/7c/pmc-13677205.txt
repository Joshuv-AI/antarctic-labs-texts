Overconfluent culture promotes cell cycle synchronization in mouse embryonic stem cells for efficient cloned embryo production
Authors: Dinh Quoc PHAM, Shogo MATOBA, Satoshi FUNAYA, Michiko HIROSE, Fumio IKE, Ayako ISOTANI, Narumi OGONUKI, Masahito IKAWA, Nguyen VAN THUAN, Atsuo OGURA, Kimiko INOUE
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMouse embryonic stem cell (ESC) cloning via nuclear transfer is a potential platform for direct generation of genetically modified mice. However, the low G1 cell population in ESCs hampers their broader use in nuclear transfer, as S-phase donors induce 1-cell arrest in reconstructed oocytes. This study aimed to enrich the G1-phase population of ESCs to improve ESC-based cloning efficiency. We demonstrated that EB3 ESCs derived from the 129/Ola strain and C57BL/6×129/Sv hybrid ESCs exhibited superior traits for nuclear transfer in terms of cleavage rate and blastocyst formation but showed reduced efficiency in development to term. Transcriptomic analyses revealed that this failure was likely due to aberrant imprinted gene expression in critical clusters, such as H19-Igf2 or Dlk1-Dio3. In contrast, although cloned offspring were successfully generated at a promising rate (approximately 6%) using EGR-R01 (BDF1×129/Sv) ESCs, early cleavage to the 2-cell embryo was inefficient. To address this, we induced cell cycle synchronization by culturing ESCs to overconfluency, which markedly increased the G1 proportion, as confirmed via FACS analysis. This alteration led to a significant improvement in first cleavage efficiency without compromising the developmental potential of ESC-derived cloned embryos to live birth. Overall, our study highlights the importance of both cell cycle synchronization and imprinting maintenance in improving ESC cloning efficiency to facilitate broad applications, including the direct generation of genetically modified mice.Keywords: Cell cycle synchronization, Genomic imprinting, Mouse embryonic stem cells, Somatic cell nuclear transfer

Somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT) is the only reproductive technique that can generate cloned offspring from a single nucleus. To date, SCNT has been successfully applied to clone more than 20 mammalian species, including sheep [1], cattle [2], mice [3], and macaque monkeys [4], significantly facilitating several applications in genome conservation, including the generation of genetically modified animals to manufacture recombinant human proteins or bio-organs for human transplantation [5,6,7,8,9].

In mouse cloning, the commonly used donor cell types can be classified into undifferentiated embryonic stem cells (ESCs) [10] and differentiated somatic cells, including cumulus and Sertoli cells [3, 11]. Among these, ESCs are generally considered effective for nuclear transfer [12, 13] because of their pluripotency, which enables them to give rise to all embryonic lineages [14, 15]. ESCs also exhibit a globally active transcriptional profile [16] and possess a hyperdynamic open chromatin structure [17], which collectively promote more efficient reprogramming than differentiated somatic cells [18]. However, to date, only a few studies have attempted to optimize their culture conditions for SCNT, and efficient procedures to improve ESC cloning outcomes are still not fully understood.

Cell cycle synchronization is considered critical for cloning success, as nuclear–cytoplasmic compatibility at the time of nuclear transfer influences reprogramming ability [19]. Unlike cumulus cells, which are mostly in the G0/G1 phase [20], the much lower proportion of G0/G1 cells and the rapidly proliferative status of mouse ESCs limit their practical application for SCNT [19, 21], as S-phase donors inevitably lead to 1-cell arrest of reconstructed oocytes in mice. In previous studies, synchronization of the cell cycle using technical strategies such as serum starvation [10] or high confluency (80–90% cell confluence; hereafter referred to as normal confluency, NC) culture [22] was commonly used to enhance the efficiency of ESC-based cloning. However, such methods led to only slight improvements, and the G0/G1 proportions were not increased [22]. Therefore, the rapid proliferation of ESCs may be a barrier to successful cloning, and increasing the population of cells in the G0/G1 phase may better facilitate ESC-based cloning applications.

In addition to cell cycle constraints, maintaining genomic imprinting of donor cells also plays a crucial role in ESC-based cloning efficiency. Imprinted genes are tightly regulated via DNA methylation at imprinting control regions and exhibit monoallelic expression patterns [23]. Previous studies have shown that prolonged in vitro culture and suboptimal conditions lead to aberrant expression of imprinted genes [24, 25] and that such defects compromise proper embryonic development after nuclear transfer [26]. However, an effective method to synchronize ESCs at the G0/G1 phase without affecting their genomic imprinting status for nuclear transfer cloning remains elusive.

In this study, we attempted to enrich the G0/G1 population of ESCs by culturing them to overconfluency (> 90% cell confluence; hereafter referred to as OC). In contrast to the reported synchronization methods for ESCs, our approach alters the cell cycle distribution and increases the number of G0/G1 cells. Using various ESC lines as donors for mouse cloning, we demonstrated that this approach significantly improves cleavage rates for efficient 2-cell embryo production and enables the full-term development of ESC-derived cloned embryos. Therefore, our method may have broad applications, such as the production of genetically modified mice, including those carrying knock-in alleles.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Female B6D2F1 (BDF1; SLC, Shizuoka, Japan) mice (8–12 weeks old) were used for oocyte collection and as nuclear donors. Female ICR mice (8–12 weeks old; CLEA Japan, Tokyo, Japan) served as embryo transfer recipients or foster mothers. Female C57BL/6N (SLC) and male 129/Sv mice (RBRC00002; RIKEN BRC, Tsukuba, Japan) were used for the establishment of the B6×129/Sv ESC line. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals established by the Ministry of the Environment of Japan and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the RIKEN Tsukuba Branch. All mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions with a controlled light cycle (lights on from 0700 h to 2100 h).

ESC culture

Mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) derived from BALB/c (CLEA Japan) mice were treated with mitomycin C (M0503-2MG; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), cryopreserved, and used as feeder cells. All ESC lines were cultured at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. All cell lines used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Approximately 4 × 104 mitomycin C-treated MEFs were initially seeded/coated onto culture dishes before ESC seeding. Because mitomycin C treatment prevented DNA replication and cell division, the number of feeder cells remained unchanged throughout the culture. ESC continuously proliferate, and the cell number typically exceeds 2 × 106 at the time of ESC harvesting. Thus, MEFs represent less than 2% of the total cell population. Before FACS or RNA-seq analyses, the harvested cells were transferred onto gelatin-coated dishes for approximately 2 h to allow tight attachment and removal of MEFs. The supernatant enriched for ESCs with a minimal number of MEFs was collected by gentle pipetting.

EGR-R01 ([CAG-mtDsRed2]BDF1×129/Sv) ESCs, provided by the Research Institute for Microbial Diseases, Osaka University [27], were cultured on mitomycin C-treated MEF feeder layers in KnockOut Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (10829018; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ES-009-B; Merck), 1× sodium pyruvate (NaPy; 11360070; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1× MEM non-essential amino acids (NEAA; 11140050; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1× GlutaMAX supplement (32571036; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1× nucleosides (adenosine [A4036], guanosine [G6264], cytidine [C4654], uridine [U3003], and thymidine [T1895]; Merck), 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (2-ME; M7522; Merck), and 103 U/ml leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF; ESG1107; Merck). This cell line was selected for this study because it has been previously confirmed to exhibit tetraploid complementation, which demonstrates its developmental potential to term. In addition, EGR-R01 carries a CAG-mtDsRed2 transgene, which enables the convenient evaluation of direct ESC-derived offspring production and germline transmission of genetically modified mice by SCNT.

B6×129/Sv ESCs, originally established and maintained in our laboratory, were cultured under feeder-free conditions in KnockOut Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 15% KnockOut Serum Replacement (10828028; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1× NEAA, 1× GlutaMAX supplement, 0.1 mM 2-ME, 103 U/ml LIF supplement, 3 μM CHIR99021 (04-0004; Reprocell Inc., Kanagawa, Japan), and 1 μM PD0325901 (04-0006; Reprocell Inc.).

EB3 (129/Ola) ESCs, provided by the RIKEN BioResource Research Center (AES0139) [28, 29], were cultured under feeder-free conditions in Glasgow’s MEM (11710035; Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1× NaPy, 1× NEAA, 1× GlutaMAX supplement, 0.1 mM 2-ME, and 103 U/ml LIF.

Fully grown colonies were passaged, thawed, and maintained under the same culture conditions. For each experiment, the cells were either seeded in 24-well culture plates (3820024; AGC Techno Glass Co., Ltd., Shizuoka, Japan) or 4-well Nunclon Delta-treated IVF dishes (144444; Thermo Fisher Scientific) precoated with gelatin. All ESC lines were used between passages 8 and 15.

Cell cycle analysis

1 × 105 B6×129/Sv or EGR-R01 ESCs were initially seeded. Four to six days after seeding, the cells reached either normal confluency (NC; 80–90% cell confluence) or OC (> 90% cell confluence). These cells were harvested via dissociation with 0.25% trypsin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and trypsin activity was neutralized using culture medium containing 20% FBS (Merck). The cells were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (DSBN200; KAC, Kyoto, Japan) and resuspended at a 1:1 ratio in staining solution consisting of Milli-Q water supplemented with 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM NaCl, 0.7 mg/ml RNase A (318-06391; Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan), 0.1% NP-40 (85124; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and DNA staining dye (either 0.05 mg/ml propidium iodide; P4170; Merck, for B6×129/Sv ESCs or 2 μg/ml 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole; 62248; Thermo Fisher Scientific, for EGR-R01 ESCs). The samples were incubated at room temperature for 30 min and subsequently processed for cell cycle analysis using the SH800S flow cytometer (Sony Biotechnology, Tokyo, Japan). Data were analyzed using the ModFit LT 6.0 software (Verity Software House, ME, USA). All datasets showed comparable variance across experimental conditions.

Donor cell preparation for SCNT

All ESC lines were cultured until they reached NC or OC, as described above. OC was induced either gradually or rapidly. When ESCs were cultured in a 24-well plate, the cells were initially seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well for gradual OC and 2–3 × 105 cells/well for rapid OC. On the day of SCNT, cultured cells (wells) that reached the desired confluence were dissociated using 0.25% trypsin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and trypsin activity was neutralized with culture medium containing 20% FBS (Merck). The cells were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline and resuspended in the HEPES-buffered Chatot, Ziomek, and Bavister (H-CZB) medium for SCNT.

SCNT

Female BDF1 mice were superovulated by intraperitoneal injection of 7.5 IU equine chorionic gonadotropin (012969; Nippon Zenyaku Kogyo Co., Ltd., Fukushima, Japan), followed 48 h later by 7.5 IU human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG; 005553; ASKA Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

SCNT was performed based on the original Honolulu method [3, 10], with minor modifications. Briefly, 15 h after human chorionic gonadotropin injection, cumulus–oocyte complexes were collected from the oviducts, and cumulus cells were dispersed via incubation in the CZB medium containing 0.1% bovine testicular hyaluronidase (37326-33-3; Merck). Cumulus cells were used as nuclear donors in some experiments. Oocytes were enucleated in the H-CZB medium supplemented with 7.5 μg/ml cytochalasin B (250233; Merck). Donor nuclei (ESCs or cumulus cells) were injected into enucleated oocytes using a piezo-driven micromanipulator (Primetech, Ibaraki, Japan). The injected oocytes were incubated in the CZB medium for 1 h and then activated in calcium-free CZB medium containing 2.5 mM SrCl2 and 5 μM latrunculin A (L5163; Merck) for 1 h. The cells were cultured for an additional 8 h in the CZB medium supplemented with latrunculin A. In addition, for inhibitor treatment groups, embryos were cultured in an activation medium with 50 nM trichostatin A (TSA; T8552; Merck) and 1 μM RK-701, an inhibitor of G9a histone methyltransferase [30, 31]. After 9 h of treatment with or without inhibitor, the embryos were washed and cultured in the CZB medium at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator until the blastocyst stage or transferred to female ICR mice at the 2-cell stage.

Embryo transfer

Activated oocytes that developed to the 2-cell embryo stage after one day of in vitro culture were transferred into the oviducts of pseudopregnant female ICR mice at day 0.5 post-coitum (the day following sterile mating with vasectomized males). On day 19.5 of gestation, the pregnant females were examined via cesarean section after euthanasia. Live-born pups were fostered by lactating female ICR mice. After weaning, the adult clones were mated with female ICR mice to assess fertility.

RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) library preparation

Cultured ESCs were harvested as described above, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until library preparation. After thawing, total RNA was extracted and purified using the RNeasy Mini Kit (74104; QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). RNA quantity and quality were assessed using the NanoDrop One Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and TapeStation with RNA ScreenTape Kit (5067-5576; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). cDNA library and subsequent sequencing procedures were conducted by Macrogen Japan (Tokyo, Japan). In brief, libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), following the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Sample Preparation Guide. Paired-end 101-bp sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeqX platform.

RNA-seq analysis

Adapter sequences and low-quality reads were removed using fastp (v. 0.20.1) [32]. The remaining high-quality reads were aligned uniquely to the mouse reference genome (mm10) using HISAT2 (v. 2.2.1) [33] with default settings. The alignment files were processed with SAMtools (v. 1.12) [34] before transcript assembly and quantification using StringTie (v. 2.2.1) [35]. Gene expression levels were quantified as transcripts per million. Differentially expressed genes were identified using the DESeq2 package (v. 1.42.1) [36]. Transcripts with fewer than ten reads across all samples were filtered out prior to normalization. Data normalization was performed using the variance-stabilizing transformation method in DESeq2.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the R software (v 4.5.2). Proportion data were arcsine square root-transformed before analysis. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Figs. 2A, 4C, 5A) whereas multiple-group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Figs. 2B, C). Statistical details for each experiment are provided in the corresponding figure legends. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Treatment with TSA and RK-701 mildly enhances pre-implantation development of ESC-cloned embryos

First, we tested whether TSA (a histone deacetylase inhibitor) alone or in combination with RK-701 (a histone methyltransferase inhibitor) improves pre-implantation development of ESC-derived cloned embryos similar to that reported in cumulus cell-derived embryos [30, 31]. We utilized high-confluent culture (NC), a standard method for ESC cloning [22], with three existing ESC lines: EGR-R01 (BDF1×129/Sv background with CAG-mtDsRed2 marker) [27], B6×129/Sv, and EB3 (129/Ola genetic background) ESCs [28, 29] (Supplementary Table 1). Across all ESC lines, the control (non-treated) ESC-cloned embryos showed substantially higher developmental rates to blastocyst compared to the control cumulus-cloned embryos (approximately 50–60 vs. 25%; Fig. 1A and B, Supplementary Table 2). Consistent with previous report [31], when we treated the cumulus-cloned embryos with inhibitors, blastocyst formation rates were significantly improved to nearly 80% with the combination of TSA and RK-701 (Fig. 1B). However, a similar pattern was not observed in ESC cloning. After TSA treatment, all three tested lines showed only a slight increase in blastocyst formation rate to an average of 70% (Fig. 1B). In addition, combined treatment with TSA and RK-701 did not further improve developmental competence compared with the other conditions. These findings suggest that the tested epigenetic interventions may not be critical for improving the development of ESC-cloned embryos. Based on these results, we used TSA treatment alone for subsequent experiments.

ESC-cloned embryos derived from multiple ESC lines exhibit distinct post-implantation development patterns

In the next experiment, we produced 2-cell embryos and transferred them to pseudopregnant female ICR mice to evaluate post-implantation development of ESC-cloned embryos. Unlike cumulus donor cells, nuclear transfer of all ESC lines resulted in markedly low 2-cell embryo formation rates (P < 0.05), which largely affected overall developmental efficiency. Particularly, EGR-R01 ESCs exhibited the lowest cleavage rate to the 2-cell stage (38.7%). Nevertheless, we found that EB3 and B6×129/Sv ESCs showed relatively higher 2-cell cleavage rates of 51.2 and 50.7%, respectively (Fig. 2A). Although all ESC lines showed high blastocyst formation rates (60–70%; Fig. 1B), post-implantation development varied among them. The implantation rates of EB3 and B6×129/Sv ESC-derived embryos were higher than those of cumulus-cloned embryos; however, EB3 ESC-derived embryos failed to progress beyond early gestation, and B6×129/Sv ESC-derived embryos developed to live offspring at a very low rate (0.4%; Fig. 2B and C, Table 1). Notably, despite its lower 2-cell cleavage and implantation rates, EGR-R01 ESCs exhibited the highest full-term development among the ESC lines, with 5.6% of transferred 2-cell embryos developing to term (Fig. 2B).

Post-implantation developmental failure of ESC-cloned embryos is likely associated with aberrant imprinted gene expression

EB3 ESCs were cultured and subjected to an excessive number of passages, whereas B6×129/Sv ESCs were cultured under the so-called 2i/LIF conditions (inhibitions of Mek1/2 and Gsk3β by two inhibitors in the presence of LIF), both of which induce aberrant genomic imprinting [37,38,39]. In addition, both cell lines were cultured without MEFs. To explore whether imprinting defects contribute to the developmental failure of embryos cloned from these lines, we performed pairwise comparisons of differentially expressed imprinted genes (padj < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 1) using RNA-seq. This analysis revealed distinct expression profiles among the ESC lines, with EB3 and B6×129/Sv lines showing elevated expression levels of multiple imprinted genes compared with the clonable EGR-R01 line (Supplementary Fig. 1). Notably, examination of the chromosomal distribution of DE imprinted genes between these ESCs (EB3 vs. EGR-R01 and B6×129/Sv vs. EGR-R01) revealed common enrichment of dysregulated genes in critical imprinted domains, such as H19–Igf2 on chromosome 7 and Dlk1–Dio3 on chromosome 12 (Fig. 3A and B; Supplementary Table 3), both of which have been strongly linked to developmental failure in cloned embryos [40, 41]. Specifically, B6×129/Sv ESCs exhibited aberrantly high expression of H19 and low expression of Igf2, whereas EB3 ESCs showed the opposite pattern (Figs. 3C and D). In addition, EB3 ESCs showed further imprinting defects, including low Dio3 expression and high Dlk1 expression. These findings suggest that the developmental failure of embryos derived from these ESCs is attributed, at least in part, to imprinting abnormalities in donor ESCs. Based on these findings, we selected the EGR-R01 line, which was considered to maintain proper imprinting, for subsequent experiments related to post-implantation evaluation.

Overconfluent culture increases the G0/G1 ESC population

In a previous study, ESCs cultured at 80–90% confluency (NC) showed improved cloning outcomes compared with those cultured at 60–70% confluency; however, the cell cycle distribution remained unchanged [22]. Our results also showed that cloning from NC ESCs resulted in a low 2-cell embryo formation rate, which distinctly affected overall developmental efficiency (Figs. 2A and B). Based on this, we hypothesized that OC (> 90%) further enhances cloning efficiency. To test our hypothesis, we cultured and harvested NC and OC ESCs (Fig. 4A) and subjected them to fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) for cell cycle analysis. The results showed that culturing ESCs to OC increased the proportion of G0/G1 phase cells (from 26% to 41% and from 24% to 31% in B6×129/Sv and EGR-R01 ESCs, respectively; Figs. 4B and C). To clarify the mechanisms by which OC enriches the G0/G1 cell population, we analyzed the expression levels of cell cycle regulators, specifically cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases (Cdks) via RNA-seq in the EGR-R01 line [42]. The transcriptome data revealed markedly different expression patterns between NC and OC ESCs for gene families involved in the G1/S transition (Fig. 4D). Notably, we found that several cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors such as Cdkn1a/1b/2a/2c/2d, which mediate G1 arrest, were highly expressed in the OC group (Fig. 4D). These findings suggest that contact inhibition induced by OC alters the cell cycle of ESCs by prolonging the G0/G1 phase.

Overconfluent culture improves 2-cell embryo production and allows proper development to term of ESC-cloned embryos

The cleavage ability (2-cell cleavage rate) of reconstructed oocytes is considered as a reliable indicator of successful cell cycle synchronization in mouse SCNT. Across all ESC lines, OC cultured cells exhibited significantly higher 2-cell cleavage rates compared with NC cultured cells (P < 0.05; Fig. 5A, Supplementary Table 4), most likely due to an increased proportion of G0/G1 phase cells (Fig. 4C). In addition, the number of cells in the first seeding seemed to have a significant effect on embryonic development after nuclear transfer. Gradual progression to OC (Gradual-OC), starting with a low cell number (e.g., 1 × 105 cells/well in a 24-well culture plate), resulted in more favorable OC conditions than rapid progression (Rapid-OC), which began with a higher cell number (e.g., 2–3 × 105 cells/well in a 24-well culture plate; Supplementary Fig. 2A). Although the G0/G1 population was not different between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 2B), the 2-cell cleavage rate was higher in the Gradual-OC group (Supplementary Fig. 2C). Therefore, we used Gradual-OC treatment for subsequent evaluation.

Importantly, cloned embryos derived from OC ESCs showed normal post-implantation development comparable to that of embryos derived from NC ESCs. Embryo transfer resulted in a stable live birth rate of 5.8% (Fig. 5B, Table 1). Consistent with previous reports [10, 22], postnatal survival rates were relatively low regardless of confluency conditions (approximately 30%; data not shown), likely due to the genetic and epigenetic instability inherent to ESCs [12, 24]. However, all surviving pups developed normally into adulthood. It should be noted that EGR-R01 is a transgenic ESC line carrying a dsRed2 fluorescent reporter (Fig. 5C). When mated with female ICR mice, OC ESC-derived clones were fertile and produced offspring with a fluorescent marker, as expected, indicating no apparent postnatal or germline transmission defects associated with this approach (Fig. 5D).

Although principal component analysis indicated that OC induced global transcriptomic alterations in EGR-R01 ESCs (Supplementary Fig. 3A), pairwise comparisons of differentially expressed imprinted genes (padj < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 1) revealed no widespread dysregulation of imprinted genes compared with that in NC ESCs (Supplementary Fig. 1). Furthermore, critical loci including H19–Igf2 and Dlk1–Dio3 remained stably expressed in OC ESCs (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Thus, these findings demonstrated that OC induction markedly improved 2-cell embryo production without disrupting imprinted gene expression, thereby enhancing overall cloning efficiency by approximately two-fold (Fig. 5E).

Discussion

In recent years, cloning efficiency in mice has been markedly improved by overcoming epigenetic barriers, including the inhibition of histone deacetylation [30], suppression of ectopic Xist expression from the active X chromosome [43], removal or reduction of histone H3 lysine 9 methylation (H3K9me) [31,44], inhibition of aberrant DNA re-methylation [45], and restoration of monoallelic expression of H3K27me3-imprinted genes [46, 47]. Despite significant technical advancements in mouse SCNT, ESC cloning via nuclear transfer has remained technically suboptimal since its first success in 1999 [10]. In this study, we evaluated whether advances that markedly improve cumulus cell cloning enhance ESC cloning efficiency using various ESC lines. However, in ESC-derived cloned embryos, we found that baseline blastocyst formation rates were already high (approximately 50–60%), and TSA treatment resulted in only a modest increase. Moreover, combined TSA and RK-701 treatment did not further enhance developmental competence. These findings indicate that at least some epigenetic barriers tested in the present study, which were efficiently rescued via cumulus cloning, do not represent the primary limiting factors in ESC cloning.

Importantly, we identified two key technical barriers that specifically limited the efficiency of ESC cloning in mice: the proper maintenance of canonical genomic imprinting and cell cycle synchronization. These factors have not been thoroughly addressed in previous mouse SCNT studies, which predominantly used freshly isolated cumulus cells (cells that are naturally synchronized in the G0/G1 phase and maintain normal imprinting) as donor nuclei [48]. Although ESCs offer clear advantages as donor cells due to their undifferentiated genomic state and amenability to genetic manipulation, these technical limitations hinder their broader application in both basic and translational research.

We confirmed that maintaining the correct imprinting status in donor ESCs is a critical prerequisite for supporting normal post-implantation development of ESC-derived embryos. Recent studies have shown that although 2i/LIF promotes a homogeneous naïve pluripotent state in ESCs [49], it compromises developmental potential by disrupting DNA methylation at imprinting control regions [25, 26]. In ESC-derived chimeric embryos, host-derived cells may compensate for imprinting defects in donor ESC-derived tissues. However, in ESC-cloned embryos, all embryonic tissues originate from a single donor ESC, making a normal imprinting status essential for proper development. Consistently, in our study, the ESC line cultured under 2i/LIF conditions (B6×129/Sv) showed aberrant expression of canonical imprinted genes and resulted in high embryonic lethality after nuclear transfer. EB3 ESCs, which were cultured without 2i, also exhibited imprinting aberrations, likely due to extended passaging without feeder cells, as previously reported [37]. In contrast, the EGR-R01 line, which was cultured in the classic serum/LIF medium in the presence of feeder cells, supported full-term development.

Since the birth of Dolly the sheep, cell cycle synchronization in mammalian SCNT has been achieved primarily via serum deprivation or increased cell confluency. However, accumulating evidence indicates that serum deprivation may be detrimental to donor cells, as it frequently induces cell death [50, 51]. Based on these concerns, we attempted to enrich the G0/G1 population in ESCs by culturing them to OC. Under overconfluent conditions, the G0/G1 proportion was increased. Moreover, transcriptome analysis revealed that overconfluent culture led to the upregulation of G0/G1-associated cell cycle arrest genes (Fig. 4D), suggesting that contact inhibition prolongs the cell cycle by modulating the expression patterns of key cell cycle regulators. These findings support the idea that G0/G1 enrichment induced by OC culture is closely associated with stable 2-cell embryo formation following nuclear transfer, as was consistently observed across multiple ESC lines in our study (Fig. 5A). At present, we recognize that isolation of G0/G1 ESCs prior to SCNT may further clarify the causal contribution of cell cycle status; however, such experiments are technically challenging because of the complexity of the procedure, and prolonged manipulation may adversely affect cell viability and nuclear transfer competence. Additionally, cell cycle analysis by FACS inevitably damages the DNA of the cells because the DNA-staining dye is incorporated into the DNA strands and causes DNA breaks by irradiation. Therefore, methods for demonstrating direct causal evidence need to be established in future studies.

Interestingly, we found that gradual OC induction, but not rapid OC induction, facilitated efficient 2-cell embryo production, raising the possibility that gradual adaptation may allow coordinated cell cycle synchronization necessary for successful nuclear transfer, although the underlying mechanism remains unclear. Another important aspect is that the magnitude of improvement in 2-cell embryo formation exceeded the increase in G0/G1 proportions detected by FACS analysis. During SCNT, we observed that Gradual-OC cultures contained a larger population of small ESCs, a morphology associated with G0/G1-phase cells [10], than NC or Rapid-OC cultures. Although this observation was not quantitatively analyzed in the present study, it is possible that additional factors beyond cell cycle distribution may contribute to the improved cloning efficiency. Notably, we demonstrated that transient induction of OC did not compromise the developmental potential of ESC-cloned embryos, as they developed to full term at a birth rate comparable to that of embryos derived from standard NC ESCs (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, transgenic clones (EGR-R01) generated from OC-ESCs were fertile and produced offspring that maintained normal fluorescence expression, indicating that OC had no adverse effects on subsequent generations.

In summary, our findings provide a practical strategy to stimulate cell cycle synchronization via gradual OC induction as an effective approach to enhance ESC cloning efficiency. Notably, our approach improved 2-cell embryo production in several experiments that often require a consistent number of ESC-cloned embryos. Overall, our study established an efficient method for directly generating cloned offspring from ESCs, including transgenic mice, for a wide range of applications in basic and translational research.
