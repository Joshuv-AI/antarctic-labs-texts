Relationships between leaf structural and functional traits of urban landscape plant species: implications for sustainable landscape planning
Authors: Funda Ankaya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUrban planting decisions increasingly use trait-based methodologies; yet they frequently rely on labor-intensive physiological assessments. The use of visible leaf structural traits is an effective alternative. Nevertheless, their capacity for predicting functional performance has rarely been evaluated in a comparative, multi-species urban context. This study conducted a preliminary systematic evaluation to determine if two easily identifiable structural traits, i.e., phyllotaxy (spiral, opposite, decussate, rosette) and blade morphology (simple versus compound) can function as proxies for essential functional traits across 29 prevalent urban landscape species in the field.MethodsThe relationship between two leaf structural traits, i.e., phyllotaxy (spiral, opposite, decussate, or rosette) and leaf blade morphology (simple or compound), and six leaf functional traits [relative chlorophyll index (SPAD), BET-specific surface area (SSA), moisture, and iron (Fe), magnesium (Mg) and copper (Cu) concentration in leaves] was examined. Leaf samples were collected from healthy, mature plants and target traits were examined using conventional portable meters and laboratory tests. Functional traits were standardized using z-scores, and Euclidean distances were calculated. Metric multidimensional scaling (Principal Coordinates Analysis - PCoA, 1–3 dimensions) was employed and assessed with Kruskal’s stress-1 and R-squared coefficient of determination. Species structure was investigated through k-means clustering, and pairwise trait associations were examined using two-tailed Pearson correlations (α = 0.05).ResultsPhyllotaxy exhibited stronger and consistent correlations with functional traits than blade type. Opposite/decussate arrangements frequently demonstrated higher SPAD and distinct SSA, although spiral phyllotaxy correlated with increased Mg and Cu levels. Complex blades had more moisture content, while simple blades typically displayed higher SPAD values. Pairwise relationships identified two functional axes, i.e., moisture–nutrient and pigment–micronutrient. Magnesium had a significant correlation with moisture (r ≈ 0.48, p < 0.01), whereas SPAD was associated with Cu (r ≈ 0.40, p < 0.05). Most other pairings showed slight correlations, suggesting low collinearity. Unsupervised analysis (k-means, metric MDS/PCoA) identified coherent groups corresponding to these gradients. Rosette clustered at extreme moisture–SSA, opposite/decussate taxa gravitated towards elevated SPAD, and compound-bladed species demonstrated increased moisture levels. The consistent trends support the use of identifiable structural traits to predict functional performance in the selection of urban plants.ConclusionTwo readily identifiable structural traits—particularly phyllotaxy—offer valuable insights into functional diversity in urban vegetation, facilitating more effective, on-site trait-based plant selection. Understanding these relationships might improve the selection of plant species for urban green spaces. Although the findings derive from a single season in a Mediterranean location, they encourage additional investigation across diverse climates and seasons to enhance guidelines for sustainable landscape planning.Keywords: Urban landscaping, Leaf traits, Phyllotaxy, Chlorophyll content, Cluster analysis, Plant selection

Introduction

Urban green spaces serve as vital components of cities, providing numerous ecosystem services that enhance environmental quality and human well-being [1, 2]. Vegetation in urban environments enhances aesthetic attraction [3], mitigates urban heat island effects [4], improves air quality by filtering pollutants [5], reduces stormwater runoff and waterlogging [6, 7], and decreases noise pollution [8]. Urban trees and other vegetation store carbon and emit oxygen, mostly through their leaves, which are the main organs responsible for these biochemical processes [9, 10]. Leaves serve as the interface between plants and the urban environment, playing a vital role in light capture, carbon dioxide assimilation, and microclimate regulation [11].

Leaf structural and physiological traits impact photosynthetic efficiency of plants and stress tolerance [12, 13]. Key leaf structural traits include the arrangement of leaves on the stem (known as phyllotaxy, such as spiral alternating, opposite, decussate, or rosette arrangements) [14, 15] and the complexity of the leaf blade (simple vs. compound leaves) [16]. These structural traits may influence the capacity of leaves to capture light and facilitate gas exchange with the environment [17, 18]. Essential leaf functional traits, including chlorophyll content, leaf area (or specific leaf area), moisture content, and nutrient concentrations, influence photosynthetic efficiency and growth [19, 20]. Chlorophyll content is directly correlated with a leaf’s capacity to absorb light for photosynthesis [21]. Leaf nutrients such as iron (Fe), copper (Cu), and magnesium (Mg) are required for photosynthesis [22]. Iron is essential for electron transport in photosynthesis, despite not being a component of chlorophyll [23]. Similarly, Cu is essential to chloroplast enzymes and pigments, such as plastocyanin, and affects photosynthetic electron flow [23]. Magnesium serves as the central atom in chlorophyll molecules [24]; thus, its deficiency results in chlorosis and stunted growth [25]. Consequently, fluctuations in these nutrients within leaves may significantly influence photosynthetic activities and overall plant health.

The unique functional attributes of urban trees have attracted increased interest in recent years. Several studies have assessed the impact of urban greenery on reducing carbon emissions and the consequences of climate change [26–29]. Urban trees have been investigated for their ability to absorb carbon and alleviate greenhouse gas emissions [30–33]. Some studies have explored biotechnological approaches to enhance photosynthesis in plants to improve urban environmental quality [34, 35]. Despite basic assessment, there is a need for more thorough trait-based research that links the morphological attributes of leaves to their physiological traits in urban environments. Understanding the relationships between leaf structural and functional traits could explain why particular species thrive in urban environments and assist the selection of species for certain ecological objectives [36].

Leaf functional traits often interact in a coordinated way [37], as indicated by the leaf economics spectrum [38–41], which contains trade-offs across leaf life expectancy, leaf mass per area, nutrient content, and photosynthetic rates [42, 43]. However, these findings are based on the studies conducted in natural or agricultural environments. Urban ornamental plants, including a combination of native and alien species with varied morphologies (trees, shrubs, vines, succulents), can exhibit unique trait associations owing to specific environmental stresses (e.g., pollution, heat stress) and horticultural selection [44, 45]. Leaf moisture content has recently been identified as a critical factor affecting other leaf traits [46]. A global study indicated that leaves with higher moisture content exhibit increased specific leaf area and photosynthetic capacity, highlighting the significance of moisture-related traits in leaf function [46]. In urban environments, water availability and heat stress can vary, hence plants with specific leaf water retention abilities or nutrient reserves (such as higher Mg) might display better resistance [47, 48]. Likewise, phyllotaxy may impact a plant’s light capture, thus influencing chlorophyll concentration or leaf area expansion [49]. Nevertheless, this relationship remains poorly understood.

Although the importance of leaf traits is well-known, little is known about which leaf structural traits (such as phyllotaxy and blade type) could serve as proxies for leaf functional traits in urban flora. Understanding these relationships is essential for urban forestry and landscaping. If structural traits correlate with useful functional traits (e.g., higher chlorophyll or water content), landscape planners could effectively select species that will thrive or provide improved ecosystem services. Moreover, as urban areas develop climate change adaptation plans, prioritizing the selection of plant species with traits adapted to higher temperatures, aridity, or increased pollution is crucial.

Urban heat, vapor-pressure deficit, and pollution modify canopy energy balance and foliar chemistry, favoring trait syndromes in the leaf-economics spectrum [39, 40]. Given that leaf arrangement influences self-shading and light interception, while blade complexity affects hydraulic load per node, it was hypothesized that (i) phyllotaxy and blade type would account for variability in chlorophyll index and moisture; and (ii) nutrient–pigment associations (e.g., Mg–chlorophyll) would be identifiable across species despite diverse Lineages. The study included 29 prevalent landscaping plants in Manisa Province, Turkey. The study was aimed at providing practical, field-applicable guidelines for resilient urban plantings by investigating whether observable structural traits—phyllotaxy and blade morphology—can predict functional performance. It was assessed whether these traits influence variability in chlorophyll index (SPAD) and leaf moisture, and whether nutrient–pigment associations (e.g., Mg–chlorophyll) are observable across various species, thereby guiding ecologically conscious plant selection for sustainable urban green spaces.

Materials and methods

Study area and species

The study was conducted in the Alaşehir region of Manisa Province, located in western Turkey. Alaşehir is situated at around 28°31′38″E longitude and 38°21′41″N latitude, with an altitude of 189 m (Fig. 1). The region has a Mediterranean climate, characterized by hot arid Summers and warm, precipitation-rich winters. The average annual precipitation is around 540 mm, primarily falling from December to February. Summer temperatures occasionally rise to 40 °C in July and August, whilst January is the coldest month, with average temperature of 3 °C. The native flora of the region is well-suited to this Mediterranean climatic climate. The soils of the Alaşehir plain are brown forest soils, red Mediterranean soils, Lithosols, Regosols, as well as Colluvial and Alluvial deposits. Viticulture is prevalent in the region, with Alaşehir serving as a major grape production hub, suggesting the presence of rich alluvial soils in some areas.

For the study, 29 plant species frequently utilized in urban landscaping and roadside or park plantings in Alaşehir were selected. The selected species are sufficient for correlation and multivariate trait analysis as indicated by analogous urban trait studies [40, 41]. The species included 23 deciduous and 6 evergreen plants (trees, shrubs, and one succulent herb) that are well-adapted to the local climate and used for ornamental or environmental objectives. All species were healthy, adult individuals thriving in public urban environments (parks, streets, campus, etc.) inside Alaşehir. Table 1 describes the species with their taxonomic families, functional types (deciduous or evergreen), leaf arrangements, leaf blade types, and the evaluated functional trait for each species. The species investigated included broadleaf deciduous trees (e.g., Robinia pseudoacacia, Catalpa bignonioides), evergreen shrubs (Ligustrum vulgare, Euonymus japonicus), conifers (Cedrus libani, Cupressus sempervirens), and succulent/rosette plants (Agave americana). The wide range of plant types provided an extensive range of leaf morphology and physiology for study.

Leaf sampling and trait measurements

Field sampling was conducted in July 2023, corresponding with the summer growth season when leaves were completely developed. For each species, numerous individual plants were examined in the field, and mature leaves were collected from Alaşehir district in Manisa Province. Sun-exposed (no shade exposed), healthy leaves from the outer canopy of mature plants (completely developed trees or established shrubs) were collected to ensure comparability. Each sample consisted of leaves that had matured during the latest growing season (for deciduous species) or the current year’s flush (for evergreens).

Leaf samples were collected from the public parks, including Mesir Tabiat Park (38°36’08.8"N 27°23’07.2"E), Şehzadeler Park (8°37’34.1"N 27°26’21.8"E), and Atatürk Kent Parkı (38°37’51.8"N 27°23’04.5"E). Permissions were obtained from Parks, and Landscape Department of Manisa Province. The voucher specimens of the leaves were deposited to the Herbarium collection of Manisa Celal Bayar University with voucher numbers ranging from MA-FA-2024-128 to MA-FA-2024-157. The plant specimens were kindly identified by Dr. Shahid Farooq, Department of Plant Protection, Faculty of Agriculture, Harran University, Şanlıurfa, Türkiye. Collected leaves were immediately kept in a temperature-maintained box at 4 °C for transport to the laboratory on the same day. Samples were refrigerated in the laboratory, and all measurements and analyses were conducted within five days following collection to minimize tissue loss or pigment quality. The scientific nomenclature and basic leaf traits (arrangement, blade morphology) for each species were verified using conventional botanical sources.

Chlorophyll index measurement

Chlorophyll index of leaves was evaluated non-destructively using a portable chlorophyll meter (Minolta SPAD-502). The SPAD-502 measurements provide a relative index that non-linearly corresponds with extracted chlorophyll content [50, 51], but they do not serve as an absolute measure. The SPAD units without any conversion were presented in this study. The SPAD measurements were taken from five leaves per species, evaluating each leaf at several locations, and then averaged these readings to determine the mean chlorophyll index for each species. A total of 25–30 unique SPAD measurements (5 leaves × 5–6 readings each) were collected for each species.

Leaf nutrient (Fe, mg, Cu) analysis

The concentration of Fe, Mg, and Cu was determined in the leaves of all species. A total 5–10 leaves were collected from each species depending upon leaf size, to have sufficient dry mass for chemical analysis. The samples included the youngest fully developed leaves from the current year’s growth, typically collected from the mid-canopy of the plant. The leaves were first cleaned carefully (rinsed twice with tap water and subsequently twice with deionized water) to remove any Surface Dust or impurities. The samples were then dried in an oven at 80 °C for a minimum of 48 h, until a consistent weight was attained, to stop metabolic activity and remove all moisture. The dried leaf material was ground into a fine powder using a stainless-steel grinder. Following normal plant tissue digestion techniques, 0.5 g of the powdered material was put in a porcelain crucible and Subjected to ashing in a muffle furnace at 550 °C for 3.5 h. Upon cooling, the ash was treated with 2 mL of concentrated HCl to dissolve the mineral components, then diluted with distilled water, and filtered into a 100 mL volumetric flask. The solution was diluted to a final volume of 100 mL using deionized water. The digest was further tested for Fe, Mg, and Cu through Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES; Perkin Elmer Avio 220 Max). The ICP-OES was calibrated using appropriate standards for Fe, Mg, and Cu, and quality control assessments were carried out using known reference samples to ensure accuracy. Nutrient concentrations were expressed on a dry weight basis (Fe in mg per g of dry leaf, Mg and Cu in parts per million (ppm) of dry leaf). The digestion and analysis technique followed standard protocols for plant mineral analysis [52].

Leaf surface area measurement

Leaf surface area, or specific leaf area, was determined using a Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area analyzer (Micromeritics Gemini VI Surface Area and Porosity analyzer). Specific leaf area is defined as the overall Surface area, including both sides of the leaf and any internal porosity available for the adsorbate gas, per unit mass of leaf. Specific leaf area is usually quantified as the area of one side per unit mass in plant ecology; however, BET analysis allows the measurement of Surface area, including microscale roughness and porosity by nitrogen adsorption. A subset of dried leaf samples was used for this measurement. For broadleaf species, 5 leaves per species were used, whereas 20 leaves were utilized for species with tiny or needle-like leaves to get adequate area for measurement. The previously dried samples were further dried and degassed according to instrument specifications before measurements. The BET technique absorbs nitrogen gas onto leaf material at 70 °C and quantifies the gas required to establish a monolayer on the sample surface. The surface area is calculated from this. The leaf area per unit mass (m² g−1 or, correspondingly, the reciprocal of leaf mass per area) derived from the BET measurement is used in this study. Due to BET’s potential to identify interior surfaces (such as inside dried leaf tissue structures), these results may not precisely align to conventional one-sided leaf area; yet they provide a reliable measure for interspecies comparison. A greater value indicates denser leaves per unit area, while a smaller number denotes Lighter, thinner leaves with a larger area relative to mass. Each sample took 11 h of analysis in the BET device to ensure equilibrium. Wide, thin leaves often have a high specific leaf area (low m² g−1), whereas succulent or sclerophyllous leaves show a low specific area (high m² g−1). The surface area is represented as SSA.

Leaf moisture content measurement

Leaf moisture (water content) was measured using a simple gravimetric technique. Five fresh leaf samples were collected for each species. Each sample (one leaf, or for very tiny leaves, a cluster of leaves) was weighed immediately to get fresh weight (A). Statistical analysis

The data for the 29 species were aggregated for statistical analysis. Initially, descriptive statistics were computed for each leaf trait across all species (mean, median, standard deviation, and 95% confidence interval of the mean) to understand the general range and variation. The results are presented as summary values and in Table 2. Given the major focus on the correlations between leaf structural and functional traits, the following analyses were conducted.

CI confidence interval

Species were classified based on leaf arrangement or blade morphology. The mean and standard deviation for each leaf parameter (SPAD, moisture, SSA, Fe, Mg, Cu) was calculated within each group. One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was used to assess statistically significant differences across leaf structure groups for each parameter [54]. Normality and homogeneity of variance was assessed prior to ANOVA. The sample size for each group rendered the Shapiro–Wilk test suitable for evaluating the normality of parameter distribution [55]. The Q-Q plots indicated that most characteristics exhibited normality (p > 0.05). The ANOVA F-tests revealed significant results at p < 0.05. In situations displaying significant group effects, a post-hoc LSD test was used to determine group differences.

Pearson correlation coefficients were computed for all variable pairs (moisture, SPAD, SSA, Fe, Mg, Cu) across 29 species to analyze the interrelationships among leaf traits. Each pair was evaluated for linearity and outliers prior to correlation analysis. In cases where a variable exhibited non-normal distribution or had an outlier (for instance, Agave americana had an exceptionally low specific area value that might disproportionately affect correlations related to area), Spearman’s rank correlation was also used for verification. A correlation matrix was produced, and significance was assessed at p < 0.05 and p < 0.01 thresholds, using Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons as necessary.

K-means cluster analysis was conducted on the six-dimensional trait data, treating each species as an observation. The data were normalized (mean-centered and scaled to unit variance) before clustering to ensure equal weighting of each trait. Several values of K (the number of clusters) were evaluated and assessed as the clustering results. Utilizing interpretability and the elbow technique (analyzing within-cluster sum of squares in relation to the number of clusters), K = 4 clusters was determined as the optimum classification for the 29 species. K-means clustering groups species into a certain number of clusters while minimizing within-cluster variation.

Metric multidimensional scaling (MDS) also known as Principal Coordinates Analysis was conducted to investigate the multivariate correlations across species. Euclidean distances were used across species inside the six-dimensional trait space. The MDS strives to put species inside a lower-dimensional space (e.g., 2D or 3D) such that the distances between points in that space correspond to the original trait distances. One-dimensional, two-dimensional, and three-dimensional solutions were assessed. The goodness-of-fit criteria used were Stress (Kruskal’s stress formula 1, which denotes the difference between original and fitted distances) and R-squared (RSQ, which reflects the proportion of variation in distances explained by the configuration).

Results

General leaf trait variation among species

The 29 landscape plant species exhibited an extensive range of values for each evaluated leaf trait, reflecting their diverse morphologies and functional types. Table 2 presents comprehensive descriptive statistics. The mean (± SD) for each trait across all species was leaf moisture content 37.8% (± 10.9), chlorophyll index (SPAD) 41.7 (± 11.7) SPAD units, BET-specific surface area (SSA) 0.61 (± 0.28) m² g−1, Mg 41.8 (± 17.3) ppm, Fe 1.09 (± 0.54) mg g−1, and Cu 0.13 (± 0.11) ppm. The median values resembled the means, indicating a nearly symmetric distribution for most traits; however, moisture and chlorophyll exhibited a minor right skew due to a few high-value species.

Leaf moisture content varied from 23.8% (lowest in Jacaranda mimosifolia, a deciduous tree) to 73.6% (highest in Agave americana, a succulent rosette plant). Consequently, some species’ leaves had three times the moisture content (per dry mass) compared to others, highlighting the differences between fleshy succulents and non-succulents.

Chlorophyll index varied from 22.3 (lowest in Euonymus japonicus, an evergreen shrub with variegated foliage) to 71.1 (highest in Elaeagnus angustifolia, a deciduous tree often referred to as Russian olive). This indicates a substantial difference in leaf greenness and pigment concentration. Certain resilient shrubs and evergreens exhibited comparatively low SPAD values, while species such as Elaeagnus, characterized by its silvery, slender leaves, unexpectedly demonstrated elevated SPAD values, potentially attributable to leaf structural adaptations (silvery scales coupled with high internal chlorophyll content).

Specific Surface area exhibited a lowest value of 0.01 m² g−1 in Agave americana and a highest of 1.28 m² g−1 in Eucalyptus camaldulensis. To clarify, a lower m² g−1 indicates a greater area per gram (i.e., extremely thin or fragile leaves), whereas a higher m² g−1 signifies a reduced area per gram (thicker, denser leaves). The value of Agave at 0.01 m² g−1 is very low, indicative of its succulent leaves, which possess a substantial internal surface area relative to their dry mass.

Leaf nutrient concentrations of Fe varied from 0.14 mg g−1 in Thuja orientalis, an evergreen conifer, to 2.33 mg g−1 in Ficus carica, the common fig, a deciduous tree. Magnesium concentrations varied from 16.7 ppm (minimum in Mahonia aquifolium, an evergreen plant) to 88.1 ppm (maximum in Agave americana). Copper was the micronutrient found in the least quantities, ranging from 0.05 ppm (minimum in Cedrus libani, an evergreen conifer) to 0.61 ppm (maximum in Catalpa bignonioides, a deciduous tree). The data suggests that various species accumulate these elements to varying extents. Agave exhibited exceptionally high Mg levels, likely associated with its photosynthetic apparatus and potential storage, as it is a CAM plant that typically necessitates elevated Mg for enzymatic functions. In contrast, Catalpa demonstrated significantly elevated Cu content, possibly attributable to differences in soil absorption or leaf biochemical requirements.

Decussate phyllotaxy species had the most intense foliage with 45.4 ± 2.3 SPAD units, whereas species with opposite leaf arrangement had the lowest pigmentation (35.9 ± 2.9), whilst spiral (41.9 ± 1.3) and rosette (40.7 ± 5.7) groups displayed intermediate values. The difference between decussate and opposite leaves was statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Table 3). Opposite leaves were the thinnest/most expansive (0.71 ± 0.06 m² g−1), differing significantly from all groups except spiral (0.65 ± 0.03). Rosette species, in contrast, displayed an extremely low SSA (0.01 ± 0.13 m² g−1), indicating exceptionally thick or dense foliage.

Spiral leaves accumulated the highest Mg (1.19 ± 0.06 mg g⁻¹) and differed significantly from opposite leaves (0.81 ± 0.13 mg g⁻¹). For Cu, rosette (0.09 ± 0.06 ppm) and opposite-leaved plants (0.10 ± 0.03 ppm) had the lowest concentrations, whereas decussate and spiral leaves (0.14–0.15 ppm) were richer in Cu. Leaf Fe concentrations (0.10–0.15 ppm) did not vary statistically among phyllotaxy types. The findings indicate that rosette phyllotaxy promotes water storage at the expense of specific leaf area and certain micronutrients, whereas decussate leaves optimize chlorophyll content, opposite leaves maximize area per unit mass, and spiral phyllotaxy boosts Mg and Cu intake. These patterns indicate specific adaptation strategies associated with leaf phyllotaxy.

Correlations among leaf functional traits

Pairwise correlation was investigated among leaf functional traits of all species included in the study. The correlation matrix is provided in Fig. 2. Numerous significant correlations were recorded, suggesting that certain traits tend to co-vary.

Pearson correlation matrix indicating the relationship between different leaf functional traits of studied species. R = Pearson correlation coefficient, Sig = significance, **. Correlation is significant at the 0.01 level (2-tailed)., *. Correlation is significant at the 0.05 level (2-tailed)

A significant positive correlation was noted between leaf Mg and moisture content (r = 0.479, p < 0.01). A significant positive correlation is seen between leaf Mg and moisture content (Pearson r = 0.479, p < 0.01). This indicates that species with higher leaf moisture content contain high Mg contents. This is logical as Mg crucial for chlorophyll and enzymes and may accumulate more in succulent, water-laden tissues. Succulent or thick-leaved plants such as Agave possess elevated moisture and Mg levels, enhancing correlation. Numerous evergreens and nutrient-conservative flora possess high concentrations of Mg in their leaves. The positive correlation indicates that well hydrated leaves may either maintain or require increased photosynthetic apparatus, since Mg is essential for chlorophyll. This supports global observations that leaf water content influences nutrient concentrations and photosynthetic activity.

The Cu concentration had a slight positive relationship with chlorophyll index (SPAD) (r = 0.401, p < 0.05). Plastocyanin and other chloroplast proteins absorb Cu essential for photosynthetic electron transport. Increased chlorophyll (more green leaves) was correlated with marginally elevated Cu levels. Unlike species with little chlorophyll, such as Euonymus japonicus, Elaeagnus angustifolia has one of the highest SPAD values and a low Cu concentration of 0.11 ppm. Despite the small correlation, it confirms the idea that plants investing in chlorophyll may also absorb or use greater amounts of Cu for photosynthesis.

Slight negative correlation (r = − 0.21) was noted between leaf moisture and SSA. Thinner leaves may desiccate more rapidly or possess less water per unit mass. On a dry basis, a succulent exhibits an unusually high moisture percentage, thus reversing the relationship between specific leaf area and moisture content. Fe exhibited a minor positive correlation with Cu (r ≈ 0.16, p ~ 0.4) and a negligible, non-significant correlation with Mg. An increase in one nutrient did not consistently correlate with an increase in another in the current study, illustrating species-specific nutritional profiles. The correlation between Cu and Mg was somewhat negative (r = − 0.10, non- significant). The relationships across nutrients were non-significant, indicating that each element may be independently regulated by plants or soil absorption, rather than being substantially interconnected within leaves across species.

Overall significant correlations (moisture–Mg and chlorophyll index–Cu) indicate biological ties. Adequately hydrated leaves acquire Mg, while chlorophyll-dense leaves possess sufficient Cu. The lack of correlation among other traits is also notable. The lack of a strong correlation between leaf area (mass per area) and leaf moisture or chlorophyll across species indicates that a plant’s leaf thickness and structure do not strictly determine its pigment or water content, likely due to the diverse trait combinations plants adopt based on their strategies. This independence suggests that in the planning of urban planting for specific purposes, each species should be regarded as a trait syndrome rather than just as a plant charact.

Discussion

The present study used an integrated approach including field observations, laboratory measurements, and multivariate statistics to investigate the correlations between leaf structural and functional traits across several urban landscaping plant species. Results indicated that certain leaf traits, i.e., phyllotaxy correlate with significant differences in leaf functional traits and these variations may be used to guide species selection for urban planting.

The key finding was that phyllotaxy significantly influenced leaf functional traits such as leaf water content and chlorophyll index. Species exhibiting opposite leaf patterns often demonstrated reduced leaf moisture and chlorophyll index in comparison to those with spiral arrangements. This indicates a strong connection between phyllotaxy and leaf economics. Opposite leaves often emerge in rapidly growing, light-requiring species, such as several deciduous trees including maples, ash, Ailanthus, and Jacaranda. These species may evolve smaller leaves with reduced chlorophyll density as a strategy to optimize surface area relative to biomass, characteristic of an acquisitive strategy within the leaf economics spectrum [46]. Conversely, the alternate-leaved species which often had bigger leaves [56] had a higher chlorophyll index per unit area in the current study. The observed pattern is comparable with general ecological strategies. Opposite-leaved species mainly occupied the fast-return end of the spectrum (characterized by high SSA and short leaf lifespan), whereas alternate-leaved species tended toward the slow-return end (exhibiting lower SSA and potentially longer-lived leaves) [57, 58]. The relationship between phyllotaxy and growth strategy may not be causative; rather, it indicates evolutionary clustering [14]. Several Lamiaceae (with opposed leaves) and Bignoniaceae (exhibiting opposite/decussate leaves) are characterized by rapid growth, whereas many Fagaceae and gymnosperms (with alternating or spiral leaves) exhibit slower growth rates [59].

Leaf arrangement may not directly influence leaf chemistry or water content; rather, it serves as a useful indication of underlying traits [18]. Opposite leaves may cause self-shading, with each pair potentially covering the pair below, hence potentially decreasing the optimum chlorophyll investment per area as light gets blocked by the top pair [60, 61]. Alternate leaves are positioned in a spiral formation that often enhances the exposure of each leaf while minimizing shade of adjacent leaves, shown by the Fibonacci spiral in several plants that optimizes light absorption [41]. This may enable alternating leaves to maintain high chlorophyll concentrations, since each leaf may get sufficient light. The findings of the current study suggest this effect, i.e., alternate-leaved plants exhibited increased chlorophyll concentration. Moreover, phyllotaxy may affect water use; species with opposite leaves often exhibit greater leaf area at a single node, potentially resulting in accelerated water loss if those leaves transpire simultaneously [62]. Many opposite-leaved trees, such as Jacaranda in the current study, rapidly drop leaves or wilt under drought, whereas alternate-leaved evergreens, like Olea, exhibit significant drought resistance. The elevated average water content of alternate-leaved plants, particularly evergreens, may indicate their enhanced xeromorphic adaptations, such as thicker cuticles or a more resilient vascular system that preserves water inside the leaves.

Another significant finding was that leaf characteristics were influenced by leaf blade type (simple vs. complex). Compound-leaved plants possess more moisture content; however, they have reduced chlorophyll index compared to simple-leaved species. Compound leaves, divided into leaflets, have a greater whole-leaf SSA owing to their augmented surface area relative to the supporting tissue (petiole, rachis) [63]. Gas exchange may enhance, while leaflets may become thinner or more vulnerable to moisture [64]. These plants may adapt by possessing multiple fine leaflets that efficiently absorb water from stems or by inhabiting more mesic environments. Ailanthus and Robinia thrive in riparian or wet disturbed areas with enough water, resulting in its leaves being moist. Many simple-leaved evergreens possess thick, waxy leaves and thrive in arid areas, resulting in low water content; for instance, Nerium oleander has simple leaves with less moisture but exhibits excellent drought tolerance [65]. The “dilution” effect may explain the reduced chlorophyll in compound leaves: despite their extensive surface area, they may choose rapid development above intense pigmentation. Additionally, intricate leaves such as those of Mahonia or Rosa may allocate chlorophyll resources to other functions, such as defense or aesthetic appeal.

From an urban forestry perspective, these structural-functional correlations enables to make general predictions. Plants exhibiting opposite or compound leaves, such as several deciduous ornamentals, may demonstrate elevated transpiration rates and possibly accelerated development; yet they may possess reduced drought tolerance owing to diminished leaf water stores and may need more fertile soil or more water to sustain their chlorophyll levels [41]. Conversely, plants exhibiting alternating, simple leaves, particularly evergreens, often possess resilient, long-lasting foliage that conserves water and nutrients, making them appropriate for low-input landscapes, but with the trade-off of slower growth or reduced maximum photosynthetic rates.bmcplantbiol.biomedcentral.com City planners may utilize leaf morphology as a rapid technique: for instance, if the objective is to establish a resilient, drought-resistant planting, preference should be given to species with alternate or simple leaves (such as olives, pines, and oleanders); conversely, if the aim is to achieve rapid shade or a dense canopy (assuming irrigation is accessible), species with opposite or compound leaves (such as jacarandas, ashes, and cassias) may be suitable.

The correlation analyses reveal internal leaf trait synergy. The strong positive link between leaf water content and Mg suggests that succulent or well-hydrated leaves tend to accumulate higher levels of Mg, which is obvious given that Mg is integral to chlorophyll and often abundant in fleshy tissues. This relationship corresponds to previous studies indicating that leaf water content is a crucial regulator of global leaf characteristic correlations [46]. Wang et al. [46] discovered that a rise in leaf water content correlates with enhancements in SSA and photosynthetic capability. The findings of this study indicate that increased water content correlates with elevated Mg levels, a nutrient associated with photosynthetic processes, hence supporting the concept that water-abundant leaves allocate resources towards metabolic capability. Nonetheless, a significant correlation was not observed between water content and chlorophyll index or SSA directly, indicating that within the limited selection of species (all generally adapted to warm climates), the association of water with other traits is not universally applicable – lineages (such as succulents) influence the water-Mg relationship while others vary.

This study provides a practical framework for correlating leaf morphology and functionality among 29 urban species; nevertheless, numerous limitations limit its generality. Traits were assessed solely once (mid-summer) and as single-species averages, ignoring seasonal and intra-species variability; critical physiological parameters such as leaf nitrogen, photosynthetic rate, and stomatal conductance were excluded, constraining understanding of the mechanisms underlying the observed patterns of chlorophyll, moisture, and nutrients. The current single-season, species-mean strategy excludes temporal and intraspecific variation. Furthermore, essential physiological variables (photosynthetic rate, stomatal conductance, leaf nitrogen) were not assessed, and relationships must be employed with caution. Nonetheless, the observed correlations between moisture–Mg and chlorophyll index–Cu align with general trait coordination reported outside urban environments, though the intensity of these interactions is moderate, reflecting lineage and management influences characteristic of urban plants.

Cluster-level assistance may facilitate planting design. Cluster 1 (sclerophyllous evergreens/conifers) is appropriate for arid, low-irrigation streetscapes and median strips. Cluster 2 (mesic ornamentals exhibiting elevated leaf water content and BET surface area) is Suitable for irrigated parks. Cluster 3, including nutrient-dense intermediates Such as rapidly developing ornamentals, is appropriate for mixed environments with low moisture and maintenance needs. Cluster 4 (succulents/rosette forms) is appropriate for xeric rock gardens and heat islands but provides little canopy shade. These correspond with current urban leaf-economics spectrum applications that associate conservative and acquisitive tactics with site water and heat load [41, 50, 51].

Conclusion

This study indicates that easily identifiable leaf traits can predict physiological function in urban landscape plants. Phyllotaxy accounted for significant variance among studied species. Alternative or spiral-leaved taxa had elevated mid-summer leaf water content and chlorophyll index, whereas opposite or decussate species displayed decreased values. Leaf blade type was also significant as compound leaves were often moister but had less chlorophyll index than simple leaves. Multivariate analysis categorized the species into four functional groupings that transcended taxonomy while correlating with drought tolerance, growth rate, and evergreen vs. deciduous characteristics. These groups provide a selection of supportive functions for urban greening. Drought-resistant evergreens guarantee year-round structure and resilience, whilst rapidly developing deciduous trees enhance cooling and carbon sequestration. Consequently, urban foresters may use fundamental morphological traits to match species with site conditions and intended ecosystem functions. The results support an economical, field-ready screening approach wherein phyllotaxy and leaf morphology facilitate species triage (e.g., opposite/decussate arrangements for chlorophyll-related efficacy; compound leaves for enhanced leaf hydration), complemented by instrumented or site-specific assessments for final selection. Due to the study’s correlative nature, its single-season scope, and its origin from a Mediterranean city, as well as the use of SPAD as a proxy, effect estimates must be taken with caution and confirmed in local contexts. Future research should investigate causation (light/self-shading, hydraulic load), broaden sampling across several seasons and climates with evolutionary controls, and correlate structural signals with performance outcomes (survival, cooling, pollution collection). In summary, readily identifiable leaf structure—particularly phyllotaxy—provides valuable insights on function, enabling a more efficient, cost-effective, and resilient approach to urban plant selection.
