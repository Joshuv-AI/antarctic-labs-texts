Infective endocarditis (IE) is a life-threatening disease that demands timely diagnosis and management. Culture-negative infective endocarditis (CNIE), reported in 10% to 30% of IE cases, presents unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges given the absence of pathogen identification through routine blood cultures.1,2 Contributing factors include prior antibiotic exposure and infections with fastidious or intracellular organisms such as Bartonella, Coxiella burnetii, and Tropheryma whipplei.3,4Take-Home Messages•Mechanical-assisted aspiration with the Penumbra Lightning Flash system effectively reduces vegetation burden and provides diagnostic material in patients with CNIE at high risk for surgery.•Rapid pathogen identification enables targeted antibiotic therapy, leading to clinical stabilization.•Intracardiac echocardiography enhances safety and accuracy during aspiration procedures.•Collaboration among the cardiology, infectious disease, neurology, and cardiac surgery teams ensures personalized and optimized management.

Patients with large, mobile vegetations are at increased risk of systemic embolization, particularly stroke, and they typically require urgent intervention.5 Although surgical valve replacement is often recommended in such high-risk cases, some patients are unsuitable for surgery because of recent neurologic events or other comorbidities.6 In these scenarios, emerging percutaneous strategies such as vacuum-assisted or mechanical aspiration can offer a minimally invasive option for vegetation reduction and microbiological sampling.7,8

We report a case of CNIE complicated by embolic stroke in which percutaneous aspiration using the Penumbra Lightning Flash system facilitated both microbiologic diagnosis and clinical stabilization, avoiding high-risk surgery.

History of Presentation

A 43-year-old Asian man with recent myocardial infarction in the left circumflex vessel due to embolic event requiring balloon angioplasty presented with acute left-sided weakness lasting 5 hours. He was treated as a case of acute stroke. He also had fever on the day of presentation, which was attributed to a possible viral illness. Brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed embolic infarctions in the right thalamus and the temporal and occipital lobes.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of myocardial infarction 1 year before, and coronary angiography showed single-vessel disease with 100% occlusion in the mid left circumflex artery, thought to be from a cardioembolic source. Percutaneous aspiration thrombectomy and transluminal balloon angioplasty had been performed, achieving a good result. At that time, he had a calcific lesion measuring 2 × 5 mm attached to the right aortic cusp, with mild aortic regurgitation (Figure 1, Video 1). The plan had been to schedule the patient for follow-up transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) within days in an outpatient setting, but he did not show up for follow-up until his presentation with stroke. He had no history of poor dentition or intravenous drug use.Figure 1Transthoracic Echocardiography From Previous AdmissionTwo-dimensional TTE images (Left) parasternal long-axis and (right) short-axis views show a calcific lesion measuring 2 × 5 mm attached to the right aortic cusp (arrows). Empiric antibiotic therapy with vancomycin and ceftriaxone was initiated, and blood cultures showed no growth of bacteria. On day 9, after multidisciplinary heart team evaluation, and given the patient's recent stroke, risk of hemorrhagic conversion with large heparin dose and high activated clotting time goal required during cardiac surgery, and overall surgical risk profile, a decision was made to proceed with vegetation aspiration using the retrograde aortic approach using the Penumbra Lightning Flash 16-F aspiration system. As percutaneous arterial access is required for a retrograde approach, the operators favored the use of the Penumbra system over the 22-F vacuum-assisted aspiration AngioVac system (AngioDynamics) in this case.

Vegetation Aspiration Procedural Details

On day 10, under local anesthesia, bilateral femoral access was obtained using ultrasound and fluoroscopic guidance, along with a 6-F sheath in the right radial artery. Full anticoagulation was achieved with heparin to maintain an activated clotting time >250 seconds. Root angiography was performed, and the Sentinel device (Boston Scientific) was deployed for cerebral protection. The right femoral access was upsized to a 16-F sheath, and the Penumbra Lightning Flash Indigo System 16-F catheter was introduced with support from a snare inserted via the contralateral femoral access to enhance steerability of the Penumbra system in the ascending aorta (Figure 3).Figure 3Intraprocedural Fluoroscopy Demonstrating Setup During Aortic Valve Vegetation RemovalIntraprocedural fluoroscopy demonstrating the positioning of the Penumbra 16-F Lightning Flash catheter. Guided by intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) (Video 4), TTE, and, briefly, TEE (Video 5), the vegetations were aspirated multiple times. Intraprocedural ICE images showed catheter positioning near the vegetation (Figure 2B, Video 6). Largely mobile filaments were successfully removed from the LVOT and ascending aorta. Aspiration started at the aortic valve level and continued at the left ventricular outflow level to remove most of the vegetation. A small remnant of vegetation was left in the LVOT, but ICE, TTE, and TEE confirmed significant reduction in vegetation burden and improved aortic valve function (Figures 2C, 2D, 4, and Video 7). Owing to blood loss during the procedure, 2 units of blood were transfused. Hemostasis at the right femoral access site was achieved using 1× ProGlide suture, while the left femoral artery and vein sheaths were removed manually. Outcome and Follow-Up

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis of the aspirated material identified Bartonella as the causative organism. The patient was treated with a 2-week course of gentamicin and 3 months of doxycycline, resulting in favorable clinical and imaging outcomes. Follow-up TTE performed 5 weeks later showed no residual vegetations (Figure 4A) and stable moderate-severe aortic regurgitation (Video 8) with normal left ventricular dimension and function, so surgical intervention was deferred.

Discussion

This case highlights several crucial aspects in the management of CNIE, particularly when complicated by embolic stroke and large vegetations. In recent years, CNIE has emerged as a significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenge, accounting for up to 30% of IE cases in some series.1,4 The absence of microbiological confirmation delays the initiation of targeted therapy and complicates decisions regarding surgical timing and necessity.3

Among the pathogens associated with CNIE, Bartonella species—particularly B. henselae and B. quintana—are increasingly recognized, especially in patients with valvular disease or predisposing risk factors such as homelessness, poor dentition, or exposure to cats.2,9 These organisms are difficult to culture given their intracellular nature and slow growth kinetics. In such cases, PCR and serological testing are critical for diagnosis, with high sensitivity and specificity when performed on valvular tissue or blood.3,4 This case reinforces the importance of obtaining tissue or aspirated material in culture-negative scenarios to facilitate PCR-based diagnosis and guide treatment.

Importantly, Bartonella endocarditis is associated with a high incidence of large, friable vegetations that confer significant embolic risk, especially to the cerebral circulation.2 Neurologic complications occur in approximately one-third of IE cases and are independently associated with worse outcomes.5,10 The presence of embolic stroke raises a clinical dilemma: Although surgery is indicated in patients with large vegetations and embolism, early surgery (particularly within the first week of a major ischemic stroke) may increase the risk of hemorrhagic transformation and perioperative complications.1,6

In this challenging setting, percutaneous mechanical-assisted aspiration offers a novel, less invasive strategy to reduce vegetation burden and embolic potential without the risks of cardiopulmonary bypass and general anesthesia.7,8 Although this technique has traditionally been reserved for right-sided IE, recent reports and consensus statements have supported its off-label use in selected left-sided cases, particularly when conventional surgery is deferred.11,12

The Penumbra Lightning Flash system used in the current case represents a recent advancement in mechanical aspiration technology. Compared with the 22-F AngioVac system, which has been more widely used in right-sided lesions and requires extracorporeal bypass, the Penumbra system allows for simpler retrograde access and direct aspiration of mobile material under real-time guidance.7 Alternatively, the new, smaller 18-F AngioVac and AlphaVac systems could have been used for the retroaortic approach. We selected the Penumbra Lightning Flash system because of its compatibility with a 16-F arterial sheath, making it suitable for retrograde transaortic access to aortic and LVOT masses. Larger devices such as the AngioVac were not feasible because of sheath size requirements and unavailability in our laboratory.

The use of cerebral embolic protection devices such as the Sentinel is prudent in such procedures given the manipulation of friable structures in proximity to the aortic valve.8 In our case, 1 Sentinel protection device was used; 2 Sentinel protection devices could also have been used to provide full cerebral protection by opening a third basket at the left subclavian artery origin.

ICE provided crucial procedural guidance in our case, allowing real-time visualization of vegetations, aspiration catheter positioning, and assessment of procedural efficacy without the need for general anesthesia or prolonged TEE guidance.13 ICE has been shown to improve safety and precision in structural heart interventions and is especially valuable in patients with neurologic instability.

This case adds to the emerging body of evidence supporting vacuum-assisted aspiration in high-risk IE. A growing number of case reports and small series have documented its safety and effectiveness in reducing vegetation burden, preventing embolic events, and obtaining diagnostic material.8,11 However, randomized trials are lacking, and careful patient selection remains essential. Ideal candidates are those with large, mobile vegetations, contraindications to surgery, and absence of severe valvular destruction or heart failure requiring immediate replacement.

The role of aspiration therapy may extend beyond debulking. In this case, PCR of aspirated material identified Bartonella, enabling targeted therapy with doxycycline and gentamicin, which is the recommended regimen based on observational data and treatment guidelines.2,3 Early pathogen identification not only improves outcomes but may allow for prolonged medical therapy and delayed or even avoidance of surgery in select cases.

As the field evolves, multidisciplinary collaboration between cardiology, infectious disease, neurology, and cardiac surgery teams remains central to optimizing outcomes. This case emphasizes how advanced imaging, minimally invasive techniques, and molecular diagnostics can converge to manage complex endocarditis cases that have traditionally carried poor prognoses or required high-risk interventions.

Conclusions

Mechanical aspiration can be used in the management of infective endocarditis, particularly in vague cases with culture-negative status. By facilitating organism identification and reducing vegetation burden, this approach provides a valuable alternative to traditional surgical options, especially in high-risk patients.
