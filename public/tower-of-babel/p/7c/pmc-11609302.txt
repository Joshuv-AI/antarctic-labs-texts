Phase II Trial of Regorafenib and Oral Methotrexate in Previously Treated Advanced KRAS-Mutant NSCLC
Authors: Jacqueline V. Aredo, Heather A. Wakelee, Kavitha J. Ramchandran, Joel W. Neal, Maximilian Diehn, Angela Bik-Yu Hui, Ameen Salahudeen, Bernice Kwong, Gerald J. Berry, H. ABSTRACT
IntroductionThere are no standard targeted treatment options for advanced KRAS-mutant NSCLC beyond KRAS G12C inhibitors. A computational model identified regorafenib and low-dose methotrexate as synergistic in preclinical models of KRAS-mutant NSCLC. This study evaluated the efficacy and safety of the combination in previously treated advanced KRAS-mutant NSCLC.MethodsThis single-arm phase II study included regorafenib 80 to 120 mg oral daily and oral methotrexate dose escalated to tolerability from 10 to 20 mg twice weekly during the first cycle. Both agents were administered on weeks 1 to 3 of each 4-week cycle. The primary end point was progression-free survival.ResultsIn total, 18 patients with KRAS-mutant NSCLC were enrolled. Five patients received regorafenib at a 120 mg starting dose with four discontinuing due to toxicity; subsequently, 13 patients were treated at an 80 mg starting dose, with eight dose-escalating to 120 mg after the first cycle. Median progression-free survival was 3.7 months (95% confidence interval 1.8–8.6) and median overall survival was 10.4 months (95% confidence interval 5.2–30.3). The objective response rate was 16.7% and the 8-week disease control rate was 66.7%. Grade 3 treatment-related adverse events occurred in 11 patients, most often oral mucositis (n = 2) and asymptomatic lipase increase (n = 2). One patient developed asymptomatic grade 4 lipase increase.ConclusionsCombination treatment of regorafenib and oral methotrexate in patients with KRAS-mutant NSCLC was limited due to toxicity, and the study did not meet its primary end point. Computational modeling may aid in repurposing therapeutic options though caution must be exercised with tolerability.

Introduction

KRAS mutations occur in approximately 30% of patients with NSCLC. For decades, KRAS was deemed untargetable until the development and clinical validation of KRAS G12C inhibitors in metastatic NSCLC. Despite this success, response rates and survival outcomes with these inhibitors are suboptimal.1,2 Furthermore, other KRAS mutation subtypes lack a targeted treatment option, with subtype-specific inhibitors and pan-RAS inhibitors in development.

Regorafenib is an oral multikinase inhibitor with targets that include RET, VEGFR1-3, KIT, PDGFRα/β, FGFR1/2, RAF-1, and BRAF. Regorafenib is approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration for previously treated metastatic colorectal cancer, gastrointestinal stromal tumor, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Regorafenib has been studied in lung cancer as a single agent in refractory NSCLC and in combination with cisplatin and pemetrexed chemotherapy in treatment-naive NSCLC.3,4 In the combination study, three patients had KRAS-mutant tumors, with two experiencing a partial response and all a decrease in target lesions.4 Methotrexate is a competitive inhibitor of dihydrofolate reductase involved in purine nucleotide and thymidylate synthesis. A historical placebo-controlled randomized study compared low-dose twice weekly intramuscular injections of methotrexate with placebo in patients with lung cancer and unknown molecular status,5 with objective responses observed (11%–21%).

In a computational simulation model of cancer pathway interactions occurring at the genomic, transcriptional, and proteomic levels, regorafenib and methotrexate were found to be synergistic in KRAS-mutant lung cancer cell lines (Supplementary Appendix).6,7 This synergy was confirmed in vitro in KRAS-mutant cell lines. Notably, the dose of oral methotrexate required for synergy with regorafenib was lower than the dose traditionally used in the treatment of cancer and only slightly lower than the dose used in the historical methotrexate trial.5 This is consistent with the repurposing of methotrexate for use in combination with regorafenib in KRAS-mutant NSCLC. Herein, we report the results of this pilot phase II study.

Material and Methods

This was a single-institution, single-arm, phase II trial of regorafenib in combination with oral methotrexate in patients with recurrent or metastatic KRAS-mutant nonsquamous NSCLC who progressed on prior systemic therapy. Patients with previously treated or asymptomatic less than 1 cm untreated brain metastases were permitted to enroll. Key exclusion criteria included previous treatment with regorafenib, clinically significant cardiovascular disease or hemorrhage, and uncontrolled symptomatic pleural effusion or ascites. This study was performed at the Stanford Cancer Institute and approved through the Stanford University Institutional Review Board (NCT03520842); all patients provided informed consent before participation.

The primary end point for the study was progression-free survival (PFS) as determined by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 criteria. Secondary end points included objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR) at 8 weeks, safety with adverse events (AEs) graded with the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 4.03, and pharmacokinetic parameters (trough, Cmax) of methotrexate when combined with regorafenib. An exploratory analysis correlated circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) as measured through the CAncer Personalized Profiling by Deep Sequencing (CAPP-Seq) platform with clinical outcomes in patients with detectable ctDNA at baseline (Supplementary Appendix).8

Regorafenib was initially administered at 120 mg oral daily on days 1 to 21 of each 28-day cycle. After four of five patients experienced toxicities that led to treatment discontinuation, the study protocol was amended to administer regorafenib at a starting dose of 80 mg oral daily. Dose escalation to 120 mg daily was permitted after the first cycle though not required. Methotrexate was administered twice weekly on days 1 to 21 of each 28-day cycle at a starting dose of 10 mg oral twice weekly. Methotrexate was increased to 15 mg twice weekly on week 2 of treatment if tolerated and to a maximum dose of 20 mg twice weekly on week 3 if tolerated, to minimize gastrointestinal toxicities while maintaining bioavailability based on preclinical studies. Dose escalation of methotrexate could continue after the first cycle if 20 mg had not been reached.

Our null hypothesis assumed a median PFS of 2 months. To detect an alternative median PFS of 8 months, 18 patients would provide 80% power to reject the null hypothesis at a one-sided significance level of 5% (Supplementary Appendix). Given that the combination had not been previously studied, dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) were evaluated in the first cycle. A DLT was generally defined as any CTCAE grade 3 or 4 toxicity that was clinically significant and related to study treatment, with details noted in the Supplementary Appendix.

To determine the pharmacokinetic parameters of oral methotrexate when combined with regorafenib, serum trough and peak levels were measured during the first cycle (Supplementary Fig. 1). Mean trough levels were 0.04 μmol/L (the lower limit of the assay) throughout the cycle. Mean peak levels ranged from 0.43 to 0.60 μmol/L throughout the cycle. On the basis of in vitro cell line data, the target maximum serum concentration for methotrexate was at least 0.25 μmol/L.9

Baseline ctDNA was detectable in seven patients, and there was no consistent correlation with best radiographic response (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

This phase II clinical trial of regorafenib and oral methotrexate in advanced KRAS-mutant NSCLC did not meet its primary end point, with a median PFS of 3.7 months and an ORR of 16.7%. The combination led to considerable toxicities which improved with a lower starting dosage of regorafenib but still led to early treatment discontinuation in almost 40% of the cohort and possibly limited the efficacy of this therapy. Thus, in the era of big data and digital transformation of oncology, this study highlights the importance of including safety checkpoints in the translation of promising therapeutic approaches to patient care.

In phase III studies of single-agent regorafenib, grade 3 TRAEs emerged in 46% to 58% of patients, and AEs resulted in treatment cessation in 6% to 10% of patients.10, 11, 12 The randomized phase II ReDOS study evaluated single-agent regorafenib in metastatic colorectal cancer using a dose-escalation strategy, starting at 80 mg daily with weekly escalation by 40 mg to 160 mg total, compared with a standard-dose strategy (160 mg daily).13 The study’s primary end point was met with a significantly greater number of patients who initiated cycle 3 in the dose-escalation group compared with the standard-dose group, while achieving similar survival outcomes, suggesting that dose escalation of regorafenib may help to improve tolerability and treatment adherence. In line with this analysis, we found that reducing the starting dosage of regorafenib to 80 mg daily in this study improved treatment tolerability, with 61.5% (8 of 13) of patients subsequently able to dose escalate regorafenib to 120 mg daily and 92.3% (12 of 13) of patients able to dose escalate methotrexate to the target 20 mg twice weekly.

This study was designed and conducted before the clinical availability of KRAS G12C inhibitors and rapid development of KRAS targeted therapies. Although the combination therapy was significantly limited due to toxicity, we identified some potential signals in patients with KRAS non-G12C mutant tumors, for which there are no clinically available targeted treatment options. Two patients with KRAS G12D mutations achieved partial responses and another patient with KRAS G12D achieved prolonged stable disease. The various KRAS mutation subtypes were found to have differential activation of downstream effector pathways in preclinical models which may affect their sensitivity to different classes of agents.14,15 KRAS G12D mutations exhibit a particular sensitivity to sorafenib, a similar though less potent multikinase inhibitor to regorafenib.15 It is possible that regorafenib could exploit a mechanistic vulnerability unique to the KRAS G12D-mutant subtype. Nevertheless, this observation is purely hypothesis generating at this time. Future evaluations of regorafenib could be considered in a narrower KRAS G12D-mutant NSCLC subset or in another KRAS G12D-dominant malignancy, such as pancreatic cancer.

This study is limited by a small sample size and a high proportion of early treatment discontinuation due to toxicity from the higher regorafenib starting dose. Most patients (89%) were previously treated with immunotherapy, possibly contributing to higher toxicities. Although a variety of KRAS mutation subtypes were represented, enabling identification of a potential signal in KRAS G12D, this trial did not include broader next-generation sequencing to evaluate co-mutations in tumor suppressors, and baseline ctDNA levels were only detectable in seven patients.

In conclusion, computational modeling may aid in identifying cancer therapeutic options for clinical trial assessment, though caution must be exercised when advancing combinations given potential toxicity burden.
