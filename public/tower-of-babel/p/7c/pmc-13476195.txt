Hospitalisation Burden of Human Metapneumovirus and Respiratory Syncytial Virus in Adults by Age and Comorbidity Status in Scotland: A Retrospective Analysis
Authors: Durga Kulkarni, Richard Osei-Yeboah, Kate Templeton, Harish Nair
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is well recognised as an important contributor to respiratory tract infections (RTIs) in adults in Scotland. However, there is a lack of recognition of the role of human metapneumovirus (hMPV) in RTIs in this population. We compared RSV- and hMPV-associated hospital burden in Scottish adults according to age and comorbidity status before RSV vaccine introduction.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of adults (≥ 18 years) using routinely collected and linked national laboratory, hospital, ICU, and mortality data in Scotland (1 July 2017–30 June 2023). Comorbidity status was defined using UK influenza clinical risk groups mapped from Systematised Nomenclature of Medicine—Clinical Terms (SNOMED-CT) to International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 codes. Negative binomial regression was used to estimate adjusted relative hospitalisation rates by virus, age, comorbidity burden (0, 1, > 1), and season. Logistic regression examined associations between virus and long length of stay (LOS), ICU or high-dependency unit (HDU) admission, and 90-day mortality, adjusting for demographic characteristics, number of underlying comorbidities, and COVID-19 pandemic.ResultsRSV was associated with higher laboratory-detected hospitalisation rates than hMPV (relative rate ratio 2.31, 95% confidence interval [CI] 2.20–2.43). Hospitalisation rates increased with advancing age, particularly among adults aged ≥ 65 years and with increasing comorbidity burden. Rates varied substantially by season, with marked reductions during the COVID-19 pandemic and a resurgence in 2022/23. Compared with hMPV, RSV was associated with lower odds of prolonged length of stay and ICU/HDU admission, while no substantial difference was observed in 90-day mortality. Across both viruses, increasing age and comorbidity burden were the strongest correlates of adverse clinical outcomes.ConclusionsRSV and hMPV are associated with a substantial hospital burden in adults. Incidence differences likely reflect testing variation. Clinical severity among hospitalised patients was broadly similar between the viruses. Age and multimorbidity were stronger correlates of severe outcomes than virus. The findings highlight the importance of host factors and the need for improved, consistent surveillance to generate robust estimates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01410-8.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and human metapneumovirus (hMPV) are commonly associated with human respiratory tract infections. Both viruses are associated with a substantial burden of hospitalisation and severe disease, particularly in older adults and individuals with comorbidities [1–5]. In Scotland, RSV is relatively well characterised and is also the focus of newly introduced vaccines [6]. hMPV, however, is less consistently tested for in routine clinical practice [7]. This results in important gaps in understanding its population-level epidemiology and clinical burden.

Direct comparison of RSV and hMPV within the Scottish healthcare system therefore provides an opportunity to contextualise hMPV-associated hospital burden across age groups and comorbidity strata. This is particularly important given the limited population-level characterisation of hMPV and the absence of robust population denominators stratified by clinical risk factors. Using RSV for comparison, therefore, provides a benchmark for interpreting hMPV burden within a shared diagnostic and healthcare context.

This comparison is timely given the recent licensure of RSV vaccines and the ongoing development of combination vaccines targeting both RSV and hMPV. It is also relevant in the current context of ageing populations and increasing multimorbidity in Scotland, both of which are associated with a higher risk of severe respiratory outcomes [8, 9]. Comparative estimates for hMPV and RSV are therefore needed to inform health service planning and prevention strategies.

This study compared the hospital burden of RSV and hMPV in adults in Scotland between 2017 and 2023 (pre-RSV vaccine era), using laboratory-confirmed incidence of hospital admissions and clinical severity across age and comorbidity strata.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using Scottish national laboratory and hospital registry data from 1 July 2017 to 30 June 2023.

Eligible persons in Scotland were offered the RSV vaccine from 1 August 2024 [6]. Prior to this, no vaccines against hMPV or RSV were implemented through the national immunisation programme during the study period.

Case Definitions

We defined adults as individuals aged ≥ 18 years.

Comorbidity status was defined as those in clinical risk groups eligible for influenza vaccination according to the UK Green Book guidelines [10]. This includes individuals with specified chronic conditions such as cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, hepatic, neurological disease, diabetes, immunosuppression, and morbid obesity.

We included inpatient and non-routine hospitalisation episodes. Day cases and routine admissions were excluded from the analysis, using the relevant classification codes, as these are expected to have different resource utilisation patterns and a low likelihood of representing hospitalisation linked to RSV or hMPV infection.

Virus-specific hospital episodes were defined as admissions with a positive RSV or hMPV test from a specimen collected within 7 days before admission or up to 3 days after admission. The 7-day pre-admission window was chosen to reflect the known incubation period of RSV and hMPV (approximately 3–6 days), allowing infections acquired shortly before admission to be attributed to the hospital episode [11]. The 3-day post-admission window was included to capture patients tested shortly after admission, while minimising misclassification of hospital-acquired (nosocomial) infections. A 28-day washout period was applied at the patient level so that repeat laboratory tests within 28 days were considered part of the same infection; only positive tests separated by more than 28 days were counted as distinct test results.

As with our previous analyses, we defined an (annual) season as the period between 1 July of one year and 30 June of the next year [12]. The first three seasons (2017/18, 2018/19, and 2019/20) were classified as pre-COVID-19 pandemic seasons, and the subsequent three seasons (2020/21, 2021/22, and 2022/23) as COVID-19 pandemic/post-COVID-19 pandemic seasons, long length of stay (LOS) was defined as a hospital stay of more than 5 days [13].

Intensive care unit (ICU) admissions included both ICU and high-dependency unit (HDU) admissions within RSV and hMPV hospitalisation episodes.

Ninety-day mortality was defined as the deaths occurring up to 90 days post-discharge, including in-hospital deaths. Stratification by in-hospital and post-discharge deaths was not feasible in the analysis because of low counts (< 5) in multiple groups, leading to unstable estimates and data protection concerns.

Data Sources and Data Linking

Hospital episode data were obtained from the Scottish Morbidity Records 01 (SMR01), which includes all inpatient and day case episodes from non-obstetric and non-psychiatric specialties in Scotland [14].

Laboratory-confirmed RSV and hMPV infections were obtained from the Electronic Communication of Surveillance Scotland (ECOSS), based on routine clinical testing [15].

ICU or HDU admission data were obtained from the Scottish Intensive Care Society Audit Group (SICSAG) [16]. Mortality data were obtained from the National Records of Scotland (NRS) [17].

Data were linked using pseudonymised patient identifiers. SMR01 and ECOSS records were additionally matched using admission dates and specimen collection dates (as described above). SMR01 (hospital admission episode) records were linked to SICSAG (ICU/HDU) data using hospital admission dates and corresponding ICU/HDU admission dates, within the same hospital stay.

The Systematised Nomenclature of Medicine—Clinical Terms (SNOMED CT) codes for individuals eligible for influenza vaccination during the 2025 winter season were obtained from the NHS Digital Cohorting as a Service (CaaS) platform [18]. Although defined for England, these criteria align with the Joint Committee on Vaccination and Immunisation (JCVI) recommendations and apply to Scotland [10, 19].

SNOMED CT concepts were mapped to ICD-10-CM codes using the SNOMED CT Snapshot Extended Map Reference Set and linked using SNOMED CT identifiers [20]. Where multiple mappings existed, the highest-priority mapping was selected based on the Map_Priority variable.

Population estimates used to calculate incidence rate ratios (IRR) were obtained from the open-access National Records of Scotland [21, 22]. For each season, mid-year population figures corresponding to the end of the season were used.

Ethical Approval

The Public Benefit and Privacy Panel for Health and Social Care (PBPP-HSC) approved data access to the required data in the National Safe Haven (Approval number 2223-0019). Data were linked using pseudonymised patient identifiers assigned before data receipt. The active consent of patients was not required. The data were collected routinely through NHS Scotland, with patients informed of potential use and their rights through various PHS and NHS Scotland privacy notices.

Statistical Analysis

All data processing and analyses were conducted using RStudio (version 4.5.3).

A complete-case approach was used, whereby observations with missing data for any variables included in a given model were excluded from that analysis.

We used negative binomial regression to estimate adjusted relative rates of hospitalisation by virus, age, number of comorbidities (0, 1, > 1), and season. Because population denominators stratified jointly by age and comorbidity were unavailable, age-specific population denominators were used as exposure offsets. Consequently, estimates for comorbidity represent adjusted relative hospitalisation rates based on the available season and age-specific denominators and should not be interpreted as true population incidence rate ratios by comorbidity. Profile likelihood confidence intervals were used to improve robustness in the presence of sparse data and small cell counts.

Observed proportions for intensive care unit (ICU) admission, prolonged hospital length of stay (LOS), and 90-day mortality were calculated as the number of events divided by the total number of hospital episodes within each stratum defined by virus type, age group, and comorbidity count. Low event counts did not allow further stratification by season leading to data protection concerns and unstable estimates. Estimates were expressed as percentages for interpretability. The 95% CI were estimated using nonparametric bootstrap resampling.

Logistic regression was used to examine associations between virus type and severity outcomes, adjusting for age, sex, SIMD, comorbidity count, and pandemic period. Ethnicity and season were excluded because of sparse data. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using profile likelihood methods to improve robustness under sparse data conditions. Model performance was assessed using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), Hosmer–Lemeshow tests, generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF), and diagnostics for separation and influential observations [23–25].

Inference was based on effect sizes and 95% CI. P values were not reported, as emphasis was placed on estimation rather than formal hypothesis testing.

Results

This analysis included 3561 laboratory-confirmed RSV and 1386 laboratory-confirmed hMPV hospital admissions among adults in Scotland, between 1 July 2017 and 30 June 2023. Most admissions occurred in adults aged ≥ 65 years (RSV 64.5%; hMPV 59.96%). Sex distribution was similar across viruses. Most admissions occurred in individuals with at least one comorbidity. Marked temporal variation was observed, with reduced admissions during the pandemic period and increased RSV activity in 2022/23. Clinical severity outcomes were broadly similar between viruses.

Discussion

This study provides an assessment of RSV and hMPV using routinely collected laboratory and hospital data in Scotland, examining differences in laboratory-detected incidence and associated clinical outcomes across age and comorbidity strata. Overall, RSV was detected more frequently than hMPV across most groups and seasons, although the magnitude of these differences varied across strata. Across all analyses, age and comorbidity were key correlates of hospitalisation and clinical severity for both viruses. The between-virus differences were modest especially for severity outcomes. Higher overall observed detection of RSV during the study period likely reflects a combination of true differences in viral circulation and variability in testing practices over time and across clinical settings [7, 26]. As a result, the estimates should be interpreted as differences in laboratory-detected hospitalised disease rather than true population infection rates, and may represent a lower bound of the true burden for both viruses.

Differences between RSV and hMPV hospital admissions were most evident in older adults, while patterns by comorbidity and younger age groups were less in magnitude. This suggests that age-related vulnerability was strongly associated with severe respiratory viral disease in this population, whereas comorbidity effects were more heterogeneous. However, the associations with comorbidity number are likely influenced by differences in underlying health status for individual comorbidity groups, coding completeness, and testing practices across risk groups and also by residual variation in comorbidity ascertainment and differential testing behaviour across clinical subgroups. As the negative binomial model was inferential rather than predictive, diagnostics were used to assess stability and adequacy of the regression framework rather than predictive performance. The observed residual deviance reflects heterogeneity inherent in routinely collected surveillance data and does not affect the interpretation of the estimated associations.

In severity analysis, age and number of comorbidities showed the strongest and consistent associations with long LOS and 90-day mortality, with increasing odds observed in older age groups and those with higher comorbidity burden for both RSV and hMPV. In contrast, ICU/HDU admission showed a different age pattern, with lower odds observed in older age groups compared with younger individuals. This pattern may reflect differences in escalation of care, underlying frailty, or treatment-limitation decisions; however, these cannot be directly assessed in the present study because of the lack of clinical severity and decision-making data, and this interpretation remains speculative [27].

SIMD did not demonstrate consistent associations with severity patterns. This may reflect residual confounding, incomplete capture of relevant social correlates, or heterogeneous pathways linking deprivation to healthcare access and outcomes. Admission during the COVID-19 pandemic/post-COVID-19 pandemic period was associated with higher odds of long LOS and 90-day mortality but not ICU/HDU admission. This likely reflects multiple concurrent system-level effects, including altered admission thresholds, ICU capacity constraints, changes in healthcare-seeking behaviour, infection control-related discharge delays, and shifts in patient case-mix due to disrupted respiratory virus circulation [28, 29]. These factors may have acted in different directions across outcomes, precluding any assumption of a uniform bias. Model diagnostics supported the validity of the estimated associations, although the models are intended for explanatory inference rather than individual-level prediction.

Comparisons with external studies should be interpreted cautiously because of heterogeneity in testing practices, case definitions, and surveillance systems across settings. In terms of severity of disease, we have previously examined similar severity outcomes of laboratory-confirmed hMPV and RSV hospital admissions in Scotland over the same period among older adults (≥ 60 years), regardless of comorbidity status [12]. In the current analysis, the observed proportions of prolonged length of stay, ICU admission, and 90-day mortality among younger individuals with more than one comorbidity were comparable to the proportions of these outcomes observed in that study. Similar findings have been reported for RSV in other UK-based studies, particularly for individuals with immunosuppression and chronic respiratory disease (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cystic fibrosis, and poorly controlled asthma) [30]. Although we did not stratify by individual comorbidities, the observed associations between increasing age, higher comorbidity burden, and worse clinical outcomes for hMPV and RSV are broadly consistent with findings from other high-income countries, including Canada, France, and the USA [31–33]. Overall, this suggests that younger patients with multiple comorbid conditions represent an important high-risk group for severe outcomes and should be considered alongside older adults when planning preventive strategies.

This study included all individuals with laboratory-confirmed RSV or hMPV infection, without restriction to those presenting with specific respiratory symptoms. This approach was used to ensure case ascertainment was based on virological detection rather than clinical phenotype, as atypical presentations are common in older and multimorbid populations. Moreover, certain bacteria commonly colonise the respiratory tract and do not always indicate active disease [34]. In comparison, respiratory viruses are less consistent with long-term colonisation, and evidence from studies in older adult populations suggests that viral detection is frequently attributable to clinically relevant disease episodes [35]. Although this evidence is limited to older adults, it supports the use of laboratory-confirmed viral detection as a marker of acute infection in hospitalised populations.

This study has several limitations. First, testing practices may have varied by virus, patient characteristics, region, severity of illness, and time, with implications for both case ascertainment and outcome measurement. As a result, estimates reflect laboratory-detected disease within routine clinical testing rather than true underlying infection rates. Differences between RSV and hMPV may therefore partly reflect variation in testing intensity and clinical ascertainment rather than true epidemiological differences. Similarly, severity analyses were restricted to laboratory-confirmed cases and may be influenced by differential testing of more severe presentations (e.g. prolonged hospital stay or ICU/HDU admission). Accordingly, findings should be interpreted as patterns of detected disease within the healthcare system rather than unbiased estimates of infection burden.

Second, residual confounding is possible due to the exclusion of ethnicity and season from the severity models because of sparse data and instability. Although pandemic period was included as a variable to partially account for system-level disruption, unmeasured confounding from social, behavioural, and clinical factors cannot be excluded.

Third, comorbidity classification based on SNOMED-to-ICD mapping may be subject to misclassification. Furthermore, grouping comorbidities into broad categories (0, 1, > 1) may mask clinically important heterogeneity between conditions, including respiratory disease, cardiovascular disease, and immunosuppression. Fourth, administrative data may incompletely capture comorbidities, particularly those defined partly by medication use (e.g. asthma and immunosuppression under UK Green Book criteria), as prescription data were not available [10]. This may have led to non-differential misclassification between the viruses, likely biasing the estimates towards the null.

Conclusions

This study indicates that hMPV and RSV are associated with a substantial burden on hospital services. Differences in detected incidence between the two viruses should be interpreted in the context of potential variability in testing and ascertainment. Clinical outcomes showed consistent associations with age and comorbidity burden, with broadly similar 90-day mortality between the viruses and only modest differences in other severity measures. Overall, these findings suggest that host factors are key correlates of disease severity, alongside virus-related differences in observed hospitalisation burden. These findings highlight the importance of improved surveillance and consistent testing practices to better characterise the burden of these respiratory viruses.
