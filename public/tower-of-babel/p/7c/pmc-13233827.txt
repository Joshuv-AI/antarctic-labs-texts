Quantitative susceptibility mapping and MRS-based multimodal machine learning for early Parkinson’s disease classification
Authors: Yuan Tian, Yaqiang Zhang, Yingzhe Cui, Dong Nan, Jingyi Liu, Qi Wang, Junhong Duan, Jianxiu Lian, Song Tian, Liangjie Lin, Huailei Liu, Pengfei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson’s disease (PD) is a complex, progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by high heterogeneity and diagnostic challenges in its early stages. This study aimed to develop and validate a multimodal machine learning model for early PD detection by integrating neurochemical metabolites and QSM-based radiomic features from multicenter PD cohorts. Several model architectures, including Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Light Gradient Boosting Machine, were evaluated for comparing appropriate methods. The XGBoost model demonstrated superior predictive performance, achieving AUC values of 0.984 and 0.973 in the training and test cohorts, respectively. Feature importance analysis identified key diagnostic biomarkers using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and enhanced model interpretability. This study can provide new insights into the neurobiological mechanisms underlying early PD.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the fastest-growing neurological disorder worldwide, with the number of affected individuals projected to reach 25.2 million by 20501,2. Late diagnosis hampers the development and evaluation of Disease-Modifying Therapies3–5. Early diagnosis is therefore crucial for timely intervention and improved patient outcomes; however, reliable diagnostic tools for early-stage PD remain lacking.

The advancement of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has provided new opportunities for the noninvasive early diagnosis of PD6, and multimodal MRI integration has been shown to enhance diagnostic accuracy7,8. Proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (¹H-MRS), a noninvasive technique, enables the quantification of neurochemical metabolites within selected brain regions9. In particular, the Mescher–Garwood point-resolved spectroscopy (MEGA-PRESS) sequence has demonstrated robust capability in measuring the concentration of the inhibitory neurotransmitter γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) in vivo10,11. Our previous study used MEGA-PRESS to quantify GABA levels in the sensorimotor cortex (SMC) for PD diagnosis, achieving excellent discriminative performance (AUC = 0.933)12.

Quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM), by quantifying brain iron content, reveals abnormal iron accumulation in PD patients13–15, reflecting underlying neurodegenerative processes. Díaz-Galván et al. developed a QSM-based diagnostic model that demonstrated considerable diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.74)16. However, conventional susceptibility metrics mainly provide global quantification of iron deposition and fail to capture its spatial heterogeneity. However, MRI data are often high-dimensional, heterogeneous, and nonlinear, making it difficult for traditional analytical methods to fully exploit their latent information.

Radiomics is a high-throughput computational approach that extracts and analyzes large numbers of quantitative features from medical images to uncover disease-related patterns. Especially, it enables comprehensive analysis of the spatial distribution and texture characteristics of iron deposition by extracting high-dimensional quantitative features from QSM images, offering deeper insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying PD17. Kang et al. constructed a PD diagnostic model based on QSM-derived radiomic features, which achieved significantly improved diagnostic accuracy compared with models relying solely on mean susceptibility values18.

In addition, machine learning is transforming the field of neuroimaging by offering effective solutions to such data complexity19,20. Machine learning classifiers including Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM). The application of multimodal variables combined with machine learning algorithms has been shown to improve model accuracy21. Nevertheless, current studies still face multiple challenges, including limited sample sizes, single-modality diagnostic approaches, and single-center research designs, all of which restrict the establishment of reliable and generalizable diagnostic models for early-stage PD22.

In this study, our goal was to establish a more precise and interpretable classification model, paving the way for earlier therapeutic intervention in PD by integrating multicenter PD datasets with explainable machine learning techniques. We used GABA levels in the sensorimotor cortex with radiomic features extracted from QSM as input variables for machine learning classifiers, including RF, SVM, XGBoost and LightGBM to differentiate PD patients from healthy controls. Two independent PD cohorts were incorporated to enhance the robustness and generalizability of the models. Finally, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were employed to elucidate the contribution of each feature to model predictions, thereby improving the interpretability of the predictive framework23.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the subjects

The study finally included 170 early-stage PD patients and 115 healthy controls (HCs). Among them, the Harbin Research Center had 120 PD patients and 75 HCs, and the Third Xiangya Hospital of Central South University had 50 PD patients and 40 HCs. In this study, the Harbin cohort was used as the training cohor for model development, and the Changsha cohort served as an independent test cohort to evaluate the model’s generalization performance.

The demographic and clinical characteristics of all participants are summarized in Table 1. No significant differences were observed between the training and test cohorts in terms of sex (p = 0.868) or age (p = 0.312). Among PD patients, disease duration, dominant body side, and Hoehn–Yahr stage did not differ significantly between the two cohorts. Similarly, no significant inter-cohort differences were found for clinical scales, including the Movement Disorder Society–Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale Part III (MDS-UPDRS-III), Non-Motor Symptoms Scale (NMSS), and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) scores (all p > 0.05). Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores were also comparable between the training and test sets (p = 0.406).

Differences in the GABA, and Glx measurements

No significant differences were observed in MEGA-PRESS data quality metrics (fitting error and FWHM), nor in the proportions of gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) within the voxel of interest (VOI) (Table 1). After adjusting for age, sex, and GM fraction, Glx/Cr and Glx/water levels in the SMC did not differ significantly between the training and test cohorts. However, GABA + /Cr and GABA + /water levels showed significant differences between the two datasets.

Within-cohort comparisons demonstrated that GABA + /Cr levels were significantly lower in the PD group than in the HC group in both the training cohort (PD: 0.057 ± 0.023 vs. HC: 0.104 ± 0.022, p < 0.001) and the test cohort (PD: 0.059 ± 0.016 vs. HC: 0.103 ± 0.017, p < 0.001). Similarly, GABA + /water levels were significantly reduced in PD patients compared with HCs in both the training (PD: 1.078 ± 0.463 vs. HC: 1.705 ± 0.352, p < 0.001) and test cohorts (PD: 1.172 ± 0.531 vs. HC: 1.673 ± 0.334, p < 0.001).

Spearman correlation analysis revealed no significant correlation between GABA levels in the SMC and age in either the training cohort or the test cohort (Supplementary Table S1). In partial correlation analyses controlling for GM fraction, GABA levels in the SMC likewise demonstrated no significant correlation with age (all p > 0.05).

Discussion

Given that the diagnosis of early-stage PD remains challenging, this study proposed a multimodal diagnostic model based on machine learning algorithms, innovatively integrating radiomic features from QSM and MEGA-PRESS spectroscopy to achieve accurate classification between early PD and healthy controls. The results demonstrated that the multimodal model based on XGBoost achieved superior predictive performance compared with other state-of-the-art models.

Considering potential differences in PD severity assessments among centers, this study focused on binary classification—distinguishing PD from HC—rather than staging or predicting disease severity24. Model performance was further validated using five-fold cross-validation to enhance robustness and generalizability. The results showed that the XGBoost model achieved AUC values of 0.984 and 0.973 in the training and test cohorts, respectively, demonstrating excellent classification performance and strong generalization ability. Other models also showed good classification performance in both cohorts.

The integration of multimodal data provides a more comprehensive approach for distinguishing PD from HC, reducing potential bias and limitations associated with single-modality analyses25. The key novelty of this study lies in its innovative integration of complementary modalities to enhance PD prediction. Using MEGA-PRESS, we analyzed changes in GABA + /Cr levels in the SMC of PD patients, which were significantly lower than those in HCs after adjusting for age, sex, and GM fraction, consistent with findings reported by Van Nuland et al.26. Additionally, radiomic features extracted from QSM images were used to characterize the spatial distribution of iron deposition in PD patients. By integrating neurotransmitter concentration data and pathological iron deposition using machine learning, we constructed a multimodal diagnostic model. Compared with previously reported multimodal machine learning models27–29, our model achieved superior classification performance.

To further assess the performance difference between unimodal and multimodal models, we constructed XGBoost classifiers using either SMC GABA levels or radiomic features as unimodal inputs and evaluated them using five-fold cross-validation. The results (Supplementary Tables S4–S5 and Supplementary Figs. S12, 13) revealed that the multimodal model substantially outperformed the unimodal models in classification efficacy (Delong’ s test p < 0.05). The study further compared the performance of support vector machine models based on radial basis function kernels versus those using linear kernels. The results demonstrated that the model employing a radial basis function kernel outperformed the linear kernel-based model in distinguishing between early-stage Parkinson’s disease patients and healthy controls (Delong’s test p < 0.05) (Supplementary Tables S6–S7 and Supplementary Figs. S14–S17). Previous research by Pinamonti et al. has similarly indicated that the radial basis function kernel is more suitable than the linear kernel for processing neuroimaging data30.

To improve model interpretability, we applied SHAP analysis to identify key diagnostic biomarkers and better understand how the machine learning models generate predictions, thereby supporting their potential clinical application (Supplementary Figs. S6–S11). SHAP results indicated that GABA levels in the SMC contributed the most across all four machine learning models, followed by LALGLE. Previous studies by Li and Kolasinski et al. have demonstrated a positive correlation between general cognitive ability and baseline GABA levels31,32. In our study, a significant positive correlation between SMC GABA levels and MoCA scores was observed across the entire PD cohort (Spearman’s rho = 0.479, p < 0.001). This association remained significant after controlling for age (r = 0.445, p < 0.001) and further adjusting for age, sex, age at onset, and GM fraction (r = 0.441, p < 0.001) in partial correlation analyses (Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Fig. S1), consistent with prior findings by Li and Kolasinski.

Furthermore, we examined the correlations between radiomic features in the substantia nigra and clinical scales (Supplementary Table S2). After adjusting for age, sex, disease onset age, and GM fraction, MAD (r = -0.275, p < 0.001) and LALGLE (r = -0.322, p < 0.001) were both significantly correlated with MoCA scores (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Previous studies have mostly relied on single-center cohorts33–36, often limited by small sample sizes and single imaging modalities, which can lead to overfitting and poor generalizability, thereby restricting clinical applicability. To address these limitations, the present study incorporated multiple machine learning algorithms, multicenter datasets, and multimodal variables. Four distinct machine learning models were used to diagnose early PD, leveraging the strengths of different algorithms. Tree-based algorithms such as XGBoost and LightGBM are effective in modeling complex variable interactions for classification, SVMs employing a radial basis function kernel can construct optimal classification hyperplanes in high-dimensional spaces37,38, and Random Forests enhance accuracy and generalization through ensemble decision trees25.

This study has several potential limitations. First, as a cross-sectional design, it cannot assess the model’s predictive performance for disease progression, which warrants future longitudinal studies. Second, although the study included multicenter PD cohorts (n = 2), the number of datasets remains relatively small. Future research should incorporate more independent cohorts to further validate the model’s generalizability, robustness, and interpretability. Third, although pathological confirmation and additional biomarkers such as CSF α-synuclein or DaTSCAN imaging would further strengthen diagnostic certainty, these data were not available in the present cohort and should be considered in future longitudinal or multimodal studies. Fourth, advanced biomarker screening for prodromal Parkinson’s disease was not performed, limiting the early identification of at-risk individuals. Future studies should integrate such screening to improve early detection and intervention strategies.

In conclusion, this study highlights the potential of explainable machine learning models in distinguishing early-stage PD from HC. Among the architectures investigated, the XGBoost-based multimodal model achieved the highest classification performance while maintaining interpretability through SHAP analysis. This model framework holds promise for extension to PD subtype classification and clinical implementation. This study is intended to provide objective imaging-based markers that complement clinical assessment, particularly in early or diagnostically challenging cases.

Methods

Study population

This study included two independent cohorts of participants. Subjects were consecutively recruited from the following centers:(1) Harbin Cohort: A total of 365 patients with early-stage PD (Hoehn & Yahr stage 1–2) and 212 HCs were enrolled at the First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University between 2023 and 2025;(2) Changsha Cohort: Seventy-eight early-stage PD patients and Seventy-one HCs were recruited at the Third Xiangya Hospital of Central South University.

The inclusion criteria for PD patients at the Harbin center were as follows: (1) A diagnosis of PD confirmed by experienced movement disorder specialists according to the UK Parkinson’s Disease Society Brain Bank (UKPDSBB) criteria, with Hoehn & Yahr stage ≤ 239; (2) No evidence of cerebrovascular disease, brain tumors, or head trauma on MRI; (3) No history of psychiatric or other neurological disorders; (4) Adequate image quality without significant motion artifacts.

Healthy controls underwent the same screening procedures. Exclusion criteria included: (1) A history of CNS diseases such as brain tumors, head trauma, or any psychiatric disorders; (2) Contraindications to MRI scanning; (3) Prominent motion artifacts on MRI. The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria for the Changsha cohort are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Motor and non-motor symptoms in PD patients were assessed using the Movement Disorder Society–Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale Part III and the Non-Motor Symptoms Scale, respectively. Sleep quality was evaluated with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, and cognitive function was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment. All PD patients discontinued antiparkinsonian medications for at least 12 h prior to MRI scanning to ensure an “OFF” state. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (2025283) and the Ethics Committee of The Third Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (2025-S706). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

MRI acquisitions

MRI data from the Harbin research center were acquired on a 3.0 T scanner (Ingenia Elition, Philips Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands) using a 32-channel head coil.

T1-weighted (T1W) images were obtained with the following parameters: resolution = 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm³; TR/TE = 8.2/3.7 ms; flip angle = 8°; matrix = 256 × 256; and field of view = 24 × 24 cm².

A 3D 10-echo strategically acquired gradient echo (STAGE) protocol was used to obtain QSM data with the following parameters: TR = 43 ms; TE1 = 4.1 ms; ΔTE = 4.1 ms; flip angle = 20°; pixel bandwidth = 775 Hz; field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 × 256 mm3; matrix size was 256 × 254 × 70 with 1 × 1 × 2 mm3 voxel resolution (scan time 6 min 22 s).

GABA and Glx levels were quantified using a MEGA-PRESS sequence localized to the sensorimotor cortex (voxel size = 3.0 × 3.0 × 3.0 cm³). Acquisition parameters were as follows: TR/TE = 2000/68 ms; spectral bandwidth = 2000 Hz; data points = 2048; signal averages = 320 (160 ON and 160 OFF scans); water suppression = VAPOUR (variable power RF pulses with optimized relaxation delays); editing pulse frequencies = 1.9 ppm and 7.5 ppm; editing pulse bandwidth = 100 Hz; and total scan time = 10 min 50 s.

MRI acquisition parameters for the Third Xiangya Hospital of Central South University are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Table S8).

To mitigate image intensity bias caused by magnetic field inhomogeneity, all MRI data were preprocessed using the N4 bias field correction algorithm40. This correction improves image uniformity and enhances tissue contrast, thereby reducing potential bias and artifacts that could affect analytic accuracy. Furthermore, all MRI images were normalized to minimize variability arising from scanner settings, subject positioning, and acquisition parameter differences.

QSM reconstruction

The raw phase images were first processed using a Laplacian-based phase unwrapping algorithm, and a brain mask was generated from the magnitude images using the Brain Extraction Tool (BET). Background field removal was performed with the variable spherical mean value filtering (V-SHARP) method, followed by quantitative susceptibility map reconstruction using the iterative least squares with QR decomposition (iLSQR) algorithm.

On the MATLAB R2020b platform, spatial co-registration between the 3D T1-weighted structural images and QSM maps was performed using SPM12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/download/). The co-registered QSM maps were then normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard space with a voxel resolution of 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 using the CAT12 toolbox (http://www.neuro.uni-jena.de/cat/). All normalized QSM images were visually inspected by an experienced neuroradiologist, and manual corrections were performed when necessary (Fig. 5).

MRS processing

MEGA-PRESS data were processed and analyzed using Gannet 3.1.3 (https://gith.ub.com/markmikkelsen/Gannet) on the MATLAB R2020b platform. The preprocessing pipeline included 3 Hz line broadening, frequency and phase correction, outlier suppression, time-domain averaging, and eddy current correction. Edited difference spectra were generated by subtracting the averaged “OFF” spectra from the averaged “ON” spectra. The difference spectra were then modeled using a single Gaussian fit for the GABA signal at 3.02 ppm and a double Gaussian fit for the Glx signal at 3.8 ppm, yielding quantitative GABA/Cr and Glx/Cr ratios. The MEGA-PRESS-detected resonance at 3.02 ppm was referred to as “GABA + ” rather than GABA, as it also includes contributions from macromolecules and homocarnosine41. All spectra were visually inspected to exclude artifacts, and only spectra with a fitting error below 15% and a full width at half maximum (FWHM) value below 25 Hz were included in the subsequent analyses. An example spectrum acquired from the SMC is shown in Fig. 5.

MRS voxel tissue composition was derived using SPM12. Each MRS voxel was co-registered to the 3D T1-weighted structural image, which was then segmented into gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid compartments. The volumetric fractions of each tissue type within the MRS voxel were calculated and recorded.

Radiomics feature extraction and selection

Based on previous studies by Trist et al, degeneration of dopaminergic neurons and abnormal α-synuclein aggregation in the substantia nigra are the main pathological substrates of Parkinson’s disease clinical symptoms42,43. Therefore, the substantia nigra was selected as the region of interest (ROI) in this study. The study uses the Automatic Anatomical Labeling atlas (AAL3)44 as the baseline brain template, and extracts the regions of interest with the help of the nibabel toolkit under the Python 3.9 and Anaconda 3 platforms. The AAL3 template is used for the automatic segmentation and regional labeling of brain images by dividing the brain into multiple standardized anatomical regions. The nibabel toolkit accurately identifies each anatomical region of the brain by aligning a single brain image with the AAL3 standardized space. The study selects the substantia nigra (AAL: 161, 162, 163, 164) as the ROI, and saves the extraction results as NIFFTI files. All segmented ROIs underwent visual inspection and quality verification by an experienced imaging specialist.

Radiomic features were extracted from the QSM ROIs using the Python-based pyradiomics library, in accordance with the Image Biomarker Standardisation Initiative (IBSI)45. Six classes of radiomic features were extracted: (1) first-order statistics; (2) gray level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM); (3) gray level run length matrix (GLRLM); (4) gray level size zone matrix (GLSZM); (5) neighboring gray tone difference matrix (NGTDM); and (6) gray level dependence matrix (GLDM). Detailed definitions of all radiomic features are available on the pyradiomics website. In total, 120 radiomic features were extracted from each ROI of each subject’s QSM map.

Statistical analysis and feature selection were performed using R software packages. In the training cohort, dimensionality reduction and feature selection were carried out via the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression method. The optimal λ value was determined by calculating the area under the curve across different λ values. Tenfold cross-validation (CV) was used to evaluate model performance at each λ, and the optimal regularization parameter was selected based on the trade-off between model performance and the number of retained features. Feature weights were derived from the corresponding coefficient lists, and features were ranked by importance for inclusion in subsequent machine learning modeling.

Model construction, training, evaluation

Data preprocessing involved several standardization procedures, including z-score normalization, quantile normalization, ComBat, and M-ComBat harmonization to reduce site-related and batch effects. Four classification algorithms were evaluated: Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, eXtreme Gradient Boosting, and Light Gradient Boosting Machine.

To robustly assess model generalizability and prevent overfitting during hyperparameter optimization, a five-fold cross-validation strategy was employed on the training cohort. Hyperparameter tuning was conducted using a grid search approach. The diagnostic performance of the final models was evaluated on the independent test cohort.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R software version 3.5.0 (R Development Core Team, Vienna, Austria). The chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables between groups. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was applied to assess data normality. Normally distributed continuous variables were analyzed using independent-sample t tests and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed data were evaluated with the Mann–Whitney U test and expressed as medians.

Group differences in MEGA-PRESS–derived metabolite levels were analyzed using a general linear model (GLM), with age, sex, and the proportion of gray matter within the spectroscopy voxel included as covariates. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Bonferroni adjustment. Spearman correlation and partial correlation analyses were employed to examine age-related variations in basal ganglia GABA levels and to explore associations between SMC GABA levels, substantia nigra radiomic features, non-motor symptom scores, and disease severity. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Diagnostic performance of the machine learning models was further assessed using ROC curve analysis. The Youden index was applied to determine the optimal cutoff point, AUC, sensitivity, specificity, balanced accuracy, and accuracy were calculated. DeLong’s test was used to compare AUCs among different models.
