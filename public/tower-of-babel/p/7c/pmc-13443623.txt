Delta feature and random forest–enhanced LSTM–attention forecasts with probabilistic postprocessing for rainfall forecasting
Authors: Seyed Mohammad Miri, Mohammad Reza Kavianpour, Mohamad Javad Alizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reliable rainfall forecasts are crucial for real-time flood control and managing water resources. Advances in deep learning, high-performance computing, and Internet of Things (IoT)-based synoptic measurements are making rainfall predictions increasingly accurate. The contributions of this work are threefold: (1) leveraging information from neighboring stations with variable time lags to model spatial propagation and regional rainfall dynamics; (2) introducing delta-based features that capture temporal gradients in meteorological variables; and (3) developing a hybrid Attention-Long Short-Term Memory (Attention-LSTM) framework with probabilistic postprocessing for extreme value forecasting. Data from the target station and its seven neighboring stations, along with six meteorological variables, were considered. To capture temporal dependencies, delta and time-lagged features were created, and the most informative inputs were then selected using Principal Component Analysis (PCA), correlation analysis, and Random Forest. The LSTM model was used as a baseline, and several enhanced models were evaluated, including Attention-LSTM, Progressive Graph Convolutional Network-LSTM (PGCN-LSTM), and PGCN-LSTM with attention. The models tended to underestimate extreme rainfall events, likely due to the imbalance in the number of extreme samples. These values were then refined using threshold-based postprocessing with a Weibull distribution. In terms of hyperparameter tuning of the models, regularization and dropout played a key role in preventing overfitting, ensuring robust training of the deep learning framework for short-term rainfall forecasting. The proposed hybrid framework provides satisfactory predictions for general and extreme rainfall (correlation ≈ 0.69), while high-intensity convective events remain partially underestimated.

Introduction

Rainfall is a fundamental driver of the hydrological cycle and a cornerstone of water resource management. Short-term rainfall forecasting (e.g., 0–6 h lead time) is especially critical for disaster risk reduction, flood early warning systems, and agricultural decision-making1,2. Yet, accurately predicting rainfall remains a formidable challenge due to its high variability and nonlinear behavior, which stem from intricate atmospheric dynamics and diverse topographic influences3,4. Rainfall forecasting models are broadly categorized into two main types: Numerical Weather Prediction (NWP) models and data-driven approaches5. Advancements in high-performance computing and artificial intelligence have significantly enhanced the capabilities of both approaches. NWP models simulate atmospheric processes by solving physical equations that govern fluid dynamics and thermodynamics, enabling the prediction of variables such as precipitation6. Improvements in spatial and temporal resolution have notably increased their accuracy, particularly for convective system forecasts7. Nevertheless, short-term forecasts using NWP models continue to face limitations, including spin-up errors, challenges in data assimilation, rapid error propagation and high computational demands. In recent years, AI-based models have emerged as powerful alternatives by learning complex nonlinear patterns directly from data. These models offer faster, more adaptive forecasts and do not rely on explicit physical assumptions, making them particularly attractive for short-term prediction tasks.

Both NWP and AI-based rainfall forecasting models rely on diverse data sources, notably satellite-based remote sensing and ground-based observations. Satellites such as the Geostationary Operational Environmental Satellite (GEOS) and radar networks provide real-time data suitable for short-term and nowcasting applications8,9. Meanwhile, products like Precipitation Estimation from Remotely Sensed Information using Artificial Neural Networks (PERSIANN) and Soil Moisture to Rain (SM2RAIN) are mainly used for merged rainfall estimation, combining multiple data sources to improve accuracy over broader spatial and temporal scales10,11. However, these systems often experience latency between observation and data delivery and limited spatial or temporal resolution, which can hinder their effectiveness in operational, real-time forecasting. Ground-based precipitation data, especially from synoptic stations and IoT enabled sensors, are essential for validating satellite estimates and training AI models. The integration of IoT technologies enables real-time data acquisition and feedback, supporting smart hydrological systems and digital twins. As AI-based approaches continue to evolve, their synergy with IoT-enhanced ground observations offers a promising pathway toward more accurate, scalable, and context-aware rainfall prediction systems.

AI-based rainfall forecasting has evolved from simple regression models to sophisticated deep learning architectures. Early ML models like Random Forests (RFs) and Support Vector Machines offered baseline improvements but lacked temporal awareness. The advent of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) enabled spatial feature extraction from satellite imagery, while Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks captured sequential dependencies in time series data12–14. Akbari Asanjan et al.1 demonstrated that combining LSTM with satellite-derived Cloud-Top Brightness Temperature (CTBT) from the PERSIANN system enhances short-term (0–6 h) precipitation forecasts. The proposed approach outperformed traditional methods, including Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), optical flow, and numerical models like RAPv1.0, particularly in detecting convective rainfall across U.S. regions. LSTM networks are effective at capturing temporal patterns but often fail to capture interactions between hydro-meteorological variables. Hybrid models that incorporate CNNs, GNNs, or attention mechanisms can address this, though they tend to increase computational demands15. Apart from these models, transformer-based architectures such as the spatiotemporal Pathformer have demonstrated strong performance in modeling complex spatiotemporal dependencies in hydrological processes16. Recently, attention mechanisms and graph-based models have advanced rainfall forecasting17,18. Architectures like Progressive Graph Convolutional Network LSTM (PGCN-LSTM) demonstrate superior performance by adaptively learning both spatial and temporal correlations19. Similar architectural innovations have been applied across domains. Zhang et al.20 combined Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN) and dynamic graph neural networks to predict significant wave height by capturing both long- and short-term spatiotemporal relationships. Liu et al.21 developed temporal attention layers using multi-head self-attention, transformer, and informer modules to improve modeling of complex spatiotemporal patterns in traffic data. In offshore drilling, Yuan et al.22 showed that attention-augmented BiLSTM models effectively capture key dependencies among drilling parameters, improving both the accuracy and stability of real-time predictions. These applications highlight the versatility of advanced spatiotemporal architectures in dynamic environmental and engineering systems.

Despite advances in deep learning for rainfall forecasting, predicting extreme precipitation remains challenging due to rainfall’s stochastic nature, rare-event imbalance, and complex atmospheric dynamics. Consequently, models may achieve high overall accuracy but miss critical outliers, limiting their usefulness in flood-prone regions23. To address this, probabilistic postprocessing, such as Weibull-based calibration, has emerged as an effective method to adjust model outputs and better capture observed precipitation extremes24–26. In addition to model architecture and postprocessing, feature engineering plays a critical role in enhancing forecasting performance. Using datasets from multiple stations and diverse variables can enhance model richness and generalizability. However, this complexity may also increase the risk of overfitting. In this regard, feature engineering techniques (e.g., correlation analysis, Principal Component Analysis (PCA)) can help identify the most informative inputs.

While most existing models primarily utilize raw observations and lagged values, this approach may overlook subtle temporal dynamics in rainfall patterns. In this study, we introduce a unified framework with three key methodological innovations. First, we incorporate delta-based meteorological features, defined as step-to-step gradients in temperature, humidity, and other variables, to explicitly capture rapid temporal shifts that precede rainfall events. To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically incorporate delta-based meteorological features for short-term rainfall prediction using ground-based observations. In addition, we leverage spatially distributed ground-based observations and incorporate variable time lags from neighboring stations. This allows the model to represent rainfall propagation across the region instead of assuming that all stations respond at the same moment. Third, the baseline LSTM model is explored with attention mechanisms and progressive graph representations. This hybrid setup is combined with a Weibull-based postprocessing step to address the typical underestimation of extreme rainfall in deep-learning outputs. Feature-engineering methods such as correlation analysis, PCA, and RF selection are also embedded to manage high-dimensional inputs and highlight the most informative predictors. Together, these elements form a practical framework that blends temporal gradients, spatial propagation, and advanced modeling to improve short-term forecasts of both general and extreme rainfall from ground-based station data. The remainder of this manuscript is organized as follows: Sect. 2 describes the study area and data; Sect. 3 presents the methodology, including LSTM models, feature engineering, advanced architectures, postprocessing approaches, and the proposed hybrid framework; Sect. 4 discusses the results; and Sect. 5 provides the conclusions.

Study area and data

Golestan Province, in northeastern Iran, experiences diverse rainfall types due to its varied topography and climatic influences. The region receives frontal rainfall during winter when moist air from the Caspian Sea interacts with the mountains, and convective rainfall in spring and summer, characterized by short-duration, intense storms that can trigger flash floods. Orographic rainfall is common in the mountainous areas, while occasional monsoonal influences bring additional moisture. Annual precipitation varies significantly across the province, from less than 200 mm in the northern arid regions to around 900 mm in the southern plains. The Aliabad synoptic station (focus of this study), supported by neighboring stations (Fig. 1), provides critical data for monitoring these rainfall events. Accurate short-term rainfall forecasts are therefore essential for early warnings, flood mitigation, and protecting communities, infrastructure, and agriculture from destructive floods such as those in 2001, 2017, and 2019.

Methodology

The methodology for short-term rainfall forecasting integrates both temporal and spatial modeling to capture complex patterns in meteorological data. It combines feature engineering, deep learning, attention mechanisms, graph-based techniques and postprocessing to improve predictive accuracy. To maintain clarity and readability, this section provides a concise overview of the key components, while full mathematical formulations and implementation details are referenced from established literature. The following subsections describe each component.

Feature engineering

Feature engineering plays a critical role in improving model performance by transforming raw meteorological data into informative predictors that enhance learning and generalization. In this study, data from eight synoptic stations and six meteorological variables were used. To capture short-term temporal dependencies, up to two or three lagged values of each variable were generated. Moreover, a new set of step-to-step gradient (delta) features was developed to describe the temporal evolution of meteorological conditions. These step-to-step gradients describe the short-term temporal evolution of meteorological conditions, capturing rapid transitions and fluctuations associated with convective rainfall. Physically, they reflect short-term changes in temperature, humidity, and other key variables that often precede rainfall events. Combining the original, lagged, and delta-derived features resulted in more than 200 potential input variables. However, many of these features may exhibit multicollinearity or redundant information, which can introduce noise and increase the risk of overfitting.

To reduce feature dimensionality and retain only the most relevant predictors, three complementary feature selection techniques were applied: correlation analysis, PCA, and RF feature importance. Correlation analysis is a simple and interpretable technique for detecting linear relationships among variables. It helps remove redundant features with high pairwise correlation (|r| > threshold), reducing dimensionality and multicollinearity. However, it only captures linear dependencies and may miss nonlinear relationships relevant to prediction.

PCA29 was applied to transform correlated variables into a smaller set of orthogonal principal components that retain most of the data variance. RF was used as a filter-based method to rank predictor importance in the high-dimensional feature space.

Probabilistic Postprocessing with Weibull Distribution

Statistical postprocessing enhances weather and extreme event forecasts by correcting biases and improving reliability34,35. Raw model outputs often exhibit systematic biases, especially for short-duration and extreme precipitation events. It often employs probabilistic techniques such as Quantile Mapping (QM), Bayesian Model Averaging (BMA), and the Weibull method, each offering distinct advantages in calibration and temporal consistency. Unlike QM, which corrects each quantile independently and may distort the sequential consistency of rainfall time series, the Weibull method applies a continuous parametric transformation. This preserves the temporal ordering and physical coherence of rainfall events while still correcting distributional biases36. This property is particularly important for hydrological applications where the timing and persistence of rainfall are as critical as its magnitude. Generally, the Weibull distribution is defined by shape and scale parameters that control the peak and spread of its Probability Density Function (PDF). The mathematical description of a Weibull PDF for a continuous random variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:x\ge\:0$$\end{document} is given by:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{f}\left(\mathrm{x}\right)=\frac{\mathrm{k}}{\mathrm{A}}{\left(\frac{\mathrm{x}}{\mathrm{A}}\right)}^{\mathrm{k}-1}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{x}\mathrm{p}\left[-{\left(\frac{\mathrm{x}}{\mathrm{A}}\right)}^{\mathrm{k}}\right]$$\end{document}

where \:k>0 and \:A>0 are the shape and scale parameters, respectively. The Weibull distribution parameters can be estimated using the Maximum Likelihood Estimation method37. The post-processing procedure consists of fitting Weibull distributions to both observed and forecasted training data to estimate their parameters. The forecast parameters are then adjusted using derived correction factors, and an inverse Weibull transformation is applied to generate the final test forecasts.

The Weibull distribution is widely used for modeling non-negative, right-skewed hydrometeorological variables such as precipitation intensity. From a theoretical perspective, the Weibull form also arises as one of the three limiting families in the Generalized Extreme Value (GEV) framework, which supports its relevance for modeling upper-tail behavior. This makes it particularly suitable for hourly rainfall, which is typically zero-inflated, highly skewed, and characterized by a single right tail. In addition, the Weibull distribution has only two parameters, leading to more stable parameter estimation than the full three-parameter GEV distribution. This is especially advantageous for hourly rainfall, where extreme events are relatively sparse and GEV shape-parameter estimation can become unstable.

The proposed framework and model evaluation metrics

In this study, ground-based meteorological data from eight synoptic stations were utilized, incorporating six primary variables along with lagged values and delta features. Although LSTMs inherently capture temporal dependencies through their memory cells, manually including lagged features provides explicit information about specific past time steps that are most relevant for prediction. This complements the LSTM’s internal memory, guiding the model toward important temporal patterns without replicating data or introducing inconsistencies. The dataset covered the period from 2019 to 2025 at an hourly temporal resolution, enabling the models to learn from diverse seasonal and climatic conditions. The base LSTM model was trained using approximately 240 input features to forecast short-term rainfall at lead time of \:t+1. The LSTM and other deep learning models use Mean Squared Error (MSE) as the loss function. To assess the contribution of delta features, two separate models were developed, one including delta features and one excluding them. The dataset was chronologically divided into training, validation, and testing subsets to maintain temporal consistency, and all input variables were normalized to ensure uniform feature scaling during model training.

To mitigate noise and reduce overfitting, feature engineering was performed using correlation analysis, PCA, and RF importance ranking. The most effective features, selected based on high correlation scores or dominant principal components, were retained for model training. In the next phase, a spatiotemporal attention mechanism was integrated to evaluate its ability to enhance forecast accuracy by capturing dynamic dependencies across time and space. Additionally, a Progressive Graph Convolutional Network combined with LSTM (PGCN-LSTM) was employed to model spatial relationships among stations.

Given the high imbalance in the rainfall dataset, where zero-rainfall instances vastly outnumber non-zero events, the data distribution was highly skewed. To address this, a threshold was defined so that the Weibull distribution was applied only to rainfall values exceeding a minimum meaningful amount. Specifically, we define non‑zero rainfall as ≥ 1 mm to avoid fitting the distribution to instrument noise and negligible drizzle. This threshold is consistent with gauge resolution and common hydrological practice, as smaller hourly amounts have limited physical or hydrological relevance. In addition, a preliminary sensitivity analysis was performed during the training period using thresholds of 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.25, and 1.5 mm. The 1 mm threshold provided stable parameter estimates and a good balance between sample size and distributional fit. Finally, to improve model accuracy in forecasting extreme rainfall values, probabilistic postprocessing using the Weibull technique was implemented for values above the defined threshold. Results and Discussion

LSTM Baseline model and contribution of delta features

Building on the methodology outlined in Sect. 3, this subsection first reports the baseline performance of the LSTM model. In the baseline configuration, the LSTM model was trained using six primary meteorological variables from 8 stations and their three lagged values, yielding about 192 input features. When delta features, representing short-term changes in these variables, were included, the total feature count increased to roughly 240, enabling the model to capture rapid atmospheric transitions preceding rainfall. To improve generalization and mitigate overfitting in high-dimensional, collinear input space, L2 regularization and dropout layers were incorporated into the network. These regularization techniques constrained weight magnitudes and prevented neuron co-adaptation, helping the model maintain balanced performance across training, validation, and test datasets. The results of the three configurations—without regularization/dropout (No_reg/dropout), without delta-baseline LSTM (Nodelta), and with delta features (Plusdelta)—are summarized in Fig. 4, illustrating model performance across the training, validation, and testing sets.

Because the high-dimensional input structure contains substantial collinearity and noise, the LSTM models showed a tendency toward overfitting, with noticeably lower generalization accuracy on the test data compared to the training phase. The No_reg/dropout model achieved the lowest training error but exhibited much higher validation and test errors, confirming poor generalization due to the lack of regularization. In contrast, the Nodelta and Plusdelta models demonstrated more stable performance across datasets, indicating that dropout and regularization effectively mitigated overfitting. Moreover, incorporating delta features (the Plusdelta model) improved validation and test accuracy, reflecting enhanced temporal feature learning. Probabilistic postprocessing

To enhance the prediction of rainfall events exceeding a specified threshold, a Weibull-based probabilistic postprocessing technique was applied to forecasts generated by the best-performing model, the feature-attention LSTM. This approach aims to correct systematic biases in the raw forecasts and improve the representation of extreme rainfall. Figure 9 illustrates the impact of postprocessing by comparing the PDFs and Cumulative Distribution Functions (CDFs) of raw forecasts, corrected forecasts, and observed values.

Figure 9 shows that the raw forecast (red) tends to overestimate moderate rainfall (around 1–2 mm) compared to observations (black). The bias-corrected forecast (blue) matches the observed distribution much better, particularly by reducing the overestimated peak (top panel). The raw forecast also builds up more quickly at low rainfall amounts, suggesting it overpredicts light rain, while bias correction improves the match with observed accumulation across most rainfall values (bottom panel). Regarding higher rainfall amounts, the corrected forecast outperforms the raw forecast in representing heavy rainfall events (e.g., 4–8 mm), showing better alignment with the observed distribution (both panels). However, for extreme rainfall events (> 10 mm), both forecasts continue to underrepresent the observed values. This underrepresentation likely stems from the rarity of such events in the historical record, which limits the model’s exposure to sufficient training examples. In hydrometeorological modeling, extreme events are often caused by nonlinear dynamics and localized convection, which are hard for coarse models or corrections based on typical events to capture accurately.

The PDF of the observations shows a clear right-skewed tail, which is typically associated with short-lived, high-intensity convective rainfall events. In contrast, the central portion of the distribution reflects more frequent, lower-intensity rainfall that is generally linked to stratiform precipitation. After applying the Weibull-based postprocessing, the corrected forecasts align much more closely with the observed distributions, particularly in the main body of the PDF and CDF. However, some underestimation remains in the upper tail of the distribution. This likely reflects the difficulty of capturing rare convective rainfall events, which occur infrequently and exhibit large natural variability. Overall, the results highlight that model performance is strongly dependent on rainfall regime, with more reliable predictions for stratiform rainfall and increased uncertainty for convective extremes.

Conclusions

Short-term rainfall forecasting (1-hour lead time) remains crucial for hydrometeorological risk management and early warning systems. This study introduced a deep learning–based hybrid postprocessing framework for rainfall prediction, integrating a new input feature, the temporal gradient (Δ), to capture short-term variability. Multiple meteorological variables, including wind speed, temperature, humidity, solar radiation, rainfall, and pressure from eight synoptic stations and the target station, were used to forecast rainfall at time t + 1. Given the high-dimensional input space (over 200 potential predictors derived from raw values, lags, and gradients), dimensionality reduction was applied using three feature engineering approaches: correlation analysis, PCA, and RF–based feature selection. Separate LSTM models were trained with each reduced feature set to identify the most accurate configuration. Additionally, attention mechanisms (feature, temporal, and combined) and a simple PGCN were examined to enhance temporal and spatial dependency learning. Finally, to improve predictions for moderate to heavy rainfall, a probabilistic postprocessing scheme based on the Weibull distribution was implemented.
