Exploring tribo-mechanical behavior of B₄C Reinforced LM25 aluminum matrix composites for structural applications
Authors: P. S. Raghavendra Rao, D. T. Arunkumar, Ashutosh Pattanaik, Mantra Prasad Satpathy, Pravat Ranjan Pati, Seyfu Tiruneh Debebe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study reports a comprehensive evaluation of the impact on the mechanical and tribological properties of boron carbide (B4C) particles reinforced with aluminum (LM25) metal matrix composites (MMCs). The synthesis of four different MMCs using the stir casting method and modifying 3, 6, and 9 wt% of 50 μm B4C particulates ensured the reliability of the results. Tensile strength and hardness increased with the rise in B₄C content. 9 wt% B₄C particulates to the LM25 matrix enhanced the hardness from 54.69 BHN to 68.54 BHN and increased the tensile strength from 198.48 MPa to 273.27 MPa. Various tribological (specific wear rate (SWR) and coefficient of friction (COF) characteristics were investigated. A novel hybrid metaheuristic sparrow search algorithm (SSA) was applied in the current study to provide the optimum process parameters and desired SWR and COF values of 2.102 mm3/Nm and 0.476, respectively. Scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy dispersive spectroscopy was used to assess the indigenously developed composites and understand the wear mechanism. The fabricated composites exhibited enhanced mechanical and tribological properties under specified experimental conditions, suggesting their suitability for further exploration in wear-resistant engineering applications.

Introduction

Over the last two decades, metal matrix composites (MMCs) have emerged as a significant topic of study in materials science and engineering. Many structural applications in the aircraft, automotive, and sports sectors may benefit from the use of lightweight composites, and researchers have taken several steps in this direction. This can be attributed to their exceptional characteristics, including robust wear resistance, specific coefficient of thermal expansion, and notable fatigue strength. These sectors prioritize the development of innovative metal composites owing to their low weight, high stiffness, and high thermal resistance1. Various types of fibers, nanotubes, whiskers, and particulates can be used as reinforcements in aluminum matrix composites (AMCs)2. When silicon nitride reinforcement is incorporated into these lightweight MMCs, their mechanical characteristics are enhanced, leading to lower operating costs, and they exhibit improved environmental compatibility. Owing to their remarkable castability properties, these materials are utilized to produce cylinder blocks, connecting rods, brake pads, drive shafts, and alloy wheels3. Moreover, these MMCs can be manufactured using various techniques with a reinforcement weight% of up to 40%. To manufacture MMCs, scientists have developed a wide range of techniques, including stir casting, squeeze casting, powder metallurgy, and plasma spraying. However, the cost of these MMCs is still higher than that of their competitors4. Thus, adopting simple fabrication methods and using cheaper reinforcement materials can reduce MMC costs without compromising quality. Stir casting is the easiest, most effective, and least expensive method to produce AMCs5. It has several benefits, including applicability to a wide range of materials, creating good bonding between the matrix and reinforcement particles, simplicity in design and operation, good wettability, minimal porosity, and cost-effectiveness over other methods. The quality of the fabricated samples can be maintained by controlling the stirring temperature, speed, and time6.

Numerous studies have been conducted to develop new MMCs. Silicon carbide (SiC), titanium carbide (TiC), and carbon nanotubes (CNT) particulates have primarily been used to reinforce aluminum alloys or iron metal matrices. Umanath et al.7 synthesized an AA2618 matrix reinforced with Si3N4, ZrB2, and AlN particles using the stir-casting technique. In this study, the tribological behavior of MMCs under various operating temperature settings was examined. Sharma et al.8 described AA6061 filled with SiC and Al2O3 particles and their wear properties. The wear surface of the hybrid composite was found to be rougher than that of the non-reinforced aluminum alloy based on the SEM analysis. The incorporation of nano-sized SiC particles into an aluminum alloy matrix to enhance the mechanical, physical, and interfacial properties of these MMCs has also been explored. SEM analysis showed a uniform distribution of nanoparticles in the matrix. Ramesh and Keshavamurthy9 brazed oxidized silicon nitride with aluminum using the squeeze-cast method. Two distinct reactive layers were observed at the interface, and the thicknesses of both layers were similar. Surya et al.10 cast Al6061-Si3N4 MMCs and predicted their tribological behavior using an ANN technique. The prediction results indicated that the predicted and experimental values were in good agreement. Wang et al.11 fabricated a multilayer Al/Si3N4 MMC by varying the layer thickness from 10 to 500 nm. These MMCs were prepared using magnetron sputtering. In this study, the connection between the layer thickness and mechanical characteristics, along with the deviations in deformation behavior, were investigated. Hybrid Al/Si3N4 composites were prepared using different volume fractions of nanosized Al/Si3N4 particulates. These composites were fabricated using a powder metallurgical process, followed by hot extrusion and microwave sintering. Kumar et al.12 demonstrated that the mechanical properties, such as the friction coefficient and roughness of the MMC can be achieved by depositing a thin layer of Si3N4 film.

Boron carbide (B4C) is a potential ceramic material because it is very hard, absorbs neutrons well, and does not react with other chemicals13. Aluminum matrix composites containing B4C have attracted considerable interest because they are stronger and more resistant to wear14. Research findings indicated that the incorporation of carbides, including boron carbide can enhance fatigue properties, strength, creep resistance, abrasion resistance, and rigidity. Many researchers have utilized stir casting to fabricate aluminum matrix composites because it is relatively inexpensive and effective15–17. However, it is not easy to distribute reinforcing particles, such as B4C, uniformly throughout the aluminum matrix16. Recent studies have concentrated on improving processing methods, such as stir casting, to obtain a uniform distribution of B4C particles throughout the aluminum matrix18. The results demonstrated that the worn surface of the Al6061–SiC–B4C composite had fewer grooves and less delamination than the Al6061 alloy. Moreover, the composite was resistant to wear because the particles were evenly distributed. For strong bonding and adequate dispersion in aluminum matrix composites, reinforcing particles (such as B4C or SiC) must wet the aluminum matrix well19,20. The research demonstrated that the wettability of the molten metal was enhanced by preheating the SiC and Gr particles21. In a similar manner, Vardhan et al.22 preheated alumina at 830 °C during the production of LM25 aluminum composites reinforced with B4C to promote moisture removal from the reinforcement phase. Inadequate wettability can cause particle aggregation, which results in poor mechanical characteristics23. This phenomenon occurs because a consistent distribution of reinforcements makes it more likely that particles will interact with the matrix and touch the interface24. Consequently, a strong interfacial connection between the reinforcement and matrix during production contributes to improved tribological characteristics. The incorporation of a wetting agent in aluminum matrix composites (AMCs) has been observed to improve the wettability of the reinforcements25. Numerous investigations have revealed that magnesium, typically added at 1–2 wt%, serves as a common wetting agent in AMCs. Ahmad et al.26 described the impact of Si3N4 addition on the microstructure of an LM 25 alloy and its mechanical performance. Microstructural investigation suggested a homogeneous distribution of Si3N4 particulates in the LM 25 alloy, and little clustering was observed when the reinforcement percentage was increased. Ajithkumar and Xavior27 investigated the effect of nanosized B4C reinforcements on composite performance. The research revealed that Al7075 matrix composites reinforced with nano-SiC, B4C, graphene, and MWCNTs had diverse machinability traits, with the type and wt% of reinforcement substantially affecting the cutting forces, surface roughness, and chip formation.

Grey relational analysis (GRA) serves as a method for multi-criteria decision-making and effectively addresses issues characterized by intricate interdependencies among various factors28,29. In the field of composite materials, GRA has been extensively utilized for the multi-objective optimization of machining parameters, material selection, and process parameter optimization30. GRA is an essential method for the tribological examination of metal matrix composites31. To reduce the wear rate and friction coefficient of composites such as Al 7075 reinforced with ZrB2, factors including the applied load, sliding velocity, and reinforcing content were optimized in this study using GRA. Similarly, Tarasasanka et al.32 employed composites incorporating Al 7075 reinforced with ZrB2 and Al6061–SiC–B4C. The findings from these investigations frequently underscore the proficiency of GRA in identifying ideal parameter combinations necessary for improved wear resistance. Furthermore, methods such as scanning electron microscopy are commonly employed to analyze worn surfaces and elucidate wear mechanisms. Although it has recently emerged, the sparrow search algorithm (SSA) has shown encouraging outcomes in multiple optimization challenges owing to its robust search efficiency and rapid convergence rate33. Although direct applications of SSA in optimizing tribological parameters are still emerging, its nature-inspired methodology renders it highly effective for addressing intricate, non-linear interactions between tribological parameters and performance metrics34. The integration of GRA and SSA presents a robust methodology for optimizing process parameters in materials science investigations. GRA facilitates the integration of various objectives into a unified performance index, whereas SSA effectively explores the parameter space to identify an optimal solution. This hybrid methodology utilizes the advantages of both techniques, allowing researchers to efficiently enhance intricate processes characterized by several conflicting objectives.

Despite extensive research on aluminum matrix composites, obstacles persist in comprehending the intricate interactions among the reinforcement content, processing factors, and tribological performance, particularly under dry sliding conditions. Although optimization methods such as Taguchi techniques, ANN, and GRA have been widely used for aluminum-based composites, their applicability is often limited to identifying the best discrete levels within fixed experimental matrices. Furthermore, the precise influence of the B4C particle size and shape on the tribological performance of aluminum matrix composites requires further investigation. This study presents a thorough examination of the dry sliding tribology of LM25 aluminum alloy reinforced with different weight percentages of 50 μm B4C ceramic particles using the stir-casting technique. The current study innovatively utilized GRA in conjunction with SSA, a novel optimization method, to identify the ideal process parameters for reducing the wear rate and friction coefficient. This allows this study to move beyond discrete “level” selection to a continuous, multi-objective optimization of tribological characteristics. This study facilitates the production of high-performance Al-B4C composites with improved wear resistance for rigorous tribological applications by determining the optimal combination of reinforcement content and operating conditions of the composites.

Experimental details

Materials

The selection of suitable matrix alloys is mainly dictated by the particular application demands of the composite material. Conventional light-metal alloys are frequently employed as matrix materials in the manufacture of lightweight composites. Owing to rapid solidification during powder processing, special alloys are suitable for use in powder metallurgy. This can facilitate the development of metastable phases with distinct properties that cannot be achieved under equilibrium conditions, thereby enhancing the overall attributes of the composite material. The incorporation of ceramic particles may affect the solidification process, resulting in localized discrepancies in the cooling rates. Such structures are devoid of the segregation issues that arise during conventional crystallization. This study employed an LM25 aluminum alloy alloy with favorable properties, such as a good weight-to-strength ratio, high toughness, and high thermal conductivity. It is primarily used in automotive piston manufacturing. This aluminum alloy (density: 2.68 g/cm3) was considered the base matrix. Reinforcement improves the strength, stiffness, and temperature resistance of MMCs while lowering their density. To accomplish these features, the selection relies on strengthening and manufacturing techniques, as well as the chemical compatibility of the matrix. Considering this, B4C ceramic particulates with a size of 50 μm were selected as the reinforcement material to increase the wear resistance of the piston. However, boron carbide has a low thermal shock resilience owing to its poor thermal conductivity. The selection of B4C is determined by its additional advantageous characteristics, such as excellent wear resistance, remarkable hardness, and durability. These B4C particles also have a lower density (2.52 g/cm3), excellent thermal conductivity, and chemical stability comparable to those of SiC and Al2O335. Composite fabrication

A conventional stir casting procedure was employed in this study to fabricate MMCs owing to its cost-effectiveness36. Figure 1a shows a schematic representation of the stir-casting apparatus. Figure 1b shows an image of the fabrication of MMCs in the stir-casting furnace. The LM25 aluminum alloy matrix material was melted in an electric furnace and then placed in a graphite crucible. The entire process was conducted in an argon gas atmosphere, which acted as an inert shielding medium to prevent oxidation of the molten metal and ensure the production of sound castings. The argon gas flow was regulated at a constant rate of 8 L/min, initiated approximately 10 min before melting, and was maintained throughout the stirring and casting processes to ensure a stable environment. Meanwhile, in the molten metal, preheated B4C reinforcement particles with different weight proportions were progressively added. The mixture was then stirred at a rotational speed of 350 rpm for 6 min. This ensures uniform mixing and distribution of reinforcing particles in the molten metal mixture. Subsequently, this mixture was poured at 760 °C into a preheated (300 °C) steel mold and allowed to solidify. These parameters were selected based on established stir casting optimization studies for LM25-based systems, indicating that excessive stirring speeds (> 600 rpm) lead to higher porosity levels, whereas insufficient stirring results in the non-uniform distribution and settling of the B4C reinforcements. The MMCs solidified from the die arrangement were cylindrical rods with a diameter of 20 mm and a length of 150 mm. To examine the influence of the reinforcements, a series of test specimens were meticulously developed to analyze the mechanical characteristics of the LM25-B4C MMC. These test specimens were prepared in accordance with the strict requirements outlined by ASTM standards using a computer numerical control (CNC) lathe machine. Figure 2 illustrates the four distinct types of machined samples. Table 3 presents the compositions of the synthesized composites. The 3, 6, and 9 wt% of B4C represent the optimal range for maintaining uniform dispersion and structural integrity in LM25-based stir-cast composites.

Sliding wear test

Specimens for the dry sliding wear tests were fabricated in accordance with ASTM G99 standards. The wear behavior of the MMCs was evaluated under dry conditions using a pin-on-disc testing apparatus (Model: TR-20LE-M108, DUCOM, India) (Fig. 3). The test samples were prepared in a cylindrical form (12 mm × 30 mm) using a machining operation. These samples were held stationary and positioned against a revolving EN31 steel disc with a Vickers hardness of VHN112. The disc was driven by a motor. The test specimen was used as a pin in the experimental setup. The dry sliding wear tests were conducted in strict adherence to ASTM G99 protocols. It was gripped by an arm with balancing weights, as shown in Fig. 3. The track diameter was set to 60 mm throughout the experiment. Before testing, each specimen and the disc were polished using emery papers with grit sizes of 1000, 1200, 1500, and 2000. A normal load was applied to the cantilever beam, which provided a uniform load on the test specimen for contact with the counterface of the disc. Environmental conditions were strictly controlled at an ambient temperature of 25 °C and approximately 50% relative humidity to ensure the stable formation of the mechanically mixed layer, which significantly influences both the specific wear rate and the coefficient of friction.

A linear variable differential transducer (LVDT) was attached to the cantilever beam for precise measurements. The wear occurring on the surface of the test pin resulted in slight dimensional variations, which were transferred to the LVDT to measure the sliding wear37. Several experiments were conducted by varying the sliding distance and normal loading at different sliding velocities. Before and after each wear test, a consistent cleaning technique was performed on the specimens to determine mass loss. The specimens were cleaned with acetone to remove particles from their surfaces. This was followed by 5 min of ultrasonic cleaning in acetone to remove all particles from the contact surface. The specimens were then thoroughly dried. Prior to weighing, the samples were allowed to settle at room temperature for approximately 10 min. Its weight was estimated using a precision electronic microbalance with an accuracy of ± 0.1 mg, and the specific wear rate (SWR) was determined using Eq. Microstructural and mechanical tests

SEM images of the fabricated LM25 aluminum alloy-boron carbide MMC were captured. A sliding wear test specimen was prepared by cutting a section from the fabricated specimens. These samples were first polished with a belt polisher and then with emery sheets ranging from 180 to 1200 grit size. Finally, the samples were polished with a 0.4-µm alumina suspension on Sylvet cloth to obtain a superior surface finish. Upon completion of the fine finishing process, the samples were etched using Keller’s reagent to enhance the visibility of their microstructures. The etchant composition was 95 mL distilled water, 2.5 mL HNO₃, 1.5 mL HCl, and 1.0 mL HF. Etching was performed at atmospheric temperature for approximately 10–15 s. During etching, the specimen was gently cleaned with cotton. The samples were thoroughly washed with distilled water, followed by ethanol to quench the reaction and prevent excessive etching. The specimens were then dried with warm air. The dispersion of B₄C particles inside the LM25 aluminum alloy matrix and the associated wear parameters were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL® JSM-6084LV) with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). At room temperature (25 °C), the hardness of the LM25-B₄C MMC was assessed using a Brinell hardness tester (Zwick® Recall) in accordance with the ASTM E10 guidelines. The test employed a 2.5-mm steel indenter ball exerting a force of 72.5 kgf for 15 s, and measurements were recorded at three different locations to ensure precision. The tensile strength of the composite was evaluated using an Instron® 3300 universal testing machine (UTM) equipped with a 20 kN load cell at a strain rate of 2 mm/min. Tensile test specimens were prepared according to the ASTM E8 standard.

Methodology

Taguchi design of experiments

The approach developed by Taguchi is typically utilized in experiments to minimize their total number and variance. This philosophy employs the orthogonal experimental array (OA) concept, incorporating signal-to-noise (S/N) ratios as the objective functions. This DOE methodology encompasses three distinctive categories of S/N ratios: lower-is-better (LB), nominal-is-best (NB), and higher-is-better (HB). The current work involves SWR and COF as performance measures. Exemplary output is achieved with a lower SWR and a higher COF. The use of LM25-B4C metal matrix composites in brake rotors makes this possible. A high friction coefficient is required for effective braking, and low wear ensures that the rotors last for a long time. Similarly, reduced wear is necessary for automobile connecting rods or pistons to preserve dimensional precision and prevent engine failure. Thus, the LB and HB criteria were selected for SWR and COF, respectively. Finally, the highest S/N ratio determines the optimal parametric setting combination. Grey relational analysis (GRA)

However, obtaining the GRG is a cumbersome process that involves various steps to achieve these values. The first stage in this process is the normalization of the performance measures, which range from zero to one. This step reduces the variance in the dataset. The second stage of this analysis was to determine the individual gray relational coefficients (GRCs). At this stage, the normalized data obtained from the initial phase were examined to ascertain the correlation between the desired outcomes and measured investigative results. Finally, the average GRCs is used to compute the overall GRG.

Sparrow search algorithm (SSA)

An innovative swarm intelligence (SI) optimization technique was implemented to solve various complex problems in the metal matrix composite fields. However, such metaheuristic algorithms may provide good results for specific issues but demonstrate inferior performance for different problems. Thus, the hybridization of other techniques is gaining popularity to control these limitations and solve complex global optimization problems38,39.

SSA represents a novel SI optimization method derived from the foraging and anti-predation behaviors observed in sparrows. This approach enhances optimization outcomes by integrating refined exploration and exploitation strategies within the search space. Results and discussion

Microstructural characterization (LM25-B4C MMCs)

A microscopic image of the B4C ceramic particles is presented in Fig. 5, which was used to identify their presence in the samples. As shown in Fig. 5, the average particle size of the B4C reinforcement was 50 μm (determined using the ImageJ program). This particle size was selected to ensure optimal wettability and uniform dispersion during the stir casting process. Typically, these micro-sized reinforcements are preferred over nano-sized alternatives to avoid the risks of severe agglomeration and excessive porosity during MMC fabrication. Moreover, these micro-sized particles are effective at bearing the external load and protecting the LM25 matrix from severe abrasive wear by forming a stable, hard-phase barrier against plastic deformation41. However, a certain amount of agglomeration of the B4C particles is observed during the stir-casting process, despite attempts to ensure uniform dispersion. Consequently, higher apparent particle sizes were concentrated in specific regions. Figure 5 also shows SEM micrographs of different LM25 alloy matrix composites, illustrating the impact of varying the weight% of B4C particles. Because the first fabricated specimen contained 0 wt% B4C particulates in the LM25 matrix, it was not considered for micrographic analysis. However, Fig. 6 shows the homogenous distribution of 3, 6, and 9 wt% B4C particles in the matrix for all the remaining three types of fabricated specimens. The charged white spots are identified as B4C particles, and the dark gray region around these spots is considered to be the LM25 alloy matrix. Although the micrographs demonstrate the homogenous distribution of the reinforcement particles, as the weight% of B4C increased, the microstructure revealed that a small amount of B4C reinforcement particulates were clustered in some regions. This non-homogeneous dispersion of particulates is due to the differences in the densities of the B4C and LM25 alloys. Moreover, impurities in the composite can cause this type of clustering to occur. Solidification shrinkage defects and voids in the agglomerated particles were also observed in some regions of the MMCs. However, this was not distinct at this temperature42. The integration of B4C reinforcements into the Al matrix was verified by energy-dispersive spectroscopic characterization of the manufactured LM25- B4C MMCs. The region marked in the green box in Fig. 6 was selected for EDS analysis and is presented in Fig. 7. EDS analysis was used to verify the presence of Al, B, C, and Mg in the composite material, indicating the effective integration of B4C particles into the LM25 alloy matrix. Additionally, the EDS results provide information regarding the elemental distribution within the MMC, which is essential for overall performance. Analysis of the microstructure, in conjunction with EDS, played a vital role in assessing the homogeneity of the composite material.

Mechanical performance of LM25-B4C MMCs

The mechanical performance of the LM25-B4C MMCs was determined using hardness and tensile tests. The repeatability and statistical reliability of the mechanical characterization were ensured by conducting four trials for each composite. The hardness was assessed at three distinct locations of each fabricated sample, demonstrating an increase corresponding to the increasing concentration of B4C particulates within the LM25 matrix, as shown in Fig. 8. The hardness values improved from 54.69 BHN to 68.54 BHN when the percentage of B4C reinforcement changed from 0% to 9%. This trend is corroborated by studies of similar LM25-based hybrid systems, in which the addition of B₄C led to a 26.3% improvement in hardness relative to that of the base alloy43.

Fig. 7EDS spectrum analysis of LM25 metal matrix composites featuring (a) 3 wt%, (b) 6 wt%, and (c) 9 wt% B4C ceramic particulates.

EDS spectrum analysis of LM25 metal matrix composites featuring (a) 3 wt%, (b) 6 wt%, and (c) 9 wt% B4C ceramic particulates.

A higher percentage of reinforcement results in an increased quantity of B4C ceramic particulates within the base-Al matrix44. Thus, these particles effectively resisted plastic deformation during indentation. The tensile test results of the LM25-B4C MMCs demonstrated that the strength increased from 198.48 MPa to 273.27 MPa when the percentage of B4C reinforcement increased from 0% to 9% (Fig. 9). The enhancement of the tensile strength of these MMCs can be attributed to several distinct factors. First, the B4C reinforcement particles were highly compatible with the LM25 aluminum alloy. Second, the hard filler particles were consistently distributed throughout the matrix, acting as barriers to dislocation movement during deformation during tensile testing45. Additionally, the increasing dislocation density can be attributed to the mismatch in the thermal expansion coefficients between the reinforcement particles and aluminum matrix. The variation in thermal expansion limits dislocation motion during solidification, enhancing the tensile behavior of the composite. Consequently, an increased volume percentage of B4C resulted in elevated thermal dislocation strengthening values.

Statistical analysis results

Effect of process parameters on SWR

The Taguchi philosophy defines the quality characteristics of the experimentation process using S/N ratios. Table 6 lists the S/N ratios associated with the SWR performance of the proposed model. The S/N ratios for the SWR must be minimized to obtain the best results; thus, the LB criterion was selected. In the Taguchi design, the impact of each variable can be analyzed at various levels by calculating the average of the associated signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio values. The delta values in these tables were calculated by determining the differences between the peak values. The highest delta value signifies the most important factor affecting the response. As presented in Table 6, the normal load (B) exhibited the highest delta value of 3.454, suggesting that it had the most substantial influence on the SWR. The influence of this factor was subsequently affected by the sliding velocity (A), sliding distance (C) and B4C content (D).

Figure 10 illustrates the effects of the process factors on SWR. The SWR decreased with an increase in sliding velocity. This was attributed to the formation of a tribo-interlayer. The aluminum in this composite has a distinctive characteristic of forming an external oxide layer on its outer peripheral surface. Thus, a high velocity increases the temperature at the contact interface, which oxidizes the material. This phenomenon causes the movement of materials from the sliding zone to mix with the oxide layer, and the mechanically mixed layer, often referred to as a tribo-interlayer, plays a major role in influencing wear behavior. As the velocity increases, this tribo-interlayer functions as a barrier between the contacting surfaces, resulting in an overall reduction in the SWR46.

As the normal load increased from 20 N to 35 N, the SWR increased notably, as shown in Fig. 10. The surge in the SWR persisted as the load increased from 35 N to 65 N. The escalation of the SWR is likely due to the elastic deformation of the workpiece material and an increase in the interfacial temperature at the sliding surface. The temperature reached a maximum at a load of 65 N, resulting in the plastic deformation of the wear surface. Consequently, the pin surface began to bond with the disc, resulting in further material removal from the sliding surface47.

Figure 10 illustrates the decrease in the specific wear rate (SWR) with increasing sliding distance. A substantial reduction in SWR was evident between 1500 and 2000 m, in contrast to the more gradual decline noted from 2000 to 2500 m. This behavior can be attributed to the presence of hard reinforcing particles that infiltrated the surface asperities of the material. At shorter sliding distances, the contact area between the protruding reinforcing particles in the composite and disc was constrained, resulting in elevated wear rates and greater friction at the initial contact. As the B4C content increased, the SWR increased to a specific value, followed by a decrease with further increase in the B4C content. Development of regression model

This experimental study employed the Taguchi method in conjunction with GRA to simplify the multi-objective optimization challenge into a single-objective framework. Currently, GRG is an absolute single response instead of the SWR and COF responses. Thus, regression analysis was performed to address the non-linear relationship between GRG and the input parameters. Furthermore, the analysis was performed at a 95% confidence level (α = 0.05). This relationship is a quadratic polynomial equation expressed in Eq. (14). The analysis yielded a coefficient of determination (R²) of 0.9572 and an adjusted R² of 0.8787. The R2 value indicates that this model is 95.72% capable of explaining the variance in the GRG results, and the adjusted R2 value elucidates the impact of the predictors incorporated into the model. This level of statistical modeling is frequently absent in empirical AMC studies.

Optimization using coupled GRA-SSA approach

The current study focuses on the optimized SWR and COF results. In the case of a single-objective optimization model with two different objective functions, the first objective is to find the optimal parameter values to obtain the maximum SWR, and the second is to obtain the minimum COF. However, the current problem is a multi-objective problem, and the two performance indicators are combined to form a single quality indicator, that is, the GRG. SSA is an innovative metaheuristic technique employed in this study to solve this multi-objective problem. It offers higher search accuracy and faster convergence compared to conventional metaheuristic approaches. The practical superiority of SSA stems from its producer-scrounger architecture and early warning mechanisms, which enable a more robust exploration of the non-linear search space54. By integrating the above-developed quadratic polynomial regression model into the SSA methodology, a MATLAB program was developed. This approach was tested with different population sizes and iterations to achieve the desired GRG value with the best parameter combination. Solving the current problem requires considerable effort because it involves a complex quadratic equation. The control factor values used to perform the optimization using the SSA methodology are presented in Table 11. In conclusion, this approach used a population size of 30 sparrows with 50 iterations to obtain the most effective input parametric combinations. The convergence of the solution showed no significant variations. The best outcomes were obtained at the 50th iteration, as shown in Fig. 12. The best overall GRG value was achieved as 0.99 at a velocity of 160 cm/s, load of 65 N, distance of 1000 m, and B4C content of 9 wt%.

Microstructural analysis of wear surface

The examination of the sliding wear surfaces of composites using SEM is essential for understanding their wear behavior qualitatively. Figure 13 shows the microstructural characterization of the wear surfaces of the composites. The SEM micrographs, along with the selected parameters obtained from the GRA integrated with the SSA, reveal the unique characteristics of the wear surfaces of the Al-B4C composite. The optimal response uncovered by the GRA-coupled SSA technique was the basis for the selection of the sample with 9 wt% B4C, which was tested at a sliding velocity of 160 cm/s, normal load of 65 N, and sliding distance of 1000 m. Figure 13 a, b, and c present SEM micrographs illustrating the diverse microstructural characteristics of the Al-B4C composite. These images show the dispersion of B4C particles in the Al matrix and their effect on the wear surface morphology. The identified microstructural characteristics, such as delamination layers, distinct grooves, micropits, debris, and cracks, reflect the wear mechanisms operating under these conditions. SEM images of the worn surfaces reveal grooves aligned with the sliding direction, indicative of the plastic deformation of the material.

These defects were present; however, the lower wear rate experienced with a higher B4C content indicates that the reinforcement successfully reduced wear, even though these defects were on the surface. As mentioned in previous studies, adding ceramic particles, such as B4C, to metal matrix composites has proven to be an effective way to produce wear-resistant composites55. Furthermore, the addition of B4C particles reduced the severity of surface damage and enhanced surface integrity, which is likely attributable to the enhanced hardness and wear resistance conferred by ceramic reinforcement18,56. However, deep grooves indicate a loss of material, whereas the presence of debris and cracks indicates both brittle fracture and surface fatigue. The relationship between a higher B4C content and a decrease in the wear rate suggests that the reinforcement effectively obstructs plastic deformation and material removal, even in the vicinity of delamination and micropits. Similarly, an increased velocity results in an increase in the interface temperature, leading to material oxidation and the creation of a tribo-interlayer57. Conclusions

The stir casting method was used to fabricate an LM25 aluminum-alloy matrix reinforced with B4C particles. The results of this investigation revealed that B4C particles can be used as a potential reinforcement material in this composite. This study yielded the following conclusions:

(i)The fabricated composites demonstrated enhanced hardness and tensile strength with increasing weight percentages of the reinforcement particles in the LM25 aluminum matrix. The composite with 9 wt% B4C particles in the LM25 aluminum alloy attained a maximum hardness of 68.54 BHN and a tensile strength of 273.27 MPa.(ii)The effects of the parameters on the sliding wear performance were analyzed using Taguchi’s L16 orthogonal array. Tribological analysis showed that the SWR increased with increasing normal load owing to the heightened contact stress and abrasive action of the B4C particulates. The COF decreased at the maximum load of 65 N.; however, this trend reversed with increasing sliding distance and velocity. Among the factors influencing the SWR, the normal load had the most significant impact, whereas velocity and distance served as secondary contributors. Similarly, the sliding distance had the greatest effect on the COF results, followed by the velocity and load.(iii)A complex quadratic polynomial regression model was established to couple the input parameters to the GRA results by combining the performance measures of SWR and COF into a single objective. A hybrid optimization technique was used, combining GRA with the metaheuristic SSA, to obtain optimal solutions by the 50th iteration. The optimal tribological performance was achieved at a sliding velocity of 160 cm/s, applied load of 65 N, sliding distance of 1000 m, and B4C reinforcement of 9 wt%.(iv)The SEM results of the LM25-B4C MMCs revealed a homogeneous distribution of B4C particles in the LM25 aluminum matrix. However, for 9 wt% B4C particles in the matrix, a small amount of clustering was observed. The EDS results confirmed the presence of Al, boron, carbon, and Mg in this composite. SEM analysis of the wear surface also revealed various features, such as delamination layers, grooves, and cracks. A uniform distribution of B4C particles was observed in the matrices.
