Diagnostic challenges in identifying myocardial infarction due to coronary vasospasm in pregnancy complicated by partial HELLP syndrome: a case report

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is rare in young females and typically occurs in middle-aged or older patients with underlying coronary artery disease. On the other hand, AMI in younger populations is more likely attributed to coronary vasospasm (CVS) or spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD) rather than atherosclerotic lesions. Coronary vasospasm is rare in western countries but common in Asia.1 While its prognosis is generally better than that of atherosclerotic lesions, CVS, especially when affecting multivessel lesions, can lead to fatal complications such as AMI and sudden cardiac death.2 Although the pathophysiology of CVS is not fully understood, it is primarily attributed to endothelial dysfunction and heightened vascular smooth muscle reactivity. Established risk factors include smoking, alcohol use, and sympathomimetic agents, all of which can provoke vasospasm by exacerbating endothelial injury and smooth muscle hyperreactivity.1 In addition, physiologic and hormonal changes during pregnancy,3 as well as inflammation and altered vasoreactivity in autoimmune conditions,4 have also been implicated CVS. The above changes during pregnancy may also weaken the coronary arterial wall, leading to SCAD.5

This case describes a female with a history of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), and recent pregnancy complicated by partial haemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet (HELLP) syndrome and AMI who was later diagnosed with CVS. We highlight the diagnostic challenges and clinical management of CVS-induced AMI in a pregnant patient with autoimmune disease and fetomaternal crisis secondary to partial HELLP syndrome.

Case presentation

A 39-year-old woman at 37 weeks and 1 day of gestation presented with 5–6 h of non-exertional chest discomfort without diaphoresis or radiating pain. She had never experienced similar symptoms prior to pregnancy. Her medical history included SLE and APS, contributing to two prior miscarriages. Otherwise, she has no known risk factors for coronary artery disease and did not smoke or consume alcohol during her pregnancy. On presentation, her blood pressure was 153/89 mmHg and laboratory findings revealed thrombocytopenia (85 000/μL: the normal range, 150 000–400 000/μL) and mildly elevated aspartate aminotransferase (55 IU/L: the normal range, 13–30 IU/L) and alanine transaminase (38 IU/L: the normal range, 7–23 IU/L) without haemolysis, consistent with partial HELLP syndrome. Systemic lupus erythematosus disease activity index was five, indicating mild activity. Electrocardiography (ECG) showed sinus rhythm without ST-segment changes (Figure 1A), but cardiac troponin I (TnI) was elevated (200 pg/mL: the normal range, <15.6 pg/mL). However, the on-call physicians considered that this was attributed to oxygen supply–demand mismatch (i.e. type 2 AMI) due to partial HELLP syndrome. Mechanisms of AMI such as CVS and SCAD were overlooked in the setting of the patient’s obstetric emergency. Due to non-reassuring foetal heart tracings and the development of partial HELLP syndrome, she successfully underwent an emergency caesarean section and her chest discomfort resolved following delivery.

Electrocardiogram on initial presentation and readmission. The initial electrocardiogram shows no significant ischaemic changes (A). The electrocardiogram on readmission demonstrates ST-segment depression in inferolateral leads (B).

Nine days postpartum, the patient presented with recurrent chest pain, similar to that of her previous hospitalization. Physical examination now revealed a holosystolic murmur at the apex and an audible fourth heart sound. Electrocardiography showed ST-segment depression in the inferior, anterior, and lateral leads (Figure 1B), and TnI had increased to 5452 pg/mL. Echocardiography demonstrated normal left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 60%, no regional wall motion abnormalities, and mild mitral regurgitation caused by mitral valve prolapse. Given her age, absence of traditional cardiovascular risk factors, and diagnosis of APS, the consulting cardiologists considered a differential diagnosis that included thromboembolism, SCAD, and CVS. Prior to the caesarean section, lower limb venous ultrasonography ruled out deep vein thrombosis. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed no intracardiac thrombus or overt evidence of a right-to-left shun, though a microbubble contrast study was not performed. Continuous electrocardiographic monitoring over approximately 7 days—encompassing both the initial caesarean admission and the subsequent hospitalization for AMI—showed no episodes of atrial fibrillation. Collectively, these findings made thromboembolism an unlikely aetiology. Urgent coronary angiography was performed on the day of admission but no dissection, stenosis, or occlusion was observed (Figure 2A and B; Supplementary material online, Videos S1 and S2), which is suggestive of myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA). An acetylcholine provocation test with 50 μg induced diffuse 90%–99% stenosis of the left anterior descending artery and left circumflex artery, accompanied by chest pain and ST-segment elevation in precordial leads (Figure 3; Supplementary material online, Videos S3–S6). The induced stenoses were reversed with intracoronary nicorandil and nitroglycerin. The patient was discharged on diltiazem 100 mg twice daily and enalapril 2.5 mg once daily for long-term vasodilatory effects and prevention of cardiac remodelling. Safety for breastfeeding was confirmed with her obstetrician, and both the patient and her newborn have done well since discharge.

Acetylcholine suppresses the atrioventricular conduction, leading to complete atrioventricular block or asystole. In clinical practice in our country, a temporary pacemaker is routinely placed in the right ventricle to prevent patient discomfort or syncope.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of CVS in an Asian woman with multiple autoimmune comorbidities and a recent pregnancy complicated by partial HELLP syndrome. This case underscores the importance of Hickam’s dictum, which emphasizes considering multiple coexisting conditions rather than a single unifying diagnosis, especially in complex presentations where fetomaternal outcomes are at stake. When physicians encounter complex patients with chest pain and elevated cardiac troponin such as in this case, both cardiac and non-cardiac causes should be explored. Among pregnancy-related AMI, SCAD is the most frequently reported.6 Further, other mechanisms such as coronary thrombosis, embolism, and CVS are also critical and should be considered in differential diagnosis. These mechanisms are included under the working diagnosis of MINOCA. In addition to MINOCA, ischaemia caused by oxygen supply–demand mismatch (i.e. type 2 AMI) should also be considered. Severe hypertension due to pre-eclampsia can lead to this clinical setting, which should be distinguished from MINOCA in terms of pathophysiology and prognosis.7,8 Non-coronary causes in the setting of autoimmune diseases are also relevant. In SLE, chest pain may result from pericarditis, myocarditis, or pulmonary hypertension, all of which can present with positive cardiac troponin. Additionally, HELLP syndrome may lead to hepatic infarction or haematoma, both of which may occasionally elevate troponin through increased demand (i.e. type 2 AMI) or direct inflammation. Given the rarity of CVS and its poorly understood pathophysiology, accurate diagnosis requires a detailed medical history and serial ECGs and TnIs for continued surveillance. However, interpreting TnI is challenging during pregnancy. TnI has been shown to be elevated pre-eclampsia related syndromes, likely due to hypertension-induced cardiac strain and endothelial dysfunction.9 This overlap between obstetric and cardiac sources of TnI elevation can lead to missed AMI diagnoses, particularly when urgent foetal distress necessitates immediate obstetric intervention. In our patient, an emergency caesarean section relieved her symptoms without haemodynamic compromise but led to a delay in cardiac assessment. This case reinforces the importance of early multidisciplinary collaboration, as timely cardiology involvement may have better elucidated our patient’s cardiac pathology leading to more appropriate management.

The interplay between autoimmune disease, pregnancy, and ethnicity with CVS is another key takeaway in this missed peripartum AMI. Systemic lupus erythematosus is associated with chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and microvascular abnormalities, all of which can predispose patients to SCAD and CVS. Pregnancy further exacerbates this risk through hormonal and haemodynamic changes that strain the coronary vasculature.3,10 Finally, the patient’s partial HELLP syndrome raises further concern for vascular dysfunction. Although studies are limited, HELLP-associated AMI have been reported with both SCAD11 and CVS,12 highlighting the need for greater awareness of non-obstructive AMI in peripartum patients. Despite the shared pathophysiology between SCAD and CVS, our patient’s ethnicity may influence clinical suspicion for CVS as it is relatively more prevalent in the Asian population. Nevertheless, epidemiological risk factors alone are insufficient and definitive diagnosis requires coronary angiography. In cases where angiography does not reveal obstructive or dissecting lesions, acetylcholine provocation testing is essential to assess for vasospasm. Following diagnosis, prognosis is generally favourable with timely initiation of calcium channel blockers and nitrates. However, patients with CVS-induced AMI, multivessel involvement, and risk factors such as smoking and medication non-adherence may have a higher risk of recurrent angina, AMI, and even sudden cardiac death despite treatment.2,13 Thus, regular follow-up and patient education are critical components of management and decision for recurrent pregnancy may require more in-depth risk stratification. This case highlights the diagnostic complexities of AMI during pregnancy and the postpartum period, particularly in women with autoimmune diseases and HELLP syndrome. It emphasizes the need to consider non-obstructive causes, such as CVS, in young patients without traditional atherosclerotic risk factors.

Finally, it is important to assess the safety of future pregnancies. Although data specific to CVS is limited, general principles for pregnancy after AMI suggest caution, particularly in cases SCAD14 or if severe left ventricular dysfunction (LVEF < 30%) is present.15 While the patient is not in such a concerning condition, potential future risks—including the teratogenicity of medications such as diltiazem and enalapril—must be carefully balanced against the risks of discontinuing these therapies. Though she is not currently planning for pregnancy, if she wishes to conceive in the future, individualized care and shared decision-making will be essential.

In conclusion, a multidisciplinary approach, along with the timely use of diagnostic tools like coronary angiography and acetylcholine provocation testing, is essential for accurate diagnosis and management. Future studies are warranted to better understand the interplay between pregnancy, autoimmune disease, and CVS, which could guide improved clinical strategies and outcomes for similar populations.
