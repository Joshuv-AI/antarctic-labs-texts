A multifunctional 2D goldene monolayer with strain-sensitive optoelectronic and thermoelectric properties
Authors: Kamal Kumar, Abhishek Dhasmana, Abhishek K Mishra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGoldene, a two-dimensional (2D) monolayer (ML) of gold (Au) atoms, has recently gained attention due to its distinctive structural and electronic features. Driven by the possibility of modifying material properties through strain engineering, this work explores the structural, electrical, optical, and thermoelectric properties of goldene ML using density functional theory (DFT). Both the pristine ML and its response under applied biaxial mechanical strain (compressive and tensile) are systematically analyzed. Our results show that goldene remains metallic under all applied biaxial strains, with no band gap opening, while strain induces notable modifications in its electronic density- of -states and transport properties. Goldene exhibits a strong low-energy reflectivity peak, reaching 0.55 under − 10% compressive strain and 0.37 under + 10% tensile strain, highlighting the tunability of its optical response via strain engineering. The static refractive index increases from 0.74 (unstrained) to 0.77 under − 10% compressive strain, while tensile strain reduces it to 0.71, indicating that strain can effectively tune its optical confinement and dielectric response. Power factor (PF) is strongly enhanced under compressive strain, reaching a maximum of 9.10 × 1011 W/mK2s at -2% strain and 300 K, nearly an order of magnitude higher than the pristine value of 1.3 × 1011 W/mK2s, highlighting the effectiveness of strain engineering for thermoelectric optimization. *corresponding author: akmishra@ddn.upes.ac.in (email).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47366-0.Subject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

Fabrication and design of novel materials is the cornerstone of progress in materials science and technology1. Synthesis of new materials provides a deep and broad understanding of the fundamentals of device fabrication and engineering2. Moreover, if this material is a nanomaterial (having at least one dimension in the nanometer range)3, it provides the ability to tune its physical and chemical properties, along with a wide array of applications4. Based on dimension, these materials can be classified into zero-dimensional/0D (quantum dots, nanoclusters, etc.)5, one-dimensional/1D (nanowires, nanotubes, nanorods, etc.)6, and two-dimensional/2D (nanosheets, thin films, etc.) materials7. Due to excellent mechanical strength, high thermal and electrical conductivity, and large surface area, 2D materials have opened new possibilities in the energy sector and optoelectronics8,9. Graphene holds a unique position in this because its synthesis has accelerated the procedure of designing and fabricating new 2D materials10. It is a 2D planar structure of sp2-hybridized and hexagonally arranged carbon atoms, resulting high surface-to-volume ratio along both planar sides11. The great success of experimental synthesis of graphene has encouraged the investigation of a plethora of novel 2D materials, including 2D transition metal-oxides (TMO)12, transition metal carbides and nitrides (MXenes)13, Xenes14, and hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN)15.

In the past few years, there has been a notable increase in research towards 2D mono-elemental Xenes16. This growing research is particularly due to exceptional progress in borophene17 and phosphorene18, which possess outstanding performance in various nanotechnological applications19. Materials of this category have strong potential to deal with global issues of environmental science, electronics, healthcare, and energy20. Particularly in the domain of biomedicine, Xenes are found to be a suitable candidate for diagnosis due to their unique electronic and optical characteristics21. Silicene22, stanene23, plumbene24, gallenene25, arsenene26, antimonene27, are some widely explored Xenes for these mentioned applications28. Engineering of 2D materials is key to modifying their properties for advanced and next-generation technologies in various domains such as catalysis, sensors, optoelectronics, and energy storage29. Doping30, strain31, chemical functionalization32, defect creation33, and heterostructure formation through stacking34 are some commonly used engineering techniques that are used to tailor the properties of materials for certain applications35. In recent years, strain engineering has emerged as an effective approach for tailoring the electronic and optical properties of 2D materials without altering their chemical composition36. The application of mechanical strain alters the interatomic distances and orbital hybridization, which can lead to significant changes in the electronic band structure (EBS) and carrier dynamics37. Consequently, strain can induce band-gap modulation, band dispersion variation, and changes in optical transition probabilities, thereby strongly influencing the optical absorption and electronic transport characteristics of 2D systems38. Both compressive and tensile strains have been widely reported to efficiently tune the band gap and optoelectronic response of various 2D materials36–38.

In 2015, Yang et al. predicted a highly robust and stable atomically thin monolayer (ML) of Au atoms through first-principles calculations39. In this ML, the Au atoms were arranged in a hexagonal close-packed crystal lattice that can survive up to 1400 K39. Their theoretical results prove that the Au ML is a diamagnetic metal, similar to bulk Au39. Different research groups have tried to experimentally synthesize a ML of Au atoms through different approaches and techniques40–43. In 2019, Ye et al. synthesized two atomic-layer-thick (0.47 nm) freestanding gold nanosheets at 20 °C through a one-step aqueous approach. In their synthesis process, methyl orange was used as a confining agent40. They found that synthesized Au nanosheets show excellent catalytic performance and unique peroxidase-mimicking activity40. Subsequently, Zang et al. synthesized Au nanosheets of thickness 2–8.5 nm through ionic layer epitaxy at the interface of air-water41. Their study also proved that Au nanosheets exhibit high PDT efficiency and very low cytotoxicity due to their uniform 2D surface morphology41. Also, these nanosheets are capable of killing cancer cells after 5 min of infrared (IR) irradiation with less heat generation41. This performance reported by Zang et al. is equal to 10 times the mass loading of nanoparticles of Au atoms41. Finally, Sharma et al. reported the technique of synthesizing single-atom-thick gold crystals and named them goldene42. They also confirmed honeycomb and herringbone lattices of goldene using high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) imaging42. They confirmed the chemical activity of goldene through Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, selective area electron diffraction, and High-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (HR-XPS)42. Recently, Kashiwaya et al. reported the successful experimental synthesis of a free-standing ML of Au, i.e., goldene (of 5 nm lateral thickness)43. This ML was achieved by the removal of Ti3C2 from Ti3AuC2 through wet chemical etching43. They confirmed the free-standing nature and stability of goldene MLss through scanning electron microscope and molecular dynamics simulations, respectively43. Our recent DFT study revealed that B and N-doped goldene MLss can effectively activate CO2 with the adsorption energies of −138.9 and − 163.7 kJ/mol. The study confirmed the potential application of goldene in the electrochemical conversion of CO2 into value-added products44. Thus, goldene is one of the newly synthesized members in Xenes’ family. Despite being a novel 2D ML, its electrical, optical, and thermoelectric properties have not been explored yet.

Computational approaches such as Density Functional Theory (DFT) and molecular mechanics (MM) are widely used to investigate material properties45–47. Compared with experiments, they are cost-effective, faster, and provide atomic-scale insights48–51. These methods allow systematic tuning of structure, defects, and strain, enabling accurate prediction of electronic, optical, and mechanical properties before experimental realization45–51. Among these methods, DFT is particularly effective for studying 2D materials52. It provides an accurate description of electronic structure, enabling reliable prediction of optoelectronic, thermoelectric, and mechanical properties52–55. DFT can also capture the effects of defects, strain, and surface modifications, making it essential for designing advanced 2D materials52–55. Here, we report the structural, electrical, optical, thermoelectric, and mechanical properties of goldene under uniform bi-axial mechanical strain through DFT.

Computational Methodology and Calculation details

All the calculations were performed using DFT within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)56. Specifically, the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE)57 exchange-correlation functional was used to balance computational efficiency and accuracy in electrical and structural properties. The interactions between ion cores and valence electrons were modeled with the projector augmented-wave (PAW) method58, as implemented in the Quantum Espresso (QE) software package59. To ensure the accuracy of the calculations, a k-point convergence test was performed by varying the Monkhorst–Pack mesh. As shown in Figure S1 of the supplementary information (SI), the total energy becomes well converged beyond the 9 × 9 × 1 grid; therefore, this mesh was adopted for the SCF calculations. For the thermoelectric transport calculations, non-self-consistent field (NSCF) calculations were performed using a denser 18 × 18 × 1 k-point mesh to achieve improved sampling of the electronic states near the Fermi level for reliable evaluation of the Seebeck coefficient and power factor (PF). During SCF calculations, electronic occupations were handled with the smearing method. For NSCF calculations, the tetrahedron method was used to improve the accuracy of the density of states and optical property evaluations. A plane-wave kinetic energy cutoff of 570 eV was applied throughout. EBS calculations involved sampling 100 k-points evenly along high-symmetry paths in the Brillouin zone using the crystal coordinate system. Geometry optimizations continued until residual atomic forces dropped below 0.01 eV/Å, and total energy converged within 10− 6 eV per atom. To avoid unnecessary interactions from periodic boundary conditions, a vacuum spacing of 12 Å was included in the out-of-plane (z) direction. Van der Waals (vdW) interactions were incorporated using the empirical dispersion correction scheme DFT-D3, developed by Grimme60, to accurately capture weak interatomic forces. For mechanical and thermodynamic property evaluation, the thermo_pw code61, an extension of QE, was used. The thermoelectric properties were calculated using semi-classical Boltzmann transport theory with the Bolz Trap code62. Optical properties were derived from the epsilon.x utility in QE, relying on the electronic structure obtained from SCF calculations. Norm-conserving pseudopotentials were employed to accurately evaluate the frequency-dependent dielectric function within the independent-particle approximation.

Results and discussion

Structural and electrical properties

Figure 1(a) and (b) illustrate the top and side views of the goldene ML, respectively. The 2D goldene ML is a hexagonally close-packed (HCP) nanosheet composed of Au atoms, crystallizing in the P6/mmm space group (No. 191)63. In goldene, the unit cell is constituted by only one Au atom as shown in a blue rhombus in Figure 1(a). In contrast to the buckled arrangements observed in silicene and plumbene, the goldene ML preserves a perfectly flat configuration, analogous to graphene. With a unit cell parameter of 2.75 Å, goldene occupies an intermediate position between silicene (3.86 Å)64 and graphene (2.46 Å)65. Our calculated cell parameter, Au–Au bond length, and bond angle for pristine goldene are 2.74 Å, 2.73 Å, and 60.01°, respectively, which agree well with previously reported experimental39 and computational results43. Figures 2 and 3 present the projected density of states (PDOS) and EBS of goldene under biaxial strain conditions ranging from +10% (tensile) to −5% (compressive). In the case of tensile strain (+2%, +5%, +10%), the overall band dispersion broadens slightly (Figure 2), and the conduction and valence bands shift relative to the Fermi level. Similarly, under compressive strain (−2%, −5%, −10%), the bands become slightly narrower, with changes in band curvature at high-symmetry points such as K, M, and Γ. These variations reflect strain-induced modifications in electronic interactions and orbital hybridization. In all cases, a finite density of states is observed at the Fermi level, confirming the robust metallic character of goldene irrespective of the applied strain. The states near the Fermi level are primarily derived from Au–d orbitals, with minor contributions from Au-s and Au-p orbitals (Figure 3). Under both tensile and compressive strain, slight shifts in the intensity and peak positions of the d states are evident, reflecting orbital rehybridization and redistribution of electronic states. Nevertheless, no band gap emerges, ensuring the preservation of the metallic nature of the system. This invariance of electronic character under strain underscores the intrinsic stability of goldene and highlights its potential in applications demanding strain-resilient electronic transport. In all EBSs plots (Figure 2), one energy band consistently crosses the Fermi level. This band originates from the weakly bound 6s¹ electron of Au, which can readily delocalize and move freely in the conduction band at room temperature. Moreover, the goldene ML remains flat in the presence of both compressive and tensile strain. Additionally, to assess the influence of spin-orbit coupling (SOC)66,67, SOC-included EBS calculations are performed for pristine goldene ML (Figure S2). We found that EBS shows minor splitting near the band edges without significant changes in band gap or dispersion. Thus, SOC does not qualitatively modify the strain-dependent optical and thermoelectric behavior. The metallic nature of goldene was further verified by recalculating the PDOS using an ultrasoft pseudopotential68. The PDOS profile remained unchanged, confirming the metallic character of goldene (Figure S3). It should be noted that while hybrid functional or GW methods may provide improved quantitative band-gap values69–73, the PBE functional reliably captures the strain-induced trends in the electronic structure, which are the primary focus of the present study. Although the dynamical stability of pristine goldene has been reported in previous studies39, we performed Gamma-point phonon calculations as an additional verification. The calculations show no imaginary frequencies, confirming the dynamical stability of the unstrained structure. While previous studies reported the dynamical stability of goldene under biaxial strains up to ±6%74, we performed phonon calculations at ±10% strain values to further verify stability. No imaginary frequencies were observed across the Brillouin zone for any strained configuration (Table S1 of the supplementary information). Optical properties

The optical properties of the pristine and strained goldene MLs were examined by calculating both the real εr(ω) and imaginary εi(ω) components of the dielectric function ε(ω). From these calculations, several important optical constants were derived, including the refractive index n(ω), extinction coefficient k(ω), reflectivity R(ω), absorption coefficient α(ω), electron energy loss spectrum (EELS), and joint density of states (JDOS).

On the other hand, the real part εr(ω) is determined by the Kramers-Kronig relation given by Eq. (3).

Where, is the real part of ε(ω) at a certain frequency and P is the principal value of the integral. ω is a dummy variable which represents the angular frequency of a photon. This component of ε(ω) describes how a material responds to electromagnetic (EM) waves in a dispersive manner, reflecting its capacity to store energy through polarization. It influences key optical properties such as the refractive index, phase velocity, and dispersion. When εr(ω) takes on negative values (as seen in metals), the material exhibits plasma-like behavior and can support surface plasmon resonances. Figure 4 presents the dependence of εr(ω) of goldene on photon energy under biaxial strains ranging from − 10% to + 10%. Across all applied strains, εr(ω) remains strongly negative in the low-energy region (below ~ 4 eV), reflecting pronounced metallic screening and plasma-like behavior. The photon energy at which εr(ω) changes sign defines the bulk plasmon resonance energy EP, marking the transition from a predominantly reflective to a more transmissive optical response. For goldene, EP is found to lie between ~ 3.8 and 4.2 eV, exhibiting only minor strain-dependent shifts. These small variations arise from strain-induced changes in free-electron density and band dispersion. At higher photon energies (> 5 eV), εr(ω) approaches zero, indicating a weakening of the dispersive response and a reduced capacity for EM energy storage. The overall similarity in the spectral profiles for both tensile and compressive strains demonstrates that the dispersive optical characteristics of goldene are largely preserved under lattice deformations within the studied strain range.

The electron energy loss function (EELF), L(ω), describes the energy lost by high-speed electrons as they pass through a material and is derived from the complex dielectric function as shown in Eq. (4).

In L(ω), prominent peaks signify plasmon resonances, which are collective oscillations of conduction electrons. The energy associated with the most intense peak defines the bulk plasmon energy, which is directly linked to the free electron density and serves as a key parameter for characterizing the plasmonic properties of the material. For metals and plasmon-active 2D systems such as goldene, the presence of strong L(ω) peaks within the visible to ultraviolet (UV) spectral range highlights their potential for plasmon-enhanced optoelectronic and sensing applications. Figure 4 shows the L(ω) of goldene under biaxial strains from − 10% to + 10%. A dominant peak is observed in the range of 6.5–8.5 eV, which corresponds to the bulk plasmon energy (EP) associated with collective electron oscillations. The position of this peak shifts slightly with strain, where tensile deformation generally induces a redshift and compressive strain results in a marginal blueshift. These shifts originate from strain-induced modifications in the electronic band dispersion and free-electron density. Additional smaller peaks at higher energies (> 15 eV) are attributed to interband transitions involving deeper electronic states. The sensitivity of EP to strain, while modest, indicates that controlled strain engineering could be used to fine-tune the plasmonic response of goldene for specific optoelectronic or sensing applications. The plasmon resonance in a material is generally associated with the energy at which εr(ω) approaches or crosses zero, indicating the collective oscillation of conduction electrons. On the other hand, the electron energy loss spectrum (EELS), given by Im[−1/ε(ω)], represents the probability of energy loss of fast electrons traversing the material. Consequently, the main EELS peak may appear at slightly higher energies than the zero-crossing of εr(ω) due to damping and interband transition effects. Therefore, the zero-crossing of εr(ω) marks the onset of plasmon excitation, while the EELS peak corresponds to the energy where this excitation leads to the strongest energy loss signal.

The spectra display pronounced strain-dependent variations in intensity, with tensile strain leading to an overall enhancement of the JDOS amplitude across the visible to UV range, whereas compressive strain tends to suppress it. These trends suggest that mechanical strain serves as an effective means to tailor the density of accessible optical transitions, thereby modulating the absorption properties and plasmonic response of the goldene.

Under tensile strain, the static absorption shows a slight decrease (from 2.88 to 0.74 for + 10%) accompanied by a redshift in the peak position and moderate reduction in peak intensity, particularly for + 10% strain, where the peak value drops to 5.17 × 105 at 19.37 eV. Refractive index n(ω) and extinction coefficient k(ω) are calculated by Eqs. (7) and (8), respectively.

n(ω) describes how the phase velocity of light changes as it propagates through a medium and directly reflects the strength of light–matter interactions determined by the electronic structure. Its frequency dependence reveals dispersion and interband transition effects, while the static value n(0) is linked to the dielectric response in the low-energy limit, making it a crucial parameter for photonic devices, waveguides, and light-harvesting systems. For goldene, the strain-dependent results (Fig. 5; Table 2) show that in the unstrained, SV of n(ω) is 0.74 with a prominent 2.15 at low photon energies, indicating strong optical confinement. Compressive strain (− 2% - to − 10) generally increases SV up to 0.77, with the − 2% case showing the largest PV​ of 2.92 due to strain-induced modifications in the EBS that enhance interband transitions. Conversely, tensile strain (+ 2% to + 10%) lowers SV​ to 0.71–0.73 and reduces PV, implying weaker dielectric screening and reduced light confinement. The observed peak shifts in n(ω) spectra under strain arise from changes in JDOS and transition matrix elements, demonstrating that goldene’s refractive index can be effectively tuned via strain engineering for targeted optoelectronic applications. In summary, the photon of energy 0.54 eV suffers at a biaxial strain value of −2% suffers the maximum disturbance while travelling through the plane of the goldene ML.

The extinction coefficient k(ω), representing the imaginary part of the complex refractive index, quantifies EM wave attenuation and is directly linked to optical absorption and penetration depth. For goldene, k(ω) shows a distinct low-energy peak across all strain states, characteristic of metallic intraband (Drude) behavior, with peak energies in the far-IR to THz range (~ 0.06–0.12 eV). The maximum PV (46.67 at 0.06 eV) occurs under + 5% tensile strain, indicating enhanced free-carrier optical losses. The unstrained condition exhibits a peak of 28.36 at 0.12 eV, while mild compression (− 2%) slightly elevates the peak to 28.73. Strong compression (− 10%) further raises the peak to 33.95, whereas high tensile strain (+ 10%) decreases the extinction coefficient to 0.0073 with a peak of 29.00 at 0.06 eV. At higher photon energies, k(ω) rapidly declines, reflecting reduced intraband absorption and minor interband contributions. These results demonstrate that strain effectively tunes goldene’s low-energy optical losses and skin depth, with + 5% tensile strain producing the most pronounced Drude-like response and compression shifting the absorption peak toward ~ 0.12 eV with moderate enhancement.

Reflectivity is a fundamental optical property that measures the fraction of incident light reflected from a material’s surface, impacting metallic luster, energy shielding, and plasmonic performance. In goldene, the reflectivity spectra under varying strain conditions feature a pronounced low-energy peak arising from the metallic (Drude-like) free-electron response. The static reflectivity, R(0), is highest under − 10% strain (0.55), followed by − 5% (0.49), − 2% (0.47), unstrained (0.46), + 2% (0.44), + 5% (0.41), and lowest at + 10% strain (0.37), indicating compressive strain enhances low-frequency reflection while tensile strain diminishes it. Peak reflectivity values also vary with strain: 0.54 (− 10%), 0.48 (− 5%), 0.49 (− 2%), 0.46 (0%), 0.45 (+ 2%), 0.47 (+ 5%), and 0.43 (+ 10%). The peak positions shift slightly with strain: the unstrained goldene peak occurs near 0.80 eV; compressive strain induces a blue shift to approximately 0.78 eV (− 10%) and 0.79 eV (− 5%), remains near 0.80 eV at − 2%, while tensile strain causes a marginal blue shift to ~ 0.81 eV (+ 2%) followed by red shifts to ~ 0.76 eV (+ 5%) and ~ 0.74 eV (+ 10%) at higher tensile strains. These shifts reflect strain-induced changes in EBS affecting carrier density and plasma frequency. Beyond the low-energy region, reflectivity drops sharply under all strain states due to decreased interband contributions and the onset of interband transitions, stabilizing below 0.1 in the visible to UV range. This comprehensive strain-dependent reflectivity study reveals that compressive strain significantly increases both static and peak reflectivity with slight peak energy shifts to higher values, whereas tensile strain lowers reflectivity and shifts peaks to lower energies, enabling precise tuning for IR optics and plasmonic device applications.

From Table 3, it is clear that goldene exhibits optical properties that differ significantly from graphene, silicene, and phosphorene. The absorption coefficient (α) of goldene is 2.88, considerably lower than graphene’s high value of ~ 5 × 105, indicating that goldene absorbs less incident light. Its refractive index (n) is 0.74, which is noticeably smaller than the typical ranges for graphene (2.3–2.7), silicene (2.0–2.5.0.5), and phosphorene (1.5–3.5), reflecting a distinct light-bending behavior. The extinction coefficient (k) of goldene is only 0.03, far below the values reported for graphene (1.0–1.5.0.5), silicene (0.5–1.2), and phosphorene (0.5–2.5), suggesting weaker light absorption losses. The reflectivity (R) of goldene is 0.42, although comparative data for the other materials are not provided. Overall, goldene demonstrates lower refractive and absorptive responses than these well-known 2D materials, highlighting its unique optical characteristics that could be advantageous for applications requiring reduced light absorption and scattering. Thermoelectric properties

Thermoelectricity refers to the direct conversion between heat and electrical energy, arising from the interplay of a material’s thermal and electronic properties. It operates on two fundamental principles: the Seebeck effect, where a temperature difference produces an electric voltage, and the Peltier effect, in which an electric current induces heat transfer. These phenomena are utilized in practical applications such as harvesting energy from waste heat and achieving solid-state cooling. In this section, we present a systematic investigation of the thermoelectric properties of goldene under both pristine conditions and applied uniform biaxial strain, encompassing tensile and compressive regimes. We examine the impact of strain on critical parameters, including the Seebeck coefficient (S), electrical conductivity (σ), and thermal conductivity (κ), and assess their combined influence on PF. The variation of these thermoelectric parameters with temperature is shown in Fig. 6. For the unstrained configuration, goldene exhibits a positive Seebeck coefficient of 5.42 × 10− 4 V/K, indicating hole-dominated transport. When tensile strain is introduced, the Seebeck coefficient increases markedly, attaining a peak value of 7.03 × 10− 4 at + 2% strain. However, further tensile stretching up to + 10% results in a steep decline, reducing it to just 0.55 × 10− 4 V/K. At low temperatures (below 200 K), the Seebeck coefficient shows a pronounced enhancement, reaching its maximum within the 100–150 K range (Fig. 6(a)), after which it gradually decreases with further temperature rise. In contrast, applying compressive strain reverses the Seebeck coefficient (Table 4), leading to n-type conduction. At − 5% strain, S reaches − 2.99 × 10⁻⁴ V/K, while at − 10% it shifts to a large positive value of 8.45 × 10− 4 V/K, indicating a strain-driven electronic reconstruction that restores p-type behavior. This transition highlights the strong sensitivity of goldene’s band edges to lattice compression.

Figure 6(b) presents the variation of electrical conductivity (σ) of goldene with temperature under different biaxial strain conditions, while the corresponding values at 300 K are listed in Table 4. In the absence of strain, goldene exhibits a relatively high electrical conductivity of 4.32 × 1020, which gradually decreases with rising temperature due to enhanced phonon scattering. The application of tensile strain causes a pronounced suppression of conductivity and at + 10% strain, σ drops to 1.10 × 1020, attributable to reduced carrier mobility from band flattening and higher effective mass. Even at moderate tensile strains (+ 2% and + 5%), conductivity shows a considerable decline compared to the unstrained case, consistent with strain-driven weakening of charge transport pathways. In contrast, compressive strain enhances the electrical transport characteristics of goldene. At − 5% strain, the conductivity peaks (Table 4) at 5.73 × 1020, surpassing the unstrained value. This improvement is particularly prominent in the 100–300 K range, where reduced phonon scattering and strain-driven band modifications facilitate higher carrier velocities. Even at − 2% and − 10% strain, the conductivity remains generally higher than that under tensile strains, though a slight reduction is observed at elevated temperatures.

In the pristine state, the thermal conductivity (κ) of goldene increases with temperature, reaching 3.63 × 1014 at 300 K, primarily due to stronger phonon contributions at elevated temperatures. The application of tensile strain leads to a notable suppression of κ. At + 2% strain, the conductivity drops (Fig. 6(c))to a minimum of 1.58 × 1014, marking a significant reduction compared to the unstrained case. Similar decreases are observed at + 5% (2.68 × 1014 W/mK) and + 10% (2.93 × 1014 W/mK), demonstrating that tensile strain softens the lattice, reduces phonon velocities, and intensifies phonon–phonon scattering, thereby diminishing heat transport capabilities. Compressive strain also leads to a suppression of thermal conductivity. At − 2%, − 5%, and − 10% strain, κ decreases to 1.67 × 1014 and 1.69 × 1014 W/mK, respectively (Table 4), all lower than the pristine case. The nearly unchanged values across these compressive strains indicate that lattice compression enhances anharmonic effects and phonon scattering, thereby restricting heat transport efficiency.

Figure 6(d) illustrates the temperature-dependent PF (PF = S²σ) of goldene subjected to various biaxial strain conditions. In its pristine form, PF of goldene remains relatively low, not exceeding 1.3 × 1011 W/mK2s at 300 K, due to a moderate balance between the Seebeck coefficient and electrical conductivity. Under tensile strain, the PF improves moderately. for instance, at + 5% strain, it rises to 5.98 × 1011 W/mK2s, whereas + 2% strain results in a smaller increase to 1.64 × 1011 W/mK2s. However, excessive tensile strain (+ 10%) suppresses the PF to 3.32 × 1011 W/mK2s, primarily due to a significant drop in electrical conductivity despite a modestly enhanced Seebeck coefficient. The most pronounced improvement occurs under compressive strain, especially at − 2%, where the PF attains a maximum of 9.10 × 1011 W/mK2s at 300 K, nearly ten times higher than the pristine value. At − 5% strain, the PF remains high at 5.12 × 1011 W/mK2s, while − 10% strain still sustains a relatively strong PF of 2.61 × 1011 W/mK2s. As illustrated in Fig. 6(d), the PF at − 2% strain peaks sharply in the 300–400 K range, exceeding 5 × 1012 W/mK2s, significantly outperforming all other strain states. This exceptional behavior results from the combined effects of a moderately large Seebeck coefficient, enhanced electrical conductivity under compression, and reduced thermal conductivity.

The thermoelectric characteristics of goldene display significant differences compared to graphene, silicene, and phosphorene, as summarized in Table 3. Goldene demonstrates an extraordinarily high electrical conductivity of approximately 4.32 × 1020 S/m, far surpassing graphene (1.46 × 106 S/m) and phosphorene (4,000–6,000 S/m), Its thermal conductivity is also exceptionally large (~ 3.63 × 1014 W/mK), exceeding the reported values for graphene (4,000 W/mK), silicene (9.4–20 W/mK), and phosphorene (10–30 W/mK) by several orders of magnitude. The Seebeck coefficient of goldene is 5.42 × 10− 4 V/K. Importantly, the PF, a key indicator of thermoelectric efficiency, reaches a remarkably high 1.27 × 1011 W/mK2s for goldene, well above graphene (10.35 × 10− 2 W/mK2s), silicene (0.5–3 × 10− 3 W/mK2s), and phosphorene (5 × 10− 3 W/mK2s). These results highlight goldene’s exceptional potential for thermoelectric energy conversion, indicating promising applications in highly efficient thermoelectric devices.

Conclusion

In summary, the structural, electrical, optical and thermoelectric properties of goldene ML have studied in the presence of uniform biaxial compressive and tensile mechanical strain through DFT. The PDOS and EBS analyses confirm that goldene exhibits robust metallic behavior with no bandgap opening under varying strain conditions. From εr(ω), it is evident that goldene maintains metallic-like dispersive behavior with a plasmon resonance in the 3.8-4.2 eV range, largely unaffected by strains up to ± 10%. This robustness highlights its potential for strain-tolerant plasmonic and optoelectronic applications. L(ω) spectra reveal a dominant plasmon peak in the 6.5–8.5 eV range with minor strain-induced shifts, indicating the possibility of tuning goldene’s plasmonic response via lattice deformation. The Seebeck coefficient attains its maximum of 7.03 × 10− 4 V/K at + 2% tensile strain, maintaining p-type transport, while compressive strain induces n-type behavior, with a minimum of − 2.99 × 10− 4 V/K at − 5% and polarity reversal at − 10%. In contrast, electrical conductivity improves significantly under compression, reaching the highest value of 5.73 × 1020 S/m at − 5%, whereas tensile strain reduces it drastically to 1.10 × 1020 S/m at + 10%. Thermal conductivity, relative to the pristine case (3.63 × 1014 W/mK), decreases under both tensile and compressive strains, with the lowest value of 1.58 × 1014 W/mK at + 2%, a trend favorable for thermoelectric applications. Taken together, these strain-driven variations demonstrate that moderate compressive strain (–2%) produces the highest PF of 9.10 × 1011 W/mK2s, confirming strain engineering as a powerful approach to optimize the thermoelectric performance of goldene.
