Diplotaxis tenuifolia (L.) DC.: phytochemical profiling and multifunctional biological evaluation of extracts obtained by maceration and ultrasound-assisted extraction
Authors: Enis Fuat Tufekci, Sumeyra Basyigit, Mustafa Ocal, Ferhat Ulu, Mehmet Cengiz Baloglu, Milena Terzić, Gokhan Zengin, Ismail Senkardes, Zoltán Cziáky, József Jekő, Yasemin Celik Altunoglu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diplotaxis tenuifolia is an edible Brassicaceae species recognized for its phytochemical richness and health-promoting potential. This study evaluated six extracts obtained by maceration (MAC) and ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) using ethyl acetate (EtOAc), methanol (MeOH), and water. LC-MS/MS profiling revealed solvent- and method-dependent metabolite occurrence, including glucosinolates, flavonol glycosides, phenolic acids, and long-chain fatty acids, with representative compounds such as glucoraphanin, quercetin- and kaempferol-derived glycosides, p-coumaric and ferulic acids, and α-linolenic, linoleic, and erucic acids. UAE enhanced phytochemical diversity, particularly in water extracts, increasing detected compounds from 16 to 30. Total phenolic and flavonoid contents ranged from 15.80 to 24.07 mg GAE per g and from 2.06 to 31.53 mg RE per g, respectively. In antioxidant assays, MeOH extracts showed the highest DPPH activity (8.55 mg TE per g), whereas EtOAc-UAE exhibited the strongest CUPRAC and metal-chelating activities (45.67 mg TE per g and 22.14 mg EDTAE per g). In enzyme inhibition assays, MeOH-UAE showed the highest acetylcholinesterase inhibition (1.99 mg GALAE per g), while EtOAc-UAE was most active against butyrylcholinesterase, α-amylase, and α-glucosidase (3.97 mg GALAE per g, 0.89 mmol ACAE per g, and 0.64 mmol ACAE per g). EtOAc extracts were the only fractions active against Bacillus subtilis (6.7 and 8.7 mm for EtOAc-MAC and EtOAc-UAE), while EtOAc-UAE reduced Staphylococcus aureus biofilm formation to 13.6% of the control. EtOAc-UAE also showed the strongest cytotoxicity in MDA-MB-231 and PC-3 cells (IC50: 306.3 and 389.6 µg mL−1), with RT-qPCR confirming approximately 2-fold bax and 3-fold LC3B upregulation. These findings demonstrate that solvent polarity and extraction method critically determine the phytochemical and biological profiles of D. tenuifolia extracts.

Introduction

The Brassicaceae family is an economically and nutritionally important botanical group, comprising approximately 3500 species and 350 genera distributed worldwide. Many Brassicaceae vegetables, including broccoli, cabbage, turnip, and rapeseed, are widely consumed and valued for their distinctive sensory properties and rich phytochemical composition. Their biological relevance is largely associated with diverse bioactive constituents, particularly glucosinolates and their hydrolysis products, as well as phenolic compounds, carotenoids, tocopherols, minerals, and vitamins. These compounds have contributed to the growing interest in Brassicaceae species as dietary sources with potential functional value.1,2

Within this family, the genus Diplotaxis is represented in the flora of Türkiye by five species: Diplotaxis tenuifolia (L.) DC., Diplotaxis viminea (L.) DC., Diplotaxis muralis (L.) DC., Diplotaxis acris (Forssk.) Boiss., and Diplotaxis erucoides (L.) DC.3 Among these, D. tenuifolia, commonly referred to as “wild rocket” or “perennial wall-rocket,” is a widely distributed edible species. Although native to the Mediterranean basin, D. tenuifolia is a ruderal species with broad ecological adaptability and an extensive distribution range, including the Inner Anatolia, Marmara, and Black Sea regions of Türkiye, where it occurs in fallow lands, roadsides, cultivated soils, and other disturbed habitats.3,4

D. tenuifolia has been increasingly valued for its culinary applications. Its young and basal leaves are consumed fresh or cooked owing to their distinctive bitter-pungent aroma and flavor, contributing to its growing significance in the food industry. Accordingly, the plant has been commercially cultivated in several countries, with Italy being one of the leading producers.5,6 Beyond its culinary value, D. tenuifolia has been reported in Mediterranean and Near Eastern traditional medicine for digestive, expectorant, antidiarrheal, and aphrodisiac purposes.7 These traditional uses are pharmacologically plausible given the presence of various bioactive compounds in the plant's aerial parts, including glucosinolates, flavonoids, ascorbic acid, and carotenoids.4,8

Glucosinolates (β-thioglucoside-N-hydroxysulfates) are sulfur-containing secondary metabolites characteristic of Brassicaceae species. Specifically in D. tenuifolia, glucosativin has been reported as the predominant glucosinolate compound, while other glucosinolates such as glucoerucin and glucoraphanin have also been identified in lower concentrations.9 Glucosinolates are of particular interest due to their role in plant defense mechanisms and their potential health-promoting effects in humans. Their biological functions are largely mediated through enzymatic hydrolysis, catalyzed either by endogenous plant myrosinase or by microbial enzymes in the human gut, resulting in the formation of various biologically active compounds, including isothiocyanates, thiocyanates, nitriles, and epithionitriles. Among these derivatives, isothiocyanates have been widely studied for their broad-spectrum biological activities, including anticancer, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and antioxidant effects.10

Flavonoids are a diverse group of polyphenolic secondary metabolites widely distributed in the plant kingdom. They are well-known for their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties, which are largely mediated by free-radical scavenging, modulation of signaling pathways, and regulation of gene expression.11 The aerial parts of D. tenuifolia have been reported to be rich in flavonoids, with isorhamnetin, kaempferol, and quercetin derivatives frequently identified as major constituents.4,8 Among them, quercetin-based flavonol glycosides have been particularly highlighted in methanolic leaf extracts, including quercetin-3,4′-diglucoside-3′-(6-sinapoylglucoside) and quercetin-3,3′,4-triglucoside. In addition to flavonoids, the aerial parts of the plant are rich in other antioxidant molecules, including ascorbic acid and some carotenoids, such as lutein, β-cryptoxanthin, β-carotene, zeaxanthin, and violaxanthin, which may further enhance its health-promoting potential. Together, these bioactive compounds may contribute to the health-related properties of D. tenuifolia and support its use as a functional food.6

Previous studies have explored the biological activities of D. tenuifolia extracts using a variety of approaches, and some have assessed different solvent systems, reporting promising antimicrobial, cytotoxic, or antioxidant effects.12–16 However, studies that comprehensively investigate the effects of both extraction techniques and solvent types within a single experimental framework remain limited. In the present study, six extracts were systematically prepared using two extraction techniques (ultrasound-assisted extraction and maceration) and three solvents (ethyl acetate, methanol, and water), and each extract was independently evaluated to characterize its biological properties.

Accordingly, this study aimed to investigate the chemical composition and multifunctional biological activities of D. tenuifolia extracts, including their antimicrobial, antibiofilm, antioxidant, enzyme inhibitory, and cytotoxic properties, by systematically evaluating six extracts prepared using two extraction techniques and three different solvents. By integrating these extraction approaches with a broad set of bioactivity assays, the study seeks to provide a comprehensive understanding of the bioactivity profile of D. tenuifolia extracts and highlight their prospective value as a multifunctional natural source of bioactive compounds with potential therapeutic relevance.

Materials and methods

Plant collection

Plant samples were collected in 2022 from the Kartal, Istanbul, Turkey. Dr İsmail Senkardes officially carried out the botanical identification. A reference specimen bearing the code MARE-22454 was placed at Marmara University's Faculty of Pharmacy's herbarium. The aerial parts were separated immediately after collection and allowed to air dry at room temperature in a well-ventilated, shady area. The plant material was pulverized into a fine powder once it had dried completely. The powdered samples were kept under constant conditions in light-proof containers to maintain chemical stability and prevent spoiling.

Preparation of extract solutions for bioassays

These extracts were obtained by using three solvents – ethyl acetate (EtOAc), methanol (MeOH), and water-in two extraction methods (maceration (MAC) and ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE)). In the MAC technique, the plant materials (10 g) were individually macerated with the solvents for 24 h at room temperature. Regarding UAE, the plant materials (10 g) were sonicated in a ultrasound bath for 20 min at room temperature. All extracts were filtered and then solvents were removed by using a rotary-evaporator. Then, water extracts were lyophilized and all extracts were stored at 4 °C until analysis.

UHPLC-MS/MS analysis

The chemical characterization of the tested extracts was performed using ultra high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) coupled with an ESI/Orbitrap-MS system (Thermo Q-Exactive, Thermo Scientific, USA). A gradient UHPLC separation was carried out using a Dionex 3000RS Ultimate system. All analytical details are given in SI.

Total phenolic and flavonoid contents

Total phenolic content (TPC) and total flavonoid content (TFC) were determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu and aluminum chloride (AlCl3) colorimetric assays, respectively, following previously described protocols.17,18 Standard curves were generated using gallic acid (for phenolic content) and rutin (for flavonoid content), allowing for the calculation of results as GAE and RE per gram, respectively.

Antioxidant assays

Antioxidant activity was determined through a battery of in vitro tests, adhering to the method described by Grochowski et al.19 Reducing power was assessed via FRAP and CUPRAC, whereas radical scavenging was measured using DPPH and ABTS. Results from these four assays were converted to Trolox equivalents and expressed as mg TE per g of dried extract. Total antioxidant capacity was additionally quantified via the phosphomolybdenum (PBD) assay (mmol TE per g), and mg EDTA equivalents per gram (mg EDTAE per g) was used for evaluating the metal chelating ability.

Enzyme inhibitory activity assays

Using established colorimetric protocols,19 the extracts were screened for enzyme inhibition against five relevant targets: acetylcholinesterase (AChE), butyrylcholinesterase (BChE), tyrosinase, α-amylase, and α-glucosidase. To enable comparability across samples, inhibition values were quantified relative to reference standards. Inhibition data were normalized using reference standards, with cholinesterase inhibition expressed as mg GALAE per g, α-amylase and α-glucosidase inhibition as mmol ACAE per g, and tyrosinase inhibition as mg KAE per g.

Antimicrobial activity assay

Microorganisms and culture conditions

To evaluate the antimicrobial potential of the extracts, a panel of reference microorganisms was selected, comprising foodborne pathogens, food spoilage-associated bacteria, and clinically significant opportunistic microorganisms. The antimicrobial activity of the extracts was evaluated against six Gram-negative bacterial strains (Acinetobacter haemolyticus ATCC 19002, Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 13883, Klebsiella aerogenes ATCC 13048, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853, Salmonella Typhimurium ATCC 14028), three Gram-positive bacterial strains (Bacillus subtilis ATCC 6633, Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923), and one yeast strain (Candida albicans ATCC 10231).

All strains were American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) reference strains retrieved from the Department of Medical Microbiology culture collection at the Faculty of Medicine, Kastamonu University. Bacterial and yeast cultures were prepared by incubating them on nutrient agar (NA, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and Sabouraud Dextrose agar (SDA, Merck), respectively, at 35 °C for 24 h.

Disk diffusion assay

The antimicrobial activity of the extracts was assessed using the disk diffusion method based on the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) guidelines for bacteria20 and with minor modifications based on the Method for Antifungal Disk Diffusion Susceptibility Testing of Yeasts; Approved Guideline21 for C. albicans. In brief, the inoculum was prepared from fresh microbial cultures at a standard McFarland density of 0.5 (∼1.0–2.0 × 108 CFU mL−1 for bacteria and ∼1.0–5.0 × 106 CFU mL−1 for C. albicans). The inoculum was then evenly spread on Mueller–Hinton agar (MHA, Merck) for bacteria and SDA for C. albicans using a sterile cotton swab. Subsequently, blank discs were placed onto the agar surfaces and impregnated with 20 µL of extract stock solutions (20 mg mL−1), yielding 400 µg of extract per disc. The plates were then incubated at 35 °C for 18 ± 2 h for bacterial strains and 24 h for C. albicans. Levofloxacin (5 µg) and fluconazole (25 µg) served as positive controls for the bacterial strains and C. albicans, respectively; DMSO (the extract solvent) was included as the negative control. Following incubation, inhibition zones were measured with a standard ruler and reported as diameters (mm). Extracts with inhibition zones ≥16 mm were considered to exhibit potent antimicrobial activity, those with zones between 11–15 mm were regarded as demonstrating moderate antimicrobial activity, and extracts with zones ≤10 mm were considered to show weak antimicrobial activity.22 The assay was performed independently on three separate occasions.

Inhibition of biofilm formation assay

The inhibition of biofilm formation by the extracts was evaluated against S. aureus ATCC 25923 using the crystal violet (CV) assay, following the protocol of Coffey and Anderson,23 with minor modifications. The assay was performed in flat-bottomed 96-well microtiter plates. An overnight culture of S. aureus was diluted 1 : 100 in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth (NZYTech, Lisbon, Portugal), and 190 µL of the diluted bacterial suspension was dispensed into each well. Subsequently, 10 µL of extract working solutions (5 mg mL−1) were added to reach a final concentration of 250 µg mL−1.

This concentration was selected based on preliminary results obtained from the broth microdilution method, in which the extracts were tested using a two-fold serial dilution scheme to determine their minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC). Although the MIC values of the extracts against S. aureus were >1000 µg mL−1, 250 µg mL−1 was selected as a working concentration for the biofilm assay to reduce the likelihood of complete growth inhibition while enabling assessment of inhibition of biofilm formation. In the control group, 10 µL of sterile distilled water was used for water extracts; 10 µL of 20% DMSO (v/v) for EtOAc extracts, resulting in a final DMSO concentration of 1%; and 10 µL of 10% DMSO for MeOH extracts, yielding a final concentration of 0.5%.

The plate was incubated statically at 35 °C for 24 h. Following incubation, non-adherent planktonic cells were removed by gentle aspiration, and wells were washed three times with distilled water. The plates were then air-dried at 35 °C for approximately 15 min and stained with 0.1% (w/v) CV for 5 min at room temperature. Excess stain was removed by washing, and the bound dye was solubilized with 96% EtOH.

Absorbance was measured at 550 nm using EtOH as the blank in a spectrophotometer (Multiskan Go, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Biofilm formation (%) was determined using the formula: [(ODextract/ODcontrol) × 100], where ODextract is the mean absorbance of the extract-treated wells, and ODcontrol is the mean absorbance of the corresponding solvent control—sterile distilled water for water extracts, 1% DMSO for EtOAc extracts, and 0.5% DMSO for MeOH extracts. Each condition was tested in triplicate wells, and the experiment was independently repeated three times.

Cytotoxicity assay

Cancer cell lines and growth conditions

In this study, two metastatic cancer cell lines were employed: MDA-MB-231, a triple-negative breast cancer cell line lacking progesterone (PR), estrogen (ER), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2); and PC-3, an androgen receptor (AR)-deficient human prostate cancer cell line that does not produce prostate-specific antigen (PSA).24,25 The MDA-MB-231 and PC-3 cell lines were kindly provided by the Department of Molecular Biology and Genetics, Boğaziçi University, and the Department of Biochemistry, Faculty of Science, Selçuk University, respectively.

Cells were propagated in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 1% (v/v) non-essential amino acids (NEAA) without l-asparagine and l-glutamine (Pan-Biotech GmbH, Aidenbach, Germany), 0.1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin (10 000 U mL−1 each; Lonza, Basel, Switzerland), and 0.01 mg mL−1 human insulin (Pan-Biotech). Cell cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

MTT assay procedure

The cytotoxic activity of the extracts was evaluated against MDA-MB-231 and PC-3 cell lines using the MTT [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide] assay. Cells in the proliferation phase were harvested and counted using a hemocytometer. Subsequently, 1.0 × 105 cells per mL in a 100 µL volume were seeded into each well of a flat-bottom, TC-treated 96-well microplate and incubated for 24 h to allow for cell attachment and stabilization under standard culture conditions. After this period, the extracts were applied at final concentrations of 1000, 500, 250, 125, and 62.5 µg mL−1 by adding 10 µL of extract working solutions (10, 5, 2.5, 1.25, and 0.625 mg mL−1, respectively). The working solutions were prepared by two-fold serial dilution of 20 mg mL−1 stock solutions with sterile ultrapure water. Aqueous DMSO was used, when necessary, to ensure complete solubilization of the stock solutions. Treatment was carried out for 48 h under standard culture conditions. Vehicle controls consisted of sterile ultrapure water for all extracts except EtOAc-UAE, which was evaluated using concentration-matched DMSO controls.

At the end of the treatment period, prior to further processing, the cells were examined under an inverted microscope (Leica DMi1, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) at 100× magnification, and representative images were captured for morphological assessment. The medium was aspirated, and 100 µL of MTT working solution (0.5 mg mL−1 MTT and 0.5% FBS in DMEM) was added per well for 4 h. Thereafter, the reagent was removed, and the formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 µL of pure DMSO. Absorbance was read at 570 nm against a DMSO blank. Cell viability (%) was calculated using the formula [(ODextract/ODcontrol) × 100], where ODextract and ODcontrol represent the average absorbance values of the extract-treated and control wells, respectively. The cytotoxicity data for EtOAc-UAE were normalized to concentration-matched DMSO-treated controls corresponding to the final DMSO concentrations present in each treatment condition (0.25–4%). The remaining extracts were assessed relative to the sterile ultrapure water-treated control. For each condition, values from three technical replicates were averaged, and the assay was performed in three independent experiments.

IC50 values, defined as the extract concentration causing a 50% reduction in cell viability, were computed using nonlinear regression analysis (log[inhibitor] vs. normalized response, variable slope model) with GraphPad Prism version 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).26

Analysis of apoptotic and autophagic gene expression

To investigate the effects of the extract with the lowest IC50 value (EtOAc-UAE, determined in MDA-MB-231 cells) on the expression levels of apoptotic and autophagic marker genes, quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis was conducted. For this purpose, the cell line in which the selected extract exhibited the strongest inhibitory effect on viability was treated with the extract at its IC50 concentration (306.3 µg mL−1) for 48 h. After the incubation period, cells were harvested, and total RNA was extracted using the GeneJET RNA Purification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions.

The isolated RNA was treated with DNase enzyme at 37 °C for 30 min and subsequently converted into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., Hercules, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Gene expression analysis was done on a Rotor-Gene Q real-time PCR system (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany).

The target genes included the pro-apoptotic gene bax and the autophagy-related genes beclin-1 and LC3B. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene. The primer sequences used for each gene are listed in Table S1. The amplification protocol and primer sequences were adopted from previously described methods.27,28 Gene expression levels in EtOAc-UAE-treated cells were compared with those in vehicle-control cells treated with DMSO at the final concentration corresponding to the EtOAc-UAE IC50 treatment condition (1.2%).

The 20 µL PCR reaction comprised 10 µL of 2× SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad), 500 nM of each primer, and 50 ng of cDNA; nuclease-free water was used to make up the reaction volume. Cycling parameters are summarized in Table S2. Relative transcript levels were determined using the comparative Ct (ΔΔCt) approach described by Livak and Schmittgen.29

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Pairwise comparisons were performed using the Independent-Samples t-test for parametric data and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-parametric data. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test was used. The statistical analysis was carried out with IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A confidence level of 95% (p < 0.05) was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Total phenolic and flavonoid content

The polyphenolic profile of D. tenuifolia extracts was strongly modulated by both solvent polarity and extraction technique, as reflected in the pronounced variability of total phenolic content (TPC) and total flavonoid content (TFC) across all extraction conditions (Table 1). TPC values ranged from 15.80 mg GAE per g (water-MAC) to 24.07 mg GAE per g (EtOAc-UAE), whereas TFC exhibited a substantially broader variation, from 2.06 mg RE per g (EtOAc-UAE) to 31.53 mg RE per g (MeOH-UAE). Notably, MeOH extracts yielded the highest flavonoid recovery, with MeOH-UAE (31.53 mg RE per g) exceeding MeOH-MAC (27.37 mg RE per g), corresponding to an approximate increase of ∼15%, while water extracts showed a similar UAE-driven enhancement (∼35% increase), confirming the efficiency of ultrasound-assisted extraction in improving mass transfer and disrupting plant cell matrices through cavitation phenomena.

A marked decoupling between TPC and TFC was observed in EtOAc fractions, which exhibited relatively high TPC values (21.08–24.07 mg GAE per g) despite minimal flavonoid content (2.06–2.24 mg RE per g). This inconsistency likely reflects the well-documented lack of specificity of the Folin–Ciocalteu assay, which is prone to interference from non-phenolic reducing agents, particularly lipophilic constituents such as long-chain fatty acids abundant in these fractions. Consequently, TFC provides a more selective and chemically relevant indicator of flavonoid abundance across solvent systems in the present study.

When compared with previously reported data, the TPC and TFC values obtained herein are generally consistent with, and in several cases higher than, those reported for Diplotaxis spp. Ressurreição et al.6 reported TPC values of 4.30–13.80 mg GAE per g and TFC values of 1.35–6.71 mg CE per g, which fall below or within the lower range of the present findings, particularly for MeOH and water fractions. The enhanced polyphenolic recovery observed in this study can be attributed to the synergistic effect of optimized solvent polarity and ultrasound-assisted extraction, where acoustic cavitation promotes cell wall disruption, enhances solvent penetration, and facilitates the release of both free and bound phenolic compounds.

In contrast, substantially higher TPC values reported by Abdalrahman et al.30 (144.49 mg GAE per L) are not directly comparable due to differences in expression units, plant material, and extraction conditions, all of which significantly influence apparent phenolic yield and complicate cross-study comparisons. Similarly, TPC values reported by Bahloul et al.31 for aqueous Diplotaxis extracts (up to 29.30 mg CAE per g) are in good agreement with the upper range observed in the present study, further supporting the notion that D. tenuifolia is a relatively rich source of polyphenolic compounds within the genus.

Overall, the results clearly demonstrate that extraction strategy is a critical determinant of both the quantity and apparent profile of phenolic compounds in D. tenuifolia. In particular, ultrasound-assisted extraction consistently enhances flavonoid recovery and improves extraction efficiency, underscoring its suitability as a green and high-performance technique for the isolation of bioactive phenolics from complex plant matrices.

Chemical composition

LC-MS/MS analysis revealed a clear polarity-dependent phytochemical distribution across all six extraction conditions (Tables 2 and 3). Polar phenolics (phenolic acids, flavonol glycosides, glucosinolates) were predominantly recovered in MeOH and water fractions, whereas long-chain fatty acids were confined to EtOAc extracts. This tripartite distribution corresponds to the chemotaxonomic profile of the Diplotaxis genus reported by Ressurreição et al.,6 who identified flavonol glycosides, glucosinolates, and fatty acids as core chemical markers across multiple species, and is consistent with previous characterization of D. tenuifolia phenolics by Bell and Wagstaff.9

MeOH extracts exhibited the highest chemical complexity, dominated by quercetin-, isorhamnetin-, and kaempferol-derived glycosides. Quercetin-3,4′-di-O-glucoside and its acylated derivatives (sinapoyl and feruloyl forms) were among the most abundant constituents, in agreement with earlier reports defining these compounds as characteristic metabolites of D. tenuifolia.6,9 Glucosinolates (glucoraphanin and gluconasturtiin) were also consistently detected in MeOH fractions, confirming the species-specific glucosinolate profile previously described,4,10 together with hydroxycinnamic acids and their glycosides.16 EtOAc fractions were characterized by long-chain fatty acids spanning C18–C30, with erucic acid (C22:1n-9) serving as a diagnostic Brassicaceae marker.6 To our knowledge, the systematic LC-MS/MS characterization of the fatty acid profile of D. tenuifolia EtOAc fractions, encompassing eleven long-chain fatty acids from C18 to C30, has not been previously reported and represents a novel contribution to the phytochemical knowledge of this species. Water extracts contained mainly low-molecular-weight phenolic acids and flavonol monoglucosides, while lacking acylated flavonols, glucosinolates, and fatty acids, consistent with their restricted polarity range.32

Ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) significantly increased overall phytochemical diversity compared to maceration. Under maceration, MeOH-MAC yielded 45 compounds, EtOAc-MAC 17, and water-MAC 16, with only three compounds shared across all fractions (malic acid, citric acid, p-coumaric acid). UAE increased these values to 46 (MeOH), 22 (EtOAc), and 30 (water) compounds, respectively. The most pronounced effect was observed in water fractions, where compound diversity increased by 88% (16 to 30 compounds), reflecting enhanced cell wall disruption and mass transfer induced by acoustic cavitation.16 MeOH fractions showed minimal change (45 vs. 46 compounds), consistent with the inherently high extraction efficiency of MeOH due to its broad polarity range and ability to disrupt hydrogen-bonded plant matrices.32 EtOAc fractions also showed increased diversity under UAE (17 to 22 compounds), indicating improved release of moderately polar metabolites. UAE additionally increased cross-fraction overlap, with eight compounds detected in all three solvent systems compared to three under maceration. These included ferulic acid, kaempferol-3,4′-di-O-glucoside, isorhamnetin derivatives, and glucosinolates, indicating broader metabolite redistribution across polarity boundaries under ultrasonic conditions. Overall, more than 50 compounds were identified across all extracts, exceeding values reported in previous single-solvent studies on D. tenuifolia.6,9 This demonstrates that multi-solvent, UAE-based extraction provides a more comprehensive phytochemical representation of the species compared to conventional approaches.

Collectively, the data confirm that extraction strategy directly determines both the depth and apparent composition of the phytochemical profile. UAE provides a broader and more representative chemical fingerprint of D. tenuifolia, and constitutes a more efficient and analytically robust approach for phytochemical profiling of Brassicaceae matrices.

Antioxidant activity

The chemically heterogeneous composition of D. tenuifolia extracts, encompassing polar flavonoids, hydroxycinnamic acids, and lipophilic fatty acids, necessitates a multi-assay antioxidant evaluation, as no single method captures the full spectrum of mechanistically distinct antioxidant pathways.33 Accordingly, six complementary assays were employed: DPPH and ABTS (hydrogen atom transfer/single electron transfer), CUPRAC and FRAP (reducing power), MCA (metal chelation), and PBD (total antioxidant capacity) (Table 4).

ABTS results followed a consistent trend, with MeOH-MAC exhibiting the highest activity (21.91 mg TE per g), confirming the dominant contribution of polar phenolics to radical scavenging capacity.

In particular, polyunsaturated fatty acids may participate in cupric ion reduction via electron-rich double bond systems, as previously suggested in lipid redox models, although this mechanism requires further experimental validation in D. tenuifolia extracts.34 FRAP activity was highest in MeOH-UAE (19.01 mg TE per g) and MeOH-MAC (17.37 mg TE per g), confirming the established role of polyphenolic hydroxyl groups in ferric ion reduction and aligning with previous findings for D. tenuifolia water extracts.6

Metal chelating activity (MCA) was most pronounced in EtOAc fractions, with EtOAc-UAE (22.14 mg EDTAE per g) and EtOAc-MAC (21.73 mg EDTAE per g) significantly exceeding MeOH and water extracts. This behavior is likely associated with the ability of free carboxylic groups in long-chain fatty acids to coordinate transition metal ions via ligand–metal interactions, independent of classical phenolic redox mechanisms. Comparable patterns have been reported in other Diplotaxis species,35 suggesting a conserved genus-level functional feature. PBD values were highest in EtOAc-UAE (2.12 mmol TE per g) and EtOAc-MAC (1.92 mmol TE per g), reflecting their combined reducing and chelating capacity, while water extracts showed the lowest values (0.78–0.86 mmol TE per g).31

Overall, the antioxidant profile of D. tenuifolia extracts demonstrates a clear mechanistic dichotomy between polar and non-polar fractions. MeOH-derived extracts predominantly mediate hydrogen atom transfer and ferric-reducing activity through flavonoid- and hydroxycinnamic acid-rich compositions, whereas EtOAc fractions contribute more strongly to metal chelation and cupric-reducing capacity via lipophilic constituents. This functional complementarity confirms that antioxidant activity in D. tenuifolia is inherently mechanism-dependent and cannot be adequately described by a single assay, underscoring the necessity of a multi-assay, multi-solvent approach for accurate evaluation of complex plant matrices.

In contrast, BChE inhibition was strongly shifted toward EtOAc fractions, with EtOAc-UAE showing the highest activity (3.97 mg GALAE per g), followed by EtOAc-MAC (2.78 mg GALAE per g), while MeOH-UAE and both water extracts showed no detectable inhibition. Tyrosinase inhibition represented the most consistent activity across active fractions, with MeOH-UAE showing the strongest effect (33.61 mg KAE per g), followed by MeOH-MAC (29.97 mg KAE per g), EtOAc-UAE (29.81 mg KAE per g), and EtOAc-MAC (26.85 mg KAE per g), while water extracts were inactive. The superior activity of MeOH fractions is attributable to hydroxycinnamic acids (ferulic, sinapic, p-coumaric acid) acting as copper-chelating or competitive substrates, together with isorhamnetin derivatives modulating melanogenesis pathways.38 Comparable tyrosinase inhibitory ranges (20–40 mg KAE per g) have been reported for Brassicaceae species rich in hydroxycinnamic acids.6 The absence of activity in water extracts, despite their expanded metabolite profiles under UAE, confirms that structural specificity rather than compound abundance governs tyrosinase inhibition.

α-Amylase inhibition was observed across all extracts, with EtOAc-UAE (0.89 mmol ACAE per g) and EtOAc-MAC (0.78 mmol ACAE per g) showing the highest activity, followed by MeOH fractions (0.42–0.45 mmol ACAE per g), while water extracts were weak (0.05 mmol ACAE per g). α-Glucosidase inhibition was more selective, with activity detected only in EtOAc-UAE (0.64 mmol ACAE per g) and negligible or absent in other fractions. The difference between EtOAc-UAE and EtOAc-MAC likely reflects the presence of flavonol glycosides (isorhamnetin-3-O-glucoside, kaempferol-3,4′-di-O-glucoside, isorhamnetin-3,7-O-diglucoside), detected only under UAE conditions, which may act synergistically with fatty acids in enzyme binding.11

The influence of extraction method was consistent across all enzymes. UAE enhanced AChE inhibition in both MeOH and water fractions (e.g., 0.46 vs. 0.17 mg GALAE per g in water extracts), mirroring the substantial increase in water-phase chemical diversity observed under UAE conditions. Similarly, EtOAc-UAE outperformed EtOAc-MAC in BChE inhibition and was the only fraction with detectable α-glucosidase activity, highlighting the role of ultrasound-assisted extraction in reshaping bioactive profiles rather than merely increasing yield.

All extracts showed lower inhibitory activity than reference standards (galantamine, kojic acid, acarbose), which is expected for crude plant matrices where synergistic and antagonistic interactions among constituents may modulate net bioactivity.6,39

Collectively, these results reveal a clear structure–activity relationship: polar MeOH fractions, rich in flavonoids and hydroxycinnamic acids, primarily target AChE and tyrosinase; EtOAc fractions, dominated by lipophilic constituents, preferentially inhibit BChE and carbohydrate-hydrolyzing enzymes; while water fractions contribute modest but consistent radical-associated activity. UAE emerges as a decisive factor in enhancing both chemical diversity and targeted bioactivity, particularly in non-polar and aqueous fractions, underscoring its role as a key green extraction strategy for maximizing functional phytochemical output from D. tenuifolia.

Antimicrobial activity

Given the global rise in antibiotic resistance, there is an increasing need to explore new antimicrobial agents from natural sources, including plants rich in bioactive compounds.40 In this context, the present study investigated the antimicrobial activity of D. tenuifolia extracts prepared using two extraction methods (UAE and MAC) and three solvents (EtOAc, MeOH, and water) against a panel of clinically relevant microorganisms.

Among all tested extracts, only the EtOAc-based extracts exhibited detectable antimicrobial activity, which was limited to B. subtilis, a Gram-positive bacterium. The inhibition zones were measured as 6.7 ± 0.6 mm and 8.7 ± 0.6 mm for the MAC- and UAE-derived extracts, respectively, indicating weak but reproducible antimicrobial activity. In contrast, no inhibition was observed for MeOH or water extracts against B. subtilis or any of the other tested microorganisms. The positive control levofloxacin produced a zone of 33.0 ± 1.0 mm, while DMSO showed no inhibitory effect (Fig. 1).

The selective activity observed against B. subtilis suggests that the EtOAc extracts exhibit a limited antimicrobial spectrum, potentially reflecting species-specific susceptibility to certain phytochemical constituents. However, this selective pattern cannot be attributed to the mere presence or absence of any single phytochemical, since multiple compound classes, including phenolic acids and flavonoids, were detected across all extracts. LC-MS/MS profiling indicated marked solvent-driven differences: whereas MeOH and water extracts predominantly contained highly polar flavonoid glycosides, EtOAc extracts prominently featured lipid-like constituents, especially long-chain fatty acids (e.g., linoleic, α-linolenic, oleic, stearic, and erucic acids), and also contained semi-polar phenolics and flavonol derivatives (mainly mono- or diglycosides). Consistent with these compositional differences, hydrophobic, lipid-like constituents, including long-chain fatty acids, may contribute to antibacterial activity by interacting with bacterial membranes, disrupting membrane organization and permeability, and thereby limiting growth.34

There are limited studies specifically evaluating the antimicrobial activity of D. tenuifolia against clinically relevant microorganisms. Moghaddam et al.15 reported moderate antimicrobial activity of methanolic extracts of D. tenuifolia against S. aureus, B. subtilis, and E. coli, although no compositional data were provided to contextualize these findings. In the present study, the lack of activity in MeOH extracts, despite their rich flavonoid glycoside content, underscores the importance of compound polarity and molecular form, rather than mere phytochemical diversity, in determining antimicrobial outcomes.

Studies on other Diplotaxis species further emphasize the role of lipophilic constituents. Hashem and Saleh41 demonstrated that volatile isothiocyanates and fatty acids isolated from D. harra exhibited antibacterial activity, while Benzekri et al.42 identified sulforaphane as the principal antibacterial compound in bioactivity-guided fractions of D. harra. Collectively, these findings suggest that antimicrobial effects within the Diplotaxis genus are often linked to semi-polar to hydrophobic (lipid-like) constituents, which may be preferentially recovered using solvents such as EtOAc.

Taken together, the present results suggest that the weak but selective antimicrobial activity of D. tenuifolia EtOAc extracts against B. subtilis is extraction-dependent and chemically plausible, likely reflecting differences in compound enrichment rather than the exclusive presence of unique metabolites. These findings highlight the critical role of solvent choice in shaping the antimicrobial potential of plant extracts and underscore the value of integrating phytochemical profiling with biological assays.

Inhibition of biofilm formation

Bacterial biofilms are structured microbial communities that adhere to surfaces and exhibit increased tolerance to antimicrobial agents, thereby contributing to persistent infections and treatment failure. For this reason, the identification of plant-derived compounds capable of interfering with biofilm formation has attracted considerable attention, particularly in clinically relevant species such as S. aureus.43 The effects on biofilm formation of D. tenuifolia extracts prepared using different solvents and extraction techniques were evaluated against S. aureus using the CV microtiter plate assay. Biofilm formation levels were calculated relative to the corresponding control group, which was set at 100% (Fig. 2). EtOAc-UAE reduced biofilm formation to 13.6% of the control (p < 0.05). Water extracts also decreased biofilm biomass (water-MAC: 47.3%; water-UAE: 58.2%; p < 0.05), while MeOH-UAE reduced biofilm formation to 80.5% (p < 0.05). In contrast, EtOAc-MAC and MeOH-MAC were associated with higher biofilm levels than their respective controls under the tested conditions. Overall, these findings indicate that the inhibition of biofilm formation depends on both solvent and extraction method. In line with the LC-MS/MS profiles, EtOAc extracts mainly contained semi-polar to hydrophobic, lipid-like constituents, whereas MeOH and water extracts mainly contained highly polar, often glycosylated metabolites. However, the divergent outcomes within the same solvent group (e.g., EtOAc-UAE vs. EtOAc-MAC; MeOH-UAE vs. MeOH-MAC) suggest that solvent class alone does not explain the observed effects.

The pronounced biofilm reduction observed with EtOAc-UAE, the most effective extract against biofilm formation, may be partly associated with the presence of phenolic acid and flavonol glycoside derivatives detected under UAE conditions, including dihydroxybenzoic acid-O-hexoside, kaempferol-3,4′-di-O-glucoside, and isorhamnetin glycosides, together with long-chain fatty acids. The less pronounced but significant inhibition observed for MeOH-UAE and water extracts may also be related to polar phenolics, flavonol glycosides, and indole/glucosinolate-related metabolites detected in these active fractions. Previous studies on related compound classes provide possible mechanistic explanations for the inhibition of S. aureus biofilm formation observed in the present study. For example, hydroxybenzoic acid-related derivatives have been reported to interfere with quorum-sensing regulation,44 whereas flavonol derivatives, including kaempferol-, quercetin-, and isorhamnetin-related compounds, have been shown to reduce early surface attachment, surface protein-mediated adhesion, adhesion-associated gene expression, and biofilm-associated transcriptional regulation.45,46 Long-chain fatty acids have also been reported to impair biofilm formation by reducing exopolysaccharide accumulation and altering cell-surface properties.47 In addition, indole derivatives and glucosinolate degradation products have been shown to disrupt membrane potential and energy metabolism, reduce ATP production, increase oxidative stress through effects on staphyloxanthin biosynthesis, and suppress adhesion-related genes and bacterial attachment to surfaces.48,49 Therefore, although the present study did not test isolated compounds, the biofilm-inhibitory activity of the active extracts may plausibly reflect the combined contribution of phenolic acid derivatives, flavonol glycosides, sulfur-containing metabolites, and lipid-like constituents detected in these extracts. On the other hand, EtOAc-MAC and MeOH-MAC yielded higher CV-stained biomass than the corresponding controls under the tested conditions, which may reflect condition-specific biofilm enhancement and/or matrix effects on the assay readout.

To date, limited information is available regarding the effects on biofilm formation of plant extracts from the Diplotaxis genus. However, inhibition of biofilm formation has been reported for several members of the Brassicaceae family. For example, Hu et al.50 reported that extracts from several cruciferous vegetables (including kale, radish, red cabbage, and mustard) reduced E. coli O157:H7 biofilm formation. In a follow-up study, extracts of Raphanus sativus var. longipinnatus L.H. Bailey, R. sativus L., and Brassica oleracea L. var. acephala were also shown to inhibit E. coli O157:H7 biofilm formation on abiotic surfaces.51 In addition, plant-derived products from Eruca sativa Mill. and Camelina sativa L. Crantz have demonstrated inhibition of biofilm formation against different bacterial species.52,53 Collectively, these reports support Brassicaceae as a relevant source of antibiofilm agents, potentially linked to polyphenolic constituents and sulfur-containing metabolites characteristic of this family (e.g., glucosinolates).

Taken together, these findings indicate that extraction-dependent differences in chemical composition may influence the ability of D. tenuifolia extracts to inhibit S. aureus biofilm formation. Further studies using isolated compounds are needed to identify the constituents responsible for this activity and to clarify their mechanisms of action.

Cytotoxic activity

Cancer continues to be a significant global health burden, prompting ongoing efforts to identify novel therapeutic agents with enhanced safety and efficacy.54 In recent years, edible plants have attracted increasing scientific attention as potential sources of bioactive compounds with possible cytotoxic effects on cancer cell viability.33 Although several studies have investigated the cytotoxic potential of D. tenuifolia, comprehensive evaluations across extraction strategies and cancer cell models remain limited. Here, the cytotoxic activity of six D. tenuifolia extracts prepared by UAE or MAC using EtOAc, MeOH, or water was assessed in MDA-MB-231 and PC-3 cells via the MTT assay following 48 h exposure (62.5–1000 µg mL−1).

Among all tested extracts, the EtOAc-UAE extract exhibited the greatest reduction in cell viability in both cell lines. In MDA-MB-231 cells, viability decreased from ∼91.2% at 125 µg mL−1 to ∼5.8% at 1000 µg mL−1, with a similar decline in PC-3 cells (∼88.9% to ∼6.8% across the same range). EtOAc-MAC also caused a concentration-dependent reduction in viability, reducing viability from ∼87.2% at 250 µg mL−1 to ∼2.0% at 1000 µg mL−1 in MDA-MB-231 cells, and from ∼85.0% at 125 µg mL−1 to ∼3.8% at 1000 µg mL−1 in PC-3 cells (Fig. 3 and 4). In contrast, MeOH and water extracts generally maintained viability above 85% in both models; the main exception was MeOH-MAC, which reduced viability to ∼63.6% (MDA-MB-231) and ∼57.9% (PC-3) at 1000 µg mL−1 (Fig. 3 and 4).

IC50 estimates further supported the cytotoxic potential of EtOAc extracts: 306.3 µg mL−1 (EtOAc-UAE) and 526.4 µg mL−1 (EtOAc-MAC) in MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 5a and b), and 389.6 µg mL−1 and 403.0 µg mL−1 in PC-3 cells, respectively (Fig. 6a and b). At these concentrations, microscopy revealed shrinkage, rounding, and partial detachment in both cell lines (Fig. 5c, d and 6c, d). By comparison, MeOH and water extracts showed IC50 values > 1000 µg mL−1 and no notable morphological alterations under the tested conditions. According to the National Cancer Institute (NCI) criteria, crude plant extracts are generally considered cytotoxically active when their IC50 values are below 20–30 µg mL−1.55 Therefore, although the EtOAc extracts were the most active in the present study, their IC50 values remain well above the threshold generally associated with marked cytotoxic activity.

The relatively greater reduction in cell viability observed for the EtOAc extracts may be related to their enrichment in semi-polar and lipophilic constituents. In particular, these extracts contained long-chain fatty acids, including linoleic, α-linolenic, oleic, stearic, and erucic acids, accompanied by bioactive phenolic/flavonol-type metabolites such as ferulic acid, p-coumaric acid, and isorhamnetin- or kaempferol-related derivatives. In contrast, MeOH and water extracts were characterized mainly by highly polar metabolites, including glycosylated phenolics, flavonol glycosides, and organic acids. This polarity difference may partly explain why the EtOAc extracts showed greater effects on cell viability, as these long-chain fatty acids can interact more readily with cell membranes and affect membrane integrity and mitochondrial function, thereby reducing cell viability.56,57 Furthermore, these lipophilic components may have supported the cytotoxic contribution of other bioactive constituents present in the EtOAc extracts, including phenolic and flavonol-type compounds.

Previous studies indicate that the cytotoxic potential of D. tenuifolia depends on both the extraction strategy and the cancer cell model. Durazzo et al.12 reported growth inhibition in Caco-2 cells for a polyphenol-rich leaf extract associated with G1-phase arrest, although IC50 values were not provided. Using hydroalcoholic leaf extracts, Marrelli et al.13 observed no inhibition in MCF-7 cells and only limited effects in LoVo (∼40%) and HepG2 (∼10%), again without IC50 estimates. Ramos-Bueno et al.14 described selective cytotoxicity of a chloroform–MeOH extract toward HT-29 cells (IC50 ∼200 µg mL−1) with minimal impact on CCD-18 normal colon fibroblasts, whereas a water–ethanol extract was insufficiently active to derive an IC50. Radziejewska-Kubzdela et al.16 reported mg mL−1-range IC50 values for wild rocket leaf juices in Caco-2 and HepG2 cells, with minimal toxicity in IEC-6 normal intestinal cells, and identified phenolics (e.g., quercetin, hydroxycinnamic acids) together with glucosinolates as major constituent classes. Overall, these studies suggest that D. tenuifolia may have extract- and model-dependent cytotoxic effects.

Based on the lowest IC50 value among the tested extracts, EtOAc-UAE was selected for RT-qPCR profiling of apoptosis- and autophagy-related markers in MDA-MB-231 cells at the IC50 concentration. After 48 h of exposure, the mRNA levels of bax, beclin-1, and LC3B were evaluated (Fig. 7). Bax is a pro-apoptotic regulator involved in mitochondrial membrane permeabilization, beclin-1 plays a central role in autophagy initiation, and LC3B is an autophagy-related marker associated with autophagosome formation.58 In this study, bax was upregulated approximately 2-fold, while LC3B mRNA expression increased nearly 3-fold. On the other hand, beclin-1 expression remained largely unchanged compared to the control. This pattern is consistent with the morphological changes observed at IC50 exposure and supports the possible involvement of regulated cell-death-associated responses under the tested conditions.

Among the compounds detected in the EtOAc-UAE extract, isorhamnetin- and kaempferol-related flavonol glycosides may be particularly relevant to the observed upregulation of bax. Isorhamnetin has been reported to promote apoptosis in MDA-MB-231 cells by increasing bax and caspase levels, reducing Bcl-2 expression, and triggering mitochondrial cytochrome c release. Similarly, kaempferol has been shown to induce programmed cell death in MDA-MB-231 cells through activation of caspase-dependent apoptotic pathways.59 Phenolic acids detected in the extract, including ferulic acid and p-coumaric acid, have also been associated with lipid accumulation and excessive autophagy-mediated cell death in MDA-MB-231 cells.60 Saturated fatty acids detected in EtOAc-UAE may contribute to mitochondrial apoptosis-related responses by reducing cardiolipin levels and mitochondrial membrane potential, thereby promoting cytochrome c release and caspase-3 activation in MDA-MB-231 cells.61 Taken together, the concomitant upregulation of bax and LC3B may be linked to the presence of flavonol glycosides, phenolic acids, and long-chain fatty acids detected in EtOAc-UAE.

Overall, these findings suggest that EtOAc-UAE induces a detectable cytotoxic response in MDA-MB-231 cells, supported by dose-dependent viability loss and induction of bax and LC3B. Mechanistic clarification will require protein-level validation and pathway-resolving assays (e.g., Bax/Bcl-2 balance, caspase activation). A limitation of the present study is the lack of non-cancerous cell lines, which precludes assessment of selective cytotoxicity; future studies should incorporate normal cell models to more robustly define the safety profile.

Conclusion

This study provides the first systematic multiparametric evaluation of D. tenuifolia extracts obtained using two extraction methods and three solvents, integrating LC-MS/MS phytochemical profiling with comprehensive biological activity assessment. The results establish a coherent structure–activity framework driven by solvent polarity and extraction approach. MeOH fractions, enriched in flavonol glycosides and hydroxycinnamic acids, were consistently associated with radical scavenging, AChE inhibition, and tyrosinase inhibition, whereas EtOAc fractions, dominated by long-chain fatty acids with co-extracted phenolics, displayed a distinct bioactivity profile characterized by selective BChE inhibition, enhanced reducing and metal-chelating capacity, and selective antimicrobial and antibiofilm effects, together with measurable cytotoxic activity. RT-qPCR analysis further showed upregulation of bax and LC3B in MDA-MB-231 cells, suggesting possible involvement of apoptosis- and autophagy-related responses; however, these transcript-level findings require further validation. Water fractions, despite lower compositional complexity, retained notable radical scavenging capacity. Ultrasound-assisted extraction consistently expanded phytochemical diversity and enhanced bioactivity, particularly in water and EtOAc fractions, demonstrating that UAE qualitatively reshapes extract composition rather than merely increasing yield. Notably, this study provides the first detailed LC-MS/MS characterization of fatty acid profiles in EtOAc fractions of D. tenuifolia and suggests their possible contribution to enzyme inhibition and cytotoxic responses. Collectively, these findings position D. tenuifolia as a chemically versatile and biologically multifunctional species and establish a rational basis for tailoring extraction strategies toward specific bioactive fractions, supporting further investigation of their biological relevance and application potential. Future studies should focus on the isolation of active constituents, validation of apoptosis- and autophagy-related pathways at the protein and functional levels, and assessment of bioavailability, toxicity, and efficacy in relevant in vivo models. Such studies will be beneficial for clarifying the translational potential of selected D. tenuifolia fractions for nutraceutical, functional food, or pharmacological applications.
