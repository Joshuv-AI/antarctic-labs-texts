Severe thrombocytopenia in antiphospholipid syndrome: a retrospective study of 432 patients
Authors: Carine Schmidt, Matthias Papo, Dov Taïeb, Raphaël Lhote, Miguel Hié, Micheline Pha, Julien Haroche, Fleur Cohen-Aubart, Pierre-Louis Cariou, Nassim Ait Abdallah, Alexis Mathian, Quentin Moyon, Zahir Amoura

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAntiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune disease characterised by obstetric morbidity and recurrent venous and/or arterial thrombosis. It is frequently associated with systemic lupus erythematosus, and thrombocytopenia is a common manifestation. This study aimed to compare patients with APS, with and without thrombocytopenia, and to describe management and outcomes of severe thrombocytopenia.MethodsWe performed a retrospective single-centre study that included 432 patients with APS. Patients were divided into three groups according to the platelet count nadir: severe thrombocytopenia (< 50 G/L), moderate thrombocytopenia (between 50 and 130 G/L) and no thrombocytopenia (≥ 130 G/L).Results142 patients developed thrombocytopenia during follow-up (32.9%): 57 (13%) had severe thrombocytopenia (< 50 G/L) and 85 (19.4%) had moderate thrombocytopenia (50–130 G/L). Patients with thrombocytopenia more frequently experienced thrombotic manifestations, including lower limb deep vein thrombosis (49% vs 37%, p=0.014), coronary thrombosis (15% vs 6.6%, p=0.006) and catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS) (27% vs 2.1%, p<0.001). Thrombocytopenia was more frequently associated with pre-eclampsia (16.8% vs 3.7%, p<0.001) and other APS manifestations, including skin (ulcer 4.9% vs 1.4%, p=0.046, cutaneous necrosis 7% vs 0.7%, p<0.001), cardiovascular (MINOCA 7% vs 0.7%, p<0.001, valvular disease 19.7% vs 9.3%, p=0.002), renal (18.3% vs 3.1%, p<0.001) and pulmonary (5.6% vs 1%, p=0.007) manifestations. Aetiologies of severe thrombocytopenia were mainly immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) and CAPS. In patients with severe thrombocytopenia, treatment followed the approach used for primary ITP, and anticoagulation was generally maintained even in the presence of bleeding episodes. Overall survival was poorer in patients with severe thrombocytopenia (p<0.0001).ConclusionOur results suggest that thrombocytopenia is associated with a more severe APS phenotype and increased mortality.Keywords: Antiphospholipid Syndrome, Thrombocytopenia, Mortality

Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune disease characterised by obstetric morbidity and recurrent thrombotic events, frequently associated with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Thrombocytopenia is relatively common in APS with a prevalence ranging from 16% to 53%, depending on the platelet threshold used and the population studied.1 2 It is usually moderate, between 50 and 130 G/L, although some patients experience severe thrombocytopenia.3 4 The pathophysiology of thrombocytopenia in APS remains unclear. Studies comparing patients with APS, with and without thrombocytopenia, have shown that thrombocytopenia is associated with more severe disease and appears to be a thrombotic and obstetric risk factor.5,9 Several hypotheses have been suggested to explain thrombocytopenia in APS,4 including peripheral platelet destruction secondary to antiphospholipid antibodies, activation-induced platelet destruction and immune-mediated platelet destruction.

There are no guidelines for anticoagulation management in patients with severe thrombocytopenia. A recent review10 summarises the debate on the need for anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapy in thrombocytopenia associated with APS. The debate remains open, but previous data suggest that discontinuation of curative anticoagulation could be more deleterious than its continuation in patients with severe thrombocytopenia.

Taken together, the evidence presented so far suggests that patients with APS and thrombocytopenia may have a higher thrombotic risk compared with patients without thrombocytopenia. However, these data are based on a few studies with a small number of patients and therefore do not allow definite conclusions.

This study aimed to compare patients with APS, with and without thrombocytopenia, and to describe management and outcomes of severe thrombocytopenia.

Methods

Patients

We performed a retrospective, observational and single-centre study on a population of patients with primary or secondary APS diagnosed between January 1970 and September 2023. This study included a convenience sample of patients with APS from our referral centre, with data collected from existing medical records; no systematic screening or consecutive inclusion was performed. Patients were classified according to the 2023 American College of Rheumatology/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology APS criteria. Patients with missing platelet count during follow-up were excluded.

A total of 432 patients with APS were included. Patients were divided into three groups according to the platelet count nadir: severe thrombocytopenia (<50 G/L), moderate thrombocytopenia (between 50 and 130 G/L) and no thrombocytopenia (≥130 G/L). Clinical and biological parameters, as well as outcomes, were compared between these groups.

Severe haemorrhagic episodes were defined as a Khellaf score greater than 811 or life-threatening events. For patients with severe thrombocytopenia, complete remission was defined as a platelet count greater than 100 G/L and partial remission as a platelet count between 50 and 100 G/L. Relapse was defined as a new episode of severe thrombocytopenia following partial or complete remission.

Data collection

All data were retrieved retrospectively from the computerised patient record system, the biology software and paper records from archives for older cases.

Collected data included: demographic characteristics; cardiovascular risk factors; associated autoimmune and coagulation disorders; clinical manifestations of APS; antiphospholipid antibodies and other autoantibodies; and therapeutic history.

For patients with severe thrombocytopenia, additional data were collected on: context and timing of first severe thrombocytopenia episode; treatments at that time; bleeding and thrombotic complications; treatment of severe thrombocytopenia; need for interventional treatment; management of antithrombotic treatments; remission and relapse; laboratory tests including complement levels, anti-platelet antibodies, anti-PF4 antibodies, von Willebrand factor activity and bone marrow aspiration if available.

Statistical analyses

Patient characteristics were described as percentages for categorical variables, and as medians and IQRs for continuous variables. Comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon, χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. Overall survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and survival curves were compared using the log-rank test. A p value ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (R project for Statistical Computing).

Results

Study population

138 patients with primary or secondary APS were followed up between January 1970 and September 2023. Six patients were excluded due to missing data on platelet count. Among the 432 patients included, 142 patients had thrombocytopenia (platelet count nadir<130 G/L) (32.9%) and 290 patients had no thrombocytopenia. 57 patients were classified as having severe thrombocytopenia (< 50 G/L) and 85 as moderate thrombocytopenia (50–130 G/L) (online supplemental figure 1 and figure 1).

Thrombocytopenia was more frequent in patients with associated SLE (45% vs 25.9%, p<0.001) and hypertension (33.1% vs 22.8%, p=0.022).

There was an increased frequency of lower limb deep vein thrombosis (49.3% vs 36.9%, p=0.014), coronary thrombosis (14.8% vs 6.6%, p=0.006) and CAPS (26.8% vs 2.1%, p<0.001) in patients with thrombocytopenia. The proportion of patients with pre-eclampsia was higher among patients with thrombocytopenia (16.8% vs 3.7%, p<0.001).

Among non-criteria APS manifestations, skin (including skin necrosis, 7% vs 0.7%, p<0.001) and cardiac manifestations (including valvular disease, 19.7% vs 9.3%, p=0.002), APS nephropathy (18.3% vs 3.1%, p<0.001) and diffuse alveolar damage (5.6% vs 1%, p=0.007) occurred more frequently in patients with thrombocytopenia.

Triple antiphospholipid antibody positivity was more frequent in patients with thrombocytopenia (43% vs 21%, p<0.001).

During follow-up, patients with thrombocytopenia were more frequently treated with vitamin K antagonists (9.9% vs 76.9%, p<0.001), corticosteroids (54.9% vs 21.4%, p<0.001) and intravenous immunoglobulins (17.6% vs 0.7%, p<0.001).

Comparison of severe versus moderate thrombocytopenia in patients with APS

Compared with patients with moderate thrombocytopenia, those with severe thrombocytopenia were significantly younger at diagnosis (26 vs 36 years, p=0.005) and more frequently women (91.2% vs 71.8%, p=0.005). There was no difference in the proportion of primary APS between the two groups (51.8% vs 54.4% in severe thrombocytopenia, p=0.76). Platelet count nadir was 25 G/L (IQR 15–36) in severe thrombocytopenia and 96 G/L (IQR 76–116) in moderate thrombocytopenia. Comparison of patients’ characteristics with severe or moderate thrombocytopenia is presented in online supplemental table 1.

There was no difference in the frequency of venous or arterial thrombosis between the two groups. CAPS was more frequent in patients with severe thrombocytopenia (43.9% vs 15.3%, p<0.001). Among obstetric events, pre-eclampsia was significantly more frequent in this group (25% vs 9.8%, p=0.032). There was no difference regarding non-criteria APS manifestations between the two groups.

No significant difference was observed in anticoagulant treatment between the two groups. Corticosteroids (75.4% vs 41.2%, p<0.001), intravenous immunoglobulins (40% vs 3.6%, p<0.001) and rituximab (19.6% vs 0%, p<0.001) were more frequently used in patients with severe thrombocytopenia.

Survival outcomes according to platelet count nadir

Overall survival differed significantly between the three groups of patients (severe thrombocytopenia, moderate thrombocytopenia and absence of thrombocytopenia) (p<0.0001, figure 2). Among patients with thrombocytopenia, overall survival from the date of platelet count nadir was significantly lower in those with severe thrombocytopenia (p=0.001, online supplemental figure 1).

Main causes of severe thrombocytopenia in patients with APS

Apart from unknown causes, most thrombocytopenia events were related to CAPS, followed by immune-mediated causes or HELLP (Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelet count) syndrome.

In patients with severe thrombocytopenia, the cause was unknown in only one case. Most of these patients experienced immune thrombocytopenia (38.6%), CAPS (31.6%) or HELLP syndrome (8.8%) (figure 3).

Among patients with severe thrombocytopenia, clinical and biological characteristics and aetiologies did not differ between primary and secondary APS (online supplemental table 2).

Complications of severe thrombocytopenia were predominantly thrombotic, with 50.9% of thrombotic episodes and 34.1% of severe haemorrhagic episodes. Fewer than 11% of patients died following the first episode of severe thrombocytopenia, and 42.2% experienced at least one relapse.

Among patients receiving anticoagulant therapy (39 of 57), four required temporary treatment discontinuation (three patients due to bleeding complications and one for other clinical reasons), while three other patients were switched to a prophylactic dose or to monotherapy (if previously on combined therapy with antiplatelet agents), also due to bleeding. For most patients (82.1%), therapeutic-dose anticoagulation was maintained. Detailed treatment data are provided in online supplemental tables 1, 3 and 4.

Description of severe thrombocytopenia in patients with secondary immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP)

Twenty-two patients were diagnosed with immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) at the time of their first episode of severe thrombocytopenia (online supplemental table 3). ITP was defined based on clinical evaluation and exclusion of alternative causes. The prevalence of ITP was similar in primary APS and in secondary APS associated with SLE (32% vs 46%, respectively; p=0.28; online supplemental table 2). 86.4% of patients with ITP were women, and more than half had associated SLE. Median age at the first episode of severe thrombocytopenia was 28.5 years (IQR 19.5–50) and 24.5 years at diagnosis of APS (IQR 19.5–36.5). Median platelet count nadir was 21 G/L (IQR 9–30), and half of the patients had triple antiphospholipid antibody positivity. Anti-platelet antibody testing was performed in six patients, all positive. Complement consumption (low C3 and/or C4) was observed in seven out of nine patients. Bone marrow aspiration was performed in 10 patients, confirming a peripheral origin in all cases.

Approximately 67% of patients were already receiving anticoagulation, three of whom were also on antiplatelet therapy. Two patients were receiving corticosteroids, and four were on immunosuppressants.

Thrombotic complications occurred in two patients, while three had severe and six had moderate haemorrhagic complications.

Among patients on anticoagulant therapy (14 cases), treatment was maintained in nine, temporarily interrupted in one and unknown for four.

Over 90% of patients received corticosteroids and just under half intravenous immunoglobulins; immunosuppressants were used in 40.9% of cases, always in combination with corticosteroids.

One patient died during the first episode due to cardiogenic shock. Complete remission was achieved in most patients; however, 83.3% relapsed, usually due to ITP, and five patients developed CAPS during follow-up.

Description of severe thrombocytopenia in patients with CAPS

18 of the 57 patients with severe thrombocytopenia were diagnosed with CAPS (online supplemental table 4). Most patients were female, and 50% had associated SLE, similarly to patients with ITP. The prevalence of CAPS was similar in primary APS and in secondary APS associated with SLE (29% vs 34.6%, respectively; p=0.65; online supplemental table 2). Median age at diagnosis of APS was 35.5 years (IQR 23–47), and median platelet nadir was 23 G/L (IQR 16–38). Six patients presented with inaugural CAPS leading to APS diagnosis. Low C3 and/or C4 levels were observed in 6 out of 10 patients, and anti-platelet antibody testing was investigated in only one patient and was positive.

At the time of the initial episode of severe thrombocytopenia, 12 patients were already receiving anticoagulation. 22% and one-third of patients were already treated with corticosteroids and immunosuppressants, respectively.

Most patients had a definite diagnosis of CAPS, although four were considered pre-CAPS with fewer than three organ involvements. Severe bleeding complications occurred in almost half of the patients. Thrombotic complications were observed in all patients, predominantly of microcirculatory origin (55.6%), but could affect multiple vascular beds simultaneously. Anticoagulant therapy, alone or in combination with antiplatelet agents, was introduced in four patients; it was temporarily interrupted in two due to severe bleeding and reduced in two others. Interventional management of bleeding was performed in four patients.

83% of patients received corticosteroids, two-thirds intravenous immunoglobulins and 44.4% immunosuppressants. Three patients required no treatment beyond anticoagulant therapy.

Three patients (16.7%) died during their first episode: two from multiorgan failure and one from cardiac arrest after haemodialysis. All other patients achieved complete remission. Severe thrombocytopenia relapsed in four patients, including three with CAPS and one with a solid tumour.

Discussion

Our results suggest that thrombocytopenia is associated with a more severe APS phenotype and increased mortality.

The proportion of thrombocytopenia in our study (32.9%) is in line with previous studies (16–53%).1 2 To our knowledge, this is the first study to report a detailed description of patients with APS and severe thrombocytopenia during follow-up. Although our study is based on a convenience sample from our referral centre, it provides insight into severe APS cases. The very long-term follow-up of some patients (median 11.3 years, IQR 6.4–17.4 years, maximum 52.8 years) includes diagnoses made before routine antiphospholipid antibody testing, potentially introducing classification bias.

Patients with APS and thrombocytopenia had specific characteristics. The significant association with arterial hypertension is consistent with the literature.6 We also observed an increased frequency of lower limb deep vein thrombosis and coronary thrombosis. Several studies have reported a similar trend with an increased risk of arterial or venous thrombosis.5,7

Between 5.8% and 16.5% of women experienced at least one obstetric complication, consistent with a population-based study,12 reporting 15% of obstetric complications. The cumulative incidence of obstetric morbidity was also higher in patients with thrombocytopenia as reported by Shi et al.6

Non-criteria APS characteristics occurred more frequently in patients with thrombocytopenia. In particular, the association with cutaneous manifestations has been previously reported,8 while valvular manifestations were consistent with previous studies.8 9 Unlike other studies,5 8 9 we did not observe an association with neurological manifestations.

Review of treatments during follow-up revealed an expected association between thrombocytopenia and the use of corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulins and some immunosuppressants.5 6 Regarding anticoagulation, our results were similar to those of Shi et al and Pardos-Gea et al6 7 but differed from those of Gamal et al,5 who reported no association with thrombocytopenia. In patients with severe thrombocytopenia, the treatment strategy followed the approach used for primary ITP, with a high relapse rate after the first episode (83.3%), similar to primary ITP, in which approximately two-thirds of cases progress to chronicity.13 14 Clinicians primarily decided to maintain anticoagulant therapy, even in the presence of bleeding episodes.

Thrombocytopenia, in particular below 50 G/L, is associated with a more severe APS profile and increased mortality, which is consistent with previous studies describing poorer prognosis and higher thrombotic risk.5 7 Given the very long-term follow-up in our cohort, survivorship bias may have led to underestimation of complications rate.

Studies focusing on severe thrombocytopenia in APS are rare. In the study by Shi et al,6 no significant differences were observed between moderate and severe thrombocytopenia regarding APS manifestations. Pardos-Gea et al7 reported increased mortality in patients with persistent low-to-moderate thrombocytopenia, as opposed to acute and severe thrombocytopenia, contrasting with our results. Their analysis was based on 29 patients with thrombocytopenia, and the proportion of severe or low-to-moderate thrombocytopenia was not specified.

Our data did not reveal clear differences in the characteristics of severe thrombocytopenia between primary and secondary APS. ITP was the leading cause of severe thrombocytopenia, followed by CAPS. Distinguishing primary from secondary ITP in APS is challenging, particularly in patients with associated SLE. Our findings suggest that ITP occurrence was not significantly influenced by the presence of SLE. However, the absence of a systematic assessment of SLE activity remains a potential residual confounder.

Incidence of CAPS in APS is estimated at 1–7%.2 15 We observed a higher frequency (10.2%) in our cohort. The high prevalence of CAPS in our cohort may be explained by referral bias related to the tertiary nature of our centre, as well as by the fact that CAPS was primarily assessed in patients with severe thrombocytopenia. CAPS was more frequent in patients with thrombocytopenia (26.8%), particularly in those with severe thrombocytopenia (43.9%). Patients with thrombocytopenia more frequently had associated SLE (45%), a higher rate than previously reported by Krause et al (34%).8

We observed that 14.3% of patients with ITPs and severe thrombocytopenia experienced severe non-cerebral haemorrhage, compared with 9.6% of primary ITPs in a 2015 systematic review.16 No cerebral haemorrhage was reported in our cohort, compared with 1.4% in the same review. A recent study comparing ITPs with or without antiphospholipid antibodies17 found no association between antibody positivity and bleeding events. Since patients with ITP are not on anticoagulant therapy, this suggests that haemorrhagic complications in patients with APS are mainly related to anticoagulation rather than antiphospholipid antibodies. However, thrombotic risk was increased in ITP with antiphospholipid antibodies. Indeed, we observed 9.5% of thrombotic complications in our group during the first episode of severe thrombocytopenia.

17% of CAPS with severe thrombocytopenia died during the acute episode, a lower proportion than in the literature, which can reach 56%.18,20 However, 22.2% of these patients were classified as pre-CAPS and therefore had less severe disease.

The development of a clinical prediction score including thrombocytopenia could improve the prediction of high-risk patients. Existing thrombotic risk scores, such as the antiphospholipid score21 and the Global Anti-Phospholipid Syndrome Score, which combines clinical (hyperlipidaemia, hypertension) and biological (aCL, anti-B2GPI, LA, anti- phosphatidylserine/prothrombin antibodies) data,22 23 do not include thrombocytopenia. Their use is limited, notably because some tests are not routinely performed.

In conclusion, thrombocytopenia is a common manifestation of APS, with unknown pathophysiology and multiple aetiologies, and appears to be an essential factor in the thrombotic risk assessment and the prognosis of patients with APS. Further large-scale prospective studies are needed to confirm these results.
