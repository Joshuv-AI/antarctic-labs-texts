Lived experiences, health-seeking behaviour, quality of life, and self-esteem among transgender women: a mixed methods study from the Northern Himalayan region
Authors: Aanchal Thapa, Rajesh Kumar, Rajarajeswari Kuppuswamy, Mukesh Bairwa, Anindya Das
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTransgender people are more likely to experience heightened risks of physical and psychological distress and tend to report a poorer quality of life compared to the general population. This study aimed to determine the quality of life and self-esteem, health-seeking behaviour, and to explore the lived experiences among transgender women.MethodsUsing the snowball sampling technique, 73 transgender women were included in this mixed-methods study. Quantitative data were collected through the Short-Form-12 (SF-12) and the Rosenberg Self-esteem Scale (RSES). Maximum variation sampling was employed to select 10 participants for the qualitative component, aiming to gain in-depth insights into their personal experiences and health-seeking behaviours. Appropriate descriptive and inferential statistics were applied for analysis. A thematic analysis approach was used to represent qualitative findings.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 30.93 (SD = 4.70) years. Findings revealed that participants had poor mental (Mean µ = 38.80) and physical (Mean µ = 38.08) health. The cohort demonstrated a notably high level of self-esteem (Mean µ = 30.93 ± 4.70). A substantial proportion (76.7%) of participants experienced discrimination or mistreatment while seeking healthcare services, stating unfavorable experiences (19.2%) and strongly unsatisfied (5.5%), whereas more than half (56.2%) avoided giving any opinions. From the qualitative analysis, three major themes emerge: discovering my true identity as transgender person, survival in a world of discrimination, violence, and trauma, and struggle with economic, professional, and legal challenges.ConclusionsIn our study, participants reported lower mental and physical health, experienced discrimination, and had negative perceptions while accessing health services. The study carries important implications for the development of inclusive and high-quality healthcare services to ensure transgender people’s access to and utilization of healthcare.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25045-5.Keywords: Transgender, Self-esteem, Health-seeking behaviour, Quality of life

Introduction

Transgender people are increasing in different settings, including hospitals, society, and around the globe [1]. The term trans (or transgender) refers to people whose gender identity does not align with their sex at birth based on their sexual characteristics [2]. This umbrella term consists of a range of gender-variant individuals, including Kinnars, Hijra, Kothi, Dhurani, Janana, Aravani, Jogatas, and transgender or third gender [3]. Hijra is an Urdu term referring to individuals who are biologically male but adopt a feminine gender role, often engaging in traditional cultural practices such as dancing at weddings and offering blessings during childbirth [4, 5]. It is important to note that while Hijras fall under the broader transgender umbrella, not all transgender individuals identify as Hijra. The term Kothi is used to describe socioeconomically marginalized, gender-variant males who express femininity and are typically same-sex desiring [6]. Region-specific identities include Janana, commonly used in Hyderabad and other parts of South India, and Dhurani, a colloquial term prevalent in Kolkata and cities of Eastern India to describe gender-diverse individuals, often those engaged in sex work [6]. In South India, Aravani is a common term for transgender women, while Jogatas refer to gender-diverse individuals who serve as ritual performers or devotees, particularly in Maharashtra and Karnataka [7–9].

In a recent update, transgender individuals, including the ‘Hijra’ community in India, are recognised as having a history spanning over 4,000 years. According to the 2011 Census, their population was recorded at over 4.8 million [10] in India. As far as the global population is concerned, the country’s statistics for the number of trans people can range anywhere from 0.6 to 3% [11, 12]. However, the exact size of the transgender community is unknown and perhaps unknowable [8]. Indeed, despite being a huge share of the population, the majority of transgender people are invisible [10] and not getting the due incentives and benefits from various government and non-governmental organisations and other welfare schemes, including education scholarship, reservation in education and job, health insurance, legal aid, inclusion in government documents, housing, and rehabilitation schemes [8, 13, 14].

Uttarakhand, a northern Indian state, encompasses a total area of 53,483 square kilometres with a population of 12.45 million. As per the 2011 Census of India, Uttarakhand reported a population of 4,555 transgender individuals [15]. Transgender individuals in Uttarakhand continue to navigate a complex interplay of socio-cultural marginalization and evolving legal protections. Traditionally confined to occupations such as Badhai (ritualistic performances), many transgender persons rely on these roles for socioeconomic survival. However, a small segment has begun to challenge these conventional norms by pursuing formal education and alternative livelihoods. Uttarakhand became the 13th Indian state to establish a Transgender Welfare Board in 2013, signalling early policy recognition [16]. Further progress was marked in August 2024, when the state amended school regulations in alignment with the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019, thereby facilitating name and gender changes in educational records [17]. In addition, schemes such as the Pandit Deendayal Samajik Sahayata Suraksha Yojana have been introduced to support entrepreneurship among transgender individuals [18]. Despite these advancements, critical gaps remain, particularly in the implementation of inclusive civil frameworks such as the Uniform Civil Code (UCC), and ongoing concerns around safety and discrimination persist [19].

Further, the social response to transgender people is not very receptive and remains discriminatory and prejudiced due to their behaviour and characteristics are viewed as different from or inferior to societal norms [20]. Transgender people often face societal stigma, discrimination, and violence, which are direct consequences of societal gender norms [21]. Growing evidence suggests a paramount social stigma, discrimination, education deprivation, healthcare access, and other employment opportunities [8]. Indeed, transgender people may have higher chances of discrimination from birth by their family members, at the workplace, in homeless shelters, by healthcare providers, before judges, by landlords, and even by law enforcement personnel [8, 9, 22].

Earlier research proved that discriminatory experiences have serious implications for psychological health and are linked to poor access to healthcare services, and ultimately reduce the healthcare quality of the population [8, 9, 22]. Transgender people often experience a high level of victimization [23], social stigma, and internalized stigma [24, 25]. The lack of social recognition and social exclusion adversely affect their self-esteem [26], resulting in many psychological issues, i.e., psychological distress, anxiety, depression, low resilience, and other psychiatric conditions [22, 27–29]. The transgender community is also vulnerable to many physical problems, i.e., high risk for contracting sexually transmitted diseases (STDs) and Human Immunodeficiency Virus/Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (HIV/AIDS) [30], asthma, autism spectrum disorder, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hepatitis [31], substance abuse [32] and self-medication practices [33, 34]. Unfortunately, such self-medication practices result in kidney failure, cardiovascular diseases, and overall poor quality of life [33].

Despite these alarming health consequences, transgender people show a conservative attitude toward seeking healthcare [35]. Studies have shown that fear of discrimination by healthcare professionals contributes to poor access to health services among transgender individuals, adversely affecting their overall quality of life (QoL) [36]. Negative or prejudiced perceptions of healthcare providers will raise discomfort and develop negative attitudes toward medical professionals and, ultimately, discourage the vulnerable population from seeking healthcare [8, 37]. This negative perception hinders the efforts of policymakers to develop culturally sensitive resources for transgender people and tailored intervention training programmes for healthcare professionals. It has been reported that around 20% of transgender people experienced mistreatment and discriminatory behaviour [38] and an unwelcoming approach by the healthcare providers [39].

Although the body of research on experiences, quality of life, health-seeking behaviour, and self-esteem is limited in this area. Addressing this gap, the current study aimed to explore the lived experiences and health-seeking behaviour among transgender people in Northern Himalaya, Uttarakhand. Additionally, it sought to describe the relationship between self-esteem and quality of life and to examine the association of social determinants with the quality of life of the participants.

Materials and methods

Design, Setting, and study population

This sequential mixed methods study was conducted in the Northern Himalayas of Uttarakhand between November 24 to January 25, 2025. The strengths of sequential mixed methods for exploring complex social phenomena, particularly among marginalized populations, are well established in the literature [40, 41]. This approach combines the complementary strengths of quantitative and qualitative data, allowing for a more comprehensive understanding of the multifaceted and nuanced lived experiences of transgender individuals. The purposive selection of this design is grounded in robust theoretical, methodological, and applied evidence supporting its effectiveness in health and social research focused on transgender populations [42].

The total target population is approximately 120 transgender people in four study districts of Uttarakhand, Northern Himalayas [43]. Transgender women who were equal to or older than 18 years of age and could communicate in Hindi or English, and provided consent, were included in the study. However, transgender people who identified themselves as intersex, refused to consent, as well as individuals diagnosed with or undergoing treatment for comorbid conditions such as cancer, diabetes mellitus, or psychiatric disorders, were excluded from the study. The researchers assumed that the presence of existing comorbid conditions could potentially lower self-esteem and quality of life, thereby introducing bias into the study findings, and hence, were excluded from this study.

Sample size estimation

The sample size for the study was calculated using Epi Info software, considering the total population (120) of the study participants in the region [43]. The relevant non-government organizations (NGOs) in the districts are contacted to collect information on the tentative transgender population. An absolute precision of 5%, design effect, (d = 1) at 95% confidence interval using the formula, n = [DEFF*Np(1-p)]/[(d2/Z21−α/2*(N-1) + p*(1-p)] considered to calculate sample size. The final sample size for the study was reported to be 91. However, due to time constraints of the data collection, the researchers only made contact with 91 participants. After screening the questionnaires, 71 were deemed suitable for analysis, following the exclusion of 20 questionnaires due to missing or incomplete data. The data saturation technique [44] was followed to reach an optimum sample size in the qualitative trend of the study.

Data collection

Using snowball sampling techniques, 73 participants completed the questionnaires within the time constraints of the study. The interview was conducted with 10 participants using maximum variation sampling after compiling the survey findings, and took an average of 30–45 min to complete.

Measurements

Participants completed a pre-tested and validated set of tools, including consent forms and informant participant sheets (IPS). Data were collected using three questionnaires: a demographic survey, the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale, and the Short Form-12 (SF-12). Additionally, a semi-structured interview schedule was employed to explore participants’ health-seeking behaviour and lived experiences. The details of the study instruments are as follows;

Socio-demographic data sheet

This structured, pretested, and validated proforma consists of information on age (years), education level, marital status, living arrangement, employment status, monthly income, assigned sex at birth, gender identity, gender transformation, sexuality, and monthly expenditure on health.

Lived experience

A semi-structured interview schedule was prepared after conducting an extensive review [45] and experts’ inputs to extract information on the personal experiences of transgender people. 7 experts from psychology, sexually transmitted diseases (STDs) clinic, community medicine, non-governmental organisations (NGOs), psychologists, burn and plastic, and nursing validated the interview. The semi-structured interview was divided into different areas, including a psychological and social appraisal of biologically determined sexual identity and its incongruence with preferred gender identity, reactions from family and society for being born as a transgender people, discrimination, stigma, and violence in society, workplace and health seeking behaviour and professional and legal challenges. (Supplementary File 1).

Short form 12 (SF-12) measure

A modified version of the SF-12 questionnaire used to assess quality of life [34]. The 12-item questionnaire is divided into physical health (physical component summary, PCS) and mental health (mental component summary, MCS). Additionally, it includes a general health item (‘In general, would you say your health is.’), measured on a five-point Likert scale ranging from poor (1) to excellent (5). The SF-12 demonstrates strong convergent validity (PCS: 0.60–0.73, MCS: 0.54–0.83) and moderate reliability (α = 0.50), as established in a prior validation study [34]. The questionnaire follows a continuous scale, where higher composite scores indicate better quality of life and vice versa. The scores ranged from 0 (worst health) to 100 (best health), with a mean close to 50.0 and a standard deviation (SD) close to 10.0 [46]. Scores below 50.0 for both measures (PCS and MCS) are considered undesirable and may indicate the need for medical attention [47].

Rosenberg self-esteem scale

The Hindi version of the scale was used to measure the participants’ self-esteem [48]. The scale consists of 10 items; 1, 3, 4, 7, and 10 are positively worded items, while 2, 5, 6, and 9 are negatively worded. The questionnaire has a Likert response scale on a 4-point scale, 1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = agree, and 4 = strongly agree. The negatively worded items were reverse-coded before analysis, with a total score ranging from 10 to 40, where higher scores indicate higher self-esteem and vice-versa [48, 49]. Further, a mean score equal to or greater than 31 was treated as higher self-esteem in this group [50]. The interclass correlation coefficient (ICC) demonstrated good reliability (r = 0.76). Cronbach’s alpha of the Hindi version scale was 0.73, suggesting excellent reliability [48].

Health seeking behaviour

A structured questionnaire was developed after an extensive literature review and discussions with 5 experts in psychiatry (2), public health (1), nursing (1), and experts working in Anti-retroviral therapy (ART) clinics (1). The questionnaire was pre-tested on a similar population before data collection to ensure validity and reliability. The questionnaire explores health-seeking behaviour among transgender people, including healthcare services utilized, problems experienced, i.e., discrimination, stigma, or any adverse events while accessing health services, financial aspects, direct and indirect expenditure on health services, modes of payment, and health insurance schemes [9].

Statistical analysis

Quantitative analysis

73 questionnaires were found to be deemed appropriate for analysis and transferred to Microsoft Excel. The results were then analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 23.0. Standardized Z values of skewness and kurtosis were used to check the normality of the distribution. Z values between 1.96 and 1.96 were assumed to be normally distributed [51]. Data were also examined for outliers using boxplots and standardized z-scores, and no outlier values were identified. An analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the means of the SF-12 between different variables of the participants. Descriptive statistics, including frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation, are used to describe the participants’ characteristics and other variables. The α level was set at p < 0.05 (two-sided) for significance.

Qualitative analysis

Researchers employed open-ended, non-leading, and affirming language during data collection and maintained a reflexive journal to document personal reflections and monitor potential biases, thereby upholding transparency. Additionally, a clarifying rather than judgmental approach was adopted to ensure a consistently non-judgmental stance throughout the research process.

We used NVivo software to build qualitative data themes and sub-themes. Qualitative analyses followed the steps suggested for content analysis [52, 53]. Audio-recorded interviews verbatim were transcribed, translated into English (2 English experts in the native language), and validated by language experts to ensure content accuracy while preserving participants’ original expressions. Anonymity of the verbatim was maintained through coded identifiers. Two data coders, the principal investigator, and a nursing faculty member, participated in intensive verbatim reading several times before finalizing the principal themes and sub-themes. It was ensured to be neutral while reading the narratives/verbatims without being judgmental while extracting the themes [52–54]. To ensure consistency, the coding process was validated by two other independent researchers.

We viewed themes as reflections of the narrative that described the experiences conveyed while offering a structure and meaningful whole [53]. The principal investigator (PI) was pivotal in organizing meetings with respondents, facilitating discussions, and maintaining the highest standards of professionalism while deliberating key arguments based on nurses’ data.

Through iterative readings, primary themes and sub-themes were identified, analysed for congruence and divergence, and systematically refined. Later, themes and subthemes were then developed and analysed in alignment with the study objectives. A non-judgmental and neutral stance was upheld to enhance credibility, dependability, and transferability, accounting for diverse work profiles during thematic extraction.

Data trustworthiness

Trustworthiness of the data was ensured through rigorous methodological practices, including the use of open-ended questions to elicit diverse responses and the involvement of a heterogeneous team of coders comprising students and medical and nursing faculty. During the interview phase, data collection was consistently conducted by a single research team member to maintain uniformity and minimize potential biases. In the reporting phase, a transparent analytical process was employed, systematically linking themes and subthemes to respondents’ original narratives [54]. The final emergent themes demonstrated a high degree of consensus among research team members, reinforcing the credibility of the findings.

Positionality statement

As researchers from [healthcare professionals and academic researchers], we recognise that our personal experiences and cultural contexts influence how we approach and interpret this study. We are aware that our perspectives might shape our interactions with participants and the analysis of data. To mitigate potential biases, we maintained reflexive journals throughout the research process and engaged in peer debriefing to ensure balanced and respectful representation of participants’ voices.

The observed variation in physical and mental health may be attributed to the comparatively stronger social cohesion and informal support systems in rural areas. In contrast, transgender individuals residing in urban settings often encounter increased visibility and social anonymity, which leads to higher discrimination, exclusion, and chronic stress, resulting in poor quality of life [23]. Additionally, those incurring higher health expenditures may be dealing with more complex health needs, including untreated medical conditions, gender-affirming hormonal therapies, or comorbid mental health issues, and therefore, reported poor quality of life.

Relationship between Self-esteem and quality of life

Pearson’s product-moment correlation coefficient was employed to determine the relationship between quality of life (PCS and MCS) and self-esteem. Findings reported no significant association between self-esteem and PCS (r = 0.132, p = 0.265 and MCS (r = 0.134, p = 0.260). However, a significant negative correlation (r=−0.300, p = 0.010) was observed between PCS and MCS in this study population.

Health seeking behaviour

Regarding perceptions of access to healthcare services, all participants (100%) reported visiting hospitals for either general (57.6%) or specific (42.5%) consultations, engaging with doctors (50.7%), nurses (31.5%), and other health professionals (17.8%), with the majority (87.7%) bearing the expenses out of pocket. More than one-third of participants (38.4%) reported positive experiences during hospital visits, while the majority (42.5%) either refrained from commenting or expressed negative experiences (19.2%). Authors speculated that participants may refrain from sharing hospital experiences due to repeated discrimination, including misgendering and denial of care, leading to internalized stigma and mistrust in healthcare systems. Alarmingly, 76.7% of participants reported experiencing discrimination while accessing healthcare services.

Regarding disease profiles, 86.3% had no history of chronic illness, although 71.2% reported contracting common infections in the past year. 34.2% visited private hospitals or doctors when seeking medical attention, while 15.1% accessed public healthcare facilities. Notably, over half of the participants (56.2%) declined to comment on their satisfaction with the health services received, and only 38.4% reported their experiences as satisfactory with other details (Supplementary File 2). Past institutional neglect may reinforce the belief that voicing concerns will not lead to meaningful change, prompting participants to remain silent as a protective response.

Qualitative analysis

Six participants lived with family members, three with friends, and one alone. Six lived in urban areas and four in rural areas, participant ages ranged from 19 to 45, with education levels ranging from 8th standard to graduation. 50% were self-employed, two were professionals, and the other two were engaged in traditional Badhai work.

The analysis of the experiences is grouped under various themes and sub-themes. The following three themes emerged from the data: discovering my true identity as transgender person, survival in a world of discrimination, violence, and trauma, and the struggle with economic, professional, and legal challenges. We have discussed various sub-themes under the major themes (Table 4).

Major-theme 1. Discovering my true identity as transgender person

Participants narrated their journey of understanding, accepting, and asserting their transgender identity.

Sub-theme: self-awareness

Participants discussed their early realizations of being different and the internal reflections that shaped their self-awareness and moments of realizing their unique identity, often from an early age.

…. around 6–7 years of age, I started noticing the differences. I wonder, “why am I like this? This girl is different; this boy is different; there was confusion… like I told you earlier, since childhood itself. These things are within from childhood…as a child, I would wonder, “What am I? During childhood, there was no understanding of my identity. But now, with awareness, I understand that transgender people have traits of both genders. Back in my childhood, there was no internet or awareness like today’s children have by 15–16 years of age…(Ramya, 35 years).

I think I realized it when I was two years old. Right from childhood, I knew I wasn’t the same as my brothers or sisters. My grandfather understood, and I came to understand it. ………See, after two years, I never felt like a boy. I always felt that I am a girl. I used to wear my sister’s clothes. But as I grew older, around class 6 or 7, the physiological changes began. It became difficult to carry out daily activities in school and the neighborhood. (Mamta, 45 years).

I realized I was transgender around 14… I started noticing that I had an attraction to boys, not girls…if I go back to my school days, I used to feel that people would compare me to others and judge me a lot. For example, in school, if I were around the more “manly” boys, they wouldn’t want to be around me. They would judge me, thinking, “If we associate with someone like her, how will our image look? This made it hard to make male friends, but I made many female friends during that time. (Savita, 19 years)

From early childhood, each individual sensed they were different, experiencing confusion about their gender identity in the absence of awareness or societal support. With time and growing self-awareness, they recognised their transgender identity, often facing social judgment and isolation during adolescence.

Sub-theme: exploring identity

For participants, this involved navigating societal norms, understanding gender as a spectrum, and finding terminology that resonated with their experiences (i.e., “transgender people” instead of derogatory terms like “hijra”).

.I always felt like a girl. I wanted to play with girls and do girly things. I liked boys just like normal girls—thinking, “Oh, what a handsome boy!” (Ramya, 35 years).

Even before I went to school, I never felt like a boy. I wore my sister’s clothes and felt happy. Now, after COVID-19, I had to leave the ashram for my livelihood; that’s the time when I started wearing sarees,………… to make my own livelihood. (Kripa, 38 years).

……I started connecting with people from my community through social sites, and that’s when I realized I wasn’t alone……… It helped me articulate what I had been feeling all along.” But growing up, I realized that it’s natural. Whatever is natural is natural. And this isn’t something new; it’s been written about for a long time. (Kasturi, 26)

From an early age, they identified with femininity, expressing joy in behaviour aligned with their personalities. Social media and lived experiences later affirmed their identity, helping them embrace their authenticity and realize they were never alone.

Sub-theme: fighting for recognition

The participants often shared struggles to be acknowledged as their authentic selves by their families, friends, and broader society.

It’s still a fight to prove ourselves. Even though laws exist, they’re not implemented everywhere. (Kripa, 38 years).

Getting my official identity documents changed was like fighting a battle at every step. (Soumya, 24 years).

My family got outraged. They would say things like, ‘don’t come here, don’t do these things, stop these behaviour’. They tortured me a lot………They would scold me often, but I secretly continued doing what I wanted. Even if I have to leave the house, I still prefer this life, where I am more comfortable, especially with men. They (family) don’t accept it; they say, “You’re a boy, you’re going in the wrong direction. (Ranjan, 24 years).

Despite legal provisions, transgender individuals continue to struggle for acceptance, facing bureaucratic hurdles, family rejection, and societal non-acceptance. Their journey often involves persistent battles to assert identity, claim rights, and live authentically.

Major-theme 2. survival in a world of discrimination, violence, and trauma

Participants’ cumulative experiences of frequent violence, discrimination, and different kinds of abuse at different avenues.

Sub-theme: unspoken trauma

Participants experienced pain and resilience associated with systemic violence and discrimination in their surroundings.

Everything is fine except for one brother who misbehaves with me sometimes. He gets angry and calls me derogatory names, ‘chakka.’ Sometimes, he abuses me and says hurtful things. Sometimes I respond, sometimes I ignore. (Ranjan, 24 years).

People’s words were often cruel, but I chose to rise above them. They (people) think I am not fit to be on this earth. But it’s not my choice; it’s God’s will. If it’s difficult for them, it’s a difficulty in their mind, not mine. (Mamta, 45 years).

Participants described facing verbal abuse and stigma from both family and society. While one recounted hurtful behaviour from a sibling, another emphasized inner strength and resilience in the face of societal rejection.

Sub-theme: physical violence

Participants reported multiple incidences of threat and violence due to their gender identity. Participants reported cumulative experiences of physical violence, including being kicked, beaten, hit, and pushed, often occurring in public spaces.

I’ve been physically hurt by people who didn’t understand me. (Ranjan, 24 years).

There was a bad trauma in my childhood…One day, my brother hit me over these things, and my face hit the wall. It gave me a concussion, and I had ringing in my ears. That trauma has stayed with me. I was in class 9 or 10 at that time. I stopped talking to my brother after that, and two and a half years later, he passed away. That was very hard for me. I didn’t want to lose him, but I had to…. (Kasturi, 26 years).

I once confronted someone who tried to harass me in public. I stood my ground, but it was terrifying when people turned on me, scolded and shouted, used bad words, and made me responsible for everything. (Mamta, 45 years).

Participants described enduring physical violence and trauma, often from family members who struggled to understand their identities. Such incidents resulted in lasting emotional wounds and complicated familial relationships. Instances of confronting harassment in public were met with hostility and victim-blaming, exacerbating feelings of fear and isolation despite efforts to assert themselves.

Sub-theme: sexual violence, abuse, and assault

Participants reported experiencing distressing and exploitative sexual encounters across multiple settings, including within their families, schools, and workplaces.

I faced sexual harassment regularly when I was working in a menial job. People assumed I was weak and took advantage of me. (Soumya, 24 years).

Right from the beginning, when I was in school, I faced problems. It was mostly sexual harassment. It’s not that I didn’t face it; I did. There were moments when I had to fight back against unwanted advances, even in places where I thought I was safe. (Mamta, 45 years).

Participants revealed experiences of sexual and physical abuse, often by individuals they initially trusted, which left lasting psychological scars. Many faced regular sexual harassment, particularly in low-paying or vulnerable employment, where they were perceived as easy targets.

Sub-theme: psychological abuse

Participants often quote emotional manipulation and rejection, giving them feelings of unworthiness or shame.

Society’s judgment makes us feel unworthy, weakening our confidence and degrading our morals to live the life I wanted. Discrimination obviously weakens you mentally. When you tell a white thing that it’s black, it reduces your enthusiasm by 50%. Mentally, you will be weak. (Mamta, 45 years)

Participants shared that societal judgment and discrimination deeply affected their self-worth, eroded their confidence, and led to significant mental distress, making it difficult to live authentically.

Major-theme 3. Struggle with Economic, Professional, and legal challenges

Participants reported systematic barriers to achieving economic stability, professional inclusion, and legal challenges while going outside for a job or any other opportunities.

Sub-theme: job loss

Participants frequently faced termination from jobs upon disclosure of their gender identity, leaving them vulnerable to financial instability.

It’s hard to find stable work when society sees us as different, I like to do things like working at a good job or having a good partner. (Ranjana, 24 years). I experienced multiple rejections in job interviews due to my identity. (Ramya, 35 years).

Participants reported facing significant workplace discrimination, including repeated job rejections and pressure to conform to traditional gender norms. They are being threatened with dismissal for expressing femininity, highlighting the challenges of maintaining authenticity in unsupportive work environments.

Sub-theme: workplace discrimination

Participants reported facing discrimination and hostile work environments, including denial of promotions and harassment due to their gender identity. Limited access to stable, well-paying jobs often pushed them into begging or sex work for survival.

I faced many challenges everywhere. I got a job, but I was dismissed for being feminine. I decided to prioritize make-up artists because it wouldn’t create problems for me, and I have done well as a make-up artist. (Krishna, 24 years).

People call us various derogatory names, they don’t rent rooms to us, they won’t hire us for work, and some won’t even allow us to work as servants in their homes. A taboo is created, labelling trans people as rude or immoral, saying that they do strange things, not even acknowledging our gender identity, and calling us fake or fraudulent. (Kasturi, 26 years)

Participants described facing pervasive discrimination in employment and housing, often being dismissed or denied opportunities due to their gender expression. Some are pushed into low-paying or informal jobs to avoid further discrimination.

Sub-theme: relationship struggles and related trauma

Participants expressed emotional hardships and relational challenges, i.e., prioritizing certain relationships at the cost of family or personal well-being, which often left them in sadness and isolation.

I have felt very sad because I had given up everything for the person I was with. I ended up isolating myself from many people, staying away from my family. I used to say that I prioritize that person over my family. At that time, my family was not important to me; my first priority was them. (Savita, 19 years).

Participants expressed deep emotional experiences related to personal relationships. One shared feeling of regret and sadness over prioritizing a partner above family, leading to social isolation. Another described a meaningful, though one-sided, relationship that offered emotional support and acceptance, encouraging self-care and embracing transgender identity.

Sub-theme: lack of legal protection

Participants voiced frustration over the inadequate and rigid implementation of legal protections, highlighting the gap between existing laws and their practical enforcement in safeguarding their rights and preventing discrimination.

The laws exist, but they don’t work properly. If something happens to us, and we go to the police station, it is just for show. It is like a formality. They don’t really take action. Even though we have the law, it doesn’t really benefit us. (Tara, 24 years).

Without registration, many can’t access government benefits, leaving them vulnerable and abandoned in society. It became difficult to get the benefits of state-run welfare schemes for us. (Mamta, 45 years).

Participants highlighted the futility of existing laws and formal processes, describing police action as merely procedural without real enforcement. Additionally, lack of official registration barred many from accessing essential government welfare benefits, increasing their vulnerability.

Sub-theme: societal discrimination

Participants encountered significant barriers in accessing medical care and legal aid due to ongoing discrimination and societal stigma from healthcare providers and law enforcement officials.

Participants’ accounts reveal varied interactions within healthcare and law enforcement systems. While some experienced respectful treatment, others described subtle yet persistent forms of exclusion, such as structural inefficiencies and gender-insensitive protocols. Navigating public services often requires self-restraint and heightened awareness due to perceived power imbalances. These encounters underscore the emotional and psychological strain resulting from institutional practices that fail to acknowledge gender diversity. They also described being treated dismissively by police, highlighting a broader pattern of institutional stigma and exclusion.

Discussion

In this mixed-method study, all participants identified as transgender women and reported a notably poor quality of life alongside comparatively high self-esteem. Alarmingly, they experienced negative attitudes and low satisfaction in accessing healthcare services, highlighting persistent barriers within the healthcare system. The demographic profile of participants in the study region revealed a predominance of trans women, consistent with previous research in the region [43]. Comprehensive and current data on the transgender population in Uttarakhand, particularly distinguishing trans women and trans men, remains limited.

Participants with high income held professional or white-collar jobs, such as working with NGOs or running businesses like make-up artistry, whereas many transgender individuals rely on traditional livelihoods like Badhai and Cholla, consistent with a regional study [43] and other regions [34]. Though culturally rooted, these occupations are physically and mentally demanding, socially stigmatized, and reinforce negative transgender stereotypes. They also increase vulnerability to exploitation, including sexual abuse and coercion into commercial sex work [34]. Systemic discrimination, limited education, and scarce job opportunities force many transgender individuals to rely on traditional roles like Badhai, dancing, or begging due to a lack of safer livelihoods [25]. The government should enforce anti-discrimination laws, improve access to education, create inclusive jobs, and implement welfare schemes while raising awareness to reduce stigma and promote social inclusion.

While over 70% of the participants reported good to very good overall general health, which does not match the story of physical and mental health in PCS and MCS scores. Researchers spent a significant amount of time collecting data and exploring the experiences, speculating that the participants are in denial, considering themselves healthy despite looking at their poor living conditions and all other health indicators. Furthermore, prolonged exposure to social adversity may foster psychological resilience among transgender people, enabling positive reframing of their circumstances [55]. This adaptive coping strategy can lead them to perceive and report their health more favourably, even in the presence of underlying physical or psychological hardship. Additionally, due to entrenched mistrust in healthcare systems and fear of further marginalization, many transgender people may minimize or downplay health concerns, constructing protective narratives to preserve dignity and self-worth [56]. However, denial is not a healthy coping mechanism in the long run, as it can lead to a compromised quality of life and survival while fighting daily challenges [34, 57, 58].

When considered altogether, transgender people reported notably higher self-esteem and poor physical and mental quality of life. Similar findings emerged from participant narratives, highlighting experiences of psychological distress, discrimination, and stigma across familial, educational, and workplace settings. Gender-based rejection and denial of promotion at the workplace were reported to diminish motivation, undermine self-worth, erode confidence, and contribute to significant mental distress, ultimately impairing both psychological and physical well-being and hindering their ability to live authentically. The study findings align with some previous research reporting high self-esteem among transgender individuals [27, 59], while others report low self-esteem [60]. These discrepancies may stem from methodological differences and varying access to support services. Additionally, the differing stages of LGBTQI recognition and legal protections between developed and developing countries, such as India, may contribute to these variations.

Our study cohort reported poorer physical health (PCS µ = 38.59) compared to Hijra, Kothi, and transgender individuals in Kolkata (PCS µ = 49.0) [34], and heterosexual adults with food addiction (PCS µ = 45.8) [61]. Similarly, mental health scores (MCS µ = 38.09) were lower than these groups, highlighting the need for targeted interventions. The QoL scores in our study (PCS µ = 38.6, MCS µ = 38.08) were also significantly lower than those reported for transgender people in Kerala (SF-36 Mean µ = 68.85), likely reflecting Kerala’s stronger welfare programmes, healthcare access, and peer support [62]. Another South Indian study found higher satisfaction with quality of life, possibly due to better education, financial security, and access to gender-affirming care [45]. In contrast, our cohort’s poorer health outcomes may stem from financial hardship (59% earning < ₹15,000/month) and prevalent discrimination (76.7%).

Discussions with participants revealed that they struggled to establish their identity and faced multiple challenges, including difficulty in identifying their orientation [63], poor social acceptance [64], and physical, verbal, and sexual abuse by siblings, family, peer groups, and teachers [65]. These findings are in line with the previous work [66], which reported that transgender people often experience early gender incongruence, leading to identity exploration. Additionally, the apathy of law enforcement agencies in safeguarding the rights of transgender people [5] could be a plausible risk factor contributing to their poor physical and mental health. Findings on perceived discrimination, abusive behaviour of society, and lack of social support are corroborated by the recent study findings [67] and the minority stress model [68], suggesting that stigma and discrimination contribute to adverse mental health outcomes in marginalized populations. Furthermore, it has been reported that poor coping mechanisms are reported to be a significant predictor of poor mental health [67] among transgender people, which corroborates our study findings. Findings on the high prevalence of societal stigma were also reported in another study on transgender people [30].

Not all social determinants showed significant differences in MCS and PCS scores. The findings indicate that individuals with an expenditure of ≤ 1000 INR reported better physical health than those with higher or no expenditure. In this context, transgender people earning well may allocate some funds for daily expenses while saving the remaining money for future use, as revealed by the participants. Income is a crucial social determinant for marginalized groups [34], given the discrimination, stigma, and limited job opportunities they face, as highlighted by many participants during interviews. The higher expenditure is also linked to the fact that the majority of participants migrated from areas with inadequate health services and other essential facilities. Their current residence offers greater access to healthcare services, prompting them to spend more time addressing their health-related concerns. Moreover, higher expenditure can also be attributed to greater educational opportunities, which make participants more aware of their health concerns, including hormonal therapies, gender-affirming surgeries, and other medical conditions.

Apart from direct expenditure cost, participants from rural areas exhibited higher PCS and MCS scores than participants from urban areas. This disparity can be attributed to several factors. The findings on health between rural and urban areas are in contrast to previously published literature that reported that transgender people residing in rural areas experienced more challenges and reported poor quality of life and life satisfaction [69, 70]. That’s a captivating observation, highlighting a nuanced aspect of rural transgender people’s experiences. However, the existing literature does show inconsistencies regarding the health and quality of life of transgender people populations in rural versus urban settings [71]. Researchers postulate that these discrepancies may stem from small sample sizes, diverse methodologies, and the heterogeneity of transgender people’s experiences across different regions and cultures. In our study cohort, during interviews, it was observed that family dynamics in rural settings appeared to be a protective factor for transgender people by limiting their exposure to external discrimination, which might positively influence mental and physical health. However, this protection is restrictive, limiting personal freedom, social interaction, and access to opportunities. We recommend that future studies include larger and more representative samples to ensure consistency in these observations.

Findings on access to healthcare services are alarming, and reported negative experiences, discrimination, and stigma against transgender people in public hospitals. These findings align with earlier research highlighting widespread healthcare discrimination against transgender people [72] in healthcare access. Similarly, this mirrors the findings from [73, 74] where participants preferred private healthcare due to the prevalent stigma and discrimination in public facilities. These findings are in line with another work [73] reported that 96% of the participants were seeking treatment in private hospitals and clinics. Participants also reported reluctance to seek public healthcare due to fear of exposure and societal judgment, which is consistent with previous study findings [1].

Along with this, there is a lack of clear treatment guidelines by the Indian government, social prejudices, and law, i.e., Sect. 377, recently decriminalized, used by the Indian police to harass and bully those engaged in same-sex practices [75], are common causes for making life miserable among transgender people. These issues made transgender people more insecure and made it difficult to access health services. This study, therefore, makes a significant contribution to the literature on trans health by understanding the role of the many potential factors that have not been explored previously in the trans community in the study province in India.

The government should implement inclusive treatment guidelines and ensure mandatory sensitization training for healthcare providers. Dedicated transgender health units and strict enforcement of anti-discrimination laws will help to safeguard the rights of this community. Legal reforms must be supported by institutional change, and transgender individuals should be included in national health coverage schemes to ensure affordable and respectful care.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered while interpreting the findings. First, the study employed a questionnaire survey along with an interview technique on a limited sample size (n = 73), which may be too small to generalize the findings. It is challenging to assert that this sample is representative of the entire population residing in the state. However, the use of interviews allowed for an exploration of participants’ first-hand experiences, lending depth and validation to the findings.

Second, only a small portion of the data reported a link between PCS, MCS, and self-esteem. The limited sample size and composition, with all participants being transwomen, may have introduced bias, potentially yielding results that differ from those observed in a larger, more homogenous population and may restrict generalization on all transgender people. Additionally, the small sample size may have influenced the statistical analysis, impacting the robustness of the findings. Third, the influence of social desirability bias, wherein participants may have overreported positive attributes or underreported negative experiences to align with perceived societal norms or to present themselves in a more favorable manner.

Conclusion

This study shows that transgender women experience poor physical and mental health, but they reported relatively good self-esteem. Participants also raised concerns about discrimination, violence, verbal and sexual abuse, and other gender identity struggles in society. The findings raise concerns about the urgent need to support transgender people’s communities in improving their physical and mental health. This evidence can help create pathways for affordable health services and targeted interventions, ensuring safety by preventing discrimination and reducing stigma.

We recommend further research focusing on the barriers and challenges of accessing affordable care faced by trans-specific populations across India. There is a pressing need for more research on the health needs and access to healthcare services for transgender people to support the development of a more inclusive and trans-friendly society. Additionally, future research should include transgender men to enhance the generalizability of findings to the broader transgender population.
