Hypertension management in São Paulo: insights from a computational simulation approach
Authors: Pei Shan Loo, Mariana Silveira, Yara Carnevalli Baxter, Álvaro Avezum, Luciano F Drager, Luiz A Bortolotto, Johannes Boch, Daniel Cobos Muñoz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundHypertension poses significant global health challenges, especially in urban settings where systemic complexity exacerbates its burden. This study aimed to assess whether integrated intervention strategies, tested using a system dynamics model (the Simulator), can improve hypertension control and reduce cardiovascular disease (CVD) events in São Paulo, Brazil.MethodsThe Simulator was constructed in Stella Architect to project the intervention impacts on hypertension outcomes. It quantifies a causal loop diagram using São Paulo-specific data on hypertension prevalence, cascade of care, and CVD events over 2011–2045. Validation comprised sensitivity and global sensitivity analyses to assess uncertainty.FindingsThe Base scenario reflecting interventions introduced in 2018–2019, showed improvements in screening and diagnosis compared to a no-intervention scenario. Integrated interventions of patient engagement and health system improvements increased controlled cases by 41% (95% UI: 33% to 47%) and reduced CVD events rates by 19% (UI: −29% to −6%) at 15 years. These interventions heightened diagnostic and treatment demand, highlighting the need for expanded healthcare capacity. Expanding this capacity further increased controlled cases by 44% (UI: 37% to 53%) and reduced CVD events rates by 20% (UI: −31% to −8%). The most comprehensive scenario, incorporating prevention measures, achieved a 44% increase in controlled cases (UI: 36% to 53%) and a 22% reduction in CVD events rates (UI: −32% to −10%), showing long-term impacts of prevention.InterpretationA combined strategy that engages patients, expands service capacity, and delays disease onset offers the greatest gains. It highlights the need to balance individual, systemic, and preventive approaches to reduce hypertension burdens in complex systems for health. Future steps include institutionalizing the Simulator within São Paulo's municipal strategies, and adapting it to other Latin American cities, fostering region-wide, evidence-based planning.FundingThis study was funded by the Novartis Foundation.Keywords: Hypertension, São Paulo, Simulation, Policy testing, Cardiovascular diseases

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We conducted a structured search of the literature to identify existing evidence on the use of mathematical and simulation modelling for hypertension management, hypertension care cascades, cardiovascular disease outcomes, and health system interventions, with particular attention to urban settings and low- and middle-income countries. We searched PubMed/MEDLINE and screened Google Scholar for recent and in-press studies. The search covered publications from January 2000 to December 2025 and used combinations of terms related to system dynamics, simulation modelling, hypertension, cascade of care, cardiovascular disease, urban health systems, and Latin America.

While mathematical modelling has been applied to hypertension management in diverse global contexts (e.g. improving treatment retention in Nigeria, modelling disease progression in Singapore, and assessing multi-risk factor interventions in the United States), its application in Latin America remains underexplored. This gap limits understanding of how systemic challenges, such as inequitable resource distribution and fragmented urban healthcare systems, influence hypertension management outcomes in the region. São Paulo, Brazil's largest city, faces these challenges similarly to other megacities, due to its urbanization-driven inequities and underfunded healthcare infrastructure for the city's need. This study addresses these gaps by transitioning from qualitative analysis to a quantitative, data-driven approach tailored specifically to São Paulo's complex public healthcare landscape.

Added value of this study

Building on prior qualitative work from our research team, which used causal loop diagrams to map hypertension management dynamics in São Paulo, this study advances to a quantitative mathematical modelling, operationalizing systemic insights to address key challenges in the care cascade. Unlike previous studies, this model integrates real-world data and stakeholder inputs to simulate intervention scenarios and project their impacts on key hypertension outcomes, such as controlled cases and cardiovascular disease (CVD) events. The findings highlight those integrated strategies, combining patient engagement, healthcare system capacity enhancement, and preventive measures, are essential for addressing blocks in the hypertension care cascade. The study further emphasizes the importance of balance and knowing how to prioritize individual-level interventions with systemic investments and prevention strategies to achieve sustainable health outcomes.

Implications of all the available evidence

This study highlights the need for innovative, evidence-based tools to address systemic challenges in complex hypertension management in São Paulo and other urban centers across Latin America, and potentially worldwide. The Simulator demonstrates the utility of mathematical modelling as an adaptable decision-support tool with potential for scalability across urban health systems. Its flexibility allows for tailoring to diverse urban healthcare systems, supporting evidence-based resource allocation, informing public health strategies, and addressing chronic disease burdens.

Collaborating with municipal governments, will be pivotal to institutionalizing the Simulator within public health strategies, ensuring its long-term sustainability and alignment with local policies. Future research should focus on local adapting the Simulator for other urban contexts and refining its prevention component to enable more nuanced assessments of specific preventive interventions.

Expanding the model's scope to include preventive interventions, economic analysis, and additional chronic conditions would further amplify its relevance, facilitating region-wide efforts to transition public health systems toward more sustainable, equitable, and evidence-based care.

Introduction

Hypertension remains a significant global public health challenge, affecting over 1.3 billion people and contributing to approximately 10 million deaths annually.1 In Latin America, the prevalence of hypertension varies widely, with estimates ranging from 17.7% in Peru, 30.1% in Colombia, to 52.5% in Brazil.2 This burden is particularly pronounced in urban areas, where rapid urbanization intersects with fragmented healthcare systems and inequities in access, compounding the challenges of effective management.3,4 The escalating costs associated with treatment and complications underscore the urgent need for policymakers to prioritize comprehensive prevention and management strategies, aligned with global health goals like the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 3).4, 5, 6

In São Paulo, Brazil's largest city with over 12 million residents, urbanization, lifestyle changes, and disparities in healthcare access create unique obstacles for resource allocation and intervention prioritization.7, 8, 9, 10 A municipal telephone-based health survey estimated that approximately one in four adults (23.2%) has hypertension, with prevalence rising to 34.5–51.3% among those aged 50–69 years.3,11 Insights gained from addressing these challenges in São Paulo have the potential to guide scalable and equitable hypertension management strategies for urban centres across the region. Notably, the city of São Paulo voluntarily committed to the 2030 Agenda on February 2, 2018, through Municipal Law No. 16,817, which adopted the 2030 Agenda as a guiding framework for the formulation of public policies at the municipal level. The Agenda Municipal 2030, which formally embodies the city's commitment to the 17 SDGs, was officially launched via a webinar on 6 July 2021 hosted by the City Hall to introduce its objectives and targets.12 Within this policy context, persistent systemic challenges underscore the urgent need for innovative, data-driven approaches to strengthen care outcomes and advance progress toward the SDGs.

Building on prior research that mapped the feedback loops influencing hypertension care in São Paulo using a causal loop diagram.13 It was developed through an iterative process combining literature review and participatory stakeholder sessions with primary care physicians, cardiologists, epidemiologists, and implementation specialists, ensuring that the feedback structure captured both evidence-based mechanisms and operational realities. This study advances from causal loop diagram to a system dynamics modelling named as “the Simulator.” While the causal loop diagram provided a qualitative understanding of feedback loops and systemic bottlenecks, the Simulator enables quantitative analysis of intervention scenarios, uncovering actionable strategies to improve hypertension care.

Previous applications of system dynamics modelling in hypertension management have demonstrated its utility in diverse contexts, such as improving treatment retention in Nigeria, modelling disease progression mechanisms, and assessing multi-risk factor interventions in Singapore.14,15 However, such approaches remain underexplored in Latin America, and other world regions, leaving a critical knowledge gap on how systemic challenges (inequitable resources, fragmented care) affect outcomes. By addressing these gaps, we developed the Simulator, a system dynamics model, to evaluate integrated intervention scenarios. The aim was to assess the impact of integrated strategies on hypertension control and CVD events using this validated framework. We hypothesised that interventions combining patient engagement, system capacity strengthening, and preventive actions would achieve greater gains than individual strategies alone.

Methods

This study employed system dynamics modelling to analyse the hypertension management in São Paulo. Model construction and analysis were performed using Stella Architect 3.7.3 modelling software.16 System dynamics modelling (SDM) offers a mathematical modelling framework for understanding the nonlinear interactions, delay in cause and effect, and feedback loops within a complex system.17

The Simulator was developed using data from local public healthcare systems, epidemiological studies, and validated sources to strengthen its robustness and relevance. Time series data regarding the population estimates, gender ratios, and age-specific fertility rates were obtained from the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics, IBGE18 through its database system, SIDRA.19 Age-specific death rates were sourced from the Mortality Information System, SIM,20 which provides detailed mortality data for Brazil.

Hypertension prevalence data were derived from,21 as part of the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study. Additionally, data on the cascade of care, covering the proportions of the population that are undiagnosed, diagnosed but untreated, treated but uncontrolled, and controlled, were used to model transitions across the stages of hypertension management. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) metrics, including event rates, prevalence, and deaths, were obtained from the Sistema de Informações Hospitalares do SUS (SIH/SUS) records filtered by relevant ICD-10 codes.22 Where local data were unavailable or insufficient, national-level estimates from the Global burden of cardiovascular diseases and risks, 1990–2022 report published in the Journal of the American College of Cardiology were used to supplement model inputs.23 Where applicable, intervention effect sizes were calibrated using local evidence, including published evaluations of cardiovascular health initiatives in São Paulo.24

We conducted a comprehensive validation procedure to ensure the robustness of the system dynamics model, following the guidelines proposed by Barlas25 and Forrester and Senge.26 The validation process encompassed direct structure tests, structure-oriented behavior tests, and behaviour reproduction tests, ensuring the Simulator delivers actionable insights for scaling hypertension interventions in São Paulo and similar urban contexts. Crucially, the Simulator serves as a decision-support tool, not a predictive tool, designed to explore intervention scenarios and understand systemic dynamics within hypertension management.25,27

The Simulator features an interactive interface (Fig. 3 designed to operationalize the system dynamics model for hypertension management. This interface allows users to explore various intervention scenarios, assess outcomes, and adjust parameters in real time. The interface enhances the usability of the Simulator by providing policymakers and stakeholders with a decision-support tool that combines scientific rigor with accessibility.

Interactive interface of the hypertension Simulator. The interface enables real-time exploration of intervention scenarios and adjustment of key parameters. Output panels display projected trends in undiagnosed, diagnosed, treated, and controlled hypertension populations, as well as cardiovascular disease event rates over the 2011–2045 time horizon. Users can compare scenarios and explore sex-disaggregated outputs to assess differential impacts of interventions.

Sex-disaggregated data were incorporated into the Simulator to account for differences in hypertension prevalence, treatment uptake, and outcomes, recognizing the critical role of sex and gender in shaping health dynamics. At the same time, system dynamics modelling typically begins with a population-level approach to address systemic challenges and capture key feedback loops that impact the broader health system. This manuscript builds on the systemic insights identified in the causal loop diagram,13 focusing on feedback loops related to diagnosis, treatment, and prevention. These feedback loops are central to understanding hypertension management at the population level, as they highlight bottlenecks and systemic inefficiencies that require equitable resource allocation and scalable strategies.

While this study prioritizes population-level insights to address overarching challenges, the Simulator is designed to enable users to explore sex- and gender-specific dynamics through its interactive interface as shown in Fig. 3. This capability provides valuable insights into how systemic interventions may differentially impact men and women, paving the way for more granular analyses in future research. Moving to a second phase of analysis, such as disaggregated approaches, will ensure alignment with equity-focused guidelines like SAGER39 and support the development of gender-responsive health policies. This phased approach underscores the flexibility of system dynamics modelling to evolve from addressing broad systemic issues to exploring targeted equity considerations.

Building on this systemic perspective, the current study tests key intervention scenarios designed to evaluate their impacts on hypertension outcomes and CVD event rates over a 30-year horizon (2011–2045). The scenarios were designed to represent different combinations of interventions, ranging from baseline measures to comprehensive approaches integrating prevention, patient engagement, and capacity enhancement. In the scenario incorporating prevention, the Simulator simulates the impact of reduced prehypertension onset rates by adjusting this parameter to reflect the potential effects of upstream preventive measures. It is important to note that the Simulator does not include specific, targeted prevention interventions such as salt reduction or lifestyle modification programs but instead uses adjusted onset rates as a proxy to assess their potential impact. As the interventions modelled in this version of the Simulator reflect the downstream leverage points identified through the causal loop diagram and design thinking workshops,13,40 upstream prevention components will be incorporated in the next phase of development, consistent with the broader intervention pathways highlighted in the CLD and in the Discussion.

Base scenario vs. no intervention

The Base Scenario, which includes foundational interventions such as screening corners, active case-finding, and public awareness campaigns implemented in 2018 and 2019, serves as a critical starting point for hypertension control. Although these interventions improve detection, their downstream impact remains limited.

At 15 years, the No Intervention scenario results in 1,292,886 people with undiagnosed hypertension, compared with 1,050,523 under the Base scenario, corresponding to a 23% higher undiagnosed population (UI: 12% to 43%), equivalent to 242,363 additional individuals.

The screened population is substantially lower under No Intervention, with 241,626 individuals screened compared with 1,526,484 under Base, representing an 84% lower screened population (UI: −88% to −70%), equivalent to 1,284,858 fewer individuals screened. The diagnosed population is 1,466,916 under No Intervention vs. 1,657,419 under Base, corresponding to an 11% lower diagnosed population (UI: −22% to −5%), or 190,503 fewer diagnosed individuals. The treated population is 555,364 compared with 576,344 under Base (4% lower; UI: −10% to 2%), and the controlled population is 970,608 compared with 986,337 under Base (2% lower; UI: −7% to 3%).

Despite substantial differences in screening and case detection, there is no meaningful difference in hypertension prevalence or CVD event rates at 15 years, with CVD event rates differing by less than 1% (UI: −10% to 18%), suggesting that detection-focused strategies alone are insufficient to alter long-term disease progression.

Health system focus vs. patient focus scenarios

At 15 years, the Health System Focus scenario delivers greater population-level improvements than the Patient Focus strategy. The controlled population reaches 1,247,694 individuals, representing a 26% increase compared with the Base scenario (UI: 19% to 33%), whereas Patient Focus achieves 1,080,655 individuals controlled, corresponding to a 10% increase (UI: 4% to 14%). These differences translate into divergent cardiovascular outcomes: Health System Focus reduces CVD event rates by 14% (UI: −24% to 0%), while Patient Focus achieves only a 3% reduction (UI: −14% to 14%).

These differences reflect contrasting cascade dynamics. Under Patient Focus, the treated population increases substantially to 679,426 individuals, corresponding to an 18% increase (UI: 12% to 26%). However, the undiagnosed population rises to 1,167,173 individuals, representing an 11% increase (UI: 3% to 24%), highlighting limited gains in case detection. The screened population remains markedly lower than Base (502,419 individuals; 67% lower, UI: −80% to −50%), as interventions primarily target adherence rather than expanded screening capacity.

In contrast, Health System Focus reduces the undiagnosed population to 691,097 individuals, corresponding to a 34% reduction (UI: −42% to −16%) relative to Base. Although the treated population increases more modestly (605,328 individuals; 5% higher, UI: 0% to 12%), strengthened diagnostic and system-level capacity enables more efficient progression to sustained control. The screened population under Health System Focus remains lower than Base (336,404 individuals; 78% lower, UI: −86% to −58%), reflecting faster movement through the cascade rather than accumulation in early stages.

Taken together, these findings indicate that system-level strengthening more effectively translates cascade improvements into durable blood pressure control and measurable cardiovascular risk reduction than patient-centered adherence strategies alone.

CARDIO scenario: integrated improvement

The CARDIO Scenario (orange line) combines patient engagement strategies with health system strengthening measures, while excluding specific enhancements to diagnostic and treatment capacity. At 15 years, the CARDIO scenario produces substantial gains in both hypertension control and cardiovascular outcomes. The controlled population reaches 1,390,913 individuals, corresponding to a 41% increase compared with the Base scenario (UI: 33% to 47%), equivalent to 404,576 additional individuals achieving blood pressure control. This translates into a 19% reduction in CVD event rates (UI: −29% to −6%), outperforming both Patient Focus and Health System Focus strategies.

Upstream, CARDIO reduces the undiagnosed population to 527,493 individuals, corresponding to a 50% reduction (UI: −65% to −32%), equivalent to 523,030 fewer individuals remaining undiagnosed. Although the screened population is 502,419 individuals, representing a 67% lower screened population compared with Base (UI: −80% to −50%), more rapid transition through the cascade rather than reduced engagement. Individuals transition more rapidly from screening to diagnosis and treatment rather than remaining in earlier stages. The treated population increases to 719 831 individuals, corresponding to a 25% increase (UI: 17% to 34%) relative to Base.

These findings demonstrate the synergistic effect of integrating patient-centered engagement with system-level strengthening, translating detection and treatment gains into sustained blood pressure control and measurable reductions in cardiovascular risk.

CARDIO + Diagnostic & Treatment Capacity

Adding Diagnostic and Treatment Capacity to the CARDIO intervention (pink dotted line) further strengthens downstream performance of the hypertension care cascade. The controlled population reaches 1,417,717 individuals, corresponding to a 44% increase compared with the Base scenario (UI: 37% to 53%), equivalent to 431 380 additional individuals achieving sustained blood pressure control. This translates into a 20% reduction in CVD event rates (UI: −31% to −8%), representing the greatest cardiovascular reduction observed to this point.

Upstream, the undiagnosed population decreases to 345 613 individuals, corresponding to a 67% reduction (UI: −82% to −52%), equivalent to 704 910 fewer individuals remaining undiagnosed. The treated population increases modestly to 605,328 individuals, representing a 5% increase (UI: 0% to 12%) relative to Base; however, enhanced diagnostic and treatment capacity enables a greater proportion of treated individuals to transition to sustained control.

Overall, these findings indicate that expanding diagnostic and treatment capacity amplifies the synergistic effects of integrated patient and system-level interventions, producing substantial gains in blood pressure control and measurable reductions in cardiovascular events.

Prevention + CARDIO + Diagnostic & Treatment Capacity

The Prevention + CARDIO + Diagnostic & Treatment Capacity scenario (green dashed line) is the most comprehensive strategy and achieves the largest reduction in cardiovascular risk across all scenarios. The CVD event rate decreases by 22% (UI: −32% to −10%), corresponding to the lowest projected event rate observed.

The controlled population reaches 1,421,586 individuals, representing a 44% increase compared with the Base scenario (UI: 36% to 53%), equivalent to 435,249 additional individuals achieving sustained blood pressure control. This level is comparable to the gains achieved under CARDIO + capacity alone, indicating that preventive interventions primarily reinforce and sustain downstream control rather than further increasing it.

The undiagnosed population decreases to 325,676 individuals, corresponding to a 69% reduction (UI: −84% to −55%), equivalent to 724,847 fewer individuals remaining undiagnosed. Preventive interventions act upstream by reducing incident hypertension, thereby lowering future inflow into the cascade and stabilising gains achieved through patient engagement and system strengthening.

These findings indicate that while integrated detection and treatment strategies drive improvements in blood pressure control, the addition of prevention delivers the greatest long-term reductions in cardiovascular events and overall disease burden.

Discussion

The findings of this study provide key insights for improving hypertension management in São Paulo. The results emphasize that sustainable improvements in hypertension outcomes require both patient engagement and healthcare system capacity expansion. While patient-focused interventions, such as campaigns and adherence support, enhance initial engagement, they must be accompanied by investments in diagnostic capacity, treatment access, and workforce training to prevent bottlenecks and ensure sustained hypertension control.

The Scenario 5 (CARDIO scenario), which integrates patient engagement and health system improvements, resulted in substantial reductions in undiagnosed cases and marked increases in both treated and controlled populations. This comprehensive approach effectively identifies hypertensive individuals while ensuring sustained treatment and disease control. These findings align with research demonstrating that healthcare interventions are most effective when structural improvements complement patient-centred strategies.41,42 Nonetheless, while these interventions enhance access to care, they also increase demand for long-term hypertension management, placing sustained pressure on healthcare resources.

The simulated findings validate and expand upon the systemic dynamics identified in the causal loop diagram, particularly Feedback Loop R1 (Chronic Care Loop), which illustrates how treatment success reinforces long-term healthcare demand.13 As more patients receive treatment and live longer with controlled hypertension, follow-up care needs expand, creating a reinforcing feedback loop where system strain increases over time. This aligns with studies on chronic disease management, showing that while effective interventions reduce mortality, they also expand the patient pool requiring ongoing care, gradually intensifying pressure on system capacity.43 Without parallel investments in infrastructure and workforce expansion, blockages in diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up care may emerge, limiting the long-term sustainability of hypertension management.

To address this growing demand, Scenario 6 expands diagnostic and treatment capacity, demonstrating a notable impact on reducing undiagnosed cases and improving treatment and control rates. Investments in diagnostic equipment, workforce training, and resource allocation enhance treatment access. However, as highlighted by Mogueo, Defo,44 healthcare system limitations influence patients’ ability to access care and manage chronic conditions effectively. In addition, Busnello, Melchior45 found that system-related factors were associated with patients discontinuing hypertension treatment. While system expansion initially improves treatment coverage, findings align with research by Tchoe and Nam,46 Meijer, Wouterse,47 Wang,48 showing that as the hypertensive population grows, healthcare infrastructure eventually reaches a saturation point where treatment capacity can no longer keep pace with increasing demand.

Fig. 2 illustrates this trend, showing how the percentage of treated patients under Scenario 6 and Scenario 7 initially rises steeply from 2024, peaks in 2038, and then declines. Although the absolute number of treated patients continues to grow, the rising prevalence of hypertension inflates the denominator, leading to a plateau in treatment rates and an eventual decline in coverage. This reinforces the limitations of system expansion alone, as the growing patient pool begins to outstrip available resources. These findings are consistent with Meijer, Wouterse,47 who emphasize that without efforts to reduce new cases, healthcare systems eventually become overburdened, leading to declining treatment coverage despite ongoing efforts.

Proactively addressing this challenge requires a shift toward prevention. The importance of prevention becomes evident in Scenario 7, which reduces CVD event rates by 22% (UI: 10% to 32%) by delaying hypertension onset. Rather than further increasing hypertension control, prevention alleviates long-term system strain by reducing the inflow of new cases, thereby stabilizing gains achieved through diagnostic and treatment capacity expansion and delaying capacity bottlenecks over time. These findings underscore the strategic importance of upstream preventive approaches that act on the social and environmental determinants of hypertension, such as food environments, urban design, and lifestyle-related risk exposures, even when such determinants are not explicitly modelled. This aligns with research by Basharic, Janati,49 Piccini, Facchini6 and Shin, Konlan,50 which highlight that community-based prevention programs, such as lifestyle interventions and dietary modifications, effectively reduce hypertension incidence and long-term healthcare burden. Unlike system expansion, which eventually faces structural limitations, prevention reduces patient inflow, helping to maintain system efficiency over time and reinforcing the need to complement health system interventions with broader, cross-sectoral prevention strategies in future phases of the Simulator.

These findings reinforce the need to embed prevention into municipal health strategies, particularly in São Paulo, where urban planning policies, dietary regulations (e.g. salt reduction), and community-based health initiatives could further mitigate prehypertension onset and ease long-term system pressures.48,51 Future models should incorporate specific preventive strategies, such as salt reduction policies, urban planning for healthier diets, and active lifestyle initiatives,52 to fully capture their long-term impacts. Enhancing the Simulator with these prevention components would strengthen its ability to guide evidence-based policymaking and ensure the sustainability of hypertension management efforts across urban Latin America.

Beyond its role in intervention testing, the interactive nature of the Simulator fosters stakeholder learning by experimentally illustrating the outcomes of various scenarios, promoting systemic thinking and a deeper understanding of interconnected health dynamics.14 As noted by one partner, “I am a dietitian, I should be well aware of this (prevention of hypertension), but I was focused more on other interventions instead. I was blind before; the simulations enable me to see this model as a disease management model. There is so little change even if we maximize all the interventions because we are not preventing people from having the disease in the first place.” This insight highlights how the Simulator serves not only as a decision-support tool but also as a learning platform, fostering dialogue among stakeholders and encouraging the adoption of upstream preventive measures, in line with studies by Freebairn, Atkinson,53 Rooney-Varga, Coleman.54 This realization prompted discussions about incorporating prevention strategies into broader public health initiatives and advocating for the Simulator's adoption at the federal level, including collaboration with organizations like the Heart Association of Brazil.

The Simulator's core structures, including the hypertension cascade of care (screening, diagnosis, treatment, and control), feedback loops, and dynamic interactions, are designed as adaptable frameworks suitable for diverse urban settings. By adjusting input data and parameters, the Simulator can be tailored to reflect local healthcare systems, epidemiological profiles, and policy priorities in cities across South America. However, successful adaptation requires more than technical adjustments, it necessitates participatory workshops, iterative validation, and strong stakeholder engagement to ensure alignment with local realities and policy goals.

With its interactive interface and scalability, the Simulator has the potential to become a region-wide decision-support tool. Cities such as Bogotá, Colombia, or Buenos Aires, Argentina, can leverage this approach to evaluate intervention scenarios, optimize resource allocation, and improve public health outcomes. By addressing shared challenges such as fragmented healthcare systems and rapid urbanization, the Simulator offers a pathway toward consistent, evidence-based strategies for tackling hypertension and other chronic diseases across South America.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. While the Simulator is built on real-world data and has undergone expert validation, certain long-term projections and age- and sex-disaggregated population subgroups required additional assumptions. As with any simulation, results illustrate possible outcomes under given assumptions rather than precise predictions. Although the Simulator was calibrated against historical trends and validated with expert input, further integration of newly available data could refine its robustness. Additionally, real-world implementation and evaluation would be needed to confirm these effects.

Additionally, while stakeholder engagement was incorporated throughout model development, expanding participation to include a broader range of stakeholders, such as community representatives, patients, and private-sector health providers, could further enhance its applicability. Unfortunately, due to reasons related to other prevailing priorities at the time, key institutional partners such as São Paulo's city hall and the Ministry of Health were not directly involved in this phase of development, which the impact of this absence will certainly be mitigated by the confirmed participation in the upcoming phases of this process's maturation, thereby ensuring political relevance and long-term adoption. Strengthening collaboration with these institutions will be critical.

Engaging with São Paulo's city hall will be a pivotal step toward institutionalizing the Simulator and embedding it into municipal public health strategies and decision-making processes. This collaboration would enhance its sustainability and influence in shaping long-term health policies. Building on this effort, future work should focus on replicating the Simulator in other Brazilian cities to assess its adaptability and scalability. Tailoring the Simulator to reflect localized interventions and healthcare contexts is essential to ensure its relevance and utility across diverse urban settings. Additionally, expanding the scope to include cost analyses will provide valuable insights into the economic implications of various interventions, further supporting evidence-based resource allocation decisions. In particular, future work will integrate cost-effectiveness and budget impact analyses to assess the financial feasibility of capacity expansion and prevention strategies, recognizing that economic viability is a critical consideration for municipal and national decision-makers.

Conclusion

This study highlights the need for a systemic approach to hypertension management, integrating patient engagement, healthcare system capacity improvements, and preventive measures. The Simulator transitions from prior qualitative causal loop analyses to a quantitative model, providing actionable insights for evidence-based public health strategies. Integrated CARDIO interventions significantly improved hypertension control and reduced cardiovascular events. Their sustained success, however, depends on simultaneous health system investments in diagnostic and treatment capacities. Preventive measures further alleviated system strain, ensuring more sustainable resource use and better long-term outcomes. Addressing systemic inequities and inefficiencies, the Simulator provides a generic framework adaptable to other urban contexts in Latin America. Collaborative efforts between researchers, policymakers, and healthcare providers will be crucial for scaling this model and achieving sustainable improvements in chronic disease management across urban healthcare systems.
