Conceptualisation and operationalisation of social support in health interventions: a scoping review
Authors: Jessica Kersey, Sangjin Lee, Lisa Tabor Connor, Sarah Shyres, Elnaz Alimi, Noemi Rojas Serrano, Moon Young Kim, Phan Van Sang, Noelle Casem, Christian Le, Kim Lipsey, Elena Donoso Brown
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesSocial support influences health outcomes for individuals with chronic conditions, yet there is variability in how it is conceptualised and operationalised in health research. We examined how social support is conceptualised, measured and addressed through health interventions.DesignScoping review.Data sourcesPubMed/Medline, Elsevier EMBASE, NIBAHL and APA PsycInfo.Eligibility criteriaStudies were included if they examined the effects of an intervention that aimed to build or strengthen social support in an adult population with a chronic health condition or disability.Data extraction and synthesisWe extracted data on study characteristics (study design, sample size, diagnostic group), social support outcome measures, social support domains addressed and intervention characteristics.ResultsAcross 110 studies, interventions predominantly addressed emotional support (85.1%) and informational support (54.4%), with limited attention to tangible support (5.3%). Despite previous research suggesting that affectionate support and positive social interaction are highly valued social support domains, these were addressed less often. Interventions were predominantly delivered in dyadic formats (43.9%), by peer supporters (35.1%) and mental health professionals (35.4%). Psychosocial skills development and social network expansion were the most frequently employed intervention components, each included in 45.6% of interventions. Relationship quality was the primary focus of social support assessment tools.ConclusionsThis review suggests that social support is most commonly conceptualised as a dynamic relational process built on emotional connection and shared experiences, rather than merely the assistance provided. Future interventions should better integrate affectionate support and positive social interaction, along with adaptability to accommodate cultural and social preferences.Keywords: Scoping Review, Public Health, Social Cohesion

Introduction

Social support has been identified as a critical factor for adults with chronic illness and disabilities, influencing a wide range of physical and mental health outcomes and overall well-being.1,9 Strong social support is a well-established protective factor against functional decline, chronic disease, mental illness and mortality—the magnitude of its effect on mortality is comparable to that of risk factors such as smoking, physical inactivity and obesity.1,11 Given its significant influence on a wide range of health outcomes, it is essential to evaluate and address individuals’ social support needs. However, the most effective strategies for doing so remain unclear.

Although social support is a potentially effective intervention target to improve health outcomes, the lack of conceptual clarity around social support poses significant challenges for systematically addressing it in intervention design and implementation.12 13 Previous studies, including other literature reviews, have described proposed theories and mechanisms of social support. Prominent social support frameworks and theories identify different social support domains, encompassing emotional support (advice, encouragement), tangible support (provision of resources or physical assistance), informational support (education or information), instrumental support (assistance with daily activities), affectionate support (intimacy, sense of feeling loved or valued), positive social interaction (enjoyable social activities) and appraisal (social comparison) dimensions.1214,17 Similarly, numerous mechanisms have been proposed to explain how social support influences health, including stress buffering, social comparison, setting expectations and psychological benefits of reciprocity.913 17,19 While social support is clearly multidimensional, this theoretical variability leads to inconsistencies in how social support is operationalised and measured within health research.12 17 20 Additional complexity emerges when considering the diverse sources and contexts of social support, differing priorities and values among social support recipients, and the varying quality of provided support.1719 21,24 There is no single unified conceptualisation of social support or prevailing theory describing its influence on health.

Although numerous interventions have been developed to enhance social support and improve health-related outcomes, these interventions have targeted different domains of social support and these domains have shown varying correlations with health.925,27 Research suggests that perceived social support—or a person’s subjective perception of their support—consistently demonstrates stronger and more reliable associations with health-related outcomes than received social support—or the objective amount of support actually received.9 12 24 25 28 29 Perceived social support is highly influenced by the quality of the closest one or two relationships in a person’s life.13 Research suggests that the quality of relationships within one’s social network is more important than the size of the social network itself.25 29 30 Despite the complexity of social support, few studies have specifically designed interventions aligned clearly with existing theories or frameworks. This gap highlights the importance of understanding how current health research addresses social support assessment and intervention.

Addressing this gap begins with identifying how to improve the perceived social support that originates from personal social support networks. Our team conducted a scoping review to describe how social support is conceptualised, operationalised and measured in health intervention research. The findings of this review provide a more comprehensive understanding of what social support is and how it can be built or leveraged to improve health.

Materials and methods

The Arksey and O’Malley framework for scoping reviews31 guided the design of this review and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis extension for Scoping Reviews guided its reporting.32 This study did not require IRB approval. We searched the literature using a broad combination of standardised subject terms, keywords and wildcard searching for the concepts of Peer Group, Social Support, Support Psychosocial, Self-Help Groups, Peer Mentoring, Emotional Support, Social Network*, Peer relations, Friends, friend*, “Friendship, Peer Counseling, Perceived Social Support, “Encouragement, Interpersonal Relations, Social Interaction, Support Groups, dyads, Adults and intervention*. These strategies were developed in collaboration with a medical librarian and were based on concepts from social support theories, as well as our preliminary scan of the literature. Searches were executed in the PubMed/MEDLINE (1946-), Elsevier EMBASE (1947-) Cumulative Index of Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL, 1937-) and APA PsycInfo (1800s-) from 2003 to 2023 of each database. The full search strategy is available in online supplemental file 1.1. All results were exported to EndNote.

Data management, eligibility screening and data extraction were conducted using the Covidence online systematic review tool, REDCap and Microsoft Excel.33 We screened articles using the following inclusion criteria: (1) addressed an adult population with a chronic health condition or disability, (2) included an intervention that focused on building social support and (3) included outcome data from a social support, mental health or physical health outcome measure to support our efforts at examining measurement approaches. Studies were excluded if they (1) did not present follow-up data on the outcomes of interest (eg, qualitative analyses, protocol papers and reviews were excluded), (2) examined palliative care or end-of-life interventions (because our goal was to examine efforts to build long-term support) or (3) the social support was provided only by a healthcare provider, organisation or paid caregiver, who may not continue to provide support outside of paid services.

Given the lack of conceptual clarity around social support, our team used a broad definition of social support during our title and abstract screening process, then narrowed our criteria for the full-text screening process. We defined social support as a person’s perceived social resources, involving emotional, informational, tangible or affectionate support. We defined social support interventions as interventions that intentionally aimed to build on social support by actively expanding or strengthening personal sources of social support. At all stages, studies were either preliminarily included or marked for discussion if there was a question about eligibility. Our team of social support experts then reviewed these to decide on eligibility and determine whether clarifications to criteria or definitions were required. We made the following decisions to guide our full-text eligibility screening process:

Chronic disease self-management interventions were excluded unless they were supplemented with other intervention components that were intended to enhance social support and were not typically included in self-management interventions.

During the screening process, we tagged relevant review papers, protocol papers and secondary analyses of eligible interventions without an eligible outcome. We conducted hand searches of these papers to identify additional studies for screening. Each title/abstract and full text was independently reviewed by two reviewers, and conflicts were resolved by a third reviewer.

We extracted data on the following study characteristics: (1) country where the study was conducted, (2) setting where the intervention was delivered (inpatient, health clinic, home, community, other), (3) study design, (4) health condition of focus, (5) sample size, (6) sample mean age and (7) social support outcome measures used, if any. To describe interventions, we extracted data on the following: (1) intervention recipient (patient only, patient and one family member (dyad), patient and multiple family members (family), group of people with a similar health condition), (2) intervention provider, (3) domains of social support addressed in the intervention (emotional support, tangible support, informational support, affectionate support, positive social interaction and appraisal), (4) social support intervention components (which were iteratively developed throughout our review process through group discussions), (5) learning mechanisms, processes or structures used to build social support, (6) dosage and (7) brief description of the intervention. Social support domains included tangible or instrumental support, informational support, emotional support, affectionate support, positive social interaction and appraisal. Our team generated these domains based on common concepts within social support theories and frameworks.12 14 15 17 18 All data extraction was conducted by two independent reviewers and conflicts were resolved by a third reviewer.

We conducted descriptive statistics and synthesised study characteristics to describe the included literature. We then compiled the included social support outcome measures, grouped them thematically and calculated their frequency of use across studies. Finally, we examined the frequencies at which each social support domain was addressed in the included studies, and the frequencies at which each social support intervention component was included across studies.

Results and discussion

Findings

Our final search, last updated on 16 October 2024, yielded a total of 68 167 unique articles for screening. After screening 699 full texts, we included 110 articles in this review. The details of our search and screening processes are presented in figure 1. The characteristics of these studies are provided as online supplemental file 1.2 and are summarised in table 1. The final sample of studies had global representation and included participants with a wide range of health conditions, delivered by a wide range of providers. Most studies (74.5%) were randomised controlled trials and were delivered in either a health clinic or other community setting (92.7%).

Table 2 presents the social support assessments used across studies and their frequency of use. Most studies included only one measure of social support; of those that included multiple social support measures, these typically used multiple approaches to measuring dyadic and marital relationships. The most common type of assessments (n=20) evaluated dyadic and marital relationships, and among these, the Dyadic Adjustment Scale (or its counterpart, the Dyadic Adjustment Scale-Revised)34 35 was the most commonly used social support assessment across studies. Table 3 provides a summary of each study’s intervention characteristics. The full details of these characteristics are presented in online supplemental file 1.3. Interventions were primarily delivered to dyads, or the person with a health condition and one supporter, often a spouse (43.9%).37,86 Many studies described interventions as being ‘family-based’, but on closer inspection, most of these only included one family member and were therefore classified as dyadic interventions. Only 2.6% of studies examined a truly family-based or social network intervention, in which multiple members of the social network were included in the intervention along with the patient.87,89 Only 27.2% of studies were delivered to only a person with a health condition as a 1:1 intervention,4243 49 60 79 89,118 and the remainder were delivered to groups of people with similar health conditions.66112 119,146 While the frequent use of dyadic, family and group interventions reflects social support development as being a fundamentally social activity, it is a notable finding given health system and insurance structures that sometimes limit availability and coverage of group-based and family-based interventions, and cultural and societal norms that affect the involvement of social networks in healthcare.

The most common providers of interventions were peer mentors or peer supporters (35.1%). Of the interventions delivered by health professionals, most were delivered by mental health providers (psychologists, psychiatrists or counsellors; 25.4%) or nurses (20.2%). Given the close association between social support and mental health, it is unsurprising that mental health researchers may be interested in developing more interventions that incorporate elements of social support. As a team of predominantly rehabilitation researchers, we were surprised at the low incidence of interventions delivered by rehabilitation therapists, despite the importance of social support to key rehabilitation outcomes, including participation and independence in daily life.147 148 Rehabilitation researchers must do more to communicate our ability to address social support through intervention and address implementation barriers related to our workflows and reimbursement models to provide effective social support interventions.

The included studies examined interventions that covered all social support domains. Most studies built emotional support (85.1%) and informational support (54.4%). Only 5.3% of the interventions addressed tangible or instrumental support. This again aligns with social support theories that emphasise the importance of close relationships and the perspectives of people with chronic illness and disability, who prioritise emotional support over tangible support.30 36 149 Further, tangible support may be easier to supplement through organisations and paid services. However, social support theories and studies have demonstrated that even more important than emotional support are affectionate support and positive social interaction: people believe their support is most effective when they feel that someone truly loves and values them and will continue to see them as more than just a care recipient.13 29 30 36 Interventions that enhance this sense of affectionate support and that facilitate continued engagement in shared meaningful activities remain critical and were under-represented considering the value placed on them by people in need of greater support.

A full presentation of each study’s intervention components is presented in onlinesupplemental files 1.3 1.4. The vast majority of studies examined complex interventions that incorporated multiple social support components, along with supporting structures, learning mechanisms and non-social support components. The most commonly used social support intervention components were the development or expansion of the social network and reciprocal psychosocial support skills (45.6% each). Development of the social network typically occurred through group or peer interventions that added one or more new sources of support. Psychosocial support focused on developing skills to manage stress and cope with stressors, both as individuals and as a dyad or family. Around a third of the studies incorporated elements of relationship strengthening and development of communication strategies to facilitate sustainable and effective support. The least commonly used intervention component was health behaviour support (9.6%), which was typically present in health behaviour change and self-management interventions. The frequent use of psychosocial skills development, relationship strengthening and communication skill development aligns with social support theories and previous research demonstrating the importance of stress management and relationship quality.13 15 22 29

On the other hand, expansion of the social network on its own may not align with what people seek in social support.28,3036 However, the frequent use of peers as a means of expanding the social network may meet this need by providing the understanding, shared experiences and emotional support that people value in their support relationships. Interventions often added peers to the social network to serve as sources of emotional and information support. Yet, these varied in the degree of social learning and social support that were intentionally fostered. Simply expanding the social network is unlikely to meet the needs of people with chronic health conditions, and interventions should carefully design peer interactions to maximise the effectiveness of support and foster closer relationships.1628,30 36 Peer-based interventions may include structured opportunities to share experiences, provide reciprocal psychosocial support, participate in shared social activities and develop emotional connections; these activities may maximise the effectiveness of peer relationships and support.

Online supplemental file 1.2 presents study characteristics by global region. We observed some trends that may highlight important cultural differences in the way social support is operationalised. The use of dyadic interventions appeared more common in studies conducted in North America and Asia, suggesting that a close family member may play an important support role in these regions. We also noted a higher rate of interventions that had been culturally adapted within North America (eg, promotora-delivered health management interventions for Mexican Americans and their families, culturally adapted and non-traditional medicine-focused interventions for Asian Americans), possibly highlighting the demographic diversity within the region. Europe had a relatively higher rate of studies that addressed positive social interaction as a domain of social support, possibly illustrating the importance of socialisation and social quality of life.150 Finally, every included study that was conducted in either Africa or the Middle East included peer-based intervention components, possibly highlighting the importance of finding health-focused social support outside of traditional healthcare settings or health system structures that more comprehensively incorporate peers and laypersons into healthcare delivery.151 152 It is important to note that most global regions were under-represented in this study, and we suggest these cultural considerations with caution.

Limitations

This review had several limitations. First, because we were examining a construct with conceptual ambiguity, we continuously refined our definitions and criteria throughout this process based on our experiences screening studies for inclusion. While we started broad and narrowed the focus later, it is possible that we applied these definitions and criteria inconsistently, especially early in the abstract screening process. There may be additional relevant literature that was not included. The risk of missing relevant literature is also increased by the possibility that our search strategy did not include all relevant search terms. We used social support frameworks and theories to inform our search strategy. Still, many studies that included social support elements included these as a secondary focus of the intervention. There may be similar studies that did not appear under our selected search terms. Finally, we drew on social support theory and our own experiences as social support researchers to develop a classification scheme for social support domains and intervention components. We may be missing important social support constructs in this classification scheme and may also have missed some nuance within each of these classifications.

There are also important limitations within this body of literature. First, while this review included studies published all over the world, there may be important cultural considerations in how social support is conceptualised that were not addressed in this analysis. Different cultures may define family roles, emotional expression and help-seeking behaviours in vastly different ways, which influences how social support interventions should be designed and implemented. This variability suggests the need for culturally responsive frameworks that acknowledge diverse expectations and practices, and flexible intervention approaches that can accommodate varied cultural values. Future research should explore how social support interventions can be adapted to honour cultural values while maintaining theoretical integrity, and how practitioners can assess cultural beliefs about support when developing treatment plans.

Further, while this review included many community-based interventions, there was limited explicit focus on health equity. Many chronic conditions disproportionately affect marginalised communities who may face structural barriers to accessing strong social support networks. The reliance on dyadic interventions (often requiring spouses) may inadvertently favour those in traditional family structures, while overlooking those who are single or have non-traditional support networks. Similarly, time-intensive interventions may be less accessible to those facing transportation barriers, financial constraints or scheduling difficulties due to multiple jobs or competing responsibilities. Future research on social support interventions must be designed with health equity considerations at the forefront, ensuring that those with the greatest social support needs can access appropriate interventions.

Conclusions and implications

This review affirms that social support is a multidimensional construct. This is evidenced by the range of assessment approaches used and the finding that nearly all interventions addressed multiple social support domains and included multiple social support intervention components. The frequent use of dyadic, family and group interventions underscores that social support is also fundamentally relational and context-dependent, suggesting that intervention approaches be adaptable to each person’s needs and situation. The findings of this review, in combination with existing social support research, suggest that social support is best conceptualised not merely as resources or assistance provided, but as a dynamic relational process built on emotional connection, reciprocity and often, shared experiences. These are elements that can be systematically developed through targeted interventions focusing on relationship quality and development of skills to effectively use social support, rather than merely expanding social networks.

Social support interventions reflected the multidimensional nature of social support and typically included multiple social support intervention components. Interventions involving peers provided opportunities for emotional and informational support, while relationship strengthening interventions provided opportunities for stronger and more sustainable affectionate support. More interventions are needed to foster opportunities for meaningful positive social interaction with new or existing members of the social network to enhance holistic perceived social support. There may also be opportunities for future research to focus on applying social support to enhance health and quality of life, similar to approaches taken in health behaviour change and self-management interventions.

These findings have important implications for delivering care that addresses both medical and social needs. The predominance of interventions delivered by peers and mental health professionals suggests a need for broader professional engagement in social support intervention research, as many people do not have access to affordable and timely mental health services. This may result in the development of social support interventions that are more feasible for implementation across settings and disciplines. Health systems, interdisciplinary healthcare providers and payers must evolve to accommodate family participation, group formats and peer support integration—approaches that dominate social support intervention research. Additionally, professionals across disciplines will likely require training in relationship-centred approaches that address social support as an integral component of health, not merely an adjunct to medical care. This shift towards interdisciplinary, relationship-focused care could significantly enhance outcomes for individuals with chronic conditions by addressing the vital intersection between medical and social determinants of health.
