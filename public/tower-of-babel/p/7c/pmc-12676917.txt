Acetaminophen Inhibits Fatty Infiltration without Impairing Tendon-Bone Interface Healing in a Rat Model of Acute Rotator Cuff Repair
Authors: Jong Pil Yoon, Sung-Jin Park, Dong-Hyun Kim, Yoon Seong Choi, Hyun Joo Lee, Jun-Young Kim, Chul-Hyun Cho, Seok Won Chung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroudRotator cuff (RC) tears are a common shoulder disorder associated with pain, dysfunction, and progressive muscle degeneration. Postoperative muscle atrophy and fatty infiltration are key factors contributing to poor healing outcomes, increased rerupture rates, and long-term functional impairment. While acetaminophen (APAP) is widely used for pain management, its potential effects on muscle fatty infiltration and tendon-bone interface (TBI) healing remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate whether APAP influences fatty infiltration and TBI healing after RC surgery. We hypothesized that APAP would reduce fatty infiltration by downregulating peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ) while preserving histological and biomechanical TBI integrity.MethodsA RC repair rat model was created using 12-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats. Ten rats in the APAP group received an oral dose of 30 mg/kg APAP daily for 4 weeks after RC tendon transection, and 10 rats in the control group were administered only 0.9% saline. All rats were sacrificed 4 weeks after surgery. Fatty infiltration was analyzed qualitatively and quantitatively in the proximal part of the supraspinatus muscle. TBI was evaluated for RC healing effect through general tissue staining and biomechanical strength measurements.ResultsFour weeks after tendon repair, the APAP group showed significant prevention of supraspinatus fatty infiltration compared to the control group (p < 0.001). These results were confirmed through immunohistochemical results showing that PPAR-γ, a marker gene for fatty infiltration, was significantly decreased in the APAP group (p < 0.001). Histological healing assessments between the 2 groups were not significantly different (p = 0.634). Additionally, mechanical strength was not negatively affected.ConclusionsAPAP treatment was associated with prevention of supraspinatus fatty infiltration without adversely affecting TBI healing in an RC repair model.

Rotator cuff (RC) tears are one of the most common adult shoulder conditions, causing severe pain, muscle weakness, and loss of movement. Prevalence increases significantly with age, affecting more than half of those over the age of 50 years, and 80% of older adults, aged over 80 years.1) Surgical procedures generally involve repairing the torn tendon to its original attachment site for primary repair. In cases of large and/or irreparable rupture, allogeneic or synthetic grafts are used to reinforce the tendon.2) Despite successful surgical interventions, muscle and tendon pathophysiology can cause pain, muscle weakness, shoulder dysfunction, limited range of motion, and impaired tendon-bone interface (TBI) healing.34) Additionally, because RC tendons have a limited ability to self-repair, secondary complications and re-rupture rates are still unacceptably high.56) Therapeutic management to prevent muscle and tendon degeneration and improve healing after suture repair in large tendon ruptures is, thus, essential.

After RC repair, patients typically manage their pain by using oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and acetaminophen (APAP), while those with severe tears may also be given opioids.78) Pain management is important for patient rehabilitation and improvement of the initial range of motion.9) It is also very important for controlling inflammation for tendon regeneration.10) Previous clinical studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of NSAIDs following orthopedic surgery. In particular, NSAIDs reduced pain in the first week after arthroscopic RC repair and lowered opioid requirements.11) Conversely, studies that examined RC tendon-bone healing suggest that preoperative and postoperative NSAIDs can slow healing, reduce collagen formation, and decrease load to failure.121314) For these reasons, most shoulder surgeons are wary of the use of NSAIDs after RC reconstruction.15) Unlike NSAIDs, which are cyclooxygenase enzyme inhibitors, APAP relieves pain by inhibiting the synthesis of prostaglandins in the hypothalamus.16) APAP is indicated for patients who cannot tolerate NSAIDs due to gastrointestinal problems, such as ulcers or bleeding risks. For this reason, APAP is often used as a first-line option for managing mild to moderate pain in orthopedics. Compared to NSAIDs, APAP’s effects on RC tendon healing remain unclear. In particular, little is known about its effects on muscle fatty infiltration after RC repair.

Muscle degeneration and fatty infiltration are significant concerns after RC surgery, as they can negatively impact functional recovery and increase the risk of rerupture. While NSAIDs are commonly used postoperatively, their potential to impair tendon healing has raised concerns regarding their widespread use. Given these challenges, alternative pain management strategies that do not compromise tissue healing are of particular interest. APAP, often considered a safer alternative to NSAIDs, has primarily been used for pain control; however, its potential effects on muscle quality and tendon healing remain unclear. Recent studies have highlighted the role of inflammatory signaling and adipogenic transcription factors, particularly peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ), in promoting fatty infiltration after tendon injury.3) Beyond its analgesic role, APAP has also demonstrated anti-inflammatory properties, including modulation of cytokine signaling and reduction of oxidative stress.

Optimizing anti-inflammatory analgesics before and after RC surgery is crucial for effective pain management while preserving tissue healing. Given the limitations of NSAIDs and the potential benefits of APAP, further investigation into its effects on muscle and tendon healing is warranted. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of oral APAP administration on preventing muscle fatty infiltration and preserving TBI healing following RC repair. We hypothesized that APAP would suppress fatty infiltration by downregulating PPAR-γ and preserve TBI integrity without compromising mechanical strength.

METHODS

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Kyungpook National University (IACUC No. KNU 2024-0202). And the animals were obtained from Hana Biotech for medical research.

Animals

Twelve-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats were obtained from Hana Biotech (Business registration No. 552-87-01234) for medical research. The temperature of the breeding facility was maintained at 22 ℃ ± 2 ℃, and unlimited food and water were provided. Light and darkness were set to 12 hours/12 hours. Power analysis calculated 5 animals per group based on measurements of fatty infiltration in previous studies (2 groups: mean difference 2,831 µm2, standard deviation 612.5 µm2, α error = 0.05, β error = 0.2, two-tailed).17) To check for unexpected variability, a total of 20 rats were randomly divided into 2 groups (Fig. 1). To ensure consistency in interpretation, this study utilized an acute RC tear model, which reflects the early-phase healing response following tendon injury. Four weeks after tendon repair surgery, all rats were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and tendon-bone samples were collected from both shoulders. No individual animal died prior to sample collection.

Surgical Procedures

Anesthesia was induced in the animals by inhalation of 5% isoflurane in an induction room with oxygen supplied at 4 L/min. To maintain anesthesia, 2% isoflurane was continuously injected through the nose cone during surgery. Sterile conditions were maintained by disinfecting the shoulder area before the incision. The scapula was identified and a 3-cm long skin incision was made. The deltoid muscle was separated, and the supraspinatus muscle was exposed (Fig. 2A and B). After separating the exposed deltoid muscle with a Metzenbaum, the blade was used to completely cut the tendon at the tendon-bone region (Fig. 2C). After cutting the tendon, the tendon was sutured to the greater tuberosity footprint in 2 rows with nonabsorbable poly(ethylene terephthalate) sutures (Ethibond, Ethicon) (Fig. 2D). After tendon repair, oral APAP (Tylenol; Janssen Korea) was administered daily at a dose of 30 mg/kg/day for 4 weeks. All the rats were sacrificed, and the supraspinatus tendons were harvested 4 weeks after RC tenotomy surgery.

Oil Red O Staining

Adipocytes and triglycerides were histologically visualized using Oil Red O staining. Collected proximal supraspinatus samples were frozen at −80 ℃, and the frozen samples were sectioned at 8-mm thickness for Oil Red O staining (MAK194, Sigma-Aldrich) and then counter-staining with hematoxylin. Subsequently, the samples were dyed with silane. The slide specimens were qualitatively evaluated using light microscopy for fatty infiltration, and the percentage area of fatty infiltration and muscle fiber cross-sectional area were quantified using ImageJ software (public domain JAVA image processing program, National Institute of Health).17) Images were captured at ×100 magnification, and the entire stained area (range of interests [ROIs]) in the original images was quantitatively analyzed to ensure consistent evaluation. ROIs were defined as the red-stained area (Fig. 3A and B), excluding muscle fiber bundles and inter-fiber voids from the total image area, and were calculated using ImageJ software. Fatty infiltration was measured as a percentage of the total muscle area in each ROI, and the final value was derived from the mean of all evaluated ROIs. The evaluation was independently conducted by 2 authors (JPY and SJP) and intraclass correlation analysis was performed to assess evaluation consistency.

Histological Evaluation

Tendon-bone tissues were harvested and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson’s trichrome, and picrosirius red to evaluate histological healing. Briefly, the general staining protocol was as follows. Harvested tendon-bone tissues were placed in neutral buffered 10% formalin (pH 7.4) and paraffin blocks were prepared. Paraffin blocks were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with H&E, Masson’s trichrome, and picrosirius red, respectively.18) Immunohistochemistry was used for qualitative and quantitative analysis of PPAR-γ protein. For quantitative analysis of collagen alignment in TBI, we used the collagen alignment index (CAI) as a previously established calculation method.19) Image analysis was performed under a microscope (Leica DM IL LED, Leica Microsystems) using the LAS V4.8 (Leica Microsystems) imaging system. The quantification of TBI healing was performed in a blinded manner by 2 authors with more than 7 years of experience (JPY and SJP) using the Bonar score method.20)

Biomechanical Evaluation

Four weeks after tendon repair surgery, the supraspinatus tendon-bone portions of the right shoulders were sharply separated. Biomechanical analysis procedures were performed according to previous studies.20) Briefly, the cross-sectional area and width of the middle part of the supraspinatus tendon were measured using a Digimatic Micrometer (MDC-25SB, Mitutoyo). Tendon-bone samples were placed on a universal testing machine (OTT-03, Oriental TM) and fixed along the anatomical direction (Fig. 4). The type of damage (insertion fracture or intermediate material breakage) was mechanically measured using a 20-kg load cell. The failure and ultimate stress parameters were measured at a load rate of 10 mm/min. In this study, interstitial rupture suggests relatively weak TBI healing, whereas mid-substance rupture suggests strong TBI healing.

Statistical Analysis

Data were statistically analyzed using SPSS Statistics version 12.0 (SPSS Inc.). Independent t-tests were performed to evaluate the quantitative comparison of supraspinatus fatty infiltration and PPAR-γ protein contents, Bonar score, and biomechanical measurements between the 2 groups. Data were expressed as mean and standard deviation. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

APAP Prevented Supraspinatus Fatty Infiltration through Downregulation of PPAR-γ

An acute RC repair rat model to verify the effects of APAP was created with the surgical procedure shown in Fig. 2. Fatty infiltration was analyzed qualitatively and quantitatively using Oil Red staining in the supraspinatus muscle samples 4 weeks after tendon repair. In Fig. 3B, the APAP group had an overall more homogeneous muscle fiber arrangement and narrower interfiber voids compared to the control group. Adipose tissue formed in the interfiber space was significantly higher in the control group (Fig. 3A and B). The quantitative analysis results showed that fatty infiltration in the APAP group (0.27% ± 0.35%, p < 0.001) was suppressed by approximately 6-fold compared to the control group (4.75% ± 0.96%) (Fig. 3C). Qualitative and quantitative analysis of PPAR-γ content, a marker of muscle fat infiltration, showed that it was downregulated in the APAP group (0.76% ± 0.14%, p < 0.001) compared to the control group (3.22% ± 0.54%) (Fig. 3D–F). These results suggest that oral APAP administration after RC repair surgery may prevent supraspinatus fatty infiltration.

APAP Does Not Affect Histological Healing of TBI

H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining were performed to determine the oral APAP-induced structural and histological changes in the tendon-bone tissues. Fig. 5 shows samples after H&E staining. Overall, there were no differences in TBI porosity and neovascularization in the APAP group compared to the control group. Considering the Bonar scores, cell morphology (APAP: 2.00 ± 0.00, control: 2.30 ± 0.46, p = 0.081), vascularity (APAP: 1.10 ± 0.54, control: 0.80 ± 0.60, p = 0.279), collagen density (APAP: 1.50 ± 0.67, control: 1.20 ± 0.87, p = 0.424), collagen arrangement (APAP: 1.20 ± 0.87, control: 1.00 ± 0.63, p = 0.584), cellularity (APAP: 1.30 ± 0.64, control: 1.50 ± 0.50, p = 0.470), and total score (APAP: 7.10 ± 1.51, control: 6.80 ± 1.07, p = 0.634) did not differ significantly (Table 1). Additionally, in the qualitative and quantitative analysis of unidirectional CAI, which is a major factor in tensile strength in TBI healing, no significant difference was observed in the APAP group (0.65 ± 0.20) compared to the control group (0.59 ± 0.23) (p = 0.737) (Fig. 6). Taken together, systemic APAP use did not adversely affect TBI healing histologically.

APAP Is Not Detrimental to TBI Mechanical Strength after RC Repair

To improve TBI biomechanical healing after RC repair, tendon-bone samples were loaded into a universal testing machine, and their mechanical strength was measured (Fig. 7). In the biomechanical analysis of TBI healing, there were no significant differences in the cross-sectional area (APAP: 6.29 ± 3.45 mm2, control: 4.34 ± 1.83 mm2, p = 0.240), load to failure (APAP: 0.70 ± 0.24 kgf, control: 0.75 ± 0.14 kgf, p = 0.592), ultimate strength (APAP: 1.30 ± 0.70 MPa, control: 1.72 ± 0.54 MPa, p = 0.217), and elongation (APAP: 44.60% ± 7.43%, control: 40.91% ± 11.26%, p = 0.474) between the 2 groups (Fig. 7). Our results showed that the use of APAP after tendon repair did not negatively affect TBI healing biomechanically.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we investigated the effects of APAP on fatty infiltration and TBI healing following RC surgery. Our findings demonstrated that APAP administration effectively prevented supraspinatus fatty infiltration. This effect was associated with a significant downregulation of PPAR-γ, a key transcription factor involved in adipocyte differentiation. Additionally, APAP administration did not negatively affect histological or biomechanical TBI healing. These results suggest that APAP may have potential therapeutic benefits in reducing fatty infiltration while preserving tendon integrity in RC surgery.

In the biology of RC healing, TBI regeneration after an injury does not restore the original structure and function, and healing occurs due to disorganized fibrous scar tissues.21) As a result, even after successful arthroscopic repair, the RC tendon is prone to secondary complications and the re-rupture rate is high.222324) Initially, upon tendon injury, immune cells, such as basophils, monocyte, and macrophages, are recruited to the damaged area. The 3 tissue regeneration stages are regulated by various signals, such as pro-inflammatory cytokines, released by the immune cells at the site of injury.3) Controlling inflammation during tendon tissue regeneration has a significant impact on improving muscle degeneration and TBI healing. Moreover, anti-inflammatory analgesics taken for pain relief can affect muscle degeneration and TBI healing during this process.23) However, the effectiveness of NSAIDs is controversial, with conflicting results among available studies. Additionally, the effects of APAP are unknown. In the current study, we found that systemic APAP administration prevented supraspinatus fatty infiltration and had no adverse effect on TBI healing. Our results strongly support the use of APAP for pain relief and inflammation control after RC tendon repair.

Massive RC tendon rupture induces inflammation in the connected supraspinatus muscle, and this inflammatory response influences the degree of muscle fatty infiltration and intracellular changes.25) Quality deterioration due to muscle degeneration associated with tendon rupture is also an important factor in determining secondary complications and re-rupture after tendon repair.26) Our findings demonstrate that APAP significantly reduces supraspinatus fatty infiltration, as confirmed by histological and molecular analyses. Importantly, PPAR-γ expression, a key regulator of adipogenesis, was markedly downregulated in the APAP-treated group, suggesting that APAP’s effects extend beyond simple pain relief. Mechanistically, fatty infiltration after RC injury is driven by a complex interplay of muscle atrophy, inflammatory cytokines, and adipogenic signaling pathways. Previous studies have identified PPAR-γ as a central transcription factor regulating adipocyte differentiation and lipid accumulation, and its overexpression is strongly correlated with increased fatty infiltration in RC muscle.17) The inhibition of PPAR-γ has been shown to prevent fatty infiltration and improve muscle quality after tendon repair. Our results align with these findings, demonstrating that APAP treatment led to a significant reduction in PPAR-γ expression, thereby mitigating fatty infiltration. In addition to its known analgesic properties, APAP has been reported to modulate inflammatory signaling, particularly by influencing tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin-6 pathways, both of which contribute to muscle degeneration and adipogenic differentiation.2227) Given these effects, we propose that APAP’s ability to prevent fatty infiltration may be linked to its role in reducing inflammatory cytokine activity, thereby indirectly suppressing PPAR-γ expression and subsequent adipocyte formation in the supraspinatus muscle. There are currently no approved drugs effective in inhibiting RC fatty infiltration, and drugs such as thiazole, an Food and Drug Administration approved drug, are known to cause side effects when used chronically.24) The identification of medications for muscle and tendon health and postoperative pain management remain important to surgeons. Our study suggests that oral APAP may be an effective treatment to manage postoperative pain and inhibit fatty infiltration through controlling supraspinatus inflammation after RC repair.

Compared to NSAIDs, studies on the impact of APAP on TBI healing are few. Caro et al.28) examined whether analgesics affect TBI healing in an RC rat model study. Their results showed poor tendon biomechanical properties in the NSAIDs group compared to the control group, whereas APAP showed no significant difference. Although the results were confusing because the animal study used methods that did not ensure exact quantitative drug administration, it was the first study to report the safe use of APAP for TBI healing. In our study, our observers administered the accurately quantified drug orally every day for 4 weeks. In this respect, our results present a more reliable finding that APAP does not histologically impair TBI healing. Collagen density and arrangement are important for dispersion of tensile strength in tendon regeneration.22) Low collagen density and uneven arrangement are also key characteristics of fibrous scar tissue, which cannot withstand sufficient tensile strength. APAP not only did not damage collagen density and arrangement during tendon regeneration, but also had no adverse effects on cell activity-related factors such as cell morphology and angiogenesis. In summary, NSAIDs after RC surgery may impair TBI healing, while APAP suppresses supraspinatus fatty infiltration and does not adversely affect TBI healing.

In the United States alone, approximately 400,000 to 500,000 arthroscopic RC repairs are performed each year. The standard treatment includes prescribing postoperative opioids. Prescribing protocols that reduce the use and potential abuse of narcotic drugs remain important.29) In response to opioid concerns, various forms of pain therapy are being studied, such as multiple non-opioid drug therapies, including APAP.30) Of course, complete elimination of opioids after RC surgery is impossible, but non-opioids that provide at least equal or better pain relief and do not affect RC tendon regeneration are attractive options. Results from this current study support the use of oral APAP after RC surgery as an effective analgesic and therapeutic agent. The current study has several limitations. First, no additional analgesics were used in the control group, ensuring that the observed effects were due to APAP. While pain relief may influence recovery, fatty infiltration is primarily driven by inflammatory signaling rather than analgesia alone. APAP significantly downregulated PPAR-γ (p < 0.001), suggesting its effect was biological rather than solely pain-related. However, the interaction between pain control and tendon-to-bone healing requires further investigation. Second, we did not evaluate the effect of oral APAP on improving shoulder functions other than its effect in preventing fatty infiltration of shoulder muscles. The effect on shoulder functions is an important outcome variable to consider when evaluating drug effectiveness in clinical practice. Future studies investigating whether the inhibitory effect of APAP on fatty infiltration affects shoulder range of motion after RC rupture are warranted. Third, the use of an acute RC injury model may have favored healing and may not fully replicate the extent of fatty infiltration observed in chronic degenerative RC tears. However, previous studies have shown that fatty infiltration can occur even in acute models, and our results confirmed significant fatty accumulation in the control group. Additionally, the underlying biological mechanisms of fatty infiltration, including PPAR-γ activation and inflammatory cytokine signaling, are consistent across acute and chronic models, making our findings relevant to postoperative healing. Future studies will employ a chronic RC injury model with preexisting fatty infiltration to further validate the long-term effects of APAP on muscle quality and tendon healing. Despite these limitations, the current study is the first to report the effectiveness of APAP in preventing fatty infiltration and improving TBI healing.

As a result, the current study investigated the effect of orally administered APAP on reduced supraspinatus fatty infiltration and TBI healing in a rat model of acute RC rupture. The results showed that APAP effectively prevented supraspinatus fatty infiltration. Additionally, oral APAP administration is safe for histological and biomechanical TBI healing. APAP may be an effective alternative for managing pain and preventing muscle fatty infiltration after RC repair.
