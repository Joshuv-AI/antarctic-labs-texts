Managing Atrophic Thin Crest by Ridge Splitting Technique before Implant Placement
Authors: Nada Kriouach, Samir Erraji, Zouheir Ismaili
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Critical alveolar ridge defects can result from several causes. Ridge expansion is a technique that aims to increase the width of the bone crest. A male patient presented an edentulous segment which leads to be careful concerning the adjacent teeth. Through this case report, the used technique was explained with all steps and was combined with guided bone regeneration (GBR). The particularity of this case was the membrane exposure during the healing period before placing the crowns, resulting in bone resorption. This resorption was stable after restauration and follow-up. Ridge split is indicated in cases where the width of the ridge is at least 3.5 mm. The procedure demonstrated a safe and predictable alternative for implant placement in a knife-edge ridge, with a high survival rate of ridge expansion. The patient presented good healing at the follow-up with a little resorption that remained stable in time after crown placing.

Introduction

Critical alveolar ridge defects can result from dental loss, fractures, and pathological processes. After tooth loss, resorption is maximal within 3–6 months after extraction and then continues for up to a year. It is greater in the vestibular bone than the lingual bone; the healing of the socket results in a finer ridge offset lingually.[1]

Tan et al. conducted a systematic review to assess the magnitude of dimensional changes of both the hard and soft tissues of the alveolar ridge following tooth extraction in humans which is important for decision-making and comprehensive treatment planning. They found that in human hard tissue, the percentage of vertical dimensional change was 11%–22% at 6 months. The percentage of horizontal dimensional change was 29%–63% at 6–7 months. Soft-tissue changes demonstrated a 0.4–0.5 mm gain of thickness at 6 months on the buccal and lingual aspects.[2]

To replace avulsed teeth by implants, it is so important to evaluate the buccolingual dimension; the minimum of bone thickness in buccal and lingual is 1 mm for the two sides, i.e., to say, it must be at least 1 mm of bone around the implant. In case of insufficient crest, numerous techniques could be used: guided bone regeneration (GBR) “GBR,” autogenous bone transplant “autograft,” or ridge expansion.[3]

Ridge expansion or crest splitting is a technique that aims to increase the width of the bone crest and has not had any effect on its coronoapical dimension. This augmentation technique is applicable to both the maxilla and mandible,[4] but it is mainly performed in the anterior maxillary sector.[5]

The aim of this case report is to demonstrate how ridge expansion could be useful in managing atrophic thin crest to restore function.

Case Report

A 45-year-old male patient presented at the clinic with a partially edentulous condition on the lower left side arch. The patient’s medical history evaluation revealed no significant personal or family medical conditions or systemic issues. Furthermore, there were no reported instances of ongoing medication use that might impact the treatment plan.

Relevant past interventions with outcomes: None.

Following clinical and radiographic evaluations [Figure 3], the patient presented with a narrow ridge, requiring a comprehensive evaluation of bone augmentation techniques to support the implant. The patient was informed of his case and the available therapeutic options.

Discussion

Bone expansion is a reliable and minimally invasive technique and is considered an alternative to conventional methods: GBR and block bone grafting (BBG). It is difficult to predict the final thickness achievable with this technique; it may vary from 2 to 5 mm. In fact, it depends on numerous factors such as ridge width, use of biomaterials for filling, maxillary/mandibular arch, flap thickness, and instrumentation.

Due to its viscoelastic nature, thinner ridges (<3.5 mm) can be expanded with better-controlled instrumentation, resulting in less risk of fracture, trauma, and bone perforations. Therefore, bone expansion is easier and more predictable when the bone is less dense.[8]

The initial thickness of the edentulous ridge and the degree of resorption are important. According to Beolchini et al. 2015, the vestibular wall thickness will affect transverse augmentation.[9] In very thin ridges with a thickness of 3–4 mm, some authors recommend filling the surgical site. The nonuse of the filling biomaterial requires simultaneous placement of the implant that will act as a space maintainer. According to Stricker et al. 2014, the vestibular bone wall thickness is greater after expansion associated with GBR than after expansion alone.[10]

For the instrumentation, the practitioner can use piezosurgery or rotary instruments. The piezosurgery offers advantages such as a better visibility and no risk of overheating or injury to the surrounding soft structures. However, its major disadvantage is the increased operating time. As for the rotary instruments, they have the advantage of having a linearity of the cutting line, which is efficient as well as reduced operating time. However, there are also some disadvantages associated with their use such as the risk of trauma to the surrounding soft tissue, generation of heat, and difficulty of use in cases where adjacent teeth are present, as well as a risk of slipping.[11]

This technique has been compared with two other techniques in terms of transverse bone augmentation: GBR and BBG, the first generally allows an average of bone gain between 2 and 4.5 mm, and the second has an advantage of the osteogenic potential but has also some disadvantages such as second operative site, limited volume of harvesting, and requiring an experienced practitioner. This technique allows an average bone gain of 4–6 mm.[12]

This technique presents some complications:

The fracture of the alveolar bone represents the main complication that can occur at the time of the separation of the ridge, preparation of the implant site, and implant placement. To prevent this complication, it is necessary to use a gradual sequence of instruments and pay attention to bone density[13]

Conclusion

There are several methods for augmentation of implant placement in deficient alveolar ridges, of which ridge split is indicated in cases where the ridge width exceeds 3.5 mm. The procedure demonstrated a safe and predictable alternative for implant placement in a knife-edge ridge, with a high survival rate of ridge expansion.

The most crucial factor for successful ridge split cases is careful patient selection and bone evaluation. The site generally heals similarly to bone fracture repair, where the gap is filled by a clot that organizes over time and is eventually replaced by woven bone and later by load-bearing lamellar bone at the interphase.

Therefore, to achieve the ideal goals of implant dentistry, augmentation of deficient alveolar ridges is an important aspect of dental implant therapy. The ultimate goal is to provide a functional restoration that is in harmony with the adjacent natural dentition, as demonstrated in this case report. The use of transverse ridge expansion allows to avoid more costly/heavier procedures such as bone grafting when the conditions are met.
