Bio-inspired synthesis of silver selenide (Ag₂Se) binary chalcogenide nanoparticles mediated by Punica granatum L. peel extract and a comprehensive evaluation of their biological activities
Authors: Siddharth Satpathy, Pallavi Samal, Arun Kumar Pradhan, Manoranjan Arakha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The growing threat of antibiotic-resistant bacteria calls for the urgent development of novel antimicrobial agents. Binary chalcogenide nanoparticles, particularly Ag2Se nanoparticles, have emerged as promising candidates due to their enhanced antibacterial properties, biocompatibility and multifunctional applications. This study presents an eco-friendly synthesis of Ag2Se nanoparticles using pomegranate (Punica granatum L.) peel extract, leveraging its rich polyphenols and flavonoids as natural reducing and stabilizing agents. The synthesized nanoparticles were characterised using UV-Vis spectroscopy, FTIR, XRD and FE-SEM, confirming successful synthesis, different bonds and functional groups and their structural and morphological properties. The average crystalline size of Ag2Se nanoparticles determined to be 11.52 nm. DLS and zeta potential analysis revealed that Ag2Se nanoparticles of size 282.1 nm were stable in nature. Antimicrobial assays demonstrated potent activity against both Gram-positive (Staphylococcus aureus with 100 µg/mL MIC, Staphylococcus epidermidis with 250 µg/mL MIC) and Gram-negative (Escherichia coli with 100 µg/mL MIC, Acinetobacter baumannii with 250 µg/mL MIC) bacteria, achieving upto ~ 95%-100% inhibition at 500 µg/mL with significant membrane disruption observed through cytoplasmic leakage and SEM analysis. Additionally, the Ag2Se nanoparticles exhibited notable antioxidant activity in DPPH with an IC50 of 142.6 µg/mL, and H2O2 assays with 158.3 µg/mL, alongside < 5% hemolysis at 25 µg/mL concentration. These findings highlight the dual functionality of Ag2Se nanoparticles as effective antimicrobial and antioxidant agents, offering sustainable solutions for biomedical applications such as wound healing, antimicrobial coatings and drug delivery systems. This green synthesis approach aligns with global efforts towards eco-friendly nanotechnology.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is emerging more rapidly than the discovery of novel antibiotics, posing a major challenge to the clinical management of crucial pathogens such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus1. This issue is especially noticeable in biofilm-associated infections, where restricted drug penetration and stress-adapted phenotypes significantly diminish therapeutic efficacy2,3. The rise of antibiotic-resistant bacteria poses a significant threat to global health, necessitating the urgent development of novel antimicrobial agents4. Among the promising solutions, binary chalcogenide nanoparticles have gained attention due to their enhanced antibacterial properties, biocompatibility and multifunctional applications5. Noble metal nanostructured chalcogenides exhibit distinctive characteristics arising from variations in particle size and morphology, and have garnered significant interest among nanomaterials researchers owing to their enhanced physical, chemical, and thermal properties, along with a size-dependent band gap6. Silver selenide (Ag2Se), also known as naumannite, is a fascinating I-VI group semiconductor material, typically found in bulk form but rarely as a natural mineral. Among diverse semiconductor materials, Ag2Se nanoparticles stand out for their physicochemical and biological properties, making them highly promising for biomedical and therapeutic applications. Due to its versatile applications in electronics and biomedicine, Ag2Se is a highly valued chalcogenide nanomaterial. It also acts as a mixed ionic conductor, undergoing a phase transition at atmospheric pressure, shifting from a low-temperature orthorhombic phase (β-Ag2Se) to a high-temperature cubic phase (α-Ag2Se)7,8. Additionally, orthorhombic β-Ag2Se is the most widely accepted crystal structure of Ag2Se nanoparticles owing to its relatively high Seebeck coefficient (thermoelectric power, -150 µV K− 1) at 300 K with an unusually low lattice thermal conductivity, coupled with high electrical conductivity9. The study proclaimed that the orthorhombic structure of nanoparticles exhibited both photocatalytic and fluorescence activity. Moreover, such a compound has been utilised as a photosensitizer in photographic films, thermo-chromic materials for nonlinear optical devices and photovoltaic cells10. Ag2Se nanoparticles are typically synthesized using various methods such as chemical conversion, hydrothermal reactions and hot injection. In contrast, microstructured Ag2Se can be fabricated via electrodeposition and thermal evaporation techniques11. Such traditional synthesis methods for nanoparticles often involve toxic chemicals, high energy consumption, and environmental hazards11. In contrast, green synthesis using plant extracts as reducing and stabilizing agents offers an eco-friendly, cost-effective and sustainable alternative12. Pomegranate (Punica granatum L.) peel, a rich source of polyphenols, flavonoids and other bioactive compounds, serves as an excellent natural medium for nanoparticle synthesis. These biomolecules not only facilitate the reduction of metal ions but also enhance the stability and biological activity of the resulting nanoparticles. This study explores the green synthesis of Ag2Se nanoparticles using pomegranate peel extract, followed by a comprehensive evaluation of their biological activities. The nanoparticles were characterized for their structural and morphological properties, and their antimicrobial efficacy was evaluated against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Additionally, their antioxidant potential, and hemocompatibility were assessed to determine their suitability for biomedical applications. Although Ag2Se nanoparticles have been synthesized previously using conventional chemical routes and evaluated for selected applications, most reports rely on toxic reagents and high-energy processes. Plant-mediated approach using pomegranate peel extract, which not only provides a green synthesis route but also enhances biocompatibility through natural phytochemical capping. Hence, we present a comprehensive evaluation of the biological activities of these nanoparticles, including antimicrobial efficacy against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, antioxidant potential, and hemocompatibility. This dual functionality, combined with eco-friendly synthesis, underscores the originality of our work and its contribution to advancing biomedical applications of chalcogenide nanomaterials. By leveraging the natural reducing power of pomegranate peel, this research aims to develop a sustainable, non-toxic, and highly effective nanomaterial with dual functionality, combating microbial infections while mitigating oxidative stress.

Experimental

High-purity laboratory-grade chemical reagents were used in this study. Sodium selenate anhydrous (Na2SeO4) and nutrient broth were obtained from Central Drug House (CDH) Pvt, Ltd., New Delhi. Bacterial strains, including Acinetobacter baumannii (MTCC 1245), Staphylococcus aureus (MTCC 96), Staphylococcus epidermidis (MTCC 435) and Escherichia coli (MTCC 443) were obtained from the Institute of Microbial Technology (IMTECH), Chandigarh, India. 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) and ascorbic acid (Vitamin C) were purchased from SRL Pvt. Ltd, Mumbai, India. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was purchased from Molychem Pvt. Ltd. Fresh pomegranate (Punica granatum L.) fruits were procured from a local market in Bhubaneswar, Odisha, India. All experiments were conducted using deionized water.

Extraction of Punica granatum L. peels

Fresh pomegranate (Punica granatum L.) fruits were thoroughly washed with running water to remove surface contaminants. The outer peel was carefully separated, chopped into small pieces and gently dried using tissue paper. The chopped peels were then sun-dried for a week to ensure complete moisture removal. The drying process was carried out at an average temperature of 32 ± 2 °C and relative humidity of 55–65%, as monitored throughout the drying period. These parameters were maintained consistently to minimize environmental variability, which can influence phytochemical composition. Subsequently, the dried peels were finely ground using a mechanical grinder. For phytochemical quantification, the PPE extract was subsequently evaluated for total phenolic content (TPC) and total flavonoid content (TFC).

Determination of total phenolic content (TPC)

To evaluate the TPC of the Pomegranate peel extract (PPE), we followed the Folin-Ciocalteu method, using gallic acid as a standard, and adopted the protocol outlined by Martins et al. with some modifications13. The aqueous PPE extract with different concentrations (10, 25, 50, 75, 100 ppm) was mixed with Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and the mixture was incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Absorbance readings at 760 nm were compared with the gallic acid standard curve to determine total phenolic content. The results were interpreted as mg GAE/g of sample.

Determination of total flavonoid content (TFC)

Aluminium trichloride (AlCl3) colourimetric method is a widely accepted procedure for the quantitative assay of flavonoids in plant extracts, based on the formation of stable complexes between AlCl3 and specific groups in flavonoid molecules. Quercetin served as the calibration standard in this assay. Ethanolic PPE was filtered and diluted appropriately before use. Typically, 0.5–0.6 mL of different concentrations (10, 25, 50, 75, 100 PPM) of PPE extract was mixed with 0.6 mL of 2% AlCl3 solution. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 20–60 min to maintain the absorbance. The absorbance was measured at 420 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer, with the calibration curve prepared from quercetin. Results were interpreted in mg QE/g of sample.

Biosynthesis of chalcogenide nanoparticles

We synthesized the biogenic chalcogenide nanoparticles following the protocol outlined by Sibiya et al. with some modifications14. Pomegranate peel extract was added with distilled water at a 1:100 (w/v) ratio. In brief, the peel extract was added to distilled water to a concentration of 10 mg/mL. The solution was homogenized using a stirrer for 15 min. The resultant mixture was heated to 60 °C prior to centrifugation. After that, the solution was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 20 min. The obtained supernatant was filtered using Whatman filter paper. To 50 mL of filtered plant extract solution, 1 mM silver nitrate (AgNO3) was added, and the reaction mixture was incubated at 120 rpm for 48 h. Post-incubation, 50 mL of filtered plant extract solution was mixed with AgNPs solution, and the total volume was made up to 100 mL. In the first step, AgNPs were synthesized using pomegranate peel extract, where polyphenols and flavonoids acted as natural reducing and stabilizing agents. In the second step, the AgNPs were re-exposed to fresh extract to provide additional reducing equivalents. This strongly reducing and stabilizing environment facilitated the gradual reduction of selenate (Se6+ to Se2-) and promoted Ag-Se bond formation15. The presence of pre-formed AgNPs served as nucleation centers for Ag2Se growth, while biomolecules in the extract prevented uncontrolled aggregation. Thus, the two-step approach ensured efficient reduction of selenium precursor, stabilization of the chalcogenide nanoparticles and improved uniformity and biocompatibility compared to a single-step reaction. Then, 25 mM of a selenium precursor (sodium selenate) was added to 100 mL of the AgNPs and plant extract solution, followed by incubation at 120 rpm for 24 h. The reduction of selenate (Se6+) to selenide (Se2-) is not a direct one-step process mediated solely by plant phytochemicals; rather, a strong reducing environment is required. Instead, phytochemicals present in pomegranate peel extract facilitate a gradual reduction through intermediate states such as Se4+, while the simultaneous presence of Ag+ ions promotes stabilization of Se2- via Ag-Se bond formation. The synergistic mechanism ultimately drives the precipitation of Ag2Se nanoparticles, consistent with previous reports of biogenic selenium reduction16. Post-incubation, a clear colour change in the reaction mixture was observed, indicating the successful formation of silver selenide binary chalcogenide nanoparticles. The biosynthesis was conducted under alkaline pH conditions, which were measured at 8.2. No external pH adjustment was applied, as the study aimed to demonstrate an eco-friendly synthesis under ambient conditions. The nanoparticle solution was further centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 20 min. The obtained pellet was washed with distilled water and dried at 70 °C to obtain powdered nanoparticles.

Characterization of chalcogenide nanoparticles

The absorbance of Ag2Se nanoparticles was studied employing a UV-vis spectrophotometer (HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan). Bond-level analysis was performed using an FTIR spectrophotometer (JASCO, Japan). The hydrodynamic size of colloidal Ag2Se nanoparticles was measured by DLS (Malvern Panalytical Ltd.). The surface potential of colloidal Ag2Se nanoparticles was measured by a zeta sizer (Malvern Panalytical Ltd.). XRD of Ag2Se nanoparticles was achieved by Rigaku X-ray Diffractometer (Tokyo, Japan), applying Cu-Kα radiation with an angular range of 200 to 800. The availability of various phases in the formulated nanoparticles was determined using the X’Pert HighScore software. Morphological analysis was estimated via FE-SEM (JEOL JSM-IT800).

Evaluation of antioxidant activity of chalcogenide nanoparticles

DPPH assay

The DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) assay was used to evaluate the antioxidant activity of biogenic Ag2Se nanoparticles. Precisely, 0.2 mM DPPH was dissolved in methanol to prepare the stock solution. Here, ascorbic acid (Vitamin C) was considered as a positive control. A 96-well microtiter plate containing 100 µl of different concentrations of Ag2Se nanoparticles (50, 100, 250 µg/mL) and 100 µl of DPPH methanolic solution was incubated in a dark environment with constant shaking at 35 °C for 30 min. Post-incubation, absorbance was monitored at 517 nm employing a Biorad Imark microplate reader. Later, the scavenging activity was evaluated by the formula.

Radical scavenging activity (%) = (Acontrol – Asample / Acontrol) x 100.

To correct for background absorbance caused by the nanoparticles themselves, parallel control wells containing Ag2Se nanoparticles without radical substrates were included. The absorbance values from these nanoparticle-only controls were subtracted from the experimental readings before calculating scavenging activity. The result was obtained by plotting a graph of % DPPH scavenging versus Ag2Se nanoparticle concentration.

H2O2 assay

Antioxidant activity of Ag2Se nanoparticles was also assessed by H2O2 scavenging assay. Precisely, 40 mM of H2O2 solution was prepared in phosphate buffer. A stock solution of Ag2Se nanoparticles was prepared in phosphate buffer.1 mL of different concentrations of Ag2Se nanoparticles (50, 100, 250 µg/mL) was mixed with 1 mL of H2O2 followed by incubation for 10 min at room temperature. Post-incubation, the absorbance was monitored at 230 nm. The percentage of scavenging was evaluated by the formula.

Radical scavenging activity (%) = (Acontrol – Asample / Acontrol) x 100.

To correct for background absorbance caused by the nanoparticles themselves, parallel control wells containing Ag2Se nanoparticles without radical substrates were included. The absorbance values from these nanoparticle-only controls were subtracted from the experimental readings before calculating scavenging activity.The result was obtained by plotting a graph of % H2O2 scavenging versus Ag2Se nanoparticle concentration.

Assessment of antimicrobial activity of chalcogenide nanoparticles

Bacterial growth inhibition

Bacterial growth inhibition in the presence of Ag2Se nanoparticles was evaluated against a panel of Gram-positive bacteria, including S. epidermidis and S. aureus, and Gram-negative bacteria, including E. coli and A. baumannii, using a growth kinetics study. Precisely, a loopful of bacteria was collected from the mother culture and inoculated into the nutrient broth, followed by overnight incubation at 37 °C and 120 rpm. Furthermore, an appropriate amount of Ag2Se nanoparticles was dissolved in deionized water and sonicated for 20 min in order to prepare the stock solution of nanoparticles. Various concentrations of Ag2Se nanoparticles (25, 50, 100, 250, 500 µg/mL) were used in 96-well plates for growth rate evaluation. The reaction mixture without Ag2Se nanoparticles was considered as control. 20 µl of bacterial culture (106 CFU/mL) was dissolved in each reaction mixture, and the total volume was made up to 300 µl using nutrient broth. The Bio-Rad Imark plate reader was used to assess the growth kinetics. Optical density (O.D) was measured at 600 nm.

Evaluation of cytoplasmic leakage activity of chalcogenide nanoparticles

For analysis of leakage of bacterial cytoplasmic contents, such as DNA and proteins, Gram-positive bacteria, such as S. epidermidis, and Gram-negative bacteria, such as E. coli, were cultured and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Later, the microbial culture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The obtained pellet was suspended in PBS buffer (PBS 7.4). The bacterial cell count was set to 105 cells/mL. Cell suspensions were mixed with Ag2Se nanoparticles and were incubated at room temperature for 2, 4 and 6 h. The bacterial culture without nanoparticle treatment was considered as control. After that, the cultures were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min. The obtained supernatant was measured at 260 nm for DNA leakage and at 280 nm for protein leakage.

Evaluation of hemocompatibility of chalcogenide nanoparticles

The present study investigated the hemocompatibility of Ag2Se nanoparticles following the protocol outlined by Pal et al., with minor modifications17. This study is focused on the degree of haemolysis caused by the tested sample. For this purpose, goat blood was collected in EDTA vials to ensure proper anticoagulation and prevent clotting during hemolysis assays. The blood was diluted with PBS at a 4:5 ratio. To validate haemolysis, 0.5 mL of 0.01 N hydrochloric acid (HCl) was added to PBS and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Post-incubation, 0.05 mL of diluted blood was added to the mixture to maintain the final volume at 10 mL. Later, the solution was incubated for 2 h. The optical density (OD) was monitored at 545 nm using a spectrophotometer. Here, HCl was used as a positive control, as it effectively ruptures red blood corpuscles (RBC). Similarly, 0.05 mL of diluted blood was further diluted with 10 mL of PBS. This solution was incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Post-incubation, the solution’s OD was measured at 545 nm. Here, PBS is considered as a negative control because it is known to cause the least RBC rupture. As the standard ODs were estimated, 0.5 mL of the tested sample was added to PBS and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C to maintain temperature equilibrium. Later, 0.05 mL of diluted blood was added to the solution and incubated for an additional 2 h. Post-incubation, the sample OD was monitored at 545 nm. To minimize interference from residual compounds, the pomegranate peel extract was thoroughly centrifuged and filtered prior to nanoparticle synthesis and the resulting Ag2Se nanoparticles were properly washed with deionized water after synthesis. These steps ensured that hemolysis results reflected the intrinsic activity of Ag2Se nanoparticles rather than residual phytochemicals. As per established criteria, less than 5% haemolysis of the test material was preferred to be highly haemocompatible and less than 10% haemolysis was considered to be haemocompatible. Additionally, a qualitative hemocompatibility study was done with the agar well-diffusion method. Precisely, in the autoclaved agar, a certain amount of goat blood was mixed and poured into petriplates, and the plates were kept for solidification. Later, 6–8 mm wells were made on the plates with a sterile cork borer. Different concentrations of Ag2Se nanoparticles were put into the well, and the plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C. Here, HCl was taken as a positive control. Post incubation, inhibition zones were clearly observed.

Evaluation of bacterial morphology in the presence of chalcogenide nanoparticles

Precisely, bacterial morphology study upon treatment with Ag2Se nanoparticles was evaluated following the protocol outlined by Arakha et al., with some modifications18. In brief, a Gram-negative bacterium E. coli was inoculated in nutrient broth and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The next day, the bacterial sample was taken from the stationary phase of growth kinetics and directly put on the cover slide. Later, the tested Ag2Se nanoparticles were mixed with the bacterial culture and also put on another cover slide. Only the bacterial sample on the cover slide was considered as a control. The slides were kept overnight at 37 °C. Post-incubation, the slide surfaces were gently washed with 1X PBS. Then it was soaked on tissue paper. To preserve bacterial cell morphology, cells were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde on the slide surface and incubated for 1 to 2 h at room temperature. The glutaraldehyde was soaked on the tissue paper, followed by washing with 1X PBS. Later, the slides were dehydrated with increasing concentration of ethanol (50%, 70%, 90% and 100%). The fixed, washed and dehydrated cells were now prepared for monitoring the morphological structures of both control and treated samples.

Statistical analysis of data

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results were reported as mean ± SD. The data analysis was done with one-way ANOVA using Minitab software. The statistical analysis was done with Tukey’s multiple comparison test. P ≤ 0.05 was considered significant.

Results and discussion

Evaluation of total phenolic and flavonoid contents of Punica granatum L. peel extract

Biosynthesis of nanoparticles can be attributed to various polyphenolic groups present in the plant extract. In this context, we have evaluated the availability of total phenolic contents in PPE extract using a gallic acid calibration curve and found it to be 95.25 mg GAE/g. Additionally, the solvent type as well as extraction conditions are crucial in the yield of bioactive components and biological activities of the plant materials. In this regard, Hadjadj et al., suggested the TPC value of PPE extract to be 170.5 mg GAE/g19. Additionally, we have also evaluated the total flavonoid content of the PPE extract using a quercetin standard curve and it was found to be 42.28 mg QE/g. In this regard, Yan et al. observed polyphenolic constituents in peels of six pomegranate cultivars and proclaimed that TFC values varied between 45.45 and 84.02 mg RE/g20.

Synthesis and optical charaterization of chalcogenide nanoparticles

The aqueous extract of Punica granatum L. was utilised for the synthesis of Ag2Se nanoparticles from silver nitrate (AgNO3) and sodium selenate (Na2SeO4) as silver and selenium precursors, respectively. The biosynthesis of Ag2Se nanoparticles using pomegranate peel extract can be attributed to the abundance of polyphenolic compounds such as tannins, flavonoids and phenolic acids. These phytochemicals act as both reducing and stabilizing agents during nanoparticle formation. As reducing agents, polyphenols donate electrons to metal precursors, facilitating the reduction of Ag+ and SeO2 into Ag2Se under mild eco-friendly conditions21. As stabilizing/capping agents, the hydroxyl and carboxyl groups of polyphenols bind to the nanoparticle surface, preventing agglomeration and ensuring colloidal stability22. This capping effect also influences particle size, morphology and dispersibility. The dual role of polyphenols thus ensures controlled nucleation and growth, resulting in stable, uniformly distributed nanoparticles without the need for harsh chemical stabilizers. These findings are consistent with earlier reports on plant-mediated nanoparticle synthesis, where polyphenols have been found to provide both reduction and stabilization capacity23. The two-step synthesis strategy was chosen to generate stable AgNPs prior to selenide incorporation. Re-exposure to fresh extract provided additional reducing equivalents and reinforced capping, creating a strongly reducing environment that facilitated selenate reduction and Ag-Se bond formation. This approach improved nanoparticle stability and uniformity compared to a single-step reaction. The discolouration of the reaction mixture might be due to the excitation of the surface plasmon resonance of silver ions in the Ag2Se nanoparticles11. The present findings on biosynthesized Ag2Se nanoparticles using pomegranate peel extract are consistent with, yet distinct from, previously reported Ag2Se nanoparticles synthesized through both green and conventional methods3. The existence of a well-defined absorption band between 300 and 400 nm with a maximum at ~ 359 nm confirmed the successful synthesis of the Ag2Se nanoparticles (Fig. 1a). While this feature is consistent with charge-transfer transitions in Ag-Se systems, it does not provide conclusive evidence of Ag2Se formation, since Ag2Se lacks a distinct plasmonic peak like metallic Ag nanoparticles. UV-Vis spectroscopy alone cannot confirm structural identity. Therefore, the assignment of Ag2Se was validated by XRD (orthorhombic β-Ag2Se phase) and EDAX (Ag and Se elemental composition). The UV-Vis data are thus interpreted as supportive of the optical properties of the nanoparticles. This aligns with Sibiya et al., who reported shape control of Ag2Se nanoparticles using green capping molecules24. Furthermore, our study also aligns with reports from conventional chemical reduction methods, which also demonstrated similar charge transfer transitions. The distinct absorption peak at ~ 359 nm is characteristic of Ag2Se nanostructures rather than individual Ag or Se nanoparticles. Ag nanoparticles typically exhibit surface plasmon resonance around 400–450 nm, while Se nanoparticles show broad absorption in the UV region (250–300 nm). The intermediate position of 359 nm band is arising from charge transfer transitions between Ag+ and Se2− ions within the crystalline lattice. The absence of these features and the presence of a single band at 359 nm confirm the formation of Ag2Se. This band corresponds to excitonic transitions in the orthorhombic β-Ag2Se phase, consistent with earlier reports. Furthermore, FTIR spectra, XRD analysis (naumannite structure), and EDAX elemental composition collectively validate that selenium was reduced to Se2− and incorporated into the Ag2Se lattice. In this range, the single absorption peak offers evidence towards shape, size, as well as stability/aggregation of the nanoparticles25,26. In this context, other studies have demonstrated similar results11,27,28. Moreover, during the synthesis of Ag2Se nanoparticles, initially, the selenium ions are reduced to Se2 followed by co-precipitation with Ag cations. Therefore, persistent reaction time along with the reduction of selenium by the biomolecules shows the existence of a biomolecular matrix as a stabilizer of Ag2Se nanoparticles29,30.

FTIR analysis of chalcogenide nanoparticles

The bond level characterization of biosynthesized binary chalcogenide nanoparticles was performed by FTIR analysis (Fig. 1b). The peaks obtained at 2928 cm− 1, and 2860 cm− 1 can be assigned to C-H stretching vibrations, indicating alkane groups. Such vibrations specifically fall within the range of 2850–3000 cm− 1. Similar results have been demonstrated by Ahmad et al.31 Peak obtained at 2363 cm− 1 likely arises from atmospheric CO2 or from residual plant metabolites. Additionally, the presence of other functional groups involved in the synthesis or capping of the nanoparticles could also contribute to the overall spectrum, potentially influencing the intensity and shape of the peak at 2363 cm− 1. Furthermore, the peaks obtained at 1675 cm− 1, 1631 cm− 1 may be assigned to the stretching vibration of a carbonyl group (C = O). The peak at 1569 cm−1is likely due to C-H bending vibration of the capping agents present on the nanoparticle surface. The FTIR peak at 1451 cm− 1 can be attributed to the bending vibration of the –CH2- group and the distortion of the –CH3 group. In other words, it is associated with the presence of alkanes or other organic molecules containing carbon-hydrogen bonds. Additionally, the peak at 1041 cm-1 is typically attributed to C-O stretching vibrations of ligands or capping agents on the nanoparticle surface. Such a peak can also be indicative of the presence of specific functional groups, such as esters or alcohols. The FTIR peaks at 756 cm-1 and 533 cm -1 are attributed to Ag-Se interactions.

X-ray diffraction analysis of chalcogenide nanoparticles

Study suggested that Ag2Se can exist in various forms, like orthorhombic β-phase or a cubic α-phase11. Kumashiro et al. stated that Ag2Se is a non-stoichiometric compound in both phases and can exist as a single phase32. The XRD pattern of Ag2Se shown different characteristic diffraction peaks observed at different 2θ values such as 27.46o (002), 32.3o (101), 38o (110), 44.08o (020), 64.44o (111) and 77.34o (102), which can be assigned to orthorhombic β-Ag2Se phase with naumannite structure (JCPDS file no. 24-1041)27(Fig. 1c). Also, we have evaluated the average crystalline size of biosynthesized Ag2Se nanoparticles employing Debye Scherrer’s equation: D = Kλ/βcosθ. The crystalline size of the nanoparticles was found to be 11.52 nm. Additionally, other studies reported that Ag2Se nanoparticles synthesized through conventional methods also possess orthorhombic structures27.

Hydrodynamic size analysis of biosynthesized chalcogenide nanoparticles

The hydrodynamic size of Ag2Se nanoparticles was determined by DLS analysis (Fig. 1d). From the graph, the average size of the synthesized Ag2Se nanoparticles was obtained to be 282.1 nm and the PDI (polydispersity index) value was found to be 0.454. Study proclaimed that, plant-mediated synthesis often results in larger hydrodynamic diameter due to adsorption of polyphenols and flavonoids, which enhance colloidal stability but increase the particles size1.

Zeta potential analysis of biosynthesized chalcogenide nanoparticles

Zeta potential analysis was performed to investigate the stability of nanoparticles in a colloidal solution. The biosynthesized Ag2Se nanoparticles revealed a zeta potential value of about − 34.92 mV (Fig. 1e). The magnitude of zeta potential indicates that the synthesized Ag2Se nanoparticles are negatively charged in nature. Subsequently, the Ag2Se nanoparticles were stable in a colloidal solution.

To study the morphology, chemical composition, and purity of the obtained Ag2Se nanoparticles, FESEM-EDX analysis was performed. Morphologically, FESEM images revealed uniformly distributed nanoparticles with minimal agglomeration(Fig. 2a). The EDAX spectrum confirmed the presence of silver and selenium in the synthesized Ag2Se nanoparticles (Fig. 2b). An oxygen peak was also observed, which is expected in plant mediated synthesis and most likely arises from surface oxidation or residual phytochemicals acting as capping agents3. The particle size distribution showed a broad distribution, with an average diameter of 29.06 nm (Fig. 2c).

Antimicrobial activity of chalcogenide nanoparticles

This study systematically evaluated the concentration-dependent antibacterial efficacy of Ag2Se nanoparticles against a panel of Gram-negative bacteria such as A. baumannii, E, coli and Gram-positive bacteria like S. epidermidis, S. aureus etc. (Fig. 3a-d). Bacterial growth kinetics were monitored through optical density (OD600) measurements, revealing distinct patterns of microbial inhibition corresponding to increased nanoparticle concentrations (25–500 µg/mL). The control groups for all tested species exhibited characteristic exponential growth curves, confirming viable bacterial proliferation in the absence of treatment. MIC values were observed for E. coli and S. aureus at 100 µg/mL. This potent bactericidal effect was particularly evident after 6–8 h of exposure, coinciding with the typical log-phase growth period of untreated cultures. In contrast, S. epidermidis and A. baumannii required higher concentrations (250 µg/mL) to achieve significant growth inhibition. Additionally, Ag2Se nanoparticles demonstrated progressively stronger growth suppression at higher doses. At 500 µg/mL, the nanoparticles achieved near-complete inhibition of E. coli and S. aureus (~ 95–100%). While all tested pathogens were susceptible, growth kinetics varied between species. The differential sensitivity patterns may reflect variations in cell wall composition among tested Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria33. In this regard, Ahmad et al. studied the antibacterial activity of Se-Ag nanocomposites and reported that their superior antibacterial activity may be due to the combined antibacterial effects of Ag and Se in the formulated nanocomposite. Here, the Se-Ag composite serves as a large reservoir of biologically active silver, and a slow, sustained release of Ag and Se could be responsible for its better efficacy compared to its individual counterparts34. Additionally, these findings not only confirm the broad-spectrum antibacterial properties of Ag2Se nanoparticles but also highlight their potential for tailored antimicrobial applications. The antimicrobial activity demonstrated in our work, with inhibition percentages and MIC values, is in agreement with the mechanisms described for Ag-based nanomaterials produced by conventional routes, where ROS generation, membrane disruption and DNA/protein interference are key contributors35. The concentration-dependent efficacy, particularly against resilient pathogens like MRSA and multidrug-resistant A. baumannii makes these nanoparticles promising candidates for developing novel antimicrobial coatings, wound dressings or therapeutic formulations. The mechanism of action of the Ag2Se nanoparticles may vary against various bacterial species. Further studies on investigating the precise mechanisms of action could enhance their clinical application.

Evaluation of cytoplasmic leakage activity of chalcogenide nanoparticles

The cytoplasmic DNA and protein leakage assay of Ag2Se nanoparticles was performed against Gram-positive bacteria, such as S. epidermidis, and Gram-negative bacteria, such as E. coli. (Fig. 4) The cytoplasmic DNA leakage assay evaluates the membrane-disrupting effect of Ag2Se nanoparticles on E. coli by measuring absorbance at 260 nm, which indicates released DNA over a 6-hour period. The control sample shows minimal absorbance, confirming intact bacterial membranes, while increasing concentrations of Ag2Se nanoparticles (100, 200 and 500 µg/mL) exhibit a dose dependent increase in DNA leakage (Fig. 4a). At the highest concentration (500 µg/mL), Ag2Se nanoparticles caused severe membrane damage in E. coli, evident from a sharp absorbance increase and substantial cytoplasmic DNA release. Similarly, in S. epidermidis, treatment with Ag2Se nanoparticles led to a marked rise in extracellular DNA levels compared to the untreated control, confirming significant membrane disruption and leakage (Fig. 4c). This marked elevation in DNA release indicates significant membrane damage, suggesting that the nanoparticles effectively disrupt the structural integrity of the bacterial cell membrane36. The observed leakage likely results from nanoparticle-induced oxidative stress and direct physical interactions with the membrane, leading to pore formation or rupture. These findings strongly support the bactericidal action of Ag2Se nanoparticles through membrane destabilization and subsequent intracellular content leakage37. Additionally, Ag2Se nanoparticles disrupt E. coli cell membranes, leading to significant protein leakage. Unlike the untreated control with negligible release, treated samples show a sharp rise in extracellular protein levels, evidenced by increased absorbance (Fig. 4b). Similar trend was observed in case of S. epidermidis where Ag2Se nanoparticles cause effective disruption in cellular integrity resulting in cytoplasmic protein leakage (Fig. 4d). This confirms that the nanoparticles compromise membrane integrity, likely through oxidative stress and structural destabilization, leading to significant leakage of cellular contents38. This phenomenon confirms the potential of Ag2Se nanoparticles as an effective antimicrobial agent for dealing with various resistance mechanisms. These findings reinforce Ag2Se nanoparticles as a promising antimicrobial agent that effectively compromises bacterial membrane integrity, with antibacterial activity intensifying at higher doses.

Evaluation of antioxidant activity of chalcogenide nanoparticles

DPPH assay

The DPPH assay is considered to be the most widely used in vitro method for the determination of antioxidant activity of nanoparticles39. The DPPH assay is a simple, widely used method to evaluate the antioxidant properties of nanoparticles by measuring their ability to scavenge DPPH free radicals40. The free radicals like superoxide, hydroxyl radicals can be generated from normal metabolic processes, causing several serious medical complications like cancer, ageing and diabetes mellitus etc.41,42. Therefore, neutralization of such free radicals is highly essential in order to safeguard the normal cells from their lethal effects43. In this study, we have estimated a dose-dependent free radical scavenging activity of Ag2Se nanoparticles by taking ascorbic acid as a positive control (Fig. 5a). In this assay, the Ag2Se nanoparticles with antioxidant properties interact with DPPH by donating electrons or hydrogen atoms to neutralize the free radical appearance of DPPH, thereby converting into its reduced non-radical form (DPPHH). This reduction process highlights the radical scavenging ability of Ag2Se nanoparticles, with the activity increasing in a concentration-dependent manner. The efficiency of this antioxidant action is strongly influenced by the surface chemistry and electron density of the nanoparticle, which facilitates effective electron transfer. These findings suggest that Ag2Se nanoparticles are not only stable during the assay but also act as potent and sustained radical scavengers, making them promising candidates for biomedical applications aimed towards mitigating oxidative stress as well as for environmental uses. Additionally, ascorbic acid, as a positive control, showed effective scavenging activity. Ascorbic acid reveals effective potential of DPPH radical scavenging activity via two mechanisms, such as hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) and single electron transfer followed by proton transfer (SET-PT)44. In HAT, ascorbic acid donates a hydrogen atom (H+) to the DPPH free radical, converting it into a stable, non-radical compound. Additionally, following the SET-PT mechanism, ascorbic acid first donates an electron, followed by a proton in order to neutralize the radical. Therefore, in our study, ascorbic acid showed effective scavenging activity even at lower concentrations, while Ag2Se nanoparticles showed their antioxidant potential at higher concentrations. The Ag2Se nanoparticles exhibited notable antioxidant activity showing IC50 values of 142.6 µg/mL. All scavenging values were corrected for nanoparticle background absorbance to ensure accuracy. In this regard, SeNPs have also exhibited similar antioxidant properties as suggested by various studies45–47. Moreover, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) have also exhibited significant radical-scavenging potential, as reported in previous studies48.

H2O2 assay

We have also evaluated the antioxidant potential of Ag2Se nanoparticles via hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) scavenging assay. For comparison, ascorbic acid was taken as a standard (Fig. 5b). The H2O2 scavenging assay reveals a concentration-dependent antioxidant activity of both Ag2Se nanoparticles and ascorbic acid (Vit.C). At lower concentration (50 µg/mL), Ag2Se nanoparticles exhibited moderate scavenging activity, while Vit. C demonstrated superior efficacy, showing nearly 65.3% scavenging. As the concentration increased to 100 µg/mL, Ag2Se showed progressive improvement. At the highest tested concentration (250 µg/mL), Ag2Se shows significant scavenging activity, though Vit.C remains the more potent antioxidant. Ag2Se exhibited effective H2O2 scavenging activity due to its ability to catalyse the decomposition of H2O2 into less harmful products like water and oxygen, and also due to the inherent antioxidant properties of selenium49. The combination of silver and selenium may lead to a synergistic effect. The Ag2Se nanoparticles exhibited notable antioxidant activity showing IC50 values of 158.3 µg/mL. All scavenging values were corrected for nanoparticle background absorbance to ensure accuracy. In antioxidant pathways, selenium plays a critical role as a redox-active element. Ag2Se nanoparticles can mimic glutathione peroxidase-like activity, scavenging excess free radicals and reducing oxidative stress in biological systems. The dual functionality, such as ROS generation in microbial cells and ROS modulation in host environments, provides a balance between antimicrobial potency and antioxidant protection.16 Such properties highlight the biomedical potential of Ag2Se nanoparticles, particularly in applications where oxidative stress contributes to disease progression50. Additionally, the size and morphology of the nanoparticles can also influence their H2O2 scavenging activity. Smaller nanoparticles generally have a larger surface area, which may lead to stronger interaction with H2O2 molecules and more efficient decomposition51. The findings suggested that Ag2Se could serve as an excellent antioxidant agent, particularly in applications where traditional antioxidants may be less suitable. Further optimization of nanoparticle composition or concentration may enhance their scavenging potential.

Evaluation of haemocompatibility of chalcogenide nanoparticles

Haemocompatibility is a crucial property of nanoparticles for biomedical applications, as blood is the primary site of interaction, distinct from their general toxicity52. Nanoparticles, upon interaction, rupture the RBC in the circulatory system, releasing haemoglobin, which results in various chronic disorders such as anaemia, jaundice, various pathological disorders and kidney failure53. Therefore, in our study, quantitative and qualitative assessment of the haemolytic potential of Ag2Se nanoparticles was carefully evaluated to determine blood compatibility. The findings suggested a concentration-dependent RBC lysis induced by Ag2Se nanoparticles with hemolysis levels of approximately 7%, 11% and 42% for the concentrations 25 µg/mL, 50 µg/mL and 100 µg/mL, respectively (Fig. 6). Here, HCl was considered as a positive control. Studies suggest that materials with hemolysis below 5% can be considered highly haemocompatible and below 10% as haemocompatible17,54. At lower concentrations, our studied nanoparticles showed the least lysis activity, confirming compatibility for various biomedical applications. In this context, Chahardoli et al. studied the haemocompatibility of quercetin-assisted silver nanoparticles, which showed around 9% haemolytic activity at 1 mg/mL but negligible lysis at lower concentrations, indicating that these nanoparticles are non-toxic and haemocompatible. Studies have shown that Ag2Se nanoparticles not only exhibit good haemocompatibility but also promote blood clotting, offering potential applications in wound healing and antithrombotic therapies55. Additionally, the qualitative analysis showed that the haemolytic activity of Ag2Se nanoparticles was lowest at 25 µg/mL. As the concentration increased to 50 and 100 µg/mL, the nanoparticles showed higher haemolytic activity, with the maximum effect at 100 µg/mL (Fig. 7). Therefore, both qualitative and quantitative analyses of the haemolytic potential of Ag2Se nanoparticles demonstrated that a lower concentration of Ag2Se nanoparticles confirms its compatibility, while a higher concentration may cause toxicity.

Surface morphology of bacteria upon treatment with chalcogenide nanoparticles

Bacterial membrane deformation upon Ag2Se nanoparticle treatment was observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The images obtained showed clumping or aggregation of bacterial cells. The images confirmed significant clumping and membrane rupture in treated cells compared with untreated cells, due to the interaction of nanoparticles with the bacterial cell membrane. The nanoparticles can accumulate in the cell wall, causing deformation and, eventually, cell lysis. Additionally, they can release silver ions, which further disrupt cellular processes, including DNA binding and respiration, ultimately leading to cell death. However, nanoparticle treatment induced a significant reduction in bacterial population density in comparison to control samples (Fig. 8a and b). The Ag2Se nanoparticles appeared to induce cell lysis and delay biofilm formation, thereby reducing the bacterial population. These findings suggest that the Ag2Se nanoparticles effectively compromise bacterial membrane integrity and disrupt biofilm structures, indicating their antimicrobial activity. In this context, Li et al. have performed a comparative analysis of antibacterial activity, dynamics and effects of silver ions and silver nanoparticles, suggesting strong alteration in bacterial morphology, thereby showing effective antibacterial activity56,57.

Conclusion

This study represents a significant advancement in the green synthesis of binary chalcogenide silver selenide (Ag2Se) nanoparticles using pomegranate peel extract, offering an eco-friendly and sustainable alternative to conventional chemical methods. The biosynthesized Ag2Se nanoparticles demonstrated remarkable biological activities, positioning them as promising candidates for biomedical and therapeutic applications. Furthermore, the research highlights dual functionality of Ag2Se nanoparticles as both antimicrobial and antioxidant agents, with potential applications in wound dressings, antimicrobial coatings and drug delivery systems. Future work should explore their synergistic effects with existing antibiotics and in vivo biocompatibility to understand their clinical potential. By influencing plant-based synthesis, this study also contributes to the growing field of sustainable nanotechnology, aligning with global efforts towards greener scientific practices.
