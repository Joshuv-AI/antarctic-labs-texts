Comparative Efficacy of Interventions for the Prevention and Treatment of Postpartum Depression: A Bayesian Network Meta-analysis
Authors: Archana Mishra, Krishna Nikhila Bonga, Biswa Ranjan Mishra, Anand Srinivasan, Rituparna Maiti, Debasish Hota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Postpartum depression (PPD) is a highly prevalent but under-reported and undertreated disorder that affects mothers and infants alike. The management of PPD has to be thoughtfully catered to limit the exposure of infants to harmful drugs. This network meta-analysis (NMA) was conducted to assess the efficacy of pharmacological interventions for the prevention and treatment of PPD. We searched PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, Scopus, Google Scholar, Cochrane databases and clinical trial registries to find studies to be included, and data were extracted. Random effects Bayesian NMA was performed using Markov chain Monte Carlo simulations. A network graph was built, the rank probability of each treatment was determined, and the surface under the cumulative rank probability curve was calculated. The risk of bias assessment, including publication bias, was assessed. Data from 54 studies, including 7,438 participants, were included for this NMA. No pharmacological intervention demonstrated robust superiority over placebo for the treatment of PPD, though wide credible intervals (CrI) and limited sample sizes preclude definitive conclusions. Trazodone (−1.6; 95% CrI: −2.7 to −0.41) and diphenhydramine (−1.4; 95% CrI: −2.5 to −0.20) are the two treatments that showed an efficacy superior to placebo in the prevention of PPD as seen by symptom severity scores. There was no publication bias, as observed by the funnel plot and Egger’s regression test. Based on single small trials, trazodone and diphenhydramine showed statistically superior efficacy to placebo in preventing PPD. Safety considerations in the perinatal and breastfeeding context must be carefully evaluated before these agents are considered in clinical practice.

INTRODUCTION

Postpartum depression (PPD) or postnatal depression has been defined differently in various guidelines; collectively, it may be advocated to include depressive symptoms that start with the onset of pregnancy till 12 months post-delivery for consideration of PPD [1,2]. During the perinatal period, one in five women is prone to develop mental health conditions, and the estimated prevalence of PPD in women is 10−16% [3,4]. Depression during pregnancy, a history of antenatal depression, poor social and emotional support, stressful life events, and negative attitudes toward pregnancy are some of the risk factors responsible for PPD [5]. The extent to which the intricacies of PPD and non-perinatal depression are similar or different is unknown [6]. Levels of hormones like estrogen, progesterone, melatonin, oxytocin, and thyroxin typically fluctuate during pregnancy and after delivery, and it has been observed that women who are more sensitive to these changes may be at risk for depression [7]. Genetic factors, abnormal neurotransmitter levels, or activity may also be responsible for an increased risk of PPD [8].

The symptoms of PPD are indistinguishable from major depression seen otherwise, like dysphoria, anhedonia, impaired concentration, decision-making, worthlessness, and excessive guilt [6]. The perceived notion of happiness after childbirth, social stigma, and fear of separation from their infants lead to underreporting of the symptoms by the mothers to healthcare setups [9]. There are a number of adverse consequences of PPD, like reduced breastfeeding, poorer healthcare of infants, impaired mother-infant bonding, marital discord, cognitive impairment of the child, suicidal ideation of the mothers, etc [10,11]. The primary treatment for PPD is psychotherapy, and it is preferred by many females as it does not put the fetus or lactating baby at risk of exposure to antidepressant medications [12-14]. However, there is subjectivity in administration, and accessibility as well as comfort with psychotherapy also vary greatly. To address these limitations, pharmacological interventions are administered to a majority of patients. Pain and anxiety during childbirth and the prevalence of insomnia during pregnancy may also be contributing factors to PPD. Thus, apart from antidepressant drugs, many nutritional supplements, hormones, antihistaminics, anaesthetics, and analgesic agents have been evaluated for their possible action in the prevention or management of PPD [15,16]. However, the efficacy of these has not yet been established in properly controlled trials.

There are a few randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating the agents mentioned above for their efficacy in PPD prevention and treatment, but the number of participants evaluated in such studies is limited, and there are very limited head-on comparisons to establish the efficacy of one intervention over others. To the best of our knowledge, no network meta-analysis that evaluates the effect of pharmacological interventions in patients with PPD. Thus, this study was planned to compare the efficacy of various pharmacological agents for the prevention and treatment of PPD. For clarity, throughout this article the term “preventive” refers to pharmacological strategies administered to pregnant women without a current diagnosis of depression, with the aim of reducing the incidence or severity of PPD after delivery; the term “treatment” refers to strategies administered to women already diagnosed with major depression during pregnancy or within 6 months postpartum, with the aim of reducing established depressive symptoms. These two populations were kept analytically separate, with independent networks constructed for each, to preserve clinical homogeneity and the transitivity assumption.

METHODS

Protocol Development and Registration

PRISMA-P guidelines were followed in developing a standard protocol for this network meta-analysis, and the protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO ID: CRD42023466491) [17]. PRISMA extension statement for network meta-analysis (PRISMA-NMA) was followed for the conduct and reporting of this NMA [18].

Search Strategy

We searched PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, Scopus, Google Scholar, Cochrane databases and clinical trial registry databases for possible inclusion of RCTs for this NMA for articles published till September 2025. Search terms were structured for PubMed with ((“depression, postpartum”[MeSH Terms] OR (“depression”[All Fields] AND “postpartum”[All Fields]) OR “postpartum depression”[All Fields] OR (“postpartum”[All Fields] AND “depression”[All Fields])) AND (“depression, postpartum”[MeSH Terms] OR (“depression”[All Fields] AND “postpartum”[All Fields]) OR “postpartum depression”[All Fields] OR (“postnatal”[All Fields] AND “depression”[All Fields]) OR “postnatal depression”[All Fields]) AND (“therapeutics”[MeSH Terms] OR “therapeutics”[All Fields] OR “treatments”[All Fields] OR “therapy”[MeSH Subheading] OR “therapy”[All Fields] OR “treatment”[All Fields] OR “treatment s”[All Fields] OR (“prevent”[All Fields] OR “preventability”[All Fields] OR “preventable”[All Fields] OR “preventative”[All Fields] OR “preventatively”[All Fields] OR “preventatives”[All Fields] OR “prevented”[All Fields] OR “preventing”[All Fields] OR “prevention and control”[MeSH Subheading] OR (“prevention”[All Fields] AND “control”[All Fields]) OR “prevention and control”[All Fields] OR “prevention”[All Fields] OR “prevention s”[All Fields] OR “preventions”[All Fields] OR “preventive”[All Fields] OR “preventively”[All Fields] OR “preventives”[All Fields] OR “prevents”[All Fields]))) AND (clinicaltrial[Filter]). The search terms were adapted for other databases. References of the included articles were searched manually for possible articles. The international clinical trial registry platform was searched for any unpublished data.

Study Selection Criteria

RCTs on pharmacological agents for the prevention and treatment of PPD were considered for inclusion. Data for symptom severity score after intervention administration and change in symptom severity score were extracted for analysis. Studies included the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) or Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS), Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) scores, or any equivalent scores for measuring the outcome. All the RCTs published in peer-reviewed journals in English were included, irrespective of the date of publication. Case reports, case series, and letters to editors were excluded; studies where the diagnosis of depression was made prior to conception were also excluded.

Types of Participants and Intervention

Studies with participants ≥ 18 years of age within 12 weeks of gestation to 6 months postpartum were included in this NMA. The NMA consists of two parts: (a) those studies with agents used for the prevention of PPD included pregnant mothers after 12 weeks of gestation till childbirth but not diagnosed with depression antenatally; (b) those studies with agents for the treatment of PPD included participants who included participants within 12 weeks of gestation to 6 months postpartum who were diagnosed with major depression as per Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)-III/IV/V. Any pharmacological agent that was tested in trials where depression scores were evaluated as an outcome measure was considered an intervention; placebo, control or any other intervention was considered control.

A total of 23 interventions viz. Chamomile tea, docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), docosahexaenoic acid plus arachidonic acid (DHA + AA), diphenhydramine, eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), esketamine, fish oil, flavonoid, folic acid, probiotic (HN001), intravenous ketamine (iv_ketamine), magnesium sulfate, omega-3 fatty acid, probiotics plus folic acid, progesterone, remifentanil, ropivacaine, ropivacaine plus esketamine, subcutaneous ketamine (sc_ketamine), thyroxine, trazodone and zinc sulphate were included amongst the retrieved literature for prevention of PPD. For treatment, Brexanolone, cognitive behavioural therapy, celecoxib, crocin, dexmedetomidine, esketamine, essential oil, fluoxetine, hyaluronic acid gel, ketamine, nortriptyline, oestrogen, omega-3 fatty acid, oxytocin, oxytocin gel, paroxetine, saffron, sertraline, transdermal estradiol, vitamin D, vitamin D plus calcium and zuranolone; a total of 20 interventions were evaluated. Though cognitive behavioural therapy has been mentioned as a comparator in some studies, we do not comment on the pooled data as none of the studies have been considered.

Types of Outcomes

The efficacy outcome chosen was the mean symptom severity score for preventive strategy and the change in symptom severity score for treatment strategies. The studies reporting any of the scores like EPDS, HDRS, MADRS, etc, irrespective of the duration of the study, were included. Given the use of heterogeneous depression rating scales across included studies, all continuous outcomes were converted to standardized mean differences (SMDs) prior to pooling, which allowed synthesis across different scales. Where multiple follow-up time points were reported within a single study, the assessment closest to the end of the defined intervention period was selected as the primary time point to minimize heterogeneity in timing of outcome measurement. Sensitivity to these choices is acknowledged as a limitation.

Study Selection and Data Collection

The selection of the studies was done stepwise. First, the databases were searched for possible inclusion, and duplicates were removed. Nest, the titles and abstracts of the studies retrieved from database searches were screened based on eligibility criteria laid down a priori. The full text of the articles was retrieved in the next step, and the text was read thoroughly to sort articles for final inclusion. Three reviewers extracted data independently, and any discrepancies were sorted in agreement with the fourth reviewer.

Data Extraction and Management

The data pertaining to study characteristics (study design, population, intervention, comparator, outcome), risk of bias, outcome measures, and follow-up duration were collected in a data extraction sheet. The data were extracted by two reviewers, and disagreements were resolved in consultation with the third reviewer. When data was available only in the form of plots, a web plot digitizer tool was used to extract the relevant data [19].

Data Analysis

The data was presented in a long format, i.e., arm-wise data for conducting Bayesian analysis. The data for the interventions that were evaluated for the prevention of PPD and those for the treatment of PPD were kept in separate Excel sheets; the analyses were run separately. All the analyses were performed in RStudio using the “gemtc” package [20]. A network graph was created to assess the associations between the interventions in the included clinical trials. Markov chain Monte Carlo simulations were used to calculate posteriors for pooled treatment effects. Random effects consistency models with non-informative priors were used for the purpose. Specifically, normal (0, 100) distributions were used as non-informative priors for treatment effect parameters, and a half-normal prior was used for the between-study heterogeneity (random-effects standard deviation), following standard practice in Bayesian NMA. These vague priors were selected to allow the posterior estimates to be predominantly data driven. Multi-arm trials were handled by accounting for the correlation between arms sharing a common control group. Where multiple reports pertained to the same trial, data from each independently randomised unit were treated as distinct observations to avoid unit-of-analysis errors and double-counting. A permutation of burn-in and inference iterations was used to obtain a best-fit model. The convergence of the model was tested with the Brooks-Gelman-Rubin diagnostic tool. Time series and density plots were visualized to confirm the findings. The simulation model with 5,000 burn-in iterations and 1,00,000 inference iterations with a thinning factor of 10 yielded a potential scale-reduction factor of 1.0009 for preventive strategies and 1.0007 for treatment strategies, meaning an optimal convergence for both models. The influence of each data point on the model was evaluated using leverage statistics and residual deviance to understand the outliers. The relative effect of all the interventions compared to placebo was plotted in a forest plot of relative effects, and the relative effects of each intervention (SMD with 95% credible interval [CrI]) compared to all other interventions were tabulated in the new league table. Rank probabilities for each intervention for each rank were estimated in a matrix and were plotted. Surface under the cumulative ranking (SUCRA) scores were calculated to determine the posterior probability of each intervention to be amongst the best options available. Meta-regression was done to assess the effect of duration of intervention on the outcome.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Cochrane Collaboration Risk of bias 2 (RoB2) tool was used to evaluate the risk of bias in the five domains, viz. arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, selection of the reported result and the overall risk of bias [21]. The risk was determined to be low risk, some concerns, or high risk based on the algorithms provided for determination.

Quality of Evidence

Quality of evidence was graded very low, low, moderate, or high based on the seriousness of concerns in the domains of study design, indirectness, imprecision, risk of bias, and inconsistency. The grading was done separately for direct and indirect effects. The higher of the two grades was taken to be the evidence grade for the network estimate.

RESULTS

Study Results and Study Characteristics

Fifty-seven studies were sorted for possible inclusion in the network meta-analysis. The study selection process has been depicted in Figure 1. Characteristics of the included studies have been represented in Table 1. The risk of bias in all five domains and the overall risk of bias in the individual studies has been depicted in Table 2.

Summary of Network Geometry

Of 57 studies, 28 reported the effect of various agents on preventing PPD (Fig. 2), out of which three studies [22-24] formed separate nodes and were excluded from the final analysis. Finally, 25 studies (4,395 participants) reported the effect of interventions in the prevention [25-49], and 29 studies (31 reports; 3,043 participants) reported the effect of the pharmacological agents on the treatment of PPD [50-78]. One report by Meltzer-Brody et al. [70] contains data from two multicentric RCTs, and a study by O’Hara et al. [59] has reported the data of the two trial sites separately in the same report. Twenty-four interventions each have been studied for the prevention and treatment of PPD. There were 19 two-arm studies, five three-arm studies, and one four-arm study for preventive agents. Out of 29 studies for the treatment of PPD, only two were three-arm studies. The network geometry of studies reporting interventions for treatment (Fig. 3A) and prevention (Fig. 3B) has been created separately.

Analysis of All Possible Comparisons

Separate consistency models were built with preventive and therapeutic agents used in PPD. The standard deviation for random effects was 0.188 for the preventive model and 0.833 for the therapeutic model. Inconsistency was formally assessed using node-splitting analysis (see below) as the primary method; the node-splitting results, which showed no significant inconsistency (p > 0.05 for all closed loops), are the primary basis for concluding no major concerns for inconsistency. The SMD and 95% CrI of interventions with respect to the rest of the interventions have been determined and tabulated in Table 3A for preventive strategies and Table 3B for therapeutic strategies. A relative effect plot was constructed and has been shown in Figure 4A for preventive and Figure 4B for therapeutic strategies. Amongst preventive therapeutic strategies, diphenhydramine (−1.4 [95% CrI: −2.5, −0.20]) and trazodone (−1.6 [95% CrI: −2.7, −0.41]) were more efficacious than placebo for prevention of PPD. None of the other interventions were significantly different from placebo. No treatment strategy demonstrated robust superiority over placebo for the management of PPD, although wide CrIs and small individual evidence bases limit the precision of these estimates (Fig. 4B).

There were no closed triangles for preventive strategies. However, node-splitting analysis was done for five closed therapeutic strategies, and direct evidence was found to be consistent with indirect evidence (p > 0.05) (Fig. 5). A probability matrix for each intervention for each rank was obtained and plotted for each strategy: Figure 6A for preventive and Figure 6B for therapeutic strategies. The SUCRA scores for preventive and treatment strategies have been tabulated in Table 4A and 4B respectively.

Meta-regression Analysis

Meta-regression analysis was done to assess the effect of duration on prevention or reduction in severity of symptoms in patients with PPD. There was no significant effect of duration on the severity of symptoms for PPD (Fig. 7).

Publication Bias

The comparison-adjusted funnel plot was created for standard error against mean difference, and Egger’s regression test was done. There was no evidence of publication bias for either of the preventive or treatment strategies (Fig. 8).

Quality of Evidence

The certainty of the evidence for diphenhydramine and trazodone evaluated for prevention of PPD was rated to be of moderate quality, whereas for all other interventions it was very low to low.

In summary, the principal findings of this NMA are as follows: (i) Among preventive strategies, trazodone and diphenhydramine showed statistically significant reductions in PPD symptom severity scores compared to placebo, supported by moderate-quality evidence from single small trials; all other preventive agents did not differ significantly from placebo. (ii) Among treatment strategies, no pharmacological agent demonstrated robust superiority over placebo; agents such as saffron, essential oil, and ketamine ranked highly on SUCRA but did not achieve statistical significance, reflecting imprecision rather than confirmed efficacy. (iii) The overall certainty of evidence across the network is predominantly low to very low, driven by small sample sizes, limited numbers of trials per intervention, and reliance on indirect comparisons, which substantially limits confidence in the estimated effects.

DISCUSSION

The present NMA analysed data of 54 RCTs involving 7,438 participants conducted on various pharmacological agents for prevention and treatment of PPD. Data from 25 studies on preventive strategy shows that diphenhydramine and trazodone were more efficacious than placebo for prevention of PPD. However, the analysis of other 29 studies on therapeutic strategy revealed that no pharmacological agent demonstrated statistically robust superiority over placebo for the treatment of PPD; this finding should be interpreted in the context of wide CrIs, small and often single-trial evidence bases, and considerable clinical heterogeneity across included studies. Notably, SUCRA rankings reflect relative probability of ranking and should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical superiority; several agents (e.g., saffron, essential oil, ketamine) rank highly in the treatment network despite lacking statistically significant differences from placebo, largely because their CrIs are wide due to small sample sizes. The certainty of the evidence for diphenhydramine and trazodone evaluated for prevention of PPD was rated to be of moderate quality. It is indeed intriguing to note that drugs otherwise effective in depressive symptoms of nonpuerperal causes, like sertraline, also failed to show benefit in our NMA. This finding may be due to an underpowered study or involvement of a causal pathway other than the monoaminergic hypothesis in PPD.

Perinatal mental health is a cause of concern for the well-being of mothers and infants alike. Management of PPD becomes tricky as the exposure to the fetus or infant needs to be thoughtfully weighed against the expected risks in addition to safety concerns for the mother [79]. Psychopharmacology studies also rely largely on drug registries and databases for risk assessments, as with other drugs that are to be used for pregnancy and lactation [80]. The etiopathogenesis of PPD is multifactorial, and a definitive causal pathway has not yet been identified. Thus, many pharmacological agents that are implicated in treating other conditions during pregnancy, labour, and postpartum, including nutritional supplements, etc., have been studied for their possible role in PPD.

Pregnancy is associated with metabolic and hormonal changes, and the loss of sedative effects of progesterone is an important attribute. The decreased quality of sleep and frequent nocturnal awakenings peripartum may lead to mood disturbances [81]. Diphenhydramine is a first-generation antihistaminic agent that is known to possess antagonistic action on a wide range of monoaminergic receptors, i.e., dopamine, serotonin, and norepinephrine [82]. Trazodone, an atypical antidepressant with 5-HT2A/5HT2C antagonist and 5HT1A partial agonist action, is known for its antidepressant and anti-anxiety properties in adults [83]. The action of these receptors also leads to improved quality and duration of sleep. This shows that improvement in sleep quality by the two drugs also led to improvement in mood and lesser scores on depression scales. However, it is critically important to note that the evidence supporting trazodone and diphenhydramine for PPD prevention is derived from a single small trial each, with moderate but indirect evidence quality. These findings should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating signals rather than grounds for clinical recommendation. Important safety considerations warrant attention: diphenhydramine carries a significant anticholinergic burden and may cause daytime sedation, and limited mechanistic plausibility exists for its use in mood disorders. Both agents have limited safety data in the breastfeeding context and require careful evaluation against established perinatal safety databases before any use in lactating mothers. Any clinical translation of these findings would require confirmatory RCTs with adequate sample sizes and rigorous safety endpoints in perinatal populations.

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) clinical practice guidelines for the treatment and management of mental health conditions have recommended the use of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) as first-line pharmacotherapy for patients based on the relative rankings of the safety profile [84]. But, there are concerns about efficacy, and thus, the use of antidepressants should be reserved. A meta-analysis by Wang et al. [85] showed that the administration of esketamine has a significantly reduced incidence of PPD. While our study did not include the incidence of PPD as an outcome measure, no difference when compared to placebo was observed in the mean scores of depression symptom severity. A matching-adjusted indirect comparison meta-analysis was conducted to compare the effect of zuranolone (the only drug approved for PPD by the Food and Drug Administration) with SSRIs and observed a greater symptom improvement with zuranolone [86]. However, in our network meta-analysis, we did not find any significant difference for zuranolone with placebo or SSRIs, possibly due to the inclusion of additional articles in our NMA.

This network meta-analysis is the first to combine the effects of all interventions for the prevention or treatment of post-partum depression. We used a Bayesian approach in this NMA which enables network meta-analysis to derive a posterior probability distribution of all relative treatment effects, facilitating the estimation of these effects to quantify uncertainty in parameter estimates and rank all treatments within the network. This analysis suggests that the treatment strategies employed thus far have shown limited or no significant benefits, highlighting the need to explore alternative therapeutic approaches in patients.

There are certain limitations of this NMA. First, we had to exclude three studies due to the formation of disconnected sub-networks. Second, RCTs on most of the antidepressants were missing, and thus, a comprehensive statement about all available therapies cannot be made. Third, the results must be interpreted with considerable caution: many interventions are supported by only a single trial, several with very small sample sizes, resulting in wide CrIs and substantial imprecision. This imprecision means that apparent differences from placebo, as well as apparent similarities, should not be over-interpreted. Fourth, this NMA focused on comparative efficacy and did not assess comparative safety; the safety profile of included agents (particularly SSRIs, serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, trazodone, diphenhydramine, ketamine/esketamine, brexanolone, zuranolone, and herbal agents such as saffron) in the perinatal and breastfeeding context is an important clinical consideration that is not captured by this analysis. Any change in clinical practice would require separate and careful safety evaluation.

In conclusion, trazodone and diphenhydramine showed statistically superior efficacy to placebo for the prevention of PPD based on current limited evidence; however, these findings originate from single small trials and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing. No pharmacological agent demonstrated robust superiority over placebo for the treatment of PPD, a finding that requires cautious interpretation given the wide CrIs and small evidence bases for most agents. Even antidepressants with established efficacy in nonpuerperal depression failed to show clear benefit, possibly reflecting distinct causal pathways in PPD or underpowered studies. Safety considerations in the perinatal and breastfeeding population must be rigorously evaluated before any of these agents can be recommended, as this NMA focused on efficacy outcomes only. Adequately powered, placebo-controlled RCTs incorporating both efficacy and safety endpoints in well-defined perinatal populations are needed to generate evidence sufficient for clinical recommendations.
