Comprehensive safety assessment of novel dicholine folate vitamin
Authors: Tom Jonaitis, Martin Ulmann, Gerd Wiesler, Kristine V. Lukasik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Novel vitamin salt forms can offer formulation advantages while retaining the biological activity of the parent compound. This safety assessment evaluates dicholine (6S)-5-methyltetrahydrofolate (dicholine L-5-MTHF), a reduced folate source designed to deliver biologically active L-5-MTHF with improved water solubility and stability relative to existing reduced folate salts. As L-5-MTHF salts dissociate to yield L-5-MTHF and their respective counterions, the safety of dicholine L-5-MTHF was assessed using a weight-of-evidence approach that integrates published data for existing L-5-MTHF salts together with information on choline exposure. A standard in vitro genotoxicity battery conducted with dicholine L-5-MTHF, including bacterial reverse mutation, chromosome aberration, and mammalian micronucleus assays, was negative and consistent with the broader genotoxicity profile of other reduced folate sources. In addition, subchronic oral toxicity studies in rats with calcium and sodium L-5-MTHF salts identified no adverse effects at the highest doses tested, and human clinical studies with L-5-MTHF salts support tolerability, including at intakes above the adult UL established for folic acid. Collectively, these data support the safety of dicholine L-5-MTHF at the proposed use level.

Introduction

(6S)−5-methyltetrahydrofolic acid dicholine salt (“dicholine L−5-MTHF”’; CAS# 2416833–67–3) is the choline salt of (6S)−5-methyltetrahydrofolic acid ((6S)−5-MTHF; L−5-MTHF; Fig. 1), which is the predominant, naturally occurring form of folate found in foods. Folates are essential B vitamins required for one-carbon metabolism and comprise a group of compounds containing a pteridine ring linked to para-aminobenzoic acid with variable glutamate residues [1]. Folic acid (pteroylmonoglutamic acid), a pro-vitamin, is the fully oxidized and stable form of folate that is synthetic and is the form commonly used in vitamin supplements and in fortified food products. Upon ingestion, folic acid undergoes sequential reduction in the body by dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) to dihydrofolate (DHF), which is a slow, rate-limiting process, followed by reduction to tetrahydrofolate (THF). THF then participates in one-carbon metabolism to form 5,10-methylene-THF (e.g., during serine-to-glycine conversion), which is ultimately converted to L−5-MTHF [2].Fig. 1Molecular structure of dicholine L−5-MTHF.

L−5-methyltetrahydrofolate (L−5-MTHF) acts as a coenzyme in one-carbon transfer reactions essential for nucleotide synthesis, amino acid metabolism, and numerous methylation processes [3] (see Fig. 2). L−5-MTHF contains two stereogenic centers: C−6 in the pteroyl (pterin) moiety and the α-carbon of the glutamate moiety, which in principle yields four possible diastereoisomers [4]. In biological systems, however, the glutamate moiety is effectively fixed as the natural “L” (αS) configuration, so reduced methylfolates are typically discussed as the two (6S,αS) and (6 R,αS) diastereoisomers, with the 6S αS form generally considered the biologically active form. Consistent with this, Willems et al. [5] measured the individual 6S- and 6R−5-MTHF diastereoisomers in blood following oral dosing and reported that dosing with racemic 6[R,S]−5-MTHF yields measurable circulating levels of both isomers, with the non-natural 6 R isomer still detectable one week later (including following folic acid administration), suggesting retention/storage of 6 R in the body. To simplify nomenclature, reduced-methylfolate diastereoisomers bearing the natural 6S (L-form) configuration are grouped to as “L-diastereoisomers,” while those with the non-natural 6 R (D-form) configuration are grouped as “D-diastereoisomers” [6].

With regard to the fate of ingested L−5-MTHF, absorption occurs mainly in the duodenum and jejunum within an acidic microenvironment, where uptake is dominated by an active, saturable, pH-dependent carrier process, principally via the proton-coupled folate transporter (PCFT), with lesser contribution from the reduced folate carrier (RFC) [3], [7]. This active absorption occurs alongside a passive, non-saturable component that occurs primarily in the ileum with minor absorption also reported in the large intestine.

Polyglutamated food folates found in the diet are first hydrolysed to monoglutamates by jejunal γ-glutamyl hydrolase prior to transport [3], whereas when L−5-MTHF is ingested directly (e.g., as choline salts) it is already a reduced monoglutamate, consistent with the principal circulating form that undergoes intestinal transfer.

After absorption, folates are exported via carrier-mediated processes into the portal vein and systemic circulation, and conversion to 5-MTHF is considered to occur in both enterocytes and the liver [3]. In contrast, folic acid requires DHFR-dependent reduction, and a fraction may reach the liver unchanged, particularly when conversion capacity is exceeded. A detailed overview of this process is provided in Fig. 2.

Folic acid supplementation before and during early pregnancy has been established to substantially reduce the risk of fetal neural tube defects and is a cornerstone of public health recommendations, including food fortification worldwide [8], [9], [10]. Adequate folate status is also important for DNA synthesis, methylation, and homocysteine metabolism, with associations reported in relation to various chronic diseases and other birth outcomes [11], [12], [13].

The Recommended Daily Allowance (RDA) for folate as defined by the IOM (1998 [14]) is 400 µg dietary folate equivalents (DFE)/day in adults (≥19 years), with adjustments for pregnant women (600 µg DFE/day) and lactating women (500 µg DFE/day). For reference, 1 µg DFE equates to 1 µg natural food folate, but 0.6 µg folic acid from fortified foods or supplements taken with meals (i.e., a 1.7x conversion). For L−5-MTHF (e.g., calcium or glucosamine salt), EFSA [15] derived DFE conversions that mirror folic acid at typical intakes where DFE = natural folate + 1.7 × folic acid + 1.7 × 5-MTHF for fortified foods and for supplements providing < 400 µg/day, but proposed a higher factor at higher supplemental intakes (2.0 × L−5-MTHF for ≥400 µg/day) based on evidence of greater bioavailability at that dose range. EFSA [15] also concluded that the L−5-MTHF cation would have negligible impact on bioavailability and therefore the proposed equation would apply to other cations as well. This comparable bioavailability between salt forms of L−5-MTHF was noted in the individual EFSA opinions [4], [16], [17]. However, several researchers have reported that higher systemic exposure metrics were measured for certain L−5-MTHF salts relative to folic acid, including for sodium and calcium L−5-MTHF [18], [19] and dicholine L−5-MTHF [20], which supports the interpretation that lower nominal doses of certain L−5-MTHF salts may achieve systemic folate exposure comparable to folic acid, depending on dose, population specifics, and study conditions.

The tolerable upper intake level (UL) for folic acid (folate) of 1 mg/day in adults and scaled by body weight for children, as set by the IOM [14] and EFSA [3], is based on the risk of progression or masking of neurological symptoms in vitamin B12-deficient individuals at doses above the UL. The neurological masking effect underlying the folate UL has been demonstrated for folic acid, but has not been demonstrated for reduced folate forms, such as L−5-MTHF salts; however, EFSA [3] applied its UL to reduced forms of folate as a precaution in the absence of specific data to the contrary.

Reduced folate salts have a long history of use in foods and dietary supplements and have been approved or undergone regulatory clearance in the United States (U.S.), the European Union (EU), and elsewhere. Dicholine L−5-MTHF successfully cleared the US FDA New Dietary Ingredient Notifications (NDIN) process without objection for use in dietary supplements (NDIN 1297 [21]). Calcium L−5-MTHF was successfully GRAS notified to the US FDA in 2021 for use as a source of folate in non-exempt and exempt infant formulas (GRN 915 [27]).

In Canada, Health Canada [28] lists dicholine, calcium, and glucosamine L−5-MTHF salts in the Natural Health Products Ingredients Database (NHPID) as source of folate in dietary supplements.

In the EU, calcium L−5-MTHF was accepted as a safe source of folate in foods at up to 1 mg/day for particular nutritional uses and in foods intended for the general population by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) in 2004 [17]. Similarly, EFSA accepted the safety of glucosamine L−5-MTHF as a source of folate in 2013 [4] and of sodium L−5-MTHF in 2023 [16] under the same use conditions.

The Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA) also accepted the safety of calcium L−5-MTHF as a source of folate in food supplements, foods for special dietary uses and other foods in 2005 [29]. In Australia, calcium L−5-MTHF was accepted for use in foods by Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ) in 2005 [30] and in dietary supplements (i.e., “complementary medicines”) shortly thereafter by the Australian Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA).

Advances in the chemistry and formulation of reduced folates have led to the development of new salt forms, such as dicholine L−5-MTHF. In particular, this reduced folate has higher bioavailability than folic acid [20] but may also offer improved water solubility and stability relative to existing reduced folate salts. These characteristics could enable formulation approaches that are less feasible with existing folate forms, including sublingual applications. Therefore, dicholine L−5-MTHF is intended to be used as a partial or complete substitute for folic acid and other folate salts and will be equivalent to all or part of the RDA established by the IOM [14].

Throughout the numerous safety assessments on reduced folate salts conducted by the US FDA, EFSA, JECFA and others, the pivotal element underpinning the assessments is the conclusion that regardless of the specific cation, the reduced folate salt will dissociate to the L−5-MTHF anion and associated cation and that the safety of these ingredients can wholly be based on these individual moieties. The EFSA Panel [4] noted that clinical studies support that there was no biologically relevant difference in bioavailability of folate from either calcium L−5-MTHF or glucosamine-L−5-MTHF and that that the nature of the cation associated with L−5-MTHF is likely not to greatly affect its bioavailability, even though differences in solubility may exist, depending on the cation. As such, the safety of dicholine L−5-MTHF under proposed conditions of use can similarly be adequately supported by the safety of L−5-MTHF and dicholine, which will be presented herein. Accordingly, the key safety question for dicholine L−5-MTHF is whether the dicholine salt form introduces any toxicological concern beyond the established safety profiles of L−5-MTHF and choline.

This manuscript presents a comprehensive safety assessment of dicholine L−5-MTHF based on available toxicology data that relates to structural and metabolic analogy to established reduced folate salts, and the results from three in vitro genotoxicity assays. The assessment aligns with current toxicology principles that integrates validated, non-animal data and read-across into a weight-of-evidence framework, while minimizing reliance on unnecessary animal testing. For example, the EFSA Panel [16] considered repeat-dose toxicity studies for sodium L−5-MTHF unnecessary given the available data on methylfolate salts for read-across purposes. Therefore, given the robust existing safety dataset for L−5-MTHF salts, a similar evidence-based approach was considered appropriate for dicholine L−5-MTHF. Accordingly, a targeted literature review was conducted to identify pivotal published preclinical and clinical evidence relevant to the safety evaluation of L−5-methyltetrahydrofolate salts in support of the present assessment.

Although reduced L−5-MTHF salts have been established as non-genotoxic, with multiple folate salts demonstrating negative results across standard bacterial and mammalian genotoxicity assays [4], [16], [17], [27], [31], [32], confirmatory testing for a new folate derivative or salt form is still considered appropriate and, in some cases, required for regulatory clearance. This reflects the expectation within food ingredient safety assessments that genotoxicity should be excluded for novel compounds, even when the active moiety is well-characterized. The introduction of a new counterion could in theory alter physicochemical properties, impurity profiles, or dissociation behavior, and in vitro genotoxicity assays provide a broad, sensitive screen to detect any unforeseen reactive species. Because these assays are validated, non-animal methods aligned with the 3Rs principles, they represent the minimal and appropriate toxicological data needed to support a weight-of-evidence safety evaluation. Accordingly, the three genotoxicity studies conducted for dicholine L−5-MTHF evaluate whether the new salt form introduces any DNA-reactivity.

Experimental design

Bacterial reverse mutation assay

The GLP-compliant study was conducted using Salmonella typhimurium strains TA1537, TA1535, TA98, TA100, and Escheria coli WP2 uvrA (pKM101) in the presence and absence of a metabolic activation system (S9 fraction prepared from Aroclor 1254 induced male Sprague Dawley rat liver, 5% v/v) and was conducted consistent with OECD Testing Guideline No. 471 (2020 [33]). A preliminary cytotoxicity assay was conducted first, followed by two independent experiments using the plate incorporation method (Experiment I) and pre-incubation test (Experiment II). The test article (Batch No. STP7.9.21) had a reported purity of 99.36%.

The preliminary cytotoxicity assay was conducted using tester strains TA98 and TA100 with test material at concentrations of 3.16, 10.0, 31.6, 100, 316, 1000, 2500 and 5000 μg/plate in the presence and absence of metabolic activation (S9 liver microsomal fraction was obtained from Trinova Biochem, GmbH, (Gießen, Germany). Vehicle control (purified water) and strain-specific positive controls, 2-aminoanthracene (2-AA), 4-nitro-o-phenylene-diamine (4-NOPD), sodium azide (NaN3), and methylmethanesulfonate (MMS), were also tested along with the test article. All conditions were tested in triplicate. Plates were evaluated for signs of cytotoxicity, including background lawn inhibition and reduction in the mean number of revertant colonies.

Based on a lack of precipitation in preliminary cytotoxicity assay results, test concentrations of 31.6, 100, 316, 1000, 2500 and 5000 μg/plate were used in the experiments with tester strains TA1537, TA1535, TA98, TA100, and TA102 both in the presence and absence of metabolic activation. Samples of each tester strain were grown by culturing for 12 h at 37 °C in S. typhimurium medium (Nutrient Broth) and E. coli medium (Lysogeny broth), respectively, to the late exponential or early stationary phase of growth (approx. 109 cells/mL). For the initial pre-incubation method, 100 μL of the test item-preparation was pre-incubated with the tester strains (100 μL) and sterile buffer or the metabolic activation system (500 μL) for 60 min at 37 °C prior to adding the overlay agar (2000 μL) and pouring onto the surface of a minimal agar plate.

In vitro chromosome aberration assay

Dicholine L−5-MTHF (≥99% (w/w) purity; STP7.9.21) was evaluated for the ability to cause structural chromosome aberrations using cultured human peripheral blood lymphocytes in a GLP-compliant study, conducted according to OECD Testing Guideline 473 (2016 [34]).

Human peripheral blood lymphocytes from a healthy, non-smoking adult donor with no known recent exposure to genotoxic agents or ionizing radiation were used. Blood was collected from a single donor to reduce inter-individual variability. The donor was an adult volunteer laboratory employee and participation was strictly voluntary and declining had no consequences for employment. Written informed consent was obtained prior to collection, and donor confidentiality was protected through coding/anonymization such that no directly identifying information or clinical records were available to study personnel or the sponsor. Collection and use of samples were conducted in accordance with applicable local (German/EU) laws and regulations and laboratory internal procedures, consistent with generally recognized ethical principles for the use of human biological materials (including the Declaration of Helsinki, where applicable). Blood was collected by venipuncture into heparinized tubes and stored under sterile conditions at 4°C for up to 4 h prior to culture. Whole blood samples were then pre-cultured in the presence of phytohaemagglutinin (PHA).

A solubility test was performed up to the maximum recommended concentration of 2000 µg/mL. Based on the results of the solubility test minimum essential medium (MEM) cell culture medium was used as the solvent (MEM + 0% fetal bovine serum). The solvent was compatible with the survival of the cells and S9 activity.

The metaphases were prepared 24 h after start of exposure to the test article. The exposure interval was for either 4 h with (S9 mix) and without metabolic activation (short exposure, Experiment I) or 24 h without metabolic activation (continuous exposure, Experiment II). All concentrations and the vehicle control were tested in duplicate. For each concentration, 150 metaphases were scored per culture for structural chromosomal aberrations, for a total of 300 metaphases across two cultures. All structural chromosome aberrations such as breaks, fragments, deletions, exchanges and chromosomal disintegration were recorded. Gaps were recorded as well but were not included in the calculation of the aberration rates. Cytotoxicity was assessed by the mitotic index (% cells in mitosis, by counting the number of mitotic cells in 1000 cells) and values were compared with negative/solvent controls.

The negative control, as well as the highest test article concentration evaluated were treated in the presence of 5-bromo−2′-deoxyuridine (BrdU), parallel to the treatment groups, to reassure the proliferation index and/or replication time of the cultured lymphocytes. The proliferation index was determined by scoring the number of first, second and third metaphases in 100 cells per culture, if available. A pre-experiment for cytoxicity was conducted at the following concentrations without and with S9 mix: 5.8, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, 1500, and 2000 μg/mL. Vehicle control (treatment medium) was also tested concurrently. The x² Test for trend was conducted to test for concentration-related increases in chromosomal aberrations.

In vitro mammalian micronucleus assay

The GLP-compliant in vitro mammalian micronucleus assay was conducted to evaluate the potential of dicholine L−5-MTHF (≥99% purity; E00001) to induce micronuclei in human lymphocytes, following OECD Testing Guideline 487 (2023 [35]).

Peripheral blood lymphocytes were obtained from another individual donor using the same donor recruitment, informed consent, confidentiality, and sample collection procedures described in Section 2.2.2. Blood samples were drawn via venous puncture, stored in heparinized tubes at 4°C for up to 4 h, and pre-cultured (44–48 h) with PHA to stimulate cell division.

The assay included both short-term (4 h; Experiment I) and long-term (44 h; Experiment II) exposure conditions, without metabolic activation and a short-term (4 h; Experiment I) assay with S9 mix prepared from phenobarbital and β-naphthoflavone-induced Wistar rat liver microsomes (Eurofins Munich GmbH). Cytotoxicity was assessed using the cytokinesis-block proliferation index (CBPI) and cytostasis calculations, with a cytotoxicity threshold of 30% cytostasis.

A preliminary toxicity assay was conducted consistent with the protocol for the 4-hour exposure study, without and with S9 mix at concentrations of 3.9, 7.8, 15.6, 32.3, 62.5, 125, 250, 500, 1000, and 2000 μg/mL. Based on the results of this preliminary study, the following concentrations were evaluated in both experiments (4-hour and 44 -hour exposure): 62.5, 125, 250, 500, 1000, and 2000 μg/mL. However, only the 3 highest concentrations were evaluated for micronuclei frequency. At the end of the 4-hour incubation, the treatment medium was removed and the cells were washed twice with PBS + 10% fetal bovine serum. Subsequently the cells were incubated in complete culture medium + 6 µg/mL cytochalasin B for 40 h at 37°C and 5% CO2. For Experiment II, the test article was added in complete culture medium. One hour later, 6 µg/mL cytochalasin B was added and the cells were incubated for an additional 43 h at 37°C. At the end of the exposure period, the cell culture medium was removed and the cells were prepared for microscopic analysis. All analyses occurred in duplicate.

Micronuclei were analyzed in 2000 binucleated cells per concentration, with cells stained using acridine orange or propidium iodide and 4’,6-diamidino−2-phenylindole (DAPI). Negative controls (culture medium) and positive controls (MMS and CPA [clastogenicity controls], and colchicine [aneugenicity control]) were included to validate the assay. Analysis is performed manually or using the automated scoring system Metafer Scan System (Metasystems, Germany).

Statistical analyses, including the non-parametric χ² test and Cochran-Armitage trend test, were performed to evaluate the significance of micronucleated cell frequency and concentration-related effects.

Bacterial reverse mutation assay

Dicholine L−5-MTHF salt was not cytotoxic at any of the tested concentrations compared to the vehicle control and did not increase the number of revertant colonies (base pair changes or frameshifts) in any of the tested strains, at any concentration, as compared to the negative control samples with or without S9 activation (See Table 4, Table 5; Supplement Tables S2 and S3). Comprehensive safety assessment and weight-of-evidence integration

The present safety assessment addresses two related but distinct questions: first, whether dicholine L−5-MTHF introduces any genotoxic hazard as a novel salt form, and second, whether human safety can be supported by the established toxicology database for L−5-MTHF salts and choline. The negative bacterial reverse mutation, chromosome aberration, and micronucleus assays directly address the first question by covering gene mutation, structural chromosomal damage, and numerical chromosomal damage under standard OECD-compliant in vitro conditions. The second question is addressed through the expected dissociation of dicholine L−5-MTHF, the established biological role and safety profile of L−5-MTHF, and the low incremental choline exposure associated with the proposed use.

The sponsor commissioned laboratory analysis that confirmed that dicholine L−5-MTHF is freely soluble in water (650 g/L) and readily dissolved in aqueous solutions containing 85 mmol/L NaHCO3 and 40 mmol/L NaCl at pH 7, 3, and 1.8 within 30–60 s [20]. Based on a conductivity test, the conductivity of a 1.5 mM dicholine L−5-MTHF solution (220 µS/cm) was comparable to a 1.5 mM calcium L−5-MTHF salt solution (225 µS/cm). The conductivities of the ions formed indicate the same degree of ionization or dissociation. Therefore, the bioavailability of the component ions, L-methylfolate from choline and calcium salts, respectively, in humans are considered to be biologically equivalent. As expected, this provides confirmation that dicholine L−5-MTHF freely dissociates and can be assessed in this manner.

Although no repeat-dose oral toxicity study was conducted specifically with dicholine L−5-MTHF, this was not considered necessary for the present assessment because systemic safety is primarily informed by the established safety database for L−5-MTHF salts and choline, together with evidence that dicholine L−5-MTHF dissociates under physiologically relevant conditions. Therefore, the toxicological question specific to the dicholine salt form was addressed through the genotoxicity battery conducted directly with dicholine L−5-MTHF, while repeat-dose and clinical safety were evaluated using the broader weight of evidence for established L−5-MTHF salts and the low incremental choline exposure associated with the intended use.

Consistent with this approach, the three in vitro assays conducted on dicholine L−5-MTHF were all negative and therefore confirm a lack of mutagenic or genotoxic activity under the conditions of each assay. This is consistent with the findings of all calcium, glucosamine, and sodium L−5-MTHF salts that have been evaluated in similar assays [4], [16], [17], [27], [31], [32].

With regards to the oral safety of dicholine L−5-MTHF, several repeat-dose studies have been conducted with calcium L−5-MTHF and sodium L−5-MTHF that support a lack of adverse effects. Niederberger et al. [31] evaluated the safety of calcium L−5-MTHF in a 90-day subchronic toxicity study, prenatal developmental toxicity study, as well as a bacterial reverse mutation assay, in vitro mammalian gene mutation test, in vivo mammalian erythrocyte micronucleus test, and in vivo unscheduled DNA synthesis test in rat hepatocytes. All mutagenicity and genotoxicity assays were negative. In the subchronic study, consistent with OECD 408 testing guideline (date not reported), male and female Hanlbm:Wistar (SPF) rats were administered calcium methylfolate via gavage for 90 days at doses of 0, 25, 100, and 400 mg/kg bw/day. The highest dose was concluded to be the NOAEL, as no adverse effects were noted in any of the groups, as compared to the control animals. One finding reported was creatine kinase (CK) levels being numerically lower than those in the control group in all calcium methylfolate-dosed males, reaching significance in the low- and high dose groups; however, these remained within historical control values, while the control group mean values were higher than the historical control range. In high-dose females, CK levels were statistically higher than controls. In the developmental toxicity study, pregnant female rats were administered the test article by gavage at doses of 0, 100, 300, or 1000 mg/kg bw/day on gestation days (GD) 5–19. Based on a lack of adverse effects in dams or fetuses at any dose level, the NOAEL was concluded to be 1000 mg/kg bw/day, the highest dose tested.

In a subchronic study conducted by Morris-Schaffer et al. [32], sodium L−5-MTHF was administered by gavage to rats (10 animals/sex/group) at doses of 0, 10, 300, or 1000 mg/kg bw/day for 90 days. No test article-related adverse effects were observed at any dose level of either sex and the NOAEL was concluded to be 1000 mg/kg bw/day. A statistically significant reduction in creatine kinase (CK) was measured in males in the 2 highest dose groups, as well as females in the highest dose group. This finding is consistent with the findings by Niederberger et al. [31], with calcium methylfolate administration to rats at up to 400 mg/kg bw/day for 90 days. This was considered an adaptive and not adverse effect, related to the role of 5-MTHF and creatine in the one-carbon cycle (initial increase in creatine followed by compensatory reduction). This conclusion, for it being an adaptive response, was noted during the EFSA Panel’s review of calcium methylfolate [17] and accepted by the US FDA (GRN 915 [27]). Therefore, this is not considered to be a toxicological concern.

A large number of clinical studies have established the general safety and tolerability of reduced folate salts following repeated intake over several months or years in healthy adults, pregnant women, and other study populations at doses up to 15 mg/day, which is well above the adult UL established for folic acid [36], [37], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44]. These studies include interventions designed to evaluate folate status, homocysteine lowering, pregnancy-related supplementation, and other clinical or nutritional endpoints, and collectively provide substantial human exposure experience with L−5-MTHF salts.

Importantly, this clinical evidence has also been reviewed by authoritative scientific and regulatory bodies, including EFSA, JECFA, and US FDA, and has been considered adequate, together with supporting toxicological data, to support the safe use of established L−5-MTHF salts as folate sources. Although adverse-event monitoring was not uniformly reported across all studies, and many studies were not designed primarily as safety studies, no consistent pattern of treatment-related adverse effects has been identified. Overall, the available clinical evidence supports that reduced folate forms are well tolerated in humans at nutritionally relevant intake levels and at higher supplemental doses used in clinical settings.

Unmetabolized folic acid

Synthetic folic acid is absorbed largely unmetabolized and must be reduced by dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) to enter the biologically active folate pool. Human hepatic DHFR activity is slow and highly variable, in part due to genetic differences, which can result in the appearance of unmetabolized folic acid (UMFA) in circulation at higher intakes [41], [45]. Folic acid doses above 200–300 mcg (up to 600 mcg) have been reported to result in elevated UMFA in the blood stream [20], [38], [39], [46], [47].

Circulating UMFA has been reported to be potentially associated with adverse health outcomes, including masking of vitamin B12 deficiency, reduced natural killer cell activity, promotion of certain preexisting cancers, and neurological effects [41], [45], [48], [49]. Notably, based on in vitro assays, rodents exhibit substantially higher DHFR activity than humans [41], which may limit the relevance of some preclinical folic acid toxicity studies for human risk assessment. In contrast, reduced folate forms, including dicholine folate, do not require DHFR-mediated reduction and therefore do not contribute to circulating UMFA. While additional research is warranted, these differences suggest that reduced folate forms may mitigate concerns associated with high folic acid intake.

Choline safety

Dietary choline is absorbed in the small intestine via carrier-mediated transport. A portion of ingested choline is metabolized by gut microbes to betaine, which can function as a methyl donor, or to methylamines, which do not [14]. Choline occurs in foods as free choline and in esterified forms, including phosphocholine, glycerophosphocholine, sphingomyelin, and phosphatidylcholine. Lecithin is a phosphatidylcholine-rich fraction commonly used interchangeably with phosphatidylcholine [14]. Pancreatic enzymes liberate free choline from esterified dietary forms. Free choline enters the portal circulation to the liver, whereas phosphatidylcholine may be absorbed via lymph in chylomicrons. Choline is distributed to tissues by diffusion and transporter-mediated uptake, including a specific carrier across the blood-brain barrier. The liver and kidney are major sites of choline uptake, with renal oxidation of choline to betaine contributing to one-carbon metabolism [14].

An adequate intake (AI) for total dietary choline has been set by the National Academy of Medicine (NAM) in the United States at 550 mg/day for men and 425 mg/day for non-pregnant women, 450 mg/day for pregnant women and 550 mg/day for lactating women [14]. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) [50] set an AI of 400 mg/day for adults and 480 mg/day and 520 mg/day for pregnant and lactating women, respectively. Although data are limited, several dietary intake surveys have calculated mean choline intake estimates in adults ranging from 269 to 468 mg/day based on studies in several EU countries, USA, Canada, New Zealand, some of which included pregnant women [51], [52], [53], [54], [55], [56], [57], [58]. As reviewed by Zuk et al. [59] these public dietary intake surveys remain the predominant data set. In another recent Norwegian survey [55], it was reported that the geometric mean (95% prediction interval) energy-adjusted total choline intake was 260 (170, 389) mg/day. In pregnant Canadian women, only 24.8% of women were reported to meet the AI for choline based on dietary intake [57], although a large portion of the population were consuming folic acid above the UL. This latter element underlines the importance of appropriate folate supplementation in light of concerns of elevated unmetabolized folic acid being associated with adverse health outcomes (as discussed in Section 4.2).

As such, reported dietary intakes of choline in some populations are below the AI established by the IOM and EFSA, which may further decrease in populations decreasing meat consumption [56], [60]. While this does not allow quantification of inadequacy at the population level, it suggests that current dietary intake patterns may be insufficient to ensure adequacy for all individuals, particularly those with increased requirements. This is because by definition, “Adequate Intakes” are set in the absence of sufficient data to establish an Estimated Average Requirement (EAR) and therefore cannot be used to estimate the prevalence of inadequacy in populations.

The IOM [14] reviewed the clinical literature to establish a UL for choline intake. Choline doses that are orders of magnitude greater than estimated intake from food have been associated with body odor, sweating, salivation, hypotension, and hepatotoxicity in humans. On the basis of considerations of causality, relevance, and the quality and completeness of the database, hypotension was selected as the critical effect in deriving a UL, whereas fishy body odor was selected as the secondary consideration. There were no adequate data demonstrating a clear NOAEL in relation to excess choline intake. A LOAEL of approximately 7.5 g/day of choline was identified by IOM from evaluation of a pilot study that reported hypotension in seven patients treated with choline for Alzheimer senile dementia [61] and reports of fishy body odor in individuals treated with choline for tardive dyskinesia and Huntington’s disease [62], [63]. Boyd et al. [61] treated seven older adult patients with 4 g/day of oral choline as choline chloride for 2 weeks followed by 2 weeks of choline at 7.5 g/day. At 4 g/day of choline, daily blood pressure recordings revealed no hypotension. In addition, there were no reports of nausea or diarrhea or other evidence of cholinergic effects at this dose level. At 7.5 g/day of choline, nausea, diarrhea, and a small decrease in blood pressure were reported in some patients. IOM [14] selected a UF of 2 to apply to the LOAEL from human studies reviewed in the derivation of a UL of 3.5 g/day (scaled to body weight limits for infants and children). Current reviews on the nutritional necessity and safety of choline supplementation still endorse this upper limit due to a lack of any new findings [64].

Therefore, under the proposed conditions of use, the intake of an additional 187 µg/day of choline in adults will also not have an appreciable impact on overall or cumulative intake, as compared to the AI for choline between 450 and 550 mg/day in adults.

Overall conclusion

These data support the safety of dicholine L−5-MTHF for its anticipated use as a dietary folate source. A lack of genotoxic potential was confirmed in a standard in vitro battery conducted with dicholine L−5-MTHF, consisting of a bacterial reverse mutation, chromosome aberration, and mammalian micronucleus assays. In addition, a clinical study conducted with dicholine L−5-MTHF confirmed it provides bioavailable L−5-MTHF, consistent with other reduced folate salts. Due to the physiological dissociation of reduced folate salts under physiological conditions, the safety of dicholine L−5-MTHF is established based on its individual ions, choline and L−5-MTHF. As such, existing studies on other folate salts, including preclinical and clinical studies, provide sufficient data to support the safety of dicholine L−5-MTHF under intended conditions of use. The preclinical studies provide very high margins of safety, while clinical studies have long supported safe intakes of various reduced folate salts at doses even above the UL of 1 mg/day. Overall, the available evidence supports the conclusion that there are no safety concerns with the proposed use of dicholine L−5-MTHF.
