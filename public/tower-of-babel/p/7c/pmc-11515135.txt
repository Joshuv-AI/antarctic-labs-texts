Nonlinear parametric models of viscoelastic fluid flows
Authors: C M Oishi, A A Kaptanoglu, J Nathan Kutz, S L Brunton
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractReduced-order models (ROMs) have been widely adopted in fluid mechanics, particularly in the context of Newtonian fluid flows. These models offer the ability to predict complex dynamics, such as instabilities and oscillations, at a considerably reduced computational cost. In contrast, the reduced-order modelling of non-Newtonian viscoelastic fluid flows remains relatively unexplored. This work leverages the sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics (SINDy) algorithm to develop interpretable ROMs for viscoelastic flows. In particular, we explore a benchmark oscillatory viscoelastic flow on the four-roll mill geometry using the classical Oldroyd-B fluid. This flow exemplifies many canonical challenges associated with non-Newtonian flows, including transitions, asymmetries, instabilities, and bifurcations arising from the interplay of viscous and elastic forces, all of which require expensive computations in order to resolve the fast timescales and long transients characteristic of such flows. First, we demonstrate the effectiveness of our data-driven surrogate model to predict the transient evolution and accurately reconstruct the spatial flow field for fixed flow parameters. We then develop a fully parametric, nonlinear model capable of capturing the dynamic variations as a function of the Weissenberg number. While the training data are predominantly concentrated on a limit cycle regime for moderate Wi , we show that the parametrized model can be used to extrapolate, accurately predicting the dominant dynamics in the case of high Weissenberg numbers. The proposed methodology represents an initial step in applying machine learning and reduced-order modelling techniques to viscoelastic flows.Keywords: viscoelastic fluids, computational fluid dynamics, data-driven models, sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics, reduced-order models, machine learning

Introduction

Viscoelastic fluids are an important class of non-Newtonian materials that exhibit both viscous (liquid-like) and elastic (solid-like) properties. Due to their importance in a wide range of applications, computational methods have been widely adopted to solve viscoelastic fluid flows, leading to new insights in non-Newtonian mechanics. Despite advances in scientific computing for modelling and simulating viscoelastic fluid flows [1], many problems remain computationally challenging, such as resolving viscoelastic instabilities and elastic turbulence [2]. Therefore, it is essential to develop enhanced numerical schemes for non-Newtonian fluid mechanics based on machine learning algorithms and data-driven strategies.

Reduced-order models (ROMs) have been successfully developed and applied to a wide range of Newtonian fluid flows [3–16]. The goal is to develop a low-dimensional surrogate model that captures the dominant coherent behaviour of a fluid flow at a fraction of the computational cost of a high-fidelity simulation. Classical approaches typically involve Galerkin projection of the governing Navier–Stokes equations onto a low-dimensional data-driven basis obtained via proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) [3,4,8]. The POD procedure is data-driven, providing a generalization of the Fourier transform that is tailored to a particular flow of interest. The Galerkin projection procedure results in a low-dimensional set of nonlinear ordinary differential equations for the amplitudes of these orthogonal modes, and these differential equations may be useful for efficient prediction, estimation, and control. Although the POD step is data-driven, the Galerkin step is intrusive and often unstable [11], requiring a flexible working code to simulate the flow, and the nonlinearity typically becomes quite complex except for incompressible flows. There are non-intrusive extensions based on operator inference [10,13–15] and several extensions to exotic physics, including compressible flows [17] and plasma physics [18]. However, there is an increasing trend to replace the Galerkin projection step entirely with machine learning approaches [19–22].

The sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics (SINDy) [23] has been particularly useful for learning accurate and efficient dynamical systems models of complex fluid flows entirely from data. SINDy represents the differential equation governing POD mode amplitudes as a sparse combination from a library of candidate functions that might describe the dynamics; for fluid flows, this library is often chosen to be polynomial [24]. SINDy has been widely applied to learn data-driven nonlinear models across a range of application domains, including fluid [24–30] and plasma dynamics [18,31], turbulence closures [32–34], nonlinear optics [35], and numerical integration schemes [36]. Similarly, several extensions have been introduced, including to identify partial differential equations [37,38], tensor formulations [39], control theory [40], integral and weak formulations [41–45], and systems with stochastic dynamics [46,47].

In this work, we demonstrate the broad applicability of SINDy-based reduced-order modelling techniques for viscoelastic flows (see figure 1). In particular, based on the ideas of the viscoelastic POD [48,49], we have identified coherent structures that can be well characterized by SINDy. This work builds upon the limited ROM efforts in non-Newtonian flows, which include a POD-Galerkin reduced-order method for a viscoelastic model [50] and corresponding stabilized version of this method [51]. In both works, the number of POD modes required is quite high (e.g. r>30 ). The current work shows that with partial knowledge of the physics, it is possible to develop accurate models with significantly fewer modes. Recently a similar approach has been successfully applied to study electroconvection [27,46] and magnetohydrodynamics [18].

From numerical measurement data of oscillatory flows on the four-roll mill geometry using the Oldroyd-B fluid, we first obtain a low-dimensional linear subspace by POD in order to extract dominant coherent structures in the flow. The SINDy algorithm is then applied to identify the dynamical system for flows characterized by a fixed set of parameters. We find a sparse ROM that produces efficient and provably bounded forecasts of the solution of the complex dynamics, as well as accurately reconstructs the flow fields of the viscoelastic stress tensor on the governing equations. The elaborated methodology is then shown to be useful for learning parametric models that can capture variations in the Weissenberg number, an important non-dimensional parameter widely used to describe the elastic effects on viscoelastic flows [1,52].

Based on molecular theories, the adoption of the conformation tensor is a well-known way to derive constitutive viscoelastic equations assuming that polymer molecules can interact with the flow. Specifically, when using the dumbbell model of a polymer molecule, the conformation tensor is mathematically defined as the tensor product of the end-to-end vector, which connects the extreme beads at the ends of the chain. In simpler terms, the conformation tensor is a tool used in viscoelastic models to describe how polymer molecules behave when they flow. It is based on the idea that these molecules can stretch and move with the flow. When we use a simple model called the dumbbell model, we imagine each polymer molecule as a pair of beads connected by a spring. The conformation tensor is then created by looking at the vector that goes from one bead to the other and using that vector to form a tensor, which helps us understand the molecule’s shape and orientation in the flow.

We will demonstrate the methods developed in this work on the canonical four-roll mill problem. The four-roll mill was first applied by Thomases & Shelley [54] to investigate the effects of an extensional flow (stagnation point flow) in viscoelastic fluids (see also the experimental study of [55]). This benchmark has also been applied to investigate viscoelastic instabilities [49] and has led to new insights into elastic turbulence [56]. The geometry is given by a regular array of cylinders that are rotating, driving a rich sequence of behaviours and bifurcations. The presence of stagnation points between the rollers can cause high stretching of the polymer stress [57], which is of particular interest for computation and experiment [58,59]. From a numerical perspective, the presence of stagnation points poses computational challenges, such as controlling the exponential growth of the stress tensor (2.1d ) in regions close to these points. Some recent advances in numerical schemes for solving viscoelastic fluid flows with stagnation points, geometric singularities, and regions with a high rate of deformation have been discussed in [1,60–62].

In summary, we explore the four-mill benchmark, simulating the system of equations (2.1a–d) in a square domain [0,(n)2π]×[0,(n)2π] subject to periodic boundary conditions for all fields. Equations (2.1a–d) were solved by the open-source software Basilisk [63], which solves the mass and momentum equations using a projection scheme combined with the Bell–Colella–Glaz advection scheme in a finite-difference context. This stabilization scheme is essential to numerically preserve the symmetric positive definite property of the conformation tensor 𝐂 during the transient fluid flow. Reduced-order model framework

The primary contribution of this work is to develop data-driven techniques for obtaining ROMs of viscoelastic flows. System identification

One of the goals of decomposing 𝒒 into a low-dimensional POD basis is to find a ROM for the low-dimensional temporal modes 𝒂(t) . There is a long history in fluid dynamics of building POD-Galerkin models [4,8,12,17] obtained by substituting the POD expansion of 𝒒 into the governing Navier–Stokes (or in our case, the viscoelastic analogues) and integrating out the spatial degrees of freedom through orthogonal projection to obtain a differential equation for the evolution of 𝒂 :

These POD-Galerkin models have been used as computationally efficient ROMs for decades. However, this procedure is intrusive, requiring access to a working simulation where it is possible to isolate individual terms in the governing equations to compute 𝒇 . Moreover, POD-Galerkin models are often unstable [11].

Recent data-driven techniques enable the non-intrusive learning of a model 𝒇 without requiring the governing equations. We learn viscoelastic ROMs with the SINDy [23] method, which uses sparse regression to learn a model 𝒇 with the fewest terms possible from a library of candidate functions that might describe the dynamics.

Model stability

ROMs do not generally come with guarantees that their predictions of the dynamics will stay bounded, even if the original system has global boundedness [11]. One advantage of the identified SINDy models here is that a nonlinear stability analysis illustrates the conditions under which the resulting system is stable for any initial condition 𝒂(0) , i.e. the model is long-time bounded for any initial condition [71,72]. Results for a fixed Weissenberg number

We first explore the use of SINDy to develop ROMs for viscoelastic fluids at a fixed Weissenburg number. The majority of applications of SINDy in fluids have developed models for a fixed set of parameters, with some notable exceptions [28,73]. Developing a model for a fixed parameter (e.g. so that Nw=1 ) is typically simpler, making it a reasonable starting point for our analysis.

Simulations were performed using a uniform mesh size of 128×128 for the two levels of periodicity n=1 and n=2 and the time-step was δt=0.0001 for all cases. To capture the essential transition from a steady state flow to an oscillatory regime, we run the simulation for a long nondimensional time, i.e. tend=750 . We fix the Reynolds number at Re=1 to reduce the inertial effects during the transient flow, allowing us to focus on the onset of viscoelastic instabilities. The mesh and Reynolds numbers used here are similar to those in [56]. In addition, consistent with previous numerical studies [49,74], we use βWi<0.5 for all simulations. As commented in [55], the viscosity ratio β is another important viscoelastic parameter that can influence the flow transitions. The recent work of [75] demonstrated that the flow transitions in viscoelastic systems, with purely elastic flow structures, can be numerically captured with β in the range 0.6≤β≤0.95 . In addition, there is an important connection between the elastic and viscous forces [52], which can be captured by changing the Weissenberg number as well as the viscosity ratio. Since high values of β contribute to the stability of the method, we have fixed β=0.9 .

To illustrate the elastic effects on the four-roll mill geometry, two simulations with n=2 are shown in figure 3. This figures shows the transient behaviour of the first component of the conformation tensor 𝐂 , denoted here as Cxx , for a sensor located at the central stagnation point of the domain. For a low Weissenberg number ( Wi=2 ), the flow becomes steady after the first transient peak, while for Wi=4 there is a transition of the flow into an oscillatory regime. These results are qualitatively in agreement with those presented in [49,56], e.g. the choice of the Weissenberg number is critical to characterize the long-time dynamics and bifurcations from steady symmetries to oscillatory regimes in this geometry. We have limited the Weissenberg number to Wi<5 in our study to avoid elastic turbulence, which is beyond the scope of this work. Figure 3 also shows the snapshots of the trace of the conformation tensor 𝐂 , denoted here as tr(𝐂) , at the final simulation time. The trace of the conformation tensor is frequently used in the viscoelastic literature [1] to quantify the elastic energy of the system. For Wi=2 the flow is steady with symmetric patterns for 𝐂 , while for Wi=4 the flow becomes asymmetric, resulting in an oscillatory regime. When computing a POD basis, we use data after the first peak [49] so that our ROMs capture steady-state or long-time dynamics.

Stability of the system

To assess the stability of system (4.1), we investigate the conditions for K˙<0 :

It is now clear that δ1≤0 is a sufficient condition for K˙<0 for large ‖𝒂‖2 by considering a1(t)=0 and a2(t)≠0 , or vice versa. Likewise, we conclude that a sufficient condition is δ1+δ2<0 from the line a1(t)=a2(t) . With these conditions, we see that the term proportional to (δ3−δ4) remains subdominant for large enough ‖𝒂‖2≫1 and therefore there is no boundedness-related restriction on these coefficients.

To summarize, for large enough a1≫1 , a2≫1 , these conditions on δ1 and δ2 guarantee that the temporal derivative of the energy is negative, meaning all trajectories fall into a monotonically trapping region, similar to that found for some quadratic fluid models [71,72]. These conclusions are unchanged when the Weissenberg number is later included in the model as a control parameter, since the new terms only provide low-order contributions to the energy that are irrelevant far from the origin. ROMs built satisfying these constraints can be trusted to produce bounded predictions of the dynamics even with new and arbitrary initial conditions and arbitrary time intervals.

Results for system parametrized by Weissenberg number

As a challenging test case, we develop a SINDy model that is parametrized by the Weissenberg number Wi and show that this model is valid over a wide range of values. In particular, we apply SINDy with control [40,76] using the library Θ(𝒂,w) with the control input defined as w=1/Wi . For the roll-mill simulations with n=1 , we train on the values Wi={4,4.35,4.5} and test on the values Wi=4.2 and Wi=5.0 . The Wi values chosen for training are specifically selected to maintain the flow in an oscillatory regime. Consequently, the dynamics for the interpolation value Wi=4.2 also exhibit the same oscillatory behaviour, as evident in figure 8a . However, for Wi=5.0 , the flow undergoes a dynamic transition to chaotic behaviour, driven by increasing elastic effects, as illustrated in figure 8b . This test value is chosen to demonstrate the extrapolation capabilities of our model, particularly in capturing the transition from an oscillatory regime to a chaotic one. The increase in Weissenberg number also leads to a significant increase in the first component of the conformation tensor at the stagnation point, shown in figure 8, aligning with findings from prior studies (see [56]). Discussion

This work explores the sparse reduced-order modelling of viscoelastic fluid flows with the SINDy algorithm. SINDy has found extensive applications in fluid dynamics, yet its application to reduced-order modelling of viscoelastic flows is still in its early stages. This study is the first investigation in this domain and serves to showcase the ability of sparse modelling to capture complex, canonical viscoelastic fluid dynamics. Importantly, the models developed are stable by construction, interpretable, and highly structured, possessing symmetries in the model coefficients. Furthermore, we show that these models can be used to extrapolate beyond the training data.

In addition, we construct models that are explicitly parametrized by the Weissenberg number, which controls the dominant bifurcations in the flow. Most SINDy models in the literature are not explicitly parametrized by a dominant non-dimensional number, which is a more challenging modelling problem. Our findings indicate that these parametrized models are accurate for both interpolation and limited extrapolation tasks. Moreover, based on our initial tests at higher Weissenberg numbers (see appendix A), it appears necessary to expand the ROM dimension to accurately predict transitions and behaviours associated with elastic turbulence [77,78], which is a subject of ongoing work.

Here we consider a canonical viscoelastic configuration using the Oldroyd-B model in the four-roll mill benchmark geometry. Our modelling strategy begins with the viscoelastic POD to reduce the dimensionality and extract a few dominant viscoelastic coherent structures in the flow. We then model the dynamics of the first leading POD modes with SINDy and develop algebraic for the higher harmonic modes in terms of these driving modes. The comparisons conducted between the simulation data and the results obtained from our surrogate models serve as compelling evidence, confirming the stability and accuracy of our ROMs. Therefore, these models provide precise predictions while requiring only minimal computational resources. Furthermore, we show that accurate full flow field reconstructions are possible by recombining the POD modes using the mode coefficients predicted by the SINDy model.

We also discuss the interpretability of the model, as this is a crucial challenge for data-driven system identification. Motivated by the POD-Galerkin models, our formulation resulted in a compact and interpretable dynamical system model directly from the simulation data of an oscillatory viscoelastic fluid flow. This work also investigates the performance of our model at higher Weissenberg numbers that were not included in the training dataset. The SINDy model shows reasonable qualitative agreement on this extrapolation task, with a maximum reconstruction error below 10% ; however, future work will investigate how to improve the model in the chaotic regime.

There are a number of future directions based on this work. A first improvement in the context of SINDy is the incorporation of statistical representation of the coefficients obtained during the system identification process, as recently presented in [79,80]. These Bayesian-enhanced SINDy methodologies are fundamental for constructing data-driven ROMs in the context of elastic turbulence since quantifying uncertainty in the estimated parameters will be a challenging task. We considered the Oldroyd-B model, although this model fails for extensional flows at high Weissenberg number [2]. A natural extension is the development of ROMs for nonlinear viscoelastic models, such as the Phan-Thien and Tanner (PTT) and Finitely Extensible Nonlinear Elastic (FENE-P) fluids, in non-viscometric flows, for instance cross-slot geometry [1]. Further, it will be interesting to include the parametric effect of viscosity ratio and extensibility factor in addition to the Weissenberg number. Because our strategy exhibits exponential scaling with the number of parameters, addressing multiparameter viscoelastic models (such as PTT and FENE-P) will require more sophisticated SINDy optimization algorithms. This becomes particularly crucial for enhancing computational efficiency in future models. Ongoing work by the authors will also investigate the asymptotic behaviour of the model. Finally, data-driven investigations of more complex non-Newtonian systems involving elastoviscoplastic fluids [81] would be interesting.
