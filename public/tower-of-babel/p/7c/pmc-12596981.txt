Untargeted metabolomics reveals phenolic compound dynamics during mung bean fermentation
Authors: Shuangjing Lang, Lijuan Liu, Zhenjiang Li, Shilin Liu, Jiaxin Liang, Lele Lu, Lidong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to elucidate the changes and enrichment mechanisms of phenolic in mung beans subjected to solid-state fermentation by Aspergillus Niger. Whole mung bean seeds were fermented, the total phenolic, flavonoid content and antioxidant capacity were measured during fermentation. Phenolic at different fermentation stages was performed using UHPLC-FTMS. The results showed that fermentation significantly increased the total phenolic and flavonoid content, antioxidant capacity of mung beans (P < 0.05). A total of 61 phenolic differental metabolites were identified, the top 22 phenolic expression levels with significantly upregulated increased significantly with the time, the 32 phenolic esexpression was significantly positively correlated with antioxidant capacity. The composition and enrichment of phenolic compounds during fermentation were primarily influenced by the biosynthesis pathways associated with flavonoids, flavonols, isoflavones, phenylpropanoids, and other plant secondary metabolites. In conclusion, SSF with Aspergillus Niger is an effective strategy for improving the phenolic content and antioxidant capacity of mung beans.

Introduction

Vigna radiata L. (commonly known as mung bean) is an annual herb that is widely cultivated worldwide. Mung beans are important edible beans and traditional functional foods that are rich in a variety of nutrients (e.g., starch, protein, fat, and dietary fiber), and bioactive substances (e.g., polysaccharides, peptides, and polyphenols) (Zhou et al., 2022). Among these bioactive compounds, polyphenols, which are secondary metabolites composed of benzene rings and phenolic hydroxyl groups, are particularly noteworthy. The primary classes of polyphenols in mung beans include phenolic acids (e.g., melilotoside and dattelic acid), flavonoids (e.g., naringenin, apigenin, daidzein, and dihydrokaempferol), and anthocyanins (e.g., pelargonidin 3-rutinoside) (Wu et al., 2025). Numerous studies have highlighted the potential health benefits of polyphenols, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer activities, as well as the prevention of coronary heart disease and mitigation of diabetes-related complications (Lu, Li and Zhang, 2025). The polyphenol content of mung beans varies significantly among different cultivars owing to factors such as the growing environment, storage conditions, and processing methods. Conventional approaches to enhance the polyphenol content of mung beans include gene mutation and grafting; however, these methods are often hindered by low success rates and technical challenges (Zhao et al., 2017). Germination-based strategies have also been explored to increase polyphenol content, although their efficiency is limited and may constrain the broader application of mung beans in food products (Basha & Maheswaraiah, 2017; Li et al., 2023).

Solid-state fermentation (SSF) is a traditional food-processing biotechnology that has been historically employed in the production of wine, vinegar, soy sauce, and various regionally distinctive foods (e.g., liquor, fermented bean sauce, and fermented bean curd). SSF offers several advantages, including a relatively simple fermentation process, strong resistance to contamination, ease of product extraction, and minimal wastewater generation (Jin et al., 2024). Recent studies have shown that SSF can effectively modify the content, composition, and distribution of polyphenols in whole grain. For instance, Cyanobacteria, Monascus, and Morchella fermentation significantly increased the total phenol content and antioxidant capacity of chickpeas, Job's tears, and barley, respectively, via the upregulation of flavonoids, anthocyanins, and phenylpropane biosynthesis pathways (Xiao, 2018；Du et al., 2022； Jiao et al., 2019). Phenylalanine aminolyase and tyrosine aminolyase are key enzymes in polyphenol biosynthesis, and microbial fermentation influences their gene expression and enzymatic transformation, promoting polyphenol accumulation (Yu et al., 2024). The selection of suitable strains is a key factor in the SSF. Safe strains commonly used in fermented food processing include lactic acid bacteria, yeast, and molds. Our previous research revealed that molds, particularly Aspergillus niger, are the dominant microorganisms that enhance phenolic substances in mung beans (Liu et al., 2024). Aspergillus Niger, an aerobic filamentous fungus, is certified as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) by the US Drug Administration. It exhibits remarkable capabilities for producing cellulase, amylase, and protease and is characterized by high enzyme yields, non-toxicity, and strong reproductive capacity (Zhang et al., 2024). Despite its frequent application in feed fermentation, the use of Aspergillus niger in fermented food remains relatively limited, and the specific compositional changes, regulatory mechanisms, and enrichment pathways of mung bean polyphenols during Aspergillus niger–mediated SSF have yet to be fully elucidated.

This study investigated the dynamic changes in the total phenol content and antioxidant capacity of mung beans during Aspergillus niger-mediated SSF over different fermentation periods. Using untargeted metabolomics, we analyzed the alterations in the mung bean metabolome at different fermentation periods, identified differential polyphenolic metabolites, and elucidated the enriched biosynthetic pathways. Our findings aimed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of polyphenol accumulation during Aspergillus niger fermentation and support the development of novel functional foods derived from fermented mung beans.

Materials and methods

Materials

Mung beans were purchased from Heilongjiang Cang Natural Ecological Agriculture Co., Ltd. Aspergillus niger (CGMCC 3.3928) was obtained from the China General Microbial Conservation Center. Czapek Agar (Qingdao High-tech Industrial Park Haibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), gallic acid (≥98 %), and rutin (≥98 %) standards (Shanghai Maclin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.) were of analytical grade. Folinol reagent, ferrous sulfate, 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6- sulfonic acid)ammonium salt (ABTS), and 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl(DPPH) were obtained from the Research Institute of Biological Engineering, Nanjing, China. Methanol, acetonitrile, formic acid, deionized water, and propanol (chromatographic grade) were purchased from Fisher Scientific.

Solid-state fermentation

Preparation of Aspergillus Niger seed liquid: Under aseptic conditions in a laminar flow cabinet (SN-VD-650, Shanghai Shangp Instrument Equipment Co., Ltd.), freeze-dried Aspergillus Niger powder was inoculated into Czapek agar slant medium. The culture was incubated in a constant temperature and humidity incubator (HWS-250S, Tianjin Lepotri Instrument Co., Ltd.) at 28 °C for 5–6 d to reactivate the strains. Subcultures were restricted to 1–2 generations to ensure their metabolic vitality. The activated Aspergillus Niger was further cultured on Czapek agar slants at 28 °C until abundant mature spores were grown. The spores were carefully washed from the slant surface with sterile water to prepare a spore suspension. Viable spore counts were determined using the dilution plating method, and sterile water was used to adjust the spore suspension to a final concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL.

Pretreatment of mung bean raw materials: Mung beans were rinsed thoroughly and soaked in ultrapure water (1:3 w/v) at 25 °C for 12 h. Defective seeds (e.g., split, discolored, or insect-damaged seeds) were manually removed from the sample. 50 g of hydrated beans (wet weight basis) were transferred into a 250 mL sterilized conical flask. The beans were boiled for 8 min, exposed to ultraviolet sterilization on a laminar flow bench for 30 min, and then cooled to room temperature before use.

Based on prior optimization of fermentation conditions, Aspergillus Niger was used to conduct SSF on pretreated mung beans. Specifically, an 8 mL/100 g aliquot of the Aspergillus Niger seed suspension (1 × 108 CFU/mL) per 100 g of mung beans was added to the sterilized conical flask containing the beans. The contents were thoroughly mixed, and the flask was incubated at 26.5 °C and 80 % relative humidity. Samples were collected on fermentation days 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 and labeled as unfermented mung beans (UFM), FM2, FM4, FM6, FM8, and FM10, respectively. Immediately following each sampling, the fermented materials were rapidly freeze-dried using a CTFD-10S-U freeze dryer (Qingdao Yonghe Chuangxin Electronic Technology Co., Ltd.) and stored at −80 °C for further analyses.

Determination of phenolic substances

Phenolic compounds were extracted according to the protocol described by Cristian et al. (2019).

Free phenol extraction: Mung bean seeds were finely ground and passed through an 80-mesh sieve. An aliquot (5 g) of the ground sample was accurately weighed and mixed with 50 mL of 80 % (v/v) ethanol. The mixture was sonicated (KQ-250E, Kunshan Ultrasonic Instrument Co., Ltd.) for 2 h and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 20 min (TD25-WS, Hunan Xiang Instrument Experimental Instrument Development Co., Ltd.). The resulting supernatant was collected, and the precipitate was re-extracted using the same procedure. The combined supernatants were concentrated to approximately 5 mL using a rotary evaporator (RE-3000B, Zhengzhou Yarong Instrument Co., Ltd.) and adjusted to a final volume of 15 mL using absolute ethanol.

Combined phenol extraction: The residue from the free phenol extraction was collected, followed by the addition of 20 mL of 2 M sodium hydroxide. The mixture was digested for 1.5 h, and the pH was subsequently adjusted to 2. An equivalent volume of n-hexane was added to remove the fat layer. Subsequently, 20 mL of ethyl acetate was added, and the mixture was extracted for 10 min. After centrifugation and removal of the supernatant, extraction was repeated thrice. The pooled organic phase was evaporated to a final volume of 10 mL using ultrapure water. The extract was filtered through a 0.5 μm organic membrane. Finally, the free and combined phenolic extracts were mixed to obtain the mung bean polyphenol extract, which was stored at −20 °C until further use.

Determination of polyphenol content: Total phenolic content was determined according to the method described by Xie et al. (2021) using the Folin–Ciocalteu colorimetric assay. A standard curve was constructed by plotting the gallic acid concentration (mg/L) on the horizontal coordinate and absorbance on the vertical coordinate, yielding y = 0.0091× + 0.0153 (R2 = 0.9984). After diluting the mung bean polyphenol extract eight-fold, the absorbance was measured according to the standard curve, and the total phenolic content was calculated.

Determination of flavonoid content: A standard curve was established by plotting the rutin concentration (mg/L) on the x-axis against the absorbance on the y-axis, yielding y = 0.7972× + 0.0574 (R2 = 0.9969). After an eight-fold dilution of the extract, the absorbance was determined, and the flavonoid content was calculated based on a standard curve.

Determination of antioxidant capacity

DPPH radical scavenging capacity was analyzed as described by Sobia et al. (2024). Briefly, 1 mL of the sample extract was mixed with 4 mL of DPPH solution (adjusted to OD517 = 0.7). The mixture was shaken thoroughly and kept in the dark for 30 min. The absorbance was recorded at 517 nm using an enzyme spectrometer. A standard curve was prepared by plotting the VC concentration (μM) on the x-axis against the DPPH scavenging activity on the y-axis using the following equation: y = 0.0046× + 0.034 (R2 = 0.9956). The results were expressed as as μmol VC equivalents per gram (μmol VC/g).

The ABTS+· radical scavenging capacity was determined according to Khatun and Mollah I (2024). Briefly, 7 mmol/L ABTS solution was mixed with 2.5 mmol/L potassium persulfate solution and incubated in the dark for 16 h to form the ABTS+· working solution. Ethanol was then used to adjust the absorbance to an OD734 of 0.7. Then, 1 mL of the sample extract was mixed with 4 mL of the working solution, and the absorbance was measured at 734 nm after 6 min. A standard curve was prepared by plotting the VC concentration (μM) on the x-axis against the ABTS+· scavenging capacity on the y-axis using the following equation: y=0.0069x+0.0521 (R2=0.9956). The results were expressed as μmol VC/g.

Ferric reducing power was determined according to Chanya et al. (2024). A 1 mL of sample extract was incubated with 5 mL of FRAP working solution and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at 593 nm. A standard curve was obtained by plotting the concentration of ferrous sulfate (mmol/L) on the x-axis and the absorbance on the y-axis. The regression equation was as follows: y = 0.0024×-0.0076 (R2 = 0.9994). The reducing power was expressed as mmol/L of FeSO4.

Untargeted metabolomics

Sample preparation

Solid samples (50 mg) were accurately weighed, and metabolites were extracted using 400 μL of a methanol: water (4:1, v/v) solution containing 0.02 mg/mL L-2-chlorophenylalanin as an internal standard. The mixture was allowed to stand at −10 °C, and then homogenized at 50 Hz for 6 min using a high-throughput tissue homogenizer (Wonbio-96c, Wanbo Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Subsequently, the samples were subjected to ultrasonic treatment at 40 kHz for 30 min at 5 °C. Protein precipitation was induced by placing the samples at −20 °C for 30 min. After centrifugation at 13000 g at 4 °C for 15 min, the supernatant was carefully transferred to sample vials for LC-MS/MS analysis (Xie et al., 2024).

UHPLC-MS/MS analysis

Chromatographic separation was performed using a Thermo Scientific UHPLC-Q Exactive system, following the chromatographic conditions described by (Wang et al., 2019):

Chromatographic conditions: Analyses were performed on an HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d.,1.8 μm; Waters) maintained at 40 °C, with a 2 μL injection volume and a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The mobile phases consisted of solvent A (0.1 % formic acid in water:acetonitrile, 95:5, v/v) and solvent B (0.1 % formic acid in acetonitrile:isopropanol:water, 47.5:47.5:5, v/v). A solvent gradient elution protocol was applied as follows: from 0 to 0.1 min, 0–5 % B; 0.1–2 min, 5–25 % B; 2–9 min, 25–100 % B; 9–13 min, 100 % B; 13–13.1 min, 100–0 % B; 13.1–16 min, 0 % B for system equilibration. Throughout the analysis, the autosampler was maintained at 4 °C to ensure sample stability.

MS conditions: A Thermo UHPLC-Q Exactive Mass Spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source was operated in both positive and negative ion modes. The optimized parameters included a heater temperature of 400 °C, capillary temperature of 320 °C, sheath gas flow rate of 40 arbitrary units (arb), auxiliary gas flow rate of 10arb, and ion spray voltage floating (ISVF) set to -2800 V (negative mode) or 3500 V (positive mode). Normalized collision energies were applied in the rolling mode at 20–40-60 V for MS/MS. Full MS and MS/MS scans were acquired at resolutions of 70,000 and 17,500, respectively, within a mass range of 70–1050 m/z, using data-dependent acquisition (DDA).

Data preprocessing and annotation

Following LC-MS data acquisition, raw files were processed using Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) to generate a three-dimensional data matrix in CSV format. Internal standards and known false-positive peaks were removed, followed by de-replication and peak pooling. Metabolites were annotated by searching the HMDB, Metlin, and Majorbio databases. To minimize errors arising from sample preparation and instrument instability, the response intensities were normalized using the sum normalization method, resulting in a final normalized data matrix. Variables with a relative standard deviation (RSD) > 30 % in the QC samples were excluded, and the remaining data were log10-transformed for subsequent analyses (Ren et al., 2022).

Differential metabolites analysis

Multivariate statistical analysis, including principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), was conducted using the R package ropls (v1.6.2). Metabolites with variable importance in projection (VIP) values>1 and P < 0.05 were considered significantly different. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using Fisher's exact test with Python-based tools, and metabolites were classified based on their associated pathways and biological functions.

Statistical analysis

Triplicate experimental data were analyzed using SPSS (version 22, IBM, Armonk City, NY, USA) and Origin software (version 9.1, OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA). The results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance (P < 0.05) was determined using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post-hoc test at a 95 % confidence interval.

Results and discussion

Changes of phenolic content during fermentation

The total polyphenol and flavonoid contents of mung beans during fermentation are depicted in Fig. 1. Compared to the UFM group, both total polyphenol and total flavonoid contents in the fermented samples were significantly increased (P < 0.05). As fermentation proceeded, the total polyphenol content consistently increased, peaking at 18.31 mg/g on the 10th day, which was 2.51-fold higher than that of the UFM group. In contrast, the total flavonoid content initially increased and then declined, reaching a maximum of 11.34 mg/g on the 8th day, which was 2.49-fold higher than that in UFM. These findings are consistent with the results of Xu, Guo, Li and Guo (2019), who reported that the total phenolic content in oats, quinoa, and soybeans was significantly enhanced by Agaricus bisporus fermentation to 2.09-, 1.97-, and 1.24-fold of their respective unfermented controls. One possible explanation for the observed increase in total phenolic content is that microorganisms produce diverse hydrolases during fermentation, which release phenolic compounds that combine with starch, proteins, and cellulose. Zhang (2020) similarly observed that the polyphenol content of barley bran reached 12.43 mg/g after 4 days of Bacillus subtilis fermentation, with catechins converting from a bound to a free state. Xie et al. (2021) further demonstrated that SSF of defatted rice bran by Trichoderma viridis significantly increased polyphenol levels, and disruption of hemicellulose and xylanase secretion were critical factors facilitating the release of polyphenols. Additionally, microbial activity can regulate specific metabolic and biosynthetic pathways in cereals during fermentation, leading to the synthesis and enrichment of polyphenols. Jiang et al. (2024) found that the co-fermentation of quinoa and black barley with Lactobacillus kosen significantly increased the total polyphenol and flavonoid concentrations (P < 0.05). Changes in antioxidant capacity of mung bean during fermentation

The changes in the antioxidant capacity of mung bean alcoholic extract during fermentation are depicted in Fig. 2A, B, and C. Compared to the UFM group, the DPPH· and ABTS+· scavenging capacities and iron ion-reducing power in the fermentation group were significantly increased (P < 0.05). As fermentation progressed, the DPPH· scavenging capacity displayed a continuous upward trend, peaking at 6.96 μmol VC/g on the 8th day. Similarly, the ABTS+·scavenging capacity increased over time, reaching its highest value of 5.85 μmol VC/g on the 8th day. The iron ion-reducing power also increased, attaining its highest value of 57.75 μmol FeSO4/g on the 10th day, corresponding to 2.16-, 2.18-, and 2.11-fold increases compared to UFM samples, respectively. These findings align with those of Chen et al. (2023), who reported a time-dependent augmentation of antioxidant activity in Rhizopus oryzae-fermented soybeans. By day 5 of fermentation, the DPPH·, ABTS+·, and OH−· scavenging capacities increased by 2.25-, 2.28-, and 3.83-fold, respectively, relative to the non-fermented controls, a pattern consistent with the current study. The observed increase in the antioxidant capacity of mung beans may be attributed to the accumulation of phenolic compounds during fermentation. To elucidate this relationship, correlation analyses were conducted between the total phenolic/flavonoid content and antioxidant indices (Fig. 2D). These analyses revealed a significant positive correlation (P < 0.05) between phenolic content and the antioxidant capacity. Specifically, the total phenolic content was most strongly correlated with the ion-reducing power (R2 = 0.90538), followed by the ABTS+·-scavenging capacity (R2 = 0.82103) and DPPH·-scavenging capacity (R2 = 0.75738). The total flavonoid content demonstrated the highest correlation with ABTS+· scavenging capacity (R2 = 0.9643), followed by DPPH· scavenging capacity (R2 = 0.95061) and iron ion-reducing power (R2 = 0.86731). The slight discrepancies in the correlation coefficients may be due to the presence of non-phenolic substances (e.g., proteins and vitamins) with antioxidant properties in the phenol extracts. Analysis of metabolites PCA and PLS-DA

PCA was conducted to reduce the dimensionality of the mung bean samples obtained at different fermentation times. Each experimental group (n = 3 biological replicates) and QC group (n = 3) were included in the analysis. As illustrated in Fig. 3A, the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) accounted for 60.30 % and 17.8 % of the total variance, respectively, with a cumulative contribution rate of 78.1 %. This demonstrated that PC1 and PC2 contained the majority of the metabolic variance across samples. The model exhibited strong predictive accuracy, as evidenced by an R2X value of 0.601, which exceeded the threshold of 0.5 for a robust model fit. Notably, the QC samples clustered tightly near the coordinate origin, indicating stable instrumental performance and experimental consistency during the data acquisition. The minimal distance between samples within each group demonstrated high repeatability, whereas the substantial separation between groups highlighted significant intergroup differences. A PLS-DA model was constructed to further elucidate the metabolic differences in mung beans across various fermentation periods. As shown in Fig. 3B, the samples were distinctly separated according to fermentation time, which is consistent with the PCA results. In the PLS-DA model, R2Y (cum) and Q2 represent the cumulative interpretation rate and predictive capacity, respectively. As shown in Fig. 3C, the intercepts of R2 and Q2 were 0.392 and − 0.6319, respectively, suggesting the absence of overfitting and confirming that the model has a satisfactory predictive ability (Wang, Li, Pan and Xu, 2018). Total metabolite annotation information

The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) compound database categorizes small molecules, biopolymers, and biochemical entities based on their functional roles in the biological systems. These compounds are classified based on their biological functions. As shown in Fig. 4A, the biological process classification of mung bean samples at different fermentation times revealed 162 compounds, including 52 lipids, 22 nucleic acids, 14 peptides, 20 organic acids, and 14 carbohydrates. The classification of plant secondary metabolites, presented in Fig. 4B, indicated 224 compounds, including 37 alkaloids, 71 flavonoids, 26 phenylpropanoids, and 53 terpenoids.Fig. 4KEGG classification(4 A), KEGG functional pathway(4 BCE), and HMDB classification (4D)of mung bean metabolites at different fermentation stages.Fig. 4

KEGG classification(4 A), KEGG functional pathway(4 BCE), and HMDB classification (4D)of mung bean metabolites at different fermentation stages.

The KEGG PATHWAY database describes molecular interactions, physiological and biochemical reactions, and relationships among gene products. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed to elucidate the metabolic pathways of the identified compounds and assess their involvement in biological processes. As shown in Fig. 4C, 96 functional metabolic pathways were identified, primarily encompassing amino acid metabolism, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, lipid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, and cofactor and vitamin metabolism.

The Human Metabolic Database (HMDB) is the largest and most comprehensive database dedicated to biology-specific metabolites. As shown in Fig. 4D, 2455 metabolites were annotated in the HMDB. These metabolites predominantly included organic acids and derivatives (28.51 %), lipids and lipid-like molecules (23.01 %), organoheterocyclic compounds (13.24 %), organic oxygen compounds (10.63 %), phenylpropanoids and polyketides (8.31 %), and benzenoids (7.94 %).

To further elucidate the changes in phenolic compounds during fermentation, 61 significantly altered metabolites were selected for a cluster analysis. The resulting heat map (Fig. 5) visually illustrates the upregulation or downregulation of these metabolites in each group using color gradients. As shown in Fig. 5, 22 metabolites, including flavonoids, phenolic acids, flavones, and coumarins (e.g., coniferin, apigenin, pelargonidin 3-rutinoside, dihydromyricetin, bellidifolin, coumarin, 4-hydroxycinnamic acid, astringin, wogonin, kievitone, arctiin, luteolin 7-glucuronide, taxifolin, norathyriol, scutellarein, anethole, and eriodictyol) exhibited significantly elevated expression levels that continued to rise with fermentation time, peaking between days 8–10. It is indicated that within 10 days of fermentation, these phenolic metabolites can gradually accumulate with the fermentation time, and their expression levels are positively correlated with the total phenol content and antioxidant capacity. Previous studies have reported that coniferin possesses antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects (Bhavyashree et al., 2017), whereas apigenin has antitumor, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular protection, antiviral, and antibacterial properties (Dhiman et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025). Pelargonidin 3-Rutinoside also confers antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, cancer-preventive, and anti-aging benefits (Lin, 2023), and its orange hue contributes to the yellow coloration observed in mung bean polyphenol alcohol extracts. Dihydromyricetin exhibits antioxidant, antithrombotic, antitumor, and anti-inflammatory properties, mitigates the effects of alcohol intoxication, prevents alcoholic and fatty liver diseases, and inhibits the progression of liver damage (Du et al., 2022). In contrast, 20 phenolic metabolites, including cichoriin, apiin, schaftoside, cajanol, glabranin, daidzein, 5-hydroxyferulic acid, carthamone, vitamin P, secoisolariciresinol, leucopelargonidin, fustin, gallic acid, naringenin, and tricin, exhibited their highest upregulation on day 2 of fermentation, followed by a gradual decline over time; by days 8–10, their expression was significantly down-regulated and lower than that in the UFM group. These observations indicate that a shorter fermentation period (approximately 2 days) may be more conducive to enriching these particular metabolites, whereas prolonged fermentation time likely drives their conversion into other phenolic substances via diverse metabolic pathways or leads to microbial consumption. For instance, schaftoside has been linked to hepatoprotective, anti-inflammatory, and heat-clearing effects and removal of dampness (Wang, Gao, et al., 2018). Cajanol exhibits antiplasmodial and antifungal activity. Glabranin has strong skin-whitening and melanin-inhibiting functions (Zhao et al., 2013). Daidzein effectively scavenges free radicals, promotes collagen synthesis, and delays aging (Li et al., 2020).

Meanwhile, another set of 19 metabolites, including dalbergioidin, kaempferol, kievitone hydrate, emodin, pratensein, epigallocatechin, bracteatin, isorhamnetin, luteolin 7-glucoside, trans-piceid, epicatechin, quercetin, dattelic acid, scolymoside, and luteoforol, showed significant increases on day 2 of fermentation but subsequently declined with extended fermentation time. Nevertheless, compared to the UFM group, the expression of these metabolites was significantly upregulated in all fermentation groups. Kaempferol exerts anti-cancer, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antibacterial, and antiviral effects (Duan et al., 2025), whereas quercetin exerts potent antioxidant activity, lowers blood pressure and lipid levels, and demonstrates expectorant and antitussive properties (Dong et al., 2025). In addition to its antioxidant role, luteolin 7-glucoside confers cytoprotection against oxidative stress–induced cardiovascular diseases (Ge et al., 2022). In summary, 96.8 % of the phenolic metabolites in mung beans were up-regulated following SSF by Aspergillus Niger, highlighting the beneficial role of this approach in enhancing phenolic compound enrichment. However, the optimal fermentation duration for maximizing the expression of specific phenolic metabolites varied, with the most significant upregulation observed on days 2 or 8 of fermentation. After 8 days of fermentation, the phenolic compounds showed negligible change. This indicates that the fermentation time should be determined according to the target phenolic compounds of interest, and fermentation should not exceed 10 days.

Correlation between the expression of phenolic metabolites and antioxidant capacity

Phenolic metabolites are key contributors to the antioxidant capacity of mung beans. As demonstrated in Section 3.2, the overall phenolic content was positively correlated with the antioxidant capacity. To further elucidate the relationship between individual phenolic compounds and antioxidant capacity during fermentation, we conducted a correlation analysis of the expression levels of differential phenolic metabolites in 61 samples with respect to DPPH·, ABTS+·, and ferric reducing power (Fig. 6). A total of 28 phenolic metabolites, such as bracteatin, lariciresinol, eriodictyol, norathyriol, scutellarein, 4-hydroxycinnamic acid, astringin, taxifolin, coumarin, wogonin, pelargonidin 3-rutinoside, kievitone, dihydromyricetin, bellidifolin, and quercetin 3-methyl, exhibited significant positive correlations (P < 0.001) with all three antioxidant indices. These findings suggest that phenolic metabolites play a pivotal role in enhancing the total phenolic content and antioxidant capacity of mung beans. In addition, 13 other phenolic metabolites, including pratensein, epigallocatechin, kievitone hydrate, coniferin, emodin, kaempferol, arctiin, isorhamnetin, and epicatechin, also showed positive correlations with DPPH·, ABTS+·, and iron ion reducing power. Nonetheless, the degree of significance varies among species, likely due to differences in the number of hydrogen atoms and electrons that each compound can donate (Wang et al., 2020). Interestingly, 19 phenolic metabolites, including schaftoside, prenyl glucoside, apiin, glabranin, tricin, cichoriin, cajanol, gallic acid, melilotoside, carthamone, vitamin P, fustin, pinobanksin, and naringenin, were negatively correlated with the three antioxidant indices. In other words, the levels of these phenolic metabolites decreased as fermentation progressed, whereas the total antioxidant capacity increased. The degree of phenolic metabolite enrichment was significantly correlated with these metabolic pathways. The phenolic metabolic pathways in mung beans at different fermentation times are illustrated in Fig. 8. These results indicate that the SSF by Aspergillus Niger activates the biosynthetic pathway of phenylalanine, tyrosine, and sikimic acid, of which phenylalanine and tyrosine are key pathways for the enrichment of polyphenols. The phenylalanine pathway primarily involves the conversion of the substrate phenylalanine to trans-cinnamic acid, which is further converted to coumaric acid, ultimately leading to the synthesis of phenolic compounds. This process is mainly mediated by phenylalanine ammonolyase and 4-coumaric acid CoA ligase. In contrast, the tyrosine pathway directly converts the substrate tyrosine to coumaric acid, mediated by tyrosine aminolyase. Both pathways lead to coumaric acid formation but differ in their initial substrates. The shikimic acid pathway directly converts shikimic acid to gallic acid via shikimate hydrogenase. Among the phenylalanine-related products, L-phenylalanine was significantly upregulated in all treatment groups, whereas tyrosine-related differential metabolites were not observed. This observation suggests that SSF is conducive to polyphenol synthesis through the phenylalanine pathway. In this pathway, trans-cinnamate 4-monooxygenase converts trans-cinnamic acid to 4-hydroxycinnamic acid (coumaric acid), which is subsequently transformed into caffeic acid. Caffeic acid is converted to ferulic acid by ferulate 5-hydroxylase. Fermentation promoted the formation of both caffeic and ferulic acids, with the highest expression levels observed on day 2 of fermentation. Coumaric acid can also be converted into various lignans, such as coniferin, scopoletin, and secoisolariciresinol. The highest lignan expression was observed after 8–10 days of fermentation. Through the action of shikimate O-hydroxycinnamoyltransferase, coumaric acid is converted into 5-O-coffee acylshikimic acid, which can then be transformed into liquiritigrnin and naringenin via 6′-deoxychalcone synthase and chalcone isomerase, thus promoting the isoflavone biosynthesis pathway. The synthetic transformations of aidzein, pseudobaptigenin, genistein, and pratensein were notably enhanced between days 2 and 6 during fermentation. Naringenin can be converted to apigenin via flavone synthase I and flavone synthase II, thereby activating the flavone and flavonol biosynthesis pathways. This process involves the synthesis and transformation of luteolin, chrysoeriol, vitexin, apiin, and isovitexin. Notably, apigenin levels were higher after 8–10 days of fermentation, whereas other related metabolites peaked after 2–6 days. This discrepancy may be attributable to the higher enzyme activity facilitating apigenin transformation during the earlier stages of fermentation. However, as microbial growth and metabolism continue into the later stages of fermentation, substrate nutrients become limited, and the enzymes responsible for their breakdown are either consumed or reduced, leading to the accumulation of apigenin. Within the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway, naringenin is converted to eriodictyol by flavonoid 3′,5′-hydroxylase and flavonoid 3′-monooxygenase. Subsequently, naringenin and eriodictyol are transformed into dihydrokaempferol and taxifolin under the mediation of flavanone 3-dioxygenase. These intermediates are then converted into kaempferol and quercetin by flavonol synthase. Kaempferol also undergoes hydroxylation to quercetin through the activities of flavonoid 3′,5′-hydroxylase and flavonoid 3′-monooxygenase mediated by vitamin P, isoquercitrin, hydroxylase and flavonoid 3′ -hydroxylase, facilitating further synthetic transformations into 3,7-dimethylquercetin, epicatechin, dihydromyricetin, and epigallocatechin. Anthocyanin biosynthesis involves only one detected differential metabolite, pelargonidin 3-rutinoside, which is synthesized from dihydrokaempferol by anthocyanidin synthase and anthocyanidin 3-O-glucosyltransferase. Its expression was significantly up-regulated after 8–9 days of fermentation. Previous studies have shown that phenolic compounds involved in these metabolic pathways possess promising pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antitumor, antimicrobial, antioxidative, cardioprotective, and hepatoprotective effects (Qi et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2019). Conclusion

This study demonstrates that solid-state fermentation (SSF) of mung beans mediated by Aspergillus niger significantly enhances phenolic and flavonoid biosynthesis, with a concomitant elevation in antioxidant activity. Quantitative analyses revealed a progressive accumulation of total phenolic content (18.31 mg/g, peak at day 10) and total flavonoids (11.34 mg/g, peak at day 8) during fermentation (P < 0.05), underscoring the temporal dynamics of metabolite synthesis. Antioxidant capacity, evaluated via DPPH· scavenging (6.96 μmol VC/g), ABTS+· scavenging (5.85 μmol VC/g), and ferric reducing power (57.75 μmol FeSO₄/g), exhibited significant positive correlations with phenolic content (P < 0.05), achieving maxima at days 8–10. These findings collectively demonstrate the efficacy of Aspergillus Niger in the biotransformation of mung beans as a potent source of bioactive compounds. Non-targeted metabolomic profiling identified 96 phenolic metabolites across fermentation stages, with 58 of the 61 differentially expressed metabolites showing upregulation. The expression levels of the top 22 significantly upregulated differential metabolites of phenols increased significantly over time, indicating that these phenolic substances play an important role in increasing the total phenol content. Its expression level is significantly positively correlated with antioxidant capacity. Furthermore, fermentation activated the biosynthesis of phenylalanine, tyrosine, and shikimic acid, thus promoting the accumulation of phenolic compounds within flavonoids, flavones, flavonols, isoflavones, phenylc, and various other plant secondary metabolites. This elucidates the metabolic changes in phenolic compounds during fermentation. Therefore, SSF by Aspergillus Niger serves as an effective strategy to improve the phenolic content and enhance the antioxidant potential of mung beans. This study provides valuable insights into the metabolic mechanisms underlying polyphenol enrichment in fermented mung beans and establishes a theoretical framework for future research. However, the exact molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood owing to the lack of gene expression analysis. Future investigations integrating transcriptomic approaches are warranted to validate and refine these findings by analyzing changes in relevant gene expression.
