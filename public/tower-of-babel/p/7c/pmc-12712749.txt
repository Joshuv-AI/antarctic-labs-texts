Loss of Stat3 in Prx1+ Progenitors Impairs Molar Root Development
Authors: Xin Feng, Wangyu Luo, Yichen Yao, Lichieh Lin, Laiting Chan, Jiarui Lu, Zijing Huang, Jingyi Feng, Le Zhao, Xiaolei Zhang, Liu Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSignal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (Stat3) acts as a central transcriptional modulator coordinating cellular proliferation, survival, apoptosis, vascularization, immune regulation, and migratory processes. Human Stat3 deficiency triggers Hyper‐IgE syndrome, associated with immune dysregulation, osseous defects, and dental malformations. This study employs genetically engineered murine models to dissect Stat3's mechanistic role within mesenchymal progenitor cells during molar root formation and periodontal tissue maturation. Conditional Stat3 knockout mice (Prx1‐Cre; Stat3f/f) are generated. Comparative assessments of mandibular first molar root development between Stat3 CKO and wild‐type cohorts are performed through histomorphometric evaluation, micro‐computed tomography, cellular proliferation assays (Ki67/BrdU), and transcriptome sequencing. Stat3 ablation causes marked morphological defects in first molars, featuring reduced root length and elevated crown‐root proportion. The periodontal ligament (PDL) at the distal root exhibits diminished width in mutants. Alveolar bone displays suppressed expression of osteogenic markers (Runx2, Col1a1, Ocn), accompanied by decreased Ki67+ and BrdU+ cell populations in the PDL. Stat3 critically regulates mandibular first molar and alveolar bone morphogenesis. Conditional ablation of Stat3 disrupts the osteogenic capacity of Prx1+ mesenchymal progenitors, as evidenced across in vivo and in vitro models.Keywords: Job's syndrome, periodontal ligament (PDL), root development, signal transducer and activator of transcription 3

Introduction

Tooth development is an intricate physiological process that unfolds through distinct stages. It commences with the bud phase, progresses to the cap phase, and then reaches the bell phase.[ 1 ] Subsequently, it is succeeded by root development and culminates in tooth eruption. When crown formation is finished, root development initiates and is promoted by a complicated process affected by Hertwig's epithelial root sheath (HERS) and neighboring tissues.[ 2 ] The cellular proliferation, apoptosis, and osteoclast activity among the organisms are consistent with the morphological changes in the process of root development.[ 3 ] However, the molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying post‐embryonic root development remain largely unexplored and poorly characterized in current scientific literature. Despite recent advances in the special signaling networks that are crucial in regulating root development, the regulatory mechanisms of root formation remain to be explored.[ 4 , 5 ]

Stat3 is part of a family known as the Stat genes, which are widely involved in cell proliferation and differentiation.[ 6 ] Job's syndrome, which is also called Hyper‐IgE syndrome or AD‐HIES, exhibits immune disorders and bone abnormalities clinically, with >70% caused by Stat3 gene mutations.[ 7 ]

Recent research has focused on Stat3 signaling in modulating skeletal development.[ 8 , 9 ] It was shown that mice with Stat3 depletion in Prx1+progenitor cells induced Hyper‐IgE‐syndrome‐like skeletal dysplasia, such as craniosynostosis and spontaneous bone fracture.[ 8 ] In the in vivo environment, the absence of Stat3 in osteoblasts caused a reduction in bone formation.[ 10 ] In contrast, the absence of Stat3 in osteoclasts brought about a remarkable increase in bone mass.[ 11 ] These research findings illustrate the significant role of Stat3 in regulating skeletal development and bone homeostasis. Apart from severe primary immunodeficiencies and skeletal abnormalities, which have been extensively studied, retained primary tooth and delayed eruption of permanent tooth were also found in Hyper‐IgE‐syndrome‐like patients.[ 12 , 13 ] Furthermore, conditional knock‐out of Stat3 in epithelium results in delayed ameloblast differentiation in incisors.[ 14 ] Nevertheless, we know little about the specific role of Stat3 during the formation of molar and periodontal tissues.

As a homeobox gene, Prx1 is highly expressed in embryonic limb bud and craniofacial development, and its deletion causes severe developmental malformations in skeleton.[ 15 , 16 ] The Prx1‐Cre model has been widely used to characterize specific gene functions in craniofacial stem cells.[ 17 , 18 ] In this study, we conditionally ablated Stat3 in Prx1+ progenitors in mice by using the Prx1‐Cre transgenic line crossed with a Stat3‐floxed mouse strain. Loss of Stat3 in Prx1+ progenitors caused developmental defects of roots and periodontium, due to decreased alveolar bone formation and impaired proliferation capacity of dental mesenchyme tissue and PDL.

Through our studies, we have pinpointed Stat3 as an indispensable regulator in the development of molar roots and the periodontium. These findings not only shed light on this crucial developmental process but also offer novel perspectives on the underlying mechanisms of dental defects in patients with Hyper‐IgE syndrome.

Results

Deletion of Stat3 in Prx1+ Progenitors Resulted in Shortened Molar Roots

In mice, the development of molar roots initiates at around P3.5.[ 23 ] The mouse genotypes used are shown in Figure 1a. The analysis of the Western blot was additionally employed to validate the successful knockout of Stat3. (Figure 1b). To figure out the distribution of Prx1+ cells in molars and root support tissues, a transgenic mouse strain, Prx1‐Cre; tdTomato+, was employed to visualize Prx1+ cells via tdTomato (red). Upon analyzing Stat3 CKO at P7, it was discovered that cells expressing Prx1, along with their descendants, all marked by tdTomato expression, were located within the mesenchyme of the first molar and the alveolar bone encircling the molars. Minimal expression of Stat3 was detectable within the tissues of Stat3 CKO mice at P7 (Figure 1c). During the root development of mouse first molars, Stat3 was discovered in both the epithelium and mesenchyme, which is consistent with our observation (Figure 1c). Through histological analysis, we explored the impacts of the conditional deletion of Stat3 on the development of tooth roots. µCT analysis suggested that the mutants had reduced molar root length (Figure 1d–f). H&E staining revealed that the first molars of Stat3 CKO mice had a higher ratio of crown/root at different time points during tooth development (Figure 1g,h). These indicated that the inactivation of Stat3 in Prx1+ progenitors induced Hyper‐IgE‐syndrome‐like dental defects.

Loss of Stat3 in Prx1+ Progenitors Resulted in Disrupted Periodontal Tissue Development

Normal root development requires normal formation of PDL. Prx1+ cells are also crucial in the formation of PDL, so we hypothesized that PDL development could be influenced in Stat3 CKO mice. As we surmised, the PDL surrounding first molars was significantly narrowed according to Masson's trichrome staining (Figure 2a,b). Immunostaining using Periostin, a well–recognized marker for PDL fibers, revealed that at P10 and P14, the mutants exhibited a marked decrease in Periostin expression within the PDL (as depicted in Figure 2c,d). These results strongly indicate that Stat3 plays an indispensable role in the differentiation of Prx1–expressing dental mesenchymal progenitors into Periostin–expressing PDL cells.

Prx1‐Cre; Stat3f/f Mice Exhibited Decreased Alveolar Bone Volume

To further investigate the effect of Stat3 ablation on alveolar bone volume, we subsequently utilized µCT analysis. The µCT scans performed at P21 revealed a decrease in alveolar bone volume in the mutant mice. In comparison to the control cohort, the mutant mice exhibited decreased ratios of BV/TV, reduced bone mineral density, and thinner Tb.Th, accompanied by an elevation in Tb.Sp. Notably, no statistically significant difference was observed in Tb.N between the groups (Figure 2e,j). Osteoclasts are central to the regulation of both root morphogenesis and the development of alveolar bone. To assess the presence of osteoclasts in the bone surrounding the molars, tartrate–resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining was carried out at P14 to evaluate osteoclast activity (Figure 2k,j). The results of the TRAP staining demonstrated an increase in the number of osteoclasts within the alveolar bone region at the tooth base in the mutants when compared with the controls.

Diminished Stat3 Decreased Osteogenic Genes Transcription

To explore the specific effects of reduced Stat3 on gene expression in Prx1+ progenitors, we conducted RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) using total RNA extracted from the alveolar bone surrounding the molar roots of P3.5 Stat3 CKO and control (CON) mice. In comparison to the CON group, in the Stat3 CKO group, 231 genes were downregulated by >1.5‐fold, and 370 genes were upregulated by >1.5‐fold (Figure 3a). Gene ontology analysis results indicated that these differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in associations related to ossification, osteoblast differentiation, and skeletal system development. This finding was in line with the alveolar bone deformities observed in the Stat3 CKO mice (Figure 3c). A heatmap further demonstrated that the expression of osteogenesis‐related genes, such as Runx2, Ocn, and Col1a1, was downregulated (Figure 3b). Osteoblast differentiation is accomplished through the activation of multiple transcriptional networks, in which Runx2, Ocn, and Col1a1 play crucial roles (Figure 3d). We verified the downregulation of Runx2, Ocn, and Col1a1 in the alveolar bone of Stat3 CKO and CON at P14 using immunofluorescence cytometry (IFC) and Western blot analyses (Figure 3e–h). These results were consistent with the quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) outcomes obtained from Stat3 – deleted OMSCs after in vitro osteogenic differentiation (Figure 3l). As depicted in Figure 3i–k, following a 14‐day culture in osteogenic induction medium, the intensities of Von Kossa staining and alizarin red staining were notably reduced in Stat3 CKO OMSCs. Collectively, these results imply that during alveolar bone development, the expression of Runx2, Ocn, and Col1a1 was significantly lower in Stat3 CKO mice compared to the control group.

Ablation of Stat3 in Prx1+ Progenitors Disrupts Mesenchymal and PDL Cell Proliferation

When we compared the Ki67⁺ and BrdU⁺ (actively cycling) cells in control (CON) and Stat3 conditional knockout (CKO) mice at P10, a notable reduction in cell proliferation was observed within the dental mesenchyme of Stat3 CKO molars (Figure 4a,b).In accordance with the diminished width of the PDL in Stat3 CKO mice (Figure 2a,b), the quantities of Ki67‐positive and BrdU‐labeled cells were significantly lower in the PDL of Stat3 CKO mice at both P10 (Figure 4c,d) and P21 (Figure 4e,f). These results strongly indicate that Stat3 might be an essential factor for the normal proliferation of dental mesenchymal cells and PDL cells during the process of root development.

Discussion

Multiple signaling pathways—including Bmp, Tgfβ, Wnt, Fgf, and Shh—play indispensable roles in regulating root morphogenesis.[ 2 , 3 ] Among key molecular players, Stat3 emerges as a central orchestrator of dental cell differentiation, governing the maturation of ameloblasts, odontoblasts, dental pulp stem cells, and cementoblasts.[ 24 ] While epithelial Stat3 is known to regulate amelogenesis during mouse incisor renewal,[ 14 ] recent work implicates the miR‐21/Stat3 axis in modulating the odontogenic potential of dental pulp‐derived mesenchymal stem cells.[ 25 ] Notably, adiponectin‐induced activation of MAPK8, P38, and ERK1/2 culminates in Stat1/Stat3 phosphorylation, a cascade essential for cementoblast migration, proliferation, and differentiation.[ 26 ]

Despite its established role in skeletal development, Stat3's contribution to root formation remains poorly characterized. To address this gap, we generated a CKO model targeting Stat3 in Prx1+ mesenchymal progenitors, which populate craniofacial mesenchyme and give rise to osteoblasts, odontoblasts, and PDL cells.[ 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 ] These progenitors are enriched in dental pulp and molar base regions, where Stat3 expression localizes during root development.[ 14 ]

Prx1‐Cre; Stat3f/f mice exhibited striking root truncation in first molars, mirroring dental anomalies observed in Hyper‐IgE syndrome patients with Stat3 mutations. Root elongation requires coordinated epithelial‐mesenchymal interactions,[ 2 ] yet HERS morphology remained intact in mutants. Instead, apical mesenchymal proliferation was markedly reduced—a critical determinant of root patterning.[ 31 ] This suggests Stat3 ablation disrupts spatial regulation of mesenchymal expansion, contributing to the shortened root phenotype.

Mechanistically, Stat3 intersects with Wnt signaling pathways. The Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway plays a critical role in bone formation, with β‐catenin serving as the core signaling transducer of this pathway.[ 32 ] Upon activation of the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway, β‐catenin is stabilized, leading to its accumulation in the cytoplasm and subsequent translocation into the nucleus to regulate gene expression.[ 33 ] Studies have indicated that the bone defects observed in HIES may result from reduced Wnt/β‐catenin signaling, caused by diminished Stat3 activity during skeletal development.[ 34 ] Stat3 inactivation may also impair the nuclear translocation of β‐catenin, thereby exacerbating alveolar bone loss during orthodontic tooth movement in rats with periodontitis.[ 35 ] Besides, prior work revealed that Stat3 overexpression rescues osteogenic defects in Ror2‐deficient bone marrow stromal cells, positioning Stat3 downstream of Ror2, a receptor tyrosine kinase mediating both canonical and non‐canonical Wnt signaling.[ 36 , 37 , 38 ] Intriguingly, Ror2 deletion in dental mesenchyme phenocopies Stat3 CKO root defects.[ 22 ] Furthermore, Wnt/β‐catenin activation partially ameliorates skeletal abnormalities in Prx1‐Cre; Stat3f/f mice, implicating Stat3 as a multifunctional modulator of Wnt‐dependent bone and tooth development.[ 34 ]

Consistent with its role in osteogenesis, Stat3 ablation reduced alveolar bone mass and downregulated osteogenic markers (Ocn, Col1a1, Runx2), paralleling limb development deficits.[ 8 , 9 ] Prx1+ OMSCs drive alveolar bone formation, and their osteogenic impairment in mutants coincided with elevated osteoclast activity—a dual mechanism likely underlying bone loss.[ 38 , 39 ]

Stat3 further regulates periodontal integrity. Prx1+ progenitors in the dental follicle differentiate into PDL cells,[ 27 , 28 ] and JAK2/STAT3 signaling governs PDL remodeling during orthodontic movement.[ 40 ] Mutant mice displayed thinner PDLs with reduced Periostin, a matrix protein critical for postnatal dental structure maturation, indicating Stat3 directs PDL cell differentiation. Further investigation is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanism.[ 41 ]

Current models highlight pathway‐specific root defects: Shh inhibition reduces apical mesenchymal proliferation, while Tgfbr2 loss in neural crest mesenchyme disrupts root patterning.[ 42 , 43 ] However, due to the expression of Stat3 and the distribution of Prx1‐expressing cells in the periodontium tissues and dental mesenchyme, Prx1‐Cre; Stat3f/f mice not only exhibit impaired tooth root development but also abnormal periodontal tissue development.

Conclusion

The development of the mandibular first molars and alveolar bone in mice is significantly influenced by Stat3. In both in vivo and in vitro settings, the absence of Stat3 leads to the impairment of osteogenesis in Prx1+ progenitor cells.

Experimental Section

Mouse Breeding

All animal procedures in this study were approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of Sun Yat‐sen University (approval number: SYSU‐IACUC‐2021‐000101 for the original model and SYSU‐IACUC‐2022‐001778 for this project) and conducted in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments). C57BL/6 mice were used in this study. Prx1‐Cre (stock number: 0 05584) and Stat3f/f (stock number: 01 6923) murine models were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. To generate Stat3 conditional knockout (CKO) mice, male Prx1‐Cre mice were intercrossed with female Stat3f/f mice to produce Prx1‐Cre; Stat3f/+offspring. Male Prx1‐Cre; Stat3f/+ progeny were then mated with female Stat3f/f mice to obtain Prx1‐Cre; Stat3f/f homozygous knockout mutants. Cre‐negative littermates served as controls. Genotyping was performed via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using tail biopsy‐derived DNA, with primer sequences provided in Table 1 . Given statistical considerations and breeding complexities, experiments included three biological replicates per group across two experimental cohorts.

Histology and Immunostaining

Following overnight fixation in 10% neutral buffered formalin and a two‐week decalcification process using 15% tetrasodium EDTA, the specimens were embedded within optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound. Subsequently, 12‐micrometer‐thick frozen sections were obtained using an HM360 microtome (Microm, Germany) and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E; Servicebio, Wuhan, China; catalog number G1005). Additionally, tartrate‐resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP; Fujifilm, Japan; catalog number 294–67001) and Masson's trichrome staining (Solarbio, Beijing, China; catalog number G1343) were performed in accordance with the manufacturers' instructions. For the purpose of immunohistochemical staining, the slides were incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies targeting Stat3 (1:400 dilution; CST, USA; catalog number ab23981), Col1a1 (1:400 dilution; Abcam, USA; catalog number ab21286), Periostin (1:200 dilution; Abcam, USA; catalog number ab14041), Ki67 (1:300 dilution; CST, USA; catalog number 9129T), and Ocn (1:200 dilution; Abcam, USA; catalog number ab3697). Then, the specimens underwent staining with Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated secondary antibodies (1:800 dilution; Invitrogen, USA; catalog number A‐11094). Imaging of immunostained sections was carried out using an Olympus confocal microscope FV3000 (Olympus, Japan). Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software (version 6.4.0; National Institutes of Health), with measurements including crown‐to‐root ratio and periostin/DAPI intensity. For the analysis, a minimum of three discrete sections per sample and at least three distinct samples from each experimental group were employed.

Micro‐CT (µCT) Analysis

Mandibles were isolated from postnatal day 10 (P10), P14, and P21 mice, then underwent overnight fixation in 10% neutral buffered formalin before washing with PBS, and stored in 70% ethanol. The mandible was scanned using the µCT35 system (SCANCO Medical, Switzerland), and the first molars were reconstructed. Alveolar bone parameters at the root furcation of the first molars were analyzed at P21. Starting from the cancellous bone at the root bifurcation of the first molar, a 100‐layer section (700 µm) was selected as the region of interest (ROI). The bone parameters assessed included total tissue volume (TV), bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp).

BrdU Administration

5′‐Bromo‐2′‐deoxyuridine (BrdU; B5002; Sigma, USA) was dissolved in 0.9% saline solution to achieve a concentration of 15 mg/mL and was administered at 30 mg k−1g i.p. at 08:00 and 20:00 for 3 days. The animals were sacrificed 12 h after the last injection of BrdU.[ 19 ] Frozen sections of mandible were prepared and photographed with an Olympus confocal microscope FV3000 (Olympus, Japan). ImageJ was used to determine cell numbers.

Cell Culture and Osteoinduction of Mouse Bone‐Marrow‐Derived Orofacial Mesenchymal Stem Cells (OMSCs)

Isolation of OMSCs from Stat3f/f 4‐week‐old male mice was performed according to a protocol.[ 20 ] In essence, subsequent to the excision of adherent soft tissues, incisors, and molars, the mandibular bones of the mice were subjected to enzymatic digestion utilizing 3 mg mL−1 of collagenase type I (Biofroxx, Germany; catalog number 1904MG100) and 4 mg mL−1 of Dispase II (Roche Diagnostic, Switzerland; catalog number 1 066 024) for a duration of 60 min at a temperature of 37 °C. The resultant nucleated cells were filtered through 70‐µm cell strainers (Biosharp, China; catalog number BS‐70‐CS) and plated at densities ranging from 1 to 1.5 × 106 cells per 100‐mm dish (Biofil, China; catalog number TCD010100‐1). These cells were maintained in culture medium comprising α‐MEM (Gibco, USA; catalog number C12571500BT), augmented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin (HyClone, USA; catalog number SV30010) and 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA; catalog number 10099141C). Subsequently, the cells were transduced with adenoviral‐Cre particles, acquired from HANBIO (Shanghai, China), or adenoviral GFP at a concentration of 2 × 107 PFU mL−1 per well. Polybrene(Sigma, USA; catalog number TR‐1003‐G) was added at a concentration of 6 µg mL−1 and was removed after 12 h of co‐incubation with the virus. Osteogenic differentiation was induced by supplementing the osteogenic differentiation medium (α‐MEM with 1% PS and 10% FBS) with 50 µg mL−1 of ascorbic acid (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA; catalog number 1 043 003), 10 mm β‐glycerophosphate (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA; catalog number G5422), and 10−7 m dexamethasone (Sigma, USA; catalog number D4092). The culture medium was replaced every two days. At day 14, Von Kossa staining and Alizarin red S staining (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA; catalog number TMS‐008) were conducted in accordance with the manufacturer's guidelines. A total of three independent samples, each infected with the adenoviruses, were utilized in this study.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real‐Time PCR (qRT‐PCR)

To procure total RNA from mandibles of P10 mice and in vitro cultivated OMSCs, the Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA; catalog number 15596‐026) was employed, adhering strictly to the manufacturer's instructions. The concentration of extracted RNA was assessed utilizing a NanoDrop ND‐1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).[ 8 ] cDNA synthesis was facilitated through the use of the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (Takara, Japan; catalog number RR036A). The primer sequences employed for qRT‐PCR are outlined in Table 2 . Preparation of the qPCR reaction mixture involved a total volume of 20 µL, formulated according to the amplification system specified by SybrGreen Supermix (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, USA; catalog number 1 725 124). The quantification of relative gene expression was standardized against the endogenous control glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh) using the 2−ΔΔCT approach.[ 21 ]

Protein Extraction and Western Blot

Proteins were isolated from OMSCs and alveolar bone through a lysis assay utilizing the reagent provided by KeyGEN (Jiangsu, China; catalog number KGB5303). The protein concentration was then determined using the Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) method with the kit supplied by FudeBio (Hangzhou, China; catalog number FD2001). An equivalent quantity of protein samples was fractionated by NuPAGE 4–12% Bis‐Tris SDS‐PAGE (Invitrogen, USA; catalog number NP0321BOX). The intensity of protein bands was quantified using ImageJ software. Primary antibodies used in this study included anti‐Runx2 (1:1000 dilution; abcam, USA; catalog number ab23981), anti‐Col1a1 (1:1000 dilution; abcam, USA; catalog number ab21286), and anti‐Ocn (1:500 dilution; Signalway Antibody, USA; catalog number 29 697). The immunoblotting signals were visualized through the application of Amersham ECL Prime Western Blotting Detection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; catalog number 32 106).

RNA‐Seq Analysis

The alveolar bone of the mandibular first molar was dissected from both Stat3f/f control mice and Prx1‐Cre; Stat3f/f mice at P3.5. Subsequently, RNA extraction was carried out utilizing the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (400 753; Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Following the completion of library preparation for transcriptome sequencing, the various libraries were pooled based on their effective concentrations and the intended data output, and were then sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq platform for three pairs of samples, as previously described.[ 22 ] Quality assurance of the raw RNA sequencing reads was conducted using FASTQC (Galaxy Version 0.69), and these reads were aligned to the GRCm38/mm10 mouse genome reference sequence employing HISAT2 (Galaxy Version 2.0.5.2). The aligned read counts for individual genes were tabulated using featureCounts (subread‐2.0.8), and differential expression analysis was executed via DESeq2 (Galaxy Version 1.46.0). To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs), a significance threshold was established with an adjusted p‐value (Padj) of <0.05 and a fold change >2. The results of the DEG analysis were visualized using R Studio (version 4.2.2).

Statistical Analysis

All measurements and counts were conducted separately by two researchers who were not clear about the groups. Means of the two results were used for the statistical analysis. The data obtained in this investigation were presented in the format of mean ± standard deviation and were subjected to analysis and processing utilizing GraphPad Prism version 10.0 software. Inter‐group comparisons were conducted using one‐way ANOVA, with the application of Bonferroni's correction for multiple comparisons. The data of two independent samples were analyzed by an unpaired Student's t‐test. The sample size for each experiment is indicated in the figure legends, but was typically n = 3 biologically independent samples. Statistical significance was established at p<0.05, denoted as * p<0.05, ** p<0.01, and *** p<0.001, respectively.
