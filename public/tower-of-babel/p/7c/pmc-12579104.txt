A ubiquitin–GhUBX–GhCDC48 module regulates secondary cell wall thickening in cotton fiber
Authors: Yihao Zang, Lishan Yu, Longen Ma, Xiaoran Li, Lisha Xuan, Chenyu Xu, Yayao Zhang, Zhanfeng Si, Jieqiong Deng, Tianzhen Zhang, Yan Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe secondary cell wall (SCW) is a critical supportive and protective structure in plant growth and development. In cotton fibers, it is established that SCW thickening directly determines fiber strength by enhancing mechanical support, but the molecular mechanisms governing this process remain poorly understood. Here, we elucidate a ubiquitin–GhUBX–GhCDC48 regulatory module in which GhUBX serves as a bridge between GhCDC48 and ubiquitin, promoting GhCDC48 degradation via the ubiquitin–26S proteasome pathway. Compared to the wild type receptor (W0), transgenic cotton lines in which GhUBX is silenced or GhCDC48 is overexpressed exhibited significantly increased SCW thickness and fiber strength. Conversely, GhCDC48 silencing resulted in stunted plant growth and impaired cell wall development, consistent with its role in regulating the cell division cycle. Furthermore, we revealed that GhCDC48 abundance positively correlates with ATPase activity in fiber cells and with the transcriptional activation of key SCW biosynthesis genes including GhCesA7, GhCesA8, GhSusA, GhFSN1, GhKNL1, and GhNAC21, thereby enhancing SCW deposition. Our findings reveal the ubiquitin–GhUBX–CDC48 regulatory module that influences cotton fiber strength and provide a genetic strategy for improving fiber quality through targeted modification of genes in the ubiquitin pathway.

Introduction

The secondary cell wall (SCW) is a specialized structure deposited inside the primary cell wall of certain plant cells after cell expansion ceases. Composed primarily of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, the SCW fulfills pivotal roles in plant physiology, structural integrity, and adaptation to environmental challenges. One of its primary functions is to provide mechanical strength and rigidity to specialized cells, enabling plants to maintain their shape and withstand physical stresses. Such cells include cotton fibers, which exhibit exceptional tensile strength. In mature fibers, cellulose constitutes over 95% of the SCW (Meinert and Delmer 1977; Huwyler et al. 1979; Tokumoto et al. 2002). This unique attribute renders cotton fiber an ideal experimental system for studying the physiological and biochemical changes that accompany cell elongation and cell wall thickening.

The development of single-celled cotton fibers proceeds through four overlapping but independent stages: initiation, elongation, SCW synthesis, and dehydration maturity (Meinert and Delmer 1977; Jareczek et al. 2023). These stages are characterized by synchronized physiological and molecular events that determine the two most important aspects of cotton fiber quality, fiber length and strength. While extensive research has been conducted on fiber length, studies focusing on fiber strength are relatively limited. It has been demonstrated that SCW synthesis is a critical determinant of fiber strength, specifically the processes of cellulose deposition and microfibril arrangement (Gou et al. 2007). In the course of SCW synthesis, many cellulose synthases (CESAs) are significantly active, leading to a rapid increase in cellulose content (Huang et al. 2021). Genome-wide sequence analysis of cotton identified 32 GhCesA genes, among which CESA4/7/8 are implicated in SCW synthesis. Knock-out of any of these three resulted in loss of the cellulose synthase complex (CSC) and production of fiber cells with no SCW thickening (Wen et al. 2022). Other SCW-related proteins such as β-1,4-glucanase KORRIGAN, the putative glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored extracellular protein COBRA, the Golgi-localized STELLO1/2, and CASPARIAN STRIP MEMBRANE DOMAIN-LIKE1 have been shown to regulate SCW thickening by interacting with CSCs and influencing their stability (Roudier et al. 2005; Mansoori et al. 2014; Zhang et al. 2016, 2024). Additionally, the sucrose synthase genes GhSusA and GhSusC are strongly expressed during SCW synthesis, and their overexpression in cotton has been shown to increase fiber strength through enhanced thickening of the cell wall during SCW formation (Ruan et al. 2003; Jiang et al. 2012). The gene GhFSN1, which is specifically expressed in fibers during SCW thickening, encodes an NAC transcription factor, and its upregulation results in increased SCW thickness and cellulose content (Zhang et al. 2018). Research has also demonstrated that a β-1,3-glucanase, GhGLU18, can be directly activated by GhFSN1, which is related to SCW synthesis. This activation promotes polysaccharide metabolism and enhances cellulose synthesis (Fang et al. 2023). Meanwhile, the COBRA-like GhCOBL9 is reported to target the cell wall and bind to crystalline cellulose with high affinity; its overexpression results in increased wall cellulose content, producing thicker and stronger fibers (He et al. 2024). In addition to genes involved in sucrose or cellulose synthesis, those regulating cytoskeletal stability and organization in fiber cells play crucial roles in determining fiber strength. For instance, GhADF1 encodes an actin-depolymerizing factor, and its downregulation in transgenic cotton leads to production of a thicker fiber SCW (Wang et al. 2009). WLIM1a functions as an actin filament binding factor and promotes both fiber cell elongation and SCW synthesis (Han et al. 2016). GhFIM2, a member of the actin filament protein family, possesses actin-binding and bundling capabilities, and also positively regulates fiber elongation and SCW synthesis, thereby enhancing fiber strength (Zhang et al. 2017a). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that there is a positive correlation between SCW thickness and fiber strength.

In previous studies, we identified the GhUBX gene, located within the quantitative trait locus qFS-D3-1, as a key determinant of fiber strength (Zang et al. 2021). However, the molecular mechanisms by which GhUBX regulates SCW thickening remain poorly understood. In this work, we generated GhUBX antisense transgenic cotton lines and observed a marked increase in SCW thickness compared to the control. Furthermore, we demonstrated that GhUBX interacts with GhCDC48, influencing ATPase activity in fiber cells and modulating the expression of important fiber SCW-related genes such as GhSusA, GhKNL1, GhFSN1, and GhCESAs, thereby affecting SCW thickening and consequently influencing fiber strength. Overall, this study elucidates the mechanism by which GhUBX regulates fiber strength through modulation of the SCW thickening process; and offers valuable genetic insights transgenic materials for the improvement of cotton fiber quality, particularly with respect to enhancing fiber strength.

Results

GhUBX modulates fiber strength by regulating SCW thickening

In our previous studies, we identified GhUBX as a candidate gene for qFS-D3-1 through map-based cloning (Zang et al. 2021). To elucidate the biological function of GhUBX in fiber development, we generated ten independent transgenic cotton lines transformed with an antisense (AS) fragment of GhUBX through Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. Informed by PCR and reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), four GhUBX-AS lines (respectively named 159-AS, 163-AS, 177-AS, and 181-AS) with significantly reduced expression of GhUBX were selected and successively self-pollinated to develop homozygous lines for further analysis (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S1A). These lines exhibited significantly increased fiber strength (FS) (cN/tex: centi-newton/tex) compared to the receptor W0 (Fig. 1B). Examination of fiber cell wall thickness and the number of helixes per unit length in the GhUBX-AS lines revealed the SCW to be thickened by 11.71% to 23.83% (Supplementary Fig. S1, B and C), while helix number showed no significant difference from the receptor W0 (Fig. 1, C to F). As numerous studies have clearly established a strong correlation between cotton fiber strength and SCW thickening, we speculate that SCW thickness may be closely tied to the enhancement of FS in GhUBX-AS lines.

GhUBX interacts with the AAA ATPase GhCDC48

To investigate the molecular basis by which GhUBX regulates fiber SCW thickening, we performed yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) experiments to screen for its interacting proteins (Supplementary Table S1). A robust interaction was observed between GhUBX and ubiquitin 10, as well as with GhCDC48. Given that plant UBX is known to be involved in protein degradation, its interaction with ubiquitin aligned with our expectations. Consequently, we focused on GhCDC48, using Y2H to further validate its interaction with GhUBX (Fig. 2A). The coding domain of GhCDC48 spans 2,418 bp and encodes a protein of 805 amino acids that is homologous to the cell division cycle protein 48A found in Arabidopsis and contains a CDC48 N-terminal domain (30 to 113 aa), a CDC48_2 domain (130 to 196 aa), and two AAA domains (241 to 653 aa) (Fig. 2B). The presence of the AAA domain indicated that it belongs to the AAA ATPase family. We additionally used the STRING website (https://string-db.org/) to identify potential interactions of GhCDC48 with other proteins; this analysis highlighted plant UBX-domain-containing proteins and NPL4-like proteins (Fig. 2C), both of which families have ubiquitin-binding activity and can recognize and bind to ubiquitinated proteins. This binding activity is crucial for their functioning in protein degradation pathways, where they help in the recognition and processing of ubiquitinated substrates (Nie et al. 2024). These findings suggest that GhCDC48 may bind with ubiquitin/ubiquitin–protein ligase/protein complexes and may function in the protein processing pathway localized in the endoplasmic reticulum.

Subsequently, the interaction between GhUBX and GhCDC48 was verified by two independent methods, co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) in cotton varieties with differing fiber strength (high strength, GhUBX-Prema; low strength, GhUBX-86-1) and firefly luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. In the co-IP experiment, GhCDC48 was successfully immunoprecipitated along with GhUBX-86-1, indicating an association between GhCDC48 and GhUBX-86-1 in vivo; however, no such interaction was observed with GhUBX-Prema, possibly due to the interaction being too weak to be detected by immunoprecipitation (Fig. 2D). The LCI analysis revealed strong signals for both versions of GhUBX protein, suggesting both to interact with GhCDC48, albeit with differing strength (Fig. 2E). Finally, we investigated the interaction between GhUBX and GhCDC48 using bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC), which demonstrated strong fluorescence signals in both the cell membrane and cytoplasm (Fig. 2F). It has been reported that CDC48A/p97 typically hexamerizes to perform its function. We speculate that the punctate signals observed in this study may originate from the interaction of such GhCDC48 hexamer complexes with GhUBX; moreover, the location of this interaction was consistent with the subcellular colocalization results for GhUBX and GhCDC48 (Supplementary Fig. S2). Based on these findings, we are confident in the existence of an interaction between GhUBX and GhCDC48 proteins.

In terms of nucleotide sequence, GhUBX from the high-strength accession Prema and that from the low-strength accession 86-1 differ by only 6 bp at a location near the N-terminal UBA structural domain. Specifically, this site features a simple sequence repeat (SSR), from which one monomer is lacking in Prema, represented as “CTCGGCCTCT(GCCTCC)5GTCC,” but is present in 86-1, represented as “CTCGGCCTCT(GCCTCC)6GTCC” (Supplementary Fig. S3). To determine the exact region of GhUBX to which GhCDC48 binds, GhUBX was divided into several segments and Y2H assays conducted; the results indicated GhUBX bases 1 to 450 to encompass the region responsible for the protein interaction with GhCDC48. Further Y2H assays narrowed the region of interest to bases 213 to 450, within the SSRs in GhUBX. Notably, a stronger interaction was observed for GhUBX-86-1 and a weaker interaction for GhUBX-Prema (Fig. 2G). This suggests that the 6-bp difference in SSRs contributes to variation in the binding affinity of GhUBX for GhCDC48.

GhCDC48 is associated with fiber SCW thickness and ATPase activity

To investigate the orthologous functions of GhCDC48, we employed virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) to knock down GhCDC48 in cotton. GhCDC48-silenced plants displayed stunted growth and reduced size of newly emerged leaves (Fig. 3, A to C). Consistently, analysis of paraffin sections revealed thinner SCWs in the epidermal cells, cortex cells, phloem cells, xylem cells, and pith cells of silenced plants compared to the control group (Fig. 3, D and E and Supplementary Fig. S4). These phenotypic and anatomical observations collectively suggested GhCDC48 to regulate both cell division and SCW thickening. To further validate this hypothesis, we quantified the expression of key SCW-related genes. Notably, GhCesA7, GhCesA8, GhSusA, GhFSN1, GhKNL1, and NAC21 exhibited significant downregulation in GhCDC48-silenced cotton lines (Supplementary Fig. S5), directly linking GhCDC48 activity to transcriptional regulation of SCW biosynthesis pathways.

Next, to gain further insight into the biological role of GhCDC48 in fiber development, we developed a total of six independent GhCDC48-overexpression (OE) transgenic lines on the TM-1 background through the Agrobacterium-mediated transformation method. Based on PCR detection and expression quantification by qRT-PCR, five GhCDC48-OE lines (designated OE-2, OE-4, OE-7, OE-9, and OE-10) having a noticeably increased expression level were selected and successively self-pollinated to develop pure lines for further analysis (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. S6). Compared with the receptor TM-1, FS was increased by up to 15.8% in the transgenic lines; surprisingly, an increase in fiber length also occurred (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Table S2). Correspondingly, we examined cell wall thickness in paraffin sections from the transformed and receptor cotton lines. Compared with TM-1, the five GhCDC48-OE lines demonstrated fiber SCW thickness increases of 56.4% to 87.7% (Fig. 4, C and D). These results supported that greater thickness of the SCW could enhance mature fiber strength. In addition, we examined the basal expression of GhCDC48 in W0 fiber at 15, 20, and 25 days post-anthesis (DPA); this revealed expression during the critical phase of fiber thickening (20 to 25 DPA) to be significantly higher than during the critical phase of fiber elongation (15 DPA) (Supplementary Fig. S7A). Also, both GhUBX-silenced plants and GhUBX-AS lines exhibited significantly higher expression of GhCDC48 compared to the W0 and GhUBX-OE lines (Supplementary Fig. S7B). Overall, the expression level of GhCDC48 exhibited a positive correlation with fiber SCW thickness.

Previous studies have shown that CDC48 plays indispensable roles in plant growth and development, with its functional loss resulting in reduced tissue size, pleiotropic developmental defects in seedlings, and lower ATPase activity compared with wild-type plants. For instance, suppression of AtCDC48A leads to multiple developmental defects in Arabidopsis seedlings and pollen tubes (Park et al. 2008), while the rice CDC48 mutant (psd128) displays premature aging and death (Huang et al. 2016). The phylogenetic tree of GhCDC48 constructed in this study showed a close genetic relationship with its counterpart in Arabidopsis (Supplementary Fig. S8), prompting us to investigate its functional conservation and compensatory potential. To this end, we created GhCDC48-overexpressing transgenic plants using Agrobacterium-mediated transformation with the cdc48 mutant (SALK_023524C) and Col-0 as recipients. PCR using a triple primer method was performed to confirm homozygosity of the cdc48 mutant (Supplementary Fig. S9). A total of 13 independent GhCDC48/cdc48 overexpression lines were developed. Ectopic expression of GhCDC48 in the cdc48 mutant line rescued the stunted growth and reduced leaf size phenotypes of the cdc48 mutant (Fig. 5, A to D), restoring plant stature to wild-type levels. These results conclusively establish the functional orthologous role of GhCDC48 in regulating plant growth.

To validate the involvement of GhCDC48 in SCW development, we examined paraffin-embedded transverse sections of stems from the wild-type Col-0, the cdc48 mutant, and the GhCDC48/cdc48 complementation lines. Comparative analysis revealed no significant difference in SCW thickness between Col-0 and complementation lines, demonstrating that GhCDC48 fully rescued the SCW development defects resulting from loss of cdc48 (Fig. 5, E and F). These findings further support the orthologous role of GhCDC48 in regulating plant cell wall dynamics. Collectively, ectopic expression of GhCDC48 in the cdc48 mutant can rescue the slow growth, promote plant growth and development.

Given that GhCDC48 is a member of the ATPase family, we compared ATPase activity between transgenic and non-transgenic plants. Consistent with its proposed function, ATPase activity decreased in parallel with reduced GhCDC48 expression (Supplementary Fig. S10A), and was elevated in the overexpression lines (Supplementary Fig. S10B). Furthermore, ATPase activity in the complementation lines was restored to near-wild-type levels (Supplementary Fig. S10C).

GhUBX degrades GhCDC48 via the ubiquitin–26S proteasome pathway

In yeast, several UBX-containing proteins have been shown to act as molecular bridges connecting ubiquitin with substrate proteins (Schuberth and Buchberger 2008; Finley et al. 2012; Gallois et al. 2013). In a previous study, a cotton UBX protein was determined to interact with ubiquitin through its conserved UBA domain (Feng et al. 2018). To systematically dissect the interactions of GhUBX, GhCDC48, and ubiquitin, we performed yeast-three hybrid (Y3H) assays using the following constructs: ubiquitin as prey, GhCDC48 as bait, and truncated GhUBX variants as bridge proteins (Fig. 6A). Yeast co-expressing pGADT7-ubiquitin and Bridge-P, Bridge-8, Bridge-P-450, Bridge-8-450, or Bridge-1416 grew robustly on the SD/–Leu–Trp medium but failed to proliferate on SD/–Leu–Trp–His–Ade medium, indicating no direct interaction between ubiquitin and GhCDC48. When the same yeast cells were spotted on SD/–Leu–Trp–His–Met medium, only yeast harboring Bridge-8 or Bridge-8-450 exhibited growth, whereas that harboring Bridge-P, Bridge-P-450, or Bridge-1416 showed complete growth suppression (Fig. 6B). This differential growth phenotype unequivocally establishes that GhUBX serves as a bridge between GhCDC48 and ubiquitin, with the UBA-SSR in the N-terminal region of GhUBX being essential for binding GhCDC48. A dilution gradient growth assay further validated these findings (Fig. 6C), pinpointing the SSR-containing domain as the core structural determinant for ubiquitin–GhUBX–CDC48 complex assembly.

In vitro ubiquitination assays using purified GhUBX-GST and GhCDC48-His proteins revealed that GhCDC48 undergoes ubiquitination in the presence of E1, E2, and GhUBX-8-GST proteins (Fig. 6D), which subsequently triggers degradation of the complex. Treatment with MG132 (a 26S proteasome-specific inhibitor) stabilized both GhUBX and GhCDC48 protein levels, directly implicating the 26S-proteasome pathway in GhCDC48 turnover. Semi-in vivo degradation assays further validated this observation, showing accelerated degradation of GhCDC48 by GhUBX-8 compared to GhUBX-P (Fig. 6E). Collectively, these findings support GhCDC48 as a key regulator of cotton fiber strength through a ubiquitin–proteasome-dependent regulatory pathway. In this complex, GhCDC48, which is linked to ubiquitin by the functional E3 ligase GhUBX, is ubiquitinated and further degraded via the ubiquitin–26S proteasome-dependent pathway. Within this pathway, GhUBX acts as a functional E3 ligase, bridging ubiquitin to GhCDC48 via its N-terminal UBA-SSR domain (Fig. 6, B and C). Ubiquitinated GhCDC48 is targeted for degradation by the 26S proteasome, reducing its cellular abundance. Depletion of GhCDC48 diminishes ATPase activity, disrupting CSC trafficking and impairing SCW thickening in mature fibers, which in turn compromises fiber mechanical strength. In summary, our data delineate a GhUBX-GhCDC48 regulatory module wherein GhUBX modulates GhCDC48 stability via ubiquitination, thereby influencing SCW thickness and ultimately determining cotton fiber strength (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Ubiquitination-mediated regulatory involved in cotton fiber development

Ubiquitin serves as a post-translational modification marker that directs its substrate protein to the 26S proteasome for degradation. Extensive studies have demonstrated the pivotal role of this ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) in orchestrating diverse cell processes, including cell cycle regulation (Meyer 2005), cell proliferation and differentiation (Feiler et al. 1995), protein degradation, membrane fusion, DNA replication, gene expression, DNA damage response, apoptosis, and autophagy (Ye 2006; Ramanathan and Ye 2011; Dantuma and Hoppe 2012; Dargemont and Ossareh-Nazari 2012). Notably, ubiquitination-mediated protein turnover has been proven a critical regulatory mechanism governing fiber development in cotton. Experimental evidence indicates that GhD53 negatively regulates fiber elongation by inhibiting linolenic acid biosynthesis, while strigolactones alleviate this inhibition through UPS-dependent degradation of GhD53 (Sun et al. 2025). In addition, the E3 ligase GhHUB2 has been shown to polyubiquitinate GhKNL1, a transcriptional repressor of fiber development, thereby targeting it for proteasomal degradation. This post-translational modification subsequently activates downstream genes associated with fiber elongation and the SCW, such as cellulose-synthesis-related genes (Feng et al. 2018). Despite these advances, the role of ubiquitination in determining fiber cell differentiation and development is still to be elucidated and deserves further exploration.

CDC48, a member of the AAA + ATPase superfamily, ranks among the most abundant proteins in eukaryotic cells, constituting approximately 1% of cytosolic proteins(Ye 2006). It exhibits conserved structural architecture and orchestrates multifaceted cellular processes through its chaperone-like activities (Jentsch and Rumpf 2007). The functional versatility of CDC48 in eukaryotic systems is fundamentally dependent on its ATP hydrolysis capacity. Studies have indicated that in plant cells, it participates in the regulation of ubiquitin fusion degradation pathways, immunity, autophagy, cell division, and differentiation (Clavel and Dagdas 2021). Moreover, many of the pathways CDC48 is involved in relate to proteasome-mediated degradation (Wu et al. 2016; Boos et al. 2020; Escobar-Henriques and Anton 2020). In Arabidopsis, analysis of in situ expression patterns in relation to cell proliferation status indicated that AtCDC48 is highly expressed in proliferating cells of asexual buds, roots, inflorescences, flowers, and rapidly growing cells (Feiler et al. 1995). Studies in rice examining leaf cell status by flow cytometry combined with analysis of cell-cycle-related gene expression likewise demonstrated that OsCDC48 is involved in regulation of the cell cycle and thereby affects growth and development (Shi et al. 2019). In the present study, the Arabidopsis cdc48 mutant presented a dwarf phenotype with smaller leaves and shorter stems, and GhCDC48/cdc48 complemented this dwarf phenotype, enabling normal growth and development (Fig. 5). Correspondingly, VIGS of GhCDC48 in cotton slowed development, with thinner cell walls in the stem and lower ATPase activity, whereas its overexpression resulted in thickened fiber cell walls (Fig. 3). Collectively, these results strongly indicate that GhCDC48 is a potential genetic factor for cotton fiber SCW thickening.

The AAA ATPase CDC48 interacts with the UBX protein domain in plants

How does CDC48 regulate cotton fiber development? Our results provide a theoretical basis for understanding how GhCDC48 promotes fiber strength. Some members of the UBX protein family have been demonstrated to be auxiliary factors of CDC48 and usually function together (Schuberth and Buchberger 2008; Kloppsteck et al. 2012; Zhang et al. 2021a). There are also reports of AtCDC48 having a role in the ubiquitin–26S proteasome system in Arabidopsis (Gallois et al. 2013; Kretzschmar et al. 2018; Deruyffelaere et al. 2018). The Arabidopsis genome encodes 15 proteins with UBX domains (referred to as AtPUXs, plant UBX domain-containing) (Liu and Li 2014). Proteins containing UBX domains are divided into families based on their structural domains, but are mainly classified into two categories (Alexandru et al. 2008): those with an N-terminal UBA domain that binds to ubiquitin, and those without a UBA domain. Yeast three-hybrid analysis has shown that AtPUX7, which mainly localizes to the nucleus, can act as a bridge between AtCDC48A and ubiquitin and thereby play a role in targeted protein degradation (Gallois et al. 2013). Recent research has also identified members of the plant PUX protein family, including PUX10, as seed lipid droplet proteins. Generally, proteins with UBX domains have been shown to participate in lipid droplet formation and the degradation of lipid droplet proteins through serving as functional E3 ligases in the UPS (Zhang et al. 2017b; Feng et al. 2018). Correspondingly, the UBA and UBX domains of Arabidopsis PUX10 interact with ubiquitin and CDC48A, recruiting CDC48A to ubiquitinated oleosins and promoting their dissociation through its ATPase activity, thereby regulating the extraction of ubiquitinated lipids from lipid droplets (Deruyffelaere et al. 2018). However, there are yet no studies on the correlation of GhCDC48 activity and lipid droplets; rather, extant work focuses more on ATPase activity and the SCW.

Our Y2H results indicate an interaction between GhCDC48 and GhUBX, with the binding region in GhUBX being narrowed down to a 150-aa stretch that includes the UBA domain and a SSR with 6 bp difference between varieties of differing fiber strength (Fig. 2G). Additional Y2H assays also confirmed that the UBA domain can interact with ubiquitin (Feng et al. 2018), identifying this 150-aa stretch as a critical binding region. Further exploration of the specific interactions between GhUBX and GhCDC48 showed GhUBX-Prema to more weakly interact with GhCDC48 than does GhUBX-86-1, suggesting that the version of GhUBX in the high-fiber-strength variety Prema is less able to bind and degrade GhCDC48 proteins. By extension, the higher amount of GhCDC48 protein in Prema fiber cells likely contributes to maintaining a higher level of ATPase activity, giving Prema a greater capability for cellulose synthesis and secondary wall thickening. These findings strongly suggest that during cotton fiber development, GhCDC48 may affect the development and growth of fiber cells by interacting with the ubiquitin ligase GhUBX, being marked by ubiquitin molecules, and subsequently being degraded by the 26S proteasome.

GhCDC48 participates in SCW development of cotton fiber via affecting ATPase activity

The SCW is the primary element of mature cotton fibers and serves as a principal determinant of fiber strength. To date, only limited research has been conducted on the function of CDC48 in relation to the SCW. CDC48 responds to the UPS within the cell and regulates the ubiquitination of misfolded proteins. Recent studies have suggested that during aluminum toxicity treatment in Citrus roots, CDC48 may function as a key downstream regulatory factor, modulating uridine diphosphate glucose (UDPG), a cellulose precursor, to participate in the thickening of the root cell wall (Yang et al. 2018). We observed that silencing GhCDC48 in cotton leads to thinner cell walls in the stem, a feature shared with the Arabidopsis cdc48 mutant (Figs. 3E and 5F). Moreover, RT-qPCR revealed that during the secondary wall development stage, some genes specifically and highly expressed in fiber are downregulated in GhCDC48-silenced cotton (Supplementary Fig. S5) (Brill et al. 2011; Jiang et al. 2012; Yuan et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2018, 2021a; Wang et al. 2022). In contrast, overexpression of GhCDC48 led to a significant increase in fiber cell wall thickness compared with the recipient cotton TM-1. These data suggest that GhCDC48 may directly or indirectly control downstream genes that influence cell wall biosynthesis and hence the strength of cotton fibers. Assessments of ATPase activity in GhCDC48/cdc48 Arabidopsis and cotton supported that loss of CDC48 function leads to decreased cellular ATPase activity, while overexpression of GhCDC48 in transgenic cotton leads to increased ATPase activity (Supplementary Fig. S10). We conclude that the function of GhCDC48, as a protein that interacts with GhUBX in enhancing cotton fiber strength, may be to regulate ATPase activity. This regulation may subsequently affect the cellular abundance of proteins related to SCW formation, promote the biosynthesis of cellulose, affect the rate of secondary wall thickening, and thereby produce thicker secondary walls. This process likely regulates the viability of fiber cells, ultimately leading to increased fiber strength.

SSR variation in GhUBX correlates with fiber strength

SSRs are widespread in eukaryotic genomes as short tandem reiterations of sequence motifs. In addition to being widely used in marker-assisted breeding of plants, recent years have seen an increasing number of reports on their effects in regulating a variety of complex traits (Hammock and Young 2005). For example, microsatellites in starch-synthesizing genes have been found to regulate starch physicochemical properties in waxy rice, with 18 types of microsatellites, SNPs, and sequence-tagged sites at three genes being related to starch synthesis (Bao et al. 2002, 2006). In the current study, we have found that GhUBX binds with GhCDC48 to form a complex that, via the ubiquitin-mediated 26S proteasome pathway, may degrade GhCDC48, influencing its protein level and subsequently altering ATPase activity level. More importantly, the interaction between GhUBX and GhCDC48 differs in representative cultivars with differing fiber strength, which are genetically distinguished by a 6-bp InDel in GhUBX (Fig. 2). Our previous research discerned two categories of microsatellite alleles in cotton, distinguished by the nucleotide variation CTCGGCCTCT(GCCTCC)5/6. This 6-bp InDel has already been developed into a marker K222 and validated in a population of 268 cultivars (Zang et al. 2021). Correlation analysis suggested the interaction between GhUBX and GhCDC48 to be significantly associated with fiber strength, with GhCDC48-overexpressing transgenic lines exhibiting a high level of fiber strength; additionally, analysis confirmed SCW thickness to be significantly associated with fiber strength (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Table S2).

In this study, we proved that both silencing of GhUBX with antisense transcripts and overexpression of GhCDC48 can increase fiber strength, mediated in both cases by increased thickness of the SCW. GhUBX protein abundance was reduced in the GhUBX antisense lines, preventing the degradation of GhCDC48, which led to increased GhCDC48 content and enhanced ATPase activity, thereby improving fiber strength. In parallel, excessive expression of GhCDC48 protein resulted in elevated fiber ATPase activity in the GhCDC48 overexpression lines, leading to thickening of the SCW and a subsequent increase in fiber strength. This process can then affect downstream fiber strength genes, dynamically regulating SCW biosynthesis and ultimately impacting the final strength of cotton fiber. Thus, our study confirms that ubiquitination plays a central role in the SCW synthesis stage of cotton fiber development. In the future, it is necessary to combine multiomics technologies, gene editing, and molecular interaction research to further analyze the spatiotemporal specificity of ubiquitination networks and their synergistic mechanisms with other regulatory modules to provide theoretical support for cotton fiber strength improvement.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Upland cotton (Gossypium hirsutum) TM-1, W0, Prema, 86-1, and GhCDC48 transgenic plants were cultivated in the field at the experimental station of Zhejiang University in Jing Shan, Hangzhou. Fiber cells at different developmental stages were carefully removed from seeds and immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for DNA and RNA extraction.

Fiber quality measurement

All naturally opened bolls were hand-harvested to acquire fiber for the assessment of quality traits, including fiber length (FL, mm), strength (FS, cN/tex), micronaire (FMIC), elongation (FE), and uniformity ratio (FU). In comparative field trials, transgenic and wild-type cotton were planted in a randomized block design with three replications. Each experimental replication included approximately one 4.5-m long row, with 75 cm between rows and 35 cm between plants in each row. Five plants in the middle of each row were tagged for harvesting, and middle cotton bolls were harvested. The fiber quality testing standard was the GB/T 20392-2006 HVI Cotton Fiber Physical Properties Test Method. Fiber samples were evaluated by the Cotton Quality Supervision, Inspection and Testing Center, Ministry of Agriculture, Xinjiang, China.

The quantitative RT-PCR analysis

Specific qRT-PCR primers were designed using the Integrated DNA Technologies online tool (https://sg.idtdna.com). Histone3 (AF024716) was used as an internal control. qRT-PCR was carried out on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies, Foster City, CA, USA) in a 20-μl volume containing 100 ng of cDNA, 4 pM of each primer, and 10 μl of AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The data were evaluated using the comparative cycle threshold method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). Each reaction was performed on three biological replicates (three samples harvested from three plants, one from each), each with two technical replicates (using the same sample). Mean values and standard errors were calculated according to the data from three replicates.

Scanning and transmission electron microscope analyses

Samples of mature fibers from transgenic cotton lines and W0 were dehydrated, fixed on the observation table, sputter-coated with silver using an E-1010/E-1020 ion sputter (Hitachi, Japan), and imaged using an SU8010 scanning electron microscope (SEM) at 3.0 kV (Hitachi, Japan).

The mature fibers of transgenic cotton were also observed using a transmission electron microscope (TEM). Transverse sections of the fiber samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in a phosphate buffer overnight at 4 °C and then in 1% OsO4 for 2 h. The samples were subsequently dehydrated through the application of step-graded ethanol and embedded in Spurr's medium prior to ultrathin sectioning. Sections (90 nm thick) were cut with an ultramicrotome (EMUC7, 645 Leica, Germany) and collected on nickel mesh. The sections were air dried, stained, and viewed with a Hitachi H-7650 TEM at 80 kV (Bio-ultrastructure Analysis Laboratory of the Analysis Center of Agrobiology and Environmental Sciences, Zhejiang University). Five to ten nonserial sections per genotype were examined per line, and at least ten cell wall interfaces between the fiber and fiber base were examined per genotype. The same mature fiber samples used for fiber quality investigation were also used for SEM and TEM analyses.

Vector construction and VIGS

A 298-bp fragment of GhCDC48 cDNA was amplified by PCR. The resulting PCR product was cloned into pTRV2 to produce a vector referred to as pTRV2:GhCDC48. Agrobacterium cells carrying pTRV1 and pTRV2:GhCDC48 were resuspended in an infiltration medium (10 mm MgCl2, 10 mm MES, 200 μM acetosyringone) and adjusted to an OD600 of 1.5. Agrobacterium strains containing the pTRV1 and pTRV2:GhCDC48 vectors were mixed at a ratio of 1:1, and this suspension was injected into the cotyledons of ten-day-old TM-1 seedlings, which were placed in the dark for 24 h and then incubated at 22 °C with a 16-h light/8-h dark cycle. Empty-vector (TRV:00)-transformed plants were used as experimental controls. The cloroplastos alterados 1 (CLA1) gene was used as an indicator of the silencing effect (Gao et al. 2011). Experimental groups comprised 30 seedlings. Photos were taken two weeks after injection, and stems and leaves were collected for further detection.

Cotton transformation

For the overexpression construct, primers with added Bam H I and Sma I sites were used to amplify the open reading frame of GhCDC48, which was then cloned into the WMV062 vector under the control of the constitutive cauliflower mosaic virus 35S promoter. This GhCDC48-overexpression (GhCDC48-OE) construct was introduced into G. hirsutum acc. TM-1 via Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation as described above. Homozygosity of the transgenic plants was determined using the spectinomycin selection marker coupled with PCR-based genotyping. DNA polymerase (I5TM 2 × High-Fidelity Master Mix) was purchased from TSINGKE (Beijing, China).

Y2H and Y3H assays

Y2H assays were performed using the GAL4 vector system (Clontech, USA). Sectionalized GhUBX coding regions, as GhUBX-86-1 (UBA), GhUBX-Prema (UBA), and GhUBX (UAS-UBX), and the full-length GhCDC48 coding region were respectively cloned into the pGBKT7 and pGADT7 vectors. The constructs were then cotransformed into yeast strain Y2H. The transformed cells were adjusted to OD600 = 0.4 ∼ 0.6 and grown on SD/–Trp–Leu or SD/–Trp–Leu–His–Ade plates for three to seven days at 30 °C.

The Y3H assay was performed using the pBridge vector system (Takara Bio, Japan). The conserved ubiquitin sequence was fused to GAL4 AD in pGADT7. For the pBridge-GhCDC48-GhUBX construct, the full-length coding sequence of GhCDC48 was cloned into multiple cloning site (MCS) I of the pBridge vector fused to the GAL4 BD domain, and the sectionalized GhUBX coding region was cloned into MCS II of the pBridge vector. The transformed cells were grown in SD/–Trp–Leu–Met plates and SD/–Trp–Leu–His–Met plates for five to eight days at 30 °C.

Co-immunoprecipitation assays

co-IP was conducted as previously described (Liu et al. 2010). In brief, GhCDC48-RFP and GhUBX-P-GFP or GhUBX-8-GFP were transiently co-expressed in N. benthamiana leaves for three days. Subsequently, lysates were incubated with anti-GFP or anti-RFP magnetic beads (LABLEAD Inc., Beijing, China) at 4 °C for 2 h. The beads were washed three times with PBS, and the immunoprecipitated proteins were examined by immunoblotting.

BiFC assays

For the BiFC assays, GhUBX was cloned into YCE and fused with the C-terminus of YFP, and GhCDC48 was cloned into YNE and fused with the N-terminus of YFP. The recombinant constructs were then cotransformed in pairs into young N. benthamiana leaves, as was the construct H2B, which serves as a marker for cell nucleus localization. Fluorescence signal was observed using a Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope, Laser Line (Excitation): 514-laser line (514-nm excitation). Emission bandwidth: at 530 to 590 nm (4.5% intensity, 1,000 V master gain, 1.0 digital gain).

In vitro and in vivo protein ubiquitination degradation assays

In vitro and in vivo degradation assays were performed as previously described (Zang et al. 2021). For in vivo protein degradation analysis, a GhUBX-P-GFP sample, GhUBX-8-GFP sample, and GhCDC48-RFP sample were harvested at three days after inoculation. The four samples were separately extracted with native extraction buffer (50 mm Tris-MES [pH 8.0], 0.5 m sucrose, 1 mm MgCl2, 10 mm EDTA, 5 mm DTT) and a protease inhibitor cocktail (CompleteMini tablets; Roche, Indianapolis, IN, USA), after which the indicated concentrations of ATP and MG132 or DMSO (control) were added to the protein extract. The mixtures were incubated at 25 °C, and samples were removed at different time points for immunoblot analyses.

Determination of Ca2+/Mg2+-ATPase activity

The CheKineTM Ca2+/Mg2+-ATPase Activity Assay Kit (Abbkine Scientific Co., Ltd., China; cat#KTB1810) was used to measure Ca2+/Mg2+-ATPase activity in VIGS seedlings, cotton fiber, and Arabidopsis leaves. Briefly, 0.1 g of each sample was weighed, 1 mL of extraction buffer was added, and the material was homogenized in an ice bath for 3 min. The sample was then centrifuged at 8,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C and the supernatant collected for the assay. The test solution was prepared according to the kit instructions, its absorbance measured at 660 nm, and the Ca2+/Mg2+-ATPase activity finally calculated.

Quantification and statistical analysis

In this study, significant differences between two samples were determined by two-tailed paired Student's t-test. Error bars in figures indicate the standard deviation, n denotes the sample size, and asterisks indicate the level of statistical significance: ∗P < 0.05; ∗∗P < 0.01. At least three biological replicates were included. Statistical analysis was performed by GraphPad Prism 6.0. Determination of cell wall thickness was performed using ImageJ.
