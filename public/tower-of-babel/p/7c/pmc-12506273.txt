Developing a core collection for the conservation of Theobroma cacao’s genetic diversity
Authors: Evelyn T Todd, Fabrizio Arigoni, James A Holzwarth, Laurence Bellanger, Patrick Descombes, Eduardo Beche, Tony Lass, Mark J Guiltinan, Siela N Maximova, Mariela Leandro, Virginie Mérot, Maud Lepelley, Audrey Fillodeau
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCrop core collections serve as a means of conserving genetic variation and providing a diverse array of accessions for future research and breeding endeavours. Despite the challenges posed by diseases and climate change to global cacao production, no centralized core collection currently exists. To address this gap, we have assembled a new, general-purpose cacao core collection from accessions present in international collections.ResultsA dataset of 310 cacao accessions from international collections were selected based on material availability, geographic origin, and known phenotypic characteristics. Population structure analysis of 26,601 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from these accessions revealed ten distinct genetic groups, consistent with previous studies. A core collection of 96 accessions was subsequently selected, encompassing 99.6% of the genetic diversity within the dataset. This collection comprises representatives of all ten genetic groups, as well as accessions exhibiting high productivity and pathogen resistance.ConclusionsThis core collection will serve as a crucial genetic resource for the cacao community. Cultivating and maintaining the plants from this core collection will contribute to the preservation of genetic diversity within the population. Furthermore, this collection, when combined with appropriate phenotyping, will establish a foundation for novel breeding programs aimed at enhancing crop improvement in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-11882-6.Keywords: Cacao, Core collection, Genetic diversity, Population genetics

Background

Cacao (Theobroma cacao L.) has been an important agricultural crop for millennia since its domestication in the Upper Amazonian region around 5,000 years ago [1]. It is now cultivated in tropical regions worldwide, primarily for confectionery production, with the global cacao market is projected to reach 192.12 billion USD by 2028 [2]. However, cacao cultivation faces numerous challenges, including diseases and pests, as well as climate change-induced threats. These factors pose significant risks to long-term, stable production, and the livelihoods of farmers [3]. Furthermore, ongoing deforestation within the Amazon basin threatens the loss of wild cacao resources, endangering the genetic diversity of this precious crop [4]. Consequently, it is imperative to implement initiatives for the conservation and protection of these precious resources moving into the future.

Comprehensive genetic studies of cacao germplasm have identified ten distinct genetic groups [5, 6]. Three of these groups were traditionally used in cultivation (Criollo, Amelonado, and Nacional), where the other seven consist of wild accessions collected from different regions of the Amazon basin [5, 7]. The genetic diversity patterns of cacao in the Amazon follows a spatial distribution, with locally prevalent alleles potentially underpinning adaptation to different environmental conditions [7–9]. The unique genetic variation harboured by these wild plants may confer distinct physiological characteristics, enabling better adaptation to the evolving growing climate for cacao [7].

Genebanks serve as repositories of genetic variation for crops, although maintaining large collections of plants can be resource-intensive and challenging due to the associated costs and space requirements [10]. To address this limitation, core collections have emerged as a method for conserving germplasm resources in crops [11, 12], as they effectively capture a representative subset of diversity, whilst minimising redundancy [13, 14]. In-depth genetic and phenotypic analysis of core collections facilitate the development of disease resistant germplasm and novel varieties for breeding in crops.

The selection of accessions for a core collection typically relies on genetic data to assess and capture the genetic diversity of the population. There are multiple methods that have been proposed for selecting a core collection, depending on the purpose. The most common core collection is a general-purpose collection (CC-I), which should represent the genetic diversity of the whole dataset by minimizing the genetic distance between each accession and its closest core representative [15]. Consequently, both common and rare alleles should be represented in a general-purpose core collection.

Currently, there are 47 national collections and two international collections of cacao exist, containing both cultivated and wild germplasm [16]. Sadly, cocoa plant material can, currently, only be conserved in cryopreservation or in the field as a living collection that can only be maintained at a considerable on-going cost [17, 18]. The large number of plants and redundancy across these multiple collections and different geographic locations makes it challenging to maintain, phenotype, and conserve this valuable cacao germplasm. Some core collections of cacao plants have previously been curated based on local germplasm using a small number of genetic markers for selection [19–21]. Although useful for local breeding programs, these core collections do not capture the extensive population diversity, and restrictions on the exchange of genetic material between regions limit their use by the international cacao community.

In this study, we have curated a general-purpose core collection of cacao germplasm derived from accessions in the two international collections, consisting of 96 accessions. We propose to maintain this core collection in different countries. Many of these accessions are also maintained in the International Cocoa Quarantine Centre at the University of Reading in the United Kingdom (ICQC, R). The establishment of this core collection aims to conserve cacao genetic resources in a de-centralized and secured setting, as well as facilitating access to germplasm for the cacao community. Additionally, this core collection opens the possibility for detailed phenotyping, conducting association studies for traits of interest, and the development of new varieties in breeding programs.

Materials and methods

Selection of accessions

The two international cacao genebanks, the International Cocoa Genebank, Trinidad (ICG, T) and the International Cacao Collection at CATIE (IC3), collectively house a substantial number of accessions with comprehensive coverage of genetic diversity. The ICG, T, situated in Trinidad and Tobago, comprises of 2,263 accessions [22]. This collection holds historic germplasm collected from multiple expeditions into the Amazon between 1935 and 1970, providing valuable access to wild diversity [23]. Located in Costa Rica, the IC3 holds 1,146 accessions [24]. For more than 75 years, CATIE has been conserving the genetic diversity of cacao and actively participated in scientific studies, leading to the development of novel and improved varieties, with a particular focus on disease resistance [23]. A part of germplasm from these international genebanks, is stored at ICQC, R. For sequencing analysis and curation of the core collection described here, accessions were collected at CATIE and ICQC, R.

The initial selection process was based on the list guided by the CFC/ICCO/Biodiversity project, which focused on selecting varieties with pest and disease resistance. This collaborative effort involved the Common Fund for Commodities (CFC), the International Cocoa Organization (ICCO), and the International Plant Genetic Resources Institute (IPGRI, now Biodiversity International) [23, 25]. The accessions present in the ICQC, R (UK) that had not yet been selected were added to this list. The resulting dataset contained 361 accessions, consisting of 244 accessions from ICQC, R, 28 from CFC project, and 89 common to both the ICQC, R and CFC project.

Concurrently, a review of DNA sequences available on public databases was conducted. Accessions with higher sequencing depth of coverage, and available in ICG, T; IC3 or ICQC, R were subsequently prioritized for further consideration.

The second round of selection involved the incorporation of plant material and DNA sequences from the previously identified accessions. This process resulted in the selection of a diverse range of accessions with known geographic origins, genetic diversity, phenotypic characteristics, and/or applications in breeding programs. Based on this comprehensive evaluation, a final total dataset of 310 accessions was defined: 216 accessions from the international collections that were sequenced for this study, combined with 94 publicly available DNA sequences (Table S2).

A STRUCTURE analysis was conducted on a subset of 171 individuals to determine their genetic makeup [26]. These individuals had already been genotyped using 22 single nucleotide polymorphisms, and were compared to 100 references known to represent the genetic groups from Motamayor et al., (2008) [5]. This analysis demonstrated the representation of the ten genetic groups identified by Motamayor et al., (2008) [5], indicating that the genetic variability observed within this subset is representative of the diversity currently present in the international collections (Figure S1).

The sequencing data for 94 accessions was available on public repositories from the NCBI BioProjects PRJNA486011, PRJNA880462, and PRJNA558793. The project PRJNA486011 sourced accessions from The University of West Indes and CATIE, the accessions from PRJNA880462 were sourced from Ecuador, and the material sequenced in PRJNA558793 are maintained in Penn State Greenhouse.

In our dataset, we also included some duplicates of the same accessions from different collections and sources. This was to check the genetic conformity of plants (Table S1), as previously, plants with the same accession name have been found to have different genetic profiles. This may contribute to contrasting phenotypic properties identified for the same accession between different studies.This included 5 ICS_1 accessions, with 4 from the public database and one directly sampled from ICQC, R. We also sequenced SCA_6 sampled from ICQC, R to complement 2 accessions from the public database. The accession PA_107_PER, known to be sourced directly from Peru and held in ICQC, R was sequenced to complement a public accession. Additionally, we sequenced SILECIA_5 directly from IC3 to compare with the accession from ICQC, R.

DNA extraction and sequencing

Tissue samples were collected from fresh leaves for sequencing including 159 samples from ICQC, R, 42 samples from IC3, and 15 samples with known origins from international collections maintained by Nestlé.

Upon reception of the samples at the Nestlé laboratory (Tours, France), 12 discs (6 mm diameter) were cut from the fresh leaves and placed in collection tubes (QIAGEN, Ref 19560) containing one tungsten bead. The tubes were stored at −20 °C, and then the leaves were ground using liquid nitrogen. DNA was then extracted using the QIAGEN DNeasy® 96 Plant Kit (Ref 69181).

To reach a concentration of 50 ng/µl required for downstream sequencing, the eluted DNA was concentrated using the following steps: For the elution step, collection tubes from QIAGEN (Ref 19560) were used instead of the short microtubes from the dark blue plate provided in the kit. Precipitation was performed with 20 µl of Sodium Acetate, 3 M ph5.2 (SIGMA S-7899) and centrifuged briefly before adding 500 µl of cold absolute ethanol (−20 °C). After mixing by gentle inversion 8–10 times, the tubes were stored overnight at −20 °C and then centrifuged at 4 °C for 1 h. The ethanol was discarded, the pellet was washed with 70% ethanol, and then the tubes dried on absorbent paper for one hour, then resuspended in 40 µl of diluted Tris 10mM buffer.

After DNA extraction, the concentration of each sample was evaluated using the Invitrogen picogreen kit (Ref P7589). The DNA was then shipped to the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign (The United States of America) for sequencing.

The shotgun genomic libraries were prepared using the Illumina DNA Prep kit (Illumina cat no 20060060), then pooled and quantified by qPCR. The libraries were sequenced on two S4 lanes for 151 cycles from each end of the fragments using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (average length of 150 base pairs).

Read alignment and variant calling

Following sequencing, bcf2fastq (version 2.20) was used to demultiplex and trim adaptors from the FASTQ files. The trimmed and demultiplexed reads were aligned to the Criollo v2.0 reference genome assembly [27] using BWA-MEM2 (version 2.2.1) [28] with standard parameters. Reads were filtered using the MarkDuplicates command in GATK (version 4.0.4.0) [29], followed by samtools flagstat (version 1.3.1) [30].

Accessions with a depth of coverage over 5X were used for variant discovery (n = 257), using GATK Haplotype Caller (version 4.0.4.0) [31]. This variant panel was used to recall variants on the remaining lower coverage samples (n = 53) with the NRGene proprietary pipeline using GATK Haplotype caller. This resulted in a raw data of 26,380,748 variants (24,134,193 SNPs and 2,741,561 INDELs).

The samples were merged using bcftools merge “-m all --info-rules DP: sum” (version 1.15.1) and variants were filtered to include only bi-allelic SNPs, SNPs missing in no more than 20% of samples, SNPs with a minor allele frequency > 5%, SNPs in regions with more than four others within 100 base pairs. Variant quality filtering using GATK excluded variants based on mapping quality, quality by depth, DPsum, and strand bias in site using the recommended parameters. Variants in regions with Low Complexity Context < 1.7, and %GC < 30% or > 50% were excluded with using the LCC command in Python Bio.SeqUtils (version 1.79). Finally, variants were thinned to exclude those in LD using PLINK (version 1.9) with the “--indep-pairwise” parameter, resulting in a final panel of 26,601 SNPs for further analysis.

Population genetics analysis

To reconstruct the evolutionary relationships between accessions, a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using IQ-TREE (version 1.6.12) [32]. The best partitioning scheme was identified using the ModelFinder option (TVMe + R4) and 1000 ultrafast pseudo-bootstraps were performed using the parameters “-m MFP -bb 1000 -alrt 1000 -nt 4 -st DNA”. Nodes with a pseudo-bootstrap support below 90% (representing lower confidence resolution) were coloured in red on the tree, and the tree was midpoint rooted using the ggtree package in R (version 3.6.2) [33].

The ancestry profiles of all individuals were estimated using ADMIXTURE (version 1.3.0) [34]. To identify the optimal number of source populations (K), ADMIXTURE analyses were run for K values between 2 and 15. The K value with the lowest cross-validation error was 10, which was further confirmed by assessing the pairwise correlation matrix of residuals using EvalAdmix (version 0.962) [35].

Subsequently, the ancestry profiles from the ADMIXTURE analysis were assigned to the nomenclature described by Motamayor et al., (2008) [5] by cross-checking accessions that had previously been analysed and assigned to groups [6]. Additionally, SSR data generated for some accessions further confirmed this nomenclature.

The population structure was also visualized using a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) generated by PLINK (version 1.9) [36]. Accessions with more than 70% genetic ancestry assigned to one ADMIXTURE profile were coloured according to their ancestry group. Accessions with less than 70% genetic ancestry from one profile were considered admixed and coloured in grey.

The genetic relationships between accessions were inferred using KING (version 2.3.0) [37]. Pairs of accessions that had an inferred relationship of Duplicate/Monozygotic twins, with an HomIBS0 value of 0 were flagged. Of a total of 25 pairs, 13 consisted of accessions with the same name but from different sources. Duplicates of accessions with the same name were excluded, leaving 297 accessions in the dataset for the selection of the core collection.

Selection of the core collection

A set of accessions was selected to constitute the general-purpose core collection using ShinyCore (version 1.0) [15]. ShinyCore uses both the coverage score (the proportion of alleles in the whole dataset included in the core) and the rarity score (a measure of rare alleles in each accession) to select a core that best represents the diversity of the population. Using the matrix of 297 accessions and 26,601 SNPs, ShinyCore was instructed to select a core collection with a coverage score between 99 and 99.9%. The resulting core consisted of 90 accessions, which collectively represented 99.6% of the genetic diversity present in the input matrix.

We then augmented this initial selection with additional accessions exhibiting important phenotypic characteristics.

Phenotypic information for cacao accessions

After selecting a core collection based on diversity, additional accessions with known, important phenotypic traits were included. Resistance to Black Pod Root (BPR) disease was assessed for 60 clones from the CATIE IC3 international collection using artificial inoculation on leaves by Fister et al., (2019) [38]. Out of the 60 clones phenotyped, POUND_7 was identified as being the most tolerant accession, based on lesion size. Additionally, SCA_6 (SCAVINA 6) also showed small lesion sizes, consistent with previous reports of tolerance to Black Pod Rot and Witches’ Broom (WB) diseases [39, 40].

Resistance to Black Pod Rot and Frosty Pod Rot (FPR) diseases was assessed using artificial inoculation according to a protocol defined and detailed by CATIE [41]. Briefly, pods were artificially inoculated with fungal inoculum, then two months after inoculation, susceptibility to FPR is assessed based on external and internal tissue necrosis. For BPR, sensitivity is based on spot size 10 days after inoculation. Amongst the accessions tested and sequenced for this project, GU275E showed a high tolerance level to Frosty Pod Rot, while CATIE_1000 and EET_272_ECU exhibited the highest tolerance to Black Pod Rot (Table S3).

Additionally, CCN_51 and ICS_1 were selected to supplement the core because they are widely used in breeding programs due to their known high productivity [42–44]. IMC_67 was also selected because it is commonly employed as a rootstock, and has been associated with tolerance to water deficit [45].

Of these 8 accessions, IMC_67 and SCA_6 were already selected by ShinyCore. We included the remaining 6 accessions (POUND_7, ICS_1, CCN_51, CATIE_1000, EET_272_ECU, and GU275E) in addition to the 90 initially selected by ShinyCore, resulting in a final core collection of 96 individuals (Table S2).

The nucleotide diversity of the entire dataset and the core collection was calculated using VCFtools (version 0.1.15) [46] with the parameter “--window-pi 10000”, and averaging over all bins.

Results and discussion

Population structure of cacao

The ADMIXTURE analysis of genetic variation (26,601 SNPs) from 310 cacao accessions identified an optimal number of 10 ancestral groups, in accordance with those originally described by Motamayor et al., (2008) [5] (Fig. 1). These genetic groups corresponded to the structure of the maximal likelihood phylogenetic tree, where accessions from the same ancestral source grouped together in clusters. Accessions with an admixed ancestry clustered intermediately between the pure groups. Many accessions in the dataset exhibited an admixed genetic makeup, commonly between Amelonado (yellow), Criollo (red), and Nacional (navy), which are the primary cultivated groups.

Branches on the tree corresponding to pure admixture groups are highlighted and labelled according to the nomenclature of Motamayor et al., (2008) [5]. A core collection of 90 individuals was selected using ShinyCore (version 1.0) [15] and complemented with 6 individuals with known phenotypic properties of interest. These 96 individuals are represented by blue circles on the tree

The PCA revealed a similar genetic structure to the ADMIXTURE plot and phylogenetic tree, also showing differentiation between the ancestral groups (Fig. 2). The Guiana group exhibited extreme divergence and tight clustering, indicating the presence of a high number of private alleles and low genetic distance between group members is low. This aligns with the low heterozygosity levels in this group, which is likely the result of genetic drift resulting from geographic isolation (Fig. 3) [7].

The Amelonado group also exhibited a low level of average heterozygosity, which may be attributed to generations of selective breeding and self-compatibility [47]. The only accession with a pure Criollo genetic profile had the lowest level of heterozygosity in the dataset, reflecting the low diversity previously reported in this group [7].

Although Motamayor et al. (2008) [5] identified 10 genetic groups, subsequent studies using a limited number of genetic markers have reported different findings. Unique genetic diversity has been identified in germplasm, particularly from Bolivia (Cacao Nacional Boliviano), northern Peru (Morona) and southern Colombia (Caqueta) [48, 49]. Additionally, one analysis indicated that the original Iquitos group described by Motamayor et al., (2008) [5] was split into two clusters distinguish in individuals sourced from Peru (Iquitos) and Brazil (Rio Salimoses) [50]. Clustering analysis conducted in another study revealed that accessions originally assigned to the Maranon group were split into those found near the Amazon River and the Rondonia region [7]. Our analyses support both the Iquitos and Maranon germplasm forming single genetic groups, although they showed the highest levels of heterozygosity compared to the other ancestral groups. Despite relying on a small number of genetic markers and clustering analysis, which alone may not accurately reflect population history [51], the germplasm reported in these studies may not be fully represented in the international cacao collections. This study only includes accessions which are accessible from international genebanks. It is also plausible that additional sources of cacao diversity remain undocumented, considering the substantial species richness within the Amazonian basin. Characterising and including additional sources of diversity in international collections constitutes an important aspect of future studies aimed at conserving cacao germplasm. In the context of climate change and deforestation, the identification and preservation of this diverse wild germplasm is essential for both conservation and development of novel accessions for cultivation.

Conformity of accessions

Sequencing of 10 groups of accessions with the same name confirmed conformity between different sources (Table S2). Consistent lack of conformity between plants with the same name but cultivated in different regions worldwide is an issue in plant breeding, primarily due to open pollination and a lack of sophisticated breeding programs [52]. Our plants show consistency between accessions maintained in CATIE; ICQC, R; Penn State; Nestle; and CIRAD, indicating that plants from these sources have coherent genetic profiles.

Additionally, we identified accessions from different collection groups which had a highly similar or identical genetic makeup. This included 2 pairs of accessions from the RB series (Rio Branco, collected in Brazil, 1965) [44, 53]. Previous reports detail mixing of names in this series, which may explain the highly similar genetic makeup of these accessions.

The accession MOQ_6_95, was collected in Ecuador, in 1943, as part of a collection series showing resistance to Witches’ Broom disease [44, 54]. CRUZ_7_14 originates from a series of open pollinated seedlings collected in Brazil, 1973 [44]. These two accessions share identical genetic makeups to PA_81 and PA_71, respectively, obtained in Peru (1938) [55], from a collection also selected for resistance to Witches’ Broom. Series of accessions, collected at different times but selected for the same phenotypic traits may have inadvertently resulted in accidental duplications of the same genotypes.

Additionally, 3 accessions from the UF series are highly similar to each other, and to RIM_34. The UF accessions were collected from Matina material (Amelonado) along the Atlantic coast and Ecuador in the 1930’s. The RIM series was collected from the Pacific coast, which contains some MATINA genes [44].

Pound_7, a specimen from the Pound series, was initially collected in Peru during the 1942–1943 period and subsequently made into in new collections in 1972 [56]. Notably, Pound_7 exhibits a close genetic relationship with TRD_32, a specimen belonging to a series of accessions collected from abandoned cocoa estates between 1991 and 1992 to conserve old material [57]. This collection process may have inadvertently resulted in the duplication of Pound_7.

The 3 SCA_6 accessions (One from ICQC, R and two from the public database) exhibited a highly similar genetic profile, indicating no divergence in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1). Clustering analysis further confirmed that all three samples originated from a pure Contamana genetic source. Additionally, the PA_107_PER from ICQC, R and from the public database (SRR14022521) showed a highly similar genetic makeup, predominantly derived from a Maranon source. Two SILECIA_5 accessions, sequenced from material held at ICQC, R and IC3 also showed a high degree of conformity. These findings confirm a high level of conformity among most accessions available on public databases and maintained in different locations. Consequently, these accessions can be used with confidence in breeding programs.

Of the 5 ICS_1 accessions in the database, the accession collected directly from ICQC, R showed a highly similar genetic makeup with two of the ICS_1 public accessions (SRR23557574, SRR23557575). These three accessions, along with ICS_5 showed minimal drift on the phylogenetic tree, indicating a highly conformed genetic makeup derived from Criollo and Amelonado ancestral sources (Fig. 1). However, two other accessions named as ICS_1 clustered separately, with genetic material from Contamana, Amelonado, and Criollo groups. The non-conformity of two ICS_1 accessions to a reference obtained from ICQC, R indicates some level of non-conformity in cacao collections across the world.

This potential non-conformity may be attributed to the widespread use of ICS_1 as a commonly used clone due to its well-known disease resistance properties. Consequently, accidental fertilization from other cacao accessions during propagation could have occurred. Nevertheless, the lack of conformity among these accessions raises concerns regarding the potential for unexpected outcomes when utilizing clones in breeding programs. Therefore, it highlights the paramount importance of maintaining a diverse collection of samples that have been tested for genetic conformity before using them for breeding, phenotyping, and selection purposes.

Selection of the core collection

A core collection of 90 accessions was selected using ShinyCore, representing 99.6% of the alleles in the dataset. The placement of the core collection across the PCA confirms that it has captured the genetic diversity of the whole dataset. The admixture profiles and phylogenetic placement of the core show a higher number of selected individuals with a wild ancestral profile than those originating from cultivated background (Criollo, Amelonado, and Nacional) (Figs. 1 and 2b). This suggests that a greater number of unique alleles segregate in germplasm from wild sources. Furthermore, additional diversity from wild germplasm, as it may become available, has the potential to be further integrated into breeding programs to develop new varieties for cultivation.

To complement the ShinyCore selection, additional accessions known to possess phenotypic traits of interest important to breeding programs were selected. The accession CCN_51 is a bulk cacao plant known for its high productivity and tolerance to a broad range of environmental conditions, and is widely used in South America [42]. Additionally, the ICS_1 clone exhibits high productivity and is commonly used in breeding programs [44]. The accession IMC_67 is commonly used as a rootstock, and has been associated with tolerance to water deficit [45].

Due to their documented resistance of Black Pod Rot and Witches’ Broom diseases, both POUND_7 and SCA_6 were also selected for inclusion in the core collection. The accessions CATIE_1000 and EET_272_ECU were additionally chosen because they exhibit tolerance to Black Pod Rot, as well as GU275E due to tolerance to Frosty Pod Rot (Table S3). Although the 90 accessions already selected represent the diversity present in the entire dataset, the inclusion of these additional accessions could be important for future studies using the core collection.

The final core collection comprises 96 accessions that encompasses 99.6% of the diversity present in the entire dataset. The pi-diversity of the core collection is 4.987e-05, compared to 4.668e-05 for the whole dataset. This higher level of diversity reflects the exclusion of genetically similar accessions in the core selection, thereby minimizing redundancy within the collection.

Conclusion

In this study, we have curated a core collection of 96 cacao accessions which represents 99.6% of the diversity from a dataset of 310 accessions present in International Collections. This core collection includes accessions with important disease and pest resistant properties. By maintaining live trees from each of these accessions at multiple locations, we have created an opportunity for conducting comparative phenotypic studies. This was previously hindered by the lack of defined experimental environments and consistent genetic characterization of accessions.

Initially, the collection will be cultivated and maintained at the CATIE (Costa Rica) research centre. Subsequently, its duplication across diverse geographic locations and environments will provide valuable insights into accessions adapted to various regions and climatic conditions.

The use of this core collection as the basis for systemic phenotyping will facilitate standardisation and comparison between studies. This standardization is crucial to avoid conflicting phenotypic information recorded for numerous cacao accessions. By identifying accessions resistant to key cacao diseases and pests, with high productivity, and exhibiting distinctive sensory profiles, we can facilitate the breeding of new, elite germplasm to ensure the continued stability and sustainability of cocoa production. Furthermore, this core collection will serve as a reference database for characterising the genetic makeup of new accessions with unknown genetic backgrounds. The whole-genome-sequence data and the SNP panel from this study can be utilised as a basis to identify novel sources of diversity not represented in International Collections and gene banks. Accessions representing these novel sources of diversity, may be incorporated into this core collection in the future to ensure that this resource remains comprehensive in capturing the biodiversity of the cacao population.
