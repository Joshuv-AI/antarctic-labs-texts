Should we optimise or revise the management of initially unresectable stage III non-small cell lung cancer: rethinking consolidation therapy
Authors: Hassan Abdelilah Tafenzi, Ismail Essadi, Rhizlane Belbaraka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the PACIFIC trial transforming unresectable stage III non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) management, survival gains remain modest and heterogeneous. Current consolidation strategies are fragmented, with redundant “me-too” designs, underuse of dynamic biomarkers, and little consensus on optimal sequencing of chemoradiotherapy (CRT), surgery, and next-generation systemic agents. We synthesise emerging evidence across immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), genotype-directed tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), bispecific antibodies, and advanced radiotherapy techniques, identifying critical gaps: lack of head-to-head CRT-first vs systemic-first comparisons, inconsistent central nervous system (CNS) management, unclear optimal integration of stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) boosts, and minimal integration of dynamic biomarkers such as circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) to guide escalation or de-escalation. We further examine the potential of early ADC integration to maximise initial tumour eradication, the role of salvage and planned surgery within trimodality strategies, and the need for harmonised CNS management. Finally, we address the economic sustainability of intensification, advocating value-based pricing, outcome-linked reimbursement, and biomarker-driven duration limits.Keywords: Unresectable, Stage III, Non-small cell lung cancer, Consolidation therapy

Introduction

Historically, management of Stage III non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) began with a dual-step assessment of resectability, integrating the anatomical accessibility of the primary tumour, the location and extent of nodal disease, patient fitness, and institutional practice within the multidisciplinary team (MDT) framework given, patients with single‐station nodal involvement underwent, either surgery followed by adjuvant platinum-based chemotherapy, or preoperative platinum-chemotherapy to downstage bulky or borderline resectable disease, while those with multi-station nodes or unresectable disease received sequential or concurrent platinum-based chemoradiotherapy (CRT) depending on patients’ medical fitness, also preferences.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Since immune-checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) first proved effective in advanced NSCLC,6, 7, 8 stage III disease has been redefined into resectable versus potentially resectable categories, now designated IIIA and IIIB in the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 7th and IIIC in the 8th editions, to tailor treatment approaches.9,10 Recently, the AJCC 9th edition of the TNM classification introduced an important refinement to stage III disease by subdividing ipsilateral mediastinal and/or subcarinal lymph node involvement into single‑station versus multi‑station categories,11 implying re-staging for many patients and altering the conceptual boundary of resectability beyond simple medical operability, with direct implications for prognosis, multidisciplinary treatment selection, and the design and interpretation of stage III clinical trials. Early phase I–II clinical trials in unresectable IIIB/C cohorts tested PD-(L)1 inhibitor as monotherapy or in combination with platinum-based chemotherapy, anti-VEGF agents, or CTLA-4 blockade, typically against chemotherapy ± platinum control arms. These studies confirmed that single-agent immune modulators are feasible and safe, and they provided the first signals of enhanced tumour control, ultimately sparking a debate over the optimal agent, combination strategy, and timing.

In the early era, treatment often proceeded without routine genotyping. In 2017, the PACIFIC trial established durvalumab consolidation after definitive CRT as a new standard, regardless the oncogene status,12 demonstrating a 5-year progression-free survival (PFS) of 33.1% versus 19% and a 5-year overall survival (OS) of 42.9% versus 33.4% compared with placebo.13 Although exploratory analyses suggested attenuated benefit in the small EGFR-mutant cohort, suggesting the need for EGFR-directed consolidation rather than formal exclusion. Timely, the advent of next-generation sequencing (NGS) has reshaped this paradigm, encouraging universal baseline NGS prior to CRT to identify actionable mutations, tailored for subsequent strategies. Reflecting this, American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO)’s 2024 rapid update now gives a category 1 recommendation for post-CRT osimertinib in EGFR-mutant unresectable stage III NSCLC.14 Attempts to intensify or reshuffle immunotherapy, such as PACIFIC-2’s15 induction and consolidation nivolumab ± ipilimumab,16 failed to improve outcomes and incurred higher toxicity, exposing the limits of unselected immunotherapy escalation. Outside the immunomodulators arena, osimertinib remains the only approved agent for EGFR and its common mutant-driven disease, on the basis of the LAURA trial,17 and no standard exists for ALK, ROS1, or KRAS tumours, though next-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) are under investigation. Renewed interest in planned trimodality strategies, including scheduled surgical salvage, alongside novel ADCs and bispecific antibodies (BsAbs), could offer fresh optimism. At this crossroads, should we optimise our existing schema or fundamentally redefine consolidation to achieve truly durable control?

Intensification: ICIs, TKIs and key unanswered questions

Immunotherapy

ICIs consolidation: durvalumab and beyond

Intensification strategies in unresectable stage III NSCLC have rapidly evolved well beyond the single-agent durvalumab consolidation established by PACIFIC (Table 1). Building on that foundation, investigators have tested earlier PD-(L)1 blockade, extended consolidation durations, dual-checkpoint combinations, and alternative PD-(L)1 antibodies across phase II and III trials, either to introduce durvalumab in the earlier setting plus concurrent CRT followed by durvalumab consolidation until disease progression (PACIFIC 2)15 which failed its primary PFS endpoint (13.8 vs 9.4 months; hazard ratio [HR], 0.85; P = 0.25) and showed no OS gain, with similar grade 3–5 toxicity but more early treatment discontinuations in the concurrent arm; platinum based concurrent or sequential CRT followed by durvalumab consolidation until disease progression (PACIFIC 5)18,19; platinum-based sequential CRT followed by durvalumab consolidation up to 24-months (PACIFIC 6)20; platinum-based concurrent CRT followed by durvalumab + domvanalimab, an Fc-silent anti-TIGIT antibody, for 12-months (PACIFIC 8)21; or durvalumab + oleclumab (CD73 inhibitor) or monalizumab (NKG2A inhibitor) post-concurrent CRT for 12-months (PACIFIC 9).22,23 Beyond durvalumab, other approaches were suggested pillars CRT-based followed by other PD-(L)1 inhibitor sugemalimab consolidation up to 24-months (GEMSTONE 301)24; or CRT + pembrolizumab followed by pembrolizumab consolidation for 14 cycles with the aim to evaluate the different survival benefit related with the chemo-combination with the CRT section rather than PD-1 consolidation (KEYNOTE-799)25,26; CRT ± atezolizumab followed by atezolizumab consolidation for 12-months (DETERRED)27; concurrent CRT + nivolumab followed by nivolumab consolidation for 12-months (NICOLAS)28; or concurrent CRT followed by nivolumab ± ipilimumab consolidation for 6-months (BTCRC lun-16-081)29; or benmelstobart (anti PD-L1) ± anlotinib, (multi-target antiangiogenic TKI) consolidation post-CRT (R-ALPS)30; or pembrolizumab + olaparib as consolidation post cCRT+ pembrolizumab (KEYLYNK-012).31 Despite encouraging signals, several intensification strategies have faltered, largely due to heterogeneous targeted patient populations, tumour bulk, comparator arms with active immunotherapy, or unacceptable toxicity.

Additional trials are actively enrolling patients to further refine optimal sequencing strategies, combination partners, and consolidation durations for unresectable stage III NSCLC, including CHECKMATE 73L, evaluating nivolumab with CRT followed by nivolumab ± ipilimumab32; KEYVIBE-006, assessing pembrolizumab/vibostolimab + CRT followed by pembrolizumab/vibostolimab consolidation for 17 cycles33; and EA5181, comparing CRT + durvalumab followed by durvalumab consolidation for 12 months.34

Retrospective and prospective cohorts hint that the ICI timing matters when added to concurrent CRT (cCRT),35, 36, 37 but definitive guidance is lacking. A multicentre retrospective analysis of 98 unresectable stage III NSCLC patients demonstrated that administering PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors concurrently with CRT vs sequentially post-CRT improved median PFS (19 vs. 12.8 months; P = 0.047) and trended toward better OS, without markedly increasing high-grade pneumonitis or hematologic toxicity.38 Conversely, a prospective observational study of 39 stage III patients found only a numerical, non-significant PFS benefit at 6 and 12 months for concurrent PD-1 blockade (82%/44% vs 57%/57%), accompanied by a modest rise in grade 2–3 pneumonitis (18.2% vs 13.6%; P = 0.55).39 What remains unresolved is which patient subgroups truly derive additive synergy, especially given heterogeneity in tumour burden, PD-L1 status, or immune-milieu, and baseline pulmonary function, and how to balance marginal gains in disease control against the incremental risk of toxicity.

Immunotherapy biomarkers

Biomarker-driven patient selection, PD-L1 expression, remains the only exploratory biomarker signal in unresectable stage III NSCLC consolidation, correlating with benefit from durvalumab in PACIFIC. Although higher PD-L1 expression levels generally correlate with improved response to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, yet responses in PD-L1-negative tumours highlight its imperfect negative predictive value40; consequently, PD-L1 alone is not exclusionary.41

Tumour mutational burden (TMB) has emerged as a complementary marker,42,43 shown promise in both tissue and blood assays,44 with cutoffs around 10–16 mutations (mt)/megabase (Mb), and was incorporated prospectively in the metastatic setting only in trials such as MYSTIC (durvalumab ± tremelimumab vs chemotherapy),45 in the first-line BFAST platform (atezolizumab),46 and in the stage III with CRT + durvalumab cohorts, patients with TMB ≥ 10 mt/Mb experienced markedly lower 24-month locoregional failure (9% vs 51%) and superior PFS (66% vs 27%; HR, 0.45; P = 0.003).47 Retrospective analyses of POPLAR and OAK likewise showed that higher blood-based TMB (bTMB) correlates with enhanced ICI benefit, although neither trial used TMB prospectively for stratification.48 bTMB’s dependence on tumour burden led to the development of ctDNA-adjusted bTMB metrics, which decouple mutational load from ctDNA input mass and demonstrate an improved predictive ability for OS in advanced NSCLC (P = 0.019).49 Early shifts in ctDNA, whether identified via tumour-informed multiplex panels50 or genome-wide copy-number profiling,51 can unmask radiographically occult, high-risk residual disease. Emerging data positions on-treatment ctDNA clearance as a real-time ‘stop’ signal for consolidation ICI. In a meta-analysis of 32 NSCLC cohorts (n = 3047) across targeted therapy and ICI trials, achieving ctDNA clearance or ≥ 50% allele-fraction reduction within 6–8 weeks conferred a 68% lower risk of progression (PFS: HR, 0.32 [95% CI: 0.26, 0.40]) and a 69% lower risk of death (OS: HR, 0.31 [95% CI: 0.23, 0.42]).52 In unresectable stage III NSCLC post-CRT, ctDNA clearance after a single durvalumab infusion predicted 24-month PFS of about 74% versus 0% in non-clearers, suggesting early molecular responders might safely abbreviate or halt consolidative ICI.53 Conversely, the DART study concluded an inferior outcome with the detection of ctDNA during consolidative durvalumab after CRT using a novel tumour-agnostic minimal residual disease (MRD) assay.54 Meanwhile, complementary analyses in advanced settings showed that a ≥ 50% ctDNA drop after two ICI cycles associated with improved PFS (10 vs 2 months; HR, 0.55) and OS (18.4 vs 5.9 months; HR, 0.44), independent of PD-L1 status or histology.55

These intensification efforts have taught us that more isn’t always better: benefit plateaus quickly when toxicity and cost climb. The enduring takeaway is that personalisation, anchored in dynamic biomarkers and smart sequencing, must guide the next wave of consolidation strategies. Before exploring novel consolidation strategies, three pivotal questions remain unresolved in the post-CRT immunotherapy landscape: when, relative to CRT, should ICIs be administered for maximal synergy and minimal toxicity; which static biomarkers (PD-L1, TMB, or emerging genomic signatures) most reliably identify durable responders; and how can real-time molecular monitoring, particularly early shifts in ctDNA, be leveraged to escalate for molecular non-clearers and de-escalate for rapid clearers?

Grounded in MRD-guided paradigms, where some series report strikingly better outcomes among early ctDNA clearers while other cohorts (e.g., the DART trial) have observed worse outcomes when MRD is detectable during consolidation,54,56, 57, 58 and supported by tumour-informed assay validation data that demonstrate prognostic and lead-time advantages, early ctDNA dynamics appear to precede radiographic relapse and strongly predict recurrence risk in the early setting59, 60, 61 and, increasingly, after progression on chemoimmunotherapy.62 Prior to consolidation, serial ctDNA assessment has demonstrated that early MRD clearance measured from a pre-CRT baseline strongly predicts favourable outcomes, raising the hypothesis that patients who become MRD-negative shortly after CRT might not require routine ICI consolidation.63 Mid-treatment ctDNA kinetics, when combined with baseline radiomic features, have also emerged as a useful interim timepoint to identify patients at high risk of progression who may benefit from response-adaptive intensification.64 Pragmatically, however, routine mid-CRT ctDNA testing increases cost and logistical complexity while offering limited incremental value for most patients, as a mid-treatment sample is unlikely to demonstrate a dramatic MRD decline that would confidently justify de-escalation or omission of scheduled therapy, and transient changes could complicate interpretation by reflecting treatment-related fluctuations or early pseudo-progression rather than durable response. From another perspective, outside protocolised clinical trials, where predefined ctDNA decision rules can trigger adaptive interventions and generate high-quality evidence, mid-CRT ctDNA sampling would be most valuable in selected circumstances, in patients who are unfit for full-dose CRT, those receiving dose-reduced or abbreviated CRT regimens, or patients with an altered Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS), where interim molecular information can meaningfully guide subsequent sequencing or (de)escalation of therapy, without imposing unnecessary cost or logistical burden on the broader population. A reasonable compromise is to reserve mid-treatment sampling for patients who are MRD-positive at baseline or for trial contexts and to rely on post-CRT MRD assessment to guide consolidation decisions for those who remain persistently positive.

These observations motivate a pragmatic, hypothesis-driven approach to operationalising ctDNA for consolidation decisions while preserving clinical equipoise: tumour-informed platforms are preferred for their superior analytic sensitivity and specificity, with tumour-agnostic assays reserved as an alternative when tissue is an issue and their limitations explicitly acknowledged.65 A harmonised sampling cadence, a baseline before CRT, a secondary baseline at the end of CRT, early checkpoints at week-4 and week-8, and then surveillance every three months for the first year offer a practical framework for serial assessment. Because no single numeric cutoff has been universally validated to separate “clearers” from “non-clearers”, response categories should be framed relative to assay performance: clearance defined as undetectable ctDNA at the assay limit of detection (LOD) and meaningful decline operationalised as a substantial relative reduction (e.g., ≥ 50% fall in variant allele frequency [VAF] from the prespecified baseline), with the caveat that exact thresholds and timing remain hypothesis-generating and require formal harmonisation.

Importantly, several limitations must temper MRD-guided action. First, early MRD persistence can reflect incomplete genotyping with missed actionable drivers or true biologic resistance to ICI, whereas some immune-modulated tumours may only show ctDNA decline after ICI consolidation. In such a case, practical options include expedited enrolment into considering other platform trials (i.e., ADCs, bispecific antibodies, or rational combination regimens), focal local control for positron emission tomography (PET)-avid residual disease (stereotactic body radiotherapy [SBRT] or salvage resection), or initiation/switch to a genotype-directed TKI when an actionable driver is identified. Second, small, transient VAF fluctuations are common66,67 so borderline or low-level positives should be confirmed with repeat sampling (typically within 2–4 weeks) and interpreted alongside imaging, clinical status, and genotyping completeness. All MRD-triggered escalations should proceed through rapid multidisciplinary review and, whenever possible, be embedded within prospective protocols that include predefined confirmatory and safety frameworks. Third, plasma ctDNA has limited sensitivity for isolated intracranial disease,68, 69, 70 so molecular negativity must never replace genotype-tailored central nervous system (CNS) imaging and clinical vigilance.

As we pivot to targeted therapies, the lessons from immunotherapy intensification will inform multi-modal, biomarker-driven algorithms designed to maximise efficacy, minimise harm, and deliver truly tailored care.

Targeted therapy

Genotype-guided consolidation

As the immunotherapy era delivers adaptive, biomarker-driven consolidation guided by ctDNA clearance and PD-(L)1 status, parallel genotype-guided approaches promise to tailor maintenance to oncogenic drivers. In unresectable stage III NSCLC, several phase II/III trials now test driver-matched inhibitors after CRT across molecular subsets.

In EGFR-mutant NSCLC, the phase III LAURA trial established osimertinib 80 mg daily after CRT as the first targeted consolidative therapy in EGFR-mutant disease.17 Osimertinib prolonged median PFS from 5.6 to 39.1 months (HR, 0.16; P < 0.001) and delivered unprecedented CNS control; median intracranial PFS was not reached versus 14.9 months (HR, 0.17), with grade ≥ 3 pneumonitis in only 2.6% and dose reductions in 48%.17 Similarly, the primary analysis of PLATINUM trial evaluating lazertinib consolidation post cCRT until progression resulted in a meaningful PFS advantage, with no new safety signals by 12.7 months of follow-up.71 By contrast, the phase II ASCENT trial explored an 8-week afatinib lead-in before CRT followed by 24 months of afatinib consolidation. Induction objective response rate (ORR) reached 63%, and 2-year PFS exceeded historical benchmarks, but a high rate of CNS-only recurrences and rapid progression after TKI discontinuation underscored the need to refine both sequencing and CNS-directed strategies.72

Outside the canonical EGFR tyrosine-kinase domain lie heterogeneous EGFR missense and extracellular-domain (ECD) variants whose functional significance and drug sensitivities are incompletely defined.73,74 Large-scale, “mixed-mutant” profiling efforts have sought to map the full EGFR spectrum and identified multiple ECD substitutions that are functionally active and,73,75,76 importantly, display in vitro sensitivity to second-generation irreversible inhibitors such as dacomitinib and afatinib,77 yet no universally accepted standard of care has emerged for ECD and other uncommon EGFR variants.78 Clinical series of newer EGFR TKIs corroborate these preclinical signals showing that aumolertinib demonstrated meaningful activity in cohorts of patients with uncommon EGFR mutations (including G719X, L861Q, and S768I) with ORRs and disease control in early reports,79 and retrospective/early prospective analyses have reported substantial response rates with dacomitinib in selected uncommon-mutation populations.80

A dedicated ALK-fusion consolidation studies with next-generation inhibitors are just opening enrolment, raising questions about optimal timing, duration and CNS prophylaxis in each subtype.

KRAS G12C-mutant NSCLC studies have largely left CRT behind, focusing on stage IIIB/C or metastatic cohorts. CodeBreaK 101 evaluated a sotorasib “sandwich” with carboplatin/pemetrexed, no prior CRT, with first-line ORR 65% (median PFS 10.8 months) and second-line ORR 42% (PFS 8.3 months) at the cost of grade 3–4 neutropenia (29%) and thrombocytopenia (14%).81 Building on this, CodeBreaK 202 randomises front-line, PD-L1-negative, stage IIIB/C KRAS G12C patients to sotorasib plus platinum doublet versus pembrolizumab plus platinum doublet, asking whether targeted KRAS inhibition can supplant immunotherapy in this subset.82 Key unknowns include how to re-integrate CRT safely around potent KRAS inhibitors and whether upfront KRAS blockade alters subsequent immune or TKI salvage options.

MET Exon 14-Skipping NSCLC VISION validated as an advanced/metastatic targetable alteration: Tepotinib achieved ORRs of about 57% in treatment-naïve and 52% in pretreated patients, durable responses beyond 9 months, and a largely grade 1–2 safety profile (peripheral edema, about 40%; nausea, about 29%; diarrhea, about 23%).83,84

RET Fusion-Positive NSCLC LIBRETTO-431 (NCT04194944) is the first randomised trial in RET-fusion NSCLC, pitting selpercatinib against platinum/pemetrexed ± pembrolizumab. At interim, selpercatinib nearly doubled median PFS to 24.8 versus 11.2 months (HR, 0.46; P < 0.001),85 cut the risk of CNS progression by 72% (cause-specific HR, 0.28), and mirrored LIBRETTO-001’s tolerability.86,87 Remaining questions include whether adding anti-PD-(L)1 therapy to chemotherapy adds value in the control arm, how best to manage CNS micro-metastases, and which RET inhibitor will ultimately set the standard?

Regarding other oncogenic drivers, BRAF, KRAS, NTRK, non-sensitive EGFR mutations, and HER2 exon-20 insertions, no prospective data currently support targeted‐TKI consolidation or induction with definitive CRT. The strategy remains under evaluation for ALK and ROS1 in the ongoing phase I-III HORIZON-01 trial post CRT,88 within umbrella-design neoadjuvant platforms,89 or in locally advanced cases based on ctDNA or tissue biomarkers without mandating CRT (B-FAST/NCT03178552). In ALK-positive disease, a single retrospective analysis comparing ALK-TKI versus durvalumab versus observation consolidation post-CRT suggested a survival benefit with TKI lacking specific inhibitor generation, leaving the clinical applicability of its findings unclear.90

These mixed-modality gaps prompted a head-to-head question: what is the true added value of CRT in rare, oncogene-addicted stage III NSCLC? This is being addressed in NCT06858410, the only randomised Phase III trial comparing lorlatinib monotherapy against CRT in ALK-positive patients, aiming to clarify whether potent TKIs can supplant or should complement, standard multimodality care.

In light of the phase III LAURA trial’s unprecedented PFS and CNS benefits, we must fundamentally rethink how, when, and whether to weave driver-matched TKIs into curative‐intent CRT for stage III NSCLC. Outside of EGFR-mutant cohorts, most genotype-driven studies still relegate TKIs to post-ICI/chemotherapy failure, treating unresectable stage III disease like advanced metastatic and arbitrarily splitting patients into operable (IIIA) versus inoperable (IIIB/C) groups.

From this standpoint, focused translational studies and exploratory analyses of trials in rare driver cohorts showed that many canonical driver-positive tumours (EGFR, ALK, RET, MET, HER2, BRAF) are biologically distinct from smoking-related cancers, exhibiting lower tumour mutational burden, fewer clonal neoantigens, and less inflamed microenvironments, features that plausibly underlie the reduced and inconsistent activity of ICIs in these subgroups, highlighting the need for driver-specific treatment logic when choosing induction or consolidation strategies.91, 92, 93 Importantly, except for EGFR-driven tumours, platinum-based chemotherapy generally retains clinically meaningful activity, while KRAS-mutant NSCLC often demonstrates a different biology and greater sensitivity to chemo-immunotherapy compared to other drivers.94, 95, 96, 97 Consequently, pragmatic trial designs and clinical practice have emphasised sequencing or combining chemotherapy with targeted agents, or using targeted induction/maintenance around local therapy, rather than assuming that results from unselected chemo-immunotherapy trials apply to all oncogene-driven subgroups; instead, treatment selection must be biologically informed and individualised, and perioperative or consolidation ICI strategies should be prospectively evaluated in prespecified driver-positive cohorts with integrated translational endpoints, rather than extrapolated by analogy from unselected populations.

Such legacy designs leave us asking: since immunotherapy efficacy depends on tumor-intrinsic immune features that many oncogene-driven cancers lack,98 should TKIs be given concurrently with CRT, sequenced immediately afterward, or even replace or waive CRT entirely in select molecular subsets? Can potent TKIs be combined safely with thoracic radiation without amplifying esophagitis, pneumonitis, or cardiac risk? What consolidation duration maximises disease control while minimising toxicity, cost, and resistance? And which early biomarkers, beyond ctDNA clearance and PD-(L)1 status, will predict durable responders vs those likely to develop resistance?

Embedding genotype-matched TKIs into the consolidation setting inaugurates a new era of precision therapy, yet even the most potent inhibitors require early, accurate predictors of response, robust CNS-directed strategies, and adaptive frameworks to monitor and overcome resistance.

TKIs biomarkers

Dynamic biomarkers, chiefly ctDNA, are proving indispensable, echoing their transformative impact in immunotherapy.99 Although most evidence arises from retrospective studies with small patient cohorts, using heterogeneous sensitivity and specificity assays, a consistent paradigm shift has emerged. In EGFR-mutant cohorts, undetectable ctDNA by weeks 3–8 from baseline correlates with dramatic PFS and OS gains, whereas persistent ctDNA heralds relapse months before radiologic progression.100, 101, 102, 103, 104 Data from the LAURA MRD analysis further reinforce the clinical utility of tumour-informed MRD testing, showing that baseline MRD was detectable in 57% of evaluable patients, osimertinib cleared MRD in 84% of baseline-positive patients versus 0% with placebo, and among baseline-positive patients median PFS was 38.8 versus 5.8 months for osimertinib versus placebo; molecular progression preceded radiologic progression in 79% of cases with a median lead time of 5.1 months, and the MRD assay demonstrated 63% clinical sensitivity and 86% specificity for detecting PFS events.105 Nevertheless, in the SWOG S1403 subgroup analysis, detectable EGFR-mutant ctDNA at 8 weeks predicted poor outcomes, yet RECIST-defined partial responses remained strong predictors when adjusted for mutation allele fraction and copy-number variations.100 Similarly, the AURA 3 trial concluded that week 3 ctDNA clearance correlated with longer PFS but was not superior to radiologic response in predicting OS after adjusting for EGFR mutation subtype and performance status.101 Parallel kinetics of ctDNA have been documented in ALK- and ROS1-rearranged NSCLC, mirroring the early treatment dynamics seen with EGFR TKIs.106, 107, 108 Most of these studies, however, relied on upfront TKI without accounting for CRT and its effect on ctDNA kinetics. While most of the finding support the superiority of ctDNA in detecting progression compared to RECIST v1.1, this advantage could be argued by the timing of plasma sample, the choice of assays (NGS vs. droplet digital PCR [ddPCR]), tracked mutations, treatment combination used,63 in addition to other pathological feature that, some of them, were only evaluated in the early setting following surgery that include histological type,109,110 circulation blood biomarkers, low-volume disease (e.g., isolated pulmonary lesions) or absence of visceral metastases may yield undetectable ctDNA despite aggressive tumour biology, organ function, and late stage where the predictive accuracy may be diminished for heavily pre-treated patients. Additional real-world constraints were raised in tissue adequacy, reimbursement, and lab infrastructure.

Beyond ctDNA, a constellation of emerging biomarkers, circulating tumour cells (CTCs),111 exosomal microRNAs,112 tumour-specific methylation signatures,113 and integrated radiomic-ctDNA algorithms,114 can dissect subclonal evolution and anticipate TKI sensitivity or resistance. Yet in stage III NSCLC patients receiving consolidative TKIs, validated real-time monitoring tools beyond conventional imaging are virtually non-existent.

Despite compelling retrospective evidence showing that early ctDNA clearance correlates with marked PFS and OS improvements, several critical questions remain unresolved in the consolidation setting for stage III oncogene-addicted NSCLC. We must establish precise ctDNA variant-allele-fraction cutoffs and optimal sampling timepoints that would justify de-escalation in molecular “rapid clearers” or prompt intensification in early non-responders. For instance, can we safely truncate osimertinib consolidation in LAURA trial participants whose ctDNA clears by week 4 without compromising the exceptional CNS control observed? Serial ctDNA monitoring could also pinpoint the optimal moment to switch from afatinib to osimertinib, or to MET/RET inhibitors, ahead of radiographic progression. Achieving reproducible, multicentre results will require harmonised assays and workflows, balancing the rapid turnaround and sensitivity of ddPCR against the broader coverage of hybrid-capture NGS, and standardising pre-analytical protocols and bioinformatics workflows. Moreover, combining ctDNA dynamics with complementary CTC counts, exosomal microRNA profiles, tumour-specific methylation signatures, and quantitative radiomics promises should further refine driver-specific prediction. Ultimately, prospective, biomarker-adaptive trials that randomise consolidation strategies according to early molecular response are needed to validate de-escalation for rapid clearers and escalation for non-clearers and to move unresectable stage III NSCLC toward an adaptive, precision-guided treatment paradigm.

Integration of radiotherapy with targeted agents, whether delivered concurrently with TKIs, followed by TKI consolidation after CRT, or preceded by induction TKI, represents a substantive shift in strategy informed by ICI lessons. The PD-(L)1 experience showed that blanket intensification can reach a point of diminishing returns, with escalating toxicity, cost, and CNS failure offsetting incremental benefit. Consequently, the field should move away from one-size-fits-all escalation and toward individualised approaches, in which the management should depend on dynamic, biomarker-driven, and genotype-specific strategies that tailor radiotherapy-targeted therapy combinations to the biology and risk profile of each patient. By combining driver-matched TKIs with real-time molecular monitoring and biomarker-adaptive trial frameworks, we can tailor consolidation, escalating, de-escalating, or sequencing therapies based on early molecular response, redefining curative intent in locally advanced disease.

Early-phase studies in unresectable stage III NSCLC

Early‐phase trials are charting how next‐generation systemic agents can be seamlessly woven into radiotherapy for unresectable stage III NSCLC.

Novel checkpoint modulators are being tested with hypo-fractionated thoracic radiotherapy and PD-(L)1 blockade to amplify abscopal immunity. COAST Run-In and Domva-TIGIT FHD combine durvalumab with CD73 or TIGIT antagonists post-CRT; durvalumab plus PARP (olaparib) or ATR (ceralasertib) inhibitors to probe DNA damage-enhanced radio-sensitisation as consolidation post-RT (CONCORD).115

Meanwhile, an innovative umbrella trial matches patient by NGS‐defined drivers, assigning sunvozertinib for EGFR exon 20 insertions, crizotinib for ROS1 fusions, pralsetinib for RET, larotrectinib for NTRK, savolitinib for MET exon 14 skipping, pyrotinib for HER2 alterations, dabrafenib + trametinib for BRAF V600E, glecirasib for KRAS G12C, and ensartinib for ALK, to evaluate a molecularly tailored neoadjuvant strategy.

Beyond, immune-backbones and genotype-driven platforms several early-phase trials are now exploring truly novel forms-based CRT in unresectable stage III NSCLC. The CONVERGE trial (NCT06667908) is testing intra-tumoral and intranodal injections of a new durvalumab formulation (JNJ-90301900) alongside cCRT, followed by up to 12 months of standard intravenous durvalumab consolidation. Similarly, AN0025 (antagonist of PGE2-receptor E-type 4) is being given with CRT followed by durvalumab consolidation (NCT05358691) to probe whether blocking PGE₂-mediated immunosuppression enhances post-radiation immune clearance. Building on promising neoadjuvant results,116 the bifunctional fusion protein SHR-1701 (PD-L1 linked to the TGF-βR2 ectodomain) has moved into a post-CRT consolidation trial (NCT05177497). The CHORUS study (NCT04905316) revisits the addition of Canakinumab (anti-interleukin-1β monoclonal antibody used to treat various inflammation type117) to CRT followed by durvalumab + Canakinumab consolidation for 12 months, despite earlier negative results in the adjuvant118 and second line with docetaxel.119 To overcome PD-L1 resistance in the consolidation window, copanlisib (a PI3K inhibitor) is combined with durvalumab in NCT04895579, aiming to prolong time to progression.120 Finally, a novel approach uses papaverine, a mitochondrial metabolism inhibitor, alongside CRT (NCT05136846).

It ought to be noted that, due to inconsistency in terms of chosen words to describe the trial evaluating chemotherapy-based nab-paclitaxel-cisplatin + tislelizumab ± bevacizumab + hypo-cCRT followed by tislelizumab consolidation for 12 months (GASTO-1086 / NCT05468242), we did not understand if it is for resectable or unresectable disease; therefore, we subsequently excluded it.121 Additional trials with unknown status or withdrawn were omitted, including sintilimab (NCT03884192), icotinib (NCT03396185), nimotuzumab (NCT02549261), INCMGA00012 in combination with chemoradiation (NCT04203511), anlotinib (NCT04111913), and veliparib (NCT02412371).

Gaps, redundancies and missed learning opportunities

Despite the impressive surge of early‐phase trials in unresectable stage III NSCLC, several thematic gaps and redundancies risk blunting the translational impact. Multiple studies revisit PD-(L)1 blockade, in monotherapy, paired with immune co-inhibitors (e.g., TIGIT, CD73) or DNA-damage agents (PARP, ATR) without systematically integrating lessons from earlier negative or equivocal results. This “me-too” approach fragments an already limited patient pool into underpowered parallel arms, leaving unanswered which pathway or scheduling truly delivers clinical benefit.

Take, for example, intra-tumoral/intranodal delivery of durvalumab (JNJ-90301900) alongside CRT in the CONVERGE trial (NCT06667908). Conceptually, local administration could intensify immune priming at tumour sites, but in practice it confronts logistical and safety hurdles from heterogeneous drug distribution, difficult access to central or small lesions, proximity to major airways and vessels, and inability to address systemic micro-metastases. Unless this trial incorporates rigorous pharmacokinetic (PK)/pharmacodynamic (PD) assessments (tumour and nodal drug levels, in situ immune activation), standardised image-guided delivery metrics, and prespecified benchmarks demonstrating advantage over standard intravenous durvalumab consolidation, they risk adding complexity without clear benefit. Similarly, revisiting canakinumab consolidation after negative adjuvant118 and second-line119 experiences exemplifies how repeating a failed strategy, absent biomarker refinement, can squander effort.

Beyond therapeutic overlap, the field underutilises comprehensive biomarker feedback loops. Few trial protocols prespecify translational endpoints, such as dynamic T cell receptor clonality, ctDNA clearance, or spatial immune profiling, to explain why an arm succeeded or failed. Without standardised correlative protocols and shared data repositories, each study generates siloed data, hampering cross‐trial meta‐learning and delaying identification of predictive signatures.

The proliferating trial landscape has yielded two core mis-steps. First, inconsistent radiotherapy backbones obscure the interpretation of systemic-radiation synergy, since differing fractionation, total dose, and target volumes can independently influence both efficacy and toxicity. Second, most studies treat stage III as a monolith, neglecting heterogeneity in tumour burden, nodal involvement and sometimes genomic signature. Rarely do studies stratify or enrich for high-risk subgroups (e.g., bulky N2 disease, MRD detected via ctDNA), thus missing a chance to personalise radiation fields, intensify systemic sequences, or adapt consolidation duration. Moreover, the neoadjuvant umbrella trials lack adaptive designs that could drop ineffective arms early and reallocate resources to promising cohorts. This rigidity delays “go/no‐go” decisions and risks repeating negative signals long after they become apparent.

From this standpoints, and to avoid redundant, underpowered “me-too”, mislead outcomes from the ICIs based trials, time and resources consuming without yielding actionable lessons, particularly where genotype-guided therapies risk being misclassified by maladaptive designs, trials should be generated within broad, adaptive master protocols spanning multiple tumour sites, combining umbrella and basket elements to stratify patients by clinically actionable biomarkers and early MRD status, employ shared contemporaneous control arms to reduce sample size and limit exposure to ineffective therapies, and incorporate preplanned MRD-triggered randomisation nodes to evaluate (de-)escalation strategies, while embedding co-primary objectives that include both clinical endpoints (PFS/OS) with MRD conversion as an early intermediate endpoint, and prespecifying Bayesian or group-sequential stopping rules for futility efficacy, and safety.

In this regard, coupled with embedded health-economic endpoints to ensure scalability and payer relevance, patient allocation must be optimised by combining biomarker-first screening with response-adaptive randomisation: patients are funnelled into biomarker-defined cohorts, and allocation probabilities are updated at interim analyses to favour promising arms while preserving equipoise, and predefined decision nodes translate biomarkers into actionable pathways (for example, persistent ctDNA at week-8 triggers randomisation to intensified therapy versus current standard or placebo, with confirmatory sampling and mandatory multidisciplinary review). At this point, feasibility is supported by precedent in oncology master protocols such as Lung-MAP.122, 123, 124

In sum, the field is generating volumes of safety and feasibility data but is underleveraging real‐time analytics to accelerate identification of the most potent combinations. By concentrating resources on biologically plausible hypotheses within suggested adaptive platforms, enabling rapid graduation or closure of arms, varying radiotherapy timing from induction through concurrent CRT to SBRT consolidation or surgery, and adding new agents, will more precisely pinpoint safety thresholds, reveal immune correlates of benefit and establish optimal sequencing and combinations, laying the groundwork for ADCs, bispecific platforms and new therapeutic options to achieve deeper, more durable remissions.

The evolving landscape of stage III NSCLC treatment

Despite the substantial progress achieved in NSCLC treatment, particularly with the widespread adoption of PD-(L)1 inhibitor, long-term outcomes for many patients remain suboptimal. A critical unmet need persists due to the pervasive challenge of therapy resistance, which invariably leads to disease progression. This resistance can be primary, where tumours do not respond to initial treatment, or acquired, where they develop mechanisms to evade therapy over time.

Compounding this challenge, a significant proportion of oncogenic driver mutations identified in NSCLC still lack effective targeted therapeutic agents. This gap leads to a continuous imperative for the development of novel therapeutic strategies, with a focus on more precise and multi-pronged approaches, chiefly ADCs, bispecific antibodies and novel immune modulators. Nowadays, these innovative agents are developed or under-development, tested beyond progression, as first-line in the metastatic setting for the purpose of enhancing tumour-killing efficiency, overcoming, therefore, resistance to primary drugs and bolstering the endogenous anti-tumour immune response.

ADC

Could we implement these new agents as a new standard for stage III NSCLC? What challenges do we face? First, nearly all ongoing ADC studies have enrolled patients after they progressed on immunotherapy, targeted agents, or chemotherapy, with the primary goal of overcoming acquired resistance in both wild-type and oncogene-driven tumours, predominantly in locally advanced or metastatic settings. Extrapolating efficacy and safety from these later-line populations to treatment-naïve stage III disease will require dedicated trials that integrate ADCs into the upfront paradigm rather than reserve them for salvage. Yet bringing ADCs into first-line management offers clear advantages. TROP2,125,126 the most ubiquitously expressed antigen in lung cancer,127,128 can be targeted without pre-selection by tissue or plasma biomarker testing, simplifying patient identification and expanding applicability across histologies. Delivered as monotherapy, as induction before CRT, as consolidation after standard CRT, or in combination with reduced-volume fields, TROP-2-directed ADCs could exploit immunogenic cell death and reshape locoregional control. Early proof-of-concept already exists. For instance, in the TROPION-Lung10, evaluating Dato-DXd in combination with rilvegostomig (a PD-1/TIGIT bispecific antibody) or rilvegostomig monotherapy, compared to pembrolizumab monotherapy, included untreated patients with PD-L1 ≥ 50% and no actionable genomic alterations.129 The NeoCOAST-2, a phase II neoadjuvant study in resectable stage IIA-IIIB NSCLC regardless of PD-L1 status, reported that arms containing Dato-DXd achieved a promising pathological complete-response (pCR) rate (35.2%) and major pathological-response (mPR) rate (63.0%) among multiple investigational regimens.130 Additional trials based TROP2 or HER2/3 ADCs are evaluating their efficacy in the upfront line as monotherapy or in combination with TKI or ICI,131, 132, 133 or following progression post-TKIs/PD-(L)1 or subsequent lines with or without actionable genomic alterations,134, 135, 136, 137 which, by the way, one of them did improve only PFS2 in non-squamous histology.138

Another key concern is the toxicity profile of ADCs. Clinical trials have consistently shown that DXd-based payloads carry a significant risk of interstitial lung disease (ILD) and pneumonitis, forcing early discontinuation despite otherwise manageable gastrointestinal and hematologic side effects.139 In the absence of mature data to guide rechallenge or continuation in the face of lingering toxicity, implementing ADCs up-front will demand rigorous patient selection, proactive sequencing, and rapid identification plus treatment of pulmonary complications. Looking ahead, using ADCs as induction or consolidation, alone or combined with TKIs or ICIs, could allow smaller radiation volumes and thus lower the overall risk of lung injury.

Treatment duration remains another unresolved issue. In advanced unresectable settings, ADCs are typically continued until disease progression, whereas adjuvant trials in earlier stages commonly limit treatment at four cycles. Such fixed-duration approach may partly explain high discontinuation rates, but it is unclear whether tolerability would improve when ADCs are combined with radiotherapy. Equally important are questions of sequencing, whether concurrent or sequential administration best balances drug exposure and safety, and whether on-treatment de-escalation guided by serial ctDNA monitoring could maintain efficacy while reducing toxicity, patient burden, and cost. Prospective de-escalation trials are essential to establish an evidence-based maintenance window.

Finally, the ultimate question is whether ADCs can truly cure stage III NSCLC, either as monotherapy, as part of surgery-intent strategy, or in combination with radiotherapy. Which radiation strategy (standard concurrent CRT, an SBRT boost, or reduced-volume fields) most effectively amplifies ADC-induced immunogenic cell death? And are the encouraging PFS improvements simply a function of lead-time bias, or will they translate into durable OS gains? To date, ADCs have produced clinically meaningful PFS benefits and lower intracranial relapse rates, but only with mature OS data and prolonged follow-up we will understand their definitive impact.

As the next generation of ADCs enters clinical practice, how should we choose the optimal up-front strategy, sequencing and combination? If ADCs become first-line agents, how do we prevent or overcome resistance? Can patients still derive benefit from TKIs and ICIs, and should these be sequenced or combined? Tackling drug-tolerant persister cells, the quiescent subpopulation that survives TKI induction and evades cytotoxic agents, may be central to durable control. Unlike TKIs, which rarely achieve pCR even with intensified chemotherapy, ADCs promise deeper tumour eradication; careful assessment of pCR rates and molecular profiling of residual disease will be essential to determine whether these agents can break the cycle of relapse.

In summary, adoption of ADCs as a standard for stage III disease will depend on prospective trials that integrate these agents with radiotherapy in treatment-naïve patients, define optimal sequencing or combination with radiotherapy, while discussing the role of TKIs and ICIs, and unravel and counteract mechanisms of ADC resistance, including the role of drug-tolerant persister cells. Only through such focused efforts we can determine whether ADCs will shift initially unresectable stage III NSCLC from a chronically managed disease to one that is potentially curable.

Bispecific antibodies

In the evolving landscape beyond ICI and TKI consolidations, bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) have emerged as a novel therapeutic class designed to overcome resistance after progression on prior regimens.140 Rather than revisiting comprehensive catalogues of BsAbs in NSCLC as in previous reviews,141, 142, 143 this section focuses on their potential added value when integrated with definitive CRT versus monotherapy and on the feasibility of such approaches in real-world practice.

The general principle of BsAbs relies on amplifying and sustaining anti-tumour T-cell activity within the post-radiation immunosuppressive milieu. In the early stage, MEDI5752, a PD-1 × CTLA-4 bispecific, is under evaluation in the NeoCOAST-2 neoadjuvant trial, where early safety signals and median PR (mPR) rates suggest meaningful immunologic priming before adjuvant consolidation.144 Acasunlimab (BNT311/GEN1046), a PD-L1 × 4-1BB construct, conditionally engages 4-1BB co-stimulation only within PD-L1-positive niches. Early-phase data showed a clinically meaningful survival advantage across solid tumours145 and refractory patients in combination with pembrolizumab.146

What is the rationale for CRT integration? The efficacy of combining chemotherapy with BsAbs has already been demonstrated in advanced NSCLC.147 CRT itself elicits robust immunogenic cell death, increasing neoantigen release and MHC-I expression, but also triggers compensatory immunosuppressive mechanisms, upregulation of PD-L1, expansion of regulatory T cells, and secretion of TGF-β that blunt durable immune control.148 BsAbs can bridge these gaps by concurrently blocking these inhibitory pathways and activating co-stimulatory receptors within the irradiated tumour microenvironment, sustaining and amplifying CRT-primed cytotoxic T-cell responses. Preclinical and early clinical data from other solid tumours and orthotopic models support the premise that BsAbs combined with radiotherapy can prolong and deepen anti-tumour immunity.149, 150, 151 By analogy, PD-1/CTLA-4 and PD-L1/4-1BB BsAbs are poised to convert the transient CRT-driven “hot” tumour into a sustained immune-inflamed state.

Practical implementation of BsAb-augmented CRT in routine practice will require optimisation of dosing schedules, formulation, toxicity management, and biomarker implementation. Subcutaneous formulations have improved treatment adherence outside tertiary centres, while step-up dosing strategies mitigate infusion-related reactions without sacrificing anti-tumour potency.152,153 Predefined toxicity rules such as pneumonitis/esophagitis stopping criteria and staggered therapeutic windows (e.g., BsAb initiation 1–2 weeks after CRT) can de-risk overlapping inflammatory sequelae, as shown in an early SBRT-BsAb study with cadonilimab.151 Vigilant monitoring for 4-1BB-related hepatotoxicity and immune flares,154,155 with response and progression adjudicated per iRECIST guidelines, will be essential to distinguish true progression from immune-mediated effects. Enrichment strategies that leverage PD-L1 expression, on-treatment ctDNA kinetics, and dynamic T-cell activation markers promise to identify patients most likely to achieve durable remissions with BsAb-CRT combinations. This approach could position BsAb-augmented CRT as a compelling avenue for future phase II/III consolidation trials in unresectable stage III NSCLC.

Another critical decision in stage III NSCLC is whether to pursue BsAbs or ADCs as induction and/or consolidation following CRT, a choice for which no head-to-head evidence yet exists. In the neoadjuvant setting, ADC monotherapy has consistently outperformed BsAbs in achieving higher pCR while prolonging EFS and OS. ADCs leverage broadly expressed surface antigens, such as TROP2 targeted by Dato-Dxd or Sacituzumab-Govitecan, to deliver potent cytotoxic payloads across diverse histologies, achieving responses even in immunologically “cold” tumours. By contrast, BsAbs (e.g., PD-L1 × 4-1BB or PD-1 × CTLA-4 constructs) require both adequate target expression and the presence of functional TILs to sustain immune activation, confining their efficacy to PD-L1-positive, immune-inflamed niches (Table 2).

Tri-modality and salvage surgery strategies

Although we have explored the expanding arsenal of systemic consolidation, from ICIs and TKIs to ADCs and BsAbs, one critical pillar remains underemphasised: surgical resection in patients initially deemed unresectable.

Surgery has been the cornerstone of curative intent across malignancies, including lung cancer,156 yet its role in stage III has fluctuated with advances in CRT, patient selection, and minimally invasive thoracic techniques.157 An additional significant contributing factor to this complexity is the lack of a universally standardised definition for "resectability", as demonstrated by the regularly updated AJCC classification about tumour size, nodal stations, and atelectasis criteria.158 This definition can vary considerably across different clinical centres and is often influenced by the expertise and experience of the surgical team. The decision to pursue either planned or salvage surgery in stage III NSCLC is contingent upon a meticulous evaluation of high-resolution imaging PET/CT, biomarker-based criteria, patient fitness, and organ function adequacy, depending on the dynamic nature of the disease and treatment response.

Historically, the role of surgery has been somewhat limited due to postoperative complications,159,160 despite offering a disease-free outcome. For instance, early randomised trials, notably Intergroup 0139, showed that adding lobectomy after concurrent CRT improved PFS but did not confer an OS advantage, in part due to high pneumonectomy-related mortality when only 45 Gy was delivered preoperatively.161 Subsequent studies, EORTC 08941162 and ESPATUE,163 showed that escalating preoperative RT to ≥ 60 Gy and refining selection could yield 5-year survival rates of 40–50% in N2 disease, with perioperative mortality under 5% at high-volume centres.

Salvage surgery, defined as resection performed at least three months after definitive CRT for residual or recurrent tumour, has emerged as a safe and effective option in rigorously restaged non-progressors.164 A recent National Cancer Database analysis (2010–2020) demonstrated comparable long-term survival (10-year OS, about 34%) and perioperative mortality (30-day 2.3%; 90-day 5.1%) between planned and salvage resections, both of which markedly outperformed nonoperative management (10-year OS, 16.8%).165

Should we abandon the longstanding CRT-consolidation paradigm for initially unresectable stage III NSCLC in favour of neoadjuvant systemic therapy with curative intent? This question frames a rapidly evolving clinical debate. Emerging response-adapted strategies are now beginning to blur the historical boundary between definitive CRT and surgery by integrating early systemic therapy, radiologic reassessment, and MDT decision-making. Building on the surgical‑assessment and consolidation principles exemplified by AEGEN and PACIFIC, the MDT‑BRIDGE interim analysis, as presented at ESMO 2025, evaluated the feasibility of directing patients to surgery or to definitive CRT based on MDT reassessment after two cycles of neoadjuvant triplet therapy (durvalumab plus platinum‑based chemotherapy). The strategy was explicitly designed to “bridge” patients into the most appropriate local‑therapy pathway according to individual radiologic and clinical response, with all patients planned to receive subsequent adjuvant or consolidation durvalumab. Among patients initially classified as borderline‑resectable, 71% proceeded to surgical resection, while 93% of those initially deemed resectable underwent surgery; overall pCR was 28%, and the R0 resection rate was 94%. Complementary neoadjuvant approaches include LungMate-002,166 where anti-PD-1 plus chemotherapy produced a high objective response/conversion rate (72%), and LungMate-003, in which the anti-PD-1+chemotherapy arm achieved 50% versus 42.9% in the anti-PD-1+VEGFR-2 arm.167 In the LungMate-007,168 a TKI-first strategy with neoadjuvant aumolertinib in EGFR-mutant (ex19 del or L858R) NSCLC produced a 45.1% conversion-to-resection rate and a 70.6% ORR, with a reported pCR of 13% and an R0 of 100%.

These signals shift the emphasis from a one-size-fits-all consolidation model toward an individualised, MDT-driven approach in which omitting a planned CRT pathway for molecularly profiled, initially unresectable, stage III NSCLC, favouring response-adapted pathways, whether T-cell-engaging immunotherapy-first or targeted-therapy-first, can convert a meaningful subset of tumours to resectability. At the same time, enthusiasm must, however, be tempered by caveats, as conversion-to-resection rates are reported from early, often non-randomised series; OS and long-term functional outcomes remain immature; perioperative morbidity and standardised surgical endpoints have not been comprehensively reported; the effects of prolonged perioperative systemic exposure are incompletely characterised; and standardised definitions and MDT decision algorithms are lacking. Notably, the MDT-BRIDGE interim analysis did not fully standardise definitions for “borderline” resectability or the MDT triage algorithm, limiting generalisability and introducing potential selection bias, and prolonged perioperative exposure to aumolertinib in LungMate-007 (planned adjuvant/neoadjuvant treatment up to three years or until progression) may confound response assessment and downstream outcomes. Therefore, while neoadjuvant, response-adapted strategies offer a provocative alternative to definitive CRT-consolidation, these findings are hypothesis-generating and promising but exploratory; adoption as a new standard will require further prospective, multidisciplinary trials with rigorous surgical endpoints, harmonised resectability criteria, comprehensive surgical morbidity reporting, and mature survival follow-up.

Should we reintegrate surgery following CRT or post-consolidation in previously unresectable stage III NSCLC? First, patient selection for trimodality therapy must move beyond anatomy to incorporate formal comorbidity and frailty assessments. Tools for comorbidity assessment have been validated in thoracic surgery cohorts, independently predicting postoperative morbidity and long-term survival beyond traditional FEV1 and DLCO metrics.169 Equally important are prehabilitation protocols, structured pulmonary rehabilitation, inspiratory-muscle training, aerobic conditioning, and nutritional optimisation, which can convert 15–30% of borderline “unfit” patients into operative candidates while reducing postoperative pulmonary complications by up to 30% and shortening hospital stays by several days.170

Another concern is raised regarding AEs and quality of life. While concurrent CRT is associated with a higher risk of toxicities compared to sequential CRT, including grade 3–4 hematologic toxicities (20%), esophagitis (8%), and pneumonitis (7%), salvage surgery, performed after high-dose radiation, is inherently associated with greater perioperative morbidity due to radiation-induced tissue changes, such as fibrosis, which can complicate dissection and increase surgical time and blood loss.171 In addition, patients undergoing lobectomy often experience functional impairments, including chronic chest pain, reduced respiratory muscle function, and decreased exercise tolerance.169 Coexisting chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), interstitial lung disease (ILD), and cardiovascular disease further amplify these risks and demand rigorous patient selection.170

Yet trimodality approaches can deliver 5-year OS rates of 40% in superior sulcus tumours treated with CRT followed by surgery and 49% in upfront resection for highly selected patient populations.172, 173, 174 While full-dose SBRT has been questioned as a mere dose-escalation tool,175 an alternative strategy is to intensify local therapy at the outset to maximise locoregional control, thereby preserving systemic options for eventual progression. Delivering a biologically effective dose > 70 Gy via an SBRT boost after standard 60 Gy concurrent CRT has achieved local control rates exceeding 90% with grade ≥ 3 pneumonitis in only 10–15% of patients.176, 177, 178 Adaptive SBRT planning, redefining targets with mid-treatment PET/CT or 4D-CT, can reduce lung V20 and esophageal dose, downstage residual disease, and convert borderline unresectable lesions into surgical candidates.178,179

The interplay between radiotherapy and surgery in locally advanced disease raises multiple combinatorial questions: what is the role of salvage strategies beyond conventional resection as a “rescue” in oligo-progression, especially when lobectomy is no longer feasible? Should old-fashioned radiofrequency ablation, cryoablation or intra-operative radiotherapy be reintroduced as a rescue option? Should SBRT be introduced post-CRT as a selective boost to residual lesions? Can SBRT be delivered concurrently or sequentially alongside chemotherapy, ICIs, TKIs, ADCs, or BsAbs in a neoadjuvant setting? What is the real-world feasibility of these complex regimens, given resource constraints, multidisciplinary coordination, and potential overlapping toxicities?

For patients deemed unfit for full-dose CRT or surgery due to comorbidities or frailty, an SBRT-centric neoadjuvant regimen offers a tantalising alternative.180,181 By avoiding prolonged CRT courses, we can potentially minimise acute toxicities that preclude later surgery, exploit SBRT’s high single-fraction dose to elicit immunogenic cell death and synergise with ICIs or ADCs, while improving ECOG PS sufficiently to revisit resection or salvage CRT.

Such a paradigm shift warrants prospective evaluation, as it could extend OS while sidestepping the morbidity of CRT. Yet, merging SBRT with novel systemic agents demands robust safety data, clear sequencing algorithms, and harmonised institutional workflows. Adapting these approaches, which potentially “use up” salvage radiotherapy, pushes us to ask, what justifies front-loading treatment if it exhausts later rescue options?

A clear roadmap for prospective evaluation is urgently needed. While landmark trials such as MDT-BRIDGE182 and TRAILBLAZER183 are poised to formally integrate surgery with next-generation systemic agents and refined radiotherapy, their fragmented designs leave critical questions unanswered around sequencing, biomarker integration, and organ preservation, in addition to the highlighted trial holes.

Grounded in the philosophy of these trials, we must ask whether ADCs can be safely and effectively integrated up front with CRT and with local salvage strategies while maximising the proportion of patients who undergo definitive local therapy before or after systemic exposure without provoking excess peri-procedural toxicity. Although some clinical experience suggests that lower ADC doses may preserve activity for selected patients while prolonging exposure and reducing toxicity,184, 185, 186 this observation is drug-specific and lacks prospective validation; whether a low-dose strategy applies broadly to ADCs remains an open question. If lower dosing is feasible, we need to know how safety (perioperative complications, pulmonary toxicity) and efficacy (pCR, resection rates, event-free survival [EFS]/PFS/OS, ctDNA conversion) compare when low-dose ADC is combined with CRT and followed by SBRT or surgery. To answer these questions, trials should be explicitly designed to quantify synergy and trade-offs. Practical schemas include:1 neoadjuvant ADC ± CRT followed by surgery, with co-primary endpoints of pCR and R0 resection rate and secondary endpoints of ctDNA/MRD conversion and OS;2 CRT followed by randomised consolidation: ADC consolidation versus adjuvant ADC after surgery versus ADC after SBRT, to compare sequencing and local-therapy interactions; and3 dose-optimisation substudies (PK/PD and exposure–response) embedded within these arms to test whether reduced dosing preserves efficacy while lowering toxicity. Each schema must prespecify perioperative timing windows, mandatory confirmatory biomarker sampling, and multidisciplinary review before irreversible interventions.

Beyond specific therapeutic items, we propose an adaptive, ctDNA-stratified Bayesian platform trial in unresectable stage III NSCLC that randomises patients, within early ctDNA-clearance cohorts, to three parallel, internally controlled comparisons: first, standard CRT alone versus CRT plus optimal systemic therapy (ICIs/TKIs/ADCs/BsAbs), both followed by surgical resection; second, standard CRT versus CRT with an SBRT boost, each arm integrating systemic therapy per ctDNA stratum prior to surgery; and third, platinum-based chemoimmunotherapy versus an all-“optimal” systemic regimen, each administered before surgery. This head-to-head design simultaneously evaluates CRT-first vs. systemic-first sequencing, the incremental benefit of SBRT dose escalation, and the additive value of next-generation agents during radiation, with a prespecified OS as the primary endpoint, fixed crossover rules, equal randomisation, and early stopping boundaries for futility or success, to dynamically refines allocation ratios, streamlines multidisciplinary decision-making, and ensures long-term follow-up in line with evolving FDA approvals, clarifying how to balance efficacy, toxicity, and organ preservation, overcoming the short-lived tumour shrinkage often seen with ADCs and delivering truly durable remissions in stage III NSCLC.

From our point of view, and by the time this perspective is written, as ADCs have demonstrated unprecedented pCR, EFS, and OS improvements in neoadjuvant trials, endpoints not matched by TKIs or ICIs alone, this begs two critical questions: Should ADCs become a universal strategy regardless of oncogenic driver status? Would early ADC integration preserve subsequent lines of therapy by maximising initial tumour eradication? Biomarker-driven selection remains key, but a broad ADC-first approach could reshape the treatment landscape, provided trials confirm durability and manageable toxicity profiles.

Cost and value-based considerations

Despite compelling clinical advancements, the fiscal impact of intensive multimodal regimens raises serious questions about sustainability, equity, and real-world feasibility. Can health systems and patients absorb the cost of even more intensive regimens?

From a payer perspective, ICIs illustrate the challenge. Durvalumab maintenance after CRT now costs upwards of $100K/patient each year, yet delivers only incremental OS gains compared with shorter courses.187,188 Dual-checkpoint blockade with nivolumab plus ipilimumab adds roughly $202K in drug expenditures and generates an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of ∼ $401K/quality-adjusted life year (QALY) versus platinum-doublet chemotherapy, far above most willingness-to-pay thresholds, unless prices fall by more than 80% or treatment is curtailed to under six weeks.189,190 Even incorporating biosimilars such as bevacizumab trims only about $77K from a two-year ICI–chemotherapy program, leaving extended or dual-checkpoint strategies under urgent, value-based reassessment.191

Targeted consolidation poses similar dilemmas. Osimertinib in EGFR-mutant NSCLC disease runs approximately $14K/month, over $168K/year, for a dramatic PFS improvement (5.6 to 39.1 months) but an ICER exceeding $200K/QALY and immature OS data.192 ADC and bispecifics push budgets even higher. Agents like trastuzumab-deruxtecan and Dato-DXd cost $10K–$15K/dose, while emerging bispecifics, targeting PD-1 × CTLA-4 or PD-L1 × 4-1BB, can reach $15,000–$20,000 per infusion.141 Translating to ICERs north of $300K/QALY absent rebates or outcome-based contracts, especially in combination settings.193, 194, 195

Classic tri-modality therapy approaches a premium bracket: cCRT (∼$80K), surgery and hospitalisation ($50K–$70K), and durvalumab maintenance push cumulative costs near $250K/patient.196,197 Adding stereotactic boosts198 and layering TKIs, ICIs, ADCs and bispecifics drives total expenditures well beyond $300K.

Additional “often-overlooked” cost drivers deserve explicit mention. First, toxicity-attributable costs, hospitalisation for immune pneumonitis or ADC-related ILD, long steroid tapers, and dermatologic interventions for bispecifics can materially worsen ICERs. Second, operational costs and capacity constraints, linear accelerator time and quality assurance for SBRT, specialised staffing, and the availability of enhanced recovery after surgery pathways shape real-world feasibility and access. Third, patient-level financial toxicity (travel, lost wages, carer time) and site-of-care choices (home/ambulatory infusions, subcutaneous formulations) can meaningfully change total costs.

To offset these trends, biomarker-guided approaches using ctDNA clearance, radiologic response, and molecular MRD tracking can define patient subsets eligible for abbreviated therapy, fixed-duration schedules, or value-adjusted intensification, offer the only real promise for enhancing cost-effectiveness without compromising outcomes.

Across treatment groups, the path forward demands value-based pricing models, outcome-tied reimbursement, and biomarker-driven de-escalation trials with embedded health-economic endpoints. Without these guardrails, the financial toxicity of extended and multi-modality regimens risks undermining both sustainability and equitable access. As novel resistance-busting agents emerge, we must ask: what is the clinical, economic and operational rationality of upfront intensification when most patients will ultimately require further lines of therapy?

Conclusions

Managing stage III NSCLC demands more than choosing a fashionable regimen; it requires precise, patient-centred sequencing that aligns biology, fitness, and feasibility. The core decision is not neoadjuvant versus CRT + consolidation philosophy, it’s who benefits, when, and at what cost. Intensification for its own sake does not work, especially in inoperable, frail or elderly patients. When toxicity triggers discontinuation, we invite (oligo)progression; judicious dose reductions and fixed-duration strategies can be a virtue, not a compromise. Genotyping should shape pathways from the outset, and treatment should adapt at progression, guided by real-time biomarkers to distinguish who needs escalation, who can de-escalate, and when to switch mechanisms.

ADC are changing the conversation, particularly in perioperative settings with encouraging pCR and EFS signals, but they are not a universal solution. Their place should be proven in biomarker-defined, risk-adaptive frameworks rather than assumed. Surgery and advanced radiotherapy (including SBRT boost and adaptive planning) should be reintegrated as modular components, not afterthoughts, deployed selectively to deepen local control, salvage responders, and preserve organ function when systemic therapy alone cannot deliver durable remission.

The future standard must be defined by evidence and value, not intensity: embed ctDNA- and imaging-triggered decision points, prehabilitation to expand surgical candidacy, and clear stop rules to avoid overtreatment. Without value-based pricing, outcome-linked reimbursement, and biomarker-driven duration, the most sophisticated regimens will remain unsustainable and inaccessible. The mandate is clear: deliver cures where possible, control where necessary, and always respect the clinical, economic, and human realities of the patients we serve.

Building on the foundations laid in this work, the next phase aims to translate preliminary insights into a forward-leaning blueprint, one that anticipates paradigm shifts and readies the field for their swift translation into practice. We will independently distil and update, on a hypothesis-driven basis and prior to the availability of definitive trial results, the proposed adaptive algorithm for initially unresectable stage III NSCLC, encompassing the full management from baseline molecular profiling through sequential ctDNA monitoring, integrating or omitting definitive CRT and extending to genotype-directed therapy with TKI, ICI, BsAb, or ADC, with or without surgical resection and/or additional radiotherapy, while also outlining driver-specific CNS management strategies and clear (de)-escalation criteria that incorporate prior systemic therapies and multidimensional progression metrics such as radiologic change, clinical status, and treatment-related toxicity. Subsequently, we will extend this framework to examine global implementation, feasibility, value considerations, and digital settings the stage for truly integrated, practice-changing adoption.
