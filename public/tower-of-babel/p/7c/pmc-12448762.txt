Genetic literacy among surgeons who manage patients with cancer: a national survey of knowledge, perceptions, attitudes, and barriers
Authors: Linda YN Fei, Sandra Messiha, Zuhaib M Mir, Rachelle Dinchong, Alison Rusnak, Nicholas Cofie, Nancy Dalgarno, Rona E Cheifetz, Shaila J Merchant
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The rapid evolution of genetic testing and availability of information has necessitated increased surgeon participation in genetics-related tasks. We sought to characterize knowledge, perceptions, attitudes, and barriers pertaining to genetic literacy among Canadian surgeons who manage patients with a hereditary predisposition to or confirmed cancer.Methods:We distributed a Web-based survey to surgeons across Canada from June to December 2023 through relevant surgical societies. We analyzed quantitative and narrative data from the survey descriptively and thematically.Results:We included 57 participants from 8 provinces (response rate 10%). Many surgeons (28/45, 62%) reported performing risk assessment, but 16% (7/45) reported counselling and 29% (13/45) reported ordering genetic testing. Surgeons reported low confidence in ordering testing and in interpreting and discussing implications of testing results. Most surgeons (35/39, 90%) expressed a desire for improvement in their knowledge and in their confidence in hereditary cancer genetics. Approval and funding for testing, referral to a genetic counsellor or medical geneticist, and availability of genetics clinics were reported as extreme barriers to providing care.Conclusion:Practising surgeons in Canada participate in many genetics-related tasks, but they report low confidence and face barriers to genetic literacy. There is a need and desire for interventions targeting genetic literacy among surgeons in Canada.

The utilization of genetic information in clinical decision-making is increasing in this emerging era of personalized medicine.1 Front-line health care workers, including surgeons, are actively involved in caring for patients with or at risk of developing hereditary cancer.2 Care for such patients may include coordinating referrals for genetic risk assessment and counselling, ordering genetic testing, interpreting genetic testing results, appropriately counselling patients, arranging for screening and surveillance, and performing prophylactic surgical procedures based on genetic risk. We recently showed that, despite participating in many genetics-related tasks, surgeons report low comfort with such tasks as well as substantial barriers at the patient, surgeon, and system levels.3

Because of the limited number of certified genetic counsellors and medical geneticists, the provision of genetics-related services presents substantial challenges in Canada as there is not enough workforce capacity to meet the need for hereditary cancer services.4 Mainstreaming, which refers to oncologist-ordered genetic testing to bypass a pretest genetic counselling appointment, has been introduced as a strategy to address the bottleneck in accessing genetics-related services and improving ease of referral to hereditary cancer services.5 Mainstreaming enables health care providers without dedicated genetics training to provide basic pretest counselling and order limited genetic testing using a well-defined list of criteria, including personal and family history of cancer.6 Results are then disclosed by the ordering provider, and patients are referred to a genetics care provider for more in-depth genetic counselling if a pathogenic variant or variant of uncertain significance is identified.7

With the increased need for front-line health care providers, including surgeons, to participate in genetics-related tasks, the introduction of mainstreaming, and the increased utilization of genetic information to guide clinical care, we sought to better understand the role that surgeons currently play in this evolving landscape. We sought to describe the knowledge, perceptions, attitudes, and barriers pertaining to genetic literacy among practising surgeons in Canada.

Methods

Survey design

As a collaborative group of surgeons, surgical trainees, medical geneticists, genetic counsellors, and educational consultants with experience in survey development, we designed and developed an online anonymous survey. We grouped questions into 8 themes: surgeon demographic and practice information, previous education or experience in hereditary cancer genetics, referrals for genetic risk assessment and counselling, ordering of genetic testing, the role of surgeons in the performance of and confidence in genetics-related tasks, attitudes and perceptions toward these tasks, perceived barriers, and desire for improvement in genetic literacy (Appendix 1, available at www.canjsurg.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cjs.008424/tab-related-content).

Survey population and administration

The survey was initially pilot-tested by surgeons at 1 academic teaching hospital. It was distributed to the membership of the Canadian Association of General Surgeons, the Canadian Society of Surgical Oncology, and the Canadian Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons to reach practising surgeons who are most likely to participate in the care of patients with or at risk of hereditary cancer. Eligible participants included practising surgeons in general surgery, colorectal surgery, breast surgery, endocrine surgery, and surgical oncology. Surgeons specializing in urology or urologic oncology, gynecology or gynecologic oncology, thoracic surgery, pediatric surgery, and trauma surgery were excluded, as the study focused on the oncological practice of general surgeons and subspecialists in Canada.

The survey was administered via a Web-based platform (Qualtrics) and distributed through monthly society newsletters to subscribing members. The survey remained open for 7 months (June–December 2023), with 3 rounds of newsletter dissemination. Initially, response rates were lower than anticipated. To improve participation, we implemented 2 additional distribution strategies starting in October 2023: the survey was circulated through administrative assistants of general surgery departments at 18 academic-affiliated teaching hospitals in Canada, and a quick response (QR) code linking to the survey was presented during a podium session at the Canadian Surgery Forum, Canadian Society of Surgical Oncology session. The inclusion criteria for respondents remained unchanged throughout the study, and survey responses were screened to ensure that only eligible participants were included in the analysis. We estimate that our survey was distributed to about 600 practising surgeons in Canada. We did not offer incentives.

Data analysis

We performed the data analysis using the survey software platform Qualtrics (version March 2024).8 For Likert-type questions, we collapsed categories into 3 responses: not, slightly/somewhat, and moderate/extreme. We analyzed the analytic sample using descriptive statistics including frequencies, percentages, and means.

Results

We received 60 responses, with an estimated response rate of 10%. Of these, 3 responses were excluded as they were from a resident, pediatric surgeon, and vascular surgeon. Therefore, we used 57 individual responses in the analysis. However, not all participants answered every question, resulting in varying sample sizes for individual survey items.

Surgeon demographic characteristics

Table 1 displays the characteristics of the survey respondents. More than half of the respondents were male (29/54, 54%), and most participants were aged 31–40 years (20/54, 37%). Most participants were in the first 10 years of independent practice (32/54, 59%) and had completed fellowship training (41/52, 79%). The most common fellowship training programs completed were surgical oncology (16/40, 40%) and colorectal surgery (9/40, 23%). Eight provinces or territories were represented among the respondents, with most surgeons (48/51, 94%) practising in an academic teaching hospital or community hospital with academic affiliation.

Education in hereditary cancer genetics

Most surgeons (42/46, 91%) sought out information on hereditary cancer genetics in their current practice to help guide patient management. Surgeons considered that fellowship training (24/37, 65%), residency training (17/49, 35%), and self-initiated learning (21/49, 43%) were moderately to extremely significant to their knowledge in hereditary cancer genetics (Figure 1). Other sources of learning, including “conferences,” “multidisciplinary rounds,” “consultation reports from genetics program,” and “American Board of [Colon and Rectal] Surgery maintenance of certification process,” were described in narrative responses. Resources reported as moderately to extremely significant for acquiring knowledge included written materials (18/48, 38%), websites (17/44, 39%), and continuing medical education events (14/46, 30%) (Figure 2).

Referrals for genetic risk assessment, counselling, and ordering genetic testing

Notably, whereas 62% (28/45) of surgeons reported that they perform genetic risk assessment, only 16% (7/45) reported performing genetic counselling. Of the surgeons surveyed, 87% (39/45) reported referring their patients to medical geneticists, genetic counsellors, or hereditary high-risk programs in the past 12 months for genetic risk assessment and counselling, with 85% (33/39) referring 10 or fewer patients per year. Surgeons identified that medical geneticists or genetic counsellors were mainly responsible for the tasks of ordering genetic testing (32/45, 71%), performing risk assessment (34/45, 76%), and counselling patients (41/45, 91%) with suspected or confirmed hereditary cancer (Figure 3). Hereditary genetic testing was ordered mainly by medical geneticists or counsellors (32/45, 71%) and medical oncologists (21/45, 47%), and infrequently by surgeons (13/45, 29%). Most respondents (38/44, 86%) reported they were not involved in mainstreaming.

Role of surgeons in genetics-related tasks

All surgeons agreed that their role typically included genetics-related tasks, such as identifying patients at risk (42/43, 98%), submitting referrals for genetic risk assessment and counselling (43/43, 100%), performing appropriate surgery on patients with confirmed hereditary cancer (43/43, 100%), performing prophylactic surgery (42/43, 98%), and ongoing surveillance of patients (34/43, 79%). However, many surgeons felt that they should not participate in ordering genetic testing (24/41, 59%) and interpreting genetic testing results (31/41, 76%). There were conflicting views on whether surgeons should be involved in discussing implications of genetic testing, with 42% (18/43) disagreeing, 51% (22/43) agreeing, and 7% (3/43) neither agreeing nor disagreeing.

Confidence in performing genetics-related tasks

Genetics-related tasks that surgeons were moderately to extremely confident in performing included submitting referral for genetic risk assessment and counselling (28/38, 74%), performing appropriate surgery on patients with confirmed hereditary cancer (34/40, 85%), performing prophylactic surgery (33/39, 85%), and performing ongoing screening and surveillance of patients (28/38, 74%). Of responding surgeons, 58% (21/36) reported not feeling confident in ordering genetic testing, 50% (19/38) reported not feeling confident in interpreting genetic testing results, and 26% (10/39) reported not feeling confident in discussing implications of genetic testing results with patients (Figure 4). The highest reported confidence in hereditary cancer syndromes included colorectal (23/40, 58% moderately to extremely confident) and breast (15/40, 38% moderately to extremely confident), whereas melanoma (18/35, 51% not confident) and sarcoma (20/33, 61% not confident) were among the cancers for which surgeons reported having the lowest confidence.

Surgeon confidence in performing genetics-related tasks in patients with suspected or confirmed hereditary cancer.

Attitudes and perceptions pertaining to genetics-related tasks

Most respondents (39/40, 98%) felt that medical geneticists or genetic counsellors were primarily responsible for performing genetic counselling. Most respondents (37/39, 95%) felt that medical geneticists or genetic counsellors are primarily responsible for the interpretation of genetic testing results. Many surgeons (35/39, 90%) reported a need for additional resources to enhance their knowledge of hereditary cancer genetics.

Barriers to providing genetics-related services

The most commonly reported moderate or extreme barriers to genetic literacy were ease of approval and availability of funding for testing (21/34, 62%), ease of referral to genetic counsellor or medical geneticist (20/38, 53%), availability of genetics clinics (28/38, 74%), timeliness of referral appointments (27/36, 75%), and lack of time for counselling during busy clinic (17/36, 47%) (Table 2). In the open-ended questions of the survey, respondents noted that the additional barriers they encountered were “lack of knowledge of what test to order (panel or specific genes),” “patient lack of understanding or lack of information on their own family history,” and “limited exposure to certain cancers.”

Desire for improvement in genetic literacy

Overall, most respondents (35/39, 90%) had a desire to improve their knowledge and confidence in hereditary cancer genetics. Areas of development regarded as moderately or extremely important were information on surgical approaches in patients at risk of or with hereditary cancer (29/39, 74%), proper screening and surveillance (28/39, 72%), estimating genetic risk (27/39, 69%), referring patients for genetic testing (24/39, 62%), and appropriately ordering genetic testing (24/39, 62%).

The modality of educational material preferred by surgeons for further learning were continuing medical education (25/38, 66%), online modules (22/38, 58%), mobile application (20/38, 53%), in-person lectures (19/38, 50%), online live lectures (17/38, 45%), websites (16/38, 42%), and written materials such as medical journals and textbooks (16/38, 42%).

Discussion

We found that practising surgeons in Canada participate in many genetics-related tasks but report low confidence in and face substantial barriers to genetic literacy. There is a need and desire for interventions targeting surgeon genetic literacy in Canada. The strengths of our study include its collaborative multidisciplinary survey design with input not only from surgeons but also from medical geneticists, genetic counsellors, and educational consultants. The data represent surgeons in academic and community and small hospital practice settings in 8 of 13 provinces and territories in Canada.

In our study, 91% of surgeons reported seeking out information on hereditary cancer genetics in their current practice to help guide patient management; however, many were not confident in ordering genetic testing (58%) and interpreting genetic testing results (50%). This suggests that although surgeons are interested in and provide genetics-related services to their patients, they feel less confident in genetic test ordering and counselling. This may be because of the increased complexity of genetic information today, which may require nuanced interpretation.9 For instance, about 20% of patients undergoing hereditary cancer panel testing will have a genetic variant of unknown clinical significance, a result that requires interpretation in the context of the patient’s personal and family history, which is often done with a genetic counsellor.10

It is projected that by 2030, a total of 90 specialist physicians and 326 genetic counsellors (1.7-fold and 1.6-fold increases from 2020, respectively) will be required to provide Canadians with hereditary cancer services if current growth trends and care models remain unchanged.4 Mainstreaming has been proposed and implemented in various cancer care models as an approach to address some of these barriers in access to genetics services.11–13 Mainstreaming interventions provide health professionals with minimal or no knowledge in genetics with a systematized approach for ordering genetic testing and obtaining results for their patients.14 The Cancer Care Ontario recommendations for clinical cancer genetics services are that the physician lead (a role that can be filled by a medical oncologist, surgical oncologist, or primary care physician with some expertise in clinical cancer genetics) should facilitate expedited access to genetic testing for eligible patients.14 Surgeons in our study reported that medical oncologists were more heavily involved in genetic testing, and this may be driven by the discovery of new therapies in the field of medical oncology such as poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors that have been shown to improve survival in patients with germline BRCA mutation ovarian cancer.15

Our study shows that surgeons are more confident in performing tasks such as referral for genetic risk assessment and counselling, appropriate surgery on patients with confirmed hereditary cancer, prophylactic surgery, and ongoing screening and surveillance of patients. Between 2005 and 2017, there was a 205% increase in the rate of contralateral prophylactic mastectomies in hereditary breast cancer16 and an uptake in performing prophylactic total abdominal colectomies in patients with Lynch syndrome, hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer, and familial adenomatous polyposis.17 However, surgeons do not feel confident in ordering genetic testing, interpreting results, and discussing implications of genetic results with patients. These are areas where surgeons may require enhanced support and learning, particularly if mainstreaming were to be more widely used. Our findings are similar to those of another survey of breast and general surgeons showing that, if given the option, 85% of respondents preferred to refer patients to genetic counselling and testing.18

Our study shows that 86% of surgeons are not involved in mainstreaming, but it is clear that mainstreaming initiatives are under way, and they underscore the importance of appropriate knowledge-based interventions and support to improve genetic literacy among surgeons. In fact, there is a push for universal germline genetic testing for breast cancer in low-resource rural practice settings to improve adherence to treatment guidelines, with potential health economic benefits to patients and the health care system.19 Universal genetic testing has also been studied in solid tumours, and approximately 30% of patients with a high-penetrance variant of pathogenic germline mutations will receive modified treatment based on the genetic finding.20 The uptake of universal genetic testing at the time of cancer diagnosis can potentially eliminate the barrier surgeons face in understanding genetic testing criteria and selecting appropriate genetic tests.

It is important to reflect on the reported barrier of lack of time in clinic to provide genetic counselling, as this issue will resonate with most surgeons. An important consideration is the potential relation between surgeons’ perceived lack of time for genetic discussions in clinic and their low confidence in engaging in these conversations. It is possible that time constraints contribute to their lower confidence in genetic counselling, as surgeons may not have the opportunity to regularly engage in these discussions that would help solidify their knowledge and skills in this area. Conversely, a lack of confidence may itself discourage surgeons from dedicating time to genetic discussions, as they may feel ill equipped to provide adequate counselling. This interplay between time constraints and confidence may also influence whether surgeons perceive genetic discussions and testing as part of their role or tasks that should be primarily managed by medical geneticists. Although this study did not directly assess these relations, they warrant further exploration to better understand the barriers to surgeon-led genetic discussions and potential strategies to enhance confidence, effectiveness, and efficiencies in this area.

Our survey showed that 90% of respondents have a desire for improvement in their knowledge and confidence in hereditary cancer genetics through continuing medical education, online modules, and mobile applications. Similarly, in the field of family medicine, most survey respondents were interested in further education and identified in-person teaching, summaries of genetic disorders and testing criteria, and contact with a local genetic counsellor as most helpful in their practice.21 Online training programs with embedded videos have been shown to be effective in increasing genetic literacy and communication skills in health care professionals who discuss and coordinate genetic testing for patients with breast and ovarian cancer.22 In the United States, genomic medicine education initiatives have been developed that include immersive and experiential learning (i.e., medical school courses training students to interpret pharmacogenomics results), interdisciplinary and interprofessional education (i.e., residents and fellows work collaboratively with researchers to learn about sequence alignment, variant identification, and variant annotation, as well as with genetic counsellors regarding ethical and social issues in genomic medicine), and electronic and Web-based approaches (i.e., Web-based lectures, online resources, and integration of genomic and pharmacogenomic information into electronic medical records).23 Some of these interventions may also provide promising solutions in Canada to disseminate information widely and quickly, and in a low-cost manner.

Limitations

The primary limitations of this study include a low response rate and bias toward sampling surgeons in academic practice settings. Although the survey was open for 7 months, distributed through 3 different surgical societies, emailed to administrative assistants, and presented at a national conference, response rates were low. Nonresponders may differ from participating physicians in ways that we were not able to assess. Physicians commonly acknowledge that too many survey requests and growing constraints on their time limit their ability to participate in survey-based studies, leading to low response rates.24

This study may also be biased toward the practice patterns of surgeons in academic settings, as 94% of the respondents reported working at an academic teaching hospital or having academic affiliation. Notably, despite the overrepresentation of academic surgeons in this study, most respondents expressed a desire for improved knowledge and confidence in hereditary cancer genetics. This suggests the true national demand for enhanced genetic literacy among surgeons may be greater than estimated in this study.

Additionally, there were no responders from the provinces and territories of Manitoba, Newfoundland and Labrador, Nunavut, Yukon, and Prince Edward Island. As a result, the findings may not be fully generalizable to general surgeons in all provinces and territories.

Conclusion

Although surgeons reported participating and having confidence in several genetics-related tasks, they also reported low confidence in tasks such as ordering genetic testing, interpreting results, and discussing the implications of those results. With increased availability of and access to genetic information and a push toward mainstreaming to address resource issues, surgeons will inevitably be required to take on more. We have identified areas where targeted interventions could be developed to address surgeon gaps in genetic literacy.
