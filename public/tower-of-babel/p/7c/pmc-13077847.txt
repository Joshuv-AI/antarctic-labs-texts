Association between vaginal microbiota in early pregnancy and gestational diabetes mellitus: a nested case-control study
Authors: Xiang Hong, Mengjie Zhao, Furong Tan, Hanyue Zheng, Xiaoling Ding, Jiechen Yin, Xuening Zhang, Bei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo investigate the association between vaginal microbiota structure in early pregnancy and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and to characterize microbial signatures for early screening for GDM.MethodsThe present study was a nested case-control study recruiting pregnant women from the Nanjing Gulou Maternal-Child Health Center, China. Vaginal swabs were collected before 20 weeks of gestation for 16S rRNA sequencing. Following 1:3 propensity score matching, 45 GDM cases and 135 controls were enrolled. The final analysis included 42 GDM cases and 121 controls. A random forest model was used to explore the genera of vaginal differential microbiota associated with GDM. Based on these findings, latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted to explore potential types of vaginal microbiota, and logistic regression was used to analyze the association between vaginal microbiota types and GDM.ResultsThe GDM group exhibited elevated alpha diversity (Chao1 index, P = 0.036), and the abundance of Aeromonas in early pregnancy was associated with the risk of GDM (aOR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.01–1.29). LPA classified vaginal microbiota into five profiles: Type Ⅰ (high Aeromonas with low Lactobacillus), Type Ⅱ (Lactobacillus crispatus-dominant), Type Ⅲ (co-dominance of Lactobacillus iners and Lactobacillus jensenii), Type Ⅳ (co-dominance of Aeromonas and Lactobacillus crispatus), and Type Ⅴ (Lactobacillus iners-dominant). Compared with type II, type Ⅰ (aOR = 2.83, 95% CI: 1.25–6.40) or type IV (aOR = 3.02, 95% CI: 1.27–7.15) exhibited an elevated risk of GDM. Compared with Aeromonas abundance prediction, vaginal microbiota type-based prediction demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy (Aeromonas-AUC = 0.629, 95% CI: 0.541–0.718; vaginal microbiota profiles-AUC = 0.775, 95% CI: 0.716–0.835).ConclusionThe composition and structure of vaginal microbiota in early pregnancy are different in the two groups. The vaginal microbiota in early pregnancy, which is characterized by co-dominated by Aeromonas and Lactobacillus crispatus, was associated with a higher risk of GDM. Vaginal microbiota type-based prediction demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy than single bacteria.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04910-2.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), a transient glucose metabolic disorder first diagnosed during pregnancy [1], affects over 8.6% of Chinese pregnancies, with a globally rising prevalence exceeding 20% [2, 3]. GDM not only significantly increases the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes such as spontaneous abortion, preeclampsia, macrosomia, and neonatal respiratory distress syndrome, but also has lasting effects on maternal and fetal health [4, 5]. Recently, some studies have focused on the association between the microbiota and GDM. Koren et al. [6] documented increased beta diversity and elevated Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria abundance in the third-trimester gut microbiota of GDM patients. Similarly, Ferrocino et al. [7] observed gut microbiota of GDM patients, which was characterized by enhanced alpha diversity with Firmicutes enrichment concomitant with reduced Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria abundance. However, due to the influence of multiple factors such as dietary habits, it is difficult to determine a uniform pattern of association between gut microbiota characteristics and GDM, which increases the heterogeneity of the results and the difficulty of replication. In the population of pregnant women, the vaginal microbiota has a more direct impact on pregnancy outcomes. Zhang [8] found that the unique vaginal microbiota of GDM pregnant women were Lactobacillus listeri, Lactobacillus amylovorus, and Lactobacillus fructivorans. Most of the current studies focus on late pregnancy and lack in-depth investigation of changes in vaginal microbiota in early pregnancy to determine whether they may be predictive of the risk of GDM.

The vaginal microbiota constitutes a dynamic microbial ecosystem critical for female reproductive homeostasis [9]. Due to hormonal changes in the body, the vaginal microbiota shows dynamic changes in the whole life cycle of women [10]. Romero et al. have shown that the relative abundance of community state types (CST) I and CST III is higher in pregnant women, while CST Ⅳ is lower [11]. The results of some studies showed differences in the vaginal microbiota structure between pregnant women with GDM and healthy pregnant women [8, 12, 13]. Zhang et al. [8] reported that the vaginal microbiota of healthy pregnant women was dominated by Lactobacillus crispatus, while pregnant women with GDM were dominated by Lactobacillus acidophilus. Cortez et al. showed that the diversity of vaginal microbiological composition was lower in pregnant women with GDM than in healthy pregnant women [12]. Xiao et al. further showed that vaginal microbiota abnormalities in pregnant women with GDM could significantly increase the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes [13]. The current studies on the association between vaginal microbiota and GDM are mostly cross-sectional or retrospective in late pregnancy, which limits the causal interpretations and precludes early intervention opportunities.

Thus, this nested case-control study characterized vaginal microbiota from 163 Chinese pregnant women in early pregnancy to identify changes in vaginal microbiota corresponding to maternal health status. The aims were to elucidate the structural characteristics of vaginal microbiota in early pregnancy, identify specific genera associated with an increased risk of developing GDM, and establish an early prediction model for GDM based on biomarkers.

Methods

Study participants

This was a nested case-control study of pregnant women who had electronic health records at the Maternal-Child Health Center in Gulou District, Nanjing City, Jiangsu Province between June 2018 and May 2020. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University (2018ZDSYLL116-P01), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Clinical Trial Registration Number is not applicable.

Inclusion criteria for pregnant participants were defined as follows: (1) maternal age ≥ 18 years; (2) singleton pregnancy confirmed by first-trimester ultrasound; (3) gestational age ≤ 20 weeks at enrollment; and (4) willingness to attend scheduled follow-up visits at the designated healthcare facility. Exclusion criteria comprised (1) vaginal intercourse ≤ 48 h before sample collection; (2) recent antibiotic/antifungal use (≤ 72 h pre-sampling); (3) current administration of medications known to influence lipid or glucose metabolism; (4) incomplete or missing essential clinical data; and (5) voluntary withdrawal from study participation.

In total, 535 pregnant women satisfying the inclusion criteria were recruited. GDM diagnosis was established according to the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups [14]. All study participants underwent an oral glucose tolerance test between gestational weeks 24 and 28. GDM was diagnosed if fasting blood glucose was > 5.1 mmol/L, 1 h blood glucose was > 10 mmol/L after sugar water intake, or 2 h blood glucose was > 8.5 mmol/L. Following the application of exclusion criteria and the removal of participants lost to follow-up, 45 pregnant women with confirmed GDM were included in the GDM group. A logistic regression model was used with GDM as the dependent variable and maternal age along with the gestational week at vaginal sampling as independent variables to construct propensity scores. Matching was then performed using a caliper value of 0.001. A total of 135 pregnant women without GDM were included in the non-GDM group. After matching, the standardized mean differences (SMD) for both variables between the groups were less than 0.10, indicating a successful matching outcome. The final analysis included 163 participants, 42 in the GDM group and 121 in the non-GDM group (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Data and sample collection

Demographics and obstetric history were captured via a validated electronic Maternal Health Questionnaire. Clinical parameters included recorded clinical examination data, such as haematological analysis and vaginal secretions. Sampling operations were conducted by clinicians adhering to stringent aseptic protocols and standardized operating procedures. Vaginal swabs were collected from the posterior fornix of pregnant women during early gestation (≤ 20 weeks) and placed into sterile vaginal swab tubes. Immediately following collection, the primary swab was processed for Gram-stained smear microscopy to assess vaginal microbiota composition, while the matched samples were placed into a -20℃ incubator first and then transported back to the laboratory within 4 h. Finally, the matched samples were stored in a -80℃ refrigerator for subsequent DNA extraction and sequencing.

DNA extraction and 16 S rRNA gene sequencing

DNA was extracted and purified from vaginal swab specimens. Subsequently, the highly variable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using universal primers (5’-AGRGTTTGATYNTGGCTCAG-3’) and (5’-TASGGHTACCTTGTTASGACTT-3’), designed with Illumina adapter sequences. The DNA was amplified with 1 µL of DNA template (30 ng), 1 µL of dNTP, 2 µL of primer 338 F, 2 µL of primer 806R, 0.5 µL of TaqDNA polymerase, 5 µL of TransStartBuffer, and 38.5 µL of ddH2O to configure the PCR amplification system, mix, and centrifuge. Thirty amplification cycles were performed with initial denaturation at 95℃ for 5 min, thermal denaturation for 30 s, annealing at 58℃ for 20 s, and extension at 72℃ for 6 s. The amplified products were then incubated in the PCR buffer at 1.5 µL TransStart Buffer. The amplified products were used to judge the amplification effect by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis to construct sequencing libraries, and the sequencing was completed using the PacBio Sequel II platform. To control potential contamination from the laboratory environment, all samples were sequenced in a single batch, and a reagent blank sample was included. The reagent blank sample yielded no annotated valid gene sequences after sequencing, indicating that the experimental environment was qualified. Upstream analysis was performed on the QIIME 2 platform, and the sequences were denoised using the DADA2 algorithm, and the amplicon sequence variants were obtained. Feature sequences were annotated using a Naïve Bayes classifier combined with comparison using the SILVA 138 database as the reference database.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3 [15]. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent Student’s t-tests. Non-normally distributed variables were summarized as median (interquartile range) and analyzed via Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (%) with between-group differences assessed through Pearson’s χ² tests or Fisher’s exact tests. Before statistical modeling, relative abundance values underwent log10 transformation to address zero inflation and approximate normal distribution. These transformed data were subsequently utilized in correlation analyses to assess microbial-metabolic associations.

Differences in alpha and beta diversity indices of vaginal microbial communities between GDM and non-GDM groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon test and Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA), respectively. Alpha diversity was determined based on Chao1, the Shannon diversity index, and the Simpson index. PCoA was performed with Jaccard, Bray-Curtis, weighted, and unweighted UniFrac to evaluate beta diversity, and the permutational multivariate ANOVA (PERMANOVA) test was used to test for between-group differences.

Crucial microbiota capable of distinguishing differences between two groups of samples were identified by the random forest model. Subsequently, the association analysis between the abundance of specific bacteria and GDM was performed by logistic regression analysis. To further understand the intrinsic structural characteristics and typing patterns of the vaginal microbiota in pregnant women with GDM, latent profile analysis (LPA), was performed on the relative abundance of five selected key differential bacterial species: Aeromonas, Lactobacillus crispatus, Lactobacillus gasseri, Lactobacillus iners, and Lactobacillus jensenii. Prior to analysis, the relative abundance data were log-transformed and subsequently standardized using z-scores. The analysis was conducted using the mclust package in R. The optimal number of latent profiles was determined based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), entropy, and the proportion of individuals in each class (required to be > 5%). Ultimately, five vaginal bacteria types were identified. A logistic regression prediction model was constructed to further compare the predictive performance of markers in the vaginal microbiota and vaginal community state types for GDM risk. Due to the limited sample size in this study, a ten-fold cross-validation method was employed to generate ROC curves and calculate the area under the curve, thereby evaluating the prediction accuracy of the two models. Based on the optimal probability threshold determined by the Youden index from the ROC analysis, the predictive outcomes of the models were classified as positive or negative. Subsequently, the positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated using the standard formulas: PPV = True Positives / (True Positives + False Positives) and NPV = True Negatives / (True Negatives + False Negatives).

Results

Characteristics of the study population

A total of 163 pregnant women were completed for sequencing, including 42 GDM and 121 non-GDM. As detailed in Table 1, demographic characteristics including maternal age, gestational age at enrollment, educational attainment, and parity showed no statistically significant differences between groups (all P > 0.05), and none had a history of GDM. Notable metabolic differences were observed between the groups at the initial assessment. Compared with controls, women who developed GDM exhibited significantly higher pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) (23.04 ± 3.56 vs. 21.36 ± 2.77 kg/m², P = 0.002) and fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels (5.04 ± 0.51 vs. 4.71 ± 0.34 mmol/L, P < 0.001). However, glycated hemoglobin, type A1C (HbA1c) levels did not differ significantly between the groups (5.48 ± 0.48% vs. 5.50 ± 0.56%, P = 0.820).

Relative abundance of the vaginal microbiota between two groups

This study assessed the relative abundance rates of vaginal microbiota at three levels: phylum, genus, and species in two groups of pregnant women. At the phylum level (Fig. 1-a), Firmicutes predominated in both groups (GDM: 86.02% vs. non-GDM: 88.23%), followed by Actinobacteria (GDM: 12.50% vs. 8.02%) and Bacteroidetes (GDM: 2.50% vs. 1.96%). Genus-level profiling (Fig. 1-b) revealed Lactobacillus as the dominant genus in both groups, with marginally higher abundance in non-GDM groups (non-GDM: 83.16% vs. 79.48%). Notably, the GDM group exhibited a higher relative abundance of Gardnerella (GDM: 4.21% vs. 2.87%), Prevotella (GDM: 2.15% vs. 1.32%), and Megasphaera (GDM: 1.98% vs. 0.89%). Species-level analysis (Fig. 1-c) demonstrated Lactobacillus crispatus dominance across all participants (non-GDM: 41.31% vs. GDM: 40.49%), while the GDM group showed higher abundance of Gardnerella_vaginalis (GDM: 12.50% vs. 8.02%) and Chlamydia_trachomatis (GDM: 2.50% vs. 1.96%). The relative abundance of Lactobacillus iners (GDM: 34.40% vs. 31.94%), Lactobacillus jensenii (GDM: 3.49% vs. 3.14%), and Lactobacillus gasseri (GDM: 2.54% vs. 2.29%) was also higher than that of the non-GDM groups.

Alpha and beta diversity between two groups

As shown in Table 2, the Chao1 index was higher in the GDM group than in the non-GDM group, with a statistically significant difference in alpha diversity between the two groups (P = 0.036). To evaluate the differences in beta-diversity of vaginal microbiota between the two groups, we performed PCoA. The result showed that under the four algorithms, both GDM and non-GDM groups showed clustering of points of different colors in the PC1-PC2 plane, the between-group factor explained the small differences between all samples (R2 < 0.01), and none of the differences in community composition between the two groups were statistically significant (P > 0.05)(Supplementary Fig. S2).

Vaginal microbial variations between groups

The key microbial predictors were identified through the Random Forest model, and crucial differential genera were screened according to the Gini coefficient: Aeromonas, Escherichia, Streptococcus, and Lactobacillus were ranked as the principal discriminative genera (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Based on the significance cue of differential species, we compared the relative abundance of Aeromonas, Escherichia, Streptococcus, Bifidobacterium, and four Lactobacillus species (Lactobacillus crispatus, Lactobacillus iners, Lactobacillus gasseri, Lactobacillus jensenii) in the GDM group with that in the non-GDM group. As shown in Fig. 2, the relative abundance of Aeromonas and Escherichia was increased in the GDM group (P < 0.05), with four Lactobacillus species, Streptococus, and Bifidobacterium showed no statistical significance (all P values>0.05).

To analyze vaginal microbiota associated with GDM, we constructed a series of logistic regression models. The initial model revealed the association for Aeromonas (OR = 1.21, 95% CI:1.07–1.37). To examine the robustness of this association, we sequentially adjusted for potential confounders: first for age, education level, parity and gestational weeks at enrollment (Model 2), and subsequently for the pre-pregnancy BMI, FPG and glycated hemoglobin on the basis of Model 2 (Model 3). Association between vaginal microbiota type and GDM risk

LPA of vaginal microbial communities stratified participants into five distinct clusters based on Aeromonas and four Lactobacillus species profiles: Type Ⅰ: High Aeromonas with low Lactobacillus; Type Ⅱ: Lactobacillus crispatus-dominant; Type Ⅲ: Co-dominance of Lactobacillus iners & Lactobacillus jensenii; Type Ⅳ: Co-dominance of Aeromonas & Lactobacillus crispatus; Type Ⅴ: Dominance of Lactobacillus iners-dominant (Fig. 3).

Multivariable logistic regression using type II as reference revealed significantly elevated GDM risk in type I (OR = 2.60, 95%CI:1.17–5.78) and type IV (OR = 3.85, 95%CI:1.80–8.27). Following adjustment for age, education level, parity and gestational weeks at enrollment, type I (aOR = 2.83, 95%CI:1.25–6.40) and type IV (aOR = 3.87, 95%CI:1.77–8.49) both associations remained significant. After further adjusted the pre-pregnancy BMI, FPG and glycated hemoglobin on the basis of Model 2, type IV maintained a significant association (aOR = 3.02, 95%CI:1.27–7.15), whereas type I showed no significance (aOR = 2.42, 95%CI:0.99–5.93) (Table 4).

The abundance of Aeromonas alone showed modest discriminative capacity for GDM with an AUC of 0.629 (95%CI:0.541–0.718), a PPV of 41.2%, and an NPV of 83.1%. This performance was substantially improved after integration with LPA-based typing, resulting in an AUC of 0.775 (95% CI: 0.716–0.835), a PPV of 61.5%, and an NPV of 86.4% (Fig. 4a-b).

Discussion

Our longitudinal study reveals the relationships between early-pregnancy vaginal microbial and subsequent development of GDM. Specifically, the results showed that the vaginal microbiome of pregnant women who later developed GDM exhibited increased alpha diversity and was enriched with specific genera, including Aeromonas and Escherichia. The vaginal microbiota in early pregnancy, which is characterized by Co-dominance of Aeromonas & Lactobacillus crispatus was associated with a higher risk of GDM. The risk prediction model for the occurrence of GDM based on the classification of vaginal microbiota types was more effective.

While prior cross-sectional studies have consistently identified Firmicutes (phylum), Lactobacillus (genus), and Lactobacillus crispatus (species) as predominant in both GDM and non-GDM groups, we revealed distinct microbial signatures characterized by elevated Actinobacteriota and Bacteroidota alongside reduced Firmicutes abundance. This contrasts with a report by Cortez et al., which found Firmicutes was more abundant in the GDM group and Actinobacteriota in the non-GDM group [12]. Regarding the alpha diversity of the vaginal microbiota, we observed a significant difference in the Chao1 index between the two groups, whereas the Shannon and Simpson indices showed no statistical significance. This suggests that while microbial richness differed, the evenness of the communities was comparable. The elevated richness in the GDM group may indicate a disruption of the protective Lactobacillus-dominated state, which could be interpreted as a sign of vaginal dysbiosis potentially associated with or preceding GDM development. However, this finding contrasts with several previous reports [12, 16, 17]. Wang et al. found that the differences in the alpha diversity analyses between the two groups were not statistically significant (P > 0.05) [16]. Qiao and Cortez showed that the diversity and species abundance of vaginal microbiota in GDM decreased [12, 17]. These inconsistencies may be caused by the differences in cross-sectional design and sampling in the middle and late stages of pregnancy. As nugent score testing is not routinely conducted in Chinese clinical laboratories, it also limits our further exploration of the diverse changes in vaginal microbiota in pregnant women with GDM.

Based on the differences in composition and structure of two groups of vaginal microbiota, we explored the genera of vaginal bacteria associated with GDM through the random forest model. The results showed that the contribution of Aeromonas in vaginal was the highest in distinguishing the vaginal microbiota difference between the GDM and non-GDM groups, followed by Escherichia_Shigella, Streptococcus, and Lactobacillus. Aeromonas is generally not regarded as a resident member of the vaginal microbiota but rather as an opportunistic pathogen. It has been reported that associated with bacteremia [18] and urinary tract infections [19], suggesting that it is a noteworthy microorganism capable of impacting health. We speculate that the increase in glycogen content in vaginal epithelial cells related to GDM will disrupt the original stable flora structure dominated by Lactobacillus, thereby providing an ecological niche for the proliferation and colonization of non-dominant bacteria such as Aeromonas [20]. The presence of Escherichia_Shigella and Streptococcus may indicate the occurrence of inflammation. Microbiota dysbiosis may instigate localized inflammation, which may subsequently propagate into a systemic state via the circulation of pro-inflammatory mediators [21]. Evidence from cohort studies supports a association between a chronic low-grade inflammatory status in pregnant women and an elevated risk of developing GDM [22]. The proposed mechanism involves the impact of inflammation on the pathways regulating glucose metabolism [22]. Existing studies have shown that pregnant women who develop GDM in the second or third trimester may have altered insulin secretion and increased endogenous glucose production in the body before and during pre-pregnancy, coupled with increased obesity rates, which allows for enhanced inflammatory response in the body, which may contribute to alterations in vaginal microecology, causing it to show characteristic changes in the early stages of pregnancy [23–25].

A prediction model was established with the relative abundance of Aeromonas. The results showed that the area under the ROC curve (AUC = 0.629, 95% CI: 0.541–0.718) indicated that the abundance of Aeromonas alone was poorly predictive of the risk of developing GDM. To explore a better prediction model, vaginal bacterial types were used to establish a joint prediction model. It was found that compared with the Lactobacillus crispatus-dominant type, vaginal microbial types characterized by high Aeromonas with low Lactobacillus and those co-dominated by Aeromonas and Lactobacillus crispatus exhibited significantly elevated GDM risks. The microbial typing model also showed fairly predictive efficacy. In this study, it was found that both the single Aeromonas and the co-dominated by Aeromonas-Lactobacillus crispatus suggest the potential significance of Aeromonas. Although Aeromonas is not a recognized component of the vaginal microbiota, previous studies have also found Aeromonas in the vaginal microbiota of women [26, 27]. Based on the pollution control in the previous laboratory, we speculate that this might be due to the low-abundance colonization of Aeromonas. It might also be based on the relatively high sensitivity of the sequencing platform. Therefore, a large sample of the population is needed to verify whether Aeromonas can be routinely detected or whether there may be potential detection contamination of Aeromonas. It is important to note that the predictive efficacy of the microbiota-based model, though informative, is constrained by the lack of integration with other known risk factors. The modest PPV (61.5%) suggests that a diagnosis based solely on microbial features would yield a substantial number of false positives. Therefore, vaginal microbiota status may be better conceptualized as a contributing risk factor rather than a standalone diagnostic marker. Subsequent research integrating these insights with comprehensive clinical data holds the potential to significantly improve early risk stratification for GDM. Furthermore, the PPV and NPV in this study were derived from a 1:3 in a nested case-control study and do not reflect the model’s predictive performance in real-world populations.

The strengths of this study are that the association between the abundance of vaginal Aeromonas and GDM in early pregnancy was revealed, and the process of predicting the risk of GDM based on vaginal microbiota analysis was explored for the first time. Meanwhile, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the observational design precludes the establishment of a causal relationship. It remains possible that subclinical metabolic disturbances preceding the diagnosis of GDM may have already induced alterations in the vaginal microbiota at the time of sampling. Second, our findings are based on vaginal samples collected at a single time point in early pregnancy, which does not capture the dynamic changes in the microbiome throughout gestation. Furthermore, the study cohort was exclusively composed of Chinese pregnant women, which may limit the generalizability of our results to other ethnic populations.

Conclusions

Our analysis revealed alterations in vaginal microbial ecosystems among GDM cases compared to non-GDM controls in the first trimester. The GDM-associated microbiota demonstrated elevated alpha diversity indices accompanied by substantial enrichment of conditional pathogens, particularly Aeromonas. Notably, the high-risk microbial signature characterized by co-dominated by Aeromonas and Lactobacillus crispatus type had an increased risk of GDM. vaginal microbiota typing analysis demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy than single bacteria, which may aid GDM risk prediction and provide a microbiological basis for early screening for GDM.
