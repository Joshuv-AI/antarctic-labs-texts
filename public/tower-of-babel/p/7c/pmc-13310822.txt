Association of cerebrovascular center volume with patient outcomes
Authors: Stacey Quintero Wolfe, Justin R Mascitelli, Kyle Fargen, Peter Kan, Ketan Bulsara, Christopher P Kellner, Brian M Howard, Michael R Levitt, Rocco Armonda, Joshua Osbun, Clemens Schirmer, Mark Bain, William J Mack, Stavropoula Tjoumakaris, Adam Arthur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrent guidelines acknowledge the importance of both microsurgical and neuroendovascular expertise in the treatment of cerebrovascular disease. To achieve optimal care for these patients, it is becoming increasingly evident that procedural volumes impact patient outcomes. This is demonstrated across various cerebrovascular diseases.In this literature review, we demonstrate the association of volume with patient outcomes across multiple cerebrovascular disease states and interventions. Microsurgical aneurysm clipping remains essential even with the rise of endovascular therapy and mortality and patient-safety indicator events are clearly lower at high-volume centers for both ruptured and unruptured aneurysms. Likewise, patients undergoing surgery for cerebrovascular malformations and carotid endarterectomy have significantly better outcomes in high-volume centers by high-volume surgeons. Studies of mechanical thrombectomy and carotid stenting reinforce the association between higher procedural volumes, decreased mortality, and improved outcomes across various intervention modalities. The integration of neurocritical care has further improved outcomes, with specialized units demonstrating reduced mortality rates and lengths of stay.Modern cerebrovascular and stroke care necessitates comprehensive care by experienced providers in high-volume centers to optimize patient outcomes for both hemorrhagic and ischemic cerebrovascular disease. The relationship between higher institutional and provider case volumes and better patient outcomes is clearly delineated in the literature. Maintaining high-volume standards for endovascular and microsurgical cerebrovascular care can help ensure high-quality care across centers.Keywords: Arteriovenous malformations, Carotid stenosis, Cerebral aneurysms, Intracerebral hemorrhage, Mechanical thrombectomy, Clinical outcomes, Volume-outcome relationship

Introduction

Comprehensive and multidisciplinary care is essential for the treatment of cerebrovascular disease in stroke centers across the United States. Stroke patients must have access to multimodality expertise for stroke care, including hemorrhagic disease (cerebral aneurysms, arteriovenous malformations, intracerebral hemorrhage, etc.) and ischemic disease (acute ischemic stroke, carotid stenosis, etc.). Both microsurgical and endovascular expertise are critical for optimal, high-quality patient care, with substantial evidence that high case volumes are critical to better patient outcomes. This review highlights the ongoing need for open cerebrovascular surgery and volume-outcome relationships across the breadth of cerebrovascular disease.

Methods

This study employed a non-systematic literature review approach to examine and synthesize the existing body of knowledge on the relationship of cerebrovascular case volume and patient outcome. Relevant literature was identified through targeted searches of PubMed for (1) Cerebral Aneurysms; (2) Cerebrovascular Malformations; (3) Mechanical Thrombectomy; (4) Extracranial Carotid Disease; (5) Intracerebral Hemorrhage; and (6) Neurointensive Care. Search terms such as “procedure volume,” “case volume,” “institutional experience,” “patient outcome,” and “clinical outcome” were utilized for each disease subtype. The results were organized by disease subtype. As there are no currently accepted universal definitions of “high” vs. “low” surgical volume for each cerebrovascular disease, volumes were reported according to definitions and results from each study, reported in the Results section for each of the 6 studied cerebrovascular disease states.

Results

Cerebral aneurysms

Microsurgical (MS) clipping remains an important component of aneurysm management. Although the results of trials such as ISAT [1] and BRAT [2] favored endovascular therapy (EVT) over MS for ruptured aneurysms, MS still plays an important role in aneurysm treatment. The recently published 2023 AHA/ASA guidelines [3] for management of aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) contain treatment-related recommendations that continue to include MS in addition to EVT:

For patients with aSAH deemed salvageable and with depressed level of consciousness due to large intraparenchymal hematoma, emergency clot evacuation should be performed to reduce mortality (Class of Recommendation (COR) 1, Level of Evidence (LOE) B-R).

For patients with aSAH deemed salvageable and with depressed level of consciousness due to large intraparenchymal hematoma, emergency clot evacuation should be performed to reduce mortality (Class of Recommendation (COR) 1, Level of Evidence (LOE) B-R).

For patients with aSAH, the ruptured aneurysm should be evaluated by specialist(s) with endovascular and surgical expertise to determine the relative risks and benefits of surgical or endovascular treatment according to patient and aneurysm characteristics (COR 1, LOE C-EO).

For patients < 40 years of age with aSAH, clipping of the ruptured aneurysm might be considered the preferred mode of treatment to improve durability of the treatment and outcome (COR 2b, LOE C-LD).

In the case of unruptured aneurysms, the CURES trial [4] demonstrated superior angiographic outcomes and similar clinical outcomes for MS compared to EVT and concluded that “surgical clipping is more effective than endovascular treatment of unruptured intracranial aneurysms in terms of the frequency of the primary outcome of treatment failure.” The primary outcome in this trial was a radiographic (not clinical) outcome. Nonetheless, for both ruptured and unruptured aneurysms, MS continues to play a role in patient management.

For ruptured aneurysms, non-randomized studies from the US and around the world have shown that mortality is reduced when care is provided by experienced cerebrovascular surgeons in hospitals with larger volumes and with dedicated neurocritical care units, as was highlighted in the 2023 AHA/ASA Guidelines Systems of Care Section [3]. Evidence from the US Nationwide Inpatient Sample (NIS) of over 10,000 patients demonstrated lower risk of in-hospital mortality and higher odds of a good functional outcome for patients with ruptured cerebral aneurysms treated at high-volume centers (> 20 cases per year). [5] A nationwide retrospective cohort study from Japan including nearly 30,000 patients demonstrated a significant reduction in mortality for MS patients treated at high-volume centers (> 14 MS cases per year). [6] There were also volume-related benefits for EVT of ruptured aneurysms, though less substantial than those from MS treatment. Data from a multi-center European database including nearly 10,000 patients demonstrated that intermediate (41–70 cases per year) and high-volume centers (> 70 cases per year) had lower case-fatality for ruptured aneurysms regardless of treatment modality. [7] These large registries demonstrate the relationship between volume and outcome in the treatment of ruptured aneurysms.

With respect to unruptured aneurysms, additional data support the influence of higher case volumes on MS outcomes. An analysis from the US NIS of over 65,000 patients with unruptured aneurysms demonstrated that poor outcome, mortality, and patient safety indicator events (including pressure ulcers, retained surgical item, perioperative hemorrhage, pulmonary embolism, sepsis) were all significantly decreased at institutions achieving the volume thresholds for Joint Commission Comprehensive Stroke Center certification. [8] An additional retrospective study from a single center in Germany demonstrated improved outcomes compared to a historical cohort, highlighting improved outcomes with increased caseload and the importance of maintaining sufficient surgical volume in providing quality care. [9] Thus, the relationship between volume and outcome exists in treatment of unruptured aneurysms as well.

Cerebrovascular malformations

The volume-outcome relationship has been documented in the treatment of cerebrovascular malformations, such as arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) and cavernous hemangiomas. A study of 8227 patients in the US NIS database between 2000 and 2009 assessed the relationship between case volume (both per-hospital and per-surgeon) and outcomes, measured in terms of mortality, complications, length of stay (LOS), hospital costs, and cost-effectiveness. [10] Treatment modality (MS, EVT, and radiosurgery) was also analyzed. The study found that high-volume hospitals (≥ 16 cases/year) had shorter LOS (2 vs. 3 days, p = 0.005), more frequent discharge to home (p < 0.001), and lower mortality rates (0.7% vs. 1.16%, p = 0.010) when compared with low-volume hospitals (≤ 2 cases/year).10 Similarly, high-volume surgeons (≥ 7 cases/year) had shorter LOS (2 vs. 3 days, p = 0.03), more frequent discharge to home (p < 0.001), and lower mortality rates (0.7% vs. 1.10%, p = 0.005), compared with low-volume surgeons (≤ 2 cases/year). These outcomes were true across all treatment modalities.

The authors modeled how outcomes might improve if care were consolidated at high-volume centers and centers staffed by high-volume surgeons. Their models showed that on an annual basis, care at high-volume hospitals would result in 18.5 fewer deaths, 1252.1 fewer hospital days, 182.7 more discharges home, 48.5 fewer medical complications, and 117.4 fewer perioperative complications. [10] Similarly, if care was centralized at centers staffed by high-volume surgeons, it could result in up to 27.4 fewer deaths, 10,713.7 fewer hospital days, 370.9 more routine discharges, reduced complications, and a $51.6-million reduction in charges annually. [10] The same group also published similar volume-outcome relationships in cerebrovascular disease using more recent national databases and ICD-10 codes. [11].

Mechanical thrombectomy

Similar to the volume-outcome relationships seen in percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for acute myocardial infarction, [12–14] there exists a direct correlation between endovascular volume and patient outcomes in acute ischemic stroke and mechanical thrombectomy (MT), both for individual practitioners and for institutions. [15] Mortality, patient outcomes, procedural efficacy, morbidity, and complications have all been shown to be superior in patients undergoing MT at high-volume centers. [16–22] A recent 2023 study of the Medicare Provider Analysis and Review database showed a significant positive correlation between MT volume and home discharge (r = 0.88; 95% CI 0.58 to 0.97, p = 0.001) as well as mortality (r = − 0.86; 95% CI − 0.97 to − 0.49, p = 0.002). [16] A study of 2016–2017 Medicare data showed that higher MT volume reduced mortality by 4% per 10 interventionalist cases and had 2% lower odds of inpatient mortality for every 10 hospital cases. [17] Higher-volume stroke centers have shown faster times to treatment, higher reperfusion rates, and higher rates of good clinical outcomes.19.

A recent analysis of the 2020 NIS mirrors these findings and demonstrates a 2.6% reduction in odds of mortality per every 10 hospital MT cases (odds ratio [OR] (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.97 (0.96, 0.99), p = 0.001) (Wolfe SQ, abstract presented at AANS 2024). Institutions performing ≤ 100 MT annually have significantly greater mortality (OR [95% CI]: 1.22 [1.01, 1.50]), corresponding to a 22% increase in likelihood of death (p = 0.045) after controlling for age, race, hospital size, region, and teaching hospital status. Of note, 25% of the 600 institutions in this analysis performed < 20 MT annually. Current guidelines for PCI require 200 cases per institution annually (400 in the U.K. [23]). For comparison, US guidelines for minimum operator PCI volume require at least 50 procedures annually averaged over 2 years. [24].

Furthermore, all randomized controlled trials that demonstrated a benefit for MT over medical management were conducted at high-volume stroke centers, leading the primary investigators of the 2015 major trials to argue the trial results are generalizable only to other established, efficient, high-volume stroke centers. This question of generalizability needs further investigation, but there are no current data to support directing patients with potential ELVO to low-volume MT centers, [] though access to care must be strategized to ensure that high-volume centers are not clustered in the same area but spread across regions. While a complex issue, the preponderance of data support maintaining or increasing minimum MT case volumes to promote quality care and ensure best patient outcomes.

Extracranial carotid disease

Treatment of extracranial carotid stenoocclusive disease at high-volume centers is associated with improved outcomes for both carotid endarterectomy (CEA) and carotid artery stenting (CAS), including decreased mortality, stroke, and myocardial infarction (adjusted OR 0.76 [95% CI 0.60–0.96]). Overall hospitalization costs decrease by $2,000 per patient on average (95% CI 1,800-2,300) [25].

A study of the US NIS found that low CEA case volume was an independent predictor of poor outcome (stroke or death). [26] Specifically, an annual volume of ≥ 20 CEAs was linked to a significant reduction in poor outcomes after CEA for asymptomatic carotid stenosis. Of concern was a secondary analysis that found only 16% of surgeons met this threshold. Another study comparing outcomes of 24 CEA surgeons over a two-year period found that high-volume surgeons (defined as ≥ 30 CEAs per year) had significantly lower rates of perioperative stroke or death compared to surgeons with low (< 10) or medium (10–29) case volumes for both symptomatic (1.3% vs. 4.15%, p = 0.019) and asymptomatic carotid disease (0.51% vs. 4.6%, p = 0.0005). [27] A meta-analysis of 87 pooled studies found a significantly lower risk of perioperative stroke or death after CEA associated with higher surgeon and hospital volume [28]. The pooled adjusted OR of stroke or death was 0.50 (95% CI 0.28–0.87) and the pooled unadjusted relative risk (RR) was 0.59 (95% CI 0.42–0.83) in favor of high-volume surgeons, with similar effects seen in high compared to low-volume hospitals (pooled adjusted OR 0.62 [95% CI 0.42–0.90]; pooled unadjusted RR 0.68 [95% CI 0.51–0.92]. Taken together, these studies and others highlight the critical role that surgeon and hospital volume has on patient outcomes after CEA, particularly in serious adverse events, such as perioperative stroke and death.

Carotid artery stenting demonstrates a similar volume-outcome relationship. Individual provider CAS volume < 20 per year was associated with higher adjusted risks of 30-day morality. [29] A study of 36,128 patients with asymptomatic carotid stenosis undergoing CAS at high volume centers (≥ 79 procedures annually) demonstrated reduced 30-day and 90-day readmission rates. [30] The recent results of the CREST2 Trial demonstrating benefit for CAS over medical management (perioperative stroke and death and ipsilateral stroke within 4 years 2.8% (CI, 1.5–4.3) with stenting vs. 6.0% (CI, 3.8–8.3) medical management (P = 0.02), without significant benefit after CEA, may change the landscape of asymptomatic carotid disease treatment over the next number of years. Although CAS has gained further acceptance after trials such as CREST2 and ACST-2 for asymptomatic stenosis, [31, 32] CEA remains the gold standard across symptomatic carotid stenosis [33, 34]. It is important to discuss CAS, CEA and trans-carotid artery revascularization separately, rather than grouping these disparate modalities together as “carotid artery procedures”. Modern comprehensive stroke care requires that each center have expertise on each treatment modality, as no single procedure modality is able to treat all carotid stenosis lesions.

Intracranial hemorrhage

Treatment of patients with spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) is complex and requires coordination with multidisciplinary surgical, critical care, and interventional teams. Post hoc analysis of major trials (CLEAR-III and MISTIE-III) [35, 36] revealed that over 40% of ICH survivors with initial severe disability recover to acceptable outcome (defined as modified Rankin Scale [mRS] 0–3) at 1 year. Factors associated with poor outcome included uncontrolled diabetes (aOR 0.5 for favorable outcome), low cerebral perfusion pressure (< 60 mm Hg, aOR 0.3), sepsis (aOR 0.05), and prolonged mechanical ventilation (aOR 0.96). [37] These data suggest that critically ill ICH patients can achieve acceptable outcomes when managed by highly experienced, multidisciplinary teams. Another study of nearly 37,000 patients with ICH found that patient management in a dedicated multidisciplinary neurointensive care unit was associated with a 3.4-fold decrease in mortality over treatment in a non-neurointensive care unit [38], a trend confirmed in other large studies. [39, 40] NIS data from 2020 reveals a 23% mortality rate for hospitals that treat ≤ 25 ICH patients per year, compared to only 19% in hospitals treating ≥ 25 ICH cases annually (p < 0.05).

The results of the ENRICH trial [41] demonstrated superior patient outcomes after surgical evacuation of lobar ICH in comparison to medical management. These data are changing the landscape of hemorrhagic stroke, with a need for acute availability of surgical expertise at stroke centers to provide quality care for ICH patients in an evolving paradigm of “time is brain” to improve patient outcomes.

Neurointensive care

Implementation of the subspecialty of neurointensive care has contributed to improved outcomes, with specialized units demonstrating reduced mortality rates and lengths of stay. [42] Integration of neurointensivists, specialized nurses, dedicated pharmacists, advanced practitioners, and a physical unit resourced for complex critical care has allowed for improved care and outcomes in the spectrum of hemorrhagic and ischemic stroke. For example, neurointensive units reduced LOS, mortality, discharge to home or rehabilitation and a higher likelihood for treatment for aSAH. [43, 44] Well-coordinated care for the aSAH patient is a resource-demanding requirement which distinguish large-volume tertiary centers. Many poor outcomes in this patient population are related to the multisystem complications that accompany aSAH, such as infection, fever, acute lung injury, and vasospasm. [45] Benefit in acute ischemic stroke and hemorrhagic stroke patients, with reduction of resource utilization and improved outcomes, is also seen [46].

Limitations

This is non-systematic review including a heterogeneous body of literature ranging from institutional retrospective reviews to quality databases. A meta-analysis and/or systematic review would provide more robust and reliable results. Similarly, many of the volume cutoffs used in each reference vary from study to study, which makes it challenging to develop universal cutoff points of high vs. low volume. Lastly, we were unable to find granular data regarding individual surgeon experience / volume, which may be as important as institutional experience / volume.

Conclusion

The evolving landscape of cerebrovascular disease underscores the pivotal role of comprehensive, multidisciplinary care that involves microneurosurgical, neuroendovascular, and neurointensive care to provide optimal treatment outcomes. Across the breadth of cerebrovascular conditions, including cerebral aneurysms, cerebrovascular malformations, ischemic stroke, carotid disease, and intracerebral hemorrhage, the expertise and procedural volume of treating centers and providers appear to have an association with patient outcomes. Maintaining high-volume standards for endovascular and microsurgical cerebrovascular care can help ensure high-quality care across centers.
