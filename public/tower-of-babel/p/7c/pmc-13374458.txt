Multicentre international audit of IMACS malignancy screening guidelines in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies: insights from the myositis audit and research collaborative group
Authors: Jessica Day, Lekshmi Minikumari Rahulan, Samuel Katsuyuki Shinjo, Fernanda Oliveira de Andrade Lopes, Akira Yoshida, Shintaro Yamamoto, Maria Rosa Pellico, Shereen Oon, Ian Teh, Pallavi Vij, Iris Tang, Edmund Wong, Aman Kumar, Nicoletta Del Papa, Roberto Caporali, Ho So, Vahed Maroufy, Masataka Kuwana, Vikas Agarwal, Latika Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConsensus guidelines for malignancy screening in idiopathic inflammatory myopathy (IIM) were recently published by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies (IMACS) working group. This international multicentre study audited historical cancer screening practices against IMACS recommendations and examined the distribution of observed malignancies across IMACS-defined risk categories in a real-world cohort.MethodsThis retrospective study included patients with IIM from eight centres across seven countries/regions. Patients were stratified using IMACS-defined cancer risk categories. Historical screening was compared with the guideline recommendations. Multivariable logistic regression identified predictors of cancer and underscreening.ResultsOf the 795 patients (72.3% female), 51.7%, 37.9% and 10.4% were classified as high risk, moderate risk and standard risk, respectively. Across all sites, few high-risk (6.2%), moderate-risk (2.7%) and standard-risk (11.1%) patients underwent a full panel of IMACS-recommended tests. Within 3 years of diagnosis, 86 patients (10.8%) developed malignancy. Among observed cancers, 77.4% occurred in high-risk and 22.6% in moderate-risk patients, with none detected in the standard-risk group. Anti-transcription intermediary factor 1-gamma (TIF1γ) positivity (OR 4.64, 95% CI 2.35 to 9.16, p<0.001), smoking (OR 3.00, 95% CI 1.64 to 5.50, p<0.001), night sweats (OR 12.76, 95% CI 2.72 to 59.87, p=0.001) and increasing age (OR 1.05 95% CI 1.03 to 1.07 per year, p<0.001) were independently associated with cancer, whereas anti-Mi2 antibodies and non-dermatomyositis subtypes were protective. Cancer risk increased with each additional high-risk feature (OR 1.70) and decreased with each low-risk feature (OR 0.76). Underscreened individuals had lower rates of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (5.6% vs 14.3%, p=0.005) and anti-TIF1γ positivity (6.8% vs 31.4%, p<0.001).ConclusionObserved cancer distribution across IMACS risk categories was consistent with the framework’s stratification logic, but differential screening intensity precludes formal assessment of predictive validity. Variation between historical practice and current recommendations underscores the need for continued education and prospective longitudinal studies to assess cumulative risk, age thresholds, screening uptake and outcomes in real-world clinical settings.

Introduction

Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) are a heterogeneous group of multisystem autoimmune diseases characterised primarily by a predilection for skeletal muscle inflammation, often accompanied by a range of extramuscular manifestations. The major clinical subtypes include dermatomyositis (DM), polymyositis (PM), antisynthetase syndrome (ASyS), immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), overlap myositis (OM), inclusion body myositis (IBM) and juvenile-onset IIM (JIIM).

An increased risk of malignancy in adult-onset IIM, particularly in DM, is well established. This risk is highest within 3 years before or after an IIM diagnosis and is particularly pronounced in the presence of specific clinical features such as anti-TIF1γ antibodies.1 Cancer remains the leading cause of death among patients with IIM25 and malignancies are often diagnosed at an advanced stage.6 Accordingly, early identification of cancer is likely to improve survival and cancer-related outcomes in patients with IIM.

While the association of IIM with malignancy has been long-recognised, cancer screening practices in this population remain highly variable.7 In the absence of an international consensus, clinicians have relied on local practice patterns or individual discretion, resulting in considerable heterogeneity. Growing evidence on clinical and serological predictors of malignancy in IIM1 has underscored the need for standardised approaches. In response, the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies (IMACS) group published evidence-based and consensus-based recommendations in 2023.8 Under this framework, patients with IIM (excluding JIIM and IBM) are stratified into high-risk, moderate-risk and standard-risk categories based on demographic, clinical and serological features. All eligible patients are recommended to undergo a basic cancer screening workup consisting of comprehensive history, examination, routine blood tests, urinalysis and chest X-ray at IIM diagnosis. Moderate-risk or high-risk patients should additionally undergo enhanced screening, including dedicated imaging and tumour marker analysis.

This research addresses critical gaps in understanding how cancer incidence patterns and screening practices differ across socioeconomic contexts, where disparities in healthcare access and costs may influence both the feasibility of implementing comprehensive screening protocols and the underlying cancer risk profiles in patients with IIM.911 Here, we audit historical cancer screening practices against IMACS recommendations and describe the distribution of malignancies detected within 3 years of IIM diagnosis across IMACS-defined risk strata.

Materials and methods

Population and study design

We conducted a retrospective analysis of malignancy screening practices in patients with IIM. Data were collected from eight tertiary rheumatology centres across seven countries/regions (Japan, Australia, India, Hong Kong, the UK, Brazil and Italy). Patients (aged ≥18 years) diagnosed with IIM between 2014 and 2023 were included in this study. The study cohort was identified from institutional research registries and/or a search of electronic medical records using the International Classification of Diseases codes related to myositis. All patients fulfilled the 2017 European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR)/American College of Rheumatology classification criteria for IIM.12 Overlap myositis was defined as IIM with a concurrent systemic autoimmune rheumatic disease, such as systemic sclerosis, mixed connective tissue disease or Sjögren’s disease.13 Patients with juvenile onset myositis and those without sufficient data to perform cancer risk stratification were excluded. Patient demographics, clinical features, malignancy risk factors and details regarding malignancy screening tests were collected using a predesigned data extraction template. To ensure data standardisation, the Indian cohort used a structured glossary with predefined criteria for all clinical variables that was shared with other centres.12 Night sweats were documented as present if recorded at baseline assessment. Follow-up was retrospective, based on review of medical records to ascertain outcomes from cohort entry until last documented follow-up, cancer diagnosis or death. Myositis-specific antibodies (MSA) and myositis-associated antibodies (MAA) were tested by commercially available line-blot immunoassays (Autoimmune Inflammatory Myopathy profile 16 antigens; EUROIMMUN, Germany, RNA immunoprecipitation (IP) and IP/immunoblotting assay) or enzyme-linked assays (MESACUP, MBL, Tokyo, Japan), with thresholds determined by local laboratories.

The occurrence, timing and type of cancer were recorded relative to IIM diagnosis. Each patient was retrospectively assigned a cancer risk category (standard, moderate or high) based on the 2023 IMACS guidelines.8 This risk stratification system incorporates demographic, clinical and serological risk factors to guide screening intensity. High-risk patients were defined as those with two or more high-risk features. Moderate-risk classification requires at least one high-risk feature or two intermediate-risk features. All other patients were considered to have a standard risk. In keeping with the IMACS framework, patients with IBM were not assigned to any cancer risk category, as they are considered not to be at an increased risk of developing cancer. Nonetheless, they were included in the analysis to allow evaluation of screening practices in populations for whom extensive screening is not guideline-supported. All patients with multiple MSAs or MAAs were considered in the risk stratification process and the factor with the highest risk was considered for risk stratification.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the degree of concordance between existing malignancy screening practices and the IMACS guideline recommendations across participating centres. Secondary outcomes included the distribution of patients across IMACS-defined risk categories, the observed cancer detection rate within each risk category (acknowledging non-uniform ascertainment), clinical and serological associations with detected cancer and predictors of cancer underscreening relative to guideline-recommended investigations. We did not aim to formally evaluate the diagnostic accuracy or predictive validity of the IMACS framework, as this would require uniform application of a reference standard across risk groups.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and as means with SD or median with IQR for continuous variables. Between-group comparisons were performed using χ2 tests for categorical data and the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous data.

Clinically relevant variables associated with cancer were considered for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression to identify independent predictors. These included sex, age, IIM subtype, selected MSAs and MAAs, smoking history and clinical features. Multivariable logistic regression was also used to identify predictors of malignancy underscreening, defined as fewer investigations than those recommended by the IMACS guidelines for the assigned risk category. Specifically, high-risk patients would be considered underscreened if they did not undergo both basic and enhanced screening tests at IIM diagnosis and annual basic screening during follow-up where applicable. Moderate-risk patients were classified as such if they did not receive both basic and enhanced screening at diagnosis, while standard-risk patients were classified as such if they did not undergo the full suite of basic screening investigations at diagnosis. For this analysis, covariates included age, sex, cancer risk category, fever, weight loss, night sweats, smoking, IIM subtype and recruitment site. ORs and 95% CIs were reported.

To quantify the risk of malignancy in patients post a diagnosis of IIM, compared with the general population, we calculated the standardised incidence ratio (SIR). Patients with a history of malignancy preceding the IIM diagnosis or diagnosed simultaneously (prevalent cases) were excluded from the analysis to ensure the assessment of incident risk. Person-years at risk were calculated for each patient starting from the date of IIM diagnosis. For patients who developed cancer, time at risk was censored at the date of malignancy diagnosis. For patients who remained cancer-free, time at risk was censored at the date of the last follow-up or death. This strict censoring method was employed to prevent survival bias (immortal time bias), ensuring that the follow-up duration after a cancer diagnosis did not artificially inflate the person-years. The SIR was calculated as the ratio of the observed number of incident malignancies (O) to the expected number of malignancies (E) in the general population. The expected number (E) was derived by multiplying the total person-years accumulated by the cohort by the reference incidence rate. Global incidence rates were derived from the GLOBOCAN 2022 database. A reference incidence rate of 300 per 100 000 person-years was applied, representing the conservative global average for the 50–59 years age group, consistent with the median age of the study cohort.13 Further sensitivity analyses were performed in cohorts who developed cancer after IIM diagnosis as well as in those who completed a minimum follow-up period of 3 years and developed an incident malignancy within 3 years of IIM diagnosis. Analyses were performed using R (Version 4.5.1), with p<0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 795 patients with IIM were identified from eight institutions across seven countries/regions (online supplemental figure 1). The mean age at diagnosis was 50.2±16.3 years, with 72.2% being female (table 1). The median duration of follow-up was 70.7 (40.0–106.7) months. The mean age at diagnosis was lowest in India and highest in Australia. The most common IIM subtype was DM (388/795, 48.8%), followed by ASyS (181/795, 22.8%), OM (86/795, 10.8%), PM (76/795, 9.6%), IMNM (50/795, 6.3%) and IBM (14/795, 1.8%). There were some regional variations in subtype distribution: IBM was more common in the Australian cohort, IMNM was more common in both the UK and Australia, while ASyS was most common in the Japan and Hong Kong groups.

Malignancy risk classification

Across all patients, 50.8% were classified as high risk for malignancy, 37.2% as moderate risk and 10.2% as standard risk. The remainder (1.8%) had IBM and were not considered at risk under the IMACS guidelines. The most prevalent high-risk feature overall was age >40 years at diagnosis (70.9%), followed by DM diagnosis (39.2%) (table 2). This pattern was consistent across all countries/regions, except for Brazil, where the most common high-risk factor was persistent disease activity despite immunosuppression (67.9%). Among the intermediate-risk and low-risk factors, male sex (27.8%) and interstitial lung disease (ILD) (42.3%) were the most frequently observed overall.

Among the high-risk patients (n=404), 47.9% had two high-risk factors, 26.8% had three, 14.5% had four, 8.8% had five and 2.0% had six. Notably, although ASyS and OM are considered ‘low-risk’ diagnoses under IMACS criteria, the majority of patients with ASyS/OM (75.7%, 202/267) were classified as moderate or high risk. This was largely driven by the age criterion, with 81.2% (164/202) of these patients aged >40 years at presentation. Strikingly, among moderate-risk to high-risk patients with ASyS/OM, 27.7% (56/202) were aged only 41–50 years, indicating that risk classification may be influenced by relatively modest increases in age.

Malignancy occurrence and risk factors

Across the entire cohort, a total of 91 cancers were detected at any time point, representing an overall malignancy prevalence of 11.4% (91/795). 10.8% of patients (86/795) were diagnosed with malignancy between 3 years prior to and 3 years following their IIM diagnosis, during a total follow-up of 5459.5 patient-years. This represented an incidence rate of 1.58 per 100 patient-years, although not all patients completed the full follow-up window. The most common cancer types were breast (19/86, 22.1%), lung (12/86, 14.0%), unknown primary (6/86, 7.0%) and melanoma (5/86, 5.8%), with a total of 25 different primary sites identified overall (online supplemental table 1). Based on the expected general population rates (16.4 expected cases), patients with IIM demonstrated a significantly elevated SIR of 5.25 (95% CI 4.20 to 6.48).

A sensitivity analysis was conducted restricting the cohort strictly to new cases detected after IIM onset (0–3 years postdiagnosis). Excluding patients who had cancer prior to IIM diagnosis, 61 malignancies were observed across 768 patients contributing 5328.1 person-years of follow-up (an incidence rate of 1.14 per 100 person-years). Compared with the expected background incidence (16.0 expected cases), this maintained a highly significant SIR of 3.82 (95% CI 2.92 to 4.90). This indicates that even when excluding pre-existing malignancies, patients with IIM face a nearly fourfold increased risk of developing cancer compared with the age-matched general population.

Another sensitivity analysis conducted in a cohort patients, strictly restricted to patients who completed a minimum follow-up of 3 years and developed an incident malignancy within 3 years of IIM onset. In 608 patients contributing 1822.8 person-years of follow-up, 58 incident malignancies were observed. Compared with the expected background incidence (5.47 expected cases), the SIR was remarkably elevated at 10.60 (95% CI 8.05 to 13.71). This demonstrates that when strictly evaluating patients with robust long-term follow-up and preventing potential survival or drop-out bias, the risk of incident malignancy in the early years following an IIM diagnosis is nearly 11-fold higher than the age-matched general population.

Clinical characteristics of patients with cancer

Clinical characteristics were compared between patients with cancer diagnosed within 3 years of IIM onset (n=86) and those without cancer during follow-up (n=709) (table 3). Patients who developed cancer were significantly older at IIM onset (62.3±12.8 vs 48.7±16.1 years, p<0.001), more likely to be male (41.9% vs 26.1%, p=0.003) and more likely to have night sweats (5.1% vs 0.9%, p=0.014), dysphagia (39.5% vs 24.0%, p=0.003) and a history of smoking (38.5% vs 16.0%, p<0.001).

Notably, nine patients wih ASyS were diagnosed with IIM-related malignancy; all presented with IIM at >55 years of age. None of the patients with OM developed malignancy. The other antibody profiles showed no significant differences.

In multivariable logistic regression (online supplemental table 2), increasing age at IIM onset (OR 1.05 per year, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.07, p<0.001), smoking history (OR 3.00, 95% CI 1.64 to 5.50, p<0.001) and night sweats (OR 12.76, 95% CI 2.72 to 59.87, p=0.001) were independent predictors of cancer. Compared with DM, those with ASyS (OR 0.22, 95% CI 0.09 to 0.54, p=0.001), IMNM (OR 0.31, 95% CI 0.10 to 0.97, p=0.045) and PM (OR 0.31, 95% CI 0.10 to 0.97, p=0.044) had significantly reduced risk. Anti-TIF1γ antibody positivity remained a strong independent predictor of cancer (OR 4.64, 95% CI 2.35 to 9.16, p<0.001), whereas anti-MDA-5 antibodies trended towards being protective (OR 0.30, 95% CI 0.08 to 1.07, p=0.064).

Distribution of detected cancers across IMACS risk categories

After retrospective application of the IMACS criteria, 75.6% (65/86) of patients who developed cancer within 3 years of IIM diagnosis were classified as high risk and 22.1% (19/86) as moderate risk. None of the patients classified as standard-risk developed cancer. Two patients with IBM, excluded from the IMACS risk categories, were nonetheless diagnosed with cancer (splenic marginal zone lymphoma and non-small cell carcinoma of the lung). Patients with detected cancer were more frequently classified as high risk (77.4% vs 48.6%, p<0.001) and less frequently as moderate risk (22.6% vs 39.7%, p=0.002) or standard risk (0.0% vs 11.6%, p<0.001), which were consistent with the stratification logic of the IMACS framework.

Cancer risk burden and malignancy

The cumulative burden and balance of IMACS risk features appeared to influence malignancy risk. Patients who develop cancer typically have multiple high-risk features (commonly 2–4) and few or no low-risk features (figure 1A). In contrast, patients without cancer often had few high-risk features, but frequently also had low-risk features (figure 1B). The distinction between these groups became more pronounced when smoking and night sweats were included in the analysis (figure 1C,D).

A logistic regression model incorporating risk factor counts confirmed that each additional high-risk IMACS feature increased cancer odds by 70% (OR 1.70, 95% CI 1.43 to 2.04, p<0.001), while each additional low-risk feature conferred a 24% reduction in odds (OR 0.76, 95% CI 0.63 to 0.91, p=0.003). Smoking (OR 3.17, 95% CI 1.84 to 5.44, p<0.001) and night sweats (OR 9.60, 95% CI 2.31 to 39.84, p=0.002) continued to be strong independent predictors in this model. These findings suggest that both the burden and relative balance of risk features modulate cancer risk in patients with IIM.

Alliance between established screening practices and IMACS recommendations

A marked discrepancy between the historical screening practices and the newly introduced IMACS guidelines was observed. Across all sites, few high-risk (6.2%), moderate-risk (2.7%) and standard-risk (11.1%) patients underwent a full panel of recommended tests (table 4). Frequently underused tests (performed in <50% of indicated cases) included cervical screening, mammography, cancer antigen (CA)-125, prostate-specific antigen, fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET)/CT, lower gastrointestinal tract endoscopy and nasoendoscopy (figure 2). Among high-risk patients followed for >1 year, few (5.8%, 22/382) underwent annual screening, as currently recommended.

Screening practices varied regionally. Of the total cohort, 73 patients underwent FDG-PET/CT without prior CT of the chest, abdomen or pelvis; this approach was most common in Hong Kong (48/73, 65.8%). Malignancy was detected in 12 (16.4%) patients screened with an upfront PET/CT. There was some evidence of overscreening among the moderate-risk (6.4%), standard-risk (11.1%) and IBM (14.3%) cohorts. Commonly overperformed tests in standard-risk cohorts included chest CT and abdomen/pelvis CT.

Discussion

This is the first multicentre, international audit of historical cancer screening practices in IIM benchmarked against the 2023 IMACS recommendations, and to describe the distribution of detected malignancies across IMACS-defined risk strata in a real-world cohort. Based on the retrospective application of the IMACS criteria, 51.7%, 37.9% and 10.4% of our cohort was classified as high risk, moderate risk and standard risk. Accordingly, under the current recommendations, 89.6% of our patients would have qualified for enhanced screening at the time of IIM diagnosis. The overall cancer detection rate within 3 years of IIM diagnosis in our cohort was 10.8%.

The observed distribution of detected cancers was consistent with the IMACS stratification logic, with the majority of malignancies occurring in patients retrospectively classified as high risk. However, this observation must be interpreted with caution: because screening intensity was strongly correlated with assigned risk category (high-risk patients underwent more investigations than standard-risk patients), occult malignancies may have been disproportionately missed in lower-risk groups. Patients who developed cancer were significantly more likely to have been classified as high-risk (77.4% vs 48.6%, p<0.001) and significantly less likely to be in the moderate-risk (22.6% vs 39.7%, p=0.002) or standard-risk categories (0% vs 11.6%, p<0.001). No malignancies were observed in the standard-risk group, although two patients with IBM, excluded from the IMACS framework, were diagnosed with cancer. This underscores the need for appropriate clinical vigilance in the excluded subtypes. Notably, emerging evidence suggests an association between IBM and T cell large granular lymphocytic leukaemia.14 Further prospective studies are required to clarify malignancy risk in this population.

Several individual components of the IMACS model demonstrated discriminatory capacity in this cohort. Increasing age, DM diagnosis and anti-TIF1γ positivity were all strongly associated with cancer. In addition, we evaluated clinical ‘red flags’ features referenced in the guideline but not formally integrated into the risk stratification algorithm. Smoking and night sweats were independently associated with cancer. Notably, anti- nuclear matrix protein 2(NXP-2) antibodies were not significantly associated with cancer in our cohort, aligning with recent meta-analyses1 15 suggesting that previously reported positive associations may reflect comparisons with general population rather than with anti-NXP-2-negative IIM controls.16 17 Similarly, persistent disease activity despite immunosuppression was not significantly predictive in our analysis, although this may reflect heterogeneity in how the criterion was interpreted across sites and investigators.

Features designated as ‘low risk’ in the IMACS guidelines, such as ASyS, OM, ILD, inflammatory arthritis and Raynaud’s phenomenon, were confirmed to be significantly less common among patients with malignancy in our cohort. We also noted that the anti-Mi2 antibody was independently protective against cancer in our adjusted model, while anti-MDA-5 demonstrated a strong trend towards protection that narrowly missed statistical significance. Importantly, our data indicate that cancer risk is influenced by individual high-risk features and by the cumulative balance of high-risk and low-risk factors, a nuance not captured in the current IMACS algorithm. Each additional high-risk feature increased the odds of malignancy by 70%, whereas each additional low-risk feature conferred a 24% risk reduction. This highlights the potential value of incorporating the net risk burden rather than relying solely on binary thresholds, although these observations are exploratory and must be interpreted cautiously given the retrospective design.

Our study revealed a marked discrepancy between the IMACS guidelines and historical real-world clinical practice. Most patients, irrespective of the risk category, did not receive the full suite of recommended screening tests. Indeed, only 6.2% of high-risk patients completed all tests and annual screening efforts were low. Screening practices also varied by region. For example, FDG-PET/CT was used routinely in Hong Kong, but rarely elsewhere. Interpretation of these variations should consider contextual factors including availability of investigations, prevailing clinical norms and healthcare system structure (universal healthcare models, mixed public-private systems, predominantly private systems and hybrid models). Countries/Regions with universal healthcare systems (Australia and Hong Kong) demonstrated higher screening completion, whereas those with predominantly fee-for-service models (India) showed lower screening intensity (OR 74.18). This aligns with the global literature demonstrating that healthcare financing mechanisms substantially impact preventive care access.18 We observed that sex disparities in screening were particularly pronounced in resource-constrained settings, echoing findings from the 2023 Lancet Commission on Women, Power and Cancer.1819 Together, these findings reflect that historical practice may be shaped by system-level factors and also underscore the importance of increasing understanding of malignancy screening principles in IIM. As dissemination and awareness of the IMACS recommendations increase, alignment with these guidelines is likely to improve over time in routine rheumatology practice.

Clinical practice guidelines are inherently iterative and refined as new evidence emerges. Our results highlight aspects of the IMACS risk stratification model that warrant further evaluation in prospective studies. For example, the age criterion classified some patients with otherwise low-risk phenotypes, such as ASyS and OM, into higher-risk categories. Among intermediate-risk to high-risk patients with ASyS/OM in our study, 27.7% were aged only 41–50 years, and none in this age group developed malignancy. Interactions between age and other clinical risk features therefore warrant further prospective assessment. Similarly, the observed influence of cumulative high-risk and low-risk features and the strong associations observed for red flag features support further rigorous longitudinal evaluation of how these factors may best inform risk assessment.

Screening feasibility should also be considered. Our results indicate that the full screening protocol appears far removed from current practice, even in major tertiary centres. The challenge is likely to be even greater in regional or resource-constrained settings. Rationalising the screening algorithm to prioritise high-yield features may enhance feasibility and adherence. In some settings, FDG-PET/CT has been adopted as a streamlined first line investigation. Although cost and availability limit global applicability, FDG-PET/CT offers several advantages including cancer detection along with evaluation of assessment of IIM-related inflammatory activity.15 PET-CT may also offer diagnostic and economic advantages over multimodal testing, particularly when indirect costs (such as travel, missed work) are considered.16 Survey data also show that patients prefer simplified screening pathways and many express willingness to bear higher out-of-pocket costs for FDG-PET/CT over traditional panels.17 Future research should evaluate the comparative effectiveness and patient acceptability of single FDG-PET/CT-based strategies versus multimodal screening approaches.

A fundamental limitation of this study is the non-uniform ascertainment of cancer outcomes across IMACS risk categories. As demonstrated in our audit, screening intensity correlated strongly with assigned risk, meaning that patients in lower-risk categories underwent fewer investigations and may have had undetected malignancies. This differential verification precludes formal assessment of the sensitivity, specificity or predictive validity of the IMACS framework, and the observed cancer distribution across risk strata should be interpreted descriptively rather than as evidence of diagnostic performance. A prospective study applying a uniform reference standard across all risk categories would be required to formally evaluate the predictive accuracy of the IMACS stratification system. As a retrospective analysis, standardisation of certain variables, particularly evaluation of persistent disease activity and patient-reported symptoms such as night sweats, was challenging. Furthermore, screening performed in primary care settings may not have been consistently captured. Detailed data on immunosuppressant use, including agent type, timing and duration, were not available, precluding adjustment for treatment-related effects. Some myositis-specific antibodies (eg, anti-Ha, anti-KS) were not routinely assessed, and patients with IBM were under-represented, likely reflecting the rheumatology-focused nature of case ascertainment. As an audit of real-world practice, lung CT was not routinely performed in all patients, which may have resulted in under-ascertainment of ILD. However, as ILD is classified as a low-risk feature within the IMACS framework, incomplete detection would not have altered risk categorisation or the primary findings of this study. Follow-up duration varied across patients. Cancer outcomes were identified through retrospective review of medical records rather than uniform systematic screening and occult malignancy or malignancy diagnosed outside the study centres cannot be excluded. In addition, deaths prior to cancer diagnosis could not be systematically ascertained across all centres and may have contributed to underascertainment.

Additional limitations include potential ascertainment bias due to the tertiary care setting, variation in healthcare system access across countries/regions and heterogeneity in data capture due to retrospective abstractions from medical records. The retrospective design also limited our ability to capture patient preferences and shared decision-making processes that may have influenced screening decisions. The potential inaccuracy in some variables such as night sweats, which were not systematically collected and verification is necessary in a prospective cohort study using standardised questionnaires. Nonetheless, the international, multicentre design and large sample size strengthen the generalisability of our findings.

Conclusions and future directions

Our findings provide a real-world audit of cancer screening practices in IIM benchmarked against the IMACS recommendations and describe the distribution of detected malignancies across risk categories. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate the clinical relevance of cumulative risk burden, age-based thresholds and the contribution of red-flag symptoms. Further implementation and outcomes studies will be important to assess screening uptake, patient experience and real-world impact across diverse healthcare settings, including resource-limited contexts.
