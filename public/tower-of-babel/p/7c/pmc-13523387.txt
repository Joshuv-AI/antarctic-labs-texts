AMF-mediated samarium tolerance in Avena sativa: insights into primary metabolism, amino acid biosynthesis, and flavonoid accumulation
Authors: Amar Ali Adam Hamad, Yousef Alhaj Hamoud, Marwa Yousry A Mohamed, Hiba Shaghaleh, Emad A Alsherif, Nadia Mohamed El-Shafey, Shereen Magdy Korany, Nashwa Hagagy, Yousef Salem Alfaifi, Maria Gabriela Maridueña-Zavala, Amani A S Abdelaziz, Mahmoud M Y Madany, NHS Mousa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSamarium (Sm), a rare earth element increasingly released into the environment through industrial activities and electronic waste, can adversely affect plant growth and metabolism. Although arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) are known to enhance plant tolerance to various abiotic stresses, their role in mitigating Sm toxicity and regulating metabolic pathways in oat (Avena sativa L.) remains poorly understood. This study investigated the physiological and metabolic mechanisms underlying AMF-mediated Sm tolerance in oat plants.MethodsA greenhouse pot experiment was conducted using oat plants grown under control conditions or exposed to Sm stress (125 and 250 mg kg⁻¹ soil), with or without Rhizophagus irregularis inoculation. Plant growth, grain yield, photosynthetic performance, nutrient status, Sm accumulation, carbohydrate metabolism, nitrogen-assimilating enzymes, amino acid profiles, organic and fatty acids, and secondary metabolites were analyzed.ResultsSm exposure markedly impaired plant performance, reducing grain yield by up to 60% and decreasing the photosynthetic rate by 43.2–60% compared to the control. Sm stress also disrupted nutrient acquisition and induced substantial metabolic imbalances. AMF inoculation significantly alleviated these adverse effects by reducing Sm accumulation in plant tissues (by 21.7% and 31.5% at the low and high Sm levels, respectively) and increasing grain yield by up to 33.9% compared with non-inoculated stressed plants. AMF enhanced carbohydrate metabolism (increasing glucose content and starch synthase activity) and stimulated nitrogen metabolism by increasing glutamine synthetase and glutamate dehydrogenase activities, leading to greater accumulation of several amino acids. AMF also promoted the accumulation of organic acids, fatty acids, and protective secondary metabolites (phenolics and tocopherols). Effects on phenolics, flavonoids, and antioxidant capacity were treatment-dependent, with AMF generally enhancing these parameters under non-stressed or low-Sm conditions while modulating the stress-induced responses at high Sm concentrations.ConclusionsAMF effectively mitigated Sm-induced toxicity in oat by limiting Sm accumulation, preserving photosynthetic function, enhancing primary metabolism, stimulating amino acid biosynthesis, and promoting protective secondary metabolites. These findings provide new insights into the metabolic basis of AMF-mediated tolerance to rare earth element stress and highlight the potential application of AMF as a sustainable strategy for crop production in contaminated soils.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09375-z.Keywords: Samarium toxicity, Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, Avena sativa, Rare earth elements, Metabolic reprogramming, Oxidative stress, Nutrient uptake, Oat

Introduction

Samarium (Sm) is a lanthanide rare earth element (REE) that occurs naturally in soils at relatively low concentrations, typically ranging from approximately 2 to 23 mg kg⁻¹ [1–3]. However, the rapid expansion of rare-earth mining, metallurgical processing, industrial manufacturing, and electronic-waste generation has increased the release of REEs, including Sm, into terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems [4, 5]. Recent studies have identified REEs as emerging contaminants, particularly in regions associated with rare-earth extraction and processing, where environmental concentrations may increase by several orders of magnitude above natural background levels. For example, soils surrounding rare-earth mine tailings have been reported to contain exceptionally high REE concentrations, reaching tens of thousands of mg kg⁻¹, while contaminated watersheds near mining and industrial areas frequently exhibit substantial enrichment of lanthanides compared with background environments. These contamination hotspots raise increasing ecological concerns because REEs can accumulate in soils, alter microbial communities, and become available for plant uptake [6]. Although low concentrations of some REEs may stimulate plant growth, excessive accumulation can induce phytotoxic effects, including inhibition of seed germination, disruption of photosynthesis, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysfunction. Consequently, understanding plant responses to Sm contamination has become increasingly important for assessing ecological risks and developing sustainable management strategies for REE-affected agricultural systems.

Oat (Avena sativa L.) is an economically important cereal crop cultivated worldwide for human consumption, livestock feed, and soil conservation purposes. Owing to its cultivation in diverse agroecosystems, oat may be exposed to increasing levels of soil contaminants, including rare earth elements. Sustainable approaches that enhance plant tolerance to contaminated soils are therefore of considerable interest. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) are widely recognized for their capacity to enhance plant tolerance to diverse abiotic stresses through multiple physiological and biochemical mechanisms [7]. Beyond improving water and mineral nutrient acquisition, AMF play critical roles in maintaining nutrient homeostasis, particularly nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P), which are essential for photosynthesis, protein synthesis, and energy metabolism [8–10]. Previous studies have demonstrated that AMF enhance N assimilation by stimulating key enzymes involved in N metabolism and improving nutrient-use efficiency under drought, salinity, ozone exposure, and heavy-metal stress. AMF also strengthen antioxidant defense systems by increasing the activities of enzymes such as superoxide dismutase, catalase, ascorbate peroxidase, and peroxidases, thereby limiting oxidative damage caused by excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation [11–13].

In addition to their effects on nutrient acquisition and antioxidant protection, AMF substantially influence plant metabolic reprogramming during stress adaptation. Enhanced carbon assimilation and carbohydrate metabolism in mycorrhizal plants provide energy and carbon skeletons required for the synthesis of stress-related metabolites [9]. Amino acids such as proline, arginine, glutamate, and phenylalanine play central roles in osmotic adjustment, ROS detoxification, nitrogen storage, and cellular protection under adverse conditions. Phenylalanine is particularly important because it serves as a precursor for the phenylpropanoid pathway, leading to the synthesis of phenolic compounds and flavonoids [14]. These secondary metabolites contribute to plant stress tolerance through antioxidant activity, metal chelation, membrane stabilization, and regulation of cellular redox homeostasis. Several studies have reported that AMF promote the accumulation of phenolics and flavonoids under drought, ozone, salinity, and heavy-metal stress, thereby enhancing plant resilience and recovery. Collectively, these findings suggest that AMF-mediated regulation of nutrient homeostasis, amino acid biosynthesis, and secondary metabolite production may represent important mechanisms for mitigating samarium-induced toxicity; however, these processes remain largely unexplored in oat plants exposed to rare-earth element stress.

To date, the physiological and metabolic mechanisms driving AMF-mediated tolerance to Sm toxicity in oat plants remain virtually unknown. In particular, the exact biochemical basis by which AMF regulates primary and secondary metabolism under Sm exposure has not been deeply explored. We hypothesized that AMF inoculation acts as a metabolic anchor that directly counters Sm phytotoxicity. We expected this symbiosis to restore metabolic homeostasis, upregulate defensive amino acid and flavonoid biosynthesis, and trigger robust antioxidant and detoxification networks. To test this hypothesis, we applied a wide array of comprehensive physiological, biochemistry, and untargeted multivariate metabolic analyses. The primary objectives of this study were to definitively evaluate how AMF protects oat growth under escalating Sm stress and to unravel the complex biochemical networks responsible for AMF-driven stress mitigation.

Materials and methods

Plant growth and treatments

Sterilized soil and sand (Tref EGO substrates, Moerdijk, The Netherlands) were mixed at a ratio of 70:30 (v/v), resulting in a substrate with a water-holding capacity of 68%. The pot experiment was conducted in the greenhouse. Oat (Avena sativa L.) seeds were obtained from the Agricultural Research Center, Giza, Egypt, and surface sterilized with 5% sodium hypochlorite for 20 min before sowing. Plants were grown in 10 × 8 cm pots containing approximately 800–900 g of the soil-sand mixture. A pure commercial inoculum of Rhizophagus irregularis (MUCL 41833) was obta.

ined from the Glomeromycota in vitro collection (GINCO) (www.mycorrhiza.be/ginco-bel) in a pot (25 × 15 cm). The mycorrhizal inoculum was added as 10 g of trapped soil per pot, corresponding to approximately 500 spores per pot (approx. 50 spores per gram of inoculum). Autoclaved inoculum in equal amounts was added to the control treatment to provide equivalent nutrients aside from mycorrhizal spores. The soil contained 11.9 mg C, 12.8 mg NO₃-N, 1.3 mg NH₄-N, and 9.4 mg P per gram of dry soil, with 0.33 g water/g soil, EC of 3.4 dS/m, pH 7.56, and 2.75 meq/L K. Water content was maintained at 60%. Plants were grown in a climate-controlled chamber (21/18°C day/night, 16/8 h photoperiod, 150 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ light, 60% humidity) and irrigated regularly with tap water. Samples were analyzed as three biological replicates per treatment, where a replicate consists of 4 pooled plants from the same pot. Six pots were used per treatment in a completely randomized design, with each pot containing 4 plants. The pot was considered the experimental unit, while the pooled samples from the four plants within that pot provided the technical replicates for biochemical analyses. Six treatments were applied: (1) Control (0 mg Sm/kg soil), (2) AMF only, (3) Sm (125 mg/kg), (4) Sm (250 mg/kg), (5) AMF + Sm (125 mg/kg) and (6) AMF + Sm (250 mg/kg). Samarium was applied in the form of samarium samarium(III) nitrate hexahydrate (Sm (NO3)3·6H2O), which was dissolved in deionized water and thoroughly mixed into the soil-sand substrate before planting. Sm doses were selected based on a pre-experiment testing various concentrations (0–500 mg/kg soil), identifying 125 and 250 mg/kg as moderate and severe levels (causing 35% and 65% biomass reduction, respectively). Growth chamber conditions for the pre-experiment included 320 µmol− 2⁻² s− 1 light intensity, 60/70% humidity, 22/18°C, and a 16/8 h photoperiod. Biomass was recorded after four weeks, and samples were stored at − 80 °C. Plants were grown for five weeks for biomass, physiological, and biochemical analyses (samples were snap-frozen and stored at − 80 °C). A parallel set of plants from the same experiment was grown to full maturity (15 weeks after sowing, until grains reached the hard dough stage) for the determination of grain yield components (seed size, weight of 100 seeds, and grain number per plant).

Determination of Sm

The concentration of Sm in plant seed extract was measured using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) at a wavelength of 359 nm, employing Thermo Jarrell Ash 25 and PerkinElmer Optima 3200RL (PerkinElmer, USA).

Yield and photosynthesis

We measured the seed size, the weight of 100 seeds, and the number of grains per plant after the experiment was complete. The LI-6400 (LI-COR, Lincoln, The Netherlands) photosynthesis system was used to calculate photosynthetic parameters. The LI-COR leaf chamber was utilized under the following parameters: block temperature of 22 ◦C, saturation photon flux density of 1500 µmol m− 2 s− 1, and 400 ppm CO2. Chlorophyll a and b extracted with acetone were measured [15].

Determination of organic sugars

50 mM TAE buffer (pH 7.5) containing a blend of polyclar (0.15%), Na azide (0.02%), PMSF (2 mM), β-mercaptoethanol (1 mM), mannitol (10 mM), and NaHSO₃ (12 mM) was used to extract the soluble sugars from oat seed. The mixture was heated to 90 °C and centrifuged at 15,000 × g. For five minutes, centrifugation was repeated at 14,000 × g and 4 °C. The sugars were then rinsed using ddH2O, and high-performance anion-exchange chromatography/pulsed amperometric detection was used to quantify the various sugars [16]. The techniques of [17] and [18] were used to calculate galactomannans.

Amino acids determination

To extract the amino acids, 100 mg of seed samples were homogenized in 80% aqueous ethanol for 1 min at 7000 × g, spiked with norvaline, and centrifuged for 20 min at 14,000 × g at 4 °C. Following the clear supernatant’s vacuum evaporation, the pellet was re-suspended in chloroform. The supernatant was separated from the pellet suspended in chloroform and centrifuged again after the residue was extracted again using HPLC-grade deionized water. Using a Millipore microfilter with a 0.2 μm pore size, the centrifuged aqueous phase (14,000 × g, 10 min) was filtered. A Waters Acquity UPLC-TQD system (Milford) and a BEH amide column were used to determine the quantity of amino acids [19]. The contents were expressed as mg g− 1 fresh weight.

Evaluation of the fatty acid profile

Fatty acids were extracted and quantified according to the procedure described by [20]. Briefly, 100 mg of seed tissue was extracted with methanol at room temperature until the tissue was fully discolored. Codeine and nonadecanoic acid were added as internal standards. For GC-MS analysis, a Hewlett Packard 6890 GC coupled to an MSD 5975 mass spectrometer equipped with an HP-5 MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) was used. Fatty acids were identified using the NIST 05 and the Golm Metabolome Database.

Antioxidant activities

Plant seeds (100 mg fresh weight) were ground in liquid nitrogen using a MagNALyser and extracted with 2 mL of 80% (v/v) ice-cold ethanol. The homogenate was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 25 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was used for subsequent assays. Tocopherols (α-, β-, and γ-) were quantified in shoot tissues by normal-phase HPLC using the procedure described in [21]. A PartiSil Pac 5 μm column (250 × 4.6 mm) was used with a Shimadzu system and fluorescence detection (excitation 290 nm, emission 330 nm). Results were expressed as µg g− 1 FW. Total phenolic content was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu assay [22]. Briefly, 100 µL of extract was mixed with 500 µL of 10% (v/v) Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and 400 µL of 7.5% (w/v) sodium carbonate. After incubation for 60 min at room temperature in the dark, absorbance was measured at 765 nm using a Shimadzu UV–Vis 1601 PC spectrophotometer. Gallic acid was used as the standard, and results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per g fresh weight (FW).

Total flavonoid content was quantified using the aluminum chloride colorimetric method [23]. An aliquot of 250 µL extract was mixed with 1.25 mL distilled water and 75 µL of 5% (w/v) NaNO₂. After 6 min, 150 µL of 10% (w/v) AlCl₃ was added, followed by 500 µL of 1 M NaOH after another 6 min. Absorbance was recorded at 510 nm. Quercetin was used as the standard, and results were expressed as mg quercetin equivalents (QE) per g FW.

FRAP (Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power) assay was performed according to the method described by [19, 20]. The FRAP reagent consisted of 0.3 M acetate buffer (pH 3.6), 10 mM 2,4,6-tripyridyl-s-triazine (TPTZ) in 40 mM HCl, and 20 mM FeCl₃ in a ratio of 10:1:1 (v/v/v). Fifty microliters of extract were mixed with 1.5 mL of FRAP reagent and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 593 nm. Results were expressed as µmol Trolox equivalents (TE) per g FW using a Trolox standard curve.

DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) radical scavenging activity was determined by mixing 100 µL of extract with 3.9 mL of 0.1 mM DPPH solution in methanol. Elemental analysis

Minerals were extracted from plant samples using an ICP-MS (Antwerp University) based on a solution of HNO3 in H2O (5:1 v/v) according to the previously established technique [21] for mineral detection.

Statistical analysis

Data of all parameters were subjected to the SPSS 16.0 program and expressed as mean±(SE). The homogeneity of variance was checked with Levene’s test, while normality of distribution was checked with Shapiro–Wilk tests before statistical analysis. To evaluate the main and interactive effects of Sm and AMF as fixed variables, two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied. Differences among treatment means were evaluated with Duncan’s multiple range test and significance was set at P < 0.05. Figures with histograms showing the response of biochemical analyses were generated using GraphPad Prism 8. Multivariate analyses as hierarchical clustering heatmap and principal component analysis (PCA) were performed using OriginPro (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA), Version 2025.3.

Results

AMF colonization

AMF-inoculated oat roots were highly colonized by Rhizophagus irregularis. Root colonization percentage reached 40.54% in the AMF-only treatment and decreased significantly with increasing samarium (Sm) concentrations to 32.18% and 23.68% under AMF + Sm 125 mg/kg and AMF + Sm 250 mg/kg, respectively (Table 1). No colonization was observed in non-inoculated treatments (Control, Sm 125 mg/kg, and Sm 250 mg/kg). Similarly, hyphal length and the number of arbuscules followed the same trend. The highest values were recorded in the AMF treatment (hyphal length: 18.15 cm g⁻¹; arbuscules: 5.0 no. cm⁻¹ root), with significant reductions under combined AMF + Sm treatments. Both parameters were completely absent in non-AMF-inoculated plants.

Samarium accumulation and grain yield components

Sm content in oat plants increased with increasing Sm concentrations in the soil, while AMF alleviated Sm accumulation in oat plants by 21.7% and 31.5% under lower and higher Sm concentrations, respectively (Table 2). Grain size, weight, and quantity were negatively impacted by both low and larg concentrationsof Sm. Grain size, weight, and number decreased by 24.1%, 33.1%, and 28.5%, respectively, at the lower concentration of Sm; however, the higher concentration showed greater decreases in these parameters than the lower concentration. The results indicated that AMF significantly improved the negative effect of Sm on various yield parameters in oat plants compared to those treated with Sm only. In this regard, AMF application enhanced seed size, weight, and grain number by 26.7%, 33.9%, and 28.8%, respectively, in comparison to those treated by Sm only at a lower concentration (Table 2). Furthermore, AMF reduced the adverse effects of the higher Sm concentration, improving grain number, weight, and seed size by 21.1%, 28.3%, and 16.6%, respectively.

Photosynthetic rate

To understand the reeducation in oat yield, photosynthesis as main drive for growth was measured. It was significantly lower (− 43.2% and − 60%) in plants treated with low and high concentrations of Sm, compared with the control (Table 2). Although this photosynthetic rate was significantly lower (− 43.2% and − 60%) compared to control plants under the treatments by Sm only at lower and higher concentrations, respectively (Table 2).

Primary metabolism

Sugar metabolism

AMF caused a significant increase in glucose content by 25.5% and a non-significant increase in starch content by 7.3% (Fig. 1a, b). Both low and high concentrations of Sm exhibited significant increases in glucose content by 77.2% and 68.2%, respectively. No significant changes were recorded in starch content when stressed by Sm concentration, either at the low or high concentration. AMF, when combined with the low concentration of Sm, caused a significant decrease by 16.7% and a significant increase by 32.3% under the higher concentration of Sm. The treatment with the low concentration of Sm alone caused a significant decrease in amylase activity by 20.6%, while treatment with the higher concentration of Sm exhibited a non-significant decrease of 3%. The treatments with AMF, either alone or combined with low and high Sm concentrations, caused significant increases in starch synthase activity by 59.8%, 20.5%, and 19.6%, respectively, when compared to the control.

Organic acid profiles

AMF alone caused significant increases in lactate, citrate, succinate, and oxalate by 34%, 33.2%, 34.6%, and 21.3%, respectively (Fig. 2). The treatment by the low concentrations of Sm caused a significant decrease in lactate, citrate, and succinate by 11.7%, 11.6%, and 12.1%, respectively. The treatment with the higher concentration of Sm exhibited significant increases in lactate, citrate, and succinate by 29.6%, 28.2%, and 31.2%, respectively. The treatments with AMF, when combined with either the low or high concentration of Sm, caused significant increases in lactate by 89.2% and 62.6%, respectively, when compared to those plants treated with Sm only. The same trends were observed for citrate and succinate (Fig. 2).

Amino acid metabolism

Amino acids are important markers of plant stress responses and adaptations because they are direct byproducts of nitrogen metabolism. Significant variations in amino acid composition were noted in both stressed and non-stressed circumstances (Fig. 3). When oat plants were grown under Sm stress, the levels of several measured amino acids (12 out of 20) increased significantly in comparison to control plants. Twelve of the twenty amino acids that were tested increased when treated with AMF alone; asparagine, alanine, and histidine showed the largest increases. Most of the measured amino acids showed greater increases under the treatment with the higher concentration of Sm alone than under the treatment with the lower concentration of Sm. When AMF was applied to both Sm treatments, the amounts of several amino acids significantly increased in comparison to untreated plants. The study highlighted the elevated levels of alanine, threonine, tyrosine, and arginine, which showed increases of approximately 135.2%, 50%, 30%, and 32.3%, respectively, under treatment with the low concentration of Sm compared to control plants. The aforementioned amino acids increased by 54%, 37.5%, 56.6%, and 54.9%, respectively, when exposed to a higher concentration of Sm.

Fatty acid profiles

Generally, treatment with AMF alone increased all measured fatty acids except dodecanoic acid (C12:0). The highest increases were recorded for docosanoic acid (C22:0), Hexadecenoic (C16:1) and tetradecanoic acid (C14:0) by 47.8%, 36.4% and 30%, respectively (Table 4). The treatment by low concentration of Sm caused decreasing in all measured fatty acids. Thirteen fatty acids showed significant decreases while the remaining 6 fatty acids showed non significantly decreases (Table 4). AMF alleviated the negative effect of Sm at low concentration and at the same time it caused significant increases for 13 fatty acids in comparison to control. The higher concentration of Sm exhibited increases in the levels of six fatty acids and decreases in the other 13 fatty acids. When AMF was combined with the high concentration of Sm, it caused significant increases in eleven fatty acids; these increases were greater than those caused by the high concentration of Sm alone (Table 4).

Total antioxidant capacity

At the same time, when AMF was applied to stressed plants, FRAP significantly increased by 9% and 12.5% at low and high concentrations of Sm, respectively. DPPH and ABTS significantly increased under the treatment of AMF alone by 14.7% and 16.2% at low and high concentrations of Sm, respectively (Fig. 5d, e). The lower concentration of Sm showed significant decreases in DPPH and ABTS by 16.5% and 20.7%, respectively. In contrast, the high concentration of Sm caused a significant increase of DPPH by 77.3% and a non-significant increase in ABTS by 4.2%. AMF, when combined with Sm, caused a significant decrease in DPPH by 15.3% the higher Sm concentration. Furthermore, as antioxidant compounds together clarify the physiological reactions of plants to abiotic stress, the current study evaluated their content. The results indicated that in non-stressed plants, AMF application significantly enhanced phenolic compounds (by 17.0%), DPPH activity (by 14.7%), and ABTS activity (by 16.2%) compared to the non-treated plants.

Mineral concentrations

Since minerals are essential for plant physiological functions and stress responses, the mineral nutrient content of shoot tissues was evaluated in the current study (Table 3). The findings showed that Sm stress significantly decreased the concentrations of most nutrients. Under low Sm stress (125 mg kg⁻¹), the amounts of phosphorus (P), sulfur (S), potassium (K), magnesium (Mg), calcium (Ca), and sodium (Na) decreased compared to the control. In addition, the micronutrients zinc (Zn), iron (Fe), copper (Cu), manganese (Mn), and aluminum (Al) showed notable decreases. Under high Sm stress (250 mg kg⁻¹), reductions in S, K, Mg, Ca, Na, and Zn were more pronounced, while Cu and Mn showed slight increases. AMF inoculation considerably alleviated the detrimental impact of Sm stress on mineral uptake. Under control conditions (0 Sm), AMF resulted in a slight (non-significant) increase in shoot P concentration (40 ± 2 vs. 36 ± 0.4 µg g⁻¹ DW). When paired with low Sm concentrations, AMF significantly increased most mineral levels compared to Sm stress alone. In addition to lessening the detrimental effects of Sm, AMF raised P, S, Mg, Ca, Zn, Fe, Cu, Mn, and Al relative to the non-AMF control by 11.1%, − 6.8%, 4.1%, 14.0%, 0%, 0.9%, − 27.5%, − 28.0%, and − 14.5%, respectively (some changes non-significant). Although mineral content generally increased when AMF was added to the high Sm concentration compared to Sm alone, it remained lower than or similar to the untreated control for most elements.

A substantial metabolic reshaping driven by the individual or combined effects of AMF and Sm, with a more positive effect from 125 Sm + AMF, was revealed by the multivariate analysis (Figs. 7 and 8). The clustering heatmap separated the treatments into distinct groups, revealing pronounced metabolic changes. While the higher concentration of Sm clustered separately from the lower one (Fig. 7), the combined treatment 125 Sm + AMF clustered between AMF and the control, indicating that the alleviating effect of AMF was more obvious under the lower Sm concentration.

Discussion

The substantial decrease in grain production (up to 60%) and photosynthetic efficiency (43.2–60%) observed in this study provides proof that heavy metals like Sm have major phytotoxic effects on oat plants. Energy shortfalls and oxidative stress were directly associated with these abnormalities, which were caused by disturbed carbon and nitrogen metabolism. By lowering Sm accumulation in plant tissues (21.7–31.5%), preserving photosynthetic activity (limiting the decline to just 2.5%), and increasing grain production by up to 33.9%, inoculation with AMF (Rhizophagus irregularis) significantly reduced these detrimental effects [24]. Our findings show that AMF symbiosis reduced Sm-induced stress via a variety of mechanisms, such as enhanced nutrient uptake, defense related primary metabolism, and antioxidant defense stimulation. These results are in line with earlier research demonstrating that AMF can limit rare earth element uptake and translocation, safe guarding photosynthetic machinery and promoting metabolic readjustment in the face of metal stress [25].

Reduced Sm uptake and improved photosynthesis and mineral uptake were the basis of yield enhancement by AMF under Sm stress

Reduced Sm uptake, alongside improved photosynthesis and mineral uptake, formed the primary basis for yield enhancement by AMF under Sm stress. We found that AMF significantly mitigated the phytotoxic impact of Sm on photosynthesis, which served as a primary basis for improving the overall yield of oat plants. In accordance with our results, AMF reduces toxic metal levels in leaves, leading to a substantial improvement in photosynthetic pigments, growth parameters, and biomass accumulation [22–28]. AMF protects the plant’s photosynthetic machinery and limits the accumulation of toxic metals in the above-ground parts through a stable symbiotic relationship [29]. Additionally, AMF develops extensive hyphal networks that expand the root system’s surface area, enabling more efficient absorption of water and nutrients under metal exposure [9]. Notably, AMF immobilizes heavy metals in the soil, decreasing their movement to leaves and shoots, thereby reducing the toxicity that normally disrupts crucial functions like growth and photosynthesis [7]. In summary, AMF directly enhances plant yield by protecting photosynthetic pathways and facilitating nutrient acquisition under stress.

Heavy metal contamination in soils threatens plant health by disrupting essential mineral nutrient uptake, transport, and balance, leading to nutrient deficiencies and reduced crop productivity [30, 31]. Sm competes with essential elements for binding sites and alters membrane permeability, causing severe deficiencies in vital elements such as Ca, Mg, Fe, and Mn, which are crucial for chlorophyll synthesis and photosynthesis [30, 31]. The application of AMF greatly ameliorated the adverse effects of Sm on the uptake of minerals by oat plants by forming a symbiotic association with roots [32]. The root hyphal networks formed by AMF expand the absorptive surface area and facilitate nutrient uptake under stress, such as enhancing phosphorus acquisition to support better growth and development [33]. Additionally, AMF immobilizes heavy metals in soil and fungal structures, reducing their bioavailability by binding metals to fungal cell walls and sequestering them in vesicles [34]. Moreover, AMF secretes glomalin, a glycoprotein that improves soil fertility by binding with heavy metals, reducing metal mobility, and promoting plant growth [35].

In AMF-mediated plant stress tolerance, P and N are critical macronutrients. Under both control and Sm-stress conditions, AMF inoculation dramatically raised shoot P content in the current study. This improvement is in line with the well-known function of AMF in enhancing P acquisition via large extraradical hyphal networks, which is a main mechanism for AMF-induced stress reduction [36]. Improved P status directly enhances membrane stability, antioxidant defense mechanisms, and energy metabolism (ATP production). Similarly, our data demonstrated that AMF markedly elevated important nitrogen assimilation enzymes (GS and GDH) and raised the levels of many amino acids [37]. This implies that AMF enhanced N metabolism, which played a major role in the buildup of secondary metabolites and amino acids linked to stress protection, ultimately working together with improved photosynthesis and mineral uptake to sustain plant yield under Sm toxicity [37, 38].

Induced stress related metabolites by AMF under Sm stress

Metabolite profiling further revealed that Sm stress significantly altered the abundance of several sugars and organic acids, whereas AMF inoculation partially restored their levels to support carbon metabolism and stress tolerance (Figs. 2 and 3, and 9). Sm contamination substantially disrupted carbohydrate metabolism in oat plants, as evidenced by significant changes in glucose and starch contents, alongside altered activities of amylase and starch synthase enzymes (Fig. 1). In addition, marked alterations were observed in secondary and primary organic acids, including lactate, citrate, trans-aconitate, succinate, malate, and oxalate, which contributed to the distinct metabolic profile shown in Fig. 9. These findings indicate that Sm stress disrupted both carbon metabolism and organic acid turnover. Under heavy metal stress, sugars function as osmoprotectants and energy reserves, whereas organic acids contribute directly to metal chelation, detoxification, and cellular redox balance [39–43]. Crucially, AMF inoculation partially restored sugar metabolism and promoted the accumulation of these organic acids (Figs. 1 and 2, and 9), suggesting enhanced metabolic flexibility and detoxification capacity. These metabolic improvements were accompanied by better growth, photosynthetic performance, and nutrient status (Tables 2 and 3), demonstrating that AMF-induced modulation of carbon and nitrogen metabolism contributes significantly to the mitigation of Sm toxicity in oat plants. Similar heavy metal disruptions in carbohydrate and sucrose enzymes have been reported for cadmium, lead, and aluminum stress [44–46], while increases in malic and citric acid levels are known to aid heavy metal detoxification through chelation and sequestration [47, 48]. Ultimately, inoculation of oat plants with AMF successfully modulated the levels of sugars, amino acids, and organic acids to effectively cope with Sm contamination [49].

Exposure to Sm markedly altered the fatty acid and amino acid profiles of oat plants, whereas AMF inoculation partially restored metabolic homeostasis. In the present study, Sm stress modified the abundance of several fatty acids and amino acids associated with stress adaptation, indicating a substantial reprogramming of primary metabolism [38, 39]. Such metabolic alterations are essential components of plant resistance to heavy metal stress because oxidative damage disrupts membrane structure and cellular metabolism [38]. Changes in fatty acid composition, particularly the balance between saturated and unsaturated fatty acids, contribute to the maintenance of membrane integrity and fluidity under stress conditions [40]. Likewise, primary metabolites like amino acids (e.g., proline) function as osmoprotectants, antioxidants, and metal chelators that help stabilize proteins and membranes while reducing metal toxicity [39, 40]. Crucially, PAL activity was also induced by Sm toxicity and modulated by AMF symbiosis, serving as a key enzymatic bridge that links primary amino acid pathways directly to secondary metabolite defense networks. The increased accumulation of stress-related amino acids observed in Sm-treated oat plants (Fig. 3) is consistent with reports showing that heavy metal exposure stimulates amino acid biosynthesis as a defense mechanism [41]. Importantly, AMF inoculation substantially modified these Sm-induced responses, especially regarding amino acid and fatty acid metabolism, yielding a more favorable metabolic profile characterized by enhanced accumulation of metabolites associated with stress protection and cellular maintenance (Table 4; Figs. 3, 8 and 9). Similar mechanisms occur in other heavy metal-contaminated systems where AMF enhances protective amino acids like L-asparagine, L-glutamine, and methionine [29], stimulates amino acid biosynthesis [39], and slightly increases unsaturated fatty acids to improve membrane fluidity under metal stress [40].

Limitations and future perspectives

Although the present study provides comprehensive insights into the physiological and metabolic mechanisms by which AMF alleviate Sm toxicity in oat plants, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the experiment was conducted under controlled pot conditions, which may not fully represent the complexity of field environments where soil properties, microbial communities, and climatic factors can influence both Sm bioavailability and AMF performance. Second, only a single AMF inoculum and two Sm concentrations were evaluated, whereas different AMF species, inoculum combinations, and wider ranges of rare earth element concentrations may induce distinct plant responses. Third, although significant alterations in fatty acids, amino acids, sugars, and organic acids were identified, the underlying molecular and genetic mechanisms regulating these metabolic adjustments were not investigated. Future studies should therefore validate the observed AMF-mediated mitigation effects under field conditions and across different soil types. Further research integrating transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic approaches would improve our understanding of the molecular networks governing plant adaptation to rare earth element stress. Additionally, comparative studies involving different AMF species and oat genotypes could help identify highly efficient symbiotic combinations for enhancing crop productivity and reducing rare earth element accumulation in agricultural systems. Such investigations would contribute to the development of sustainable bioremediation and crop management strategies for soils contaminated with rare earth elements.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrates that the arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus Rhizophagus irregularis mitigates the adverse effects of samarium (Sm) stress on oat (Avena sativa L.) by reducing Sm accumulation, restoring photosynthetic performance, enhancing primary and secondary metabolism, and improving overall yield components. AMF significantly alleviated Sm toxicity through improved nutrient uptake, modulation of sugar and organic acid metabolism, increased amino acid biosynthesis, and accumulation of protective secondary metabolites and antioxidant compounds. These findings provide new insights into the beneficial role of AMF in phytoremediation of rare-earth-element-contaminated soils and highlight its potential for sustainable agriculture under heavy-metal and metalloid stress conditions.
