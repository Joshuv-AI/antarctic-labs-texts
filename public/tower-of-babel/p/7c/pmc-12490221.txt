Dynamic multi-omics profiling of islet and gut hormonal secretion and peripheral crosstalk in response to various nutrient loads
Authors: Jiachen Wang, Ling Liu, Hechun Liu, Yu Qian, Sijie Zhang, Shuai Zheng, Hemin Jiang, Yue Zhou, Xiaoliang Cheng, Qi Fu, Hao Dai, Tao Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryPostprandial metabolism is a complex and dynamic process involving diverse biomolecules, with islet and gut hormones playing crucial roles. However, how these hormones interact with biomolecules after nutrient intake and coordinate with peripheral insulin resistance (IR) remains elusive. This study characterizes postprandial multi-omics dynamics under mixed meals and four distinct macronutrient loads, investigating hormone secretion patterns, associated responsive molecules, and their relationships with IR. Postprandial multi-omics data significantly elucidate insulin and glucagon secretion, highlighting differences from the fasting state, while glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) are exclusively explained postprandially. Hormone secretion and molecular responses exhibit substantial heterogeneity among macronutrients. Postprandial multi-omics better predict IR, particularly with hepatic enrichment. Protein load shows the strongest association with both hepatic and muscular IR, while butter mostly connects with systemic IR. Several identified molecules mediate interactions between IR and islet α and β cell function, providing a molecular basis for advancing precision nutrition therapies in metabolic diseases.Keywords: pancreatic islet hormone, multi-omics, incretin, insulin resistance, macronutrient

Introduction

Endocrine hormones are closely intertwined with the pathophysiology of metabolic diseases, with islet hormones and incretins playing particularly pivotal roles in a range of metabolic disorders.1,2,3,4 Therefore, elucidating the regulation of the secretion and function of these hormones is of critical importance. Among the key factors influencing endocrine hormone secretion is dietary loading. Given that individuals with safe access to food typically consume three main meals daily, they spend nearly all waking hours in a postprandial state—a phase characterized by complex physiological processes following food intake.5,6 Previous studies have shown that hormonal and metabolic changes in response to dietary components digested and absorbed after a meal are manifested in postprandial blood.7,8 Notably, the secretion of insulin and glucagon by islet β cells and α cells exhibits significant heterogeneity in response to different disease states or the consumption of various types of nutrients.9,10,11 Similarly, the secretion of Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and Glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) by intestinal L cells and K cells in response to food intake is markedly influenced by the composition and pattern of the diet.12,13,14,15,16 These findings suggest that dietary intake engages a complex network of metabolic regulatory mechanisms. Therefore, dynamic analyses of cellular function, particularly following the intake of different macronutrients, are crucial for accurately characterizing the capacity and heterogeneity of hormone secretion patterns.

The regulation and function of hormones are highly complex biochemical processes. A wide variety of analytes, encompassing compounds with diverse molecular weights and biological categories, are involved in hormone secretion.17,18,19,20 Additionally, secreted hormones exert their effects through various mediators.1,3,21,22,23 Multi-omics analyses, through the integration of metabolomic, proteomic, and lipidomic data, provide a comprehensive understanding of biological processes and disease progression. This approach facilitates the identification of early disease biomarkers and uncovers potential therapeutic targets.24,25,26 Therefore, a systematic elucidation of the processes involved in hormone regulation and function necessitates the integration of multiple omics data. Current strategies for preventing and treating metabolic diseases, particularly those targeting islet and gut hormones, are of significant clinical importance. Interventions targeting these pathways have become a major research hotspot.27,28,29,30,31 However, the molecular regulation of hormone secretion in response to nutrient intake is not yet fully elucidated, highlighting the need for further research to identify potential key molecules and clarify the pathways through which hormones are secreted and exert their effects in vivo.

Tissue-specific insulin resistance (IR) and islet cell dysfunction are key pathological features of type 2 diabetes (T2D).32 These pathologies arise from the complex interplay between localized tissue abnormalities and inter-tissue crosstalk, ultimately resulting in systemic dysregulation. Hormones, regulatory factors, and metabolic products released by peripheral organs, such as adipose tissue, liver, skeletal muscle, and the gut, mediate the communication with pancreatic islets.33 However, most studies on the crosstalk between islets and peripheral tissues have focused on β cell function, with limited evidence regarding α cell involvement. Additionally, the gut, functioning as an endocrine organ, coordinates a series of complex events, including the local regulation of nutrient absorption, intestinal motility and secretion, and the modulation of insulin and glucagon release, etc. Alterations in the bi-directional communication between the gut and metabolically active tissues are associated with several metabolic diseases.34,35 Certain short-chain fatty acids produced and metabolized in the gut may serve as mediators of inter-organ crosstalk, contributing to the development of IR.36 However, the regulatory relationship between gut hormones and IR remains unclear.

Therefore, this study aims to integrate data from metabolomics, lipidomics, and proteomics to elucidate the dynamic molecular responses of islet and gut hormones following different macronutrient loads. Furthermore, we seek to investigate the communication and interactions between islet and gut cells and surrounding tissues.

Results

Heterogeneity in islet and gut hormone secretion in response to MMTT challenge

An mixed-meal tolerance test (MMTT) was performed in 147 participants (77 male and 70 female, Table S1A) with normal glucose tolerance, measuring fasting and postprandial levels of insulin, glucagon, GLP-1, and GIP (Figure 1A). The postprandial secretion patterns of all four hormones demonstrated significant temporal heterogeneity. The majority of participants exhibited peak insulin levels at 30 min, followed by a gradual decline (Figure 1B). The glucagon levels decrease after the MMTT load and subsequently show a slight rebound over time, returning to levels close to baseline (Figure 1C). Both GLP-1 and GIP showed significant postprandial elevations, with GLP-1 continuing to rise gradually after 60 min, while GIP peaked at 60 min before progressively declining (Figures 1D and 1E).

Levene’s test for variance was employed to assess the variability in hormonal response patterns, revealing significant heterogeneity from fasting to postprandial time points for insulin (p = 1.12 × 10−30) and GIP (p = 1.12 × 10−29) concentrations. The population coefficient of variation (CV) was higher for postprandial InsuliniAUC0-180min (71.83%), GlucagoniAUC0-180min (56.38%), and GIPiAUC0-120min (53.57%) compared with fasting values (56.31%, 51.97%, and 32.54%, respectively). In contrast, CV values are high in both the fasting and postprandial states for GLP-1 (227.18% and 131.86%, respectively; Table S1B). This observation indicates significant heterogeneity between the response patterns of different islet and gut hormones to the nutrient load, highlighting distinct regulatory mechanisms underlying their secretion.

Omics-based molecular responses to hormone secretion under fasting and postprandial states

We employed a mixed-effects model to assess the correlations between postprandial levels of insulin, glucagon, GLP-1, and GIP and multi-omics molecules (Figures 2A–2D). After adjusting for confounders such as age and gender, significant positive associations were observed between these hormones and multi-omics molecules. Specifically, insulin is significantly correlated with 89 omics substances (including proteomics, metabolomics, and lipidomics), while glucagon, GLP-1, and GIP are significantly correlated with 20, 41, and 41 omics substances, respectively (false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.05, Table S2A). In the fasting state, univariate regression revealed significant associations between 222 omics molecules for insulin and 17 for glucagon (FDR < 0.05, Figures S1A and S1B; Table S2B). Importantly, the molecular associations with insulin and glucagon secretion varied considerably between fasting and postprandial states. For insulin, 155 molecules were exclusively associated with its secretion during fasting, 22 were specific to postprandial secretion, and 67 were correlated with both fasting and postprandial levels (Figure 2I; Table S2C). In contrast, only one molecule, glycerophosphocholine (GPC), was shared in its association with glucagon secretion in both the fasted and fed states (Figure 2J; Table S2C), underscoring distinct molecular responses to islet α and β cell secretions across different feeding states. Furthermore, incretin secretion was not modulated by omics molecules under fasting conditions, indicating that omics molecules only influence GLP-1 and GIP secretion postprandially.

We further applied multivariable stepwise regression to identify omics molecules associated with islet and gut hormone secretion under fasting and postprandial conditions. Postprandially, insulin was significantly associated with 38 molecules, primarily comprising lipid molecules such as acylcarnitines (e.g., CAR 8:0 and CAR 10:0), ceramides (e.g., Cer 42:2;2O|Cer 18:1;2O/24:1), and triglycerides (e.g., TG 57:9|TG 18:2_18:2_21:5), which explained 72% of the variance in postprandial insulin secretion (p < 2.2 × 10−16, Figure 2E; Table S3A), while fasting insulin correlated with 51 molecules, including proteins (e.g., CLEC3B, complement factor H [CFH], and SERPINF1), sphingolipids (e.g., Cer 43:1;2O|Cer 18:1;2O/25:0), triglycerides (e.g., TG 50:2|TG 16:0_16:0_18:2), etc., accounting for 91% of the variance (p < 2.2 × 10−16, Figure S1C; Table S3B). For glucagon, six omics molecules, including ceramides, linoelaidic acid, and phospholipids, explained 29% of postprandial variance (p < 2.2 × 10−16, Figure 2F; Table S3C), while nine molecules explained 47% of fasting glucagon variance (p = 3.68 × 10−15, Figure S1D; Table S3D). Among these, GPC was identified as a regulator of glucagon secretion in both fasting and postprandial states. As previously noted, incretin secretion was influenced by omics molecules only postprandially, with GLP-1 significantly associated with 8 molecules, including proline, bile acids, triglycerides, etc. (R2 = 0.39, p < 2.2 × 10−16, Figure 2G; Table S3E). In addition to these molecules, GIP was also associated with indoline and allo-inositol (R2 = 0.79, p < 2.2 × 10−16, Figure 2H; Table S3F). These results highlight significant differences in omics responses to hormone secretion under fasting versus postprandial conditions, underscoring the importance of assessing both metabolic conditions to achieve a comprehensive evaluation of islet and gut hormone function.

Response patterns of islet and gut hormones to single macronutrient tolerance test

To further investigate the specific effects of individual nutrients on islet and gut hormone dynamics, an additional 24 participants, consisting of 12 males and 12 females, were administered four successive isocaloric macronutrient loads (glucose, whey protein, butter, and olive oil), each separated by a 1-week interval (Table S4). Blood samples were collected at multiple time points for the quantification of insulin, glucagon, GLP-1, and GIP, alongside proteomics, metabolomics, and lipidomics analytes (Figure 3A). Insulin exhibited the strongest response to glucose stimulation, followed by protein, with minimal changes observed following butter and olive oil intake. Glucagon showed a pronounced positive response to protein stimulation, while a negative response was observed after glucose intake. GLP-1 responds to all four stimuli. It rapidly decreases after peaking under glucose stimulation, while, under protein stimulation, it shows an initial increase followed by a decline after 120 min. GIP demonstrated the most significant response following olive oil administration (Figures 3B and S2A). The area under the curve (AUC) for each hormone’s secretion was analyzed across different macronutrient loads. Insulin, glucagon, GLP-1, and GIP exhibited the highest total secretion following glucose, protein, protein, and olive oil stimulation, respectively. Additionally, significant differences in hormone secretion were observed between the various macronutrient groups (p < 0.05, Figure 3C).

We applied the multi-omics markers identified in the previous multivariate regression model (Tables S3A, S3C, S3E, and S3F) to predict hormone secretion under single macronutrient tolerance test (SMNTT) conditions. Specifically, Tables S3A, S3C, S3E, and S3F contain 38, 6, 8, and 18 multi-omics molecules, respectively, which were used as independent variables to construct predictive models for insulin, glucagon, GLP-1, and GIP secretion. Participants were randomly assigned to training and validation cohorts in an 8:2 ratio. Pearson correlation analysis was subsequently performed to evaluate the relationship between predicted and observed hormone levels. The results indicated that insulin showed the strongest validation under glucose stimulation (Pearson correlation coefficient [cor] = 0.567, p < 0.001, Figure 3D), outperforming its validation under protein (cor = 0.473, p = 0.004, Figure S2B), butter (cor = 0.263, p = 0.130, Figure S2C), and olive oil stimulation (cor = 0.338, p = 0.047, Figure S2D). Glucagon exhibited the best validation following protein stimulation (cor = 0.623, p < 0.001, Figure 3E), followed by glucose (cor = 0.449, p = 0.007, Figure S2E) and butter (cor = 0.383, p = 0.020, Figure S2F), while the model did not perform significantly after olive oil intake (Figure S2G). For gut hormones, GLP-1 demonstrated notable predictive performance under protein stimulation (cor = 0.806, p < 0.001, Figure 3F) and also performed well under glucose stimulation (cor = 0.690, p < 0.001, Figure S2H), with diminished predictive performance observed under butter and olive oil stimulation (Figures S2I and S2J). GIP showed the best predictive performance under glucose stimulation (cor = 0.652, p < 0.001, Figure 3G) and weaker predictive correlation under protein, butter, and olive oil stimulation (Figures S2K–S2M). The results highlight that the heterogeneity in hormone responses can largely be explained by the differential molecular changes observed through multi-omics profiling.

Construction of a tissue-specific IR model based on multi-omics

IR is one of the major pathophysiology underlying metabolic syndrome and T2D. When peripheral tissues develop IR, the pancreas undergoes adaptive changes, with one of the most critical being the modulation of hormone secretion capacity. After identifying the omics molecules associated with hormone secretion, we sought to further explore the relationship between IR and multi-omics data. We calculated the relationships between omics molecules and IR indices, including HOMA-IR37 (homeostasis model assessment of IR), MISI38 (muscle insulin sensitivity index), ISIest39 (estimated insulin sensitivity index), BIGTT_SI40 (BIGTT insulin sensitivity index), and ISIc41 (ISIcomposite), under both postprandial and fasting conditions in participants from the MMTT cohort (Tables S5A–S5B). Analytes significantly correlated with at least one index (FDR < 0.05) are presented in Figure 4A (postprandial) and Figure S3A (fasting). A comparative analysis of fasting and post-load IR-associated omics molecules revealed that 41 omic moleculars were exclusively associated with IR in the fasting state, while 224 omic analytes molecular entities were solely related to IR in the post-load state. Additionally, 281 multi-omics molecules were found to be associated with IR in both the fasting and post-load states (Figure S3H). Enrichment analysis was performed on the omics molecules associated with IR in the postprandial state. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis of the proteomic data revealed significant enrichment in the complement and coagulation cascades pathway (Figure 4B). Metabolomics KEGG enrichment identified sphingolipid metabolism, as well as alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism, as the key pathways involved (Figure 4C). Additionally, tissue enrichment analysis further demonstrated that proteins in MMTT linked to IR were predominantly expressed in the liver (Figure 4D), indicating that liver-enriched proteins may play a pivotal role in modulating insulin signaling pathways.

The systemic glucose homeostatic is driven by tissue IR and involves the reduction of hepatic glucose output, increased glucose uptake into muscle and adipose tissue, suppression of lipolysis, and enhanced lipid accumulation in the liver and adipocytes.42 To further investigate the association between multi-omics molecules and tissue-specific IR, we divided the MMTT cohort into training and prediction sets in an 8:2 ratio. Stepwise regression was applied separately to postprandial and fasting omics data to construct tissue-specific IR prediction models. The proteomics, lipidomics, and metabolomics data are combined in this analysis. ISIest was used as an indicator of systemic IR,39 MISI as an indicator of muscle IR,38 and HOMA-IR as an indicator of hepatic IR.43 The postprandial models (Figures 4E–4J; Tables S5C–S5E) demonstrated superior predictive performance for IR compared to the fasting models (Figures S3B–S3G; Tables S5F–S5H). Specifically, for ISIest, the correlation was 0.671 in the postprandial models versus 0.503 in the fasting models (p < 0.001 vs. p = 0.004); for MISI, the correlation was 0.626 versus 0.340 (p < 0.001 vs. p = 0.070); and for HOMA-IR, the correlation was 0.708 versus 0.677, with p < 0.001 for both. These findings suggest that the postprandial response of omics molecules provides a more accurate reflection of peripheral tissue-specific IR, highlighting the importance of prioritizing postprandial conditions when investigating the omics-based regulation of IR.

Macronutrient-specific effects on explaining IR

Given the substantial heterogeneity in metabolic responses to distinct macronutrient challenges, we hypothesized that the effects of various macronutrient intakes on explaining IR might differ. To investigate this, we conducted a comparative analysis assessing the IR-predicted probability of four isocaloric macronutrient loads. Leveraging the associations observed between multi-omic analytes and IR indices, we utilized a Lasso-regularized logistic regression model, identifying sets of 32, 12, and 34 analytes exhibiting the highest predictive accuracy for ISIest, MISI, and HOMA-IR, respectively. These multi-omic analytes were selected based on their AUC values across 147 individuals within the MMTT cohort (Tables S6A–S6C). We then calculated the corresponding IR-predicted probability for each macronutrient load based on the odds ratios (ORs) of these analytes and their AUCs post-SMNTT load in 24 healthy individuals (Figures 5A–5C; Tables S6D–S6F). For ISIest, butter displayed the strongest relationship with IR, whereas glucose showed the weakest (Figure 5D). Notably, protein intake had the most pronounced associations with both muscle IR (MISI) and hepatic IR (HOMA-IR), with glucose consistently exhibiting the lowest association (Figures 5E and 5F). These findings indicate that different macronutrients are associated with tissue-specific IR to varying degrees, with a protein-rich diet explaining a higher probability of IR across multiple tissues.

To further investigate the relationship between a high-protein diet and IR, we conducted an experiment in which chow diet (CD) and high-fat diet (HFD) mice were administered either water or a 100 g/L whey protein solution for a duration of 28 days. This approach was adopted because whey protein is commonly consumed in beverage form.44,45,46 Moreover, a previous study has indicated that whey protein exerts no significant difference in its effect on food intake whether consumed in solid or liquid form.47 We found that adding whey protein to the drinking water did not significantly increase the total energy intake of mice (Figure S4A). There is no difference in body weight or blood glucose levels between whey protein treatment and the water control group in both CD and HFD mice after the feeding period as well as in intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) (Figures S4B–S4D). However, the AUC of glucose during the insulin tolerance test (ITT) was significantly elevated in the protein-fed groups compared to the control groups (CD, p = 0.036; HFD, p = 0.028) (Figures S4E–S4H). A significant reduction of insulin-induced protein kinase B (AKT) phosphorylation levels in mouse tissues was observed in CD-protein group (Figure S4I), indicating the development of IR in high-protein diet condition.

Mediation effects between IR and islet hormone secretion in the control of multi-omics analytes

The homeostasis of glucose and lipid is tightly regulated by the coordinated interplay between pancreatic islets and peripheral tissues. To identify the multi-omics analytes mediating the crosstalk between IR and islet function, we first identified molecules significantly associated with islet hormone secretion (FDR < 0.05, Figure 6A; Tables S7A and S7B). The secretory function of β cell was evaluated using eight metrics, including CIR48 (corrected insulin response), IGI49 (insulinogenic index), HBCI37 (homeostasis model assessment of β cell function index), BIGTT_AIR40 (BIGTT scute insulin response), Phest_1st39 (estimated first-phase insulin release), Phest_2nd39 (estimated second-phase insulin release), INSR12050 (InsAUC120/GluAUC120, total insulin release), and INSR3050 (InsAUC30/GluAUC30, early-phase insulin release), while α cell secretory capacity was assessed by measuring the AUC of glucagon in response to MMTT loading. We then performed bi-directional mediation analysis (see the STAR Methods section “bi-directional mediation analysis”) to identify analytes that exhibit a mediation effect between five IR indices and the secretory capacity of islet α and β cells (Table S7C). Figure 6B illustrates the analytes demonstrating a complete mediation effect (p_mediate < 0.05, p_direct ≥ 0.05) in the forward mediation analysis. Our results indicate that IR may modulate α cell function through metabolites such as allo-inositol, GPC, and lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) 20:5. For β cells, proteins such as Apolipoprotein L1 (ApoL1), IGFBP-2, PROS1, and SHBG, along with lipid molecules, including triglycerides, diacylglycerol (DAG), and LPC, emerged as potential mediators, suggesting their role as critical intermediaries linking IR to altered insulin secretion. Furthermore, results from the reverse mediation analysis indicated that the omics molecules mediating the bi-directional relationship between IR and islet function operate independently (Table S7C).

Similarly, we aimed to elucidate the relationship between gut hormone secretion and peripheral IR. The secretory function of intestinal L and K cells was assessed by calculating the AUC for GLP-1 and GIP following MMTT loading. However, spearman correlation analysis revealed no significant association between gut hormone secretion and IR (Table S8). This suggests that incretin secretion is unaffected by peripheral IR and may be regulated by independent mechanisms.

Discussion

In this study, we observed considerable variation in islet and gut hormone secretion trajectories following an MMTT load, with significant temporal heterogeneity in the postprandial patterns of insulin, glucagon, GLP-1, and GIP. Longitudinal multi-omics analyses uncovered biological regulatory mechanisms potentially underlying these variations. Both insulin and glucagon were found to be regulated by multi-omics molecules in fasting and postprandial states. However, distinct differences in the number and types of associated molecules between these two metabolic states highlighted significant variations in the regulatory networks governing α and β cell secretion. In contrast, incretin hormones were regulated solely by postprandial metabolic and lipidomic molecules. These findings underscore the clinical significance of monitoring these four hormones in the postprandial state.

Our postprandial multi-omics model indicates that insulin secretion is primarily regulated by lipid molecules, which play a key role in contributing to β cell disorders.51,52,53 Specifically, CAR can disrupt the balance of insulin synthesis and secretion, leading to β cell dysfunction,54,55 whereas ceramide (CER, a class of biologically active sphingolipids) acts as a trigger for β cell demise under pathological conditions.56,57 We found that GPC regulates glucagon secretion in both fasting and postprandial states. Studies have reported linoleoyl-GPC as a marker for the development of dysglycemia and T2D.58 Moreover, free fatty acids, including linoelaidic acid, have also been shown to participate in the regulation of glucagon secretion.59,60 For gut hormones, bile acids stimulate GLP-1 release by activating G protein-coupled bile acid receptor 1 located on the basolateral surface of L cells.61,62 Proline, as a component of colonic peptides, plays a role in regulating GLP-1 levels and is involved in processes related to host metabolic rhythms.63,64 Furthermore, metabolites produced by the gut microbiota, such as indole, have been shown to modulate the activity of enteroendocrine cells, thereby enhancing incretin secretion.65 Given the expression of GIP receptor (GIPR) in adipose tissue, GIPR agonism in vivo can enhance the clearance of dietary lipids into fat depots,66 and lipid metabolism and incretin signaling interact through multiple mechanisms.67,68,69 All these findings highlight the complex heterogeneity of regulatory patterns of islet and gut hormones, with the postprandial state being crucial for assessing islet and gut cell function.

Precision nutrition is emerging as a growing focus in medical research. The precise combination of three macronutrients, carbohydrate, protein, and fat, forms the basis of a reasonable diet.70,71 Modifying the consumption order of these macronutrients significantly impacts postprandial glucose, islet hormones, and incretin responses. For instance, the PATTERN study demonstrated that, in healthy subjects, a food intake sequence of vegetables first, followed by meat and rice, significantly attenuates the glycemic response while enhancing GLP-1 stimulation without increasing insulin demand.15 Advances in omics technologies have improved the prospects for the relationship between macronutrients and metabolic hormones.72,73 In this study, we investigated the heterogeneity of islet and gut hormone responses to four different macronutrients by employing multi-omics approaches to identify the molecular correlates underlying this variability. For insulin, in vivo stimulation experiments revealed that carbohydrate is the most potent insulin secretagogue, followed by protein and fat. These findings are consistent with previous observations in human islet studies in vitro.9 Although glucagon secretion in this study did not exhibit a significant acute response to butter or oil challenges, islet α cells demonstrated metabolic adaptability in long-term high-fat environments.74 Glucagon-derived peptides play a crucial role in regulating lipid metabolism under HFD conditions,75 and glucagon receptor signaling is involved in the development of metabolic-associated steatotic liver disease,76 jointly highlighting the close link between glucagon and lipid metabolism.

In the context of gut hormones, GLP-1 exhibits marked responsiveness to carbohydrate, protein, and fat, with protein being the most potent stimulator. Studies have reported that dietary proteins and protein hydrolysates can stimulate GLP-1 secretion through multiple pathways, with amino acid profiles, specific peptide sequences, and related receptors playing key roles in this process.20,77,78,79,80,81,82 Fat has been shown to stimulate GIP secretion,66 and in our study GIP exhibited the most pronounced response to olive oil intake. The stimulatory effect of fat on incretin secretion depends on the length and saturation of the fatty acids chains.83,84,85 We also observed strong GIP performance following glucose stimulation, suggesting that it is significantly regulated by both carbohydrate and olive oil intake. These results indicate that islet hormones and incretins are involved in the secretory regulatory pathways in response to different types of macronutrient loads. The heterogeneity in the body’s response to various dietary patterns may be driven by hormone secretion changes mediated through different omics molecules. Current clinical strategies for assessing the stimulation of these four hormones remain an area of ongoing investigation. Our SMNTT data underscore the importance of food selection in modulating hormone secretion responses.

The integration of multi-omics data can comprehensively provide information pertinent to health status. Recently, Takeuchi et al. combined unbiased fecal metabolomics with metagenomics, host metabolomics, and transcriptomic data to profile the involvement of microbiome in IR.86 Similarly, Krieg et al. identified that multi-omics signatures of epiploic adipose tissue reflect systemic IR and obesity subphenotypes distinct from other fat depots.87 These findings suggest potential therapeutic targets for improving IR. While previous studies primarily focused on fasting-state omics data, our research demonstrates that postprandial multi-omics profiles provide superior explanatory power for modeling IR. Notably, isocaloric protein intake was associated with the most pronounced IR in our study. Moreover, we observed that mice fed a high-protein diet exhibited a marked exacerbation of IR. Whey protein is abundant in branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), and elevated BCAA levels are commonly observed in insulin-resistant obesity and T2D patients and animal models.88 However, it should be noted that previous studies on protein or BCAA supplementation delivered via drinking water have not been uniformly associated with the development of IR. Some studies reported that high-protein diet or BCAA supplementation can lower the risk for metabolic diseases induced by HFDs and improve IR.44,45,89 On the other hand, evidence suggests that whey protein ingestion impairs glucose disposal during hyperinsulinemia.46 Mechanistically, BCAA supplementation activates the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR)/ribosomal protein S6 kinase beta-1 (S6K1)/insulin receptor substrate-1 (IRS-1) pathway, disrupting insulin signaling.90,91,92 Additionally, BCAA metabolites may drive fatty acid uptake in vascular endothelial cells, leading to excessive fatty acid accumulation in muscle tissue, and thereby trigger IR.93 In contrast, restricting isoleucine and valine from BCAAs can reprogram liver and adipose metabolism to enhance insulin sensitivity.94 However, other studies suggest that disturbances in BCAA levels may reflect the pathophenotype of IR, rather than serving as a causative factor.88,95 Therefore, future research needs to further confirm the causal relationship between high-protein diets or BCAA supplementation and IR.

The pancreatic islets coordinate with peripheral metabolic tissues to maintain glucose and lipid homeostasis, primarily adapting to tissue-specific IR through the modulation of hormone secretion. However, how islets sense IR and the intermediary signaling molecules involved remain critical unresolved questions. Previous studies have primarily focused on proteins,96 non-coding RNAs,97 and cytokines,98 whereas research on how metabolites mediate pancreatic islet adaptation to IR remains limited, with insufficient evidence from human studies. In our study, we identified several proteins, lipids, and metabolites that may serve as mediators in the interaction between IR and islet function. Notably, APOL has emerged as a regulator of islet inflammation, potentially contributing to β cell damage during diabetes progression.99 Circulating ApoL1 is associated with high-density lipoprotein dysfunction in insulin-resistant states, indicating its role in modulating insulin signaling.100 IGFBP-2, a 34 kDa plasma protein produced by the liver, has been shown to improve hyperglycemia and IR. Hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp studies demonstrated that IGFBP-2 significantly enhances hepatic insulin sensitivity and regulates glucose metabolism in ob/ob mice.101 In humans, circulating IGFBP-2 may play a role in the early improvement of insulin sensitivity102 and is also associated with insulin secretion.103 Lipids, such as triglycerides, DAG, and LPC, have been reported to participate in regulatory pathways linking IR to β cell function.104,105,106,107 Our results indicate that these metabolites may serve as key mediators in the crosstalk between IR and insulin secretion. With respect to islet α cells, we observed that molecules such as allo-inositol, GPC, and LPC may serve as mediators in the regulation of α cell function in response to IR, although the specific mechanisms require further elucidation. Notably, the omics molecules mediating IR and islet function operate independently in both directions, suggesting that distinct sets of molecules drive two processes: (1) islet adaptation to IR and (2) islet-mediated modulation of insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues. Furthermore, no significant relationship between gut hormones and IR was detected, indicating that the regulation of incretin secretion operates independently of peripheral IR.

In conclusion, this study integrates metabolomics, lipidomics, and proteomics to comprehensively elucidate the molecular basis of the heterogeneous response patterns of islet and gut hormones following different macronutrient loads. Additionally, we evaluated macronutrient-specific effects on IR explanation. Given that insulin secretion and IR constitute central regulatory mechanisms of glucose homeostasis, further investigation into the crosstalk between pancreatic islets, the gut, and peripheral tissue will provide critical insights into the pathogenesis of diabetes and offer valuable targets for personalized precision therapies.

Limitations of the study

We utilized non-targeted multi-omics approaches, which may introduce some inaccuracies in quantification. Nevertheless, non-targeted techniques offer the advantage of enabling unbiased identification of previously uncharacterized small molecules. Furthermore, the application of stringent quality control measures can mitigate these limitations, thereby enhancing the overall accuracy of the analysis. We acknowledge that the multi-omics molecular mechanisms involved in mediating the crosstalk between pancreatic islet cells and tissue-specific IR require further validation through in vitro and/or in vivo experiments.

Experimental model and study participant details

MMTT cohort

Volunteers were recruited in Nanjing, China, via poster advertisements to participate in a standard MMTT. The inclusion criteria were as follows: age ≥18 years old, no prior diagnosis of diabetes; no history of malignant tumors, severe organ disease, or significant acute or chronic infections; and no recent use of glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, or other medications known to substantially affect metabolism. A total of 147 participants (77 males and 70 females, aged 18–50 years) completed the MMTT. Among them, 54 participants were of normal weight (BMI <24 kg/m2), 38 were overweight (24 kg/m2 ≤ BMI <28 kg/m2), and 55 were categorised as obese (BMI ≥28 kg/m2). We utilized Ensure powder (Abbott Manufacturing Singapore Private Limited), a standardized nutritional meal replacement, which provides 57.4g of carbohydrate, 15.9g of protein, and 14g of fat per 100g. After fasting for 12 h, each participant was instructed to consume 84g of Ensure (360 kcal) dissolved in 250 mL of water within 3–5 min. Participants venous blood samples were collected at baseline (0 min) and at 30 min, 60 min, 120 min, and 180 min post-meal. Insulin and glucagon levels were quantified across all time points (a total of 735 samples). In contrast, GLP-1, GIP, along with non-targeted proteomics, metabolomics, and lipidomics analytes, were conducted on plasma samples collected at 0 min, 60 min, and 120 min (a total of 441 samples) (Figure 1A).

SMNTT cohort

An additional cohort of healthy volunteers was recruited for the SMNTT. The inclusion criteria were: age ≥18 years old, no history of abnormal glucose levels (fasting plasma glucose <6 mmol/L, 2-h postprandial plasma glucose <7.8 mmol/L); normal body mass index (BMI, 18–24 kg/m2); no family history of diabetes; no history of malignancy, severe organ diseases, or significant acute or chronic infections; and no recent use of glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, or medications known to significantly affect metabolism. A total of 12 healthy men and 12 healthy women, aged 18–40 years, completed the SMNTT. Participants underwent four successive SMNTT (glucose, whey protein, butter, and olive oil), each separated by a one-week interval. We used 75g of anhydrous glucose powder, 78.5g of protein powder, 40.1g butter or 33.9g olive oil for each load. Each test provided 300 kcal of energy (Table S4B). After a 12-h overnight fast, participants consumed one of the four nutrients: glucose or whey protein dissolved in 250 mL of water, olive oil consumed directly, or butter accompanied by three lettuce leaves. Blood samples were collected at fasting baseline (0 min) and at 30, 60, 120, and 180 min post-consumption (a total of 480 samples). Non-targeted metabolomics and lipidomics analyses were performed on all samples. For plasma proteomics in the SMNTT cohort, participants were stratified by gender. Within each gender group, samples from three randomly selected individuals at the same time point during the nutrient loading tests were pooled for proteomic analysis (a total of 160 samples) (Figure 3A). For the correlation analysis, the proteomic profile of each individual was represented by the average value of the three pooled samples.

The demographic information of the participants, including age, gender and ethnicity, was obtained through self-reported data. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University (2021-SR-298). All procedures involving human subjects were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Gender and age were adjusted for in the subsequent analyses.

Animal experiments

Four-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (GemPharmatech, China) were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-h light/dark cyclee and were randomly assigned to one of four groups: (1) CD-water, with free access to a chow diet (CD; 13.5% fat; 5001; LabDiet, USA) and additive-free water; (2) CD-protein, with free access to a chow diet and water containing 100g/L whey protein powder; (3) HFD-water, with free access to an HFD (60% fat; D12492; Research Diets, USA) and additive-free water; and (4) HFD-protein, with free access to an HFD and water containing 100g/L whey protein powder. All mice were fed for 28 days. Body weight and random blood glucose levels were monitored every 4 days. All experimental animal procedures were approved by The Medicine Animal Care Committee of Nanjing Medical University (IACUC2201017-1).

After 28 days of feeding, the HFD and CD groups underwent both an IPGTT and an ITT. Tail blood glucose levels were measured (Medisafe Mini, Terumo, Japan) at baseline and periodically after intraperitoneal administration of 2 g/kg D-glucose for IPGTT at 5, 10, 30, 60, 90, and 120min, and 0.75 U/kg insulin (Humulin R, Eli Lilly, USA) for ITT at 10, 30, 60, 90, and 120min (Figures S4C–S4H).

Method details

Endocrine hormones and biochemical tests

Plasma concentrations of glucagon, GLP-1, and GIP were measured using the ELISA kits (Mercodia, Uppsala, Sweden). Insulin levels were assessed via chemiluminescence detection kits (YHLO Biotech, Shenzhen, China), while plasma glucose levels were determined using the hexokinase method (AU5400; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Untargeted multi-omics measures by liquid chromatography (LC)-MS

Plasma metabolomics

Sample preparation and data acquisition. Plasma samples were thawed on ice, and 50 μL of each sample was mixed with four volumes of methanol containing a mixed isotope internal standard solution. The mixture was shaken at 1000 rpm for 10 min, followed by vortex mixing. Samples were then centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. After centrifugation, the supernatant was transferred to a protein precipitation plate for filtration, and an equal volume of the filtrate was transferred to a new centrifuge tube for freeze-drying at low temperature. The freeze-dried sample was recombined with 200 μL recombinant solvent and uploaded to 10 μL mass spectrometry for quantitative analysis. Internal standards used in this assay included: D-Fructose-13C6 (CIL, USA), L-Tryptophan-d5(CIL, USA), citric acid d4(CIL, USA), L-lysine d4(CIL, USA), Benzoic acid d5(CIL, USA), [2H5]-5-Hydroxyindole-3-acetic acid (TRC, CA), N-benzoyl-D-phenylalanine (Sigma-Aldrich, GER), DNOP-d4 (Nafushengwu, CHA). The concentration of each internal standard in the final internal standard mixed solution was 0.05 mmol/L. Finally, 10 μL internal standard mixed solution was added to each sample for sample preparation. Metabolic extracts were separated on BEH C18 column (Waters, SKU: 186002352, 100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) using a 12-min gradient with organic phase increased from 2% to 100% at 40°C (solvent A: 0.1% formic acid, 2 mM ammonium formate in water; solvent B: 0.1% formic acid, 2 mM ammonium formate in methanol).

Samples were analyzed using a Thermo Scientific Vanquish UHPLC coupled with Q-Exactive PLUS mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher, USA) under data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. A full survey scan was obtained at a 70,000 resolution for the m/z range of 70–1050 and followed by 10 MS/MS scans in HCD mode at a 17,500 resolution. Internal standard retention times and alignment as well as coefficients of variation (CVs) of metabolite quantification values were checked daily to ensure data quality including chromatography performance, mass calibration, and extraction efficiency.

Data processing

Data were firstly processed with variance stabilization normalization (VSN) method and analyzed using Compound Discoverer software (v.3.2) for feature detection, spectra deconvolution, metabolite identification and peak alignment between samples. The compound identity in the sample was confirmed if the compound matched retention time (RT difference <0.2 min), m/z (mass tolerance ≤5 ppm), predicted elemental composition (including fine structure and isotopic pattern). Compounds with a library match score ≥80 were retained for identification purposes. The fragmentation search was conducted of the unknown MS2 spectra against the mzCloud library and a search using the Thermo Scientific mzVault library constructed from MS2 spectra obtained from the laboratory-purchased standards.

Plasma lipidomics

Sample preparation and data acquisition

Ice-cold methanol and methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE, Optima brand, Fisher Scientific) was sequentially added into plasma sample, mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Phase separation was induced by adding and incubation with MS-grade water at room temperature for 10 min, and centrifuged at 1,000 g for 10 min. The upper organic phase was collected and dried in a vacuum centrifuge. All non-polar extracts were resuspended in 75% isopropanol (IPA) in water. The temperature of the autosampler during LC-MS analyses were stored at 4°C. Samples were vortexed, centrifuged (21,000g, 20 min, 4°C) and the supernatant was aliquoted into an HPLC vial for the LC-MS analysis. Metabolic extracts were separated on BEH C18 column (Waters, SKU: 186002352, 100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) using a 20-min gradient with organic phase increased from 30% to 100% at 40°C (solvent A: 0.1% formic acid, 2 mM ammonium formate in 60/40 acetonitrile/water; solvent B: 2 mM ammonium formate in 90/10 IPA/acetonitrile). LC-MS analyses were performed as described above in the metabolomics section.

Data processing

Raw spectra files were converted from ∗.raw format to ∗.abf format using Reifycs ABF converter (http://www.reifycs.com/AbfConverter/index.html). MS-DIAL software (v.4.7) was used for feature detection, spectra deconvolution, metabolite identification and peak alignment between samples. Accurate mass and MS/MS matching were used for lipid identification, with the public LipidBlast library including over 200,000 MS/MS spectra. Quantification was performed by combining data for different detected molecular species for each particular lipid. Lipid quantification metrics were normalized with VSN method.

Plasma proteomics

Sample preparation and data acquisition

Plasma samples were firstly processed with High Select Top 14 Abundant Protein Depletion Resin (A36372, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA) according to manufacturer’s instructions. And then transferred into 10 kDa ultrafiltration tube (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), cleaned with 8 M urea in 50mM ammonium bicarbonate for twice. The concentrated solution was diluted with 8 M urea and transferred into a 96-well plate (Thermo Scientific). Cysteine residues were reduced using 10 mM Dithiothreitol (DTT, Sigma-Aldrich), and alkylated using 20 mM Iodoacetamide (IAM, Sigma-Aldrich) and then quenched by 10 mM DTT. Proteins were sequentially digested for 2 h and overnight at 37°C by lysyl endopeptidase (Lys-C; Wako; Chuo-Ku, Osaka, Japan) and trypsin (Promega; Madison, WI, USA) respectively. Digestion was terminated by adding trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, Sigma-Aldrich). Resulted peptides were cleaned by SOLAμ SPE Plates (60209-001, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA) as the manufacturer’s instructions and dried in centrifugal concentrator.

Peptides were resuspended in 0.1% FA and analyzed using Q Exactive HF-X Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were eluted from an analytical column (Acclaim PepMap C18, 2μm, 100 Å, 75 μm × 25 cm) using a 90-min gradient of 1%–8% B (A: 0.1% formic acid; B: 0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile) for 4 min, 8%–30% B for 76 min, 30%–90% B for 6 min and 100%–100% B for 4 min. The Q Exactive HF-X Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer was operated under data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode using 60 DIA segments with an isolation window of 10 Th from m/z 400–1000.

Data processing

Raw spectra files were searched using DIA-NN (v1.8.0)108 against plasma proteome library as previously described.109 An FDR cutoff of 1% was applied at the peptide and protein levels. Proteins were quantified and normalized using MaxLFQ algorithm110 and VSN method,111 respectively. Proteins with missing values greater than 50% across all samples were filtered, and the missing values were imputed using KNN algorithm.

Western blot

Quadriceps femoris (Quad) tissue was harvested 10 min after an intraperitoneal bolus of insulin (5 U/kg) from overnight fasted mice. After homogenized in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China) with 1:100 dilution of PMSF Solution (ST506, Beyotime, China) and PhosSTOP (04906837001, sigma, USA), tissue extracts were centrifuged at high speed (12,000 rpm) to eliminate insoluble material. Protein concentrations in the supernatants were measured using BCA protein assay kit (P0012, Beyotime, China). Equal amount of protein lysates (20 μg) was separated by FuturePAGE 4–20% 15 Wells (ET15420Gel, ACE Biotechnology, China) and then transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). The resulting blots were blocked with NcmBlot blocking buffer (P30500, New Cell & Molecular Biotech, China) for 30 min at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C (anti-phospho-Akt (Ser473) (4060, 1:2000), anti-Akt (4691, 1:2000), and anti-β-Actin (4970, 1:1000) (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). After washed three times with TBST, incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature and washed for three times again, SuperSignal Chemiluminescent Substrates (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were applied for detection (Figure S4I).

Quantification and statistical analysis

General

Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using R (version 4.4.0). Levene’s test was utilized to evaluate the heterogeneity of hormone secretion in response to the MMTT challenge. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired t-test, while differences among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons. For skewed data, a logarithmic transformation was performed before conducting the t-test or ANOVA. Spearman’s correlation was used to examine associations between IR indices, islet and gut hormones, and multi-omics molecules, with adjustments for confounding factors such as age and gender, unless these variables were already incorporated into the calculation formulas. The Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method was used for FDR calculation. A p value or FDR of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Mixed-effects model

A linear mixed-effects model was implemented using the R package lme4 (version 1.1–35.3). Time and individual were included as random effects, while omics variables were specified as fixed effects, with hormone levels as the dependent variable. Age and gender were considered as confounding variables. The values of hormones and omics variables were log-transformed prior to analysis. The results from proteomics, metabolomics, and lipidomics analyses were corrected for multiple testing using the BH method. Omics variables with an FDR <0.05 were considered significantly associated with hormone levels.

Stepwise regression model

Separate multivariate stepwise regression models were developed for postprandial and fasting omics data to evaluate hormone secretion. In the postprandial model, the independent variables were omics analytes with an FDR <0.05, as identified in the previously described mixed-effects model, with the dependent variable was the AUC of hormone secretion. The fasting model incorporated significant variables identified through univariate analyses for further investigation. Stepwise regression, adjusted for age and gender, was performed using the step() function in the R base package. Backward selection was applied, sequentially removing the least significant variables until no further reduction in the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) could be achieved. Both hormone and omics values were log-transformed prior to analysis. The significance of the final regression coefficients was determined at p < 0.05.

Correlation analysis of predicted and actual hormone levels

As previously described, we identified omics analytes significantly associated with hormone levels under MMTT loading condition (FDR <0.05, Table S2A). These features were then subjected to mixed-effects modeling and stepwise regression, with the optimal model selected based on the AIC. This process resulted in a set of omics features capable of predicting hormone levels (Tables S3A, S3C, S3E and S3F). Subsequently, we applied these multi-omics markers to predict hormone secretion under SMNTT conditions using a multiple regression model. The SMNTT cohort was divided into training and validation sets in an 8:2 ratio. Pearson correlation analysis (two sided) was conducted after log transformation of both predicted and actual values. Correlation analysis was performed using the cor.test() function in the R stats package. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

IR predicted probability of macronutrients loads

Due to the lack of threshold values for IR/insulin sensitivity indicators, we grouped the subjects based on quartiles. Specifically, in the MMTT cohort, individuals in the top 25% of HOMA-IR values were classified as insulin-resistant. For MISI and ISIest, the bottom 25% of individuals with the lowest values were similarly categorized as insulin-resistant group. The remaining participants were classified as non-insulin-resistant. Lasso regression was applied to construct multi-omics-based predictive models for IR, utilizing the glmnet package in R. The AUC of omics substances is utilized in regression. The optimal regularization parameter (λ) was determined through 10-fold cross-validation, with the alpha parameter set to 1 to prioritize variable selection. The constructed models were then applied to the SMNTT cohort to assess the association between IR and each individual macronutrient. We calculated the IR association for each macronutrient load based on the ORs of these analytes and their AUCs post-SMNTT load in 24 healthy individuals. IR index values and omics variables were log-transformed prior to analysis. Age and gender were included as covariates to account for potential confounding effects.

The finalized model was applied to the SMNTT datasets to evaluate the predicted probability of IR under various dietary conditions. Enrichment analysis

KEGG enrichment analysis of differentially expressed or associated proteomics was performed using the R package clusterProfiler (version 4.12.0), with all human genes serving as the background. KEGG enrichment analysis for differentially expressed metabolomics was conducted using the R package MetaboAnalystR (version 4.0.0). Tissue-specific enrichment analysis for proteomics was carried out using the TissueEnrich package (version 1.24.1) in R. P-values were adjusted using the BH method, and an FDR less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Bi-directional mediation analysis

To investigate the effects of omics molecules on in silico causal relationships between IR indices and islet and gut hormone secretion capacity, we performed bi-directional mediation analysis using the R package mediation (v.4.5.0) on multi-omics data obtained under postprandial conditions. Significant associations (FDR <0.05) were first screened among IR indices, omic molecules, and islet or gut hormones. Next, we carried out bi-directional mediation analysis with interactions (y = x + m + x × m, where y is the outcome, x is the variable and m represents the mediator) between mediator and outcome taken into account using the mediate function from mediation. A complete mediation effect was considered significant if the mediation effect had a p-value <0.05 and the direct effect had a p-value ≥0.05.
