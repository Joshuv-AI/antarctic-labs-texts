A novel model using leukocytes to differentiating mild autonomous cortisol secretion and non-functioning adrenal adenoma
Authors: Xin Zhao, Jiaquan Zhou, Xiaohong Lyu, Yanan Li, Lin Ma, Yihong Liu, Hua Fan, Yushi Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMild autonomous cortisol secretion (MACS) accounts for a significant proportion of adrenal incidentaloma. Current endocrinological screening tests for MACS are complex, particularly in areas with limited medical resources. This study aimed to develop a diagnostic tool based on leukocyte-related parameters to differentiate between MACS and non-functioning adrenal adenoma (NFA).MethodsInthis retrospective case-control study, propensity score-matching was used to select 567 patients from a cohort of 1108 patients (201 MACS, 907 NFA). External validation cohort included 52MACS and 48 NFA from two hospitals, which did not overlap with the modeling cohort patients. Leukocyte-related parameters were evaluated, and the diagnostic efficacy of each parameter was assessed by calculating Youden’s J index (J) and the area under the curve (AUC). The study population was divided into training and testing samples using a 10-fold cross-validation method. Machine learning (ML) and classification and regression tree (CART) model were established.ResultsAfter propensity score matching, 567 patients were enrolled, including 197 MACS and 370 NFA. With the exception of basophil percentage, all other parameters differed significantly between the two groups. Lymphocyte count, lymphocyte percentage, eosinophils count, eosinophils percentage, and basophil percentage were lower in the MACS group compared to the NFA group. Eosinophils percentage demonstrated the highest AUC (0.650), with a sensitivity of 51.3% and specificity of 73.2%. The ML model, based on multiple parameters,exhibited better performance in diagnosing MACS (sensitivity 76%, specificity 77.4%, and AUC 0.818). A clinically usable CART model achieved an AUC of 0.872, with a sensitivity of 95% and a specificity of 75.7%. In the validation cohort, the prediction accuracy of the ML model and the CART model were 0.784 and 0.798, respectively.ConclusionTheCART diagnostic model, constructed based on leukocyte-related parameters, could assist clinicians in distinguishing between MACS and NFA.Keywords: Mild autonomous cortisol secretion, Nonfunctioning adenomas, Diagnosis model, White blood counts, Leukocyte-related parametersSubject terms: Endocrine cancer, Urological cancer

Introduction

The advancement of imaging examination techniques and the widespread use of computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have led to a significant increase in the detection rate of incidentaloma adrenal tumors. Approximately 5% of cross-sectional imaging examinations can detect adrenal tumors1,2, of which 95% are benign adrenal cortical adenomas2. The incidence of sporadic adrenal tumors increases with age, with about 3% in people under 40 and 10% in people over 703.In the largest prospective study to date, the European Network for the Study of Adrenal Tumours (ENSAT) conducted the Evaluation of Urine Steroid Metabolomics in the Differential Diagnosis of Adrenocortical Tumours (EURINE-ACT) study. This study recruited 2169 participants with newly diagnosed adrenal masses, among whom 1305 individuals had benign adrenocortical adenoma. Of these, 49.7% (n = 649) had non-functioning adenomas (NFA), while 46.3% (n = 591) had Mild Autonomous Cortisol Secretion (MACS), including possible autonomous cortisol secretion adenomas (PACA, n = 451) and autonomous cortisol secretion adenomas (ACA, n= 140)4,5.

In MACS, patients have abnormal hormone excess but lack the typical clinical manifestations of Cushing’s syndromes. However, the prevalence of comorbid conditions is significantly higher in these patients. Compared to those with non-functioning adenomas (NFA), patients with MACS have a higher morbidity and mortality rate from metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease6–11. Moreover, all-cause mortality was significantly increased in patients with MACS compared to those with NFA12. Notably, in women younger than 65, MACS were associated with higher all-cause mortality than NFA. Based on the guidelines provided by the American Association of Endocrine Surgeons, adrenalectomy is recommended for patients with MACS. In contrast, for those with NFA, close follow-up is recommended, and further treatment should only be considered when there is a possibility of malignancy13.

The practice guideline, developed in collaboration with the European Network for the Study of Adrenal Tumours, recommends that all patients with adrenal incidentalomas undergo a 1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test (1 mg DST) to exclude cortisol excess14. However, due to poor economic conditions and limited medical resources in many primary care clinics or underdeveloped regions, Cushing’s syndrome screening can only be completed in some primary hospitals. As a result, universal Cushing’s syndrome screening for these patients is unrealistic. Both MACS and NFA patients lack typical clinical presentations, making it challenging for clinicians to rapidily differentiate between MACS and NFA patients based on the symptoms and signs of Cushing’s syndrome alone. Therefore, utilizing existing tools to quickly distinguish between NFA and MACS is an urgent problem in clinical practice.

Glucocorticoids have a significant impact on general health. White blood cell (WBC) and differential counts are indicators of general health that are influenced by the immune system15. Most immune cells correlate with the degree of hypercortisolism and differ among Cushing’s syndrome subtypes16. Studies have reported that hypercortisolism could change the profiles of leukocyte-related parameters. Some studies have demonstrated that WBC count, leukocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and other indicators could be used in the diagnosis of Cushing’s syndrome. This simple method can distinguish between Cushing’s syndrome in patients with an adrenal mass and NFA with high sensitivity and specificity17–21. However, no research has been conducted to distinguish between NFA and MACS using tools built with complex algorithms containing more data.

The present study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of WBC-related biomarkers in differentiating NFA from MACS. Furthermore, we sought to establish two diagnostic models and assess their usefulness in raising suspicion of MACS or ruling out NFA.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study and its experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (I-22PJ073). All methods were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardians.

Data from patients with adrenal incidentalomas who underwent surgical treatment in the Department of Urology at Peking Union Medical College Hospital between January 2016 and October 2022 were retrieved and collected. All patients were confirmed to have unilateral adrenal cortical adenoma by postoperative pathology. We excluded patients with overt Cushing’s adenoma, primary bilateral macronodular adrenal hyperplasia, primary pigmented nodular adrenocortical disease, primary aldosterone adenoma, and adrenocortical carcinoma. Based on adrenal adenoma function, the enrolled patients were divided into two groups: the MACS group and the NFA group. The enrollment procedure is shown in Fig. 1.

Study selection flow-chart depicting patient inclusion and exclusion criteria. Abbreviations: MACS, Mild autonomous cortisol secretion; NFA, non-functioning adenoma; PSM, propensity score matching.

To further validate the predictive accuracy of the model trained in this study, we collected another cohort of patients. The cohort consisted of 52 MACS (38 from Peking Union Medical College Hospital(PUMCH) and 14 from Hainan Affiliated Hospital of Hainan Medical University (HAMU)) and 48 NFA (28 from PUMCH and 20 from HAMU). Among them, the patients from Peking Union Medical College Hospital were those diagnosed after November 2022 and did not overlap with the modeling cohort patients. Meanwhile, the validation cohort patients met the inclusion and exclusion criteria of this study.

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria of MACS were as follows: (1) Patients lacked multiple and progressive features of Cushing’s syndrome, particularly those that are more predictive of Cushing’s syndrome, such as easy bruising, facial plethora, proximal myopathy, and striae22. (2) According to the latest EJE 2023 guidelines23, we used the diagnostic criteria for MACS as inclusion criteria: serum cortisol levels between 1.9 µg/dl and 5 µg/dl after a 1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test (1 mg DST)14,23. Cortisol was measured by Siemens Atellica Solution CII direct chemiluminometry (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). (3) All patients underwent surgical treatment and had pathological confirmation.

The inclusion criteria of NFA were as follows: (1) Patients lacked Cushing’s syndrome-related clinical manifestations. (2) The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis function was evaluated as normal by endocrine examinations, including: (a) Normal 24-hour urinary free cortisol (24 h-UFC); (b) Normal serum cortisol at 8 am; (c) Normal adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) at 8 am; (d) Normal cortisol circadian rhythm; (e) Negative dexamethasone suppression test (DST) (serum cortisol ≤ 1.8 µg/dl). (3) Patients were followed up for at least 6 months before surgery, during which at least two endocrine examinations were performed, and the results were normal. (4) All patients underwent surgical treatment and had pathological confirmation. Using these criteria, we included patients with non-functioning adrenal adenomas with normal endocrine function, excluded patients with Cushing’s syndrome and subclinical Cushing’s syndrome, and ensured that all included patients had surgical and pathological confirmation.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Pathologically confirmed malignant tumors, including hematologic malignant tumors; (2) combination of other endocrine diseases, such as primary aldosteronism, pheochromocytoma, etc.; (3) combination of infectious diseases; (4) combination of diseases requiring exogenous hormones, such as autoimmune diseases; (5) use of drugs affecting leukocytes, such as glucocorticosteroids or immunosuppressant drugs, in the 2 weeks prior to the routine blood test.

Clinical information and testing indicators

The clinical information included symptoms, signs, gender, age, body mass index (BMI), abdominal Computed Tomography (CT), hypertension, highest systolic blood pressure, highest diastolic blood pressure, diabetes, blood routine test, urine routine test, serum cortisol, 8 am ACTH, 24 h-UFC, DST, and pathological results. Abdominal CT was used to assess the side and maximum diameter of adrenal tumors. Routine blood collection was perfomed at 8 am, before breakfast.

A total of 11 WBC-related inflammatory parameters in the blood routine test were analyzed, including WBC count, neutrophils count, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, basophils count, eosinophils count, neutrophils percentage, lymphocyte percentage, monocyte percentage, basophil percentage, and eosinophils percentage. In addition to these common parameters, we also included the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), defined as neutrophil count/lymphocyte count; the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte difference variable (NLDV), calculated as the difference between neutrophil count and lymphocyte count; and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte difference variable percentage (NLDVP), calculated as difference between neutrophil percentage and lymphocyte percentage. The most recent results for 8 am serum cortisol, 8 am ACTH, and 24 h-UFC were used in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was perfomed using R4.2.1 software. To adjust for differences in baseline variables, including gender, age, BMI, tumor side, maximum tumor diameter, and year of hospitalization, the case and control groups were matched 1:2 using the propensity score matching analysis method with a caliper value of 0.2. Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and differences between groups were compared using Student’s t-test. Non-normal distribution continuous variables were expressed as median (quartile range), and differences between groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Count data were expressed as percentages, and the chi-square test was used to compare groups.

Receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve analysis was employed to calculate the optimal cut-off value for leukocyte-related inflammatory parameters. The corresponding sensitivity, specificity, Youden’s J index(J), and the area under the curve (AUC) were determined. Youden’s J index was defined as (sensitivity + specificity − 1).

The 10-fold cross-validation method was used to divide the overall population into training and testing samples. In the training sample, a machine learning (ML) model and a classification and regression tree (CART) model were established using the ‘xgboost’ package and the ‘rpart’ package, respectively. The predictive accuracy of the two models for MACS was estimated in the testing sample, and the AUC and its associated 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated separately. Differences were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05.

In the propensity score matched cohort, patients in the MACS group and NFA group were comparable in terms of gender, age, BMI, tumor side, maximum tumor diameter, and year of hospitalization (P > 0.05). Female patients were predominant in both groups, accounting for 70.5% (261/370) in the NFA group and 70.1% (138/197) in the MACS group, with no significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.980). The average age of the entire cohort was 49.11 ± 11.19 years old, and female patients were older than male patients (49.81 ± 11.19 and 47.46 ± 11.05 years old, respectively, P = 0.022). In addition to the six matched characteristics, the distribution of hypertension and diabetes was similar between the MACS and NFA groups (P > 0.05) (Table 1). However, for Cushing’s syndrome-related hormones, the 8 am serum cortisol (P = 0.024) and 24 h-UFC (P < 0.001) in the patients with MACS were significantly higher than those in the NFA group, whereas the 8 am ACTH showed the opposite trend (P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Comparison of the WBC-related inflammatory parameters between the MACS group and NFA group

Thirteen out of fourteen WBC-related inflammatory parameters differed significantly between the MACS group and NFA group, with the exception of basophil count (Fig. 3). Differential diagnosis of MACS and NFA using individual leukocyte-associated parameters

We performed ROC analysis to assess the value of individual leukocyte-associated inflammatory parameters for the differential diagnosis of MACS and NFA. The optimal cut-off point, AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and J index were calculated ofr each parameter (Table 2). Based on AUC and J values, the eosinophils percentage was found to be the best parameter. The optimal cut-off value for the eosinophils percentage was 1.350%, with a corresponding sensitivity and specificity of 51.3% and 73.2%, respectively. However, overall, a single indicator was not sufficient for a comprehensive differential diagnosis of MACS and NFA.

Establishments and comparison of the ML and CART model

Given the unsatisfactory predictive accuracy of individual leukocyte-related inflammatory parameter for MACS, we constructed a predictive ML model incorporating all 14 leukocyte -related inflammatory parameters. The ML model was established based on the optimal AUC value in the testing sample. The ML model demonstrated superior performance in predicting patients with MACS, with an AUC of 0.818 (95%CI 0.706–0.930), compared to using the eosinophils percentage as a single leukocyte-related inflammatory parameter (AUC 0.652, 95%CI 0.603–0.699) (P = 0.005).

The optimal cut-off value for the predictive performance of the ML model was 0.268. Using this cut-off value, we obtained a sensitivity of 76.0% and a specificity of 77.4%. As shown in the rank of feature importance, the most important predictive features in the ML model were eosinophil percentage, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte differences variable, lymphocyte count, basophil percentage, neutrophil count, and monocyte percentage, followed by monocyte count, lymphocyte percentage, and WBC count (Fig. 4).

Feature importance ranking of 14 WBC-related inflammatory parameters in the machine learning (ML) model. The 14 parameters were divided into three clusters based on their feature importance. Abbreviations: NLDV, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte differences variable; NLR, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio; NLDVP, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte differences variable percentage.

To facilitate clinical application, we also established a CART model based on the 14 leukocyte-related inflammatory parameters. The final CART model included neutrophil-to-lymphocyte differences variable, monocyte percentage, eosinophil percentage, eosinophil count, lymphocyte count, and basophil percentage. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte differences variable was used as the judgment indicator for the first node of the CART model (Fig. 5). By utilizing the CART model, we can quickly obtain the predictive probability of MACS. The AUC of the CART model was 0.872 (95%CI 0.787–0.956), and the optimal cut-off value was 0.241, with a corresponding sensitivity and specificity of 95.0% and 75.7%, respectively. When comparing AUCs, the CART model outperformed the single-parameter model using eosinophils percentage (P < 0.001). Although not statistically significant, the CART model might have better predictive accuracy for MACS compared to the ML model (P = 0.455) (Fig. 6).

Discussion

For the first time, we have developed a clinically usable CART model to distinguish MACS from NFA in adrenal incidentalomas based on leukocyte-related inflammatory parameters. This differential diagnosis requires only routine blood results and the model flow chart itself, making the differentiation between MACS and NFA much more straightforward. By comparing the AUCs of the single leukocyte-related inflammatory parameter, ML model, and CART model, we found that the CART model has better predictive accuracy than the single parameter or ML model. The sensitivity and specificity of the CART model for diagnosing MACS were 95.0% and 75.7%, respectively.

Hypercortisolemia can lead to changes in various leukocyte counts24. Excess glucocorticoids can impair neutrophil function, eosinophil production and monocyte production. Additionally, excess glucocorticoids can influence humoral components of innate immune, inhibiting lymphocytes25. Masriiraq et al. reported that leukocytosis was present in 40% of patients in a Cushing’s disease cohort, and the decrease in UFC after treatment was positively correlated with changes in leukocyte counts26. According to a large retrospective study in children, the absolute lymphocyte count was negatively related to cortisol levels, while the total WBC and absolute neutrophil counts were significantly higher27. Another pediatric study also showed that the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio was higher in Cushing’s syndrome patients than in the control group20, which is consistent with our result in adult patients. These finding suggest that abnormal cortisol secretion substantially affects the immune system. Although the patients mentioned in the above studies had Cushing’s syndrome rather than the more subtle subclinical autonomous cortisol secretion, the literature still validates our findings to some extent.

Previous studies have used leukocyte-associated inflammatory markers to identify Cushing’s syndrome and adrenal incidentalomas19,21. However, these studies have limitations, such as using a limited variety of immune-related indicators, not combining composite models for identification, and reporting insufficient sensitivity and specificity. Moreover, the study samples in these investigations may not represent the most clinically challenging scenarios for differentiating Cushing’s syndrome from other conditions with similar presentations. The most important clinical distinction is between Cushing’s syndrome and incidentalomas of adrenal origin with no obvious symptoms. Our study bridged this gap by addressing the limitations of previous research and focusing on the most clinically relevant differential diagnosis.

Another study16 reported a subtype-specific pattern of leukocyte differential in endogenous hypercortisolism. The results showed a negative corrlation between 24 h-UFC and lymphocytes(-0.412, P < 0.0001), eosinophils(-0.477, P < 0.0001), basophils(-0.222, P< 0.005), and monocytes(-0.009, n.s). Our results were similar to those of that study, further clarifying the role of cortisol. However, the study didn’t enroll an NFA population, making it difficult to compare most of the results with our study. Furthermore, the sample size included in the study was small, with only 40 cases of automouous cortisol secretion, which may limit the interpretations drawn from the study. One other study28 evaluated the difference in erythrocyte-related parameters between patients with Cushing’s syndrome and asymptomatic pituitary tumors or adrenal incidentalomas. This study suggests that in women, erythrocyte-related parameters may also be helpful in identifying Cushing’s syndrome. It provides a new perspective for our further model optimization in the future.

The present study had several strengths. First, we enrolled only patients with unilateral adrenocortical adenomas to ensure high consistency among the samples. The samples size was relatively large, and all diagnoses were confirmed by pathology. Second, we employed propensity score matching analysis to reduce potential bias. Third, based on the univariate analysis of parameters, we established two prediction models incorporating various parameters to improve diagnostic accuracy. Lastly, the external validation cohort reflects, to some extent, the reliability of the model developed in this study in clinical applications.

However, this study also had some limitations. As a retrospective study, there may have been information loss, resulting in decreased data quality. To mitigate this issue, we focused on objective patient observations, such as gender, age, BMI, and blood pressure, ensuring the reliability of the data used. Another limitation was the absence of nocturnal salivary cortisol exmaminations, which are recommended by guidelines but were not conducted in our hospital. Lastly, as a single-center study, there may be concerns regarding overfitting, which could affect the generalizability of our findings. Further multicenter studies could help address this issue.

When conditions permit, we recommend prioritizing the 1 mg dexamethasone suppression test for the diagnosis of subclinical Cushing’s syndrome. The diagnostic model we proposed can serve as an auxiliary tool for initial screening or provide diagnostic information when the 1 mg dexamethasone suppression test is not available. Although our study excluded patients with co-infections, autoimmune diseases, use of exogenous hormones, and malignant tumors, some comorbidities or medications not easily detected clinically may still affect leukocyte parameters, thus limiting the application of this method. Therefore, when applying this diagnostic model, it is necessary to comprehensively evaluate the clinical conditions of patients to avoid potential interfering factors.

Conclusions

We developed a clinically usable CART model based on routine blood test to distinguish MACS from NFA, with satisfactory sensivitity and specificity. This model has been validated in an external cohort. This model can help clinicians determine whether further endocrinological testing should be performed. The establishment of this model may potentially impact the clinical management of adrenal incidentaloma, particularly in primary care settings. By utilizing this model, healthcare providers can make more informed decisions regarding the necessity of additional testing, ultimately improving patient care and resource allocation.
