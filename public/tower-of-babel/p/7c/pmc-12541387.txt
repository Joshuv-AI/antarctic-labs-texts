E-ABIN: an explainable module for anomaly detection in biological networks
Authors: Ugo Lomoio, Tommaso Mazza, Pierangelo Veltri, Pietro Hiram Guzzi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing availability of large-scale omics data calls for robust analytical frameworks capable of handling complex gene expression datasets while offering interpretable results. Recent advances in artificial intelligence have enabled the identification of aberrant molecular patterns distinguishing disease states from healthy controls. Coupled with improvements in model interpretability, these tools now support the identification of genes potentially driving disease phenotypes. However, current approaches to gene anomaly detection often remain limited to single datasets and lack accessible graphical interfaces. We introduce E-ABIN, a general-purpose, explainable framework for anomaly detection in biological networks. E-ABIN combines classical machine learning and graph-based deep learning techniques within a unified, user-friendly platform, enabling the detection and interpretation of anomalies from gene expression or methylation-derived networks. By integrating algorithms such as support vector machines, random forests, graph autoencoders, and graph adversarial attributed networks, E-ABIN ensures high predictive accuracy while maintaining interpretability. We demonstrate the utility of E-ABIN through case studies of bladder cancer and celiac disease, where it effectively uncovers biologically relevant anomalies and offers insights into disease mechanisms. E-ABIN is freely available on the Zenodo platform at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17062501.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of high-throughput technologies has transformed the landscape of biomedical data analysis, enabling researchers to extract meaningful insights from large-scale gene expression and DNA methylation datasets [1]. These omics data not only facilitate the discovery of novel biomarkers [2] but also allow the construction of gene-to-gene interaction networks that capture the complex structure of biological systems. Graph-based representations provide a natural and powerful framework for analyzing such networks, supporting tasks such as mutation detection, disease stratification, and drug response assessment [3].

Within this framework, graph anomaly detection has emerged as a critical technique for identifying nodes, edges, or subgraphs that deviate significantly from expected patterns [4]. Existing approaches span statistical, machine learning (ML), and deep learning (DL) paradigms. Statistical methods leverage topological features—such as node degree and clustering coefficient—to characterize outliers [5, 6], while ML-based techniques have been successfully applied to both tabular and network-structured biological data [7, 8]. However, many of these models depend on hand-engineered features and require extensive domain knowledge for validation, limiting scalability and generalizability.

Recent advances in DL, including graph neural networks (GNNs) and autoencoder-based architectures, have provided scalable solutions for modeling large biological graphs [9, 10]. These models can automatically learn complex representations of biological networks, offering improved predictive performance and interpretability. Tools such as Deep GONet [9], XOmiVAE [10], and DSAF-GS [11] exemplify how deep models can be augmented with mechanisms to generate biologically meaningful explanations. Nevertheless, existing solutions often lack accessible user interfaces, integrated support for diverse data modalities, and flexible explainability workflows, which restrict their use in applied and translational settings.

To address these challenges, we present E-ABIN (explainable anomaly detection in biological networks), a novel framework that integrates ML and DL techniques within an interactive, explainable environment. E-ABIN offers native support for both gene expression and DNA methylation data and features an intuitive graphical user interface (GUI) designed for accessibility. The platform constructs personalized biological networks using convergence–divergence models and individual-specific networks (ISNs) (i.e. networks representing the transcriptome of a single patient) [12], enabling fine-grained analysis at the patient level. E-ABIN integrates model-agnostic interpretability tools, including SHAP [13] and Captum [14], to provide transparent, multi-level explanations of predictive outcomes. Figure 1 depicts an example of a generic overview of analysis.

Overview of the E-ABIN software workflow. E-ABIN accepts gene expression or DNA methylation data as input, preprocesses the data, and constructs patient-specific gene-to-gene interaction networks using either convergence–divergence models or ISNs. The system applies anomaly detection models and integrated explainability techniques (e.g. SHAP, Captum) to provide patient-, class-, and dataset-level interpretations. Results are visualized through an intuitive GUI, supporting both exploration and decision-making.

We demonstrate E-ABIN’s performance through case studies on publicly available datasets, where it successfully identifies disease-associated molecular anomalies and reveals the most influential genes contributing to predictions. By unifying interpretability, flexibility, and usability, E-ABIN advances the state of the art in biomedical network analysis and offers a scalable, explainable AI solution for the study of complex human diseases. In the following, we report the E-ABIN architecture and experimental results supporting the effectiveness and efficacy of the tool, as also treated in the “Discussion” section.

Related Work

We report about existing solutions that can be compared with the E-ABIN proposed method. Table 1 reports a comparison among the proposed tool and similar existing ones able to identify anomaly detection in networks, where comparison is performed in terms of (i) the presence of GUI, (ii) the use of ML tools, (iii) whether based on DL modules, and (iv) whether they present an explainable module or not and whether they allow DNA methylation. Tools such as XOmiVAE [10], Deep GONet [9], and DSAF-GS [11] implement DL architectures and offer model explainability, but they are not simple to use due to lack of GUIs. This limits accessibility for users who are not familiar with command lines or settings. Moreover, these tools are not designed to process DNA methylation data. GenePattern [15] and CSAX [8] provide GUI-based environments and support classical ML methods but do not incorporate DL, explainable AI, or domain-specific functionality for methylation. E-ABIN addresses these limitations by integrating a user-friendly GUI with both ML and DL capabilities, incorporating explainability modules, and offering tailored support for DNA methylation analysis. To the best of our knowledge, it is the only tool to combine all five criteria considered essential for modern bioinformatics workflows. The architecture leverages a modular design, allowing seamless integration of interpretable DL models and domain-specific preprocessing pipelines (see the ”Materials and methods” section). This design enables E-ABIN to support researchers across disciplines, including those without programming backgrounds, and facilitates reproducible, interpretable analyses of epigenetic data.

We now report some of the characteristics of the tools reported in Table 1. Such a short description allows to focus the E-ABIN contribution in the context of existing tools and to highlight the novelties and contributions.

The XOmiVAE [10] tool is a variational autoencoder-based DL model designed for interpretable cancer classification from omics data. It reveals the contribution of each gene and latent dimension to classification predictions, enabling both supervised and unsupervised results to be explained in terms of gene and feature importance. It presents deep learning facilities for integrating multi-omics data, aiming to capture complex biological patterns. Deep GONet [9] is a self-explainable deep neural network that integrates Gene Ontology (GO) knowledge into its hierarchical architecture by mapping each neuron to a biological function derived from GO terms. This module enables accurate phenotype prediction from gene expression data and allows users to interpret predictions describing biological functions and pathways. DSAF-GS [11] uses ML- and DL-based tools and comes with an easy-to-use graphical interface. DSAF-GS is a hybrid feature selection method that combines autoencoder-based dimensionality reduction and statistical filtering to identify the most informative genes. A neural network is used for classification, with explainable AI (SHAP) used to interpret gene contributions to predictions. GenePattern [15] is a known framework in the computational biology community for its accessible graphical interface and support for a wide range of ML methods. It presents an explainability module and does not include DL modules. Finally, the CSAX [8] offers a streamlined interface and supports both traditional and DL analyses. However, it lacks in explaining on how predictions are reached. It identifies systematic anomalies in gene expression data by using the feature regression and classification [16] method to assign anomaly scores to each gene, ranking these genes, and then applying gene set enrichment analysis to detect gene sets where anomalous expression is concentrated, thereby highlighting pathways or biological processes disrupted in individual samples.

Materials and methods

E-ABIN system design

E-ABIN has been designed to provide interpretable anomaly detection for high-dimensional biological data. It is characterized by two main designed modules: (i) the core software module architecture, including algorithms and data analysis modules, and (ii) the analytical workflow process with the presentation result modules. In the following, we report the two architectural designs.

System architecture

The functional architecture of E-ABIN is composed of two modules integrated through a user-friendly GUI. As shown in Fig. 2, the architecture includes core components for network construction and analysis, machine learning, deep learning, and explainability. These modules are coordinated through a Python-based backend and rendered to the user via a modern web interface.

E-ABIN system architecture. The interface is organized into four main functional modules: data upload, which manages dataset import, preprocessing, and normalization; network construction, which builds patient-specific or similarity-based networks; AI module, which supports ML and DL models for classification and anomaly detection; and explainability, which provides interpretability at patient, graph, and disease levels using methods such as SHAP and GNNExplainer. Rounded boxes, gradient coloring, and icons highlight the modular structure, ensuring that users can intuitively follow the analysis pipeline from data ingestion to model explanation.

Analytical workflow

The E-ABIN analytical workflow describes the full sequence of operations performed on a dataset from ingestion to interpretation. This user-centric perspective is illustrated in Fig. 3 and reflects how a typical analysis is conducted. Starting from a dataset containing gene expression or DNA methylation profiles, users first upload data via the GUI and apply built-in preprocessing steps—such as missing value imputation, normalization, and duplicate removal. Data normalization is applied only inside the grid-search cross-validation of ML algorithms. Dimensionality reduction methods are applied for visualization and initial class separability assessment.

End-to-end workflow for patient-specific and cohort-based network analysis. The pipeline begins with data upload, followed by preprocessing and optional dimensionality reduction (e.g. t-SNE, PCA). Users then choose a network type: either an ISN, where each patient has a graph with genes as nodes and expression as node features, or a convergence/divergence network, where each patient is represented as a single node and edges are derived from feature similarity. After graph construction, the workflow proceeds to the analysis stage, where either ML or DL models are applied. The selected models are then trained and evaluated, and finally, their outputs are interpreted to extract patient-, graph-, or disease-level insights. Arrows indicate the logical flow across all stages, from data ingestion to model interpretation.

Users can choose between traditional ML or DL anomaly detection pipelines. By using ML, a grid-search, five-fold cross-validation is applied to optimize model hyperparameters. For both ML and DL algorithms, hyperparameters can be manually modified by the user through the analytics GUI. In the DL pipeline, data are converted into graph representations:

At the node level, all patients are represented as nodes in a single global graph.

At the graph level, a separate graph is constructed per individual, capturing personalized molecular relationships.

The performance of DL models in node anomaly detection task depends on the threshold used for edge connection in the network. For instance, the user can use the default value of 0.93 or adjust the parameter using the GUI functionalities. The anomaly detection results are visualized and interpreted using embedded explainability techniques, allowing for gene-level insights and model transparency. This two-layered design-system architecture and workflow ensures both technical robustness and ease of use, aligning E-ABIN with the needs of a broad biomedical research community. Consider that the network is built by connecting, for instance, patient data mapped to nodes and connected by means of edges weighted by using the defined threshold.

Results

We tested the E-ABIN framework using four different GEO datasets to prove the module capacity in terms of anomaly detection in both gene expression and methylation networks. For each dataset, E-ABIN was used with the default configuration; this means that no data preprocessing techniques such as normalization or NaN imputation were applied. We consider node-level anomaly detection using GNNs and convergence–divergence networks. We also performed graph-level anomaly detection using GNNs and interactome-based ISNs. E-ABIN uses ML and DL models to perform the prediction, so having the right amount of data is crucial to prevent overfitting. For instance, the limited sample size in the bladder cancer dataset (6 normal and 18 abnormal cases) and the perfect AUC score suggest that these results could be affected by overfitting, despite applying stratified k-fold cross-validation, evaluating model performance on an unseen test set, and aligning model interpretability with clinical evidence. Conversely, the colorectal cancer dataset, with a larger sample size (23 normal and 123 abnormal cases), also achieves a perfect AUC of 1.0. This confirms E-ABIN’s strong performance and robustness, particularly when datasets are sufficiently large to support reliable training and validation.

Comparison with state-of-the-art tools

We compared E-ABIN results with state-of-the-art tools designed for functional annotation and diffusion-based inference in biological networks. Some of the available methods are either not publicly available or are not actively maintained (e.g. DSAF-GS, CSAX). Among the available and functional tools (e.g. XOmiVAE), we considered Deep GONet as a representative state-of-the-art model for comparison. However, pretrained models were not available; thus, we retrained Deep GONet using the datasets we used in E-ABIN, following a similar dataset distinction in train and test. We therefore integrated and tested our approach alongside Deep GONet to assess performance under comparable conditions. Both technical feasibility and alignment with current benchmarks in the literature guided this choice.

Table 7 compares the performance of the most effective variant of E-ABIN (using LR) against Deep GONet with L1 and L2 regularizations across four benchmark datasets: GSE37817, GSE11501, GSE21510, and GSE6613. The table reports the ROC-AUC score achieved by each model on the respective datasets. The results show that the best E-ABIN model (LR) consistently matches or exceeds the performance of Deep GONet across multiple datasets. Specifically, E-ABIN (LR) and Deep GONet achieve perfect scores (1.0) on GSE37817 and GSE21510 and deliver higher performance than Deep GONet on the other two datasets as well (0.73 versus 0.5909 for GSE11501 and 0.64 versus 0.5 for GSE6613). These findings indicate that LR demonstrates superior generalizability and robustness over Deep GONet across varied genomic datasets, making it a more reliable choice for these classification tasks. Other E-ABIN models, including random forest and GCN, outperform Deep GONet in both performance and interpretability. E-ABIN offers multiple levels of explainability at the class, dataset, and patient levels. Its interpretations are more inherently understandable, using gene symbols rather than GO terms.

Discussion

Using E-ABIN, it is possible to analyze both gene expression and DNA methylation datasets. The framework accurately classifies individuals as either healthy or exhibiting abnormalities in their molecular profiles. Using the integrated explainability module, E-ABIN enables clinicians and researchers to interpret the obtained predictions in biologically meaningful ways, offering multiple layers of insight. At the patient level, the model identifies the most influential genes contributing to each prediction, allowing clinicians to evaluate which molecular features may be causally implicated in disease onset or progression. At the disease level, E-ABIN provides aggregated insights across patient cohorts, highlighting genes that consistently contribute to distinguishing pathological from non-pathological profiles. These results support the identification of potential diagnostic and prognostic markers.

To illustrate the system’s capabilities, we analyzed a DNA methylation dataset containing samples from healthy individuals and patients with bladder cancer. Both ML and DL models embedded in E-ABIN were trained to perform anomaly detection, achieving perfect predictive accuracy on the test set. Even non-trainable dimensionality reduction techniques such as PCA and t-SNE revealed clear separability between the two groups, underscoring the biological relevance of the dataset. In addition to prediction, E-ABIN provided interpretable explanations for each classification, enhancing the transparency and utility of the results in a biomedical setting.

Focusing specifically on the explainability results in bladder cancer, among the 10 most influential genes identified by SHAP, seven are also present in literature as relevant to cancer progression or metastasis. Among these, three genes, i.e. RUNX2, ITGB3, and CARD8, have been directly associated with bladder cancer. RUNX2 has been extensively validated, and it is recognized that such a gene enhances tumor progression via the regulation of glutamine metabolism. Its high expression correlates with poor clinical outcomes and advanced stages, and it plays a role in epithelial-mesenchymal transition and interactions with cancer-associated fibroblasts [20]. ITGB3 has demonstrated clinical utility in prognostic models, and it is involved in modulating immune infiltration patterns within the tumor microenvironment [21]. CARD8 has been included in bladder cancer prognostic signatures related to immune regulation and contributes to inflammatory pathways through the inflammasome mechanism [22].

Four additional genes, including HSD3B1, ADAMTSL1, PEG3, and PTPRCAP, have been implicated in other malignancies or metastatic processes. For example, HSD3B1 has been widely studied in prostate cancer and shows enhanced expression in bladder tissues, suggesting hormonal influences that may explain sex-related incidence differences [23]. ADAMTSL1 has been associated with survival outcomes across multiple cancer types and shows methylation changes in other tumors such as breast cancer and rhabdomyosarcoma [24, 25]. PEG3 is a well-characterized tumor suppressor frequently silenced through hypermethylation, with confirmed relevance in bladder, breast, and gynecologic cancers [26]. PTPRCAP has been linked to gastric cancer susceptibility and plays a role in oncogenic signaling pathways involving Src family kinases [27].

Three genes, OLIG3, C10orf26, and LOC348840, were identified by E-ABIN as potentially relevant, despite limited or no existing experimental validation in bladder cancer. OLIG3, though not directly linked to bladder cancer, belongs to a gene family with known roles in gliomas and neural development. C10orf26 remains poorly characterized but exhibited high SHAP importance, suggesting possible regulatory functions that merit further study. LOC348840, an unannotated locus, also ranked highly in model interpretability analyses and may represent an under-explored genomic element with prognostic potential, in line with prior findings where such loci were later shown to contain cancer-relevant regulatory sequences [28].

A distinguishing innovation of E-ABIN lies in its integration of ISNs and convergence/divergence network paradigms, which enable both node- and graph-level anomaly detection. The framework combines state-of-the-art algorithms—including GAANs, GAEs, and traditional ML approaches—with advanced explainability techniques, such as SHAP, GNNExplainer, and Captum. This architecture is presented through a user-friendly graphical interface, lowering technical barriers and making advanced graph-based anomaly detection accessible to a broad range of researchers, including those without extensive computational expertise. The explainability module provides actionable insights by pinpointing the key genes that most influence disease classification, as demonstrated in the identification of bladder cancer-associated molecular markers. This interpretive capacity bridges the gap between computational analytics and biological understanding, empowering users not only to identify anomalous patient profiles but also to determine the underlying molecular features contributing to those anomalies.

Experiments confirmed E-ABIN’s versatility and effectiveness across different data modalities and scales. In the case of the bladder cancer DNA methylation dataset, classical ML models—including logistic regression, support vector machines, and random forests—achieved perfect classification performance, with AUC values of 1.00. DL models, such as GCNs, also delivered excellent predictive accuracy, underscoring the tool’s adaptability and efficacy.

Conclusion

E-ABIN integrates ML and DL methodologies within a unified framework that addresses key limitations of current bioinformatics tools. Experimental results demonstrated robust performance across a range of biological datasets, effectively detecting anomalies in both gene expression and DNA methylation profiles. Crucially, E-ABIN complements these analytical capabilities with an integrated explainability module, allowing for biologically grounded interpretations of the results. We claim that, thanks to high-performance and reliable interpretable results, E-ABIN establishes a new benchmark for explainable anomaly detection in biological networks. Its ability to combine cutting-edge analytics with transparency supports critical applications in personalized medicine, disease mechanism discovery, and biomarker identification, offering clinicians and researchers a comprehensive, interpretable, and practical tool for molecular network analysis. Future work regards the possibility of extending the E-ABIN use to a larger dataset and to gather experiences by distributing the open-source codes among clinicians.
