Effects of U62631.5 on short-chain fatty acid receptor 41(GPR41) and the related intestinal bacterium Butyricicoccus pullicaecorum after fecal bacterial transplantation in nonalcoholic fatty liver disease
Authors: Xiaoyan Hao, Cong Ma, Xiangyang Gao, Fei Wang, Bingqing Han, Dong Bai, Lei Ou, Qiang Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLncRNAs exhibit diverse biological functions and may be crucial in nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) development. Our focus is on the mechanism of lncRNA U62631.5, regulating GPR41 and Butyricicoccus pullicaecorum (B. pullicaecorum), an intestinal bacterium producing butyric acid. Ensembl analysis suggests an interaction between U62631.5 and GPR41.Methods1. Male C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into control group, model group, transplantation control group, transplantation model group by using the fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) technique in this study. The liver tissue and small intestine tissue in each group were observed by hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining, and liver histopathology was quantitatively assessed using the NAFLD Activity Score (NAS). The expression of U62631.5 and GPR41 in peripheral blood, liver tissue, and small intestine tissues were detected by PCR. Fecal samples were used by16S rRNA sequencing. The contents of short chain fatty acids in intestinal were detected by gas chromatography, and the expression of B. pullicaecorum was detected by PCR. 2.Hepatic lipid accumulation was observed in HepG2 cells. GPR41 promoter function analysis was detected. After siRNA and overexpression of U62631.5, the U62631.5 and GPR41 were further detected by PCR.Results1. Background

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is one of the most common chronic liver diseases, with an estimated worldwide prevalence of 25% [1]. NALFD has the potential to become a major pathogenic promoter of steatohepatitis, fibrosis, hepatocellular carcinoma [2]. The structural changes in intestinal flora play an important role in the occurrence and development of NAFLD [3, 4]. According to the “intestinal-hepatic axis” theory, gut microbiota may regulate the progression of NAFLD [5]. GPR41, which was first identified as a fatty acid receptor in the gut, can sense short-chain fatty acids, including acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid, from the digestive tract [6]. Butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid produced by gut microbial metabolism, is suggested to be a potential therapeutic agent for NAFLD [7]. and B.pullicaecorum is considered to be an intestinal probiotic that produces butyrate [8].

Similarly, it has been confirmed in animal experiments. B. pullicaecorum is a Gram-positive anaerobic that belongs to Clostridium IV and can produce butyric acid [9]. B. pullicaecorum has been proven to be safe in vivo and in vitro [10]. These findings confirmed the good safety and tolerability profile of the combination, and indicated that B. pullicaecorum may became a next-generation probiotic [11].Changes in the intestinal flora play an important role in the occurrence and development of NAFLD. Once the intestinal mucosal barrier is destroyed, intestinal bacteria and their products can enter the liver through the intestinal-liver axis and cause a series of reactions, which will lead to the occurrence and development of NAFLD [3].

Thus, butyric acid is activated by GPR41 through the “intestinal-hepatic axis.” Some studies have also indicated that lncRNAs may play an important role in the pathogenesis of NAFLD [12]. The GPR41 gene locus is located on chromosome 19, and lncRNA U62631.5 is also located on chromosome 19. We found that U62631.5 was located on chromosome 19 neighboring the GPR41 gene and that the two genes showed an overlapping relationship in the online Ensembl database (http://asia.ensembl.org/index.html). No significant changes in hepatic parameters have been reported in patients who received allogenic or autologous fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) [13]. However, there are few reports describing the intestinal flora, long-chain noncoding genes, and short-chain fatty acid receptors in relation to the pathogenesis of NAFLD. We investigated the mechanism of lncRNA U62631.5 on regulating neighboring the GPR41 gene and related-intestinal bacteria Butyricicoccus pullicaecorum in NAFLD. Here, we systematically analyzed the FMT-related changes in the butyric acid produced by B. pullicaecorum in NAFLD mice, which was activated by GPR41, and the regulatory effects of the lncRNA U62631.5 on this process. We hope that these results will provide novel ideas for prevention and treatment of NAFLD.

Materials and methods

Animals and experimental design

Animals

Male C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were chosen in this study, and 15 mice were used in each group. All C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China) and bred at the animal center of Chinese PLA general hospital. The mice were fed in a barrier specific-pathogen-free environment. Four mice were housed in each cage. In accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines, all animals were treated with humanity, employing anesthetics and sedatives to minimize distress and discomfort throughout the experimental process. Euthanasia of the mice was carried out via intraperitoneal injection of saline-diluted Euthasol, which comprised 150 mg/kg of the active component pentobarbital sodium (390 mg/mL) and phenytoin sodium (50 mg/mL). To summarize, the mice were manually lifted, gently restrained by their scruff, and inverted with their heads positioned at a slight angle of approximately 20° downwards. The injection was administered into the lower left abdominal quadrant. Following the injection, the mice were promptly returned to their respective home cages.

Diets

In accordance with previous reports [14, 15], the NAFLD model was established by feeding the mice a high fat diet for 12 weeks. High-fat diets (TP23300; 60% kcal fat) and standard diets (TP23302; 10% kcal fat) were purchased from Trophic Animal Feed High-Tech Co.,Ltd, China. After 7 days of acclimatization, C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into regular (control group) and high-fat diet (model group) groups. The control group was continuously fed a standard diet for 12 weeks, while the model group was continuously fed a high-fat diet for 12 weeks. After the 12-week dietary intervention, the development of hepatic steatosis was noninvasively assessed and confirmed by abdominal ultrasonography in those groups. The model group and control group include recipient mice and donor mice respectively. Then, blood samples, fecal samples, intestinal tissue, and liver tissue from the groups (control and model) were used for testing. The fecal samples were evaluated by 16 S rRNA gene sequencing 12 weeks later.

Animal modeling and grouping for fecal microbiota transplantation

To further observe the changes in gut microbiota, we conducted FMT B. pullicaecorum is an anaerobic bacterium [8]. The transplantation of solely anaerobic bacteria may not be as efficacious as FMT, which encompasses a diverse array of microorganisms [16]. Simultaneously, FMT involves the transfer of a complete gut microbiome ecosystem, which encompasses a diverse array of microorganisms that play a pivotal role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis and overall health. Comprehensive FMT is capable of simulating the conditions of the gastrointestinal tract. Moreover, to simulate gastrointestinal conditions, FMT was performed in our study. After conducting FMT, it is convenient to compare the changes in gut microbiota B.pullicaecorum and GPR41 among the four groups. To maintain bacterial flora stability, the recipient and donor mice received an antibiotic mixture every 12 h for 3 consecutive days [17]. The mixture consisted of imipenem (90 mg/kg), vancomycin (72 mg/kg), metronidazole (90 mg/kg), and ampicillin (180 mg/kg). The fecal contents were extracted surgically and diluted with PBS [18]. After centrifugation at 800 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected and administered to the recipient mice by gavage immediately. FMT was performed once a day for 72 h, and then twice a week for 8 weeks. Mice received FMT using the method described above. Specifically, the transplantation control group refers to control mice that received fecal supernatant from donor model mice, while the transplantation model group refers to model mice that received fecal supernatant from donor control mice. The mice in the model and control groups that received the supernatant were assigned to the transplantation control and transplantation model groups, respectively. After completing 8 weeks of FMT, blood samples, fecal samples, intestinal tissue, and liver tissue from the FMT groups (transplant control and transplant model) were used for testing. The weight of each mouse was monitored weekly throughout the study period. Blood samples were obtained from the eye socket vein and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The liver index was calculated as the ratio of liver to body weight [19, 20]. Then, the fecal samples, intestine, and liver tissues were stored at -80 °C.

16 S rRNA gene sequencing

The 16 S rRNA gene sequencing was conducted to further observe the differences in gut microbiota among 4 groups. Six mice per group were selected for 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. The total DNA was extracted by the OMEGA Soil DNA Kit. Then the V3-V4 region of 16 S was amplified by PCR through the Illumina platform [21]. The primers (338 F/806R, 338 F: ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA, 806R: GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT) were designed in the conserved regions of V3-V4, and sample-specific Barcodes were added. The reaction conditions are as follows: initial denaturation, 98℃ 2 min, denaturation, 98℃ 15s, annealing, 55℃ 30s, extension, 72℃ 30s, final extension, 72℃ 5 min, 10℃ 25-30Cycles. Species composition analysis was calculated using QIIME2(2019.4). To describe the alpha diversity, the sparse curve and species accumulation curve were calculated using QIIME2(2019.4). Nonmetric Multidimensional Scaling analysis was used to display the difference of OTU composition among samples using R package. Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis was calculated using Python LEfSe package. The Ribosomal Database Project (Classifier v.2.2) was used to classify the OTU representative sequences.

Detection of B. pullicaecorum

We conducted this test to observe the changes in B.pullicaecorum in the four groups, and B. pullicaecorum was detected by PCR. The total genomic DNA of B.pullicaecorum was extracted from each sample by the OMEGA Soil DNA Kit.

Histopathological assays, NAS and Chiu scoring

At the end of the experiment, the mice were anesthetized via an intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium solution before humane euthanasia to minimize distress to the mice. We endeavored to examine the microstructural alterations within the hepatic and intestinal tissues across 4 groups using Histopathological assays. The liver and intestinal tissue were processed after routine paraffin embedding. Serial sections were cut and stained with HE (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The NAS was calculated as described in a previous study [22]. Specifically, the NAS provides a semiquantitative assessment of disease activity, incorporating the grade of steatosis (0–3), lobular inflammation (0–3), and hepatocyte ballooning (0–2). Image analysis of liver and intestinal tissue was performed using the Scannercope digital pathology scanning system (aperio technologies, USA). Chiu’s scoring system is used to evaluate the extent of intestinal damage in each group, and it was used to quantitatively determine the histological scores of the intestine [23].

Detection of short-chain fatty acids in intestinal contents

To observe the status of short-chain fatty acids among various groups, we conducted this examination. Fecal samples (0.02 g) were mixed with 1.2 mL of PBS (0.1 mol/L, pH 6.5) and centrifuged at 5000 × g for 5 min. The supernatant (1 mL) was blended with 2.4 mL of absolute ethanol. Next, 850 µL of the solution with 1 mL of n-hexane and 100 µL of concentrated sulfuric acid was mixed in a new bottle. The bottle was incubated at 24–26 °C for 1 h and in water at 60 °C for 1 h. The upper layer was finally dispensed into high-performance liquid chromatography vials. The concentrations of formic, acetic, propionic, and butyric acids in the fecal solution were calculated according to the corresponding standard curves.

Expression of U62631.5 and GPR41

We performed this PCR assay to monitor the alterations in the expression levels of U62631.5 and GPR41 across 4 groups. Total RNA was prepared using Trizol (Invitrogen life technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Reverse transcription (RT, Takara, China) was performed to convert the extracted RNA into cDNA with a primer (Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, China) and RT Master Mix (Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, China) following incubation at 37 °C for 15 min and 85 °C for 5 s. The expression levels of U62631.5 and GPR41 were determined by SYBR Green I-based PCR (Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, China). The reaction conditions were 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 60 s. We used primers 5.0 to design the primers.

Cell and experimental design

HepG2 cell culture and the steatosis model

The relationship between U62631.5 and GPR41 was achieved through the use of dual luciferase assay, overexpression, and siRNA technology at the cellular level. Cell experiments were performed on HepG2 cells and the steatosis model. HepG2 cells were cultured in minimum essential medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA); 0.25% trypsin (with 1 mM EDTA, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used to harvest the cells. The steatosis model was established by induction using oleic acid and palmitic acid (Sigma, St. Louis, IL, USA) at a ratio of 1:2 (0.5 mM, 12 h) [24].

GPR41 promoter activity assay

To further clarify the role of U62631.5 in activating the promoter of GPR41, we evaluated promoter activity using the double-luciferase reporter detection system. The promoter activity reporting vector of GPR41 (Plvx NC/plvx-U62631.5) was constructed and co transferred with pGL3-GPR41 plasmid into HepG2 cells. In accordance with the manufacturer instructions for the Promega dual luciferase system kit, the luciferase activity intensity was detected after 24 h. These experiments were performed in two groups: Group 1, pLVX-NC-pGL3-GPR41; Group 2, pLVX-U62631.5-pGL3-GPR41.

Loss-of-function and gain-of-function

As a loss-of-function approach, HepG2 cells were transfected with SiU62631.5 or control siRNA. This experiment was conducted in the following groups: NC (control group), NM (model group), NCK (empty plasmid control group), NMK (empty plasmid model group), SiU62631.5-NC (SiRNA NC-control group), SiU62631.5-NM (SiRNA NM-model group). As a gain-of-function strategy, HepG2 cells were transfected with pLVX-NC/pLVX-U62631 or empty plasmid. The groups were as follows: NC (control group), NM (model group), NCK (empty plasmid control group), NMK (empty plasmid model group), pLVX-U62631.5-NC (pLVX-NC-control group), and pLVX-U62631.5-NM (pLVX-U62631.5-model group). All transfections were performed with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). Then, 24 h after transfection, fluorescence microscopy was performed and RNA was extracted for real-time PCR detection.

Secondary structure and sequence analysis of U62631.5

The prediction of RNA secondary structure is instrumental in analyzing whether U62631.5 possesses functionality. By using RNA fold online prediction software [25–27], lncRNA sequences were input online to further analyze the secondary structure of lncRNA. The sequences of U62631.5 and GPR41 were analyzed in the Ensembl database to identify their locations on the genome. On the basis of the gene location, BLAST analysis was performed to further analyze the relationship between U62631.5 and GPR41.

Statistical analysis

All statistical data were analyzed by Statistical Program for Social Sciences (SPSS) 17.0 software (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The significance of difference for single and multiple comparisons was determined by 2-factor analysis of variance (ANOVA)or one-way ANOVA. Our experiments were repeated three times and a value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The sequence and the conservation of U62631.5 and GPR41

Our study found that U62631.5 is located on chromosome 19 at approximate locations chr19:35330843–35,331,920 in the UCSC database (http://genome.ucsc.edu/). Therefore, we analyzed the sequences of U62631.5 and GPR41 using the Ensembl database. The BLAST database suggested that U62631.5 and GPR41 were mostly overlapping. Several studies have reported strong co-expression of numerous lncRNAs with their mRNAs through the formation of duplex complexes [28, 29]. Using the RNA fold web server (http://rna.tbi.univie.ac.at/cgibin/RNAWebSuite/RNAfold.cgi), we predicted the secondary structure of U62631.5 (Fig. 1). The findings together suggested that U62631.5 may form a complex stem-loop structure, which indicated that U62631.5 performs a certain function. We also conducted a conservation analysis through the Ensembl database, which showed that U62631.5 and GPR41 were highly conserved in mice. Therefore, we believe that the U62631.5 and GPR41 sequences in humans are highly conserved in mice. GPR41 is conserved among species [30].

With the extension of feeding time, the two groups showed significant differences in weight changes: the weight of mice increased in the model group, while it decreased in the control group. Histopathological results

HE staining showed that in the control group, the structure of hepatocytes was normal and the hepatic lobules were clear and evenly arranged (Fig. 4). The model group showed clear ballooning injury near the central vein with inflammation and steatosis nearby. The transplantation control group showed slightly unclear hepatic lobule structure with a small amount of steatosis and necrosis and a small amount of inflammatory cell infiltration. However, in comparison with the model group, the structure of liver lobules became clearer and the steatosis of liver tissue decreased in the transplantation model group. After FMT, hepatic steatosis was improved in the model group.

The results of HE staining of the small intestine are presented in Fig. 4. The intestinal tissues showed an intact mucosa and uniform folds in the control group. The intestinal villi were deformed and shed in model group. Intestinal mucosal injury was significant in the model group. Moreover, the intestinal villi were slightly lodged without shedding in the transplantation control group. In comparison with the model group, the transplantation model group showed improved lodging of intestinal villi and reduced shedding. Intestinal mucosal injury after FMT was better than that in the model group.

After FMT, the liver index in the transplantation model group was 4.33 ± 0.09, which was significantly lower than that in the model group. In contrast, the liver index in the transplantation control group was higher than that in the control group.

The expression of PCR and short-chain fatty acids

PCR showed that U62631.5 and GPR41 expression in blood, liver, and intestine in the model group were significantly lower than those in the control group (Fig. 5; Table 3). U62631.5 and GPR41 expression in the transplantation model group was lower than that in the transplantation control group and higher than that in the model group. Thus, expression of U62631.5 and GPR41 increased after FMT in the high-fat diet group. Interaction analysis showed that the expression of U62631.5 and GPR41 in model group (blood, liver, small intestine) increased significantly by FMT. Table 4 shows the results of short-chain fatty acids in intestinal contents. The butyric acid content was 21.1 ± 1.30 mg/mL in the control group and 1.88 ± 0.42 mg/mL in the model group. Butyric acid content was lower in the transplantation model group than the transplantation control group, and was higher than that in the model group. Thus, butyric acid content increased after FMT in the high-fat diet group. The butyric acid content in the control group after transplantation was lower than that before transplantation. Thus, butyric acid content decreased after FMT in the control group.

Discussion

The present study assessed the relationship between the probiotic B. pullicaecorum and U62631.5 in NAFLD (Fig. 11). The findings showed that the intestinal flora of the model group was different from that of the control group at the animal level. Critically, histopathological evaluation using the NAS revealed a significant amelioration of hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and ballooning in the transplantation model group compared to the untreated model group, providing direct morphological evidence for the hepatoprotective effect of FMT. Furthermore, the results of 16 S rRNA sequencing showed that intestinal bacteria were different in the four groups. At the phylum level, the main dominant phyla were Firmicutes, Bacteroides, and Actinomycetes. In the model group, the intestinal bacteria after FMT were different from those before FMT, but were similar to those in the control group. These differences may have been caused by transplantation of bacteria, suggesting that the intestinal flora reached the third state after FMT. Our results were consistent with the findings reported by Markle JG et al. [32]. Second, our results found that the expression of B. pullicaecorum was higher in the control group than in the NAFLD group. F.prausnitzii is a beneficial human gut microbe belonging to the Firmicutes [33]. A study from Belgium suggests that treatments with B.pullicaecorum and the mix of six butyrate-producers improved epithelial barrier integrity [34]. F.prausnitzii has been proposed as a potential probiotic for treatment of gut inflammation, as the microbe stimulates the expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine interleukin 10 in peripheral blood mononuclear cells [35]. Based on our results and the above literatures, we speculate that B. pullicaecorum bacteria may be next-generation probiotics.

Third, our results show that the butyric acid content in the intestinal contents of mice fed a high-fat diet was lower than that in the control group. After FMT, the content was lower in the transplantation model group than in the transplantation control group, and higher than that in the model group. Liang et al. found that the butyrate content in the NAFLD group was lower than that in the control group [36]. FMT has been reported to correct the intestinal flora disorder of mice fed a high-fat diet, increase the production of butyric acid in the intestine, and reverse steatohepatitis [37]. Additionally, the higher amount of butyrate in the control group could also be due to the higher fiber content in the control diet compared to the high-fat diet that the model group received. This is because a high fiber diet provides more fermentation substrates for intestinal bacteria, thereby promoting the production of butyric acid [38]. Our findings support those results. Fourth, GPR41 was first found in the intestine. As a fatty acid receptor, it can sense short-chain fatty acids (including acetic acid, propionic acid and butyric acid) from the digestive cavity [30]. Short-chain fatty acids play an important role in adipogenesis by activating the G protein coupled receptor (GPR41) expressed in the small intestine, colon, and adipose tissue [39, 40]. The GPR41 gene locus is located on chromosome 19 (chr19: 35849362–35851387). Our bioinformatics analysis suggested that lncRNA U62631.5 (chr19: 35810163–35838263) is also located on chromosome 19. Its gene length is 435 bp, and the gene adjacent to U62631.5 is GPR41. The Ensembl database showed an overlapping relationship between the two genes.

The expression of U62631.5 and GPR41 in blood, liver, and intestine in the control group was significantly higher than that in the model group at the animal level. The results showed that the expression of U62631.5 and GPR41 increased after FMT in the high-fat diet group. Conversely, the expression of U62631.5 and GPR41 decreased after FMT in the control group. The differential effects of FMT observed between the transplantation model group and the transplantation control group can be attributed to their distinct baseline conditions. In the high-fat diet-induced model group, characterized by gut dysbiosis and metabolic dysfunction, FMT served a corrective role by restoring microbial balance, enriching beneficial taxa like B.pullicaecorum, and reactivating the U62631.5/GPR41 pathway. Conversely, in the healthy control group with an initially stable microbiota, FMT introduced a foreign microbial community that temporarily perturbed this equilibrium, leading to the subtle reductions noted in butyrate levels and B. pullicaecorum abundance. This underscores that the therapeutic benefit of FMT is context-dependent, being most evident in a state of pre-existing dysbiosis. To study the relationship between these two genes, changes in GPR41 were observed by siRNA interference and overexpression of U62631.5. GPR41 was not expressed by PCR after interference of U62631.5. Conversely, after overexpression of U62631.5, the expression of GPR41 was also increased, suggesting that U62631.5 and GPR41 have a complementary relationship. Simultaneously, luciferase assay detected GPR41 gene promoter activity after U62631.5 overexpression, and the results showed that U62631.5 significantly increased GPR41 gene promoter activity. Dietary intake of SCFAs in mice protects against HFD-induced hepatic lipid metabolism [41]. Nevertheless, our findings could not confirm whether GPR41 was a protective factor or a damaging factor [42]. Trompette et al. [6] described asthma exacerbation in GPR41 knockout mice, while Kim et al. [43] described reduced colitis in GPR41 knockout mice. Thus, GPR41 and U62631.5 together may function as a protective factor. Furthermore, we predicted the secondary structure of U62631.5 through RNAfold software, which suggested that U62631.5 formed a complex stem-ring structure, indicating that U62631.5 has a certain function. While our study provides compelling preclinical evidence for the role of B.pullicaecorum in ameliorating NAFLD features via the U62631.5/GPR41 axis, its direct clinical application in patients requires careful consideration and further investigation. To date, no clinical trials have specifically evaluated B.pullicaecorum FMT or probiotic supplementation for NAFLD treatment. However, emerging evidence from other disease contexts supports its therapeutic potential and informs safety considerations. For instance, B.pullicaecorum and its metabolite butyrate have demonstrated anti-cancer effects in bladder urothelial cells, indicating its ability to exert beneficial systemic effects beyond the colon [44]. The findings collectively suggest that is a promising next-generation probiotic candidate. Translating our findings to the clinic will necessitate rigorous, strain-specific safety evaluations, optimization of delivery methods, and ultimately, controlled clinical trials to establish efficacy and safety in NAFLD patients.

Limitations

Our study had several limitations. First, the sample size of our study was small, and future studies are needed to expand the sample size and confirm the results of this study. Second, we did not include adults in our study. In the future, we will conduct relevant research in adults. Third, although we demonstrated improvements in hepatic steatosis, intestinal barrier integrity, and butyrate-related pathways following FMT, we did not include a full panel of systemic metabolic parameters—such as serum lipids, glucose tolerance, or insulin sensitivity—which would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the metabolic impact of B.pullicaecorum-associated FMT in NAFLD. Future studies with expanded metabolic phenotyping are warranted to fully elucidate the therapeutic potential of this microbial intervention. Fourth, we only studied a specific intestinal bacterium, and we will evaluate more intestinal bacteria in future studies. A further limitation is the reliance on H&E staining alone to evaluate hepatic steatosis and injury, which may not definitively differentiate lipid accumulation from other cellular changes or fully capture the spectrum of metabolic-associated steatohepatitis. Although ultrasound findings supported the presence of hepatic steatosis, we did not assess other objective biomarkers of inflammation and fibrosis. Finally, while histology was evaluated using HE staining and NAS, the sole use of HE has inherent limitations in precisely distinguishing lipid accumulation from other cellular alterations. Future studies should incorporate additional techniques such as Oil Red O staining, hepatic triglyceride quantification, and broader molecular and histochemical analyses to strengthen histopathological and biochemical validation.

Conclusions

This study’s findings indicated that U62631.5 may play a role in NAFLD by regulating the adjacent gene GPR41 and the related intestinal bacteria. The findings suggest that B.pullicaecorum may play a protective role in this process. These findings have clinical application value as a potential treatment strategy mediated by microbiota, opening up a new approach for the prevention and treatment of NAFLD.
