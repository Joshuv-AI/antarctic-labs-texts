Introduction

Laparoscopic splenectomy is associated with several benefits over conventional open splenectomy such as lower post-operative pain, early recovery, and better cosmetic outcome [1]. The utilization of laparoscopy in severe splenic injuries following blunt abdominal trauma is limited because of difficulties encountered due to ongoing bleeding, chances of missed injury, and lack of experience [1]. However, recent reports have shown successful attempts of laparoscopic splenectomy in hemodynamically stable trauma patients in the acute setting [1,2]. The aim of this case report was to describe the utility of laparoscopic splenectomy in the setting of polytrauma in a 20-year-old patient. We report a case of grade V splenic injury successfully treated with laparoscopic splenectomy following the failure of conservative management. The work has been reported as being in line with the SCARE criteria [3].

Case presentation

A 20-year-old male who was a motorcyclist collided with a truck at high velocity. He sustained blunt injuries to the head, chest, abdomen, and left lower limb. On admission to the local hospital, his pulse rate was 108 per minute and his blood pressure was 120/85 mmHg. His Glasgow coma scale was 8/15. Examination of the chest revealed reduced air entry over the left side with increased respiratory rate. The left upper outer quadrant of the abdomen was tender. He received fluid resuscitation with crystalloids, was intubated and ventilated, and was transferred to the level one trauma center for further care. His chest radiograph showed a left sixth rib fracture with hemopneumothorax and the X-ray pelvis was normal. He had a closed subtrochanteric fracture of the left femur. Focused abdominal sonography in trauma (FAST) scan revealed moderate free fluid in the abdomen. He underwent a contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the chest and abdomen which showed a left sixth rib fracture with mild hemopneumothorax with underlying lung contusion and grade V splenic injury with abdominal free fluid (Fig. 1). His non-contrast computed tomography (CT) brain did not reveal any intracranial hemorrhage and CT cervical spine was normal. His complete blood count and basic biochemistry were within normal limits with a hemoglobin of 10 g/dl.

Contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen showing a grade V splenic injury with abdominal free fluid.

He was managed by a multidisciplinary team that consisted of a general surgeon, neurosurgeon, orthopaedic surgeon, and critical care specialist. A left intercostal drain was inserted with an initial drain of 200 ml of blood-stained fluid. The splenic injury was managed conservatively as he was hemodynamically stable. The head injury was also managed conservatively and supportive care was given in the intensive care unit. The fracture of the left femur was managed with immobilization in view if later fixation. After the initial resuscitation on day 1, the hemoglobin was 9 g/dl and was stable till day three. He did not receive any blood transfusion during the first three days. On day 3, he developed tachycardia and low blood pressure requiring a blood transfusion. The chest drain was minimal on the day of deterioration and there was no worsening of the swelling of the lower limb to suggest ongoing bleeding at these sites. Therefore, a decision was reached to go ahead with emergency splenectomy.

He was resuscitated with three pints of packed cells and was stabilized before surgery. A decision was taken to go ahead with a laparoscopic approach with a low threshold to convert to an open procedure. The patient was positioned supine with a 30 degree right lateral tilt. Access was achieved with a 10 mm subumbilical camera port and three other ports as shown (Fig. 2) with a pneumoperitoneum of 15 mmHg. The vitals were maintained during the procedure with a blood pressure of 120/85 mmHg and a pulse rate of 90 to 100 beats per minute. A large hemoperitoneum of 1.5 l was noted with a grade V splenic hilar injury and associated active bleeding. The clots were sucked out and the hilar region was packed with gauze swabs to achieve temporary hemostasis. The short gastric vessels were divided to gain better access to the splenic hilum. The hilar vascular branches were dissected and divided between haemolocks. Finally, few remaining ligamentous attachments were divided using an ultrasonic energy device to completely mobilise the spleen (Fig. 3). The spleen was placed in a sterile uribag with a purse string and brought through the subumbilical incision. The specimen was crushed and manually morcellated and delivered in piecemeal. A thorough peritoneal survey was performed and other injuries were excluded. Specifically, there was no diaphragmatic or bowel injury. The view of zone one was not as good as in laparotomy. However, it was adequate to rule out any significant retroperitoneal injuries.

The post-operative period was uneventful and he had a good recovery from the head injury as well. He later underwent internal fixation for the femur fracture and received the post-splenectomy vaccination 2 weeks after surgery and antibiotic prophylaxis. At 3 months follow up he was well and had no complications.

Discussion

The utility of laparoscopy in the management of blunt trauma is controversial. However, several series have shown acceptable outcomes for both solid organ and visceral injuries in experienced hands [4]. Furthermore, there are several reports describing the utility of the laparoscopic approach for isolated splenic injuries in stable patients [5,6]. Nasr and colleagues reported patients with grade I to III patients who underwent laparoscopic splenectomy after stabilization. There were no cases of re-opening or peri-operative complications [6]. Carobbi et al. reported 10 cases of severe splenic injuries that were treated successfully by laparoscopic approach. There was a wide variation of time taken to achieve complete hemostasis (13 to 125 min). However, there were no complications related to the abdomen in the postoperative period [5].

The algorithm for the management of splenic injury following blunt abdominal trauma has evolved over the past decades. Non-operative management and spleen-conserving procedures have replaced routine open splenectomy. Imaging with CT and proper grading of the injury is mandatory before deciding on conservative management. Hemodynamically stable patients with isolated splenic injuries without active bleeding should qualify for conservative management. Furthermore, intensive care and 24-hour surgical theatre facilities should be available in such institutions. Although conservative management is an option in severe splenic injuries, the failure rates are high ranging from 10 to 40% [7]. Therefore, early clinical decision is important based on the mechanism of injury, clinical status, and imaging to select patients for early intervention instead of a conservative approach. Polytrauma patients with severe splenic injuries should be considered for early operative management, especially in the context of multi-organ injuries. This is because subsequent clinical deterioration may give rise to clinical dilemmas and difficult decision-making due to confounding factors due to multiple injuries and subsequent complications. In our patient, there were head, chest, abdominal and limb injuries. Conservative management opted as he was hemodynamically stable as he was treated at a level 1 trauma center with intensive care and 24-hour theatre facilities. Conservative management failed on day 3 of the injury which required resuscitation with blood transfusion followed by emergency surgery. In retrospect, due to the multiple injuries, elective surgery on day 1 may have been a better option for this patient when he was hemodynamically stable.

Splenectomy is not considered for all severe splenic injuries. In resourceful settings, an angiographic embolization is an acceptable method to further define and treat severe splenic injuries [8]. Unfortunately, angiographic embolization was not performed in our patient due to nonavailability.

During laparoscopy, the key initial step is achieving complete hemostasis as early as possible. This ensures proper resuscitation and also good visibility to perform the surgery with ease and minimal intraoperative complications. In our patient, despite the haemoperitoneum of 1.5 l and grade V splenic hilar injury with active bleeding, complete hemostasis was achieved within 30 min, and subsequent procedure was performed without difficulty. However, a low threshold of conversion to an open procedure is necessary, especially in difficult cases. Postoperatively, patients should be observed for potential missed injuries and rebleeding in the acute setting.

Conclusion

We report a patient with polytrauma who underwent a successful emergency laparoscopic splenectomy following the failure of conservative management of grade V splenic hilar injury. The key initial step was achieving complete hemostasis as early as possible. The utility of the laparoscopic approach for this patient was successful without major challenges during the procedure.
