Bioethanol production using sugar beet (Beta vulgaris) juice as substrate by stress-tolerant yeast strains isolated from Areke
Authors: Mulugeta Fentahun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditionally fermented beverages are potential sources of industrially relevant microorganisms. Among alternative energy sources, biofuels have gained increasing attention as replacements for fossil fuels. This study aimed to investigate the potential stress-tolerant yeast strains isolated from Areke for bioethanol production using sugar beet (Beta vulgaris) juice as substrate. In the current study, four wild yeast strains capable of withstanding multiple stresses were isolated from Areke. Fresh sugar beets were gathered from a local supplier in Debre Markos town, and the physicochemical characterization of the sugar beet juice was determined using standard methods. Ethanol production was optimized using four selected Saccharomyces cerevisiae strains, namely MUA1F (OR209275.1), MUA3F (OR209274.1), MUA11F (OR209277.1), and MUA15F (OR209276.1), using sugar beet juice as the substrate. Most Saccharomyces cerevisiae strains showed better fermentation performance under high osmotic pressure, ethanol concentration, and temperature stress than the Methera standard yeast in ethanol production. Fermentation optimization showed that these native Saccharomyces cerevisiae strains efficiently converted sugar beet juice to ethanol without requiring many pretreatments, indicating their potential for industrial applications. These findings suggest that the Saccharomyces cerevisiae isolates MUA3F and MUA15F should be further assessed, improved, and optimized for industrial bioethanol production because of their fermentation potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45172-2.Keywords: Agro-industrial substrate, Biofuel, Fungi, Traditional beverageSubject terms: Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Microbiology

Introduction

Biofuels produced from biomass are the primary fuels that replace petroleum1. Numerous issues with nonrenewable fuels, including increasing oil prices, unstable environmental conditions, and energy supply scarcity have led to the world is now search for environmentally friendly and sustainable alternative energy sources2. Renewable biomass fuels reduce greenhouse gas emissions, improve energy efficiency, and are more biodegradable than fossil fuels3,4. Moreover, owing to the problems associated with the use of fossil fuels, biofuel production has gained increasing attention5. Sugar beet juice is gaining attention in the field of bioethanol production because of its clean and highly fermentable sugar content with minimal impurities or fermentation inhibitors that are suitable for direct fermentation6,7. This makes it an ideal substrate for obtaining high ethanol yields without requiring many pretreatments compared with molasses. Research on sugar beet juice has the potential to be a valuable feedstock in integrated biorefineries for the sustainable production of biofuels, biochemicals, and other value-added products8,9.

Indigenous fermented beverages are considered a significant source of yeasts that produce ethanol and can withstand a variety of stressful situations, such as lack of nutrients and complexity, low pH, temperature fluctuations, and high osmotic stress10–14. Several reports have stated that indigenous fermented drinks are an important habitat for yeasts in general and ethanol-producing yeasts in particular15. One of the challenges of bioethanol production is obtaining efficient microorganisms that can tolerate a wide range of stressors. This has prompted the search for robust ethanologenic yeasts that can meet the demands of the bioethanol industry16. Searching for Saccharomyces cerevisiae that can tolerate low pH, ethanol efficiency, high temperature, and osmotic pressure in industrial ethanol production is one of the top research priorities11,14,17.

The capacity of yeasts to withstand several stressors has significant potential for application in large-scale fermentation11,18. Yeast isolates with tolerance to various stress conditions in vivo have been found in previous studies14,19,20. However, the characterization of stress-tolerant yeast strains from local drinks for industrial bioethanol production remains insufficient. The development of yeast strains with high-stress tolerance is currently one of the biggest challenges in bioethanol production. The multi-stress-tolerant yeast strains in this study could serve as starter cultures and substitutes for commercial yeast strains in industrial bioethanol production. Therefore, the objective of this study was to maximize bioethanol production from sugar beet juice using stress-tolerant yeast strains under optimal conditions.

According to their capacity to withstand many stresses, four strains of yeast, specifically S. cerevisiae strains, were chosen from the NCBI with their accession numbers, namely, Saccharomyces cerevisiae isolate MUA1F (OR209275.1), Saccharomyces cerevisiae isolate MUA3F (OR209274.1), Saccharomyces cerevisiae isolate MUA11F (OR209277.1), and Saccharomyces cerevisiae isolate MUA15F (OR209276.1). Multi-stress tolerance yeasts were identified as Saccharomyces cerevisiae strains after isolation and characterization in previous Sections21. The four multi-stress-tolerant yeast strains selected in this study can be useful for industrial bioethanol production.

Materials and methods

Raw material preparation

This study was conducted in the Microbiology Laboratory of the Department of Biology at Debre Markos University. Ten kg of fresh sugar beet samples were purchased from a supplier in Debre Markos. The raw sugar beets were washed with clean water and cut into small pieces after being peeled. The pieces were crushed using a mechanical juicer to extract juice. The extracted juice was filtered through a muslin cloth to obtain a raw juice concentrate and remove residual solids. The juice was preserved at 4 °C to prevent degradation during storage.

Sugar beet juice characterization

The physicochemical characteristics of raw sugar beet juice, including moisture content, pH, specific gravity, sucrose, and sulfated ash were estimated according to the method described by22,23. The pH of the sugar beet juice was measured at room temperature using a digital pH meter (PHS-3C). A refractometer (Atagodensimeter model 2312) was used to test the Brix of the sugar beet juice. A Baume universal hydrometer was used to estimate the specific gravities of the samples. The Fehling method was used to calculate the total sugar24. The 3, 5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method was used to evaluate the amount of reducing sugar25. The oven (MB45) drying method was used to estimate the moisture contents of the samples. The ash content was estimated using a muffle furnace set at 650 °C for 2 h until the weight remained constant.

Method of bioethanol production

Fermentation medium preparation

Sugar beet juice was diluted with distilled water to achieve the required Brix using a refractometer, and 0.7 g L-1 ammonium sulfate was added to the medium and homogenized using a magnetic stirrer. Finally, dilute sulfuric acid was used to adjust the pH to 4.5, and the mixture was autoclaved for 15 min at 121 °C. The sterilized sugar beet juice medium was used for the propagation of yeast strains and fermentation to produce ethanol26.

Yeast strain used in experiments

Saccharomyces cerevisiae isolates MUA1F (OR209275.1), MUA3F (OR209274.1), MUA11F (OR209277.1), and MUA15F (OR209276.1) with their accession numbers were used in this study. The methods used for the isolation and identification of yeast strains have been described in our previous studies21. Stress-tolerant yeast strains were previously identified as S. cerevisiae using the ITS-5.8S rRNA region. Species level was assigned based on BLASTn identity score match > 99% against sequences in GenBank. Sequenced data were edited using the BioEdit software package and deposited in GenBank in the NCBI database, and accession numbers were obtained. Four native wild yeast strains were evaluated for their capacity to ferment sugar beet juice because of their exceptional ability to withstand various stresses (Supplementary Figure S1). The standard industrial yeast strain was obtained from the Methara Bioethanol Factory in Ethiopia and used as a control for comparison with other strains. The yeast strains were maintained on Yeast Extract Peptone Dextrose (YEPD) agar slants at 4 °C, and their vitality was checked by subculturing them once a month.

Strains of yeast propagation

A loop full of 48 h fresh cultures was taken from YEPD solid media and inoculated into each flask containing 100 ml of sterilized sugar beet juice. The flasks were incubated for 24 h at 30 °C. Then, to increase the cell number, 100 ml of sterilized new batch sugar beet juice was added to each flask, and further incubated for another 24 h (see Supplementary Figure S4). A 48 h propagated yeast culture was prepared as an inoculum for the fermentation of ethanol in 200 ml sugar beet juice media27.

Optimizing the parameters of the fermentation process

The concentration of sugar beet juice (10, 15, 20, and 25°Brix), temperature (25, 30, 35, and 40 °C), pH (3.5, 4.0, 4.5, 5.0, and 5.5), ammonium sulfate addition (1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 g/l), different supplements (urea, yeast extract, and ammonium nitrate), and incubation durations (24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h) were optimized using the fed-batch fermentation method28–30. The one-factor-at-a-time (VAT) method was applied. Inoculation with 48 h of propagated yeast cells was performed in 800 ml bottles containing 300 ml of sterilized diluted sugar beet juice. All treatments were performed in triplicate, and the mean and standard error were calculated.

Fermentation under optimum conditions

The effect of varying concentrations of sugar beet juice (10, 15, 20, and 25°Brix) on ethanol concentration was evaluated at an optimum temperature of 30 °C and pH of 4.5 after 72 h in fed-batch culture. Each yeast strain was cultured at varying incubation temperatures (25, 30, 35, and 40 °C) for ethanol production and was examined at the optimum 20°Brix and 4.5 pH after 72 h. The effect of various pH values (3.5, 4.0, 4.5, 5.0, and 5.5) on the ethanol production by the yeast strains was assessed at the optimum temperature of 30 °C and 20°Brix, after 72 h. Each yeast strain’s response to ammonium sulfate concentration (1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 g/l) in terms of ethanol production was assessed at the optimum 30 °C, 20°Brix, pH 4.5, after 72 h. The effects of the addition of supplements (1 g/l of yeast extract, urea, and ammonium nitrate) on each strain’s capacity to produce ethanol were also investigated at the optimum conditions of 30 °C, 20°Brix, and pH 4.5 after 72 h. The yeast strains were cultivated for varying periods of fermentation (24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h) at optimum parameters of 30 °C, 20°Brix, and pH 4.5. The ethanol concentration for each fermentation parameter was calculated at the end of the fermentation period.

Ethanol estimation

Ethanol estimation was performed using the Alcohol by Volume (ABV) method, which determines the ethanol content in a fermented sugar beet juice sample based on the change in specific gravity (SG) before and after fermentation31. Using a universal Baume hydrometer, the specific gravity of sugar beet juice was determined before fermentation began, and the result was recorded as the Original Gravity (OG). After fermentation was completed, the specific gravity of the fermented sugar beet juice was measured again and recorded as the Final Gravity (FG).

Analytical methods

A refractometer (Atagodensimeter model 2312) was used to measure the Brix of the fermented samples. UV–Vis Spectrometer (Spectrum Lab. 725 s, India) set to 600 nm was used to determine the optical density of the propagating culture optical density32. A universal Baume hydrometer was used to test the specific gravity of the fermented sugar beet juice to calculate its ethanol content33. Data analysis

All data were analyzed using version 25.0 of the Statistical Package for Social Sciences software. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to determine the means and standard deviations of the triplicate tests, and a post hoc test was used to determine whether there were significant variations in the means (p < 0.05). The difference was considered statistically significant at P < 0.05 was less than 0.05.

Results and discussion

The physicochemical properties of sugar beet juice from a local supplier in Debre Markos town were 13.21 ± 1.84% total sugar, 12.26 ± 0.44% sucrose, and 0.41 ± 0.79% sulfated ash (w/v). The quantity of reducing sugars was 0.96 ± 0.12%. The moisture content of the sugar beet juice was 76.30 ± 0.26%. Moreover, the pH of sugar beet juice was 5.53 ± 0.08. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies34,35.

The effect of the concentration of sugar beet juice

The effects of varying sugar beet juice concentrations (10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% °Brix) on the percentage V/V of ethanol were investigated (Table 1; Supplementary Figure S2). Among the factors that typically influence the rate at which yeasts produce ethanol is the amount of sugar present in the fermentation medium36. According to the current investigation, the four Areke yeast strains produced a range of ethanol concentrations from 7.43 ± 0.17 to 11.77 ± 0.10% (v/v) of the amount in Brix of sugar beet juice between 10 and 25 (°Brix). The Methera industrial yeast strain served as a control for comparison with other strains. The findings indicated that ethanol production increased with sugar concentration up to 20°Brix; thereafter, no discernible improvement was observed. The S. cerevisiae isolates MUA15F (11.77 ± 0.10% (v/v)) and MUA3F (11.71 ± 0.15% (v/v)) had the highest ethanol concentrations at 72 h, ranging between 15 and 20°Brix. At high sugar concentrations, the S. cerevisiae isolates MUA3F and MUA15F produced the highest amounts of ethanol compared to the control. The quantity of ethanol produced in this study was consistent with the findings of other researchers37,38. All yeast strains produced less ethanol at high concentrations of sugar beet juice (25%). This may be due to the high substrate concentrations being inhibitory to the fermentation process of yeasts because of osmotic stress.

Effect of fermentation temperature

Temperature significantly affects on the fermenting organisms ability to develop, metabolize, and produce ethanol24,36,39. The ethanol production results at various temperatures (25, 30, 35, and 40 °C) are presented in Table 2. All S. cerevisiae strains showed good fermentation performance within the 25–35 °C temperature range. Nevertheless, the S. cerevisiae strains differed in their extent of temperature tolerance and ethanol production. At 40 °C, the S. cerevisiae isolates MUA1F and MUA3F continued to produce ethanol at relatively high levels, achieved 8.53 ± 0.28% (v/v) and 9.81 ± 0.10% (v/v), respectively. This showed that the S. cerevisiae strains have a wider temperature tolerance and ethanol production capacity than the control. Previous research has also demonstrated that thermotolerant yeast strains can effectively produce ethanol and grow at temperatures as high as 40–50°C40,41. At 30 °C, the S. cerevisiae isolate MUA3F had the highest ethanol concentration (11.16 ± 0.08% (v/v)), followed by MUA15F (10.54 ± 0.05% (v/v)) and MUA11F (10.33 ± 0.08% (v/v)). These findings are consistent with those of previous studies39,42,43.

Effect of pH

Optimum pH levels are necessary for plasma membrane-based proteins and enzymes to function44–46. The results of the study on the effect of pH ranging between 3.5 and 5.5 on ethanol production are shown in Table 3. At pH values between 4.0 and 4.5, the majority of the S. cerevisiae strains produced the highest range of ethanol. Nevertheless, even at pH values of 5.0 and 5.5, the S. cerevisiae isolates MUA1F and MUA3F maintained high ethanol levels, MUA1F achieved 9.20 ± 0.05% (v/v) and 8.14 ± 0.12% (v/v), while MUA3F achieved 10.51 ± 0.29% (v/v) and 9.92 ± 0.07% (v/v), respectively. Similarly, the S. cerevisiae isolate MUA15F produced 9.35 ± 0.30% (v/v) and 10.04 ± 0.10% (v/v) ethanol at pH 5.5 and 5.0, respectively. According to this study, the Areke fermentation yeast strains had remarkable pH range adaptability. A similar study indicated that S. cerevisiae can readily grow over a wide pH range and produce an optimum ethanol concentration10,24. At a pH of 4.5, the S. cerevisiae isolate MUA3F produced high ethanol and reached 11.05 ± 0.08% (v/v), whereas MUA15F recorded 10.90 ± 0.09% (v/v) ethanol. Numerous researchers noticed that S. cerevisiae optimum pH was 4.539,43,47,48.

The effect of supplement concentration

Adequate nitrogen is frequently added to sugar beet juice to encourage ethanol production and yeast growth49–51. Numerous experiments have been conducted to optimize nitrogen sources and other supplements51–53. The effect of ammonium sulfate addition to the fermentation medium on ethanol production was assessed at concentrations ranging from 1 to 5 g/l (Table 4). Ethanol production showed a slight numerical increase with the addition of ammonium sulfate among the tested S. cerevisiae strains. According to these results, 1 g/l ammonium sulfate concentration was sufficient to achieve maximal ethanol production, and further supplementation offered no additional benefit. The result was consistent with other research findings54. Generally, the Areke yeast strains efficiently fermented the medium and produced the highest ethanol with minimal nitrogen supplementation. From an economic perspective, using lower ammonium sulfate concentrations is advantageous as it reduces production costs without affecting ethanol production.

The effect of incubation times

Fermentation time also greatly influences ethanol production by yeasts56,57. The effect of incubation duration on ethanol production after fermentation was determined at 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h (Fig. 1). After 72 h of fermentation, all yeast strains produced the highest levels of ethanol. The Saccharomyces cerevisiae isolate MUA3F (11.14 ± 0.08% (v/v)) produced significantly (P < 0.05) greater amounts of ethanol than the other strains after 72 h. These results are consistent with those of previous studies58,59. Ethanol production increased gradually; however, as the incubation period increased, the ethanol concentration decreased. This could be because the yeast cells were no longer able to survive and consume ethanol57,60. The present findings support those of previous studies showing that the optimization of sugar beet juice fermentation parameters, including temperature, pH, time, and substrate concentration, is crucial for maximizing ethanol production61,62.

Conclusion

The results of this study showed that stress-tolerant Saccharomyces cerevisiae strains isolated from Areke effectively fermented sugar beet (Beta vulgaris) juice without any pretreatment, showing promise for industrial bioethanol production. Among the Saccharomyces cerevisiae strains evaluated, Saccharomyces cerevisiae isolates MUA3F and MUA15F produced maximum alcohol under different optimized fermentation conditions and have the potential to be used for industrial bioethanol production. These findings highlight the potential of native Saccharomyces cerevisiae strains as robust bioethanol producers, especially in challenging industrial bioethanol production environments. The results of this investigation suggest promising future applications, such as the development of cost-effective and sustainable bioethanol production using sugar beet juice as a substrate. Traditional alcoholic beverages (Areke) may serve as a potential yeast source for producing high concentrations of bioethanol. The limitation of this study is that all experiments were conducted under controlled, and small-scale laboratory conditions only. Further studies should be conducted to analyze the performance of yeast strains in producing ethanol at a pilot scale to determine their commercial feasibility.
