Double Burden of Malnutrition in Mother and Child Pairs in an Urban Resettlement Colony of East Delhi: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Jyoti Singh, Pragti Chhabra, Somdatta Patra, Deepak Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Context:The simultaneous presence of undernutrition along with overweight or obesity is known as the double burden of malnutrition (DBM). In mother-child pairs, DBM can manifest as either an undernourished child with an overnourished mother or an undernourished mother with an overnourished child. To study the prevalence of DBM in mother and child pairs in an urban resettlement colony of East Delhi.Material and Methods:An observational, cross-sectional study was conducted among 250 mother-child pairs in an urban resettlement colony of East Delhi. Data was collected by house-to-house visits using an interviewer administered questionnaire. Anthropometric measurements were recorded. Classification of nutritional status of children was conducted according to World Health Organization (WHO) child growth standards. Anthro Plus-software 2009 (WHO) was used for calculating anthropometric parameters. The weight for age, height for age and weight for height z-scores in children were computed. The data were summarized using frequencies and proportions. The P-value was calculated to assess the association between two categorical variables.Results:Among children, 43% were underweight, 46% were stunted, and 20% were wasted. Among mothers, 24% were overweight and 4% obese, while 18.4% were thin. The prevalence of DBM was observed as underweight child-overweight mother (UC-OM) 9.6%, stunted child-overweight mother (SC-OM) 12.8%, and wasted child-overweight mother (WC-OM) 4.8%. The overall prevalence of DBM in any form among mother-child pairs was 15.2%.Conclusions:There is a need for integrated nutrition strategies addressing both undernutrition in children and overnutrition in mothers within the same household.

INTRODUCTION

Coexistence of undernutrition along with overnutrition with-in individuals, households or population is called double burden of malnutrition (DBM). At household level, the DBM can appear in several ways, for example, undernourished child-overnourished mother or undernourished mother-overnourished child. The World Health Organization (WHO) has defined DBM as the simultaneous occurrence of undernutrition along with overweight and obesity, or diet-related non-communicable diseases in individuals, households and populations, and spanning various life stages.[1] The prevalence of total DBM at household level ranges from 3 to 35% in different countries. Though, undernutrition is still prevalent in children and women in Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMIC), on the other extreme overweight and obesity is also steadily rising. The rise in over-nourishment may be attributed to dual challenge of lack of physical activity and easy access to packaged food as mentioned in available literature.[2]

Similarly, India also has experienced a decline in undernutrition across all ages including pediatric age group along with a trend of increasing overweight and obesity.[3]

Rapid quantitative as well as qualitative changes in dietary intake is now a characteristic of many developing countries including India.[4] Studies have found that in India, total calorie intake as well as consumption of industrially processed food have increased over the years with increasing urbanization.[5]

In the countries that are experiencing nutrition transition there is usually increase in obesity among adult women as well as persistence of child malnutrition.[67] There are not many studies on DBM among mother child pairs at the household level especially among the urban poor, who are at high risk due to lack of availability and accessibility to healthy food and an environment that is not conducive for physical exercise. This study was conducted to assess the coexistence of undernutrition and overnutrition within households in an urban poor setting. The aim was to estimate the prevalence of malnutrition and DBM among mother–child pairs, and to describe common DBM patterns in an urban resettlement colony of Delhi.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

The study was an observational, cross sectional, descriptive study, conducted in an urban resettlement colony in East Delhi. Children 2–5 years old and their mothers who were residing in the area for more than 6 months and enrolled in the Anganwadi centers were eligible to participate in the study. The sample size was calculated using Epi info 7 software.[8] Considering the prevalence of overweight women as 20.7% as per the NFHS 4 data,[9] and CI of 95% and absolute error of 5%, the sample size calculated was 250 mother-child pairs. Ethical clearance was obtained from Institution’s Ethics Committee. The study area was an urban resettlement colony, consisting of five blocks further subdivided into 21 sub blocks. Each sub block had a population of approximately 1500 to 2000 and consisting of two Anganwadi centers (AWC). Four sub blocks were randomly selected using a random number table. List of children of 2–5 years of age and their mothers, enrolled in registers of AWCs was obtained in the selected sub blocks. All the listed children of 2–5 years of age group and their mothers were contacted at home. Mothers were interviewed and anthropometric measurements of mothers and children were conducted after written informed consent.

Data was collected using a pre-structured, pre-tested and interviewer administered questionnaire from December 2018 to April 2020. Back translation of the questionnaire was done. Anthropometric measurements including weight and height of the mother-child pairs were performed using standard methods recommended by WHO.[10] Criteria used in the study for classifying the nutritional status of the children were according to WHO child growth standards.[11]

Children whose Weight for age (W/A), Height for age (H/A) and Weight for height (W/H) z scores were between −2SD to +2SD, were classified as normal. Children whose z scores were less than -2SD were classified as undernourished. Children whose W/H z-scores were > +2SD and > +3SD of the median were defined as overweight and obese, respectively.

Maternal nutritional status was assessed by calculating the body mass index (BMI), defined as weight in kg divided by the square of the height in meters (kg/m2). Range and interpretation of BMI (kg/m2) used in mothers are as follows: <18.5 (underweight/thinness), 18.5–24.9 (normal), 25–29.9 (overweight), and >30(obese).

Total DBM at household level was defined as households in which the mother is overweight or obese, and the child is underweight, stunted or wasted.

The data was analyzed using SPSS 20.0 (Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 20.0). WHO Anthro Plus-software 2009[12] was used for calculating W/A, H/A, and W/H z-scores in children. The statistical analyses comprised of calculating means and proportions. The Chi-square test was applied to find out association between prevalence of DBM and selected socio-demographic factors. The level of significance was decided as P < 0.05

RESULTS

A total of 250 mother child pairs were recruited for this study. The majority (76.8%) of the participant mothers belonged to 20–30 years of age group while children aged 24–35 months constituted around half of the child participants (52.0%) followed by 36–47 months (31.6%). The mean age (±SD) of mothers and children were 27.7 ± 4.3 years and 37 ± 10 months, respectively. Around half (52.8%) of recruited children were male while 47.2% were female. Majority (62.8%) of the families belonged to lower socio-economic class as per modified Kuppuswamy scale. Approximately half of participant mothers had intermediate and higher levels of education while 19.6% of mothers were illiterate.

Nutritional status as per W/A z scores of more than half of the children were normal, while 31.2% and 12.0% of children were moderately (<−2SD) and severely (<−3SD) underweight, respectively. Half of the children were of normal nutritional status as per H/A z scores while 30% and 16.0% of children were moderately (<−2SD) and severely (<−3SD) stunted, respectively. According to W/H z scores, 80% of the children were normal (−2SDto+2SD) while 13.6% and 6.0% of children were moderately (<−2SD) and severely (<−3SD) wasted, respectively. None of the child participants were found to be overweight or obese. Around one fourth (24%) of the mother participants were overweight, 4% were obese and 18.4% were thin.

DISCUSSION

The prevalence of stunting, wasting and underweight in children in the present study were 46%, 20% and 43% respectively, higher compared to National Family health Survey 5 (NFHS)-5 2019–2021 data for stunting (35.5%), wasting (19%), and underweight (32.8%).[13] Undernutrition in the country remains high, though the prevalence of stunting, wasting and underweight has decreased by a few points from NFHS 4 (2015–2016) to NFHS 5 (2019–2021). The prevalence shows a wide variation within states, being highest in Meghalaya and Bihar and lowest in Puducherry and Sikkim. Childhood malnutrition was higher in the present study compared to that for NCT Delhi, where stunting, wasting and underweight was reported as 30.9%, 11.2 and 21.8%, respectively, as per NFHS 5.[13] The possible explanation for this may be that our study area was an urban resettlement colony, having 63% participants from lower socioeconomic status while the NFHS and Delhi state data was representative of all socioeconomic status. However, it was like that reported in an urban slum in Bangalore.[14]

The prevalence of thin, normal, overweight and obese mothers was 18.4%, 53.6%, 24%, and 4% comparable to NFHS-5 data that is 18.7%, 57.3%, 24% (overweight or obese) while the state of Delhi figures was lower for thin at 10.0%, 48.6% for normal, and much higher for overweight or obesity at 41.3%.[13] This may be due to the study area being constituted by population belonging to the lower socio economic status as compared to the average Delhi population.

No overweight or obese child was observed in the present study while the prevalence of overweight/obese children was 2.1% and 3.4% as per the NFHS-4 and NFHS-5, respectively.[913] This finding is important as it iterates that undernutrition remains the major problem among the under five children in the area.

In the present study, the total prevalence of households having DBM was 15.2% consistent with reported prevalence of total DBM at household level ranging from 3% to 35% in different countries.[2] The double burden in some of the published literature was defined as stunted child and overweight mother or underweight child and overweight mother, while having a child with any form of undernutrition and overweight mother was considered as DBM by us.[151617] Dwivedi et al. conducted a study by including data from 3 waves of India’s National Family Health Survey (NFHS 2006, 2016, and 2021) and defined DBM in the form of an overweight parent and an undernourished weight or stunted child. The prevalence of parent–child DBM as reported by them increased from 15% in 2006 to 26% in 2021.[18]

The prevalence of UC-OM pairs, SC-OM pairs, and WC-OM pairs were 9.6%, 12.8% and 4.8%, respectively. According to NFHS-5, the percentage of children who were underweight, stunted, and wasted while the mothers were overweight was 21%, 27% and 21% respectively, that is higher than the present study.[13] This difference in prevalence of overweight mothers may be due to the mothers belonging to lower socio-economic status and being younger than the NFHS 5 sample.

In a study done by Dang and Meenakshi,[15] the prevalence of UC-OM pairs in urban areas of India was reported as 8% comparable to the present study that is, 9.6%. Study was conducted by Shinsugietal[16] in Sri Lanka reported the prevalence of UC-OM pairs as 5.3%, while it was 16% in Gaza strip Palestine in a study by Kishawhi et al.[17] The phenomenon of DBM as underweight-child and overweight mother has been observed in other LMIC.[2] In a study done by Jayalaxmi et al.[19] in 2019 in Kerala, India has reported the prevalence of (SC-OM) pairs as 10.7% which is comparable to finding of our study for the same pairs as 12.8%. Oddo et al.[20] in 2012 reported the prevalence for the same in rural Indonesia and Bangladesh as 11% (comparable) and 4% (lower), respectively. Lower prevalence of SC-OM pairs was reported in a study done by Anik et al.[21] in 2019 as 4.1%, 1.5%, 3.9%, and 5.5% in Bangladesh, Nepal, Pakistan, and Myanmar, respectively.

Similar to a study done in rural Indonesia we found that higher maternal education was protective against DBM but the opposite was seen in Bangladesh and a study was conducted by Dang and Meenakshi[15] in India.[1520] Association between education and obesity is complex and varies within the country too.[22] Mothers involved in outdoor activity for more than 30 minutes per day were more likely to be in households having UC-OM pairs as compared to household with NC-NM pairs. These findings may be limited by the cross-sectional design of the study and thus necessitating a prospective study to investigate the causal factors of DBM.

Though we did not find any association between socio-economic status and DBM in contrast to other studies[2223] the finding of the present study is significant as it was done in an urban resettlement colony and around two-third of the study population belonged to lower socio-economic status.

The study highlights the important issue of intra-household DBM, emphasizing that both the quality and adequacy of available food are major concerns. Persistent food insecurity forces families to prioritize quantity over quality; leading to undernutrition in children and overnutrition in adults who consume more affordable, calorie-dense, and nutrient-poor foods. Rapid urbanization also has led to lifestyle changes, including reduced physical activity and increased consumption of processed foods, contributing to the rise of overnutrition.

Existing nutrition policies may not adequately address the dual challenges of undernutrition and overnutrition, especially in urban slums and rural areas. Therefore, it is crucial to understand the causal pathways leading to DBM within households. It has been suggested that all forms of malnutrition share certain common drivers namely nutrition in early life, dietary diversity, food environment, and socio-economic factors that need to be addressed.[2425] This understanding will help in devising behavior change communication strategies that focus on improving child feeding practices and promoting healthy lifestyle choices for adults.

One limitation of this study is that no clinical diagnosis was included. Malnutrition was defined as based on anthropometric parameters only, body composition, micronutrient deficiencies or signs of malnutrition were not included. Also, the sample size, was calculated primarily to estimate prevalence, and thus limiting the validity of subgroup analyses and comparisons. Therefore, findings related to factors associated with the DBM need be interpreted with caution. The extended data collection period of more than a year due to logistical and operational constraints may have introduced temporal and seasonal variations in nutritional status, which should be considered while interpreting the study findings.

The study’s findings reveal the presence of DBM in mother-child pairs within an urban resettlement colony. The study findings show that there is co-existence of overnutrition and undernutrition in the same household, with the child being under weight, stunted or wasted while the mother is obese This underscores the urgent need to review and reform the country’s nutrition policy with a comprehensive approach that addresses both undernutrition and overnutrition.[26] Further research is required to identify the causes and associated factors for the occurrence of DBM. Community engagement is required to plan and develop strategies to tackle DBM in poor neighborhoods of urban areas, with a focus on improving child undernutrition and preventing maternal overweight through integrated nutrition and lifestyle programs at the household level. Health education strategies should particularly prioritize illiterate mothers and those engaged in outdoor work, as they were significantly associated with higher risk of undernourished child–overweight mother pairs.
