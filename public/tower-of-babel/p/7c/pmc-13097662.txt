Effect of probiotic supplement on improvement of depressive symptoms in patients with substance-induced depressive disorder: a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial
Authors: Seyed Hamdollah Mosavat, Ali Sahraian, Mehdi Kalani, Foroogh Namjoyan, Younes Ghasemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubstance-Induced Depressive Disorder (SIDD) accounts for a high health burden and requires new therapies. Bidirectional communications between the gut and the brain mediated by the microbiota point to a possible influence of probiotics as a tool for preserving mental health.Materials and methodsDouble-blind, placebo-controlled, randomized clinical trial on a sample of 40 patients with SIDD. Patients were then randomly assigned into probiotic and placebo arms and underwent interventions for four continuous weeks. The clinical assessment was done using BDI and HAM-A. Immunologic outcomes were measured through analysis of inflammatory markers, cytokines, and other immunological parameters. The trial was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, under the identifier NCT06790823 (18/1/2025).ResultsThe change in the intensity of depression and anxiety in both probiotic and placebo groups was statistically significant after intervention. No differences were also noted between the two groups. Immunologic results showed a suggestive changes in IL6 and INFG levels in the probiotic group as compared with placebo, which may indicate possible immune-modulating effects.ConclusionIn this randomized, double‑blind, placebo‑controlled pilot trial, both probiotic and placebo groups showed significant improvements in depressive and anxiety symptoms over time, with no significant between‑group differences. Probiotic supplementation was associated with non‑significant trends in selected inflammatory markers, suggesting possible immunological effects that warrant further investigation. Larger, multicenter trials with comprehensive microbiome and immunologic assessments are needed to clarify the potential role of probiotics as an adjunctive strategy in substance‑induced depressive disorder.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-07903-7.Keywords: Substance, Opium, Probiotic, Anxiety, Depression, Clinical trial

Introduction

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is one of the most frequent and disabling psychiatric conditions worldwide, and recent estimates suggest that there are 300 million1 cases [1]. It causes immense personal distress for the individuals concerned but it is also an extremely heavy burden at social levels [2]. MDD has been established as the world’s leading cause of disability, accounting for approximately 800,000 suicide deaths annually [3]. Despite the remarkable progress in uncovering the etiological underpinnings of MDD, our current knowledge remains incomplete, and available treatments often are insufficient; thus, new insights into the pathophysiology of MDD are needed. In this context, the gut microbiome, a vast community of microorganisms populating the gastro-intestinal tract, is a recent focus of interest in this field of studies [4, 5].

The gut microbiota encompasses trillions of microorganisms, mainly bacteria, which colonize the human GI tract. These microorganisms are of utmost importance regarding host health and disease conditions, with effects ranging from beneficial to pathogenic [6]. The complex interaction that exists between the gut microbiota and the physiological processes of a host has led to conceptualizing the gut-brain axis-a bidirectional communication linking the gut and the CNS [7]. The gut microbiota helps in vital functions like digesting indigested food substance, synthesizing micronutrient production, modulating the HPA axis, and producing neurologically active compounds such as gamma-aminobutyric acid and SCFAs [8–10]. Besides, it has a deep influence on the immune system and intestinal barrier integrity, which again implicates its role in a wide range of physiological and pathological processes [11–14].

The emerging evidence supports that there is a bidirectional relationship between gut microbiota and depression, representing a paradigm shift in psychiatric research [15, 16]. Moreover, studies have pointed out that certain microbial dysbiosis in the gut is related to depression, especially on the inflammatory state and gut barrier integrity [17–19]. Animal studies have presented some compelling evidence to suggest a causal role of dysbiosis in depression-like behaviours [20]. For example, dysbiosis induced by broad spectrum antibiotic treatment in mice—leading to altered behavior analogous to that of depression—was reversed by probiotic treatment with Lactobacillus casei DG [21]. Human-to-rodent studies have similarly highlighted the impact of transfer of gut microbiota in behavior modulation [22]. Transplantation of gut microbiota from patients with MDD into germ-free mice or mice depleted of microbiota induced depression-like phenotypes, further implicating gut dysregulation in depressive disorders [23, 24].

Furthermore, the relationship between MDD and Substance Use Disorder (SUD) adds complexity to understanding the mechanisms underlying both conditions [25]. MDD and SUD often co-occur, with each disorder increasing the risk of developing the other [26]. The comorbidity between these disorders has been extensively studied, revealing intricate interconnections that involve various pathways [27]. For example, MDD may stimulate self-medication with drugs, leading to changes in the brain that promote sensitivity to the reinforcing properties of drugs of abuse [28]. Alternatively, SUD may antecede MDD, alter reward pathways, and enhance vulnerability to stressful events and depression [25]. Besides, the shared genetic and environmental factors may affect reward and motivational circuitry, thereby contributing to the vulnerability for the development of both SUD and MDD [29–32].

Given the growing body of evidence linking gut microbiota dysregulation to both MDD and SUD, there is a compelling rationale to investigate the therapeutic potential of probiotic interventions in individuals suffering from Substance Induced Depressive Disorder [33]. This study aims to explore the effect of a specific probiotic supplement on the gut microbiota composition and symptom severity in patients diagnosed with Substance Induced Depressive Disorder. The aim of this study is to provide valuable insights into novel therapeutic avenues by explaining how probiotics affect the gut-brain axis in individuals suffering from the debilitating consequences of comorbid depressive and substance use disorders.

Methods

Study design

This study is a double-arm parallel design, placebo-controlled, double-blind Randomized Controlled Trial to study the efficacy of a specific probiotic supplement in patients diagnosed with Substance Induced Depressive Disorder. This design allows for tight control over variables and enhances the reliability and validity of the results. This study was conducted in accordance with the CONSORT guidelines.

Participants

The study will include 50 outpatients referred to the psychiatric clinic of Ebne Sina Hospital in Shiraz, Iran, who meet the criteria for Substance Induced Depressive Disorder according to the DSM-5. Participants were between 18 and 70 years old and should not have had significant internal diseases such as heart and lung failure, cancer, vascular disease, rheumatic disease, history of seizures, and hypothyroidism.

Inclusion criteria

Males and females, aged between 18 and 70 years.

Diagnosis of Substance Induced Depressive Disorder as per DSM-5 criteria.

Absence of significant internal diseases as per exclusion criteria.

Exclusion criteria

Pregnant or lactating. Patients who for any reason cannot comply with adequate follow-up.

Randomization

Convenience sampling performed on eligible patients attending the psychiatry clinic of Ebne Sina Hospital. Random list generated in statistical software using a simple block randomization method. Participants were randomly allocated to either the probiotic or placebo group. Patients’ allocation was blind for both participants and researchers.

Intervention

The probiotic supplement (Moodamin capsuls) provided from Amin pharmacytical company, Iran, was freeze-dried containing Lactobacillus helveticus and Bifidobacterium Longum bacteria at a dose of 2.682 × CFU and 0.318 × CFU each capsule, respectively. Excipients include xylitol, maltodextrin, plum flavor, and malic acid. A placebo product used for the control group was made up of excipients only. Participants were asked to take two capsules per day for four weeks, preferably before a meal. Compliance was checked regularly by telephone and by asking participants to return medication containers.

Standard treatment

All participants in both the probiotic and placebo groups received standard treatment for Substance Induced Depressive Disorder, which may include:

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) and/or Interpersonal Therapy (IPT) sessions tailored to address depressive symptoms and substance-related issues. The specific type and duration of therapy was determined by the treating psychiatrist based on individual patient needs.

Pharmacological Management: If deemed necessary by the treating psychiatrist, patients may be prescribed antidepressant medications according to standard clinical guidelines. The choice of medication and dosage was individualized and closely monitored.

Outcome measures

The Beck Depression Severity Questionnaire used to assess the severity of depressive symptoms in participants.

The Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A) used to assess the severity of anxiety severity in participants.

Sample size estimation

Due to the exploratory nature of this study and the limited availability of prior data on probiotic interventions in patients with Substance-Induced Depressive Disorder, a formal a priori power calculation was not feasible. Therefore, the sample size was determined based on feasibility considerations and effect sizes reported in previous pilot randomized controlled trials evaluating probiotics in depressive disorders [34].

A total of 50 participants were initially enrolled, which is consistent with sample sizes commonly used in pilot probiotic trials assessing psychological outcomes. After accounting for loss to follow-up, 40 participants were included in the final analysis. The study was designed as a pilot randomized controlled trial to generate preliminary efficacy data and to inform sample size calculations for future adequately powered studies.

Ethical issues

Providing standard treatment to both groups ensures that all participants received appropriate care for their condition. This approach aligns with ethical principles of beneficence and non-maleficence, minimizing potential harm and maximizing potential benefits for all participants. The probiotic supplement was investigated as an adjunct to standard treatment, not as a replacement. This trial adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1989 revision). The study protocol was reviewed, approved, and monitored by the ethics committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (IR.SUMS.REC.1400.442). All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. The trial was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, under the identifier NCT06790823 (18/1/2025).

Statistical analysis

Analyzes were done by SPSS and GraphPad prism software. Descriptive variables were presented as mean and standard deviation. In univariate analysis, ANOVA and Chi-square test were used in quantitative and qualitative variables, respectively, and in multivariate analysis, linear and logistic regression models were used in quantitative and qualitative dependent variables.

Immunologic outcome

The immunologic outcomes were assessed through the analysis of cytokine levels before and after the intervention. Paired-sample t-tests were conducted for each cytokine to evaluate the significance of changes. The cytokines included in the analysis were IL6, INFG, IL17F, and IL22.

The mean level of IL6 before the intervention was 28.11 (SD = 17.78), and after the intervention, it decreased to 23.30 (SD = 15.27). The paired-sample t-test revealed a non-significant change (t (25) = 1.141, p = 0.265).

For INFG, the mean level increased from 43.92 (SD = 43.26) before the intervention to 54.83 (SD = 85.83) after the intervention. However, the paired-sample t-test did not show a significant difference (t (25) = -0.550, p = 0.587).

The analysis of IL17F levels demonstrated a decrease from 26.60 (SD = 19.14) before the intervention to 40.05 (SD = 97.36) after the intervention. The change was not statistically significant (t (25) = -0.689, p = 0.497).

The mean level of IL22 before the intervention was 15.42 (SD = 28.72), and after the intervention, it slightly increased to 12.01 (SD = 24.65). The paired-sample t-test did not reveal a significant change (t (25) = 0.458, p = 0.651).

Overall, the analysis of immunologic outcomes did not show significant changes in the levels of IL6, INFG, IL17F, and IL22 after the intervention.

Discussion

The present randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot study investigated the effects of probiotic supplementation on depressive and anxiety symptoms and selected inflammatory markers in patients with Substance-Induced Depressive Disorder (SIDD).In recent years, there has been a noticeable rise in interest in complementary and alternative medicine among various patient populations. This trend is particularly evident in the use of medicinal herbs, which have gained popularity, especially in Eastern countries. Patients are increasingly seeking out alternative treatment options, including probiotics, to address a range of health issues, including mental health conditions like depression. This shift towards embracing complementary and alternative therapies underscores the importance of exploring novel approaches, such as probiotic supplementation, in the management of mental health disorders [35–39].

To contextualize our findings, we draw comparisons with several relevant studies that explored the impact of probiotics on depressive symptoms and related parameters. Akkasheh et al. in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial demonstrated that probiotic supplementation significantly decreased depression symptoms and improved metabolic status in patients with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) [34]. The study utilized a probiotic capsule with three viable strains, showing positive effects on Beck Depression Inventory scores, insulin levels, insulin resistance, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) concentrations, and glutathione concentrations. Our study aligns with these findings, suggesting a potential broad applicability of probiotics in different depressive disorders.

Chahwan et al. investigated the impact of probiotic supplements on depressive symptoms and cognitive reactivity. Although gut microbiota composition remained largely unchanged, participants in the probiotic group exhibited a significant reduction in cognitive reactivity. In our study, while we focused on SIDD, the cognitive and mood-related improvements reported by Chahwan et al. resonate with our observations, emphasizing the potential of probiotics in modulating psychological aspects [40].

Abildgaard et al. in an animal study demonstrated that probiotic treatment reduced depressive-like behavior in rats, irrespective of diet [41]. While our study is clinical in nature, the consistency of results across preclinical and clinical studies underscores the robustness of the relationship between probiotics and depressive symptoms.

Lange et al. provided a comprehensive overview of the bidirectional communication between gut microbiota and the brain [42].While acknowledging the potential of probiotics in reducing depressive symptoms, they highlighted the need for large, randomized trials. Our study contributes to this need by providing specific insights into SIDD, further expanding the evidence base.

Nikolova et al. in their meta-analysis highlighted the efficacy of probiotics as adjunctive therapy to antidepressants [43]. Like our study involve concurrent antidepressant treatment, the findings from Nikolova et al. reinforce the potential benefits of combining probiotics with traditional treatments.

Goh et al.‘s meta-analysis reinforced the significant improvement in depressive symptoms with probiotics, particularly in patients with Major Depressive Disorder [44].Our study extends this understanding to the context of SIDD, suggesting a broader application of probiotics in different depressive subtypes.

Musazadeh et al.‘s umbrella meta-analysis supports the notion that probiotics significantly reduce depression symptoms [45]. The subgroup analysis indicating a more robust effect with longer durations and higher dosages aligns with our study duration and dosage, further emphasizing the potential therapeutic benefits.

Huang et al.‘s meta-analysis supports the significant reduction in depression scores with probiotics. Our study contributes to this evidence by focusing on SIDD, providing additional insights into the potential benefits of probiotics in specific depressive subgroups [46].

Previous randomized trials and meta-analyses have reported modest benefits of probiotics on depressive symptoms, particularly as adjuncts to antidepressant therapy. However, heterogeneity in study populations, probiotic formulations, and outcome measures limits direct comparison. In contrast to some prior studies, our findings did not demonstrate a significant advantage of probiotics over placebo in SIDD, underscoring the importance of disorder-specific and adequately powered investigations.

Contrary to our initial hypotheses, there were no significant differences in anxiety and depression levels between the case and placebo groups post-intervention. Both groups exhibited a substantial reduction in anxiety and depression scores, suggesting that factors beyond the intervention, such as the passage of time or a placebo effect, may have contributed to these improvements. The absence of a significant between-group difference raises questions about the specificity of the intervention in addressing psychological well-being.

The observed reductions in anxiety and depression within both groups align with previous research highlighting the potential for spontaneous improvement or non-specific therapeutic effects in mental health interventions. It is crucial to acknowledge the multifaceted nature of psychological distress and the need for comprehensive assessments to capture the complexity of these conditions.

The findings of this study contribute to the growing body of literature exploring the interconnectedness of mental health and immune function. Although non-significant trends were observed in certain inflammatory markers, including IL-6 and IFN-γ, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The lack of statistically significant changes suggests that probiotic supplementation did not exert a robust or consistent immunomodulatory effect within the duration and sample size of this study.

The lack of significant differences in certain immunological markers, such as TNFa.after and IL17F.after, suggests that the intervention may not have a uniform impact on all components of the immune system. Exploring individual differences in treatment response, including genetic and environmental factors, may provide valuable insights into the heterogeneity of immunological responses.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This study was designed as a pilot trial with a relatively small sample size and short intervention duration, which may have limited the ability to detect subtle between-group differences. Potential confounding factors, including concomitant antidepressant use and substance-related variables, may also have influenced outcomes despite comparable baseline characteristics. Additionally, the absence of direct gut microbiota analysis restricts mechanistic interpretation. The absence of a significant between-group difference highlights the well-recognized impact of placebo effects and natural symptom fluctuation in psychiatric clinical trials. Such effects are particularly relevant in depressive disorders, where expectancy and therapeutic engagement can substantially influence outcomes.

Conclusion

In this randomized, double‑blind, placebo‑controlled pilot trial, both probiotic and placebo groups showed significant improvements in depressive and anxiety symptoms over time, with no significant between‑group differences. Probiotic supplementation was associated with non‑significant trends in selected inflammatory markers, suggesting possible immunological effects that warrant further investigation. Due to the small sample size, short intervention period, and absence of direct gut microbiota analysis, definitive conclusions regarding clinical efficacy cannot be drawn. Larger, multicenter trials with comprehensive microbiome and immunologic assessments are needed to clarify the potential role of probiotics as an adjunctive strategy in substance‑induced depressive disorder.
