Microvilli’s grip on T cell development
Authors: Allison T. Ryan, Minsoo Kim
Published: 2025

In PNAS, Soh et al. (1) report a critical role of Cdc42 in the regulation of T cell microvilli. Microvilli are actin-rich membrane protrusions with important functions that vary based on cell type. Epithelial cells in the intestine or kidney, for example, have relatively stable membrane protrusions that function primarily to increase cell surface area for nutrient absorption and secretion, with additional mechanosensing capabilities. In immune cells, microvilli are dynamic and flexible in shape, size, and composition, with specialized functions to support cell adhesion and signal transduction (2). Their rapid assembly and disassembly are required for the dynamic needs of immune sensing. In T cells, changes to microvilli length, density, and organization have proven critical for antigen sampling and cell–cell interactions, including immune synapse (IS) formation with antigen-presenting cells (APCs) (3–5).

In general, microvilli enable cells to sense and interact with their environments. Importantly, these structures work in concert with the actin cytoskeleton and associated proteins, GTPases, and receptors to translate information to the rest of the cell (6). In immune cells, especially T cells, this can result in consequential signal transduction and migration events. It is worth noting that microvilli are functionally distinct from filopodia, which are typically longer and even more dynamic in comparison, with differences in how they are anchored to the actin cytoskeleton (7). Even so, these two biological structures utilize many shared factors, including those that promote actin rearrangements, and Rac/Rho family members and Cdc42, which support membrane organization, remodeling, and signal transduction (7, 8).

Several research groups have described the role of microvilli in the context of antigen sensing and presentation, adhesion and migration, and, in some respects, IS formation; these revolutionary studies reveal that T cell microvilli are antigen sensors and major signaling hubs that are enriched with TCR microclusters critical for the adaptive immune response (4, 9, 10). Additionally, the Alon group showed that T cell microvilli are a key mediator of transendothelial migration through the high endothelial venules (HEV). As chemokine binds CCR7, a small number of LFA-1 molecules within proximity of CCR7 undergo rapid inside-out signaling to enable T cell capture and arrest. Together, these events reveal that signaling and adhesion molecules concentrated at the tips of microvilli structures are essential for T cell arrest and migration (10).

The immunological synapse is centered around TCR bound to peptide MHC complexes presented by an adjacent APC. The Dustin lab showed that these TCR clusters immobilize at the IS and bud from microvilli into microvesicles that can promote the humoral immunity via transcellular signaling (11). Later, Kim et al. coined the term “Immunological Synaptosomes” to describe the T cell microvilli particle APC signaling phenomenon (5). Using superresolution microscopy, Gilad Haran’s group built upon this knowledge to map protein composition within the T cell membrane. These findings decisively revealed for the first time that T cells universally concentrate their TCR and other important T cell signaling components to the tips of microvilli to enable robust antigen sampling across APCs and subsequent signal transduction within T cells (12). These works highlight the relationship between microvilli and focal concentration of important T cell signaling and adhesion factors within microvilli during T cell development and in the periphery. From Matthew Krummel’s group, Cai et al. showed that TCR microclusters at the tips of actively probing microvilli structures contribute to microvilli stabilization, ultimately promoting the early IS formation (4). This work continues to elucidate underlying mechanisms of ligand detection by TCR, with new evidence to link T cell activation and signaling with microvilli function via the specialized T cell synapse. In summary, there is overwhelming evidence to suggest T cell microvilli contribute to the organization of cytoskeletal components, facilitate signal transduction, and membrane and cell surface organization, including TCR microcluster formation, while directly impacting cell-to-cell interactions within the tissue microenvironments.

Despite this knowledge, our understanding of microvilli in the context of T cell development and activation is poorly understood. This highlights important biological phenomena that would offer a finer understanding of T cell sensitivity and activation thresholds, mechanosensing, and force transduction. Translationally, insight into T cell microvilli dynamics can offer avenues to tune T cell responses in autoimmune disease and immunotherapy, for a more controlled and balanced T cell response. Here, Soh et al. (1) deliver clear messages regarding T cell microvilli organization and function, including a previously unknown functional role for Cdc42 in the regulation of T cell development, maturation, and activation. The key questions they addressed here are how are microvilli and TCR microclusters functionally related with respect to antigen recognition and IS formation? Further, do microvilli actively participate in the spatial organization of TCR microclusters, or do they merely serve as a platform for antigen sensing that is later overcome by TCR microcluster-driven signaling? To answer these questions, it is essential to understand microvilli formation and structural remodeling at each stage during T cell development. The authors investigated microvilli formation during T cell development, revealing distinct structural patterns in length and density. In wild-type mice, microvilli in thymocytes were abundant at the DN stage, reduced at the DP stage, and restored at the SP stage (Fig. 1). Cdc42 KO resulted in both microvilli loss and reduced TCR expression (dynamic microvilli movements help guide TCR microclusters). This reduction in microvilli correlates with a decrease in antigen recognition, leading to diminished T cell activation, while intrinsic migratory properties remain unaffected. The reduced number and length of actin-supported microvilli impaired antigen recognition by CD4+ SP thymocytes, weakening their interaction with APCs.

Cdc42 controls microvilli formation during the transition from the double-positive (DP) to the single-positive (SP) stage of T cell development, thereby enhancing antigen recognition and facilitating APC binding during T cell activation.

Cdc42 is involved in controlling microvilli formation during the transition from the DP to the SP stage, thereby enhancing antigen recognition, promoting TCR-mediated activation, and facilitating APC binding (Fig. 1). As recent thymic emigrants, CD4+ thymocytes migrate through lymph nodes and transiently express adhesion molecules such as LFA-1 and ICAM-1, allowing them to physiologically engage with APCs in these tissues. Interestingly, T cells lacking Cdc42 exhibit a severe defect in adhesion to HEV and APCs in vivo, without affecting their migratory properties. Finally, the authors note that in the absence of Cdc42, there is a loss of a functional IS. In the thymus, this would likely lead to autoimmunity, and in the periphery, this would have large biological consequences, namely, a lack of effector activation and expansion. The mechanism governing this disrupted IS formation needs further characterization, with strong consideration of the delicate intracellular interactions required for actin-cytoskeletal rearrangements, especially considering the overlap in factors required for smaller-scale microvilli organization, and larger-scale filopodia and cytoskeleton assembly and structure.

These findings expand our understanding of the functional relationship between microvilli and T cell dynamics by revealing that microvilli are not simply antigen sensors, but functional components that support and contribute to TCR microcluster formation and functional cSMAC assembly (1). In this regard, Cdc42 plays an essential role in the organization of microvilli during T cell development, while disruption of Cdc42 results in impaired IS formation without any major effects on T cell motility.

Because cytoskeletal rearrangement would directly impact IS formation, how to functionally decouple Cdc42 defects from actin cytoskeletal rearrangement in the context of immune activation and modulation would be an important next step to advance the field. In addition, it would be interesting to assess these questions from the perspective of immune (activation or suppressive) diseases as well as in current immunotherapies like checkpoint inhibition and CAR T cells. In the case of CAR T cells, the IS is comparatively more punctate than the traditional IS. This unique composition and organization of molecules may help decouple the impact of Cdc42 on viable IS formation and major actin-cytoskeletal rearrangements. This context may illuminate the more elaborate relationship during complex IS formation (13). Finally, additional work to rule out any Cdc42-mediated differences in TCR recycling is required to completely understand potential Cdc42 regulation of TCR. As the authors highlight, a better understanding of the relationship between Cdc42 and Rac1 with TCR microcluster and microvilli assembly needs to be addressed, considering membrane-cytoskeletal dynamics and biochemical interactions at the cSMAC. Nonetheless, this study presents compelling evidence that microvilli are required for developing thymocytes, while addressing the outstanding question of T cell microvilli function with a newly proposed role of Cdc42 in microvilli formation that impacts TCR microcluster assembly and subsequent T cell activation.
