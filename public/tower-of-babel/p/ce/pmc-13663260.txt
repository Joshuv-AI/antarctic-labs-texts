SPP1-positive macrophages drive trastuzumab resistance in HER2-positive breast cancer
Authors: Donghui Wang, Guodong Li, Yajie Lu, Ruoxin Du, Xiangmei He, Jiahui Sun, Danxi Li, Siyuan Wang, Yimin Liu, Shuning Wang, Lan Hou, Juliang Zhang, Yuan Gao, Cun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTrastuzumab-based HER2-targeted therapy remains the cornerstone treatment for HER2-positive breast cancer. However, its clinical efficacy is significantly modulated by the tumor microenvironment (TME). Our single-cell sequencing analysis of clinical samples revealed that patients with poor radiologic response after trastuzumab-based neoadjuvant therapy presented significant enrichment of TIGIT+ NK cells with high immune checkpoint expression, exhausted CD8+ T cells, and immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs). Further analyses leveraging cell-cell communication, spatial transcriptomics, and multiplex immunofluorescence showed that SPP1+ tumor-associated macrophages (SPP1+ TAMs) enrichment was associated with dysfunctional NK- and T-cell states in tumors from patients with poor radiologic response. Functional validation studies revealed that SPP1+ TAMs actively induced exhaustion phenotypes in both NK cells and CD8+ T cells, thereby impairing trastuzumab-dependent antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and adaptive immune responses. In vivo experiments using humanized NCG murine models further confirmed the SPP1+ TAMs-mediated suppression of NK cell function. Significantly, HER2-positive breast cancer patients with elevated SPP1⁺ TAMs levels experienced both reduced efficacy of trastuzumab neoadjuvant therapy and diminished long-term survival prospects. In summary, our findings provide the first systematic characterization of TME remodeling following trastuzumab therapy, identifying SPP1+ TAMs as a potential driver of trastuzumab resistance. Introduction

Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), a transmembrane receptor tyrosine kinase, drives aggressive tumor progression in breast cancer. HER2-overexpressing breast cancers exhibit increased proliferative capacity, increased metastatic potential, and resistance to conventional chemotherapy [1]. Trastuzumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody that targets HER2, has revolutionized treatment paradigms through dual mechanisms: direct suppression of HER2-mediated oncogenic signaling and activation of antitumor immunity via antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) mediated by natural killer (NK) cells and adaptive T-cell responses [2]. Nevertheless, more than 50% of patients develop resistance to trastuzumab-based neoadjuvant therapies, highlighting the critical need to understand resistance mechanisms [3].

Previous studies of trastuzumab resistance have largely focused on tumor cell-intrinsic alterations in the HER2 signaling axis. These mechanisms include reduced or spatially heterogeneous HER2/ERBB2 expression, truncated HER2 isoforms such as p95HER2, and reactivation of downstream PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling through PIK3CA mutations or related pathway alterations [4–7]. However, these tumor-intrinsic mechanisms do not fully explain resistance to trastuzumab, because the therapeutic activity of trastuzumab also depends on immune effector mechanisms, particularly NK-cell-mediated ADCC and subsequent adaptive T-cell responses. Host and microenvironmental factors, including stromal-derived immunosuppressive metabolites, impaired NK-cell cytotoxicity, and adaptive immune-inhibitory pathways such as HLA-G/KIR2DL4 or PD-1/PD-L1 signaling, may uncouple HER2 antibody binding from effective immune-mediated tumor clearance [8, 9]. Therefore, resistance likely emerges not only from cancer cell-intrinsic signaling rewiring but also from tumor microenvironment (TME)-mediated immune escape.

Against this background, the immune and stromal components of the TME are particularly relevant to trastuzumab resistance. The TME is a complex ecosystem comprising tumor cells, immune cells, stromal cells, extracellular matrix (ECM), and diverse cytokines. It serves as a critical platform for tumor growth and invasion while also functioning as the site where therapeutic pressure reshapes immune-cell states and intercellular communication. An immunosuppressive TME may drive functional exhaustion of cytotoxic effectors, including NK cells and CD8+ T cells, thereby compromising trastuzumab-mediated ADCC and adaptive antitumor immunity. Among TME components, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are among the most abundant immune infiltrates and are increasingly recognized as master regulators of immunosuppressive landscapes. Their functional plasticity and phenotypic heterogeneity are dynamically modulated by TME-derived signals [10]. Recent advances in single-cell multiomic technologies have enabled the systematic deconvolution of TAMs subpopulations, revealing their functional diversity and providing actionable insights for developing precision immunomodulatory strategies [11, 12].

In this study, we performed comprehensive single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on residual tumor specimens from patients stratified by MRI-based RECIST 1.1 radiologic response after trastuzumab-based neoadjuvant therapy, integrated with bulk transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomics, and functional validation using cellular assays and humanized NCG mouse models to investigate microenvironmental mechanisms of trastuzumab resistance. We identified a distinct SPP1-positive macrophage subset that drives immunosuppression by inducing the functional exhaustion of NK cells. Our research confirmed that SPP1+ TAMs are the key regulators of the immunosuppressive microenvironment in HER2-positive breast cancer. Our conclusions suggest that targeted elimination of SPP1+ TAMs may restore NK- and T-cell immune activity and overcome trastuzumab resistance in HER2-positive breast cancer with an immunosuppressive residual tumor microenvironment.

Results

Single-cell atlas of the HER2-positive breast cancer TME

To systematically investigate the relationship between trastuzumab-based treatment response and residual TME remodeling, we performed scRNA-seq on surgical specimens from eight HER2-positive breast cancer patients who underwent neoadjuvant trastuzumab therapy. All patients exhibited residual disease without a pathological complete response (pCR) post treatment. Treatment response was classified according to the RECIST 1.1 criteria based on tumor volume changes measured by MRI: four patients who achieved a partial response (PR) were defined as radiologic responders, whereas four patients with stable disease (SD) were defined as radiologic nonresponders, also referred to as the poorer radiologic-response group (Fig. 1A).

Following quality control processes, the scRNA-seq data underwent dimensionality reduction and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) visualization, revealing 32 distinct clusters. Using established marker genes, we identified 11 major cell types: epithelial cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), endothelial cells, perivascular-like cells (PVLs), T or NK cells, B cells, plasmablasts, myeloid cells, plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), mast cells, and cycling cells (Fig. 1B-C). Intergroup and interpatient heterogeneity in terms of the cellular composition was observed (Supplementary Figure S1A-B).

Through differential expression analysis and GSEA of epithelial cells from PR and SD, we found that responder-derived epithelial cells were enriched for multiple GO pathways related to treatment-induced tumor cell death and therapeutic response. Fig. 1D shows representative significantly enriched pathways, including regulation of programmed cell death, cellular response to tumor necrosis factor, and extrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway via death domain receptors. Notably, the antitumor efficacy of trastuzumab relies on its ability to activate ADCC in NK cells and subsequently trigger adaptive immune responses. Therefore, we hypothesized that the observed disparity in tumor cell apoptosis between the PR and SD groups may be related to functional differences in NK cells and cytotoxic lymphocytes. To externally assess this hypothesis, we expanded the original bulk transcriptomic validation using multiple public HER2-positive breast cancer cohorts treated with trastuzumab or other anti-HER2-related regimens, including GSE123845 T1, GSE123845 T2, GSE66305, GSE181574, and GSE55348 [13–16]. GSVA analysis showed that patients with pCR or non-recurrence tended to exhibit higher NK cell-related functional scores, including antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, NK cell activation, NK cell chemotaxis, NK cell degranulation, cytolytic granule, and NK cell-mediated immune response to tumor cell pathways (Fig. 1E). Although cohort- and signature-level heterogeneity was observed, these external datasets collectively support the association between stronger NK-cell/cytotoxic immune function and favorable outcomes after anti-HER2 therapy. We further analyzed immunophenoscore-related features in the same cohorts, which suggested reproducible differences in global immune phenotype between treatment-benefit and unfavorable-outcome groups, particularly involving antigen presentation, effector immune-cell activity, suppressive immune components, and immune checkpoint-related features (Fig. 1F).

Differential distribution of NK-cell subsets between radiologic responders and nonresponders

Focusing on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, we reclustered NK- and T-cell subsets into three major immune effector populations using specific markers (NK cells: FCGR3A and NCAM1; CD4+ T cells: CD4 and CD40LG; and CD8+ T cells: CD8A) and cytotoxicity-related genes (GZMA, KLRD1, and NKG7) (Supplementary Figure S1C-D). We subsequently performed further dimensionality reduction and subclustering of these three major cell populations to characterize their intrinsic heterogeneity.

To delineate functional heterogeneity among NK cells, we performed a comparative analysis between PR and SD groups. Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) using NK-cell functional gene sets from MSigDB revealed enhanced activation, differentiation, proliferation, cytotoxicity, and tumor-response capacity in PR-derived NK cells compared with the SD groups, indicative of immunosuppressed NK-cell states in radiologic nonresponders (Fig. 2A).

Subclustering of NK cells revealed five transcriptionally distinct subsets, namely, SELL+ NK, PRF1+ NK, KLF3+ NK, CD96+ NK, and TIGIT+ NK cells, as defined by their marker gene expression (Fig. 2B). Proportional analysis demonstrated significant enrichment of TIGIT+ NK cells, characterized by elevated expression of inhibitory receptors (TIGIT, LGALS3, and KIR2DL1/3/4), in nonresponders. Conversely, responders showed preferential accumulation of PRF1+ NK- and KLF3+ NK-cell subsets, which were characterized by high expression of FCGR3A (CD16) and cytotoxic effector genes (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Figure S1E). Heatmap visualization confirmed distinct functional gene expression profiles across the subsets (Fig. 2D).

Functional scoring via the UCell algorithm further resolved subset-specific functions: PRF1+ NK and KLF3+ NK cells exhibited robust ADCC activity and tumor response potential, whereas TIGIT+ NK and CD96+ NK cells exhibited heightened negative regulatory features of NK-cell functionality, as well as upregulated expression of both HLA-dependent and HLA-independent inhibitory receptors (Fig. 2E). These findings collectively indicate that trastuzumab resistance likely stems from the imbalanced redistribution of NK-cell subsets, characterized by a proportional reduction in ADCC-competent cytotoxic populations and the concurrent expansion of exhausted TIGIT+ NK-cell subsets.

NK cells undergo functional and phenotypic changes due to the influence of the TME. To characterize these dynamic changes, we used the Slingshot algorithm to infer the differentiation trajectory of NK cells (Fig. 2F). Pseudotime trajectory analysis of the NK-cell density distribution revealed two distinct differentiation pathways: in lineage 1, SELL⁺ NK cells progress through PRF1⁺ cytotoxic intermediates to terminally differentiated KLF3⁺ NK subsets; whereas in lineage 2, SELL⁺ NK cells differentiate into CD96⁺ activated NK cells, subsequently developing TIGIT⁺ exhausted phenotypes. Treatment-responsive (Res.) patients presented predominant NK-cell accumulation in lineage 1 during pseudotime progression, whereas treatment-nonresponsive (Non-Res.) patients presented preferential enrichment in lineage 2 (Fig. 2G). Lineage 1 exhibited sequential upregulation of cytotoxic effectors (FCGR3A, KLRF1, and FGFBP2), whereas lineage 2 demonstrated a gradual increase in exhaustion markers (TIGIT and LGALS3). Both lineages exhibited downregulation of naive markers during differentiation (Fig. 2H). These findings collectively suggest that NK cells in the PR and SD groups exhibit distinct differentiation preferences, where divergent developmental trajectories lead to an imbalanced redistribution of subsets, which may be a key contributor to trastuzumab resistance.

Differential distributions of CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell subsets between PR and SD residual tumors

We performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to compare CD4+ T- and CD8+ T-cell subsets between the trastuzumab-responsive and nonresponsive groups. In CD4+ T cells from nonresponders, pathways associated with regulatory T-cell (Treg) differentiation, immunosuppressive cytokine activity (IL-10 and TGFβ), and hypoxia/glycolysis pathways associated with impaired T-cell function were significantly enriched (Fig. 3A). In CD8+ T cells from nonresponders, PD1-related immune checkpoint pathways and hypoxia/glycolysis pathways were significantly enriched (Fig. 3B).

Dimensionality reduction clustering revealed six distinct CD4+ T-cell subsets: CD44+ CD4+ T cells, IL7R+ CD4+ T cells, LEF1+ CD4+ T cells, CCR7+ CD4+ T cells, PDCD1+ CD4+ T cells, and Tregs (Fig. 3C). CD8+ T cells were clustered into the following subsets: CD44+ CD8+ T cells, GZMM+ CD8+ T cells, DUSP2+ CD8+ T cells, IL7R+ CD8+ T cells, ISG15+ CD8+ T cells, and PDCD1+ CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3D). Heatmap visualization confirmed unique marker gene expression patterns across these subsets (Fig. 3E). Proportional analysis revealed that, compared with those in responders, the proportion of Tregs among CD4+ T cells and the proportion of PDCD1+ CD8+ T cells among CD8+ T cells were significantly greater in nonresponders (Fig. 3F-G). Odds ratio (OR) calculations further validated the preferential enrichment of PDCD1+ CD8+ T cells and immunosuppressive Tregs in the SD cohort (Fig. 3H). Functional characterization using exhaustion and cytotoxicity-related gene signatures revealed that PDCD1+ CD8+ T cells presented the highest exhaustion scores and relatively low cytotoxic potential (Fig. 3I-J).

Collectively, these findings implicate the accumulation of immunosuppressive Tregs and functionally impaired PDCD1+ CD8+ T cells in the SD group. This finding aligns with those of our previous analyses of large-scale bulk transcriptomic data, which demonstrated that lower cytolytic activity scores and elevated immune checkpoint signature scores were significantly associated with diminished therapeutic responsiveness to trastuzumab in HER2-positive breast cancer patients.

Differential distribution of myeloid subsets in the TME of PR versus SD residual tumors

Our study revealed a relative enrichment of exhausted NK-cell subsets, CD8+ T-cell subpopulations, and immunosuppressive Tregs in nonresponders. To investigate the mechanisms underlying this broad-spectrum immunosuppression, we performed cell‒cell communication analysis using the CellChat algorithm. Comparative analysis of signaling networks received by T and NK cells between PR and SD groups demonstrated markedly greater myeloid-derived interactions in terms of strength and number in nonresponders (Fig. 4A).

Dimensionality reduction clustering of myeloid cells revealed nine functionally distinct subsets: 5 TAMs subsets (SPP1+ TAMs, MKI67+ TAMs, FOLR2+ TAMs, FABP7+ TAMs, and NEAT1+ TAMs); 1 monocyte subset (FCN1+ MONO); and 3 dendritic-cell subsets (LAMP3+ cDCs, CLEC9A+ cDCs, and CD1C+ cDCs) (Fig. 4B). The heatmap demonstrated distinct expression patterns of classic myeloid markers across these subsets (Fig. 4C). Differential gene expression analysis (FindMarkers algorithm) between PR and SD groups identified SPP1 as the most upregulated gene in the myeloid cells of nonresponders (Fig. 4D). Spatial mapping via UMAP confirmed that SPP1 expression was specifically localized to the SPP1+ TAMs subset (Fig. 4E). Proportional analysis revealed significant enrichment of SPP1+ TAMs in SD groups (Fig. 4F and Supplementary Figure S1F).

Secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1), also known as osteopontin (OPN), is a multifunctional secreted phosphorylated glycoprotein, and SPP1+ TAMs have been implicated in disease progression across multiple malignancies (e.g., nasopharyngeal carcinoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and prostate cancer). Survival analysis of HER2-positive breast cancer patients in the METABRIC cohort revealed significantly worse overall survival in patients with high SPP1 expression (Fig. 4G) or elevated SPP1+ TAMs signature scores (Fig. 4H). We subsequently analyzed bulk RNA-seq data from the GSE76360 dataset to evaluate the association between SPP1+ TAMs and trastuzumab neoadjuvant therapeutic efficacy [17]. In this study, tumor tissues were biopsied for bulk transcriptomic profiling after one cycle of trastuzumab monotherapy, followed by neoadjuvant treatment combining trastuzumab with chemotherapy. Patients were stratified post treatment into pCR and non-pCR groups, with the latter further subclassified as nonresponders (NOR) or objective responders (OBJR) based on imaging assessments. Compared with pCR patients, NOR patients presented significantly elevated SPP1+ TAMs signature scores after a single trastuzumab cycle (Fig. 4I).

Based on the communication analysis of the scRNA-seq results, we found that SPP1+ TAMs were enriched in SD tumors. The bulk transcriptome data further demonstrated that SPP1+ TAMs were associated with adverse long-term prognoses in HER2-positive breast cancer patients and adverse outcomes of early neoadjuvant therapy. These findings suggest that SPP1+ TAMs may play a key role in resistance to trastuzumab in HER2-positive breast cancer.

Multiomic approaches validate the association between SPP1+ TAMs and the immunosuppressive TME

To investigate interactions among SPP1+ TAMs and NK and T cells, we first performed intercellular communication analysis. The exhausted NK cells, CD8+ T cells, and Tregs predominantly received myeloid-derived signals from SPP1+ TAMs (Fig. 5A), suggesting their critical role in shaping the immunosuppressive TME. To validate the association between SPP1+ macrophages and functionally suppressed NK and T cells, we analyzed HER2-positive breast cancer bulk transcriptomic data using the TIMER3.0 platform. Across multiple TIMER3.0-supported immune deconvolution methods, SPP1 expression showed a consistently positive association with macrophage-related infiltration estimates and a generally negative association with CD8+ T-cell infiltration estimates. Notably, NK-cell-related estimates exhibited state-dependent patterns: SPP1 expression was negatively associated with activated NK-cell infiltration, but positively associated with resting NK-cell infiltration, suggesting a potential link between SPP1+ TAM enrichment and impaired NK-cell activation. Treg-related estimates showed method-dependent patterns (Fig. 5B). Together, these results support an association between SPP1+ TAM enrichment and an immunosuppressive immune-infiltration pattern in HER2-positive breast cancer. We further explored spatial relationships using spatial transcriptomic data from HER2-positive breast cancer samples, leveraging a dataset published by Alma Andersson and colleagues [18]. Cell2location-based deconvolution analysis revealed tissue regions with high tumor and immune cell infiltration (Fig. 5C). Spatial proximity analysis revealed regional adjacency between SPP1+ TAMs-enriched areas and regions enriched with TIGIT+ NK cells, PDCD1+ CD8+ T cells, and Tregs (Fig. 5D). Pathway scoring using the MSigDB database revealed enriched cytokine signaling in these boundary regions, which is consistent with the immunomodulatory potential of SPP1+ TAMs identified via our scRNA-seq data analysis (Supplementary Figure S1G).

To further validate the association between SPP1+ macrophage-enriched regions and immunosuppressive lymphocyte populations, we performed multiplex immunofluorescence staining on postoperative tumor samples from HER2-positive breast cancer patients classified as radiologic responders (PR) or radiologic nonresponders (SD) after trastuzumab-based neoadjuvant therapy. Representative images showed that SD tumors exhibited stronger SPP1/CD68 macrophage signals accompanied by increased TIGIT+CD56+ NK cells, PD1+CD8+ T cells, and CD4+FOXP3+ Tregs compared with PR tumors (Fig. 5E). Quantitative analysis confirmed that the numbers of these exhausted or immunosuppressive immune-cell populations were significantly higher in SD tumors than in PR tumors (Supplementary Figure S1H). Moreover, SPP1 fluorescence intensity was positively correlated with the abundance of TIGIT+CD56+ NK cells, PD1+CD8+ T cells, and CD4+FOXP3+ Tregs across patient-level mIF measurements (Supplementary Figure S1I). These findings support a regional association between SPP1-positive macrophage enrichment and an immunosuppressive residual tumor microenvironment in patients with poor radiologic response.

Using cell communication algorithms, we further explored ligand‒receptor interactions between SPP1+ TAMs and immunosuppressive/exhausted lymphocyte subsets. We identified the SPP1‒CD44 axis as the core mechanism of their communication (Fig. 5F). Through global cell UMAP projections, we analyzed the relative expression levels of SPP1 and CD44 across all cells. SPP1 was expressed in myeloid cells (Fig. 5G), especially in SPP1+ TAMs (Fig. 4E), whereas CD44 was predominantly expressed in T- or NK-cell subsets (Fig. 5H). Notably, SPP1 and CD44 expression levels were significantly elevated in the SD group, suggesting that the SPP1‒CD44 axis plays a key role in SPP1+ TAMs-mediated immune suppression.

The metabolic reprogramming of macrophages is a critical mechanism underlying their role in shaping the immunosuppressive microenvironment. Using the scFEA algorithm, we further characterized the metabolic features of myeloid cells. We observed significant upregulation of key glycolysis pathways, including the conversion of glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P) and the conversion of pyruvate to lactate, in SPP1+ TAMs. In addition to enhanced glycolysis in SPP1+ TAMs, we noted significantly elevated synthesis of adenine nucleotides, which serve as key precursors for the production of adenosine, a classic immunosuppressive metabolite that can inhibit lymphocyte immune function (Fig. 5I).

SPP1+ TAMs impair NK-Cell ADCC and CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity

Previous studies have linked SPP1+ TAMs to Treg-rich immunosuppressive microenvironments and resistance to neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy [12]. However, in HER2-positive breast cancer treated with trastuzumab, the functional effects of SPP1+ TAMs on the directly relevant cytotoxic effector populations, including NK cells and CD8+ T cells, remain unclear. To systematically investigate the immunosuppressive role of SPP1⁺ TAMs in this context, we established SPP1-overexpressing THP-1 (SPP1OE-THP-1) and empty vector control (SPP1EV-THP-1) cell lines and confirmed the phenotypes at the transcriptional and protein levels (Fig. 6A-B). We isolated NK cells (CD3- and CD56+) from PBMC and detected the expression of CD3 and CD56 by flow cytometry (Figure S2A). Given that SPP1 is a secreted protein, we used a Transwell assay, with SPP1OE/EV-THP-1 cells cultured in the upper chamber and NK cells or activated CD8+ T cells cultured in the lower chamber, to evaluate the phenotype and functional alterations of NK cells and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6C). CD56 and TIGIT antibodies were used to evaluate the expression level of TIGIT in NK cells subjected to different treatments. Flow cytometry results revealed that the expression level of the immune checkpoint receptor TIGIT in NK cells was significantly increased in the SPP1OE-THP-1 group under the Transwell co-culture conditions (Fig. 6D). NK cells subjected to different treatments were subsequently collected and co-cultured with HER2-positive SKBR3-luc breast cancer cells at a gradient effector-to-target ratio (E: T). Trastuzumab was added, and the ADCC effect mediated by NK cells was evaluated by detecting the luciferase value (Fig. 6E). The results revealed that with increasing E: T ratio, the fluorescence value in the SPP1OE-THP-1 group decreased with respect to that in the control group, showing especially significant differences at E: T ratios of 2:1, 3:1, 4:1 and 5:1 (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, the killing effect of NK cells on SKBR3 cells under different treatments was visualized by real-time live-cell imaging technology, and the specific nucleic acid dye YOYO-1 was used to label dead tumor cells. Consistent with the results of the luciferase-based cell killing assay, the green fluorescence was lower in the SPP1OE-THP-1 cell coculture group (Fig. 6F, G). A video of NK cells killing tumor cells can be found in Supplementary material Video 1. These results suggest that SPP1+ macrophages can secrete SPP1 to induce the upregulation of TIGIT in NK cells, which weakens the ADCC effect of NK cells and promotes the immune escape of tumor cells.

In addition, we isolated primary CD8+ naive T cells (Fig. 6H) and verified their naive phenotype by using CCR7 and CD45RA cell markers (Supplementary Figure S2B). Plate wells were coated with CD3/28 antibodies to activate CD8+ T cells to construct an in vitro effector CD8+ T-cell model from day 0 to day 3 (Fig. 6H). The expression of PD-1, LAG-3, IFN-γ and IL-2 in CD8+ T cells was detected by flow cytometry on days 0 and 3. The results revealed almost no expression of the above markers on day 0 (Fig. 6I), which was consistent with the immature phenotype. On day 3, the expression of IL-2 and IFN-γ was significantly upregulated, but the expression of PD1 and LAG-3 was still not obvious (Fig. 6I), indicating that the CD8+ T cells had changed from a naive phenotype to an effector phenotype after CD3/28 antibody stimulation. After the third day, all effector CD8+ T cells were harvested and reassigned. On day 12, the expression levels of PD-1 and LAG-3 in CD8+ T cells in the SPP1OE-THP-1 group were significantly greater than those in the control group, whereas the expression levels of IL-2 and IFN-γ in CD8+ T cells were significantly lower, indicating an exhaustion-like phenotype (Fig. 6I). Furthermore, CD8+ T cells were collected at the endpoint and cocultured with SKBR3-luc cells to evaluate their cytotoxic activity. The results revealed that with increasing E: T ratio, the cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells in the SPP1OE-THP-1 group significantly decreased. Significant differences were observed at E: T ratios of 2:1 and 3:1 (Fig. 6J). Real-time imaging technology also revealed that the cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells in the SPP1OE-THP-1 group was significantly weakened (Fig. 6K-L). Videos of CD8+ T cells killing tumor cells can be found in Supplementary Material Video 2. These results indicate that SPP1+ macrophages may play an immunosuppressive role by promoting CD8+ T-cell exhaustion through SPP1 secretion. Collectively, these cellular experiments demonstrate that SPP1-positive macrophages not only reduce NK-cell cytotoxicity by increasing the expression of the immunosuppressive immune checkpoint molecule TIGIT on the surface of NK cells but also promote the exhaustion of CD8+ T cells to exert immunosuppressive effects that impair trastuzumab-mediated antitumor immunity in HER2-positive breast cancer.

SPP1+ TAMs induce NK-cell functional impairment in vivo

Animal experiments were further conducted to verify whether SPP1+ TAMs within the tumor microenvironment drive NK-cells exhaustion and consequently suppress NK-cell-mediated tumor cytotoxicity in vivo. To generate humanized NCG (NOD-Prkdcem26 Il2rgem26 Gpt) mice and promote NK cell lineage differentiation, human peripheral blood was transplanted into NCG mice, followed by injection of rh-IL-15. SPP1OE/EV-THP-1 cells and SKBR3 cells were coinoculated orthotopically into humanized NCG mice to mimic the microenvironmental immune niche with high or low SPP1+ TAMs infiltration. Two weeks after rh-IL-15 administration, peripheral blood was collected from the humanized NCG mice, and the proportions of human immune cells (CD45+ >15%) and NK (CD56⁺) cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (Figure S2A) [19]. The tumor-bearing mice were divided into four groups and treated with IgG or trastuzumab. During the treatment period, the tumor size was measured, and tumor samples were collected for analysis after the end of treatment (Fig. 7A). Tumor growth curve analysis revealed no significant increase in tumor growth in the SPP1OE-IgG group compared with the SPP1EV-IgG group, suggesting that SPP1 does not directly affect the growth of tumor cells (Fig. 7B). However, the tumor niche with high SPP1 expression did not respond to trastuzumab treatment, while the tumor cells in the niche with low SPP1 expression were more sensitive to trastuzumab treatment, with significantly inhibited tumor growth (Fig. 7B). Statistical analysis of tumor weight also revealed that tumors in the low-SPP1 niche were lighter and benefited from trastuzumab therapy (Fig. 7C-D). In addition, flow cytometry and multiplex immunofluorescence staining assays were used to evaluate the tumor microenvironment after trastuzumab treatment (Fig. 7E-G). Flow cytometry showed that compared with that in the SPP1EV-THP-1 group, the infiltration of NK cells in the SPP1OE-THP-1 group was lower, whereas the proportion of TIGIT-positive NK cells was significantly greater (Fig. 7E-F), which further revealed that the immunosuppressive phenotype of NK cells was closely related to SPP1-mediated treatment resistance. The multiplex immunofluorescence results also confirmed less NK-cells infiltration and a significantly greater percentage of exhausted TIGIT+ NK cells in the high-SPP1 tumor niche (Fig. 7G). The low-SPP1 tumor niche also showed increased NK-cells infiltration and a significant reduction in the exhausted TIGIT+ NK-cell phenotype (Fig. 7G and Supplementary Figure S2 C-D), with greater sensitivity to trastuzumab treatment. In vivo experiments revealed that SPP1+ TAMs mediated the upregulation of the immunosuppressive immune checkpoint protein TIGIT in NK cells and decreased trastuzumab-mediated NK-cell ADCC, which is consistent with an SPP1-high macrophage niche impairing NK-cell function, thereby forming an immunosuppressive TME at the tumor site to promote tumor-cell immune escape and resistance to trastuzumab therapy.

Discussion

NK cells are central effectors of trastuzumab-mediated antitumor immunity. Through its Fc domain, trastuzumab engages CD16 (FcγRIII) on NK cells and triggers antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, leading to perforin- and granzyme-mediated tumor-cell killing [2]. TIGIT is an inhibitory receptor expressed by NK and T cells, and TIGIT blockade has been shown to restore NK-cell activity and enhance downstream CD8⁺ T-cell immunity [20]. In line with this biology, our scRNA-seq analysis showed that radiologic nonresponders harbored a higher proportion of TIGIT⁺ NK cells with inhibitory and exhaustion-associated features. By contrast, radiologic responders were enriched for SELL⁺ naive-like NK cells and PRF1⁺/KLF3⁺ cytotoxic NK-cell subsets. Undoubtedly, the discovery cohort was small and was designed mainly for single-cell and mechanistic exploration; therefore, larger independent cohorts are needed for validation. However, analyses of multiple public HER2-positive breast cancer cohorts treated with trastuzumab or other anti-HER2 regimens generally also supported an association between stronger NK-cell functional signatures and favorable therapeutic outcomes. Together, these findings suggest that preservation of NK-cell cytotoxic competence is associated with better radiologic tumor regression after trastuzumab-based therapy.

Following NK-cell activation by trastuzumab, activated NK cells not only mediate direct ADCC but also release proinflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α through antibody-dependent cytokine release (ADCR) [21]. This inflammatory response may promote antigen presentation and help connect innate and adaptive antitumor immunity. Consistent with this coordinated immune process, residual tumors from radiologic nonresponders showed broader lymphocyte dysfunction, including enrichment of PDCD1⁺ CD8⁺ T cells with exhaustion-associated features and increased Treg-related immunosuppressive programs. External immunophenoscore analyses likewise suggested that favorable outcomes were generally associated with stronger effector-cell and antigen-presentation features, whereas suppressive-cell and checkpoint-related features were more prominent in unfavorable-outcome groups. Previous studies showing that the efficacy of HER2-directed antibodies or antibody-drug conjugates is reduced after depletion of adaptive immune cells and can be enhanced by immune-checkpoint blockade further support the biological relevance of these observations [22, 23]. Our data therefore indicate that poor radiologic response is associated with coordinated impairment of both NK-cell and adaptive T-cell immunity rather than dysfunction of a single effector compartment.

The formation of an immunosuppressive TME in breast cancer relies on multilevel cellular and molecular communication. Using cell communication algorithms, we found that NK and T cells in the group with poor response to trastuzumab-based therapy received stronger signals from myeloid cells. TAMs can acquire protumoral and immunosuppressive states, but their plasticity limits the efficacy of TAM-targeted strategies [24, 25]. Single-cell profiling therefore provides a more precise framework for resolving TAM heterogeneity beyond the classic M1/M2 model. Here, we identified a distinct SPP1+ TAM subpopulation that was significantly enriched in tumors with poor radiologic response after trastuzumab-based therapy. Analysis of public bulk RNA transcriptomic datasets showed that higher SPP1+ TAM abundance correlated with poorer overall survival and inferior neoadjuvant therapy outcomes in HER2-positive breast cancer. Integrative analyses of single-cell, bulk, spatial transcriptomic, and multiplex immunofluorescence data further showed that SPP1+ TAM infiltration was positively associated with immunosuppressive Tregs, exhausted NK cells, and exhausted CD8+ T cells, as well as their spatial proximity, suggesting potential cell-cell interactions within the TME. The link between SPP1 signaling and Treg-associated immunosuppression has also been supported by previous experimental and spatial studies, including breast cancer lung metastasis, hepatocellular carcinoma, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, and high-grade serous ovarian cancer models [26–29]. In our study, however, functional validation focused on NK cells and CD8+ T cells, the cytotoxic effector populations most directly connected to trastuzumab activity. Functional validation using SPP1-positive THP-1 macrophages confirmed their ability to upregulate TIGIT on NK cells and PD-1/LAG-3 on CD8+ T cells, thereby impairing tumor-killing activity in vitro. In vivo, humanized NCG mice bearing SPP1OE-THP-1 tumors showed weakened NK-cell efficacy, reduced intratumoral NK-cell infiltration, and increased TIGIT+ NK-cell proportions, confirming the direct immunosuppressive effects of SPP1-positive macrophages on NK and CD8+ T cells. Together, these findings demonstrate that SPP1-positive macrophages act as active drivers of immunosuppression-mediated trastuzumab resistance by impairing NK- and CD8⁺ T-cell antitumor immunity.

Similar SPP1-associated macrophage programs have also been described in other breast cancer subtypes. In triple-negative breast cancer, elevated SPP1 mRNA expression has been associated with M2-like macrophage infiltration and poor prognosis, and spatial studies suggest that SPP1⁺ TAMs can cooperate with stromal components to form immunosuppressive niches [30–32].In hormone receptor-positive breast cancer, TIL-high tumors paradoxically show poorer outcomes and enrichment of SPP1⁺ macrophages with ECM-remodeling programs. Their enhanced SPP1-, MIF-, and FN1-mediated interactions with CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T cells suggest impaired antitumor immunity despite lymphocyte infiltration, contributing to an immunosuppressive microenvironment and poor prognosis [33]. Beyond breast cancer, SPP1⁺ TAMs have been linked to immunosuppressive TMEs across multiple tumor types. In prostate cancer, SPP1⁺ TAMs suppress CD8⁺ T-cell proliferation and effector function through adenosine signaling, thereby promoting resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade [34]. In early lung adenocarcinoma and bladder cancer, SPP1⁺ TAMs are associated with PD-L1/ARG1 or IL6-driven inflammatory programs, reduced T-cell activity, increased exhausted CD8⁺ T cells or Tregs, and poor immunotherapy responses [35, 36]. In colorectal cancer, SPP1⁺ TAMs further impair antitumor immunity by promoting CD137⁺ Treg maturation through the SPP1-CD44-NF-κB1 axis and by cooperating with TGF-β-activated stromal programs to limit T-cell recruitment, clonal expansion, and response to PD-L1 blockade [37, 38]. Collectively, these findings identify SPP1⁺ TAMs as a conserved myeloid program across diverse tumor types that actively orchestrates immunosuppressive niche formation through multiple complementary mechanisms, ultimately driving NK and T-cell dysfunction, immune evasion, and resistance to both conventional and immunotherapeutic interventions.

To further explore their immunosuppressive mechanism, we analyzed ligand-receptor interactions between SPP1+ TAMs and immunosuppressive or exhausted lymphocyte subsets and identified the SPP1-CD44 axis as a central communication pathway. UMAP projection showed that SPP1 was enriched in myeloid cells, whereas CD44 was enriched in T and NK cells; both were significantly elevated in the SD group, suggesting that the SPP1-CD44 axis is critical for SPP1+ TAM-driven immunosuppression. Klement et al. reported that SPP1 inhibits CD8+ T-cell proliferation and activation via the SPP1-CD44 axis in a dose-dependent manner [39]. Wei et al. showed that SPP1-deficient glioma mice had reduced macrophage infiltration in the spleen, blood, and brain tumors, increased activated CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, fewer immunosuppressive FOXP3⁺ T cells in peripheral blood, and prolonged survival [40]. He et al. further demonstrated that SPP1 overexpression induces CD8+ T-cell exhaustion in hepatocellular carcinoma models. Anti-SPP1, anti-CD44, or anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody monotherapy suppressed tumor growth, while combination therapy produced synergistic effects, supporting the role of the SPP1‒CD44 axis in lymphocyte exhaustion [41].

Macrophage metabolic reprogramming is another key mechanism shaping the immunosuppressive microenvironment. Using the scFEA algorithm, we characterized the metabolic features of myeloid cells and found significant upregulation of glycolytic pathways in SPP1+ TAMs, including the conversion of glucose-6-phosphate to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate and pyruvate to lactate. Macrophages maintain their immunosuppressive phenotype through lactate metabolism, while lactate accumulation promotes metabolic alterations in immune cells, T-cell exhaustion, and enhanced Treg immunosuppressive function [42, 43]. In addition to enhanced glycolysis, SPP1+ TAMs showed increased adenine nucleotide synthesis, providing precursors for adenosine, a classic immunosuppressive metabolite that inhibits lymphocyte function. Further analysis revealed elevated NT5E expression in SPP1+ TAMs, encoding CD73, a membrane-bound extracellular 5’-nucleotidase that generates adenosine from monophosphorylated nucleotides (Supplementary Figure S2E). Compared with other subtypes, TIGIT+ NK cells, Tregs, and PDCD1+ CD8+ T cells showed higher ADORA2A expression, suggesting that SPP1+ TAMs may suppress neighboring NK and T cells through adenosine metabolism (Supplementary Figure S2F). This mechanism is supported by prostate cancer research from Aram Lyu et al., which showed that enhanced adenosine metabolism in SPP1+ TAMs suppressed CD8+ T-cell function and mediated immunotherapy resistance; moreover, blocking adenosine receptors reversed SPP1+ TAM-mediated CD8+ T-cell immunosuppression in vitro [34]. Together, these findings indicate that SPP1+ TAMs mediate tumor immunosuppression through diverse mechanisms that warrant further investigation.

In conclusion, our study validates through multiomic and functional approaches that SPP1+ TAMs induce immunosuppression in NK and T cells, thereby driving trastuzumab resistance in HER2-positive breast cancer. Although SPP1+ TAMs have been identified in multiple cancers, their role in HER2-positive breast cancer—particularly as drivers of immunosuppression-mediated targeted therapy resistance—is confirmed here for the first time. Our findings demonstrate that the infiltration abundance of SPP1+ TAMs can serve as a biomarker for a poor prognosis in HER2-positive breast cancer patients. Targeting SPP1+ TAMs or combining such strategies with immune checkpoint blockade may help restore antitumor immune activity in this setting.

Methods

Clinical tissue samples

This study collected surgical specimens from 8 HER2-positive breast cancer patients who underwent neoadjuvant trastuzumab-targeted therapy combined with chemotherapy (TCbH or AC-TH) for 18–24 weeks. All patients had primary breast cancer staged as II-III based on the 8th Edition of the Cancer Staging Manual of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC), with no distant metastasis at diagnosis. Pretreatment core-needle biopsies confirmed HER2 positivity (IHC 3+) in all patients (Supplementary Table 1). During the study period, any treatments potentially affecting the immune system or HER2 expression (including radiotherapy) were prohibited. All specimens were collected with written informed consent and approval from the institutional review boards (Ethics Committee of the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University and the Independent Ethics Committee of the General Surgery Department, Second Affiliated Hospital of Air Force Medical University).

All analyzed samples showed failure to achieve a pathological complete response (pCR). Clinical efficacy was evaluated using pre- and postneoadjuvant therapy MRI results. Treatment responses were classified according to the RECIST 1.1 criteria, as follows: partial response (PR): ≥30% reduction in the sum of target lesion diameters (longest diameter for non-lymph node lesions, short-axis diameter for lymph nodes); progressive disease (PD): ≥20% increase in target lesion size or absolute growth ≥ 5 mm compared with the smallest value during treatment; and stable disease (SD): results not meeting the criteria for either a PR or PD [44]. In this research, SD was defined as radiologic nonresponse, while a PR was classified as a treatment response. Four patients with > 30% tumor shrinkage (PR) were assigned to the responder group; four patients with tumor changes between 30% shrinkage and 20% growth (SD) were assigned to the nonresponder group. In this study, the terms “responders” and “nonresponders” refer specifically to MRI-based RECIST 1.1 radiologic responders and radiologic nonresponders, respectively, and should not be interpreted as pathological complete response (pCR) versus non-pCR status, as all scRNA-seq samples were collected from postoperative residual tumors.

scRNA-seq data analysis

Raw data were processed using Seurat (v4.3.0) in R. Cell Ranger (10x Genomics, v7.2.0) was used to decode the raw sequencing data into gene expression matrices. The data filtering criteria were as follows: genes expressed in < 20 cells were removed; cells with > 5,000 or < 500 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) were excluded; and cells with a mitochondrial gene content > 15% were discarded. The filtered data were normalized using the LogNormalize method (scale factor = 10,000). The top 2,000 highly variable genes were selected based on mean expression and dispersion. Batch effects were corrected using Harmony, and PCA (50 principal components) followed by UMAP was used to visualize cell clusters (resolution = 0.8) [45, 46].

Major cell types were annotated using canonical marker genes. Subpopulation-specific markers were identified via FindAllMarkers (Seurat default parameters) for enrichment analysis.

Odds ratios (ORs) of cell subtype proportions between PR and SD groups were calculated, with OR > 1 indicating enrichment in a group [47].

Differential expression analysis between specified cell groups or cell states was performed using the FindMarkers function in Seurat. Genes were ranked by avg_log2FC from the differential expression analysis, and GSEA was performed using clusterProfiler (v4.6.2) with Gene Ontology and MSigDB gene sets. Pathways were considered significantly enriched when they met the predefined criteria of NES > 1, q value/FDR < 0.25, and BH-adjusted p value < 0.05. A positive NES indicated enrichment in the first comparison group. Representative significant pathways were selected for visualization based on statistical significance and biological relevance to the corresponding analysis context.

Pseudotime trajectories were inferred using Slingshot (v2.7.0) on normalized data. Lineages were constructed via UMAP and Leiden clustering, with curves fitted by generalized additive models (GAMs) [48].

The Ucell function was used to score individual cells in the scRNA-seq data based on Mann‒Whitney U statistics to calculate the score of the selected gene set at the single-cell level. This score is robust to dataset size and heterogeneity [49]. The gene sets of the HLA_independent inhibitor-receptor and HLA_inhibitor-receptor pathways used in the NK-cell function score are from literature reports [50], whereas the remaining pathways are from the GO and MSigDB gene sets. VlnPlot, a built-in function in Seurat, was then used to draw violin plots and compare the scores of specified gene sets among different subgroups.

Bulk RNA-seq data analysis

GSVA (v2.0.5, Gaussian kernel) was used to score predefined gene sets (GO, MSigDB) in the FPKM-normalized bulk RNA-seq data [51]. For external validation, GSVA scores of NK cell-related functional signatures and immunophenoscore-related features were compared across public HER2-positive anti-HER2 therapy cohorts with available treatment outcome annotations, including GSE123845, GSE66305, GSE181574, and GSE55348. Kaplan‒Meier curves (survminer v0.5.0) were used to compare OS in HER2-positive METABRIC patients (n = 224) stratified by SPP1 or SPP1+ TAMs marker expression (log-rank p < 0.05) [52, 53].

Spatial transcriptomic data analysis

The cellular composition of each spot was computed using cell2location (v0.1.4). The reference expression signatures of major cell types were estimated through regularized negative binomial regression combined with our integrated scRNA-seq atlas [54]. Gene set signature scores for each spot were calculated using the GSVA function.

Gene expression–immune infiltration correlation

The association between SPP1 expression and immune-cell infiltration in HER2-positive breast cancer was analyzed using the TIMER3.0 platform (http://timer.comp-genomics.org/; accessed on May 23, 2026). The BRCA-Her2 cohort (n = 82) was selected for subtype-specific analysis. Spearman correlation coefficients between SPP1 expression and immune infiltration estimates were obtained from multiple TIMER3.0-supported deconvolution methods. The multi-method results were summarized as a heatmap, and adjusted p values provided by TIMER3.0 were used to indicate statistical significance [55].

Cell culture

Primary NK cells and naive CD8+ T cells were isolated from healthy donor peripheral blood and cultured separately with TheraPEAK X-VIVO 15 complete medium (Lonza, Cat# BEBP02-054Q) or LONZA X-VIVO 15 complete medium (Lonza, Cat# 02-053Q, added 100 U/mL rh-IL-2). Human HER2-positive SKBR3 breast cancer cell lines was obtained from Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd and cultured with complete DMEM medium (Gibco, Cat# C11995500BT). All culture media were supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% 100× penicillin‒streptomycin (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 10378016). THP-1 cell lines were obtained from Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd and cultured with THP-1 Cell Complete Medium (Procell, Cat# CM-0233). All cell lines were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. Transwell inserts (Corning, Cat# 3452) with a pore size of 0.4 μm in a 24-well culture plate were used to assess the impact of SPP1-positive macrophages on NK cells and CD8+ T cells. All cells were mycoplasma-free and authenticated using short tandem repeat DNA profiling analysis.

Lentivirus transduction

SKBR3 cells were transduced with lentivirus carrying firefly luciferase (MOI = 10) and selected with puromycin (MCE, Cat# CL13900) to generate the SKBR3-luc line. THP-1 cells were transduced with lentivirus overexpressing SPP1 (MOI = 20) and selected with puromycin to generate the SPP1OE-THP-1 cell line, with SPP1EV-THP-1 cells serving as the negative control. The lentivirus used in this study was purchased from Genechem.

Primary human NK cells and naive CD8+ T cells isolation

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from healthy donor peripheral blood (obtained from Xijing Hospital, ShaanXi, China) by density gradient centrifugation (GE Healthcare) at 2500 rpm for 25 min at room temperature using lymphocyte separation medium (TBD, Cat# LTS1077). The PBMC pellet from the buffy coat was collected and treated with red blood cell lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 00-4333-57) for 2 min to remove the red blood cells. Human NK cells were isolated using a Human NK Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat# 130-092-657) and according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Naive CD8+ T-cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat# 130-093-244) was used to isolate naive CD8+ T cells according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Blood samples were collected with written informed consent and approval from the ethics committees of patients affiliated hospitals.

Activation of naive CD8+ T cells

A total of 1 × 106 primary naive CD8+ T cells were cultured with 2 mL of complete medium in 24-well plates precoated with 1 µg/mL anti-human CD3 (BioLegend, Cat# 317326) and CD28 (BioLegend, Cat# 302934) antibodies for 4 h. After 72 h of CD3/CD28 antibody stimulation, the cells were collected and used for further experiments.

Reverse transcription and quantitative PCR (RT‒qPCR)

Total mRNA was isolated from cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Ambio, 15596026CN) and reverse transcribed into cDNA using HIScriptⅡQ RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, R323-01). Furthermore, ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q311-02) was used to perform quantitative PCR in triplicate, and the products were loaded onto a 7500 Fast Real-time PCR System. The primers used for SPP1 and GAPDH as loading controls were designed by Tsingke Biotechnology. The 2(-ΔΔCT) method was used to quantify RNA levels.

Western blot analysis

The cell precipitate was harvested and lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Biosharp, Cat# BL504A). A protease inhibitor (MCE, Cat# HY-K0010) was added, and the mixture was subsequently lysed for 30 min on ice. A BCA protein quantification kit (Vazyme, Cat# E112-01) was used to determine the protein concentration. Subsequently, 20 µg of protein was separated by 12% SDS–PAGE (ACE, Cat# X12012Gel) and electrotransferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Cat# ISEQ00010). Then, the membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the corresponding primary antibodies. The membranes were washed and incubated with secondary HRP-conjugated IgG antibodies (Cell Signaling, Cat# 7074P2) for 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized and quantified by exposure of film to the membranes in a dark room with chemiluminescence (Thermo Fisher, Cat# A38556).

Flow cytometry

NK cells and CD8+ T cells were analyzed via flow cytometry (FCM) using the following primary antibodies: APC-labeled anti-human CD56 (BioLegend, Cat# 392405), PE-labeled anti-human CCR7 (BioLegend, Cat# 988902), FITC-labeled anti-human IFN-γ (BioLegend, Cat# 502505), APC-labeled anti-human IL-2 (BioLegend, Cat# 500309); APC-labeled anti-human LAG-3 (BioLegend, Cat# 369211); and PE-labeled anti-human PD-1 (BioLegend, Cat# 379209) antibodies. Briefly, 5 × 105 cells were washed and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 2% bovine serum albumin and 0.2% sodium azide and incubated with secondary antibodies for 20 min at room temperature. After incubation, the cells were washed three times and analyzed using FCM.

Multiplex immunofluorescence

Paraffin sections of tumor tissue samples from patients and mice were dewaxed, washed, and subjected to antigen retrieval. The paraffin sections were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min and incubated with primary antibodies against different cell types overnight at 4 °C. The corresponding secondary antibodies were conjugated, and the nuclei were stained with DAPI (Servicebio). Then, the paraffin sections were sealed with an antifluorescence quencher, and an image processing system (Nikon, Japan) was used for visualization of the stained sections. For quantitative analysis of multiplex immunofluorescence images, postoperative tumor specimens were collected from HER2-positive breast cancer patients who received trastuzumab-based neoadjuvant therapy and were classified as radiologic responders (PR, n = 7) or radiologic nonresponders (SD, n = 7) according to MRI-based RECIST 1.1 assessment. Three multiplex immunofluorescence panels were performed for each patient sample: DAPI/CD56/TIGIT/CD68/SPP1, DAPI/CD8/PDCD1/CD68/SPP1, and DAPI/CD4/FOXP3/CD68/SPP1. These panels were used to quantify TIGIT+CD56+ NK cells, PDCD1+CD8+ T cells, and CD4+FOXP3+ Tregs, respectively. To reduce image-selection bias, non-analyzable areas were excluded before region selection according to predefined criteria, including tissue detachment, staining contamination, necrotic areas, stromal-only regions lacking tumor tissue, and areas with poor staining or imaging quality [56]. From the remaining analyzable tumor regions, six 200 μm × 200 μm fields were randomly selected for each sample. In each field, immune-cell counts were quantified for the corresponding marker-defined immune-cell population, and SPP1 fluorescence intensity was measured in the same field. For group comparisons and correlation analyses, the six fields from each sample were first averaged, and the sample-level mean was used as the statistical unit [57, 58].

Transwell cell coculture assay

To determine the effects on the phenotypes of NK cells and CD8+ T cells under different conditions, 5 × 104 SPP1OE-THP-1 or SPP1EV-THP-1 cells in 200 µL of culture medium were seeded in the Transwell inserts in 24-well plates. A total of 5 × 104 NK or CD8+ T cells were suspended in 1 mL of culture medium and added to the lower chambers of 24-well plates. After maintenance in culture for the specified time, the cells were collected from the lower chambers to detect changes in phenotype.

Live cell imaging

SKBR3 cells (1 × 105 cells/mL) were seeded into a 6-well plate and placed in a live cell imaging system (JuLI Stage, South Korea) for cultivation and real-time observation. The cultivation conditions were 37 °C and 5% CO2, and the cells were allowed to fully adhere to the well. The SKBR3 cells were divided into four groups: (1) Negative control group; (2) Trastuzumab treatment group; (3) NK/CD8+ T cells co-cultured with SPP1OE-THP-1 cells and trastuzumab group (E: T of 4:1 for NK cells and 3:1 for CD8+ T cells); (4) NK/CD8+ T cells co-cultured with SPP1EV-THP-1 cells and trastuzumab group. The plate was placed into a living cell workstation, and the green fluorescent dead cell dye YOYO-1 (Biotium, Cat# 40090) was added at a dilution of 1:5000. 2 µg/mL trastuzumab was added to mediate ADCC effect of NK cells. The plate was incubated in a moist incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 72 h. Real-time observation was performed using a JuLI Stage Real-Time Cell History Recorder (NanoEnTek, South Korea).

Luciferase-based cytolysis assay

A total of 1 × 104 SKBR-3 cells were seeded in 96-well plates with 100 µL of complete DMEM. After different treatments, NK cells and CD8+ T cells were cultured at different effector-to-target (E: T) ratios for 24 h, and 100 µL of complete culture medium from NK cells or CD8+ T cells was added to maintain cell growth. 2 µg/mL trastuzumab was added to mediate ADCC effect of NK cells. Cell pellets were collected and lysed with 20 µL of Passive Lysis Buffer (Promega, Cat# E1910) at room temperature for 15 min. Subsequently, 100 µL of luciferase substrate was added, and the luminescence intensity was measured using GloMax 20/20 (USA). Luciferase-derived cytotoxicity was calculated as the luminescence signal of target cells cultured without effector cells minus the luminescence signal of target cells after effector-cell co-culture.

Humanized mouse BC model

Six-week-old female NCG (NOD-Prkdcem26 Il2rgem26 Gpt) mice were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. All the animal experiments were performed in strict accordance with the animal protocol approved by Air Force Medical University (Shaanxi, China). After one week of acclimatization, the mice were inoculated with SKBR3 cells (5 × 10⁶ cells per mouse) mixed with SPP1OE/EV-THP-1 cells at a 1:3 ratio to establish an orthotopic breast tumor model. Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs, 1 × 10⁷ cells per mouse), isolated from healthy volunteers, were injected via the tail vein. Moreover, rhIL-15 (2,500 IU/dose) (MCE, Cat#HY-P77699) was administered via intraperitoneal injection every 3 days to promote the differentiation and expansion of NK cells in vivo [59]. Two weeks later, tail-tip blood was collected to quantify human CD45⁺ and CD56⁺ cells by flow cytometry. Mice with a human CD45⁺ cell proportion exceeding 15% and tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm³ were considered suitable for subsequent treatment experiments [19]. Humanized NCG mice (n = 20) were randomly assigned to four groups and treated with either isotype control antibody or trastuzumab (5 mg/kg). The mice received intravenous injections of the respective antibodies every three days. During the 15-day observation period, tumor dimensions were measured every three days with a Vernier caliper, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = (L × W²) / 2. After 15 days of treatment, tumor samples were harvested and subjected to multiplex immunofluorescence and dissociated into single cells for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

Biological experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired Student’s t tests or Mann-Whitney U tests were used as appropriate. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was used. Statistical methods for transcriptomic, survival, correlation, and enrichment analyses are described in the corresponding figure legends. A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
