Efficacy of low-dose nalmefene on recovery from general anaesthesia in adult patients: a multicentre randomised controlled study
Authors: Xiaowen Ma, Jueying Liu, Yizhe Zhang, Li Li, Guangming Zhang, Lika Xing, Xiaorong Huai, Ying Tang, Yubo Xie, Diansan Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis randomised controlled trial aims to evaluate the impact of low-dose intravenous nalmefene on anaesthesia recovery in adult patients aged 18–65 undergoing surgery under general anaesthesia.DesignThis was a multicentre, randomised, double-blind trial with two parallel arms clinical study.SettingFour hospitals from ChinaParticipantsA total of 514 patients aged 18–65 who underwent elective orthopaedic, urologic and thoracic surgeries.InterventionsOn completion of skin suturing, the intervention group received a single intravenous dose of nalmefene (0.25 µg/kg). The control group received an equivalent volume of normal saline at the same time point.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the interval time between the end of anaesthesia and the Aldrete score ≥9 being achieved in the postanaesthesia care unit (PACU). The secondary endpoints included the time interval from the end of operation to extubation; Richmond Agitation Sedation Scale (RASS) score at extubation; the time at Montreal Cognitive Assessment Scale (MoCA) orientation score ≥5; visual analogue scale (VAS) score in PACU and 24 hours postoperatively.Results514 patients were included in the Intention-to-Treat (ITT) analysis. The time interval between the end of anaesthesia and Aldrete score reached ≥9 in PACU was significantly shorter in the intervention group than in the control group (mean (SD) 24.8 (11.8) min vs 33.8 (12.5) min; 95% CI −8.8 (−10.8 to −6.7); p<0.001). Furthermore, the time interval from the end of surgery to extubation was shorter in the intervention than in the control group (mean (SD) 20.0 (9.9)min vs 27.6 (11.6) min; 95% CI −7.6 (−9.43 to −5.7), p<0.001). The time at Montreal cognitive assessment score ≥5 was also shorter in the control than in the intervention group (mean (SD) 20.0 (15.9) min vs 27.0 (20.5) min; 95% CI −7.0 (−10.2 to −3.8); p<0.001). Compared with the control group, the intervention group had better analgesic effect at the recovery endpoint (P Aldrete score ≥A was achieved as well as lower 24 hours points postoperatively (p<0.05)).ConclusionsIn patients aged 18–65 who underwent elective orthopaedic, urologic and thoracic surgeries, low-dose nalmefene administered at the end of surgery can accelerate postanaesthesia recovery without compromising analgesic effects.Trial registration numberNCT04713358.

Introduction

The recovery profile and prognosis following surgical anaesthesia have increasingly been recognised as critical indicators of anaesthetic efficacy and quality.1 Anaesthetic recovery is defined as a state in which patients are conscious, easily aroused and able to clearly identify their surroundings and their own identity.2 Recovery quality primarily encompasses the return of consciousness, length of stay in the postanaesthesia care unit (PACU), and the incidence of adverse sequelae such as pain, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), and confusion. Achieving a satisfactory recovery from general anaesthesia is essential to prevent complications including tachycardia, airway obstruction, hypoxaemia, blood pressure fluctuations and arrhythmia. Providing patients with a high-quality recovery characterised by smooth and rapid extubation and a quick return to daily activities remains a major challenge for anaesthesiologists. In clinical practice, delayed emergence from anaesthesia continues to be a common occurrence. Ensuring rapid awakening from general anaesthesia remains a significant challenge for anaesthesiologists.

It is reported that only 40% of patients attained complete physiologic recovery within 40 min. 3 The principal reason for delayed awakening after anaesthesia is the effects of anaesthetic agents. Both residual muscle relaxation and sedation (benzodiazepine) can be reversed to accelerate recovery and improve awakening quality246; however, based on clinical experience, opioids are more difficult to control than neuromuscular relaxants or benzodiazepine. Opioids continue to play an essential role in perioperative management, despite the occurrence of numerous dose-related side effects such as respiratory depression, delayed awakening, PONV, pruritus and dizziness.7 8 Nalmefene, which is unique as a long-acting antagonist against all three types of opioid receptors (µ, κ and δ), has been successfully used to relieve opioid overdose and reverse the effects of opioid.912 Clinical evidence demonstrates that low-dose nalmefene (a single 0.25 µg/kg bolus) effectively counteracts opioid-mediated adverse effects while optimising postanaesthetic recovery profiles in paediatric and adult populations, exhibiting reduced incidence of treatment-related complications.71214 While another study has demonstrated that 0.25 µg/kg nalmefene can shorten extubation time,15 there is no conclusive evidence regarding its effect on reducing PACU discharge time. Moreover, prior trials were limited by homogeneous surgical populations, small sample sizes and consequently low evidence quality. The influence of surgical type on outcomes may also vary significantly.

Our study addresses these limitations by including a broader range of surgical procedures and increasing the sample size. Furthermore, we selected the Aldrete score as a key outcome measure, as it provides a more comprehensive and standardised assessment for determining the appropriate time for PACU discharge. (The maximum Aldrete score is 10 points and a score≥9 indicates that the patient can be discharged from PACU under satisfactory safety conditions.) We conducted a larger sample size randomised controlled study providing stronger evidence to determine whether low-dose nalmefene (0.25 µg/kg) can reduce the interval between the end of anaesthesia and Aldrete recovery score ≥9 (in patients aged 18–65 having orthopaedic, urologic and thoracic surgeries under general anaesthesia), as well as the incidence of PONV and pruritus without compromising analgesic effects.

If this trial successfully proves that a low dose of nalmefene helps patients achieve the target Aldrete score more rapidly after anaesthesia, it would suggest that low-dose nalmefene can facilitate a faster and smoother transition from the PACU back to the ward. This finding would offer valuable, practical guidance for optimising anaesthesia recovery.

Methods

This was a placebo-controlled, two-arm, parallel-group, multicentre, double-blind, randomised, clinical study that included patients recovering from anaesthesia. The study was approved by the ethics committees of Renji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, School of Medicine (KY2020-150); Shanghai Tongren Hospital (2021-030-01); the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (2021-032); and the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (2021-KY-0495-003), the institutions where the study was conducted. Informed consent was obtained from all patients before the surgical procedure.

Patients

From September 2021 through December 2023, patients between 18 and 65 years old who underwent elective orthopaedic, urologic and thoracic surgeries under general anaesthesia were screened for study enrolment. The inclusion criteria included: (1) patients with physical status I or II of American Society of Anaesthesia (ASA); (2) BMI (kg/m2) ≥18 and ≤30; (3) estimated anaesthesia time 1–4 hours; (4) use narcotic analgesics (sufentanil or remifentanil) intraoperatively; (5) use electronic intravenous analgesia pump after surgery.

The exclusion criteria included: (1) refused to participate in this study; (2) refused to receive intravenous analgesia; (3) a medical or family history of cognitive disorders, delirium, epilepsy, alienation, anxiety or depression; (4) recently used anticholinergic drugs, antidepressants, anxiolytics or anticonvulsants; (5) a medical history of organic brain diseases or craniovascular diseases; (6) a history of allergy to any drug used in this study; (7) a history of drug addiction, alcoholism or drug abuse; (8) comorbid conditions of severe heart and lung disease; active heart disease; severe hepatic dysfunction (Child–Pugh class C); severe renal dysfunction (undergoing dialysis) before surgery; or critical illness (preoperative physical status classification ≥3 of ASA); (9) participated in other clinical trials within 4 weeks; (10) intraoperative complications, such as cerebrovascular accident, heart failure or pneumothorax, or transfer to the intensive care unit during hospitalisation; (11) unable to communicate in the preoperative period because of coma, profound dementia, language barrier or incapacity from severe disease; (12) anaesthesia time <1 or >4 hours; (13) chronic pain, which is defined as unsatisfied pain control for at least 1 month.

Randomisation and blinding

Written informed consent and basic data were obtained from the patients. Then, the patients were randomly assigned to either the intervention or control group in a 1:1 ratio with a computer-generated random number sequence in the Statistical Analysis Software (SAS) V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC). We employed a method of randomisation stratified by centre and type of surgery with a block length of 6 in the study. After the screening and randomisation of the patients, group allocation occurred immediately in sealed and number-coded envelopes. The randomised table contents, numbers and their relationship with the groups were kept secret from the subjects during the entire study process. Envelopes containing the random numbers were distributed to each centre. The drugs were administered to the patients according to the random numbers by an independent nurse (no further involvement in this research). The study drug was prepared by mixing 2 mL of normal saline with the drug at a dose based on the corresponding patient’s body weight. The drug packages had a completely consistent appearance and could not be distinguished from each other visually. During the entire study process, the patients, investigators, intraoperative attending anaesthesiologists, data collectors, data analysers, evaluators and statisticians were blinded to the group allocation. In the case of a medical emergency, the investigators were allowed to open the corresponding emergency envelope to identify the patient’s treatment. Then, they must justify such an emergency in the patient’s medical record and case report form. Serious adverse events were determined and recorded for further analysis at the end of the study and the investigators must explore the correlation between these events and the patient’s healthcare records.

Anaesthesia and perioperative care

All patients had a peripheral vein opened venous access on the day of the surgery and were monitored continuously with pulse oximetry, ECG non-invasive blood pressure and bispectral index (BIS). General anaesthesia was induced using propofol (1–2 mg/kg), rocuronium (0.6–0.8 mg/kg) and sufentanil (0.3–0.5 µg/kg). During the surgery, propofol (1–2 µg/mL) and remifentanil (0.1–0.3 µg/kg/min) were maintained, whereas sevoflurane minimum alveolar concentration (MAC), which was age-adjusted 0.6–1.0 and cisatracurium or rocuronium were given as needed. These anaesthetic agents were adjusted to maintain a BIS value of 40 to 60. We implemented routine body temperature monitoring to maintain it at approximately 36°C; however, the specific temperature values were not recorded. At approximately 30 min before the end of surgery (last placement of suture), cisatracurium or rocuronium infusion was stopped and 0.1 µg/kg sufentanil was administered intravenously. Before the end of surgery, parecoxib sodium 40 mg was administered intravenously. All anaesthetic drugs, including propofol, remifentanil and sevoflurane, were stopped when the end of surgery (skin suturing was completed) and the intervention group received nalmefene 0.25 µg/kg, whereas the control group received the same volume of normal saline. Patients were transferred to the PACU still intubated. In the PACU, the patients were administered neostigmine (0.02–0.04 mg/kg) and atropine (0.01 mg/kg) to antagonise the residual neuromuscular block until their swallowing function recovered. Tracheal extubation was performed when the patients were conscious and had a respiratory rate >12/min and no residual muscle weakness. On extubation, we began assessing the Aldrete score at 5 min intervals. Once an Aldrete score ≥9 was achieved, the time was recorded. We then calculated both the time from the end of anaesthesia until an Aldrete score ≥9 was reached and the time of extubation. If the patient had a visual analogue scale (VAS) ≥ 4, VAS score would be judged by an anaesthesiologist, who is unaware of the treatment allocation of the institutes, at various stages of recovery, with 10 being possible maximum pain and 0 absent. Remedial analgesic, such as sufentanil (2.5 µg), was re-administered. For patient-controlled intravenous analgesia, sufentanil (2–2.5 µg/kg) and antiemetic drugs diluted with saline to 100 mL were immediately administered to the patients intravenously when they are leaving the PACU. The pump delivers a continuous background infusion at 1 mL/h, with patient-activated boluses of 2 mL available every 15 min.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was the interval between the end of anaesthesia and the time when Aldrete score ≥9 was achieved in the PACU. The secondary outcomes were as follows: (1) the interval from the end of surgery (last placement of suture) to extubation in the PACU; (2) RASS score at extubation; (3) the time at orientation score (assessed by the MoCA orientation every 5 min) ≥ 5; (4) the administration times of remedial analgesic (sufentanil) required in the PACU; (5) VAS score at the recovery endpoint (when Aldrete score ≥9 was achieved) and at 1 and 24 hours postoperatively; (6) adverse effects, including PONV and pruritus, in the PACU; (7) incidence of PONV and pruritus within 24 hours postoperatively.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were based on ITT principles. Patients who received any randomised intervention were analysed as the full analysis set (FAS). Since no subjects were lost to follow-up in either group, imputation of missing values was not required in the FAS. The per-protocol set (PPS) includes subjects who closely adhered to the protocol, considering factors such as major protocol violations: anaesthesia time >4 hours; BMI (kg/m2) >30; patients>65 years old, Physical Status III of ASA. The analysis of the primary endpoint and key secondary endpoints was conducted simultaneously in both the FAS and PPS. The Safety Analysis Set included all patients who received the study intervention and had at least one safety assessment. The general characteristics of patients were described using mean (SD) or median (IQR) for continuous variables and numbers (percentage) for categorical variables. The primary endpoint was analysed using a general linear model including the intervention and the centre effects, study centres were included in the model as a fixed effect. In addition, a sensitivity analysis was supplemented based on a mixed-effects model, in which centres were treated as random effects, and surgical type and treatment group were included as covariates simultaneously. For continuous secondary endpoints, the student’s t-test or Welch’s t-test was used to evaluate the intervention effect between the groups. For categorical secondary endpoints, the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the incidences between groups, and the Newcombe method was used to calculate 95% CIs. Safety endpoints were analysed using the chi-squared test. Subgroups of interest included age (≤60 years and >60 years), sex, history of hypertension (yes or no), BMI (≤25 kg/m2 and >25 kg/m2), ASA (I and II or III) and anaesthesia time (>120 min and ≤120 min). All analyses were performed with the use of SAS V.9.4. All comparative tests described above (including t-tests, chi-squared tests, etc.) were performed as two-sided tests and p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The sample size was determined based mainly on the interval between the end of anaesthesia and the time Aldrete score ≥9 was achieved. At present, this time interval reported in literature was controversial, ranging from 70–83 min15 to 25–40 min.16 A study by Trescot AM reported that complete physiologic recovery occurs within 40 min in 40% of patients.3 In our previous study, we found that the average interval between the end of anaesthesia and the time Aldrete score ≥9 is achieved without nalmefene administration is 30±30 min. Conversely, when nalmefene is administered to patients who underwent elective surgery, the time interval is assumed to be shortened to approximately 20 min. In the present study, the sample size was determined with PASS software V.19, employing a two-sided, two independent means two-sample t-test for comparing means within a framework of 1:1 randomisation. The total type I error was α=0.05, power of 90%. A total of 235 patients were estimated for each group (a total of 470 patients). As the sample shedding rate is usually about 10%, the final sample size was planned to be 520. The enrolled patients were randomised and equally divided into the intervention and control groups (n=260 for each group).

Results

A total of 542 patients were screened from September 2021 through December 2023 (figure 1 shows the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) flow diagram for this study). Of them, 19 were excluded due to cancelled surgery, five due to refusal to participate in the study and four due to the preoperative medication not meeting the requirements. In all, 254 patients in the control group and 260 in the intervention group were included in the FAS set. After excluding 28 patients due to protocol violations, 486 were finally included in the per-protocol analysis (figure 1). There were no patients who were lost to follow-up. The baseline characteristics of the patients were well balanced between the groups (table 1). Overall, no significant differences were observed in terms of previous medical history, anaesthesia time, intraoperative blood loss, amounts of intraoperative fluids, doses of opioids and other analgesics, intravenous anaesthetics and muscle relaxants.

Subgroup analyses

In subgroup analysis, it was found that the use of nalmefene was associated with a shorter interval between the end of anaesthesia and the time Aldrete score ≥9 was achieved in the PACU, and the results were not affected by age, sex, hypertension, weight, ASA grade and anaesthesia time (figure 2).

Discussion

In this multicentre randomised controlled trial, we demonstrated that a single low-dose nalmefene administered at the end of elective orthopaedic, urologic and thoracic surgeries significantly reduced the time to achieve Aldrete score ≥9 at the end of surgery compared with placebo, without compromising opioid analgesia. Additionally, patients receiving nalmefene showed faster recovery of cognitive orientation and maintained better analgesic control at 24 hours postoperatively. Our findings align with emerging evidence supporting the use of opioid receptor antagonists to accelerate recovery. The 10 min reduction in recovery time we observed is clinically meaningful in the context of increasing surgical volumes and constrained healthcare resources.16 17 Previous studies have reported variable recovery times, with Robert et al concluding that the interval between the end of anaesthesia and the time Aldrete score ≥9 is achieved is 70–83 min18 and Karabayirli et al found the interval to be 10–20 min.19 This variability likely reflects differences in reversal protocols and anaesthetic techniques. While Karabayirli et al achieved rapid recovery through premature discontinuation of remifentanil, this approach is procedure-specific and may not be suitable for more painful surgeries. Our protocol demonstrates that pharmacological reversal with nalmefene provides a universally applicable method without compromising postoperative analgesia.

Most previous nalmefene research has focused primarily on reducing opioid-induced nausea and vomiting.7 20 21 A previous study of 96 patients undergoing uvulopalatopharyngoplasty similarly found that 0.25 µg/kg nalmefene shortened recovery time15; however, that study’s use of unreversed midazolam and non-weight-based neuromuscular reversal introduces potential confounding. Our study expands this evidence by demonstrating its efficacy in enhancing recovery quality and efficiency. By enlarging the sample size and including more types of surgery, our study further confirms the effectiveness of nalmefene in improving recovery quality and efficiency.

The RASS score at extubation did not significantly differ between the groups. RASS can detect changes in sedation status and set against constructs in terms of level of consciousness and delirium. Patients will be divided into two categories based on agitation and sedation levels: non-agitated condition with RASS scores −5 to 0 and agitated condition 1 to 4.22 MoCA is a simple method that can be used for cognitive screening with superior sensitivity. MoCA contains the following cognitive abilities: visuospatial/executive function, naming, episodic memory, attention, language, abstraction and orientation. 23 24The orientation score is only selected in this study and includes six tasks (Date/Month/Year/What day/Location/City) with each correct answer scoring 1, the higher meaning better performance, suggesting that nalmefene effectively counteracts opioid-induced sedation without causing excessive agitation. This is particularly relevant given the growing emphasis on enhanced recovery after surgery protocols and the importance of minimising cognitive dysfunction in the postoperative period.

The VAS scores measured both on recovery and at 24 hours were significantly lower in the intervention group than in the control group. Recent studies have shown that adding nalmefene during postoperative analgesia with hydromorphone hydrochloride can enhance analgesic efficacy.20 Furthermore, some evidence suggests that low-dose nalmefene may not only promote the release of endogenous opioid peptides but also displace opioids from non-analgesic binding sites, thereby increasing the plasma concentration of fentanyl.25 26 The prolonged μ-opioid receptor occupancy of nalmefene (22–24 hours) likely underlies this sustained effect. 27Since the intraoperative combination of low-dose naloxone with high-dose remifentanil28 can mitigate postoperative hyperalgesia, nalmefene may have a similar effect. Clinically, although low-dose nalmefene has shown efficacy in improving postoperative analgesia with opioids, the precise mechanisms remain to be elucidated through further investigation.

The absence of significant differences in nausea, vomiting and pruritus suggests that a single nalmefene dose may be insufficient to significantly impact these side effects, consistent with previous findings on low-dose naloxone.29

Conclusions

This study provides robust evidence in patients aged 18–65 years who underwent elective orthopaedic, urologic and thoracic surgeries, demonstrating that low-dose nalmefene safely reduces post-anaesthesia care unit residency time without compromising analgesia. These findings support its incorporation into clinical practice for this patient population to enhance operating room efficiency, particularly in settings with constrained resources.
