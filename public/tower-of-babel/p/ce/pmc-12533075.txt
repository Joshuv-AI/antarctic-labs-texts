Computational insights into solvent encapsulation and host–guest recognition by calix[4]arene
Authors: Khalida Khalil, Béla Fiser, Magdalena Małecka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The current study investigates a series of calix[4]arene/organic solvent inclusion complexes formed with twelve carefully selected guest molecules. The analyzed guest molecules can be categorized as polar protic (methanol, ethanol, water, nitromethane), polar aprotic (dimethyl sulfoxide, acetone, acetonitrile, tetrafluoromethane), and non-polar (benzene, toluene, cyclohexane, diethyl ether) solvents. These systems were chosen to explore how solvent encapsulation influences host–guest interactions and the electronic properties of the calix[4]arene framework. Inclusion complexes were optimized in both gas and solvent phases using the dispersion-corrected B3LYP-D3 hybrid functional in combination with the 6-311+G(d,p) basis set. All described complexes are energetically favorable, with CX[4]/DMSO exhibiting the highest interaction energy of − 76.57 kJ/mol in the gas phase. Thermodynamic analyses including enthalpy and Gibbs free energy changes support the stability of the systems. The frontier molecular orbital analysis revealed shifts in HOMO–LUMO densities upon complexation, and energy gaps range between 4.46 and 5.25 eV confirming the kinetic stability of CX[4]/solvent systems. The interaction region indicator and QTAIM analysis revealed the nature and topology of non-covalent interactions. Moreover, molecular electrostatic potential maps highlight electrophilic and nucleophilic regions in the most stable inclusion complexes. These findings provide insight into the fundamental interactions governing solvent inclusion within calix[4]arene, with implications for host–guest chemistry and molecular recognition applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20260-x.

Introduction

Host–guest complexes, or inclusion complexes, are supramolecular systems formed by two or more molecules or ions held together by noncovalent interactions in a reversible manner1,2. The hosts can function as primary host receptors that have high selectivity and affinity for guest molecules because of their void cavity. Typically, macrocycles are employed as host molecules in this sense. These molecules extend to cucurbit[n]urils3, calix[n]arenes4, pillar[n]arenes5, resorcin-arenes6, cyclophanes7, cyclopeptides8, cyclodextrins9,10, cryptands11, and crown ethers12.

The literature reveals various examples of how these supramolecular structures can be used in a wide range of scientific and technological domains, including sensors, electronic devices, functional materials13, catalysts14, and the food and pharmaceutical sectors15–17. The non-covalent interactions concerning supramolecular compounds have been studied by Wang et al. from a theoretical perspective18,19. Besides this contribution, a number of applications of host–guest complexes have been reported, including their therapeutic usage as carriers and drug deliverers20, their potential employment in gene transfer for immune-modulating treatment21, the use of inclusion structures for cancer treatment22,23, and their application in crystallization as well24,25. These applications are linked to the structural and dynamic attributes of host–guest complexes.

The growth of supramolecular science has resulted in the formation of many structures with various sizes and shapes. The rapid advancement in this subject is mainly attributed to the transparency with which chemists foster molecular combinations by means of non-covalent interactions26. Among the developed macrocyclic materials, calix[n]arenes (CX[n], where n = number of phenolic units) hold an immensely distinctive position, owing to their facile charge transfer potential, tunable sizes, and derivatization27. Calix[n]arenes possess a hydrophobic cavity capable of encapsulating tiny entities via non-covalent interactions. CX[n] obtained from the alkaline condensation reaction of formaldehyde and phenol are utilized as an appropriate platform in a variety of processes such as adsorption28. Moreover, calix[n]arenes have demonstrated significant potential as substances for sensor applications owing to their sensitivity29. CX[n] are also used in the fields of food chemistry, pharmaceuticals, and catalytic processes, because of their underlying potential to form host–guest molecular interactions30,31. Previously, calix[4]arene has been studied as a sensing material for the 5-fuorouracil anticancer drug by Numan et al.28. Khalil et al. investigated the nonlinear optical properties of SA@calix[4]arene complexes32. In another study, calix[4]arene has been reported as a host to encapsulate different gaseous pollutants (N2, N2O, NO3 and CO2)1,33. Similarly, the interaction between calix[4]arenes and bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide (TFSI) ion has been reported. Significantly, the non-covalent interactions had been examined, and the most suitable host for TFSI has been selected for application in electrical technology34.

Within the CX[n] family, calix[4]arenes have a prominent role and despite their smaller cavity compared to other counterparts, their applicability has huge potential. Even though a large number of studies have been conducted, the influence of organic solvents on the properties of calix[4]arenes have not been investigated in detail. Solvents can have a great impact on the stability, solubility and polarity of calix[4]arene and thus, it is important to describe the interaction properties of CX[4]/organic solvent systems. To the best of our knowledge, no study has been reported on how organic solvents impact the geometrical, electrical, and polarity of calix[4]arenes. Therefore, the novelty of our work lies in the rigorous analysis of solvent inclusion behavior through diverse solvent types (protic, aprotic, non-polar) in calix[4]arene. In contrast to earlier studies that often focus on guest molecules like ions or drugs, this study emphasizes the thermodynamic and electronic parameters influencing solvent trapping, a topic with limited comparative data in the existing literature.

Furthermore, by integrating FMO, IRI, QTAIM, and thermodynamic studies, we present a cohesive knowledge of how various solvent types interact non-covalently with calix[4]arene. These insights aid rationalize selectivity trends, which are critical in solvent-driven molecular recognition and preorganization phenomena in host–guest chemistry. The present work focuses on describing inclusion complexes of calix[4]arenes with carefully selected polar protic (methanol, ethanol, water, nitromethane), polar aprotic (DMSO, acetone, acetonitrile, tetrahydrofuran), and non-polar (benzene, toluene, cyclohexane, diethyl ether) solvents (see Scheme S1) to expand knowledge in the field and to support material developments for potential applications in catalysis, and drug delivery35.

Computational methodology

All calculations have been performed by using density functional theory implemented in the Gaussian 16, Revision C.01 program36. GaussView 6.1.137 is used for examining the structures. Pristine CX[4], organic solvents (Scheme S1), and CX[4]/solvent complexes are optimized using the B3LYP-D3 functional and the 6-311+G(d,p) basis set. B3LYP is a hybrid DFT functional recognized for its application in conducting quantum chemical calculations to ascertain the electronic characteristics and thermodynamic stability of chemical compounds38. Moreover, B3LYP has been extensively employed for both optimization and mechanistic analysis of various systems2,3,39,40. Grimme’s dispersion correction (D3) has been employed in the calculations for the better description of non-covalent interactions41. The coordinates of studied organic solvents, polar protic (methanol, ethanol, water, nitromethane), polar aprotic (DMSO, acetone, acetonitrile, tetrahydrofuran), and non-polar (benzene, toluene, cyclohexane, diethyl ether), have been taken from the PubChem and optimized at the above-mentioned level of theory. Harmonic vibrational frequency analysis performed at the same level of theory as the geometry optimizations show no imaginary frequencies for all isolated and solvated structures, confirming that the reported geometries are true minima and that no transition-state artifacts are present. in CX[4]/solvent complexes. Other thermodynamic parameters such as enthalpy change (ΔH) and Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) are also computed in both phases. Additionally, the counterpoise approach is used to modify the interaction energies in order to eliminate the basis set superposition error (BSSE) generated by the overlapping of finite basis sets.

The reactivity parameters and frontier molecular orbitals (FMOs) have been described for CX[4]/solvent complexes to study the electronic level perturbation. Furthermore, various types of interactions between solvent atoms and CX[4] have been investigated using quantum theory of atoms in molecules (QTAIM) and interaction region indicator (IRI) analysis. Multiwfn43 and VMD44 softwares are used for additional visualization and to obtain further insights into the analysis. The electron-rich and electron-deficient regions on CX[4]/solvent complexes have also been identified by using MEP analysis.

Results and discussion

Geometrical considerations and arrangements of the complexes

The 3D macrocycle CX[4], having four phenolic oxygen atoms, has been identified as a neutral ligand capable of binding to metal cations45. The cone configuration is extensively preferred in the calix[4]arene host, that has C2 symmetry. Therefore, the structural stability of arene moieties is estimated to be determined by the reduced repulsions across the lone pairs of oxygen atoms46. Since CX[4] has methylene connections, all exclusive benzene rings form a box-shaped hole. In the optimized structure of the studied calix[4]arenes, O–H, O–C, and methylene linked C–C bond distances of the tailored CX[4] are 0.98 Å, 1.37 Å, and 1.51 Å, respectively, and are in agreement with bond lengths that have been previously reported45.

This study involves the selection of polar protic, polar aprotic, and non-polar solvents to study inclusion complexes with CX[4], aiming to explore the details of interactions within the systems. Different sensitive areas of calix[4]arenes can facilitate various potential interaction orientations. All the different orientations underwent thorough screening. Three orientations are considered during the complexation of each organic solvent with CX[4], including the inside cavity from bottom (A), top (B), and side rings (C) (Fig. 1). The cavity size is determined by calculating the internal diameter between opposite phenolic oxygen atoms of the calix[4]arene (Table 1). Upon inclusion of polar solvents, minor distortions are observed due to dipolar interactions, which either compressed or expanded the cavity based on the solvent geometry and interaction strength. In contrast, non-polar solvents (e.g., cyclohexane, toluene) induced minimal perturbations, maintaining the cone-like structure of calix[4]arene. Thermodynamic parameters

The typical thermodynamic parameters are determined by harmonic vibrational analysis.

Calculations are conducted in gas and solvent phase. In the latter case the CPCM implicit solvent model was employed to include solvent effects. The thermodynamic parameters of the CX[4]/solvent systems including interaction energy (Eint) (Table 1), enthalpy change (ΔH), and Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) (Table 2) are calculated. The interaction energies range from − 34.57 kJ/mol to − 76.02 kJ/mol in the studied CX[4]/solvent complexes. The findings show that the inclusion complex CX[4]/DMSO (with polar aprotic solvent) has the lowest interaction energy value of − 76.02 kJ/mol among the inclusion complexes with three types of solvents (polar protic, polar aprotic and non-polar) and CX[4]/water (with polar protic solvent) possesses the highest (− 34.57 kJ/mol) in vacuum. The designed systems are re-optimized to check the effect of solvent on the interaction energy. The computed results revealed that all complexes have weak interactions in solvent phase and this unusual behavior is due to the polarity of these systems in the solution. Moreover, dipole moments of CX[4]/solvent systems have been calculated in both phases (Table 2). It is anticipated that the polarity of these systems increases in the solvent phase that resulted in the decrease of interaction energies. Another factor that influences the interaction energies in solvent phase is the dielectric constant (ε) of these solvents. In general, the trend in dielectric constants of different solvents is followed by polar protic > polar aprotic > non-polar. In solvent phase, CX[4] complexes with polar protic solvents possess high values of interaction energy due to high dielectric constants. Similarly, the complexes of non-polar solvents with low dielectric constants have lower interaction energies. The overall results revealed that CX[4]/water complex with highest ε (78.35) has the highest value of Eint (− 24.57 kJ/mol) and CX[4]/cyclohexane complex with lowest ε (2.01) possesses the lowest Eint (− 50.25 kJ/mol) in solvent phase (Table 2).

Generally, the observed trend in Table 2, where polar solvents exhibit significantly lower interaction energies in the solvent phase compared to the gas phase, can be attributed to several other factors. Firstly, polar solvents such as water and DMSO form strong hydrogen-bonding networks in the bulk phase. Disrupting these interactions to accommodate a guest molecule within the calix[4]arene cavity incurs a substantial desolvation and cavitation energy penalty, which diminishes the net binding energy. Secondly, polar solvents inherently favor strong solvent–solvent interactions that can compete with or even outweigh the host–guest interactions, further reducing the effective stabilization of the complex in solution. In contrast, non-polar solvents like toluene or cyclohexane lack such strong intermolecular interactions. As a result, the energetic cost of including a guest molecule is minimal, and the interaction energies in the solvent phase remain relatively close to those observed in the gas phase. These effects collectively explain the apparent underestimation of interaction energies for polar solvents in the solvent environment.

All CX[4]/solvent complexes were evaluated using counterpoise correction to address basis set superposition error (BSSE). The resulting corrected interaction energies, ranging from − 34.01 to − 70.04 kJ mol⁻1 (as shown in Table 2), exhibit only minor deviations from the uncorrected values. This suggests that BSSE has a insignificant impact on the calculated interaction energies of the CX[4]/solvent complexes. Therefore, the use of the B3LYP functional alongside the 6-311+G(d,p) basis set is validated as a reliable approach for the current computational investigation.

All the Eint and ΔH are negative in gas as well as in solvent phase that demonstrated that these are exothermic process. In the case of Gibbs free energies (ΔG), CX[4]/solvent complexes show different behavior in both phases. In gas phase, CX[4] complexes with polar protic (except water with 3.48 kJ/mol) and polar aprotic (except tetrahydrofuran with 5.20 kJ/mol) solvents have negative ΔG values that confirm the formation of these systems is spontaneous. However, the positive Gibbs free energy for inclusion complexes with water and THF (polar solvents) indicate that the encapsulation is thermodynamically unfavorable under standard conditions. Previously reported studies indicated the limited inclusion of water in calix[4]arene cavities, primarily due to water’s small size and high hydration energy in bulk47,48. The complexes of CX[4] with non-polar solvents (except toluene with − 0.9 kJ/mol) have positive ΔG values. FMO analysis

The frontier orbital energy is crucial in defining the kinetic stability and chemical reactivity of the molecular structure. FMO analysis (particularly HOMO–LUMO gaps) is not a direct indicator of kinetic stability for noncovalent complexes but can provide qualitative insights into electronic polarization, reactivity trends, and charge transfer interactions between host and guest49,50. A molecule characterized by a small frontier orbital energy gap exhibits increased polarizability and is typically linked to enhanced chemical reactivity and reduced kinetic stability, often referred to as a soft molecule. The values of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO), along with their energy gap, indicate the chemical reactivity of the molecule. A larger HOMO–LUMO energy gap is considered a symbol of the high stability of the given molecule51. HOMO and LUMO of the complexes of calix[4]arene with polar protic, polar aprotic, and non-polar solvents have been determined (Fig. 3, Fig. S2).Fig. 3Surface plots of frontier molecular orbitals of selected CX[4]/solvent complexes and the corresponding energy gaps Eg (eV). The HOMO and LUMO orbitals are concentrated on both host and guest molecules, reflecting that mutual interactions have been established between CX[4] and the studied organic solvents (Fig. 2, Fig. S2). The computed energy gap for pure CX[4] moiety is 5.17 eV. The energy gap (HOMO–LUMO) of CX[4]/solvent systems range from 4.46 eV to 5.25 eV. All the complexes show significant values of energy gaps that indicate their kinetic stability. The CX[4]/benzene system exhibits the largest Eg value of 5.25 eV that indicates high kinetic stability of this complex. Moreover, CX[4]/nitromethane has the lowest Eg value of 4.46 eV which reveals that this complex is kinetically less stable as compared to all other complexes.

Global reactivity parameters

The global reactivity parameters for each CX[4]/solvent complex have been calculated at the same level of theory (Table 4); ionization potential (I), electron affinity (A), chemical potential (μ), global hardness (η = (I − A)/2), softness (S = 1/η), and electrophilicity (ω = μ2/2η). Across the series, η is narrow (≈ 2.23–2.63 eV) and S varies modestly (≈ 0.38–0.45 eV), indicating similar gaps among complexes, while μ (and thus χ = −μ) and ω drive most differences. The nitromethane complex is the most electrophilic (ω = 5.78 eV) and softest (η = 2.23 eV, S = 0.45 eV), followed by acetonitrile (ω = 3.79 eV), whereas DMSO, acetone, THF, and diethyl ether yield lower ω (~ 3.0–3.3 eV) and slightly larger η. Non-polar aromatics (benzene, toluene) and the protic solvents (water, methanol, ethanol) lie mid-range. Overall, polar aprotic, electron-withdrawing guests increase electrophilicity and softness of the complex, whereas less polar/weakly interacting guests leave it relatively harder and less electrophilic. Interaction region indicator (IRI) analysis

The IRI analysis is a statistical approach which provides deeper understanding of the nature of interactions between molecules or within molecules52,53. This has been carried out by examining the overlap of electron density across multiple points of interest. It comprises 2D IRI graphs and 3D isosurfaces. Further it can be expressed by using the following equation:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$IRI\left(r\right)=\frac{|\nabla \rho \left(r\right)|}{{|\rho \left(r\right)|}^{a}}$$\end{document}

Consequently, the analysis of 3D-isosurfaces and 2D-IRI graphs provides significant insights into the covalent and non-covalent interaction of CX[4]/solvent systems. Typically, 3D isosurfaces represent regions of distinct colors, such as green, red, and blue, indicating weak non-covalent (van der Waals), repulsive and covalent interactions (strong H-bonding), respectively. IRI graphs exhibiting spikes at sign(λ2)ρ < 0 signify non-bonded (repulsive) interactions, whereas those with sign(λ2)ρ > 0 illustrate weakly bonded (dispersive) forces52. The IRI-scatter molecular graphs of CX[4]/solvent (polar protic, polar aprotic, and non-polar) complexes reflect tiny red dots that are primarily observed in the center of all four rings, indicate steric repulsion (Fig. 4, Fig. S3). The H-bond interaction in the region is indicated by the emergence of blue dots at the cyclic O–-H framework at the base. It can be seen that CX[4]/methanol (polar protic), CX[4]/DMSO (polar aprotic), and CX[4]/benzene (non-polar) systems have weak van der Waals (vdW) interactions (green spikes). The isosurfaces and graphs of the remaining CX[4]/solvents systems have demonstrated similar patterns (Fig. S3). The IRI study concludes that hydrogen bonding and vdW interactions between the guest and host are vital for the stability of the encapsulated structures. Quantum theory of atoms in molecules (QTAIM) analysis

The QTAIM analysis aids the representation of multiple interaction types, including vdW interactions and hydrogen bonding54,55. As IRI analysis indicated vdW and hydrogen bonding interactions are important in the studied CX[4]/organic solvent complexes, QTAIM analysis was also carried out to gain further insights into the properties of the systems.

The topological properties, such as ρ (electron density) and ∇2ρ (Laplacian of electron density), can be useful to analyze and characterize the nature of bond and interactions. The region of highest ρ (electron density) linking the atoms in a molecule is referred to as the BCP. The values for various BCPs are directly described by employing the following equations:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${H}_{r }={G}_{r }+{V}_{r}$$\end{document}4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left[\frac{1}{4}\right]{\nabla }^{2} {\rho }_{r}=2{G}_{r}+{V}_{r}$$\end{document}where the electron density of total energy, kinetic energy, and potential energy are represented by Hr, Vr, and Gr, respectively. Bader’s virial theorem posits that a covalent contact occurs when the electron density exceeds 0.1 a.u. and a ratio of − G(r)/V(r) is below 1. The values of ∇2ρ must be negative. When the ratio (− G(r)/V(r)) is more than 1 a.u. and the ∇2ρ has a positive value, it represents the vdW forces. Moreover, for BCP if the ∇2ρ is positive and Hr is negative it implies that there is a partial covalent character in the bonds. Similarly, if both ∇2ρ and Hr are negative this indicate the presence of covalent character or hydrogen bonding.

All calix[4]arene/solvent structures display BCPs (orange-hued bonds) linking the solvent atoms (polar protic, polar aprotic, and non-polar) to CX[4] (Fig. 5, Fig. S4). Also, the values of energy density are negative for these complexes. MEP analysis

The molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) has been computed using the same theoretical level. MEP is employed to demonstrate distribution of both negative and positive charge populations in organic systems57,58. The MEP maps of pristine and CX[4]/solvent complexes are demonstrated in Fig. 6 and Fig. S5. The generated maps illustrate the correlation between the supramolecular framework and the physicochemical properties of the CX[4]/solvent structures. It specifies that which atom/s in the CX[4]/solvent system exhibit greater electrophilic or nucleophilic characteristics. The color code of these maps varies from − 0.005 a.u and 0.005 a.u (isovalue = 0.001 a.u). It was observed that the CX[4]/solvent complexes are recognized by electrophilic sites mostly covered by carbon atoms and nucleophilic sites are covered by oxygen atoms of CX[4]. The phenolic rings display a mildly negative area owing to the considerable attraction of electrons in this context. This is due to the fact that heteroatom extracts π-electron density from the rings, therefore diminishing the negative-π-potential within the cavity. The MEP maps indicate the presence of a negatively charged region surrounding the oxygen atoms. The negative areas are typically linked to the lone pairs of the electronegative atoms. Furthermore, host–guest structures are distinguished by the presence of positive charges at the methyl groups. Conclusion

A systematic theoretical study of inclusion complexes of CX[4] with three types (polar protic, polar aprotic, and non-polar) and all in all, 12 organic solvents were successfully carried out by using a DFT approach. Three orientations of each CX[4]/solvent complexes were considered and compared and the most feasible were selected and used for further analysis. The geometry optimization for the intricate structures in the solvent phase was nearly analogous to those in the gas phase, with distinct numerical differences. The study of the thermodynamic calculations revealed negative values for Eint (− 34.57 kJ/mol to − 76.02 kJ/mol) and ΔH (− 34.87 kJ/mol to − 70.97 kJ/mol), indicating that the formation of CX[4]/solvent inclusion complexes is an exothermic and enthalpy-driven process in both phases. Depending on the environment, the negative Gibbs free energies for most complexes in the vacuum phase and the positive values in all systems in the solvent phase reflect the spontaneous and non-spontaneous attributes of the entire process, respectively. The density distribution derived from the FMO analysis demonstrated the interaction between CX[4] and the guest molecules. The kinetic stability is revealed through significant energy gaps (range between 4.46 eV to 3.25 eV) of these systems. The QTAIM and IRI analyses indicated that the primary factors contributing to complex formation were weak van der Waals interactions and hydrogen bonding. Furthermore, MEP analysis validated the electrophilic and nucleophilic regions of these complexes. The feasibility of calix[4]arene derivatives for application as solvent-responsive molecular modulators or sensors in molecular recognition is highlighted by the varied inclusion behavior observed with different solvents, depending on their polarity, size, and interacting potential. Furthermore, in supramolecular catalysis, the tendency of encapsulated substances to modify the regional polarity within the calixarene cavity may be important for regulating the speed of reactions. While this work did not directly model drug delivery, the knowledge of solvent–host interactions may influence the design and optimization of calixarene-based carriers in the loading and formulation processes as well.
