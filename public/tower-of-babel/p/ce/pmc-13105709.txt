TNFAIP6, an independent prognostic monitor and therapy target, promoted low-grade glioma recurrence and progression via JAK3-STAT6 signaling
Authors: Hui Liang, Qinhong Huang, Mingming Xian, Taoliang Chen, Xinlin Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Low-grade glioma (LGG, WHO2-3 grade), a kind of common primary tumor of the central nervous system, exhibits high 5-year survival rates but frequently recurs and progresses to higher-grade tumors, leading to poor prognosis. Effective prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets are urgently needed. Tumor necrosis factor alpha-induced proteins (TNFAIPs), key regulators within the inflammatory microenvironment, have been proven to play an important role in regulating tumor invasion and metastasis via immunoinflammatory suppression. Nevertheless, their functional mechanism and clinical significance in LGG remained unclear. Lacking studies focused on the direct effects of TNFAIPs themselves on regulating the malignant biological behaviors of LGG.Materials and methods:In this study, we employed bioinformatics analysis, machine learning algorithms, and in vitro cell experiments to investigate the potential effects of TNFAIP6 on LGG.Results:It found that TNFAIP6 can be a reliable prognostic marker for LGG, which directly regulates the malignant behaviors of LGG, including proliferation, invasion, and migration, thereby promoting LGG recurrence and progression. These processes were proven to be closely associated with the activation of the JAK3-STAT6 signaling pathway.Conclusion:Our findings demonstrated that TNFAIP6 can directly contribute to the malignant behaviors of LGG, providing a theoretical basis for developing targeted therapeutic strategies against TNFAIP6 and the JAK3-STAT6 signaling axis in LGG.

Introduction

Low-grade glioma (LGG), defined as World Health Organization (WHO) grade 2–3 gliomas, was relatively benign when compared with glioblastoma, exhibiting a relatively favorable prognosis[1–3]. Unfortunately, the vast majority of LGG patients inevitably experienced recurrence or and progression to higher grade gliomas, leading to poorer clinical outcomes[4–6]. Currently, the primary treatment for glioma involves a comprehensive standardized regimen of surgical resection combined with radiotherapy and chemotherapy[7], and the targeted therapies and immunotherapy are progressively demonstrating therapeutic benefits[8,9]. The tumor microenvironment (TME) encompassed components such as blood vessels, immune cells, fibroblasts, signaling molecules, and the extracellular matrix (ECM) surrounding the tumor[12], and was recognized as a critical regulator in tumorigenesis and progression, which significantly contributed to tumor growth, invasion, and treatment resistance[13–15]. Within this complex microenvironment, members of the Tumor Necrosis Factor Alpha-Induced Proteins (TNFAIPs) family, such as TNFAIP1, TNFAIP2, TNFAIP3, TNFAIP6, TNFAIP8, and others, were proven to be important constituents of the TME[16–18]. They participated in diverse biological processes, including immune responses, inflammatory responses, signal transduction, apoptosis, differentiation, and substance transport[19–22]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that TNFAIPs can indirectly participate in sustaining the renewal and invasion of various tumors (e.g., gastric cancer, colon cancer, and breast cancer) by modulating immunity and inflammation[23–26]. However, accumulating evidence suggested that TNFAIPs can also directly act on tumor cells through autocrine actions, regulating the malignant biological behaviors of tumors[22,27–30].

Based on the above, our study utilized bioinformatics analysis and in vitro experiments to explore the direct biological function of TNFAIPs on LGG (based upon TITAN criteria)[31]. Finally, among the TNFAIPs, TNFAIP6 was identified as an independent reliable prognostic biomarker and promising therapeutic target for LGG, whose pro-cancer effects were proved to be correlated with the activation of the JAK3-STAT6 signaling pathway. This study provided a crucial theoretical foundation for the future development of targeted interventional strategies aimed at blocking this specific signaling axis, providing a scientific basis for personalized medicine for LGG.

Materials and methods

This work has been reported in line with the TlTAN criteria[31].

Data acquirement

The RNA sequencing data and the matched clinical information of glioma patients were derived from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov) and the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA, http://www.cgga.org.cn/). Specifically, CGGA-1 consisted of LGG samples from the CGGA mRNAseq-693 dataset and CGGA-2 consisted of LGG samples from the CGGA mRNAseq-325 dataset. The RNA-sequencing data of normal brain tissues were downloaded from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx, www.gtexportal.org/).

The sequencing data were transformed into transcripts per kilobase million values and were log2-transformed (value + 1) before further analysis. To evaluate the expression level of TNFAIP6 across normal brain tissues, G2 glioma and G3 glioma, we merged the TCGA-LGG dataset and GTEx dataset into a whole set and used the function “ComBat” of the R package “sva” to remove the batch effect. We performed principal component analysis for sensitivity analysis after adjusting batch effect. CGGA-1 and CGGA-2 cohorts were independent of the TCGA-LGG cohort for validation, and no information from validation cohorts was used during training. Therefore, we did not adjust the batch effects between the CGGA cohorts and the TCGA cohort.

Acquisition of immunohistochemistry (IHC) images and information for LGG patients from the HPA database

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) images of TNFAIPs in normal brain tissue and LGG (stained under identical antibody conditions) were downloaded from The Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/).

Univariable cox proportional hazards regression analysis

Univariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was employed to evaluate the potential prognostic effect of TNFAIPs on LGG patients. The analysis results were presented as hazard ratios (HRs), 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs), and the corresponding P-values. A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Univariable Cox proportional hazards regression was performed using the “survival” package within R software (version 4.3.1).

Machine learning algorithms establish independent prognostic factors

More than 100 different models were established based on 10 excellent machine learning algorithms based upon the TlTAN criteria[31], including “Ridge, Lasso, generalized boosted modeling regression, survival support vector machine (survival-SVM), elastic network (Enet), stepwise Cox, CoxBoost, Partial least squares regression for Cox (plsRcox), random survival forest, and supervised principal component (SuperPC) methods. The TCGA dataset was used as the training cohort, and two independent CGGA datasets, including CGGA-1 and CGGA-2, were used as the verification cohorts. The construction processes of signature using the combinations of 10 machine learning algorithms were based on 10-fold cross-validation. Feature selection and tuning were fully enclosed in each fold. The workflow followed the methodology provided by the representative article[32]. Detailed information on R packages and scripts can be found at https://github.com/l-magnificence/Mime. No other Artificial Intelligence tools were used for analysis and text writing. The machine learning algorithm calculated the Harrell Consistency Index (C-index) for each cohort, and the model with the highest C-index was chosen as the best model.

Kaplan-Meier analysis and receiver operating characteristic curve construction

The LGG patients were stratified into high- and low-TNFAIP6 expression subgroups based on median expression level of TNFAIP6. Kaplan-Meier (K-M) analysis was conducted to evaluate differences in prognosis between subgroups using the “survival” R package, with statistical significance assessed by the log-rank test. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted to evaluate the efficacy of TNFAIP6 expression in predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates using the “timeROC” R package. Visualizations were generated using the “ggplot2” R package.

Clinical significance evaluation

The nomogram was established based on the TNFAIP6 expression and the known clinical prognostic variables, including WHO grade, IDH mutation status, 1p/19q co-deletion status, and MGMT methylation status, using the “rms” R package. The Nomogram was the visualization of a multivariate COX regression model. According to the contribution of each independent variable to the outcome, each value level of each independent variable was scored (points), and then each score was added to obtain the total score (total points). Finally, the predicted value of the patient’s risk of outcome event was calculated by the function conversion relationship between the total score and the probability of clinical outcome events. Calibration plots (1-, 3-, and 5-year) were conducted to ensure the accuracy of the nomogram using the R package “pec.” The decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed using R package “rmda v1.6.”

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) software (v3.0) was acquired from the GSEA portal (DOI: 10.1073/pnas. 0506580102). Patients were divided into high-/low-TNFAIP6 expression subgroups. The c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.symbols.gmt subset from the Molecular Signatures Database (DOI: 10.1093/bioinformatics/btr260) was used to interrogate relevant pathways. Analysis parameters included gene set size limits (min = 5, max = 5000), 1000 permutations, with significance thresholds of nominal P-value < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25, which identified TNFAIP6-associated biological functions and signaling pathways.

Cell culture and lentivirus infection

HA (human astrocytes), SVG-p12 (human astrocytes), SW1783, and SW1088 cells (LGG cells, WHO grade 3) were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, Cat# C11995500BT) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Procell, Cat# 164 210-50) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Yong Jin Biotech, Cat# YJX002) at 37°C under 5% CO2. Tumor specimens were collected from informed and consenting patient diagnosed with IDH mutation astrocytoma [World Health Organization (WHO) grade III]. The tissues were separated into several parts, which were subjected to RNA extraction and isolation of primary glioma cells, and were named DA-G3 according to the pathological diagnosis. All methods were performed in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Ethics Committee. The detailed procedure followed the previous studies’ description[33–35]. Cells were passaged with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (ABCbio, Cat# ABC4019-1) upon reaching 80%–90% confluency and routinely screened for mycoplasma contamination using a Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Macklin, Cat# O931116-25 T/EA). Lentiviruses (GeneChem, Shanghai, China) with the following sequences were used: sh#1 5’-CCCAAATGAGTACGAAGATAA-3’, sh#2 5’-GTGGCGTCTTTACAGATCCAA-3’, NC: 5’-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT-3’, and OE 5’-CCAACTTTGTGCCAACCGGTCGCCACCATGATCATCTTAATTTACTTATTTC-3’. After optimization per the manufacturer’s protocol, an MOI of 5 was selected for SW1088/SW1783 transduction. Fresh complete medium was replaced 24 hours post-transduction. Transduction efficiency was validated through puromycin selection for 3 weeks (2 μg/mL, 72 hours) and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). All procedures complied with Biosafety Level 2 (BSL-2) guidelines.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from glioma cell lines using AG RNAex Pro Reagent (Accurate Biology, China, Cat# AG21102). The SYBR Green SupTaq HS Premixed qPCR Kit (Accurate Biology, Cat# AG11762) was employed for gDNA removal, reverse transcription, and PCR amplification. Following reaction mixture preparation, amplification was performed on an ABI QuantStudio 1 Plus Real-Time PCR System. Primer sequences were as follows: TNFAIP6 F: 5’-TCTGGCAAATACAAGCTCACC-3’, R: 5’-CTGCCCTTAGCCATCCATCC-3’; GAPDH (internal control) F: 5’-GTCTCCTCTGACTTCAACAGCG-3’, and R: 5’-ACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCAA-3’.

Western blotting

Cell protein extraction was performed using Radioimmunoprecipitation Assay Buffer (Beyotime, Cat# P0013 K) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein lysates were centrifuged, denatured by boiling, separated by electrophoresis, and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (Beyotime, Cat# ST023-50 g) and sequentially incubated with primary antibodies (4°C overnight) and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (room temperature, 1 hour). Signal visualization was conducted using the ImageQuant LAS 500 system (GE Healthcare, USA). After the image was obtained, the background correction was performed using ImageJ software, the gray value of the band was measured, and the integrated optical density (IOD) value of each band was recorded. Each sample was repeated at least three independent experiments to ensure data repeatability. Then the IOD value of the target protein was divided by the IOD value of the internal reference protein to obtain the relative expression. Finally, the mean of the control group was calculated, and the relative expression of the control group and the experimental group was divided by the mean. The obtained data were used to draw the bar charts. The original imprint images of the three independent experiments were in the Supplemental Digital Content Material, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G991. Antibodies used included primary antibodies [rabbit-derived: TNFAIP6 (1:2000; Immunoway, Cat# YT4761), JAK3 (1:1000; Immunoway, Cat# YM8250), Phospho-JAK3 (1:1000; Immunoway, Cat# YP0756); STAT6 (1:2000; Immunoway, Cat# YM8432); Phospho-STAT6 (1:2000; Immunoway, Cat# YM8686)]; and α-Tubulin (1:15 000; mouse-derived; Proteintech, Cat# 60 004-1-Ig). Secondary Antibodies: HRP Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L; 1:15 000; Abclonal, Cat# AS014) and HRP Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L; 1:15 000; Abclonal, Cat# AS003).

Cell proliferation and clonogenic assays

For EdU Incorporation Assay, cell proliferation was evaluated using the EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (Beyotime, Cat# C0078S) per the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells (8000/well) were seeded in triplicate wells. After overnight culture, cells were stained following the kit instructions and imaged under a fluorescence microscope. For CCK-8 Proliferation Assay, on day 1, digested cells were resuspended as single-cell suspensions and plated at 2000 cells per well in 96-well plates (n = 5 replicates/group). After adherence, 100 μL complete medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent (APExBIO, Cat# K1018-5) was added per well. Following 2-hour incubation, absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy HT, USA). Measurements were repeated at 48-hour intervals for 7 days. For colony formation assay, cells (2000/well) were seeded in six-well plates and cultured for 14 days. Colonies were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with crystal violet. The above data were statistically analyzed using ImageJ software. Prism9 software was used for drawing statistical charts and statistical analysis of the data. ns, no significance, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Cell migration and invasion assay

Cell migratory and invasive capacities were evaluated using Transwell chambers (Corning, 8.0 μm pore, Cat# 353 097). For invasion assays, the upper chamber was pre-coated with 50 μL Matrigel (BD Biosciences, Cat# 354 234; diluted 1:8 in serum-free DMEM) and incubated at 37°C for 2 h. SW1088 and SW1783 cells were serum-starved for 24 h, harvested, and resuspended in serum-free medium. Cells (5 × 104 for migration; 1 × 105 for invasion) in 200 μL serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber, while 600 μL medium containing 10% FBS (chemoattractant) was added to the lower chamber. After 16–24 h incubation, non-migrated cells on the upper surface were removed with a cotton swab. Migrated/invaded cells on the lower membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted under a microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti2, 5 random fields per chamber). Data were expressed as mean ± SD and analyzed by Student’s t-test. ns, no significance, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Wound healing assay

Cell migration capacity was evaluated using an in vitro wound healing assay. Briefly, SW1088 and SW1783 were seeded in six-well plates (Corning, Cat# 3516) at 5 × 105 cells/well and cultured until 100% confluency. To eliminate proliferation effects, cells were pretreated with 10 μg/mL mitomycin C (Sigma, Cat# M4287) for 2 h. Uniform wounds (~1000 μm width) were created using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip, followed by three PBS washes. Cells were maintained in free-serum medium. Wound images at identical locations were captured at 0, 12, and 24 h using an inverted microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ts2, ×10). Wound areas were quantified with ImageJ software (v1.53) by threshold analysis. Migration rate was calculated as: Closure rate (%) = (A0 − At)/A0 × 100%, where A0 and At represent wound areas at initial and time t, respectively. Data were expressed as mean ± SD and analyzed by two-way ANOVA. ns, no significance, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Rescue experiments

We chose Ritlecitinib (MCE, cat#: HY-100 754), a selective JAK3 inhibitor, to perform rescue experiments. To determine the optimal concentration of JAK3 inhibitor, we set the concentration gradients of 0 μmol/l, 5 μmol/ml, 10 μmol/ml, 20 μmol/ml, 40 μmol/ml, 80 μmol/ml, 100 μmol/ml, and 200 μmol/ml according to the reagent instructions and relevant references. The dose-response curve was determined by the CCK-8 method, and the half inhibitory concentration (IC50) of SW1088 (OE-TNFAIP6), SW1783 (OE-TNFAIP6), and DA-G3 (OE-TNFAIP6) cells were 55.57 μmol/ml, 57.34 μmol/ml, and 46.26 μmol/ml, respectively. Rescue experiments were performed according to the grouping of Vector, OE-TANFAIP6 and OE-TNFAIP6 + JAK3 (−), and we replaced the normal medium with the medium containing JAK3 inhibitor in the OE-TNFAIP6 + JAK3 (−) group, and the remaining steps and treatment conditions were as previously.

Molecular docking analysis

The 3D protein structures of TNFAIP6, CD44, and JAK3 were downloaded from the RCSB database (https://www.rcsb.org/) in PDB format. Protein-protein docking analyses were conducted via the Hdock website (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/) and visualized via Pymol software. The docking binding energy was calculated and expressed in terms of the docking score (kcal/mol). In protein-protein docking analysis, a docking score less than −200 kcal/mol was considered to have a strong affinity between proteins. The docking score between −150 and −200 kcal/mol was considered to have moderate affinity between proteins. The docking score higher than −150 kcal/mol was considered to be poor affinity between proteins.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R, GraphPad Prism 9.0 (San Diego, CA) and SPSS 26.0 (IBM, NY). Normality and homogeneity of variance were confirmed by Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests. Between-group differences were analyzed by two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparisons test. Survival data were compared by the log-rank test and presented as K-M curves. Correlation was assessed via Pearson’s coefficient (*r*). Categorical variables underwent a χ2 test with Yates’ continuity correction. All experimental statistics and statistical results were provided in Supplemental Digital Content Table S1, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G990. Data were expressed as mean ± SD unless specified. Significance levels: ns, no significance, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Results

Expression profile characterization of TNFAIPs in LGGs

In order to systematically evaluate the potential biological role of the TNFAIPs in the progression of LGG, we conducted expression profiling using the TCGA and CGGA databases. Analysis revealed significant up-regulation of all eight kinds of TNFAIPs (TNFAIP1, TNFAIP2, TNFAIP3, TNFAIP6, TNFAIP8, TNFAIP8L1, TNFAIP8L2, and TNFAIP8L3) in LGG tissues compared to normal brain tissues, and their expression levels also progressively increased with tumor grade (Fig. 1b and Supplemental Digital Content Figure 1a, d, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G989). Notably, the expression levels of TNFAIPs in LGG with “MGMT unmethylated” and “1p19q non-codel” status were also significantly up-regulated (Fig. 1c,d and Supplemental Digital Content Figure 1b, c, e, f, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G989). Given that MGMT methylation and 1p19q codeletion were acknowledged clinical prognostic indicators in LGG, these results suggested that TNFAIPs may potentially impact the prognosis of LGG. The IHC results further proved the different expression patterns of TNFAIPs between normal brain tissues and LGG at the protein level (Fig. 1e-j). Identification and validation of TNFAIP6 as an independent prognostic factor

To further assess the prognostic effects of TNFAIPs on LGG, we conducted univariate COX proportional hazards regression analysis on the above eight kinds of TNFAIPs. The results suggested that, except for TNFAIP8L3, the rest of the TNFAIPs were hazardous factors for LGG patients (Fig. 2a). Then, these hazardous factors were included for prognostic model construction based on 10 machine learning algorithms. The model constructed by the “Ridge algorithm” demonstrated the highest mean C-index (0.7253) and was considered to be the ideal model, which only included TNFAIP6 (Fig. 2b). Based on the median expression level of TNFAIP6, LGG patients were divided into high- and low-expression subgroups in each cohort. K-M curve analysis showed that the prognosis of the high-expression subgroup was significantly poorer than that of the low-expression subgroup (Fig. 2c-e). The time-dependent ROC curve (1 year, 3 years, and 5 years) further highlighted the prognostic efficacy of TNFAIP6. The results of the TCGA dataset (AUC values were 0.84, 0.82, and 0.78, respectively), CGGA-1 dataset (AUC values were 0.63, 0.73, and 0.71, respectively), and CGGA-2 dataset (AUC values were 0.80, 0.75, and 0.75, respectively) all supported the excellent performance of TNFAIP6 as an independent prognostic factor (Fig. 2f-h). To verify the reliability of TNFAIP6 as the only prognostic indicator, we further performed K-M and time-dependent ROC analyses on the other TNFAIPs, and the results supported that TNFAIP6 had the best prognostic effect among all members (Supplemental Digital Content Figure 2a-f, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G989). Clinical importance of TNFAIP6

To investigate the clinical value of TNFAIP6 in-depth, we evaluated its prognostic effect at the pan-cancer level, which showed TNFAIP6 significantly affected the overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival of various cancers, especially in LGG (Fig. 3a,b). Then, we constructed a nomogram via multivariable COX regression analysis to display the impact of TNFAIP6 and the existing clinical biomarkers on the survival of LGG. The length of the corresponding line segment of each variable in the nomogram represented the strength of the influence of each variable on the outcome. Obviously, compared to the WHO grade, IDH mutation status, 1p/19q co-deletion status, and MGMT methylation status, TNFAIP6 displayed the strongest influence on the OS of LGG patients (Fig. 3c). Meanwhile, the calibration plot indicated that the nomogram could accurately predict the real 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities (Fig. 3d). DCA suggested that the predictive strategy based on the expression level of TNFAIP6 can achieve higher net benefit when compared to the known clinical prognostic variables (Fig. 3e). Moreover, we examined the survival stratification ability of TNFAIP6 in different molecular subtypes. The result showed that the higher expression level of TNFAIP6 was associated with the poorer prognosis no matter of which LGG subtypes, exhibiting higher prognostic value than the existing clinical biomarkers (Fig. 3f-h). Up-regulation of TNFAIP6 promoted LGG progression

Considering the above results emphasized that TNFAIP6 was an important hazardous factor for LGG, which can promote its development, we performed in vitro experiments to explore the biological functions of TNFAIP6 in LGG.

TNFAIP6 contributed to LGG proliferation

To explore whether the expression of TNFAIP6 has a direct impact on LGG, we successfully constructed robust TNFAIP6 over-expression cell lines (OE-TNFAIP6) in SW1088, SW1783, and DA-G3. qRT-PCR (Fig. 4a-d) and western blot (WB) experiments (Fig. 4e-g) were used to verify the over-expression efficiency, which was repeatedly verified. CCK-8 cell proliferation experiment, EdU incorporation assay, and colony formation assay were used to investigate the effects of OE-TNFAIP6 on LGG proliferation. CCK-8 results exhibited that the proliferation ability of the three OE-TNFAIP6 cell lines was significantly enhanced. On day 5 of culture, the absorbance value (OD 450) of SW1088 cells in the OE-TNFAIP6 group was significantly up-regulated when compared with the Vector group, and similar effects were also observed in SW1783 cells and DA-G3 (Fig. 4i-k). Edu incorporation assay further proved the positive effect of TNFAIP6 on cell proliferation. In the OE-TNFAIP6 groups, the proportion of EdU-positive cells was much higher than that in the Vector group, indicating TNFAIP6 contributed to DNA replication (Fig. 4l-n). The colony-forming assay was used to evaluate the long-term proliferation and survival ability of the cells, whose results displayed that the number of clones formed by OE-TNFAIP6 LGG cell groups were significantly increased (Fig. 4o-q). The above results emphasized that up-regulating TNFAIP6 can promote the proliferation of LGG and, therefore, promote LGG progression. TNFAIP6 promoted the migration and invasion of LGG

Wound healing assay was conducted to evaluate the effect of TNFAIP6 on tumor migration, and Transwell assay was conducted to evaluate the effect of TNFAIP6 on both the migration (without Matrigel)/invasion (with Matrigel) of LGG cell lines. Wound healing assay exhibited that the up-regulation of TNFAIP6 enhanced the migration ability of LGG cells. To be specific, the wound healing rate of OE-TNFAIP6 LGG cell groups were about 15% higher than that in the vector group after 24 hours (Fig. 5d-f). Similar results were observed in the Transwell assay. In the OE-TNFAIP6 groups, the migration and invasion ability of LGG cells were significantly enhanced, and the number of cells migrating and invading was significantly higher than that in the vector group (Fig. 5g-i). These phenotypic experiments fully demonstrated that TNFAIP6 played an important role in promoting the migration and invasion of LGG cells. Combined with the experimental results of cell proliferation evaluation, we believed that the up-regulation of TNFAIP6 promoted LGG progression and recurrence by enhancing the proliferation, migration, and invasion of LGG, which directly affected the prognosis of patients. The inhibition of TNFAIP6 significantly limited the malignant biological behaviors of LGG

To explore whether the inhibition of TNFAIP6 limits the malignant behaviors of LGG and evaluate the potential of treatment translation, we established a stable TNFAIP6 knockdown model (shTNFAIP6) in SW1088, SW1783, and DA-G3 (Fig. 6a-c). CCK-8 cell proliferation experiment (Fig. 6d-f), EdU incorporation assay (Fig. 6g-i), and plate cloning experiment (Fig. 6j-l) consistently displayed that the knockdown of TNFIP6 significantly inhibited the proliferation of LGG cells. As for cell migration and invasion, the wound healing test and Transwell assay were conducted. Wound healing assay displayed that the wound healing rate was about 20% lower than that of the control group in shTNFAIP6 groups (Fig. 7a-c), while the Transwell migration/invasion assays displayed that the numbers of cells passing through the chamber were significantly lower than that of the control group in shTNFAIP6 groups (Fig. 7d-f). These results indicated that TNFAP6 could be a promising therapy target of LGG, whose inhibition can directly limit the malignant biological behaviors of LGG. TNFAIP6 enhanced LGG malignant phenotypes via the JAK3-STAT6 signaling pathway

In order to clarify the mechanism of TNFAIP6 enhancing LGG malignant phenotypes, we divided the samples of TCGA, CGGA-1, and CGGA-2 datasets into high- and low-TNFAIP6 expression subgroups according to the median expression of TNFAIP6. GESA analysis was conducted and found that pathways, such as apoptosis, focal adhesion, ECM-receptor interaction, and actin cytoskeleton regulation, were significantly enriched in the high expression subgroup (Fig. 8a-c), which were consistent with the previous experimental results. Notably, the JAK-STAT signaling pathway was highly significantly enriched in the high-TNFAIP6 expression subgroup in each cohort, and the core molecules in JAK-STAT pathways, including JAK3, STAT1, STAT2, STAT3, and STAT6, were also up-regulated in all cohorts (Fig. 8d, Supplemental Digital Content Figure 3a,c, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G989). Co-expression analysis further revealed a significantly positive correlation between the expressions of JAK3 and STAT6 (Fig. 8e, Supplemental Digital Content Figure 3b,d, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G989). These results strongly suggested that TNFAIP6 may enhance LGG malignant phenotypes via the JAK3-STAT6 signaling pathway. The biological effects of OE-TNFAIP6 can be suppressed by a JAK3 inhibitor

After confirming that the expression of TNFAIP6 was closely related to the activation of the JAK6-STAT6 signaling pathway, we used Ritlecitinib, a selective JAK3 inhibitor, in the OE-TNFAIP6 LGG cell lines, hoping to suppress the effect of OE-TNFAIP6 on the biological behavior of LGG cells. The results of CCK-8 proliferation assay, EdU incorporation assay, and colony formation assay demonstrated that the JAK3 inhibitor could partially reverse the enhancement of cell proliferation caused by OE-TNFAIP6 (Fig. 9a-i, Supplemental Digital Content Figure 4a,b, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G989). Similarly, the results of the wound healing assay and Transwell assay showed that the migration and invasion abilities of SW1008, SW1783, and DA-G3 were also significantly inhibited (Fig. 9g-l, Supplemental Digital Content Figure 4c,d, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G989). Furthermore, in order to clarify the changes in downstream signaling pathways caused by the use of the JAK3 inhibitor, we performed WB and found that the phosphorylation levels of JAK3 and STAT6 were significantly down-regulated after the use of the JAK3 inhibitor (Fig. 9m-o). CD44 was a potential intermediary of the TNFAIP6-JAK3-STAT6 signaling pathway

Currently, the interaction between TNFAIP6 and CD44 has been widely reported in a variety of cancers; therefore, we conducted an exploratory study. First, we performed molecular docking analysis between TNFAIP6 and CD44 to simulate the binding of the two proteins. The result indicated that the two proteins could bind tightly with a docking score = −257 kcal/mol (Supplemental Digital Content Figure 5a, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G989). Then, we performed molecular docking analysis between CD44 and JAK3, and the result indicated that the two proteins also had the potential to bind closely, with a docking score = −278 kcal/mol (Supplemental Digital Content Figure 5b, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G989). However, a lack of research reported that CD44 acted as an upstream molecule in JAK3-STAT6 signaling. Therefore, subsequent studies on exploring the specific role of CD44 in the TNFAIP6-JAK3-STAT6 signaling were in need. In summary, combined with the existing evidence and molecular docking results, we speculated that CD44 was a potential intermediary of the TNFAIP6-JAK3-STAT6 signaling pathway.

Discussion

TNFAIPs were initially identified as tumor necrosis factor-α- and lipopolysaccharide-induced genes in endothelial cells and are known to participate in diverse biological processes, including immune and inflammatory responses[36,37]. Accumulating evidence indicates that TNFAIPs exert immunomodulatory functions and indirectly influence tumor progression. However, the prognostic value of TNFAIPs in LGG and whether TNFAIPs were directly involved in the regulation of LGG malignant biological behavior have not been fully elucidated.

This study uncovered that the expressions of TNFAIPs were significantly increased in LGG and were closely related to the clinical prognostic indicators, such as tumor grade, MGMT methylation status, and 1p/19q co-deletion status, indicating that TNFAIPs may serve as potential prognostic biomarkers in LGG. Machine learning results demonstrated that TNFAIP6 can be used as an independent prognostic indicator for LGG, with good accuracy and robustness in OS prediction and patient stratification. High TNFAIP6 expression was strongly associated with poor clinical outcomes. Moreover, nomogram, DCA, and risk stratification consistently indicated that TNFAIP6 possesses superior prognostic value compared to existing clinical biomarkers.

Among the TNFAIP family members, TNFAIP6 exhibited the strongest correlation with inflammatory response and has been reported to exhibit potent anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive properties[38,39]. In both glioblastoma and lung adenocarcinoma, the secretion of TNFAIP6 from tumor cells was found to induce tumor-associated macrophage polarization, facilitating tumor progression[40,41]. Moreover, the up-regulation of TNFAIP6 can also directly promote tumor malignancy. In hepatocellular carcinoma cells, TNFAIP6 was found to enhance aerobic glycolysis via up-regulating the c-myc/PKM2 axis[42]. In ovarian cancer and gastric cancer, the up-regulation of TNFAIP6 directly promoted the proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of tumor cells[43]. Therefore, TNFAIP6 could be a key oncogene, which not only modulated the immune microenvironment in tumor but also directly promoted the tumor malignancy. However, lacking enough studies illustrated the biological functions of TNFAIP6 in LGG. Through in vitro experiments, our study uncovered that the up-regulation of TNFAIP6 significantly enhanced the proliferation, migration, and invasion of LGG cells, highlighting the direct involvement of TNFAIP6 in LGG malignancy. Meanwhile, these malignant behaviors can be suppressed by inhibiting TNFAIP6, suggesting that TNFAIP6 could be a promising novel therapeutic target for LGG.

In order to explore the possible mechanism of TNFAIP6 directly regulating LGG biological behaviors, we combined bioinformatics analysis and in vitro experiments and found that the up-regulation of TNFAIP6 was closely correlated with the activation of JAK3-STAT6 signaling. Especially, WB experiments exhibited that the up-regulation of TNFAIP6 was accompanied by the increase in JAK3 and STAT6 phosphorylation levels while the down-regulation displayed the opposite results, suggesting that TNFAIP6 regulated LGG via activating JAK3-STAT6 signaling. Previous studies have proved that, as an important ECM protein, TNFAIP6 can bind to cell surface receptors and initiate intracellular signal transduction[44,45], while JAK3 was mainly coupled to cytokine receptors, whose activation led to the phosphorylation of downstream STAT members, such as STAT6[46,47]. Similar to TNFAIP6, the activation of STAT6 was found to not only modulate the TME but also directly facilitate tumor progression. In breast cancer, the activation of STAT6 signaling increased M2-polarized macrophages, leading to radioresistance[48]. In pancreatic cancer cells, the STAT6 signaling directly drove pancreatic cancer cell proliferation and migration via enhancing glycolysis and activating downstream SOX21-AS1 transcriptional activation[49,50].

Apparently, similar biological functions in cancers between TNFAIP6 up-regulation and JAK3-STAT6 activation can be found. However, few studies link TNFAIP6 to JAK3-STAT6 activation. It is worth noticing that studies have indicated that ECM components can regulate the JAK-STAT pathways through receptor-mediated signals, supporting the potential of TNFAIP6 in regulating JAK3-STAT6 signaling[51–53], supporting the plausibility of TNFAIP6-mediated JAK3-STAT6 activation. Our rescue experiments also proved that the selective inhibition of JAK3, on the one hand, reversed the LGG malignancy caused by OE-TNFAIP6; on the other hand, down-regulated the phosphorylation level of STAT6. Combined with the above discussion, we believed that TNFAIP6 can directly promote LGG progression via activating JAK3-STAT6 signaling.

Last but not least, studies have revealed that CD44 was an important receptor of TNFAIP6, and their interactions promoted various cancers, such as inducing myeloid-derived suppressor cell formation in pancreatic cancer, reprogramming tumor-associated fibroblasts in colorectal cancer and triggering epithelial-mesenchymal transition in lung adenocarcinoma[16,28,41]. Therefore, to investigate whether CD44 was involved in the regulation of the TNFAIP6-JAK3-STAT6 signaling pathway in LGG, we performed exploratory analyses. Molecular docking results demonstrated that CD44 has the potential to bind tightly to both TNFAIP6 and JAK3. This suggests that CD44 may serve as a molecular bridge linking TNFAIP6 to JAK3-STAT6 activation. However, lacking research has reported the association between CD44 and the JAK3-STAT6 pathway. Considering that the linkage between TNFAIP6 and JAK3-STAT6 signaling pathway in LGG was reported for the first time in our study, and this study aimed to identify TNFAIP6 as a reliable independent prognostic factor and therapeutic target for LGG, whether and how CD44 was involved in the TNFAIP6-JAK3-STAT6 signaling axis warrants further investigation in subsequent studies.

Limitation

Our study has identified that TNFAIP6 was a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target via activating the JAK3-STAT6 pathway in LGG through bioinformatics analysis and in vitro experiments. More in vivo studies and large-scale sample cohorts were necessary to enhance the clinical translation value. In addition, more basic studies need to confirm the specific role of CD44 within the TNFAIP6-JAK3-STAT6 signaling axis, as well as its precise mechanism of interaction with TNFAIP6 and JAK3.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study identified TNFAIP6 as a reliable independent prognostic factor for LGG and uncovered that TNFAIP6 can directly increase the malignant degree of LGG via regulating JAK3-STAT6 signaling. These findings emphasized the potential value of TNFAIP6 as a prognostic and recurrent monitoring indicator for LGG patients and provided a promising biomarker for molecular targeted therapy of LGG.
