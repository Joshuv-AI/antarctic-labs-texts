Advances of extracellular vesicles isolation and detection frontier technology: from heterogeneity analysis to clinical application
Authors: Bei Li, Wei Wu, Wenrong Xu, Hui Qian, Cheng Ji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtracellular vesicles (EVs) play a crucial role in intercellular communication, participating in various physiological and pathological processes, and showing great application potential in disease diagnosis, therapy and prognosis. Nevertheless, current EVs isolation and detection technologies are confronted with numerous challenges, such as the dilemma of balancing isolation efficiency and purity, the complex and time-consuming operation process, and the limited detection sensitivity. This paper reviews the current research status of EVs isolation and detection methods, analyzes the advantages and limitations of traditional techniques like ultracentrifugation, ultrafiltration and polymer precipitation, and explores the development prospects of emerging technologies such as microfluidic technology and single-vesicle detection. The article emphasizes the impact of EVs heterogeneity on isolation and detection, and explains the potential of emerging technologies in overcoming the defects of traditional methods. In addition, it delves into the clinical translation research progress of EVs in fields like oncology and tissue repair, points out key barriers such as the lack of technical standardization and insufficient clinical validation, and proposes future research directions to promote the basic research and clinical translation of EVs. Overall, this study aims to provide theoretical and technological innovation support for EVs-related research and clinical applications.Graphical abstract Keywords: Extracellular vesicles, Heterogeneity, Isolation, Detection, Clinical application

Introduction

EVs are lipid bilayer nanoparticles released by cells and widely present in multiple body fluids and cell culture supernatants [1]. EVs are classified into three main types based on their biogenesis and physical properties: exosomes (30–150 nm), microvesicles (100–1000 nm), and apoptotic bodies (500–2000 nm) [2]. Initially considered merely as byproducts of cell metabolism, EVs have been recognized as key players in intercellular communication due to technological advancements [3]. Their biological significance is closely linked to their high heterogeneity, which is reflected not only in particle size but also in cellular origin, molecular composition, and functional diversity [4, 5]. EVs serve as endogenous information carriers, capable of shuttling bioactive molecules, including proteins and nucleic acids, and are subsequently internalized by recipient cells through mechanisms such as membrane fusion, phagocytosis, or endocytosis (Fig. 1). They play a pivotal role in various physiological and pathological processes, including immune regulation, tissue repair, and tumor metastasis [6]. Recent advances have positioned EVs as a focal point in life sciences research, with accumulating evidence underscoring their translational potential in clinical applications such as disease diagnosis, therapy, and prognosis [7].

EVs classification, biogenesis, and uptake. Cellular EVs are primarily categorized into exosomes and ectosomes based on their biogenesis and size. Exosomes, originating from the endosomal system, represent a subpopulation of EVs. In contrast, ectosomes are generated through plasma membrane budding and include microvesicles, microparticles, and larger vesicles. EVs are characterized by a phospholipid bilayer that encapsulates a variety of cellular components, such as DNA, RNA, lipids, metabolites, and proteins. They can be internalized by recipient cells via mechanisms including membrane fusion, phagocytosis, pinocytosis, or clathrin-mediated endocytosis

Traditional EVs isolation methods include ultracentrifugation, ultrafiltration, and polymer precipitation, while detection relies on techniques like Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA) and Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) [8]. These methods have laid the groundwork for EVs research but are increasingly showing limitations [9]. They struggle to balance isolation efficiency and purity, are time- and labor- intensive, may damage EVs integrity, and offer limited detection sensitivity and resolution [10]. Moreover, they cannot comprehensively analyze EVs heterogeneity or meet single cell and subtype-specific study needs, hindering in-depth exploration of EVs in intercellular communication, disease development, and personalized diagnosis and treatment [11]. To overcome these technical bottlenecks, it is crucial to develop more efficient and accurate EVs isolation and detection technologies. In recent years, emerging technologies such as microfluidics and single vesicle detection techniques have appeared [12]. These new technologies have the advantages of high throughput, high sensitivity and high resolution. It is hoped that these technologies can overcome the shortcomings of traditional methods, achieve efficient, precise and rapid EVs separation and detection, and deeply analyze their heterogeneity [13]. Therefore, these new technologies have opened up new ways for in-depth exploration of the biological functions, disease mechanisms and clinical application prospects of EVs. However, they are still in the stage of continuous development and improvement, and further optimization is needed to meet the needs of research and clinical applications [14].

Significant progress has also been made in the preclinical translation of EVs. In oncology, EVs show great potential as biomarkers for the early detection and accurate identification of various cancer types [1]. Furthermore, the use of EVs as drug delivery platforms and gene therapy tools has garnered considerable attention, underscoring the imperative to discriminate, characterize, and isolate functionally distinct EVs subpopulations [15]. In terms of tissue repair, EVs have demonstrated positive effects in the repair of the nervous system, skin and wounds, respiratory system, and musculoskeletal system [16]. Despite these advances, the clinical translation of EVs still faces numerous challenges, such as the lack of technical standardization, bottlenecks in large-scale production, and insufficient verification of long-term therapeutic effects [17].

Although recent reviews have systematically summarized advances in EVs isolation and detection for heterogeneity analysis [18–21], the present review uniquely integrates heterogeneity dissection with clinical translation. By systematically surveying high-quality studies published between 2018 and 2025, we provide a comprehensive summary of recent advances in EVs isolation and characterization, analyze the advantages and disadvantages of existing technologies, explore the challenges posed by EVs heterogeneity to isolation and detection, and outline the application value and development prospects of emerging technologies. Overcoming-and strategically exploiting-EVs heterogeneity constitutes the pivotal link between technological innovation and clinical translation. Additionally, it delves into the progress of clinical translation research on EVs in fields like oncology and tissue repair, points out key barriers such as the absence of technical standardization and inadequate clinical validation, and proposes future research directions to promote EV-related basic research and clinical translation.

Biological significance of EVs heterogeneity

As critical intercellular communication mediators, EVs regulate various physiological and pathological processes by transferring bioactive molecules [22]. However, EVs are not a uniform group. Their diverse origins, components, and functions constitute complex heterogeneity, determining their specific roles in intercellular information transfer [23]. Exploring EVs heterogeneity and its biological significance is vital for fully understanding EVs physiological functions and tapping their potential as disease biomarkers and therapeutic tools (Fig. 2).

The heterogeneity of extracellular vesicles. EVs are secreted by diverse cell types and plants and are ubiquitously present in a wide range of body fluids. They form a highly heterogeneous group of membrane structures whose morphology and types vary with their origin. EVs carry complex components, including proteins, nucleic acids and lipids. The specific components are influenced by the cell type and its physiological or pathological state. EVs play significant roles in multiple physiological and pathological processes. These include cell-to-cell communication, drug delivery, disease progression regulation, tissue repair, immune regulation, and serving as potential disease diagnosis markers

Diversity of biogenesis pathways

EVs are diverse and their biogenesis mechanisms contribute significantly to this diversity. They are mainly categorized into exosomes, microvesicles, and apoptotic bodies, which differ in formation, size, and molecular cargo [1]. Exosomes derive from endocytosis: invagination of the cell membrane forms early endosomes, which mature into multivesicular bodies and fuse with the plasma membrane to release exosomes of about 30–150 nm in diameter [6]. They are rich in transmembrane signaling molecules (e.g., CD9, CD63) and nucleic acids (e.g., mRNA, miRNA), serving as key intercellular communication carriers [24].

Microvesicles are directly budded from the cell membrane, ranging in size from 50 to 1000 nm [23]. Their formation is influenced by cellular stress or activation, and they carry characteristic components of the parent cell membrane. Their lipid composition resembles the plasma membrane but with specific modifications, such as enriched sphingomyelin, suggesting a role in acute environmental responses [25]. Apoptotic bodies result from apoptosis, containing organelle debris and nuclear fragments, with a diameter of 50–5000 nm [26]. The exposed phosphatidylserine (PS) on their surface marks them for recognition and clearance by phagocytes, and they are primarily involved in processing cell debris and immune silencing [27].

The heterogeneity of these three EV types in biogenesis pathways is closely related to their functions: exosomes are responsible for precise signaling regulation, microvesicles for rapid stress responses, and apoptotic bodies for cell debris processing. This division and coordination provide a structural basis for studying the complex roles of EVs in physiological and pathological processes.

Origins of extracellular vesicles heterogeneity

Diversity of cellular origins

EVs released by different cells demonstrate distinct functional specificity. Within the tumor microenvironment, malignant cells, immune cells, and stromal cells establish a complex signaling network through EVs secretion, collectively modulating disease progression [28, 29]. In this context, EVs can propagate both pro-tumor and anti-tumor signals, with their precise effects contingent on the characteristics of the originating and recipient cells [30]. For example, EVs released by tumor cells may carry immune-suppressive molecules such as PD-L1, thereby promoting tumor immune evasion by dampening immune cell activity [31, 32]. Colorectal cancer cells can secrete specific EVs-miRNAs to upregulate PD-L1 expression in PD-L1+ CD206+ tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), thus facilitating tumor immune evasion and progression [33]. EVs derived from immune cells (including macrophages, T cells, B cells, and dendritic cells) also regulate the expression and function of immune checkpoint proteins [34]. These EVs can transport immune checkpoint proteins like CTLA-4, thereby influencing the function of other immune cells [31, 35]. Moreover, EVs from NK cells display specific markers such as CD56 and can induce target cell apoptosis by delivering molecules such as FasL [36]. Notably, exosomes released by both tumor and immune cells exhibit dual roles in tumor progression. While exosomes can suppress cancer cell growth, they have not yet achieved complete tumor eradication, and the exact reasons and underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated [37]. Additionally, EVs released by cancer associated fibroblasts regulate tumor cell metabolism, treatment resistance, and metastatic potential by transferring non-coding RNAs and proteins [38, 39]. For instance, in pancreatic cancer, these EVs enhance tumor chemoresistance by inhibiting ferroptosis pathways [40].

EVs derived from stem cells provide therapeutic potential comparable to parental stem cells, yet circumvent the ethical and safety concerns associated with cell transplantation [41]. Research indicates that EVs from diverse stem cell sources exhibit marked functional differences. Among these, mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs (MSC-EVs) are distinguished by their high stability, low immunogenicity, and targeted tissue penetration, rendering them highly promising for clinical applications [42, 43]. For instance, proteomic analyses have revealed that EVs from hucMSCs demonstrate superior tissue repair efficacy relative to those from bone marrow- or adipose- derived MSCs [44]. In a diabetic kidney disease model, MSC-EVs effectively mitigated the fibrotic process by modulating the stability of key signaling proteins, with the mechanism involving the targeted delivery of specific kinase systems within the vesicles [45]. Regarding complications associated with metabolic diseases, engineered MSC-EVs, enriched with particular microRNAs, can precisely regulate the hypoxia-inducible factor pathway, thereby ameliorating retinal damage induced by hyperglycemia [46]. Of special note is the unique advantage of iPSC-EVs in the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases. These EVs retain the capacity for neuroregeneration while eliminating the tumorigenic risks linked to stem cell transplantation. Animal studies have confirmed that such vesicles can significantly improve pathological features and behavioral deficits in models of Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease [47].

Effects of cell state

The characteristics of EVs secreted by cells are closely associated with the physiological or pathological state of the cell. Even cells of the same type can exhibit markedly altered EVs secretion patterns and biological properties due to variations in cell cycle phase, stress state, or differentiation degree [48]. For instance, under hypoxic conditions, EVs derived from breast cancer cells display elevated levels of angiogenesis related microRNAs, thereby enhancing endothelial cell tubule formation and promoting angiogenesis [49]. Similarly, oxidative stress induces Drosophila S2R+ cells to release an increased number of EVs with specifically altered RNA compositions, such as enriched small nucleolar RNAs and select mRNAs, indicating a potential role of EVs in regulating cellular adaptability [50]. Additionally, metabolic stresses, such as nutrient deprivation or fluctuations in calcium concentration, can modulate EVs secretion levels, size distribution, and molecular cargo, thereby reshaping their intercellular communication functions [48].

Notably, the physical microenvironment of cells directly modulates EVs secretion. For example, cells cultured on high-stiffness matrices release EVs enriched with Jagged1 protein, activating the Notch pathway and accelerating tumor progression [51]. In pathological states, the functional heterogeneity of EVs becomes more pronounced. For instance, in EVs from HIV infected individuals, the Nef protein, though only accounting for 0.39% of the total secreted amount, effectively downregulates the cholesterol transporter ABCA1, thereby disrupting host metabolic homeostasis [52]. In kidney injury models, stress induced EVs exacerbate inflammatory responses and fibrosis by mediating long range cellular communication, highlighting their regulatory potential in disease [6].

Recent studies indicate that the differentiation lineage of MSCs significantly impacts the characteristics of the EVs they secrete. Specifically, MSC-EVs differentiated through a neural crest or trophoblast intermediate stage show notable differences in size, concentration, and in protein and cytokine composition [53]. This subtype specific heterogeneity reveals a close link between EVs properties and the developmental trajectory of parent cells [53]. Overall, these findings suggest that cell status influences EVs biological features across multiple dimensions, offering key insights into EVs’ dynamic role in disease progression.

Body fluid specificity

The biophysical and biochemical properties of EVs vary significantly depending on their source. EVs derived from blood and its derivatives, such as plasma and serum, are critical for hemostasis and immune responses. Their surface protein complexes are essential for mediating transport functions [54]. Under pathological conditions, blood derived EVs are closely linked to cardiovascular diseases and cancer progression through mechanisms such as inflammation and complement system activation, making them potential biomarkers for disease monitoring [55]. Urine derived EVs (uEVs) specifically reflect urinary system function. Abnormal expression of certain protein and nucleic acid markers in uEVs can signal early kidney damage or urinary tract tumors [56]. Cerebrospinal fluid derived EVs (CSF-EVs) are vital for central nervous system disease research. They carry neurospecific molecules like amyloid-beta oligomers [57], offering key clues for early diagnosis of neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s [58].

Milk derived EVs (mEVs) exhibit excellent environmental resistance [59]. They are rich in immunomodulatory molecules like lactoferrin and high abundance CD81 protein, which gives them advantages in gut disease treatment [60]. Studies indicate that mEVs can alleviate colitis by regulating inflammatory signaling pathways and restoring gut microbiota balance [61]. Saliva derived EVs are ideal for non-invasive diagnosis due to their easy collection. Changes in the abundance of EVs subpopulations in saliva and gingival crevicular fluid reflect oral microenvironment disorders, providing objective indicators for early periodontitis diagnosis [62]. In summary, EVs from different body fluids create a “molecular atlas” that reflects the body’s status. As technology for analyzing source specific EVs advances, their applications in precision medicine are continuously expanding, offering diverse research perspectives and clinical potential.

The species heterogeneity of extracellular vesicles

Bacterial-derived EVs (bEVs) have been proven to participate in the pathological processes of various diseases, such as pulmonary fibrosis, sepsis, systemic bone loss, and Alzheimer’s disease [63, 64]. For instance, outer membrane vesicles released by Gram-negative bacteria (like Escherichia coli) carrying lipopolysaccharide components can bind to Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) on the host cell surface, triggering a series of immune responses [65]. In CKD models, the abnormal increase in bEVs caused by intestinal microbiota dysbiosis can penetrate the intestinal barrier and induce cell inflammation and tubulointerstitial damage by activating the caspase-11 pathway [66]. Notably, certain bEVs exhibit therapeutic potential: melanoma research shows that the combined use of bEVs and tumor-derived EVs can enhance immunotherapy effects, and their low cost and high safety features make them suitable for individualized cancer treatment, especially increasing the proportion of patients responsive to checkpoint inhibitors [67].

Plant derived EVs (PDEVs) are widely sourced and can be isolated from various plants using diverse methods. They are highly safe and sustainable and are becoming a research hotspot as natural nanocarriers and therapeutic agents [68]. Studies indicate that multiple independent groups have demonstrated that PDEVs can mediate cross-kingdom biological information transfer using biochemical techniques [69]. For example, Arabidopsis thaliana EVs can deliver mRNA to the fungal pathogen Botrytis cinerea and inhibit its infection ability [70]. Preclinical studies have confirmed that the efficacy and certain safety of plant EVs can support the clinical application of PDEVs in cancer therapy [71]. Furthermore, PDEVs can efficiently load hydrophilic/hydrophobic drugs, resist gastrointestinal degradation, penetrate biological barriers, and their synergistic effects with drugs significantly enhance antitumor efficacy [72]. PDEVs can also be developed into novel bio-derived drug carriers, offering additional benefits compared to current DDS [73]. This natural carrier system provides an ideal platform for developing low toxicity and highly efficient nano-drugs.

Heterogeneity of extracellular vesicles in biological functions

Diverse roles in cell-to-cell communication

EVs, with their distinct molecular cargo and delivery mechanisms, play a crucial role in cell-to-cell communication. They can regulate various physiological processes and participate in pathological progression [74]. Their mechanisms of action in signal activation, gene regulation, and pathological intervention are as follows: EVs can carry ligands or receptors of transmembrane signaling proteins. These interact with surface molecules on target cells. This, in turn, regulates physiological activities like cell proliferation, differentiation, migration, and immune responses [75]. For instance, breast cancer cells secrete EVs carrying IGF-1R, which activates the ERK signaling pathway. This induces EMT, thereby promoting tumor metastasis [76].

EVs serve as natural carriers for non-coding RNAs, including microRNAs and circular RNAs. They can remodel the function of target cells through epigenetic regulation. During skeletal muscle injury repair, myofibroblasts express EVs with high levels of miR-142a-5p. This inhibits Nmrk2 gene expression, thus promoting muscle fiber regeneration [77]. MSC-EVs, through adhesion mediated by VLA-4 and LFA-1, utilize the miR-125b-5p/p53 pathway. This pathway targets the regulation of renal tubular cell cycles, alleviating acute kidney injury and promoting repair [78].

In pathological states, the communication function of EVs shows significant heterogeneity. In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), HNSCC cells secrete EVs carrying TGF-β. This protein induces the transformation of macrophages into a pro-angiogenic phenotype, thereby promoting immune evasion [79]. This functional diversity stems from the dynamic response of EVs subpopulations to the state of parent cells. Gaining an in-depth understanding of the dual role of EVs is of great significance for developing precise EVs based intervention strategies.

Diverse roles in disease development

EVs play multi-dimensional roles in disease development and have extended their application to diagnostics, therapeutics, and drug delivery. As biomarkers, EVs carried molecules like proteins and nucleic acids can precisely reflect disease characteristics [80]. For example, detecting tumor related mutated mRNAs (e.g., KRAS [81], BRAFV600 [82]) and specific proteins can aid cancer diagnosis. Exosome Diagnostics has developed EVs-based tests to detect mutations in prostate, lung cancer, and cancer associated EGFR/MAPK and PI3K [83]. EVs, obtainable from biofluids, have advanced cancer liquid biopsies [84]. Biomarker EVs, with high sensitivity, stability, and multi-marker detection ability, offer a new paradigm for early diagnosis [85].

In therapeutics, EVs have a dual role. Their natural delivery capability can be modified into targeted carriers [86]. Through surface ligand engineering, EVs can precisely deliver gene editing tools (e.g., Cre recombinase, CRISPR complexes) to specific tissues, offering a new strategy for individualized treatment [87]. This approach improves therapeutic outcomes for particular patient groups or disease subtypes. Moreover, EVs themselves can be therapeutic targets. Regulating their pathological molecular cargo (e.g., tumor promoting miRNAs or immune suppressive proteins) can influence disease progression [88, 89].

As drug carriers, EVs offer advantages over artificial delivery systems [90]. Their natural low immunogenicity prolongs drug half-life, their membrane structure enhances drug stability, and their inherent tissue-tropism enables precise delivery, meeting complex therapeutic needs [91, 92]. For instance, engineered EVs can improve targeting and therapeutic effects while reducing side effects. MSC-EVs, modified with magnetic nanoparticles, can target fibrotic kidneys, boosting local anti-fibrotic drug concentrations [93]. This “smart delivery” feature, along with multi-component co-loading ability, makes EVs an ideal platform for combination therapy in complex diseases [94]. In summary, the functional heterogeneity of EVs in diseases provides a starting point for mechanistic research. More importantly, through an integrated “diagnosis-targeting-therapy” strategy, EVs drive the advancement of precision medicine into clinical practice.

In summary, EVs display pronounced heterogeneity. While this intrinsic diversity underpins their broad biological functions and translational potential, it also imposes formidable challenges on isolation and purification. The multidimensional variability within EVs populations precludes a universal separation strategy applicable to every research context. Consequently, a deeper understanding of EVs heterogeneity has become the primary driver for the continuous development of innovative isolation technologies.

Extracellular vesicle isolation techniques: from classical to innovative

The efficient isolation of EVs is essential for understanding their functions and unlocking their clinical potential. Different isolation methods are grounded in distinct physicochemical principles-size, density, surface charge, antigenic profile, etc. Thereby intentionally capturing distinct dimensions of EVs heterogeneity [95]. It is extremely challenging to identify a single isolation method that can effectively and accurately separate all types of EVs from complex biological samples; hence, the choice of method must be dictated by the specific dimension of heterogeneity the investigator intends to interrogate. Thus, the selection of an appropriate method for isolating EVs should be tailored to the specific objectives and requirements of the research to ensure the chosen method aligns with the scientific demands of the study.

Classic isolation methods

The choice of isolation method is, in essence, a deliberate prioritization of which dimension of EVs heterogeneity to foreground. Classical techniques, despite their limited resolving power, laid the methodological foundation by exploiting fundamental biophysical disparities between EVs and contaminants-and, to a lesser extent, among EVs subtypes themselves (Fig. 3).

Ultracentrifugation (UC)

UC is the most extensively used EVs isolation technique and is one of the most commonly used and historically foundational methods. It separates particles based on their differential sedimentation rates under centrifugal force [9]. Given the density (1.10–1.19 g/mL) and size differences between EVs, lipoproteins (< 1.10 g/mL), and apoptotic bodies (>200 nm), gradients [12] (often made with sucrose or synthetic media) can be used to remove contaminants like cell debris and soluble proteins through differential centrifugation, ultimately enriching EVs [96]. The final EVs pellet can be resuspended in PBS or a storage buffer (e.g., Tris-HCl with protease inhibitors) and stored at −80 °C [97].

Despite not requiring labeling agents and being applicable to various samples [98], UC has significant drawbacks. UC isolates a mixed population defined solely by density and size, rendering it incapable of discriminating EVs subtypes that share identical biophysical properties but originate from distinct cells or possess divergent functions [10]. High-intensity centrifugation can damage vesicle structures or cause aggregation. Moreover, UC is inefficient. The standard procedure takes over six hours, with EVs recovery below 50% [13]. Its low recovery and propensity to induce aggregation further distort the authentic representation of native EVs heterogeneity [99]. While DGC improves purity, it extends the process to over 18 h and drops recovery to under 30%, limiting UC in clinical high throughput testing. However, due to its maturity, UC remains a top choice for proteomic analysis in basic research [100].

Ultrafiltration (UF)

UF is a rapid and direct EVs isolation technique, ideal for concentrating EVs pre-isolated by other methods. It leverages the size differences between EVs and other biomolecules (e.g., soluble proteins, lipoproteins) [101]. UF membranes have pore sizes of 10–300 kDa (around 3–100 nm), selected based on target EVs size [102]. UF membranes with smaller pores preferentially enrich 30–150 nm exosomes, whereas larger-pore membranes are better suited for microvesicles. EVs capture is typically performed via dead-end filtration [103](operationally simple but prone to clogging) or tangential-flow filtration (TFF) [87](scalable for large volumes with reduced membrane fouling). If required, size-exclusion chromatography can be used for further purification [104].

This method offers significant advantages: it is time-efficient (30 min to 2 h), has low equipment costs, and is scalable, making it suitable for preliminary EVs enrichment in clinical samples [105]. Its low-shear-force characteristic helps preserve EVs structural integrity, and TFF shows potential in industrial-scale preparation. Moreover, it cannot resolve EVs subpopulations of similar size but distinct biogenesis or cargo, and nonspecific membrane adsorption selectively depletes certain subsets, skewing downstream heterogeneity analyses. In high-viscosity matrices such as plasma, the membrane is prone to clogging and must be replaced frequently [106]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed several optimization strategies. For example, combining CME with UF enhances EVs purity to 3.15 times that of UC [107]. Another approach involves integrating phospholipid affinity chromatography with UF to efficiently obtain high-purity EVs from urine for metabolomics research [108]. Additionally, the co-existence of nanoparticles similar in size to exosomes is another limitation of UF [109]. Combining two or more separation methods can overcome this problem [110]. For instance, combining TFF with size-exclusion chromatography (TFF-SEC) enables large-scale separation and high-purity preparation simultaneously, offering a reliable solution for standardized production of functional EVs [111]. Furthermore, using super-water-absorbent resin (SAP) to concentrate samples reduces the volume for UF processing, indirectly optimizing the efficiency of UF steps. SAP quickly concentrates EVs by absorbing water, with a recovery rate comparable to traditional UF, but at a lower cost and without the need for equipment [112].

Method of precipitation

Precipitation-based methods are often used to isolate EVs from complex biological samples, including cell-culture supernatants and organoids, facilitating exploration of their proteomic landscape [113]. Being one of the simplest and most widely used EVs isolation techniques due to simple operation and low cost, it is especially suitable for rapid processing of clinical samples [114]. The method precipitates EVs from solution by altering their solubility or hydrating layer [115]. EVs are induced to aggregate via charge neutralization or hydrophobic interactions [116]. Based on this, several commercial kits (e.g., ExoQuick™ and Total Exosome Isolation Kit) enrich EVs in 30 min to 4 h via optimized polymer formulas, making them particularly suitable for low-concentration samples like urine and cerebrospinal fluid.

The advantages of this method are its low equipment requirements, high recovery rate, and high-throughput processing, making it a practical tool for initial EVs screening and large-sample studies [117]. However, co-precipitated contaminants like lipoproteins and protein aggregates can reduce EVs purity, and residual polymers may interfere with downstream molecular assays [116]. Commercial kits are expensive and not ideal for large samples, and they cannot effectively separate different EV types [118]. Issues with batch-to-batch consistency and high costs limit large-scale applications, and precipitation-based methods may not be the best choice for truly descriptive or functional EVs analysis [119]. To address these limitations, researchers have optimized PEG precipitation by adjusting PEG concentration, molecular weight, and incubation time. This reduced non-exosomal protein co-precipitation, enhancing exosome purity and yield [120]. In another study, optimized PEG precipitation for exosome isolation from MSC culture medium achieved a recovery rate of about 61.74%, comparable to ExoQuick and higher than UC’s 45.80% [121]. Additionally, using Ti₃C₂ and Ti₂C materials to interact with EVs for co-precipitation has shown high separation efficiency and purity, effectively removing impurities while maintaining EVs integrity and activity [122].

Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC)

SEC separates bio-particles based on differences in hydrodynamic volume. Largely preserving EVs’ native structure and bioactivity, SEC has become a key EVs isolation method [123]. It works via the sieving effect of porous media [124]. This effectively separates EVs (30–1000 nm) from common contaminants like albumin and lipoproteins [125]. Typically, exosomes (30–150 nm) are in the early elution fractions (5–8 mL), with microvesicles and contaminants eluting later [126]. When implementing SEC, column selection and buffer optimization are critical [127].

SEC is a non-destructive EVs isolation technique that doesn’t damage EVs via mechanical shearing or chemical modification, suitable for downstream functional assays to interrogate functional heterogeneity [128]. It removes over 90% of soluble proteins like albumin, boosting EVs purity 5–10 times compared to UC [129]. SEC handles sample volumes of 1–100 mL, making it suitable for fine-purifying complex samples like plasma. However, it has some drawbacks. Large elution volumes risk diluting low-abundance yet functionally critical subpopulations [11]. The media is prone to lipoprotein clogging, and its limited resolution makes it hard to distinguish between EVs subpopulations of 30–50 nm and 50–100 nm [130]. Still, SEC is suggested for quickly and easily obtaining omics-grade urinary-derived EVs in clinical settings, though residual urinary protein pollution needs further optimization [131]. Researchers have proposed several improvements. For example, combining the top layer of Sepharose CL-6B with cation-exchange fillers and the bottom layer of multimodal chromatography filler Capto Core 700 reduces co-elution of lipoproteins and albumin, enhancing EVs purity [132]. Using a Superose 6 Increase 5/150 column with an FPLC system can separate EVs from 50 µL of plasma within 15 min. The proteomics analysis of repeated samples has a correlation coefficient of over 0.9, meeting the needs of large-scale clinical cohort studies [133]. A new device combining hydrophilic porous silica gel (PSG) with a flash liquid chromatography system equipped with a UV detector and fraction collector enables rapid EVs separation [134]. Additionally, researchers have transformed SEC into a plate-based method and combined it with custom adapters to automate SEC operations on liquid-handling platforms [135]. This optimizes EVs separation with high reproducibility.

Immunoaffinity

Immunoaffinity isolation, a promising EV isolation technique, leverages the specific antigen-antibody interaction to achieve high-purity EVs enrichment by targeting surface markers [136, 137]. The process typically involves: (1) covalently linking specific antibodies (e.g., anti-CD63) to solid supports such as magnetic beads or microbeads; (2) incubating the sample to capture target EVs via the antibodies, followed by washing to remove impurities; and (3) eluting the captured EVs using pH or ionic strength adjustments, or competitive peptides.

This method is celebrated for its high specificity, enabling the direct enrichment of particular EVs subpopulations from complex samples, and is applicable for functional studies and molecular diagnostics [138]. Yet its limitations also stem from heterogeneity itself: the high cost of antibodies, the restriction of capture efficiency by antigen expression heterogeneity [139], and the susceptibility of EVs bioactivity to pH and salt concentrations [100]. The elution process may compromise EVs functionality by disrupting surface membrane proteins [140]. To tackle these issues, researchers have developed the GlyExo-Capture platform. It uses affinity-capture magnetic beads to specifically bind particular exosome glycans, completing glycosylated EVs extraction in just 11 min and enhancing miRNA detection sensitivity and specificity [141]. In another study, researchers immobilized Tim4 on the surface of Fe₃O₄@SiO₂-ILI-01 magnetic nanomaterials to capture PS on the EVs membrane surface. Results indicated that this method achieved an EVs recovery rate of over 90%, with a purity 7.5 times higher than that of the UC method [142].

Anion exchange chromatography (AEC)

AEC separates EVs based on charge-interaction. It uses the binding of negatively charged EVs groups (e.g., sialic acid, phosphate groups) to positively charged ligands in the column. In EVs separation, AEC shows unique advantages in large-scale preparation and subpopulation sorting [143]. In AEC, quaternary-ammonium-based anion-exchange resins capture EVs via electrostatic interaction, allowing neutral or positively charged particles (such as some lipoproteins) to pass through [144]. Resin selection tailored to EVs heterogeneity is therefore pivotal.Strong-anion-exchange resins (e.g., Q Sepharose FF) are good for large sample processing. Weak-anion-exchange resins (e.g., DEAE Sepharose) can reduce EVs damage. High-capacity resins (e.g., Capto Q) are suitable for viscous samples (e.g., cerebrospinal fluid).

AEC is highly valued in the EVs purification field due to its cost-effectiveness, ease of operation, high efficiency, and scalability. It introduces the critical dimension of charge-based separation in addition to particle size-based methods [145], meeting the needs of clinical laboratories for processing large-volume EVs-rich culture media [146]. In large-scale preparation, it can process over 100 mL of sample per run, with a recovery efficiency exceeding 90%, and effectively separates lipoproteins, meeting clinical-grade production standards [147]. Through gentle electrostatic interactions, it preserves EVs bioactivity and significantly reduces protein contamination compared to UC. Studies have confirmed that AEC can efficiently and massively isolate high-purity EXOs and MVs, revealing differences in protein composition, bioactivity, and charge properties, thus providing a solid foundation for subsequent research and clinical applications [148]. However, it has limitations. The desalting step after high-salt elution may introduce residual risks [148]. Its resolution is limited, making it hard to distinguish EVs subpopulations with similar charges [145]. Some vesicle subtypes show changes in size distribution and purity during elution, indicating the need for multi-parameter analysis to ensure separation purity. Combining high-performance anion-exchange chromatography (HPAEC) with TFF can enhance bEVs purity [144, 149]. Also, cation-exchange chromatography is applicable in specific scenarios, such as separating platelet-derived EVs, due to the presence of single cationic domains on EVs surfaces [150].

Asymmetric flow field-flow fractionation (AF4)

AF4 is a label-free, hydrodynamic technique that resolves EVs by size, enabling high-resolution sorting from complex biological samples and direct visualization of EVs population heterogeneity [154]. Its core principle involves the synergistic action of two mobile phases: a vertical cross-flow and a horizontal channel flow [155]. AF4 has unique advantages in separating nanoscale biological particles and has been successfully applied in the characterization of viruses, protein complexes, and synthetic nanoparticles, a powerful tool for characterizing EV heterogeneity in complex specimens [156]. Its main features are: (1) high-resolution separation capability to distinguish vesicle subpopulations with size differences as small as 10 nm; (2) label-free property avoiding interference from antibodies or foreign polymers, thus preserving the natural state of samples [157]; and (3) good compatibility with complex biological samples, usually requiring only minimal pretreatment for analysis. However, AF4 has limitations such as high instrument costs, limited single-batch processing volume (typically < 500 µL), and complex operational parameters. Systematic optimization of key conditions like cross-flow gradients and membrane material selection is needed [158].

To address the technical challenges of AF4, researchers have implemented several improvements. Optimizing cross-flow gradients, focusing time, and sample pretreatment conditions can significantly reduce lipoprotein co-isolation in plasma samples while enhancing vesicle integrity and experimental reproducibility [159]. One study combined AF4 with DGC to improve EVs purity and reduce non-EV interference [160]. Another integrated UF-SEC with AF4 and UV-MALS detection, establishing an efficient workflow for urine vesicle purification and characterization that provides size distribution and concentration data [161]. Additionally, coupling AF4 with large-volume sample stacking and capillary electrophoresis (LVSS/CE) enables single-step separation of small serum proteins and non-vesicular matrix components, yielding high-purity EVs suitable for downstream protein analysis [162].

Microfluidics

Microfluidics integrates multimodal separation principles into a single device to perform multiparametric sorting that confronts the complexity of EVs heterogeneity. Their high-speed, high-purity operation enables rapid isolation of specific subpopulations from minute clinical specimens, markedly accelerating heterogeneity-based biomarker discovery. For example, the EXODUS system uses pressure fluctuations and dual-frequency vibrations for automated, label-free exosome purification from biological samples, outperforming traditional methods in separation speed, product purity, and recovery rate [151]. Another team’s magnetic bead-based droplet microfluidic platform, leveraging immunaffinity, captures vesicles efficiently, achieving 2.5 times higher capture efficiency in shorter incubation times, with a 2 mL sample capacity and over 400 µL/h processing throughput [163]. A microchip-based platform enables personalized nanodrug delivery by capturing, enriching, loading, and eluting patient-derived tumor EVs (PT-EVs), completing the entire process within 12 h at a 65% recovery rate, surpassing ultracentrifugation [164]. Utilizing the flow-path-to-circuit analogy design strategy, researchers developed a microfluidic pulsatile filtration system. By cascading a 600-nm pore size filter membrane with a 20-nm pore size filter membrane, EVs can be efficiently and completely separated and purified from whole blood samples within 30 min, with a recovery rate as high as 80% [165].

To meet clinical sample requirements, several optimization solutions have been proposed. The FLOAT technique, grounded in acoustofluidics, efficiently isolates EVs within minutes, achieving over 90% recovery. Its ability to distinguish uEVs from high-concentration Tamm-Horsfall protein is crucial for surmounting hurdles in EVs-based liquid biopsy development [166]. The ExoCAS-2 system uses poly-cation-modified magnetic beads to obtain high-purity vesicles from plasma within 40 min, showing good reproducibility and scalability [167]. The ExoCPR platform combines bubble-driven mixing and surface-tension filtration for automated vesicle separation and gentle recovery in just 30 min [168]. Additionally, a micro-nano fluidic device combining size screening and antibody-based affinity capture has specifically enriched and analyzed liposarcoma-derived vesicles [169]. Based on a viscoelastic microfluidic platform, EVs can be isolated from human blood in a label-free manner. The results show that the purity and recovery rate exceed 97% and 87%, respectively. Notably, compared with the gold standard UC, this method also provides a significant increase in EVs yield [170].

For standardization and large-scale applications, the novel EVics system combines TFF and light scattering detection for simultaneous vesicle separation and quantification. Supporting sample volumes from 15 µL to 2 L, it offers superior separation efficiency and purity for 50-to 200-nm vesicles compared to existing technologies [171]. These technological breakthroughs not only enhance separation performance but also provide a foundation for high-throughput clinical sample analysis and precision medicine research.

Magnetic bead separation technology

Magnetic bead-based separation captures target vesicles via specific molecular recognition and uses a magnetic field for efficient isolation. It is highly selective, easy to perform, and rapid, making it ideal for precise sorting of specific vesicle subtypes. It empowers researchers to isolate specific subpopulations tailored to the biological question at hand. However, challenges like residual magnetic beads and high costs remain, necessitating stringent quality control in subsequent analyses.

In recent years, a series of innovative magnetic bead-based separation platforms have emerged. For instance, the MESS2CAN strategy uses novel lipid probes and streptavidin-modified magnetic beads. It achieves a 49.6% EVs recovery rate within 1 h and, combined with Cas12a-PER dual signal amplification, can detect target proteins at a sensitivity of 10 EV particles per microliter [172]. MagExo technology enhances separation efficiency by optimizing adsorption conditions between magnetic microparticles and vesicles, achieving double the efficiency of traditional ultracentrifugation while maintaining high purity [173]. Additionally, a PS-MIPs and NELISA-based Surface-Enhanced Raman Scattering (SERS) detection platform has been developed. This MIPs-NELISA-SERS technology enables non-invasive, high sensitivity, and rapid detection of target substances [152]. Another study developed BiMBs with Ti (IV) and DSPE modification. Their synergistic action improves affinity for EVs surface phospholipids and phosphorylated proteins. This results in over 80% recovery within 1 h, and the CD9 signal detected is 2.7 times stronger than that from UC [174]. Chitosan oligosaccharide lactate conjugated 1-pyrenecarboxylic acid (COL-Py) based self-assembled magnetic nanoclusters (CMNCs) can target the lipid bilayer of EVs, enabling efficient EVs separation under a magnetic field. Analyses show that EVs extracted via this method have a purity superior to that achieved by UC, with good structural integrity [175].

Lipid-nanoprobe-based separation technology

Lipid-nanoprobe-based separation tech captures and releases EVs efficiently by mimicking natural membrane interactions. It uses lipid-molecule binding with vesicle membranes, combined with photo- or enzyme- induced cleavage, to simplify workflows while keeping EVs structurally intact. Its key benefits include a short separation time (< 4 h), high-purity output, and no need for ultracentrifugation equipment, thus offering a feasible option for clinical translation.

Innovative applications have emerged in lipid-nanoprobe-based EVs separation. Photo-lipid probes can release near-native EVs in an hour via light-triggered cleavage, improving separation efficiency and purity [153]. The EV-FISHER platform optimizes this approach with cleavable lipid probes (PSDC) and conventional centrifugation, reducing separation time to 40–240 min. It outperforms traditional ultracentrifugation in isolation efficiency (74.2%−18.1%) and requirements (12800–135000 g) [176]. The MESSAGE strategy meets clinical diagnostic needs via a novel approach combining lectin-mediated EVs aggregation and smart-algorithm analysis. It can classify early-stage cancer and monitor metastasis using only 20 µL of plasma within two hours, showing high sensitivity and specificity [177].

Novel composite technologies are driving the field toward multifunctionality. A strategy integrating proximity ligation assays and DNA-RNA hybridization precisely identifies specific EVs subpopulations with dual-membrane-protein markers. By capturing EVs with magnetic beads and releasing them via enzymatic digestion, this method achieves high-purity EVs separation. It supports high-throughput sequencing and offers a new biomarker-screening platform for breast cancer diagnosis, showing great potential [178].

Solid-phase medium separation technology

Separation tech based on functionalized solid-phase media enables efficient EVs capture and specific recognition by tailoring material surface properties. Using chemical modification or biomolecule-coupling strategies, it allows directional binding between solid-phase materials and specific vesicle-surface components (e.g., lipids, proteins, or glycans). This approach offers high selectivity and operational flexibility, providing new ideas for separating and analyzing EVs in complex biological samples.

Typical technologies involve the development of lipid-anchored materials. Take DSPE-functionalized agarose solid-phase medium (DSPE-Beads) for example. By modifying the surface of the solid-phase medium with hydrophobic molecules, it can directly insert into the lipid bilayer of vesicles to capture them rapidly [179]. This method boasts high separation efficiency, compatibility with subsequent proteomic analysis, fine stability, and extensive target coverage. To further enhance it, researchers designed porous materials (SOM-ZIF-8-DSPE) that match the size of vesicles. This increases capture capacity and mass transfer efficiency while reducing structural interference [180]. Considering the complexity of clinical samples, new phase-transition separation technologies have emerged. By utilizing phase-separated zwitterionic (ZW) aggregates, this method can stably work in body fluids with different pH and ionic strengths. Its anti-fouling properties combined with affinity probe modification enable high-purity enrichment and damage-free release of vesicles in urine samples [181]. Additionally, DNA-nanostructure-based intelligent recognition platforms show unique advantages. By adjusting the 3D structure and antibody labeling strategy, this technology can screen vesicle subpopulations with specific sizes and surface markers. It offers a high-precision tool for studying heterogeneity and developing disease biomarkers [182].

Membrane curvature-sensing peptides

The membrane of EVs exhibits pronounced geometric curvature; the outer leaflet is enriched in anionic phospholipids and a high proportion of unsaturated lipids, generating characteristic lipid-packing defects [183, 184]. Within the framework of EVs population heterogeneity, this topological signature establishes a structural basis for curvature-sensing peptides; via curvature-dependent interactions, these peptides act as integrated ligands to enable high-efficiency EVs capture and downstream analysis [185]. Curvature-recognition mechanisms not only expand the theoretical arsenal for EVs sorting but also enable precise isolation at the subpopulation level: peptides that preferentially bind highly curved membrane domains can selectively enrich EVs subtypes with pronounced curvature differences [186]. Compared with conventional protein ligands, curvature-sensing peptides offer superior chemical stability, providing a robust foundation for their deeper integration into EVs isolation workflows [186]. For instance, a membrane-sensing peptide (MSP) integrated into a single-molecule array (SiMoA) platform acts as a universal affinity probe, eliminating the selectivity bias inherent to antibody-based methods and enabling label-free, high-sensitivity profiling of all small EVs irrespective of their protein markers [187]. In another study, the curvature-sensing peptide NIC4 exploits pH-tunable electrostatic-hydrophobic cooperativity to achieve high-efficiency, high-purity capture and release of EVs, facilitating rapid exosome isolation and in situ single-particle visualization without reliance on canonical exosomal markers [188]. ApoC-TRC₁₂, a curvature-targeted far-red probe created by conjugating the curvature-sensing amphipathic helix ApoC to the hydrophobic fluorophore TR-C₁₂, enables centrifugation-free, antibody-free “mix-and-read” detection of EVs irrespective of their protein-marker profile [189].

Performance comparison of major EVs isolation techniques

In recent years, EVs separation tech has evolved from traditional physical methods towards higher specificity, throughput, and clinical applicability. While conventional methods like UC and SEC are well-established, they struggle to balance purity and efficiency [8, 190]. In comparison, emerging technologies show significant advantages: the microfluidic platform EXODUS achieves label-free exosome purification from plasma within 30 min, with a purity 1.6 times higher than UC [151]. Magnetic nanomaterials based on Tim4 protein (Fe3O4@SiO2-ILI-01), targeting phosphatidylserine (PS), increase recovery to over 90% and support non-invasive monitoring of PD-L1-positive exosomes [142].

Technology integration has become key to overcoming sample-processing bottlenecks. When selecting technology combinations, it’s essential to consider sample characteristics and research goals. In the isolation of uEVs, traditional UC is easily affected by high-abundance urinary protein UMOD. Researchers combined 0.22 μm membrane filtration with UC (F + UC) and a TFE digestion system. This F + UC approach physically blocks UMOD formed fiber networks, achieving over 95% removal, while TFE digestion improves transmembrane protein detection by about 40% compared to traditional urea treatment [191]. For EVs isolation from solid tissues, conventional methods often lead to cell debris contamination. A team addressed this by combining mild enzymatic digestion with differential centrifugation and iodixanol DGC. This reduced intracellular marker contamination and revealed the unique protein distribution of tissue-derived small EVs [192]. To meet large-scale production needs, researchers proposed using TFF for EVs concentration and buffer exchange, followed by SEC with Cytiva™ superSEC fillers. This reduced purification cycle time from 150 to 45 min while meeting GMP standards [13]. To boost EVs yield, researchers have engineered chemical enhancers-such as NA-TPP or NA-photosensitizer conjugates-that, in a single co-culture step, suppress bacterial peptidoglycan synthesis and trigger outer-membrane protrusion, increasing outer-membrane vesicle (OMV) production by 7–65-fold [193]. The approach is operationally simple, obviates genetic manipulation, and simultaneously enables functional drug loading across diverse Gram-negative species.These innovative integrated solutions, combining physical separation, chemical treatment, and chromatographic purification, enhance EVs purity, recovery, and functional research reliability, providing robust experimental support for EVs-related studies.

Future development directions for EVs separation technology primarily focus on three aspects. First, there is an urgent need to establish standardized protocols as current EVs isolation platforms show significant differences in EVs size distribution and marker expression [151, 194]. Second, scalable production faces many challenges. Although TFF and microfluidic technologies have increased throughput to some extent, the high shear stress generated by TFF may damage EVs, while microfluidic devices are expensive and complicated to operate. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop scalable hybrid processes (e.g., TFF-SEC) to meet the growing clinical demands. Third, function-oriented separation technologies need further improvement. By targeting specific markers of EVs subpopulations, it is possible to design more targeted isolation strategies. As technology continues to advance, the application prospects of EVs separation technologies in disease diagnosis, prognosis evaluation, and therapy are becoming increasingly broad. Given this, it is essential to introduce next-generation methods to replace current EVs purification technologies and ensure a significant increase in EVs yield [119]. The development of faster, more sensitive, and specific EVs isolation technologies is of great significance for promoting the in-depth development of EVs research and will also provide more robust support for clinical applications [195].

Current state of extracellular vesicle detection technologies

Current studies indicate that most published EVs samples are affected by heterogeneous subpopulations and possible non-vesicular contaminants, challenging the reliability of downstream omics analyses [196]. Thus, establishing a quality evaluation system with key parameters (particle size, concentration, surface charge, and purity) is now a crucial prerequisite for EVs research [197].

Biophysical property detection of extracellular vesicles

Methods for detecting the physical properties of EVs can determine their key physical characteristics, such as size, shape, and surface charge (Fig. 5). Physical characterization provides the first layer of insight into heterogeneity by revealing differences in size, morphology, and mechanical properties—features that closely mirror vesicle biogenesis pathways and cellular origin [198].

Physical Characterization of Extracellular Vesicles. The physical characteristics of EVs, including size, shape, and mechanical properties, are crucial for understanding their biological functions. These can be analyzed using techniques such as TEM, SEM, Cryo-EM, AFM, DLS, NTA and TRPS, which together provide a comprehensive toolkit for studying EVs’ physical properties

Microscopy

In the field of EVs morphology characterization, Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), Scanning electron microscopy (SEM), cryo-electron microscopy (Cryo-EM), and atomic force microscopy (AFM) each have unique features. Together, they form a comprehensive technical system for studying EVs ultrastructure.

TEM is the most extensively used tool for assessing EVs sample purity verification and morphological preliminary screening, as it distinguishes individual EV from non-EV particles of similar size [199]. As a classic technique, TEM uses a high-energy electron beam to penetrate samples, resolving intricate structures down to 0.1 nm [200]. Its advantages lie in the relatively simplified operation process. When combined with negative staining, it can clearly present the typical cup-shaped morphology [12]. Moreover, specific labeling techniques, such as immunogold labeling, can accurately locate surface markers, thereby enhancing detection specificity [200, 201]. However, chemical fixation in traditional sample preparation may cause vesicle structural collapse, limiting the interpretation of the native state.

SEM creates images via surface electron signals, offering a 3D view of vesicle morphology with a resolution of 3–10 nm [202]. This makes it particularly useful for analyzing surface texture and distribution patterns [203]. Unlike the 2D projections of TEM, SEM provides more intuitive spatial imaging, allowing for detailed observation of EVs distribution and aggregation over larger areas [204]. However, sample preparation for SEM involves complex steps like dehydration and metal coating [205], which may introduce artifacts or damage vesicle integrity.

Recently, Cryo-EM has advanced rapidly, visualizing a wide range of EVs with different sizes, morphologies, lipid bilayers, and internal structures [206]. Unlike traditional TEM sample preparation that collapses EVs structures into cup-shaped forms, Cryo-EM preserves membrane shapes via cryogenic techniques [207]. Rapid freezing maintains EVs in their native hydrated state, avoiding damage from chemical fixation and staining. It also enables single particle analysis for determining 3D membrane protein structures at 0.3 nm resolution [208]. Moreover, improved overall resolution reaches atomic or near-atomic levels, confirming lipid bilayer existence and offering new insights into EVs biogenesis and function [209]. The emerging DNA affinity lattice technology overcomes limitations of traditional holey-carbon cryo-EM grids, such as low vesicle heterogeneity and concentration. By using DNA-based affinity lattices on porous carbon cryo-EM grids, it enables efficient enrichment and separation of low-concentration vesicles [208]. Despite its great potential in structural biology, Cryo-EM’s application is currently limited by high equipment and sample purity requirements.

AFM is a potent multifunctional tool that non-destructively characterizes the structure and mechanics of biological systems in aqueous conditions with unmatched spatiotemporal resolution [210]. Using nano-probe scanning, it can obtain 3D morphology of vesicles with a vertical resolution of 0.1 nm, measure EVs’ real-height, and acquire mechanical properties via force spectroscopy [211]. These features provide dynamic parameters for studying vesicle-cell interactions [212]. However, AFM imaging is slow, especially for large-area samples, and is more suitable for small-size samples. The imaging efficiency and quality may be affected for larger EVs samples.

Each microscopy technique has limitations: TEM and SEM sample preparation can introduce artifacts; Cryo-EM requires high-purity samples and advanced equipment; AFM is limited by detection efficiency. Multi-technique integration is an optimization strategy. For example, combining TEM and AFM can obtain morphological and mechanical data simultaneously, while combining Cryo-EM and SEM can comprehensively analyze the internal and external structures of vesicles. With the development of techniques like Cryo-EM tomography and high-speed AFM, it is expected that the dynamic relationship between vesicle structure and function will be revealed at higher spatiotemporal resolution in the future.

Advances in super-resolution microscopy (SRM) now allow high-resolution imaging of EVs by circumventing the diffraction limit through single-molecule localization or structured illumination. These techniques provide nanometric details of EV morphology, size, surface-marker distribution, and molecular composition, enabling precise discrimination of EVs subtypes [213]. Using STORM, researchers resolved the ring-like architecture of CD81 on individual EVs and performed in situ, multiparametric profiling of single-cell-secreted vesicles-simultaneously quantifying diameter, HLA-DR intensity, and secretion frequency-thereby uncovering striking inter-subtype differences in EVs output among macrophage populations [214]. This work underscores the unique capacity of SRM to dissect single-cell EV heterogeneity and functional phenotypes.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS)

DLS, a non-destructive analytical technique based on light scattering, measures nanoparticle sizes by detecting Brownian motion in solution. It’s widely used for assessing EVs size distribution and dispersion homogeneity and provides data on other biophysical properties [215, 216], such as polydispersity, aggregation, solubility, viscosity, and stability [217]. The core principle is that when a laser illuminates the sample, the random movement of particles causes the scattered light intensity to fluctuate over time. Mathematical analysis of these fluctuations can calculate the hydrodynamic diameter of the particles [218].

DLS is a convenient tool for quickly analyzing EVs size distribution and is suitable for the preliminary assessment of EVs sample quality, such as purity and homogeneity [19]. Its non-invasive sample preparation, which requires no chemical fixation or marking, allows for complete sample recovery. The simplicity of the operation also makes DLS user-friendly and accurate. In addition, DLS can be used to measure the zeta potential of EVs. With these advantages, DLS has become a suitable device for determining the routine use of EVs size distribution. However, DLS cannot define the concentration of vesicles, nor can it provide biochemical data or information on EVs sources [219]. It usually only provides information on particle size distribution and cannot directly determine parameters such as particle shape or structure. For high-concentration samples, multiple scattering effects may occur, affecting the accuracy of the results [220].

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

NTA, analyzing EVs via single particle visualization and dynamic tracking, can simultaneously detect size distribution, concentration, and surface characteristics, making it a key method for vesicle quantification [221]. It works through laser scattering and Brownian motion: when a laser illuminates the sample, vesicle-scattered light is captured by a microscope. Single particle trajectory tracking calculates diffusion rates, and mathematical models then determine particle sizes [222].

NTA is a central quantitative technology in EVs research. It’s highly sensitive and high-throughput, able to detect low-concentration EVs quickly and suit large-scale sample analysis [223]. Measuring both size and concentration helps assess EVs heterogeneity. Also, it’s relatively easy to operate with highly automated data collection and analysis [224]. However, NTA is very particular about sample conditions like dilution, EVs purity, and temperature, which can greatly affect results [225]. Instrument settings, video recording, and data analysis also need calibration and optimization for consistent outcomes [226]. Note that NTA’s detection range (30–1000 nm) may miss very small or large vesicles. Compared to DLS, which can be biased by large-particle signals, NTA’s size measurements are closer to the true values [227].

Tunable resistive pulse sensing (TRPS)

TRPS, a nanopore-based single-particle analysis technique, measures EV size, concentration, and surface charge with high precision by detecting resistance changes as particles pass through a pore [228]. Its core principle is that when a particle traverses a nanopore filled with electrolyte, it causes a transient change in pore resistance, generating an electrical pulse. The pulse intensity correlates with particle volume, and the pulse duration reflects the particle’s transit speed. From this, vesicle size and count can be simultaneously calculated [229].

TRPS is crucial for high-precision EVs characterization, especially in clinical research. It offers multi-parameter quantitative analysis of individual EV, providing accurate size distribution and concentration data for complex biological samples [230]. Additionally, by measuring surface charge and using zeta potential analysis, TRPS helps explore EV-cell interactions [231]. It features a fast measurement speed, simple workflow, and low sample consumption, making it suitable for high-throughput analysis [232]. However, its detection range is limited to 40 nm–10 μm, and nanoparticle quantification can be affected by ionic current fluctuations [233]. Regular calibration with standard particles before each experiment is necessary to ensure nanopore performance. Also, avoid repeated sample freeze-thaw cycles and ensure thorough mixing before detection to prevent particle aggregation or excess [97].

Biochemical characterization of extracellular vesicles

Although biophysical methods are indispensable for quantifying EVs population diversity, they fall short of exposing the molecular determinants of functional heterogeneity. Biochemical analyses penetrate this molecular core, identifying and quantifying the proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids that endow distinct EVs subtypes with specific functionalities. These biochemical analyses comprehensively support the discovery of vesicle biomarkers and their functions, enhance the understanding of EVs roles in cellular communication and disease processes, and boost their applications in biomarker discovery and therapeutic development (Fig. 6).

Detection and Identification of Chemical Properties of Extracellular Vesicles. EVs contain key biochemical components like proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, and metabolites. To support biomarker discovery and functional research, various techniques are used, including WB, ELISA, PCR, SPR, MS, FCM, SERS, and TIRF. These tools provide rich data for studying EVs’ composition and function

Western blot

Western blotting, a standard method for validating vesicle-specific protein markers, precisely detects target protein expression and molecular weight via antigen-antibody interactions [203]. In Western Blot identification of EVs, multiple markers are usually detected to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the identification results [234]. Extensive research indicates that EVs proteins typically include membrane, cytoplasmic, and nuclear proteins [98]. According to the MISEV2023 guidelines released by the International Society for Extracellular Vesicle Research (ISEV) [235], WB identification of EVs should analyze at least one protein from transmembrane proteins, cytoplasmic proteins and non-EV co-isolated structures (NVEPs), which act as EVs markers and purity controls.

ELISA

ELISA, operating on specific antigen-antibody interactions, precisely quantifies specific protein markers on/in EVs, making it key for disease biomarker screening and functional studies [155]. Compared to Western Blot, ELISA is more convenient, sensitive, and capable of high-throughput analysis [103]. It can be combined with other technologies, such as high-throughput droplet digital ELISA, which can produce 20 million droplets per minute for enhanced sensitivity and specificity [236]. While both ELISA and Western blotting have similar detection limits, ELISA is generally faster and more scalable for high-throughput measurements [237]. However, ELISA has limitations in EVs detection, like the need for high-quality specific antibodies and certain EVs purity requirements.

Mass spectrometry (MS)

Mass spectrometry is the cornerstone for unbiased discovery and comprehensive dissection of EVs molecular heterogeneity. Proteomic and lipidomic profiling can reveal striking molecular disparities among samples or isolated fractions, thereby linking physical subpopulations to specific molecular signatures and furnishing a mechanistic basis for functional heterogeneity [238]. LC-MS and MALDI-MS are widely used to characterize EVs proteins [239], lipids [240], and metabolites. After purification and digestion, EVs proteins can be analyzed by LC-MS in data-dependent or all-ion modes, identifying thousands of proteins. Combining isotopic labeling or label-free quantification allows precise quantification of target molecules. Recent advancements include workflows like Exo-PLA coupled with HRMS for discovering and validating novel EVs surface proteins [241]. CDMS analyzes particle charge-to-mass ratios, revealing heterogeneity in mEVs [242]. iEVXL elucidates key vesicle-signaling mechanisms by determining protein-complex conformations [243].

MS, with high sensitivity, resolution, and coverage, offers extensive proteomic data, aiding in understanding EVs biology and disease mechanisms [244]. However, it requires specialized equipment, skilled technicians, and has complex workflows with high requirements for EVs purity and protein extraction efficiency [245]. Despite challenges in analyzing low-abundance substances and complex data interpretation, the future development of single-EV MS imaging and multi-omics data integration is expected to enhance the application value of EVs in disease biomarker discovery and mechanistic research [246]. Systematic MS analysis can reveal EVs heterogeneity and provide a molecular basis for developing EV-based liquid biopsies and targeted therapies [247].

Flow cytometry (FCM)

FCM, with single-particle analysis and multi-parameter detection capabilities, is widely used in EVs phenotyping and subgroup sorting. However, EVs are small, heterogeneous, and hard to measure. FCM faces challenges in EVs and submicron particle analysis, with controversial results often due to instrument limitations, lack of reliable methods, and data-interpretation ambiguities [248]. The statistical description of EVs size distribution measured by FCM can be biased by the lower limit of detection (LoD). Thus, defining the lower LoD of flow cytometers is crucial when reporting EVs diameters, and caution is needed in using statistics to describe size distribution [249].

Through technological improvements, such as enhanced side scatter detection and optimized fluorescent labeling strategies, or the use of nanoflow cytometry systems (e.g., Apogee, CytoFLEX Nano), precise identification of vesicles in the 100–200 nm range can be achieved [250].The advent of nano-flow cytometry (nFCM) has been transformative, enabling simultaneous interrogation of individual EVs for both physical parameters and surface-protein markers, thereby providing direct and quantitative evidence of extensive heterogeneity in surface-molecule expression across the EV population. Through nFCM, it’s confirmed that lipophilic dye labeling doesn’t affect immunodetection of vesicle surface markers, offering theoretical support for optimized labeling [251]. nFCM has also evaluated different EVs isolation methods, finding traditional UC still yields high-purity vesicles in plasma, highlighting its reference value in standardized method development [252].

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

qPCR, monitoring fluorescence signal dynamics, can detect low-abundance nucleic acids in EVs, and each experiment takes only a few hours [253]. With high sensitivity and specificity, qPCR is vital for studying vesicle function and disease links [254, 255]. In recent years, researchers have developed various innovative strategies to enhance the efficiency and accuracy of qPCR detection [256]. Given the low concentration of nucleic acids in EVs, signal amplification technologies—such as strand displacement reaction (SDR), hybridization chain reaction (HCR), rolling circle amplification (RCA), CRISPR/Cas, and catalytic hairpin assemblies—are often employed to boost detection sensitivity [190]. Moreover, RT-qPCR faces the challenge of lacking universal and stably expressed reference genes (RGs) for EVs. To address the standardization issue, recent studies have identified four stably expressed RGs (e.g., SNRPG, OST4). These universal EVs RGs are expected to promote the use of RT-qPCR in analyzing EV-related RNA cargo [257].

Total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF)

TIRF microscopy enables high-sensitivity single-particle dynamic observation and molecular quantification of EVs by exciting fluorescence signals within a nanoscale range of the sample surface [258]. The technique allows real-time tracking of the dynamic interactions of 50 nm-sized vesicles. When combined with fluorescent-probe labeling strategies (such as quantum dots or nucleic acid-aptamers), it can also analyze the spatial distribution and content changes of specific molecules within vesicles [259].

In recent years, innovative labeling and signal amplification strategies have greatly expanded the application of TIRF technology in detection. For example, using activatable aptamer probes (AAP) specific to tumor markers like PTK7, TIRF can distinguish cancer patients from healthy individuals at the single-vesicle level and monitor treatment effects [260]. Researchers have also combined DNA probes’ transient binding characteristics to develop in-situ quantification and stoichiometric methods for miRNA markers in single vesicles [261]. In high-throughput detection, a newly-developed high-throughput nano-biochip integrated system (HNCIB) can detect vesicle surface markers and nucleic acids simultaneously [262]. Single-vesicle analysis TIRF has made progress by calculating the overlapping proportion of fluorescent signals to distinguish vesicle aggregation and correct quantitative errors [263].

Surface-enhanced raman scattering (SERS)

Conventional EVs assays rely on fluorescent or radiolabeling, which can alter surface topology, mask binding sites, or introduce nonspecific signals, thereby obscuring true biological readouts. Label-free detection circumvents these artifacts by directly transducing physicochemical changes triggered by EVs-sensor interactions, enabling unperturbed interrogation of native vesicles [264]. Moreover, the exceptional sensitivity of these platforms permits the identification of rare or low-abundance subpopulations, which is pivotal for uncovering disease-relevant EVs-such as those harboring specific oncogenic mutations [265].

SERS technology, with its extremely high sensitivity and capability of molecular fingerprint-specific identification, offers a nano-resolution detection solution for analyzing the biochemical composition of EVs [266]. Raman techniques for EVs analysis can be divided into labeled and label-free methods. Label-free Raman methods can directly detect and characterize various biomolecular components within EVs, such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, providing information on their composition and potential biological functions. Labeled methods, by using specific tags, reporter molecules, or dyes, can more accurately quantify EVs and selectively detect biomarkers [267].

SERS technology has unique photoelectronic properties, is easy to functionalize, and has special interactions between light and nano-metal materials. It can detectosomes ex sensitively without large samples and multiple phenotypic analyses [268]. Silver nanoparticle arrays combined with laser processing technologies enable fast label-free recognition of breast cancer exosomes, with single-particle-level sensitivity [269]. Integrating SERS aptamer sensors into droplet microfluidic platforms allows high-throughput screening of tumor-related exosomes in blood [270]. Detection time is shortened to minutes, making the SERS-based droplet system a potential liquid biopsy technology for early cancer diagnosis [270]. Researchers have also developed affinity-based antibody-free enrichment technologies by utilizing the affinity between titanium dioxide and vesicle membrane lipids [271]. Sample preparation and detection can be completed within 10 min, with a detection limit as low as hundreds of particles per milliliter [271]. Despite these advances, SERS-based EVs detection faces challenges such as insufficient signal stability and non-specific adsorption interference from complex samples like whole blood [272]. To address these, researchers have proposed multimodal probe designs, such as combined PTK7 and EpCAM marker detection. Dual-target validation has increased cancer patient identification accuracy to 80% [273].

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

SPR technology enables label-free and dynamic detection of EVs surface markers by monitoring optical signal changes during molecular binding [274]. SPR offers advantages like no labeling, low sample volume (microliters), fast detection (minutes), and high sensitivity for low-concentration targets [275]. It can also specifically identify and quantify EVs by combining specific antibodies or aptamers [276].

With the continuous breakthroughs in nanomaterial engineering and molecular recognition technologies, SPR is expected to develop towards higher throughput and multi-target simultaneous detection [277]. For instance, by optimizing the combination of specific aptamers and G-quadruplex DNA nanostructures, highly sensitive and specific detection of breast cancer-related vesicle markers has been achieved [278]. In another study, based on hydrogel-AuNP supramolecular spheres (H-Au), the linear range and detection limit were greatly improved by integrating the signal amplification effect of mass accumulation hydrogels and the LSPR effect of AuNPs with high specificity aptamers [279]. Additionally, SPR technology, utilizing aptamer recognition and polydopamine-functionalized gold nanoparticles (Au@PDA NPs) assisted signal amplification for exosome-specific detection, can specifically detect SMMC-7721 exosomes and has the ability to directly detect serum samples [280]. The ECL immune sensor based on the LSPR of AuNPs and Pdots can detect pancreatic cancer exosomes and improve the ECL efficiency of Pdots, providing new insights for liquid biopsy technology [281]. By optimizing the gold-silica core-shell structure to enhance the SPR effect, ultra-sensitive and selective detection of PD-L1 exosomes in serum has been achieved [282].

Single EV analysis methods

Moving beyond population-level analysis, single-EV detection techniques are crucial for deciphering functional heterogeneity, identifying rare subpopulations with diagnostic significance, and enabling ultra-sensitive monitoring of disease dynamics. It can deepen our understanding of EVs heterogeneity and cell-behavior mechanisms, helping to discover early-diagnosis biomarkers for diseases and enabling precise disease classification [283]. It enables real-time monitoring of disease and treatment responses during therapy and promote the development of relevant detection technologies, separation and purification methods, clinical applications (Fig. 7).

Integrating AFM with fluorescence microscopy enables simultaneous measurement of individual vesicle physical properties and surface marker expression. Analyzing EVs from three cell lines revealed multiple subpopulations, confirming vesicle population heterogeneity [289]. A new technique called SEVEN, combining affinity isolation and quantitative single-molecule localization microscopy (qSMLM), has quantified the number, size, shape, tetraspanin content, and heterogeneity of affinity-isolated EVs [284]. Nano-projectile secondary ion MS (NP-SIMS) with single-particle bombardment analyzes population heterogeneity at single-EV resolution, distinguishing liver cancer-derived EVs from normal ones [285].

A droplet microfluidic system with antibody-DNA conjugation detects membrane proteins and mRNA at the single-particle level, enhancing tumor-related vesicle detection sensitivity and specificity [287]. nFCM, integrating light scattering and fluorescence detection, analyzes single EV and DNA fragments. It provides physical size and nucleic acid composition information of EVs, offering a high-resolution tool for EV-DNA interaction studies at the single-particle level [286]. Polymyxin B-fluorescein probes (PmBF) targeting bacterial lipopolysaccharides combined with nano-flow cytometry to specifically identify and quantify bEVs in blood [290]. Single-exosome sequencing based on the 10x Genomics platform deciphers RNA composition and molecular sorting mechanisms of individual EV, applicable to all exosome types [291].

High-throughput nano-biochip systems (HNCIB) with integrated protein and nucleic acid detection support parallel analysis of 384 samples in six hours, efficient for large-scale clinical research [262]. The single-particle automated Raman trapping analysis system (SPARTA) compares multiple EVs spectra via visualized spectral dimensionality reduction, achieving over 95% sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing cancerous and non-cancerous EVs [292]. Digital droplet immune PCR (ddiPCR), combining immune PCR’s specificity and digital droplet PCR’s sensitivity, with machine-learning algorithms enables precise breast-cancer diagnosis via multi-subpopulation EVs counting [288]. Two high-sensitivity EV-marker detection methods using single-molecule array technology (SiMoa) analyze tumor-related EVs directly from patient plasma [293].

By integrating multi-omics strategies, investigators have systematically assessed EVs heterogeneity. Differential-density-gradient centrifugation first achieves high-purity, reproducible physical separation of large and small vesicles, laying an unbiased foundation for downstream quantification. Subsequent coupling of label-free/targeted proteomics with bulk- or single-EV transcriptomics captures the full molecular spectrum at the population level while resolving subpopulation-specific differences at single-vesicle resolution [294]. Complementarily, multivariate analytical pipelines-principally PCA followed by SVM/CNN classifiers and SHAP/PDP explainability modules-have been applied to extract and interpret molecular signatures from EV Raman spectra. These spectral differences mirror functional disparities in tumor-microenvironment remodeling, signal transduction, and immune evasion, thereby tightly linking multivariate outputs to both molecular phenotypes and biological functions of EV subsets [295].Furthermore, a method termed “EV Fingerprinting” has been developed that pairs single-particle flow cytometry with high-dimensional data reduction (UMAP and HDBSCAN) to dissect EV heterogeneity at unprecedented resolution. Membrane labeling with the fluorescent dye Di-8-ANEPPS enables simultaneous quantification of EV size, membrane composition, and membrane order through multiparametric analysis [296]. A novel liquid-biopsy protocol that employs NanoFisher nanotrap particles for high-yield isolation of plasma EVs followed by machine-learning-assisted metabolomic profiling achieved perfect discrimination between early-stage gastric cancer and non-disease controls, reliable distinction from benign gastric disorders, and an overall three-class accuracy of 92% in 150 samples, offering a non-invasive, highly sensitive, and specific one-stop solution for early gastric-cancer screening [297].

Despite advances in single-particle analysis technologies, clinical application bottlenecks remain, such as lack of standardization, high equipment costs, and complex data analysis. Future development of multimodal platforms and automated algorithms is expected to further translate these technologies into point-of-care testing and precision medicine.

Advances and challenges in preclinical translational research

Recent advances in single-particle profiling, molecular phenotyping, and high-resolution isolation now enable the precise identification and exploitation of functionally distinct EVs subsets, catalysing their tailored application in tumour diagnostics, drug delivery, and tissue regeneration. The clinical translation of EVs in theranostics has significantly outpaced early expectations [298]. Analysis of public-reporting repositories shows 471 EV-related clinical trials across over 200 diseases, with the highest proportion in diagnosis and companion diagnostics, and cancer being the most common field [299]. EVs are increasingly recognized as highly promising liquid-biopsy biomarkers whose analytical sensitivity and specificity continue to rise. Nowhere is this trajectory steeper than in oncology, where EV-based assays have achieved rapid advances in early detection, molecular subtyping, prognostication, and real-time monitoring of therapeutic response, underscoring their vast clinical potential [300]. In therapeutic trials, MSC-EVs are predominant and are used in various fields such as respiratory diseases and tissue-injury repair. Currently, over 40 MSC-EVs-based therapies are in clinical trials [301, 302]. Recently, exosomes from human cells/samples and PDEVs have been notably used in EVs clinical trials [90]. Each trial type has diverse EVs sources and uses, indicating that the heterogeneity of EVs contributes to their versatile applications [303].

Preclinical research on oncology

The emergence of new technologies has not only improved EVs isolation efficiency and detection accuracy but also provided novel strategies and advantages for tumor early diagnosis, gene therapy, and prognostic monitoring. This has greatly enhanced the clinical value of EVS in oncology. With a broad development prospect, they are expected to become a key driver in tumor precision medicine.

Biomarker

Exosomes, a subclass of EVs, have gained significant attention in cancer diagnosis due to their potential for early detection and accurate identification of various cancer types [304, 305]. A clinically applicable high-throughput integrated magneto-electrochemical EVs (HiMEX) technique has been developed. It is capable of analyzing plasma samples within one hour. The results demonstrate that detecting circulating EVs from CRC patients achieves highly accurate cancer diagnosis with an overall accuracy of over 96% [306]. The ExoPatch patch enables direct melanoma-specific exosome capture from the skin via a “single-needle” procedure, eliminating the need for separate interstitial fluid extraction. When combined with LFIA for on-site detection, it achieves a sensitivity of 10 EVs/µL for detecting the melanoma-related exosome markers MCAM/MCSP [307]. The ColonTrack model demonstrates outstanding clinical diagnostic performance in multicenter validation cohorts, achieving over 95% sensitivity in detecting early-stage colorectal cancer. It represents a significant improvement in diagnostic efficiency compared to traditional markers [308]. A detection system combining TiO₂ chemical affinity capture with label-free MS has been established. From large-scale plasma samples, this system has identified GDF15 as a promising EVs protein marker for prostate cancer [309].The salivary EVP-based 6-miRNA molecular marker system offers a novel non-invasive strategy for esophageal cancer early detection and risk stratification [310]. CAR+EVs serve as markers to predict the risk of immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome in B-cell lymphoma patients undergoing CD19-targeted CAR T-cell therapy [311]. Most EV-DNA research focuses on cancer, with many reports highlighting its potential as a biomarker. For instance, mutated DNA in small EVs from plasma, such as EGFR T790M, KRAS G12D, and TP53 R273H, are considered potential biomarkers for colon, lung, and pancreatic cancers, respectively [312]. A recent study systematically profiled the lectin-affinity glycosylation patterns (LAGPs) of plasma-derived EVs and revealed marked heterogeneity in EV glycosylation during gastric cancer initiation and progression. The authors developed an integrated, one-stop liquid-biopsy platform that combines a lectin microarray with machine-learning algorithms; in a cohort of 84 samples, the platform achieved 100% discrimination among early-stage cancer, advanced cancer, benign lesions, and healthy controls [313]. This non-invasive, high-efficiency tool offers a unified clinical solution for gastric cancer screening, prognostication, and prediction of immunotherapy response. Moreover, EVs heterogeneity markedly compromises biomarker accuracy and predictability. Sub-populations differing in cellular origin or molecular phenotype can convey diametrically opposed prognostic information. As a case in point, PD-1/CD80+ small extracellular vesicles derived from immune cells (I-sEVs) and PD-L1+ sEVs released by tumour cells exert opposing functional and prognostic effects during immunotherapy: PD-1/CD80+ I-sEVs are associated with therapeutic resistance and a “cold” tumour microenvironment, whereas PD-L1+ sEVs facilitate immune evasion [314]. Measuring circulating sEV levels of PD-L1, PD-1 or CD80 individually yields insufficient sensitivity and specificity for predicting immunotherapeutic responses. Consequently, relying solely on bulk EVs counts or a single marker is inadequate to mirror disease status or treatment efficacy. Future investigations should prioritise the isolation and functional characterisation of specific EVs subpopulations to enhance diagnostic precision and prognostic assessment.

Drug delivery platform

By leveraging the inherent heterogeneity of EVs—tissue tropism and low immunogenicity—and integrating engineering strategies to enhance their targeting specificity and drug-loading capacity, precise drug delivery within the tumor microenvironment can be achieved. Exosomes have shown great potential as therapeutic delivery vehicles in preclinical and clinical studies [315]. With natural targeting and protective transport advantages, they’ve successfully delivered chemotherapeutic and nucleic acid drugs precisely [316]. The exosome drug exoIL-12, developed through the engEx™ platform, precisely delivers IL-12 to the tumor microenvironment. It has demonstrated good safety and tolerability in clinical trials and is one of the few EVs therapies to reach this stage [317]. A new dual-targeted EVs system (dtEVs) has been developed, which, through the dual targeting mechanism of CD64 protein and CK tissue-homing peptides, enables precise delivery of mRNA, siRNA, and chemotherapeutic drugs [318]. Exosomes also show great potential in brain cancer treatment due to their ability to cross the blood-brain barrier. For example, exosomes loaded with paclitaxel can reduce cardiotoxicity. Engineered exosomes carrying siRNA or immunomodulatory molecules like TRAIL have significantly inhibited glioblastoma in preclinical models [319].

Gene therapy

In the translational application of gene therapy, clinical research now aims to move exosome-based gene therapy into human trials, bridging the gap from preclinical studies to clinical use [320]. Researchers are developing a DTMI therapy that uses tumor-cell-derived drug-loaded EVs to activate macrophages and neutrophils, effectively managing malignant pleural and peritoneal effusions. Approved for clinical use in multiple regions, DTMI therapy, when combined with PD-1 antibodies, can expand the population eligible for treatment [321]. For non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) with bone metastasis and immune resistance, the TMTP1-TSRP-EVs system has been devised, it significantly suppresses tumor growth and bone metastasis in mouse models and reverses PD-L1-mediated resistance [322]. Engineered EVs targeting cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), such as IEVs-PFD/138, have also made progress. They can reprogram the pancreatic cancer TME via regulatory molecule delivery, boosting chemotherapeutic efficacy and extending patient survival [323]. Moreover, researchers found that EVs from solid tumors, carrying PD-L1 and tumor antigens, can promote treatment resistance by inhibiting CAR-T cell function. But this can be mitigated either by suppressing EVs secretion or by modifying CAR-T cells, like developing Serpin B9-enhanced CAR-T cells, thus improving solid tumor treatment outcomes [324].

Prognostic monitoring

In exosome-based prognostic monitoring studies, researchers used TCGA data to create the EALncRI model, which includes MIR4713HG and ZEB1-AS1. This model effectively differentiates patients’ survival risks and predicts immunotherapy responses and chemosensitivity [325]. A multicenter meta-analysis covering 56 clinical trials (5,985 patients) showed that single biomarkers, molecular combinations, and vesicle counts in blood EVs are significantly associated with colorectal cancer patients’ survival (pHR = 1.87–3.97), especially in advanced cases [326]. By analyzing miRNAs in serum - derived EVs, a diagnostic and prognostic model (sEVmiR-EOC) based on 7 miRNAs was developed. It demonstrated high diagnostic efficiency (AUC of 0.913–0.973) across training, testing, and validation sets and effectively predicted patients’ progression-free survival (PFS) [327]. Through proteomics analysis of peritoneal EVs, researchers established molecular profiles related to tumor microenvironment evolution, identified tumor-promoting proteins and pathways involved in cellular communication within the tumor microenvironment, and provided a basis for personalized treatment [328].

Preclinical research on tissue repair

The advancement of EVs isolation and detection technologies has also greatly boosted the clinical translation of EVs in tissue repair. These new technologies have increased the purity and efficiency of EVs separation, ensuring the activity of EVs components for tissue regeneration. They also support precise EVs separation from diverse sources, thus speeding up the development of EV-based therapies for damaged tissues and organs (Fig. 9).

Nervous system

The research team has developed engineered EVs (EEVs) for intranasal delivery. By integrating ultra-small superparamagnetic nanoparticles (USPN), they have enhanced targeting. These EEVs also carry key miRNAs to inhibit the NF-κB pathway. In animal models and human brain organoids, EEVs have been verified to promote nervous function recovery [329]. EV-GFAP in EVs has been identified as a circulating biomarker for TBI severity, guiding early intervention through dynamic monitoring [333]. Some researchers have proposed a “pre-depletion-escape” sequential dosing strategy. Using dendritic cell-derived vesicles (DV) combined with CD47-modified vesicles (MV47), this strategy achieves long-term circulation of EVs, significantly improving vascular regeneration and functional recovery after myocardial infarction [334].

Skin and wound

A systematic review of 83 preclinical studies indicated that MSC-EVs are markedly superior to the control group in wound closure rate, scar reduction, and angiogenesis (SMD = 1.87–3.97), with particularly significant effects in diabetic wounds [335]. A research team discovered that EVs from Streptococcus mutans (Sm EVs) can activate the TLR3 pathway via tRNA variants (e.g., tRNA-Met), promoting epithelial cell proliferation and migration, and significantly accelerating skin wound healing. This mechanism has been verified in human oral mucosa organoids, offering new insights for chronic wound treatment [336]. Another team developed a novel synthetic exosome-like vesicle (LPEx) by hybridizing watermelon-derived EVs (wEV) with liposomes and encapsulating 28 key miRNAs. Results show LPEx can significantly accelerate wound healing and inhibit scar formation in a deep second-degree burn model [330]. A study on an antibacterial hydrogel (Gel/PL-5) loaded with engineered EVs targeting vascular endothelial cells (s-EV@Gel/PL-5) found that modifying the EVs surface to express sLeX ligands enhances targeting of E-selectin overexpressed in diabetic wounds, significantly accelerating diabetic infected wound healing. The hydrogel also has a sustained-release function to prolong the therapeutic effect [337].

Respiratory system

Researchers used allogeneic adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs (ADSC-EVs) for aerosol inhalation therapy for ARDS. Clinical trials (NCT04313647) showed healthy volunteers tolerated aerosolized EVs well with no severe adverse reactions. In animal models, EVs significantly reduced pulmonary inflammatory factor levels and improved survival rates [338]. Another team engineered EVs to efficiently load Wnt3a protein (Wnt3aWGEVs) to activate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Results showed intravenously administered Wnt3aWGEVs significantly improved lung function and alveolar structural abnormalities, offering a new COPD treatment strategy [331]. It was found that ADSC-EVs deliver miR-320a to inhibit the CBL/AMPK pathway, improving mitochondrial energy metabolism and reducing cell apoptosis. They significantly alleviated lung injury in a Staphylococcus aureus-induced ALI model [339]. Moreover, studies found that exosomes from hucMSCs cultured in 3D microspheres-have stronger anti-inflammatory and tissue-repair abilities than traditionally-cultured exosomes. By modulating macrophage energy metabolism, they significantly improved LPS-induced ALI models [340].

Musculoskeletal system

Researchers have developed a composite system (Rh Gel@SP-EVs). It can regulate energy metabolism, such as promoting ATP synthesis, and inhibit the JAK/STAT pathway. This helps reduce cartilage damage, promotes joint function recovery, and significantly slows the progression of osteoarthritis [332]. Another team found that flat matrix (fEVs) can promote the proliferation of aged MPCs, while nano-topographic matrix (nEVs) can enhance myogenic differentiation. By optimizing the delivery sequence, they successfully restored muscle fiber size and contractile function in aged mouse models to levels similar to those in young mice [341]. In addition, researchers used EVs derived from hypoxic bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (Hypo-EVs) combined with an injectable polymeric hydrogel. This approach significantly promoted bone regeneration in cranial defect areas. The hydrogel’s sustained-release system prolonged the local action time of EVs, thereby enhancing the therapeutic effect [342].

Other preclinical studies

Most published clinical studies on MSCs and their EVs have used intravenous administration. These studies have confirmed the safety of MSCs and MSC-EVs treatments, but their efficacy may be suboptimal, possibly due to the administration route. In contrast, nebulized inhalation has unique advantages in lung injury treatment, as it significantly increases drug bioavailability in target pulmonary areas [338]. In-depth analysis of several clinical trials revealed that in a preliminary trial, MSC-EVs nebulization accelerated pulmonary lesion absorption in COVID-19 pneumonia patients and shortened hospital stays, with no acute or secondary allergic reactions [343]. These findings have led researchers to investigate the advantages of local EVs administration. Compared to traditional intravenous injection, nebulization improves drug bioavailability in the lungs, potentially overcoming the efficacy limitations of systemic administration.

Studies have revealed the complex mechanisms of MSC-EVs in treating various diseases, which are hard to fully explain due to disease-specific differences. But by identifying and quantifying EVs attributes tied to expected bioactivity in EV-mediated therapy, we may develop robust potency assays [344]. Chromatography advances have enabled efficient clinical-grade pEVs purification. First-in-human trials confirmed that locally injected LEAP-pEVs heal wounds safely and tolerably [345]. ExoFlo, an MSC-EVs product, showed good safety and potential efficacy in treating COVID-19-related ARDS patients, with its high purity lowering allergy risks [346]. In a double-blind controlled trial on severe COVID-19-related ARDS patients, hPMSC-EVs significantly cut mortality [347]. In metabolic regulation, research found that dietary n-3 PUFAs alter EVs’ quantity, composition, and function. By changing their fatty acid and protein profiles, EVs supporting thrombin generation are highly significant for thrombosis prevention and treatment [348]. Lastly, a study on complex perianal fistulas showed that local placental MSC-EVs injection enables long - term lesion repair, with treatment effects verified by MRI [349].

Future directions and challenges in clinical translation

Clinical translation of EVs is advancing rapidly, with clinical trials targeting a spectrum of diseases already under way (Table 3). Early-phase studies have established the preliminary safety and efficacy of nebulized MSC-EVs formulations in COVID-19–associated acute respiratory distress syndrome and other pulmonary disorders [346]. Engineered EVs platforms are being driven forward through ongoing clinical investigations, underscoring their potential for precise molecular intervention. Meanwhile, EV-based diagnostics continue to demonstrate exceptional accuracy in non-invasive cancer detection. Future advances will hinge on the integrated deployment of cutting-edge technologies. Single-vesicle profiling and multi-omics analyses will dissect extracellular vesicle heterogeneity and functional attributes [350], while artificial intelligence and machine-learning algorithms will enable the discovery of disease-specific biomarkers and the optimization of isolation protocols. Research priorities should also encompass engineering EVs for targeted delivery and implementing hybrid techniques such as TFF-SEC to achieve scalable, GMP-compliant production [351].

EVs hold immense translational promise, yet their clinical implementation remains hindered by the absence of rigorously standardized isolation and characterization protocols [355]. Perspectives and challenges

As key cell-to-cell communication mediators, EVs have seen research-tech breakthroughs. Innovations span efficient isolation (AF4, microfluidic chips), precise identification (single-particle MS, SERs), and functional analysis (single-exosome sequencing, TIRF). These multi-dimensional advances have deepened our understanding of EVs, unraveling their roles in tumor metastasis, immune regulation, and tissue repair. They’ve also spurred EVs applications in liquid biopsies and drug delivery [24]. The central idea driving EVs technology is a strategic pivot from “separation” to “resolution”. Emerging tools now offer tailor-made strategies to dissect EV heterogeneity, with the choice of technique dictated by which dimension—size, origin, cargo, or function—is most pertinent to the biological or clinical question at hand. Only by resolving, rather than merely isolating, heterogeneity can we unlock the full precision of EVs for diagnosis and therapy.

Consensus definitions for EVs subtypes are urgently required. These should extend beyond particle size and biogenesis to incorporate molecular cargo, cellular origin, and functional properties. Multi-parametric frameworks that integrate physical (size, density), biochemical (protein, lipid, and nucleic-acid profiles), and functional (targeting and uptake efficiency) attributes are needed to classify EVs into biologically and clinically relevant subpopulations. A standardized EVs-typing scheme will facilitate cross-study comparisons and enhance reproducibility.

The clinical translation of EVs faces several major challenges. The lack of technical standardization is a key issue; for example, different isolation methods can result in product heterogeneity [194]. In addition, there are bottlenecks in scaled production, which is currently low in yield and high in cost [357]. EVs long-term therapeutic effects verification is inadequate, and the safety and persistence of repeated dosing are not clear [358]. Regardless of the clinical indication, exosome use must comply with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP). GMP-compliant exosome production involves top-quality materials, cells, and culture conditions, as well as advanced manufacturing techniques and skilled workers, all within strictly controlled and monitored settings. Purification and quality control analyses are also essential to ensure exosomes meet the highest standards before patient use [359].

To fully realize their potential, future investigations must adopt a multivariate analytical framework that simultaneously interrogates EVs size, composition, cellular origin, and functional attributes. Population-level analyses yield averaged metrics that are indispensable for biomarker discovery and scalable production, whereas single-vesicle technologies expose the diversity and rarity of specific subpopulations. Integrating these complementary data streams through computational modelling and machine learning will bridge the gap between aggregate trends and individual EV features, enabling more precise linkage of EVs heterogeneity to clinical phenotypes.While initial studies have shown the potential of EVs as drug delivery systems and therapeutic agents, their clinical application needs strict preclinical and clinical trials to verify safety and efficacy [360]. A report summarizing 21 human studies and clinical trials using cell-derived EVs therapy for various conditions and diseases has been used for safety meta-analysis and evaluation of effectiveness outcomes. The results indicate that EVs-based therapies are safe, with low rates of serious adverse and adverse events, suggesting they could be promising for treatment [358]. Also, selecting proper loading methods, improving the loading efficiency of drugs or bioactive molecules in EVs, and optimizing delivery routes and targeting have become key research focuses. Advances in EVs engineering offer new opportunities to enhance EVs’ therapeutic effects [361]. Technologies gene editing and synthetic biology can effectively modify EVs to improve their efficacy and targeting.

EVs-based therapy has shown disease-treatment benefits in preclinical studies, but its long-term efficacy and persistence are still uncertain [362]. In-depth research is crucial to clarify EVs’ in-vivo lifespan, drug-efficacy duration, and the safety and effectiveness of repeated dosing [363]. Relevant studies indicate that storage at −80 °C and freeze-thaw cycles may significantly alter EVs samples, altering their functional properties. Experimentally, numerous particles undergoing fusion suggest the samples have experienced intense re-modeling and generated multiple components [97]. Given this, appropriate storage and transportation conditions for EVs products are an urgent issue to resolve. Recent research on storage conditions and bioactivity of natural EVs and cargo-loaded EVs shows that lyophilization (freeze-drying) followed by room-temperature storage can maintain high bioactivity in cargo-loaded EVs [364]. These findings highlight the need for additional measures to ensure EVs stability and effectiveness when validating their feasibility as clinical products [365].

The future development of EVs encompasses several key areas: First, developing precise diagnostic markers to enhance disease detection accuracy. Second, delving deeper into EVs’ therapeutic mechanisms across different diseases. Third, optimizing targeted delivery systems to boost therapeutic effects and reduce side effects. Fourth, rigorously evaluating long-term safety and efficacy to ensure clinical reliability. Fifth, advancing translational research to fast-track laboratory findings into clinical use. Sixth, enforcing strict regulatory approval to guarantee product quality and safety. Addressing these challenges requires ongoing research investment and innovation. Interdisciplinary collaboration is indispensable in driving EVs research, involving biology, medicine, chemistry, physics, and other fields [366]. It necessitates coordinated efforts from experts in different disciplines to integrate diverse theories and technologies, and to jointly tackle EV-related challenges in disease diagnosis, therapy, and translation [42]. While EVs show great biomedical potential, their separation, detection, and standardized preparation face challenges like heterogeneous separation technologies and detection limitations [367]. Future research should focus on technological innovation and quality control. Developing more efficient separation techniques, precise detection methods, and stringent standardization processes will propel EVs’ clinical and industrial applications, enabling their widespread use in disease diagnosis and treatment.
