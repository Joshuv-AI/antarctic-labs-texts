Lignans-rich extract of Schisandra chinensis prevent alcohol-associated liver disease by regulating the gut microbiota and tryptophan metabolism
Authors: Yan-Yu Chi, Jun-Yan Xiang, Hui-Min Li, Hao-Yu Shi, Ke Ning, Hongyu Xiang, Qiuhong Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) has emerged as a global public health concern, and gut microbiota plays a decisive role in its pathogenesis. Schisandra chinensis, a functional edible berry, exhibits hepatoprotective properties. However, the preventive effects of its main active component lignans against ALD, as well as their connections with gut microbiota and intestinal metabolites remain unclear. This study employed a mouse model of ALD induced by chronic-binge ethanol feeding to evaluate the hepatoprotective effects of lignans-rich extract of S. chinensis (SCL) interventions at different doses. 16S rDNA sequencing and metabolomic analysis were used to investigate microbial and metabolite profiles in intestinal contents. The hepatoprotective activity of key metabolite responding to SCL was further verified by in vivo and in vitro experiments. The results demonstrated that SCL effectively restored intestinal barrier function and alleviated alcohol-induced hepatic injury. SCL modulated intestinal tryptophan (Trp) metabolism and restored indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) levels. Concurrently, SCL reshaped the gut microbiota composition, enriched L. reuteri. Further in vivo and in vitro investigations demonstrated that SCL-mediated enrichment of L. reuteri contributed to intestinal IAA restoration which reactivated the ethanol-suppressed AHR pathway, enhanced intestinal barrier integrity, reduced lipopolysaccharide translocation into circulation and ultimately attenuates liver injury. Collectively, SCL prevent ALD by regulating intestinal microbiota and Trp metabolism. This study provides a scientific foundation for the development of S. chinensis as a functional food for ALD prevention.

Introduction

Alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD), primarily caused by chronic or excessive alcohol consumption, has become a global public health challenge, posing substantial individual health risks and socio-economic burdens (Dukić et al., 2023). Traditional treatments for ALD that focus on abstinence from alcohol, but its implementation is difficult for people with alcohol dependence. In recent years, ALD prevention strategies targeting the gut microbiota have become a prominent research focus.

Interactions among the gut microbiota, microbiota-derived metabolites, and the liver play a crucial role in the pathogenesis and treatment of ALD (Albillos et al., 2020). Alcohol misuse facilitates the overgrowth of certain harmful gut bacterial and dysbiosis (Meroni et al., 2019), characterized by an increase in endotoxin-producing bacteria and a reduction in flora that produce beneficial metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids and indole derivatives (Rohn et al., 2017). These changes compromise the intestinal barrier integrity, allowing excessive lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to translocate into the portal circulation (Bull-Otterson et al., 2013). LPS promotes intestinal inflammation and disruption of tight junction through specific signaling pathways, while also indirectly triggering an inflammatory response in the liver through Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), a major player in the development of ALD. Ultimately promoting ALD-associated liver injury (Ceni et al., 2014, Di Vincenzo et al., 2024).

Recent studies have demonstrated that regulating tryptophan (Trp)-indole metabolism by modulating the host gut microbiota composition is an effective strategy for repairing intestinal barrier damage and alleviating ALD (Wrzosek et al., 2021). Beneficial indole derivatives are metabolites produced from Trp via the indole metabolic pathway, which is specifically regulated by gut microbiota such as Bacteroides, Clostridium, and Lactobacillus (Xue et al., 2023). Multiple indole derivatives have been identified as aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) agonists, which modulate the expression of tight junction proteins (TJPs) via the AHR pathway to maintain intestinal homeostasis. Among these derivatives, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) has been reported to alleviate intestinal barrier damage in colitis mouse models by activating the AHR pathway (L. Chen et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023). Furthermore, studies suggest that IAA can improve insulin resistance, inflammation, and oxidative stress associated with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) (Ji et al., 2019).

Schisandra chinensis, a traditional functional edible berry widely distributed in northern China, exhibits diverse bioactivities including antioxidant, anti-fatigue, and neuroprotective properties (Chen et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2016). Based on its unique flavor and rich nutritional value, S. chinensis-derived products such as teas, wines, jams, and juices have been commercially developed (Zhang et al., 2014). Lignans are the main bioactive components of S. chinensis and exhibit remarkable hepatoprotective effects. Previous studies indicate that S. chinensis lignan extracts prevent hepatotoxicity through antifibrotic, antioxidant, and anti-apoptotic mechanisms (Xu et al., 2020), or alleviate non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) by inhibiting hepatic phosphatidylethanolamines accumulation (Xue et al., 2023). However, the preventive effects of lignans-rich extract of S. chinensis (SCL) on ALD and their impact on gut-liver axis remain to be elucidated. Our previous work revealed that gut microbiota plays an active role in the alcoholic hepatitis prophylactic activity of S. chinensis crude extract (Xiang et al., 2022). Considering the poor oral absorption of lignans and the pathogenesis of ALD (Xu et al., 2013), it is hypothesized that gut microbes may serve as key mediators in SCL-mediated ALD prevention.

This study evaluated the protective effects of SCL on ethanol-induced hepatic injury in mice, specifically investigated its regulatory impacts on intestinal microbiota composition, intestinal metabolites, and barrier function. The research aimed to elucidate the gut-liver axis related mechanisms underlying SCL-mediated prevention of ALD. The findings provide novel insights into the daily health care utilization of SCL and contribute to the development of functional foods for ALD prevention.

Materials and method

Preparation of SCL

S. chinensis was purchased from a farm in Ji'an County, Jilin Province, and authenticated by Professor Yuguang Chen at the Changchun Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital. Seeds are the main site of lignans enrichment (Wang et al., 2014). After manually separating the pulp and seeds, 20 g of seeds were crushed, dried, and extracted with 600 mL of 80 % ethanol at 80°C for 2 h. The supernatant was collected, and the extraction process was repeated three times. The combined supernatant was concentrated using rotary evaporation until no ethanol odor remained. The concentrate was purified using D101 macroporous resin (Aladdin, Shanghai, China), and the 95 % ethanol-eluting fraction was collected, further concentrated, and lyophilized to obtain SCL (Wang et al., 2022).

Compositional identification of SCL

The contents of lignans in SCL were determined using the chromotropic acid-sulfuric acid method. The lignans composition of SCL were qualitatively analyzed by UPLC-Q/TOF MS. Five of the main lignans were quantitatively analyzed by HPLC (standard curve made from standard products). Detailed methodologies are provided in the Supporting Information.

Animal experimental design

Male SPF-grade C57BL/6 mice form Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Benxi, China) were fed and modeled following the methods described in our prior publication (Chi et al., 2024). The study was conducted in accordance with the standards of the Regulations on the Management of Experimental Animals in China and was approved by the Laboratory Animal Committee of Jilin University. Ethics review number: YNPZSY2023069, YNPZSY2022081. All mice were first acclimatized to Lieber-DeCarli control liquid diet (Trophic Animal Feed High-tech Co. Ltd., Nantong, China) for 5 days, followed by 10 days of prophylactic treatment feeding. Then the Lieber-DeCarli alcohol liquid diet containing 5 % alcohol was feed for 10 days of modeling. On the morning of day 21, after an overnight fast, the mice were gavage with either a 5 g/kg dose of alcohol or isocaloric maltose. Nine hours later, they were anaesthetized with ether and sacrificed for sample collection.

First round: Thirty-two mice were randomly assigned to four groups with eight mice in each group — control group (CON), alcohol group (AH), SCL low-dose group (SCLL), and SCL high-dose group (SCLH). During the prophylactic feeding period, all groups were received Lieber-DeCarli control diet, and each group was gavage with either sterile water or SCL. During the molding feeding period, the CON group continued the Lieber-DeCarli control diet, while the other groups were fed the Lieber-DeCarli alcohol liquid diet and maintained their respective gavage treatments. The SCL dosage was determined based on our previous study and the Pharmacopoeia of the People's Republic of China (Xiang et al., 2022), which recommends 2–6 g of S. chinensis per day for humans, and the dosages for mice were converted based on body surface area and the extraction yield of SCL, resulting in dosages of 46.25 mg/kg for the SCLL group and 92.5 mg/kg for the SCLH group.

Second round:Thirty-two mice were randomly assigned to four groups with eight mice in each group — control group (CON), alcohol group (AH), IAA group (IAA) and IAA + CH-223191 group (I + C) (CH-223191 is an AHR-specific inhibitor that blocks AHR activation). The specific feeding and modeling procedures were consistent with those used in the first round. The dosage for IAA was set at 500 mg/kg as previously reported (Tintelnot et al., 2023), while CH-223191 was administered at a dose of 5 mg/kg (Jia et al., 2019).

Physiological and biochemical tests

Serum lipopolysaccharide (LPS) concentration was measured using a mouse lipopolysaccharide enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (CUSABIO, Hubei, China). Kits for alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), superoxide dismutase (SOD), malondialdehyde (MDA), nitric oxide synthase (NOS; colorimetric method), and triglyceride (TG) assay were obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). Six to eight samples were randomly selected from each experimental group for these measurements.

Intestinal metabolome analysis

Colon contents from the first round of animal experiments were analyzed using untargeted metabolomics via LC-MS. The analysis was commissioned to Suzhou PANOMIX Biomedical Tech Co., Ltd (Suzhou, China), with details provided in the Supporting Information.

Gut microbiome analysis

Total genomic DNA was extracted from the ileum contents of mice from both rounds of animal experiments using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method. The 16S rDNA gene (V3–V4 region) was amplified using specific primers (341F and 806R), and the library was sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform. Initial amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were generated, with ASVs of abundance <5 filtered out. Data comparison and analysis were performed using QIIME2 by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Gene expression quantification

Total RNA was extracted from liver, ileum, and colon samples using the TransZol Up Plus RNA kit (TransGen, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Reverse transcription and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) were conducted using the PerfectStart Uni RT & qPCR kit (TransGen). Experimental procedures and conditions were based on previously published studies (Chi et al., 2024). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Six to eight samples were randomly selected from each group for analysis.

Relative quantification of intestinal L. Reuteri

Relative quantification of L. reuteri in ileum samples was performed using 16S rDNA sequenced genome samples by qPCR. Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 3. Experimental procedures followed the methods described in a previously published study (Y. Chen et al., 2023).

Protein expression analysis

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from liver and colon tissues. Primary antibodies: CYP1A1 (13241-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Relative protein levels were quantified using fluorescent secondary antibodies (Bioss, Beijing, China) and analyzed with ImageJ software.

Immunostaining

Samples from the ileum and colon were collected and immediately fixed in 4 % formalin. Paraffin embedding, sectioning, and subsequent immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry were performed by Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Semi-quantitatively analysis of the results was performed using ImageJ software. The antibodies used were AHR (A1451) and IL22 (A24612) from Abclonal (Wuhan, China), and ZO1 (21773-1-AP) and OCCLUDIN (27260-1-AP) from Proteintech.

Histopathological evaluation

Liver and ileum tissues from each group were fixed in 4 % formalin. Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining was commissioned to Servicebio.

Bacteria cultures

Lactobacillus and Clostridium strains isolated and preserved in the laboratory were streaked and activated on MRS solid medium, and Bacteroides strains were streaked and activated on RCM solid medium.

Analysis of trp metabolism capacity

Trp was added to MRS and RCM liquid media, and 100 μL of each bacterial culture (108 CFU/mL) was inoculated. After anaerobic incubation at 37°C for 24–48 h, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation and analyzed by HPLC to determine Trp metabolism and IAA production. The procedure was adapted from previous studies and with minor modifications (Chen et al., 2018). Detailed methods are provided in the Supporting Information.

Analysis of the growth-promoting capacity of SCL

An L. reuteri culture in the logarithmic growth phase (100 μL) was inoculated into 10 mL of MRS liquid medium, supplemented with either high-dose SCL or sterilized water. Cultures were incubated under anaerobic conditions, and growth curves were monitored at OD600 nm.

Based on previous studies, 0.3 % SCL was used as a glucose substitute in MRS medium (Pan et al., 2023). After inoculation, samples were collected every 6 h, serially diluted, and plated onto MRS solid medium. HPLC was used in conjunction with the standard curve in Method 2.2 to analyze whether the lignans components in the medium were consumed. Detailed methods are provided in the Supporting Information.

Caco-2 cell culture

A model of normal Caco-2 cells treated with IAA was established, specifically, Caco-2 cells were treated with 200 mM IAA for 12 h. Prior to treatment, the cells were cultured in MEM medium supplemented with 10 % heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 1 % penicillin-streptomycin, and 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA). Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and cultured until adherence.

Statistical analysis

Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation from at least three independent experiments. Significant differences among groups were determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Fisher's least significant difference (LSD) test and Duncan's multiple range test. Significant differences between two groups were analyzed using independent sample t-tests. Graphs were created using GraphPad Prism 8, with the significance level set at p < 0.05.

Results

Compositional identification of SCL

Chemical analysis revealed that SCL contained 69.50 ± 0.01 % total lignans. The lignans composition were determined by UPLC-Q/TOF-MS (Fig. 1), identifying 21 lignans (Table S1). Among them, Schizandrol A, Schizandrol B, Schizantherin A, Schizandrin A, and Schizandrin B were the most abundant. HPLC quantification showed their respective contents were 22.59 %, 4.44 %, 3.46 %, 6.84 %, and 4.36 %, respectively (Fig. S1, Table S2). SCL alleviates symptoms of ALD in mice

To evaluate the hepatoprotective activity of SCL, a mouse model of ALD was established using the NIAAA method, with prophylactic supplementation of two SCL doses (Fig. 2A). SCL had no adverse effects on the body weight throughout the experimental period (Fig. 2B). Physiological and biochemical analysis showed that alcohol consumption significantly increased serum AST, ALT, and liver MDA levels, while reducing liver SOD activity compared with the CON group (Fig. 2C–F), confirming the successful establishment of the ALD model. SCL supplementation significantly restored these abnormalities, alleviating alcohol-induced liver damage and oxidative stress, with the high dose being more effective than the low dose. SCL protects against alcohol-induced liver lipid accumulation and inflammation

The earliest hepatic response to alcohol abuse is lipid accumulation in hepatocytes, which can progress to inflammation and fibrosis (Ceni et al., 2014). Histopathological analysis showed that both doses of SCL reduced the alcohol-induced liver fatty vacuoles and inflammatory infiltrates (Fig. 3A). Notably, high-dose SCL significantly reduced alcohol-elevated expression of sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1C (Srebp-1C), fatty acid synthase (Fasn), and acetyl-coenzyme carboxylase 1 (Acc1) in liver, three key genes involved in lipid synthesis (Fig. 3B–D), indicating that SCL attenuates fat accumulation by inhibiting excessive hepatic lipogenesis. Furthermore, high-dose SCL significantly suppressed the expression of liver pro-inflammatory factors, including Tlr4: a key receptor for recognizing alcohol metabolites and pathogenic molecular patterns released by translocation of the gut microbiota, and nuclear factor kappa-B (Nf-κb): the most important effector transcription factor downstream of Tlr4 (Szabo, 2015), which were elevated by alcohol consumption (Fig. 3E and F). Suggesting that SCL inhibits liver inflammation induced by alcohol. LPS, a harmful product of intestinal microbes, interacts with Tlr4 and activates Srebp-1C, inducing hepatic inflammation and steatosis, which are crucial factors in ALD pathogenesis (Mu et al., 2021; Schoeler and Caesar, 2019; Seitz et al., 2018). Oral SCL significantly reduced alcohol-induced serum LPS levels. (Fig. 3G). SCL protects against alcohol-induced intestinal barrier disruption

Alcohol consumption disrupts intestinal tight junctions, impairs barrier function, and increases intestinal permeability. Given the low bioavailability of SCL, with only a minor proportion absorbed in the gastrointestinal tract after oral administration (H. Xu et al., 2013), it is hypothesized that SCL reduces the circulating LPS levels by improving the intestinal microenvironment. H&E staining showed that alcohol ingestion damaged the intestinal tract, causing shortening of ileocecal villi and atrophy of the mucus layer, while SCL supplementation significantly restored these histopathological abnormalities (Fig. 4A). TJP, primarily OCCLUDIN and ZO1, form mechanical barriers between intestinal epithelial cells (Ghosh et al., 2021). The gene expression levels of Occludin, Zo1 in ileum and colon tissues were significantly reduced in the AH group compared to the CON group, but were restored by SCL supplementation, with higher doses showing greater effects (Fig. 4B and C). Based on the above findings, we further focused on the impact of high-dose SCL on the protein expression levels of these tight junction proteins in ileum and colon. The results demonstrate that the changing trends of both are consistent with the genetic level (Fig. 4D–G). SCL restores intestinal Trp metabolite IAA levels and activates the AHR pathway

Microbial metabolites are produced near the intestinal epithelium, where they directly impact epithelial cells and play a crucial role in maintaining barrier function (Ghosh et al., 2021). Based on the preceding findings where high-dose SCL demonstrated optimal recovery of biochemical parameters and enhancement of barrier function, indicating high-dose as the more effective regimen. Consequently, metabolomic analysis was conducted on intestinal contents from the CON, AH, and SCLH groups to elucidate the specific mechanisms underlying the bioactivity of high-dose SCL. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that a considerable number of differential metabolites between the CON group and the AH group were enriched in the metabolic pathway shown in Fig. 5A. It revealed that alcohol consumption disrupted multiple amino acid metabolism pathways, including Trp metabolism. Volcano plots analysis showed higher levels of nalbuphine, 3,4-dihydroxyphenylpropanoate, and IAA in the CON group, while tyramine and stearidonic acid were elevated in the AH group (Fig. 5B). Further screen out the top 30 differential metabolites with VIP values between the two groups to construct the metabolite heat map. Metabolites with higher levels in the CON group and lower levels in the AH group were classified as potentially beneficial, while those with the opposite trend were considered potentially harmful. High-dose SCL significantly increased the levels of potentially beneficial metabolites, including IAA, selenohomocysteine, 3-amino-4-hydroxybenzoate, nalbuphine, 18-Oxooleate, and lovastatin, while significantly reducing potentially harmful metabolites such as dodecanoic acid and 24-methylenecycloartanol (Fig. 5C). This metabolic shift indicates SCL promotes reversion toward a healthier intestinal metabolite profile in ALD mice. Alcohol has been reported to inhibit intestinal AHR pathway, while IAA enhances intestinal barrier function by regulating TJP expression through AHR activation (Sun et al., 2020; Wrzosek et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2021). This is illustrated in Fig. 5E. Given the restorative effect of high-dose SCL on intestinal OCCLUDIN and ZO1 expression, the gene expression and protein levels related to AHR pathway were analyzed. Among the differential metabolites significantly recovered by SCL, only IAA exhibited relevant activity, suggesting that SCL may repair alcohol-induced intestinal barrier damage primarily by increasing intestinal IAA levels and restoring AHR pathway activity.

SCL regulates gut microbiota and enriches IAA-producing bacteria

IAA is an indole derivative produced by the transformation of Trp by specific gut microorganisms (Montgomery et al., 2022). In this study, neither the modeling diet nor SCL affected intestinal Trp levels (Fig. S3A), suggesting that the effects of alcohol and SCL on IAA levels may be mediated through their effects on the gut microbiota. Microbiome analysis of intestinal contents from the three groups revealed that principal coordinate analysis visualized the regulatory effect of SCL on gut microbiota. Both the AH and high-dose SCL treatments significantly altered the gut microbial composition in mice, with SCLH restoring the community structure closer to the healthy status of the CON group (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, High-dose SCL significantly restored the alcohol-reduced Shannon index and improved the gut microbiota diversity (Fig. 6B). Among the top 10 most abundant genera, SCL inhibited harmful bacteria elevated by alcohol, including Escherichia-Shigella, Enterococcus, and Dubococcus (Fig. 6C, S3A–C), while enriching IAA-producing genera such as Lactobacillus, Bacteroides, and Clostridium (Montgomery et al., 2022; Roager and Licht, 2018). Notably, Lactobacillus and Clostridium exhibited significant increases (Fig. 6D–F). To identify the microbial source of IAA, thirteen strains from the above three genera were cultivated in an in vitro Trp-added environment. Both L. reuteri and L. casei metabolized Trp to produce IAA, with L. reuteri showing a greater production capacity (Fig. 6G). qPCR using strain-specific primers showed that the relative abundance of L. reuteri was significantly higher in the SCLH group than in the AH group, while L. casei was not detected (Fig. 6H). Similarly, 16S rDNA sequencing confirmed that high-dose SCL restored the alcohol-suppressed abundance of L. reuteri (Fig. S3D). To further investigate the interaction between L. reuteri and SCL, L. reuteri was co-cultured with SCL in vitro, with SCL replacing glucose in MRS medium. Within 24 h, SCL was nearly depleted (Fig. 6I), and the viable count of L. reuteri increased during this period (Fig. 6J). Furthermore, the addition of SCL to the culture medium significantly promoted the proliferation of L. reuteri in vitro (Fig. 6K). These findings highlight L. reuteri as a pivotal probiotic for elevating intestinal IAA levels in response to SCL.

IAA prevents ALD via the AHR pathway

An experiment was conducted to investigate the ALD-preventive effects of intestinal IAA raised by SCL and to verify its hepatoprotective activity via the AHR pathway using the AHR inhibitor CH-223191 (Fig. 7A). IAA significantly upregulated the expression of AHR pathway downstream genes, CYP1A1 and OCCLUDIN in Caco-2 cells (Fig. S4A and B). In vivo, IAA gavage prevented ALD-induced liver injury, as shown by reduced liver fat vacuoles and attenuated inflammatory infiltrates (Fig. 7B). Compared to the AH group, IAA significantly reduced serum AST, ALT, and LPS levels, as well as liver index (Fig. 7C–F), while suppressing hepatic expression of inflammatory genes Tlr4 and Nf-κb (Fig. S5A and B). Similarly to SCL, IAA significantly increased the expression of colonic Ahr and Cyp1a1 (Fig. 7G and H), as well as Zo1 and Occludin (Fig. S5C and D). It also enhanced the protein levels of CYP1A1 (Fig. 7I and J), AHR (Fig. 7K and M), and IL22 (Fig. S5E and G), thereby activating the colon AHR pathway in ALD mice. Immunohistochemical staining further revealed that alcohol-reduced protein levels of OCCLUDIN and ZO1 in the colon (Fig. 7L and N) and ileum (Fig. S5F and H) were significantly restored by IAA. However, co-administration of CH223191 abolished IAA's ability to activate the AHR pathway, as well as its intestinal barrier-restoring and ALD-preventive effects. IAA regulates alcohol-induced gut microbiota disorders via the AHR pathway

The overall effects of oral IAA on the gut microbiota of ALD mice were evaluated. Principal coordinates analysis revealed that the IAA group exhibited microbial community structure most closely resembling that of the healthy CON group, followed by the AH and I + C groups, respectively (Fig. 8A). This pattern indicates a restorative effect of IAA on gut ecology. IAA restored gut microbiota richness, as indicated by a significant increase in the alcohol-reduced Chao1 index (Fig. 8B). At the genus level, the microbial composition in the IAA group was like that of the CON group, whereas the I + C group resembled the AH group (Fig. 8C). IAA significantly reduced the abundance of Escherichia-Shigella and increased beneficial bacteria, incluidng Lactobacillus, Faecalibaculum, and Akkermansia (Fig. S6A–D). However, co-administration of CH223191 reduced the Chao1 index to AH group levels and abolished IAA's effects, including its inhibition of Escherichia-Shigella and promotion of beneficial intestinal bacteria. Instead, CH223191 gavage promoted the proliferation of the harmful bacterium Enterococcus (Fig. S6E). Overall, IAA administration favorably modulated the intestinal microbiota in ALD mice in an AHR pathway-dependent manner. Discussion

S. chinensis lignans are biologically safe and have demonstrated remarkable hepatoprotective potential (Ding et al., 2019), Studies on S. chinensis have demonstrated that lignans extracts are the most effective active components in reducing liver lipid accumulation (Liu et al., 2015), exhibit potent anti-inflammatory activity both in vitro and in vivo (Yang et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2016). The experimental procedure of this study is shown in Fig. S7, where total lignans were extracted from S. chinensis seeds. The findings demonstrated that SCL alleviated liver lipid accumulation and inflammation induced by alcohol. and preliminarily confirm the ALD-preventive effects of SCL. Notably, SCL improved intestinal barrier function and reduced LPS translocation into the bloodstream by increasing intestinal TJP expression. This mechanism underlies SCL's ability to inhibit ALD progression. Schisandrin C has been reported to regulate TJP expression and improve intestinal permeability abnormalities, although the precise mechanism remains unclear (Kim et al., 2022).

Microbial metabolites are important mediators for gut microbiota in maintaining intestinal barrier integrity (Singh et al., 2019). To examine the intestinal barrier protective mechanism of SCL, a metabolome analysis was conducted. The results revealed that alcohol disrupted the host Trp metabolic pathway, significantly reducing the intestinal Trp metabolite IAA levels. Similarly, impairments in Trp metabolism and decreased levels of indole derivatives, including IAA, have been observed in both alcoholic hepatitis mice and patients (Hendrikx et al., 2019; Wrzosek et al., 2021). However, the effects of S. chinensis lignans on intestinal metabolites in ALD remain unclear. This study demonstrated that high-dose SCL significantly increased intestinal IAA levels. Evidence indicates that reduced intestinal AHR pathway activity, triggered by attenuated Trp-indole metabolism, leads to intestinal barrier dysfunction and promotes ALD progression. Conversely, activation of the AHR pathway alleviates ALD by restoring the intestinal barrier integrity (Hendrikx et al., 2019; Qian et al., 2022). IAA functions as an agonist of intestinal AHR signaling, regulating intestinal barrier function (Yang et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2021a, Gu et al., 2021b). Importantly, SCL restored alcohol-inhibited AHR pathway activity, and upregulated TJP expression. These findings suggest that IAA is a pivotal metabolite in response to SCL and may restore intestinal barrier function by activating the intestinal AHR pathway.

Reports on IAA activity remain controversial. Previous studies suggested that IAA may contribute to liver inflammation (Fu et al., 2024), while increasing evidence highlights its beneficial effects, including inhibition of liver inflammation and lipid accumulation via the liver AHR pathway (Xu et al., 2021), and anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities independent of the AHR pathway (Ji et al., 2020). This study confirmed that IAA supplementation prevents ALD-associated liver injury through the intestinal AHR pathway. In addition, the impact of IAA on gut microbiota has been rarely explored. In this study, IAA positively modulated gut microbial composition in ALD mice. However, its beneficial effects were abolished when co-administered with an AHR inhibitor, leading instead to the proliferation of the harmful bacteria such as Enterococcus and Escherichia-Shigella. These findings provide robust evidence that IAA prevents ALD via the AHR pathway.

Gut microbes regulate the metabolism of Trp into IAA and other indole derivatives (Gu et al., 2021a, Gu et al., 2021b; Wang et al., 2022), and promoting the proliferation of Trp-metabolizing bacteria is an effective strategy to enhance the production of indole derivatives, which act as AHR agonists. Consequently, further microbiome analysis was conducted with the objective of establishing a correlation between gut microbiota and IAA. Alcohol disrupts intestinal microecology, leading to increased abundance of pathogenic bacteria, such as Enterococcus, and reduced levels of beneficial bacteria, such as Lactobacillus, which produce AHR ligands in AH patients (Wang et al., 2021). Escherichia-Shigella and Enterococcus are recognized as typical harmful bacteria in ALD, while Dubococcus is positively associated with inflammation (Duan et al., 2019; Jiang et al., 2022). Our previous study demonstrated that S. chinensis crude extract regulated the gut microbiota homeostasis in alcoholic hepatitis mice (Xiang et al., 2022). S. chinensis lignans also increased the intestinal Lactobacillus and Bacteroides levels in depressed mice (Song et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2021). The microbiome analysis revealed that high-dose SCL decreased the abundance of Escherichia-Shigella, Enterococcus, and Dubococcus. Notably, ethanol-reduced abundance of L. reuteri was restored. Lactobacillus, particularly L. reuteri, expresses enzymes necessary for Trp metabolism to IAA (Montgomery et al., 2022; Roager and Licht, 2018), as confirmed by the in vitro results of this study. Additionally, L. reuteri was capable of metabolizing SCL, and its proliferation was enhanced when SCL was used as a carbon source or supplemented in vitro. Studies have demonstrated that Lactobacillus strains metabolize phenolic compounds through enzymatic activity, with the metabolic process and resultant bioactive metabolites potentially facilitating their auto proliferation (Tabasco et al., 2011). Furthermore, multiple Lactobacillus species exhibit lignan-metabolizing capabilities (Ruiz de la Bastida et al., 2021). These results prove that the increased intestinal IAA levels was attributed to the enrichment of L. reuteri by SCL.

This study confirmed that SCL possesses gut microbiota modulatory activity in ALD model and identified, for the first time, L. reuteri as the key gut microbe mediating SCL's hepatoprotective effect. Furthermore, it established a causal relationship between SCL enrichment of L. reuteri, the consequent elevation in IAA levels, and the prevention of ALD. Crucially, we innovatively revealed the central role of the Trp metabolite IAA - AHR pathway in SCL's ALD preventive activity, emphasizing the restoration of intestinal AHR signaling as a novel approach for ALD prevention. This work elevated SCL from a hepatoprotective traditional Chinese medicine component into a gut microbiota-targeting functional food ingredient, offering a new dietary intervention strategy for ALD prevention.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study confirms that SCL prevents ALD by regulating gut microbiota. SCL enriches IAA-producing bacteria-L. reuteri, restores intestinal IAA levels, activates the colonic AHR pathway, repairs alcohol-damaged intestinal barriers, and reduces circulating LPS levels, thereby preventing ALD progression. These findings provide a foundation for the development of SCL as a dietary supplement for ALD prevention, promoting the sustainable utilization of S. chinensis resources.
