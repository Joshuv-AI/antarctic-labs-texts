The Impact of Socioeconomic Status on Timely Presentation and Treatment in Pediatric Hand Fractures
Authors: Henok G. Haile, Rebecca D. Aguiar, Patrice Melvin, Valerie Ward, Andrea S. Bauer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPediatric hand fractures are common, yet disparities in their management and outcomes remain underexplored. The purpose of this study is to investigate the influence of socioeconomic status (SES) and health insurance status on time-to-presentation, treatment, and outcomes for pediatric hand fractures.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of pediatric patients who presented with hand fractures between January 2021 and December 2022, identified through electronic health records. Patient demographics, date of presentation, treatment (operative vs non-operative), and outcomes were assessed. Multivariable regression analyses were used to evaluate delayed presentation (>8 days from the initial injury, defined as 75th percentile of days to presentation) and need for operative intervention based on sociodemographics, Child Opportunity Index (COI) level (a proxy for SES), and fracture characteristics.ResultsWe included 300 patients (median age: 14 years [interquartile range: 13–16]; 76.7% male). Of these, 63 patients (21.0%) experienced delayed presentation, and 38 patients (12.7%) underwent operative intervention. forty-fiveIn total 145, patients (48.3%) had private insurance and 155 (51.7%) had public insurance. The multivariable analysis showed no statistically significant differences in delayed presentation based on sex, race, primary language, or type of insurance. However, patients living in neighborhoods with very low to moderate COI were found to have a significant delay in time to presentation compared to those with high to very high COI.ConclusionsIn this retrospective cohort of pediatric patients with hand fractures, those with lower COI were more likely to experience delayed presentation. No significant differences in time to presentation and intervention type (operative vs non-operative) were observed based on sex, insurance type, primary language, or race/ethnicity. These findings underscore the importance of socioeconomic measures such as COI as separate from race and ethnicity data and highlight the need for focused interventions to ensure equitable care for all pediatric patients.Key Concepts(1)Pediatric patients from low or very low Child Opportunity Index scores are more likely to have a delayed presentation after finger and hand fractures.(2)The observed difference is independent of race, ethnicity, language, and insurance.(3)Care pathways are needed to improve access for these underserved patients with acute injuries.Level of EvidenceIV, Case series

Introduction

There is a growing body of research focused on identifying health care disparities in orthopaedics with a goal to develop solutions to provide patients with equitable care. Despite the growing attention over the years and implementations of new policies such as the Affordable Care Act (ACA) [1], disparities in patient outcomes based on socioeconomic and insurance status continue to persist [2]. Numerous studies have shown that pediatric patients in families with low socioeconomic status (SES) experience significant delays in receiving orthopaedic care and have worse clinical outcomes [3,4]. For example, Patel et al. demonstrated that pediatric patients with anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries who live in neighborhoods with low SES were more likely to experience a delay in diagnosis, increasing their risk for long-term complications such as decreased knee range of motion [5].

Despite the expansion of health insurance coverage for people with low SES through policies such as the ACA [1] and Children’s Health Insurance Program [6], disparities persist within orthopaedics based on insurance status as access to care continues to be a challenge for those with public insurance [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. Several studies have demonstrated that patients who are publicly insured have significantly longer wait times for orthopaedic evaluation than patients who have private insurance [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Collectively, these studies highlight that simply having health insurance does not guarantee ease of access to orthopaedic providers.

For many orthopaedic injuries, delayed presentation for the initial evaluation has implications for a more difficult recovery. James et al. showed pediatric patients with ACL injury who had a delay in reconstruction for >12 weeks had an increased risk of meniscal injuries and irreparable meniscal tears [11]. Similarly, it has been shown that patients with low SES have delayed rotator cuff repairs, associated with higher retear rates and more complications in recovery [[12], [13], [14]].

Finger fractures are the most common type of fracture in childhood, with an incidence of 27 per 1,000 patients [15]. These injuries typically result from sports-related trauma, falls, crush or entrapment injuries, or lacerations. The incidence varies with age, peaking between 10 and 14 year of age, and occurs more frequently in males than in females [16,17]. While most fractures can be managed non-operatively through immobilization, timely evaluation and recognition are imperative for optimal outcomes. Surgical intervention is generally reserved for cases involving open fractures, significant angulation, displacement, or marked rotational deformity. Delay or inadequate treatment can lead to several complications such as extensor tendon adhesion, joint stiffness, malunion and decreased function [18].

The purposes of this study are to 1) evaluate the relationship between socioeconomic and health insurance status and time-to-presentation for pediatric hand fractures and 2) determine whether delayed presentation is associated with increased need for operative interventions in this cohort.

Materials and methods

Institutional review board approval was obtained from our institution. Medical records of pediatric patients aged 0–18 years who were diagnosed with fractures distal to the carpus and treated in the orthopaedic clinic of a single pediatric hospital over a 2-year period. Exclusion criteria included patients with open fractures, age ≥19 years, and those who did not have at least one follow-up visit.

The primary exposure variables were race/ethnicity, SES and insurance type (private versus public). SES was determined using the proxy measure of Child Opportunity Index (COI 2.0) [19]. COI 2.0 is a composite metric of resources and conditions describing where a child lives using 29 child-relevant census tract-level measures within three domains: education, health and environment, and social and economic. By ranking census tracts by overall average z-scores, five quintiles of COI levels are created (very low, low, moderate, high, and very high). The primary outcome measure included time from injury to presentation. Delayed presentation was defined as >8 days from injury date to date of clinic presentation which represented the 75th percentile of days to presentation for our cohort. Secondary outcomes included treatment types (operative vs non-operative) and any complications. Complications were defined as need for reoperation, infection requiring antibiotics, stiffness requiring occupation therapy, and nonunion requiring treatment.

Data were extracted from electronic health records, including patient sociodemographic (age at time of injury, sex, race/ethnicity, insurance, primary language), neighborhood factors (residence zip code, distance in miles from residence to clinic), fracture characteristics, and treatment timeline. Patients were included from the hospital system’s downtown location as well as two suburban satellites in this study. Distance was calculated from the patient’s residence to the clinic they attended. Patient zip codes were merged with the COI database to determine neighborhood level socioeconomic indicators (represented by COI quintiles). Because of sample size considerations, we aggregated the COI-level quintiles into a binary group consisting of high/very high vs moderate/low/very low COI categories.

Statistical methods

We used summary statistics to characterize our study cohort. In the univariate analyses, associations of patient sociodemographic characteristics, neighborhood factors, and clinical factors with the outcome of delayed presentation (>8 days post injury) were assessed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables. We used similar methods described to assess factors that may be associated with operative outcomes.

To identify factors associated with the two outcomes of delayed presentation and need for an operative procedure, we used multivariable backward selection logistic regression models specifying a Wald chi-square of 0.1 for dependent variable retention. In the initial models, we included patient sociodemographic data (sex, age, race and ethnicity, insurance, preferred language) and COI group. Additionally, we included clinical characteristics with a P value <.2 in the bivariate analyses. We performed all analyses using SAS v.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

As a retrospective study, the sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients treated during the study period, and interim power calculations were performed to assess the minimum number of patients necessary to detect clinically meaningful difference in outcomes.

Operative intervention

Thirty-eight patients (12.7%) required operative intervention for their fractures. The likelihood of operative intervention varied based on clinical factors. In bivariate analyses, delayed presentation, proximal phalanx fracture location, head segment, intra-articular, absence of physis involvement, and displaced fractures were all associated with undergoing an operative procedure (P < .05) Table 3.

In the multivariable logistic regression model evaluating predictors of operative management, displaced fractures (OR: 16.17, CI: 6.61–39.5, P < .001), proximal phalanx fractures (OR: 3.51, CI: 1.45–8.53, P = .006), and intra-articular fractures (OR: 10.42, CI: 3.42–31.8, P < .001) were independently associated with increased likelihood of surgical treatment. Base segment fractures were less likely to result in need for operative procedure (OR: 0.11, CI: 0.04–0.32, P < .001). Discussion

In this retrospective study of 300 patients, we examined the impact of insurance status and socioeconomic factors on time to presentation and treatment modality in pediatric hand fractures. Patients from lower COI areas were almost two times more likely to experience delayed presentation than their higher COI peers, underscoring the impact of socioeconomic disparities on health care access and timeliness of care. While the socioeconomic burden experienced by families in lower COI areas has been found to act as a barrier to receiving timely surgical care in other studies, our study found no difference in surgical rates based on COI [[20], [21], [22]].

While univariate results found that decreasing distance to the clinic was associated with delayed presentation, this did not hold in the multivariate analysis and was likely a confounding factor. Even though suburban satellites were included in our analysis, the area closest to each hospital location does have a lower COI than the adjacent towns, which may explain this finding.

Hand fractures are among the most common fractures in the pediatric population [23,24,25]. Our cohort of 300 patients demonstrated a male predominance (76.7%) and a median age at injury of 14 years, consistent with prior literature linking increased fracture rates in male adolescents to greater participation in high-risk activities and contact sports [26].

In addition, our analysis found that patients with physeal fractures presented earlier than patients who did not have physeal fractures, possibly related to the younger age of patients with physeal fractures by nature of their skeletal immaturity. As seen with other several studies, this is likely pointing to the difference in health care–seeking pattern for pediatric patients as they get older. In a retrospective study of 600 pediatric patients, Jamal et al. found that older pediatric patients were less likely than their younger counterparts to obtain follow-up orthopaedic care after an emergency department (ED) visit [27]. Similarly, Gupta et al. found an association between age and delayed orthopaedic follow-up in a retrospective study of 516 pediatric patients treated in an ED [28]. It is interesting that our study found a similar issue with delayed presentation of older children for patients who did not necessarily visit the ED prior to their orthopaedic visit.

Regarding management, most fractures (87.3%) were treated non-operatively, similar to other studies of pediatric finger fracture interventions. [25,26,29]. Surgical intervention was required in 12.7% of cases, primarily through closed reduction percutaneous pinning. While the pattern of delayed presentation requiring more invasive intervention has been seen in other orthopaedic conditions, we did not find that to be the case in our cohort. [11,14,30]. This may be because invasive interventions are overall quite uncommon in pediatric hand injuries, and only a small percentage of our cohort underwent surgery at all. This low rate of surgical intervention meant that we had very low numbers to evaluate differences such as fractures requiring an open reduction instead of percutaneous reduction, for example, because of delayed presentation.

This study also examined the influence of race/ethnicity, insurance status, and geographic factors on time to presentation and management outcomes. Prior research has demonstrated that Black and Hispanic patients, as well as publicly insured patients, are more likely to experience delays in care and worse outcomes in orthopaedic settings [31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37], [38]. However, these associations were not observed in our cohort. No statistically significant differences were observed in either delayed presentation or clinical outcomes based on race/ethnicity or insurance status.

While these findings contribute to the growing body of knowledge on pediatric hand fractures, our study has several inherent limitations related to its retrospective design. Selection bias may be present due to reliance on existing medical records and the nonrandomized nature of the cohort. Furthermore, the study was conducted at a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader population. We were also limited to information regarding the initial presentation such as whether patients initially went to their local urgent care or other treatment facilities before presenting to our hospital. Despite these limitations, the study provides clinically relevant insights that can inform future research and clinical care.

Conclusion

In this retrospective study of pediatric patients with hand fractures, while those with lower COI were more likely to experience delayed presentation, COI was not associated with the need for surgical intervention. No significant differences in time to presentation, intervention type, or complications were observed based on sex, insurance type, primary language, or race/ethnicity. These findings underscore the importance of socioeconomic measures such as COI as separate from race and ethnicity data and highlight the need for focused interventions to ensure equitable care for all pediatric patients.
