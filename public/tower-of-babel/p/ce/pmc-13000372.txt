A high-throughput method for quantifying relative telomere length in single cells in situ combined with live-cell imaging
Authors: Qingyang Sun, Zhengzhi Zou, Mingwei Min, Xing Liu
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Most somatic cells undergo a culture crisis after ∼50 divisions, a phenomenon known as the ‘Hayflick limit’ (Hayflick and Moorhead, 1961). Since its discovery, this process has been attributed to the progressive shortening of telomeres—repetitive DNA sequences and associated proteins that protect chromosome ends. Due to the end-replication problem, telomeres gradually shorten with each cell division (Allsopp et al., 1995). However, certain cell types, such as embryonic stem cells, germ cells, and some cancer cells, counteract this shortening through telomerase or alternative lengthening mechanisms (Shay, 2016). In somatic cells, the absence of telomerase activity results in telomere attrition until a critical threshold is reached, triggering cell cycle arrest or apoptosis (Artandi and Attardi, 2005). Therefore, telomere length serves as a well-established marker for cellular aging and proliferative capacity (Xu and Teixeira, 2019).

Given the central role of telomeres in aging, telomere extension has been an anti-aging strategy with intensive research. Indeed, telomerase overexpression can extend the proliferative lifespan of somatic cells (Blasco, 2005). Conversely, in cancer cells, suppressing telomere maintenance—such as by reducing telomere repeat-containing RNA (TERRA), an RNA molecule involved in telomere protection—can induce cancer cell death (Silva et al., 2022). Additionally, telomere length varies with pluripotency state in human embryonic stem cells, further underscoring its biological significance (Wang et al., 2017). Beyond telomere length, aging cells exhibit dynamical alterations, such as morphological changes and frequent mitotic skipping (Suzuki et al., 2012; Yadav et al., 2021). To better understand how these processes influence telomere, it would be valuable to integrate telomere length measurement with live-cell imaging.

Traditional methods for measuring telomere length, such as quantitative PCR (qPCR) and terminal restriction fragment (TRF) analysis by Southern blotting, are limited to population-level comparisons (Aubert et al., 2012). While flow-fluorescence in situ hybridization (Flow-FISH) enables single-cell level analysis (Hultdin et al., 1998), it loses spatial information during sample processing, making it incompatible with live-cell imaging.

To overcome these limitations and enable single-cell correlation between telomere length and cellular dynamics, we combine live-cell imaging with high-throughput quantitative FISH, termed LiHt-QFISH. This image-based method allows high-throughput quantification of telomere lengths in individual cells while preserving spatial context. Moreover, it enables direct observation of how telomere length relates to cellular events. After validating this approach, we applied it to investigate telomere length dynamics in mouse embryonic stem (mES) cell subpopulations.

The method involves high-throughput time-lapse microscopy of cells expressing the histone 2B (H2B)–fluorescence protein fusion and other live-cell reporters of choice. Following the final imaging time point, cells are immediately fixed, processed for telomere FISH, and reimaged. Cell lineages are tracked through the H2B channel and single-cell dynamics of interest are extracted. By registering the H2B images before and after FISH, we can match the telomere FISH signals to each cell’s lineage history and dynamical behavior (Figure 1A).

The telomere length quantification is based on the principle that longer telomeres contain more TTAGGG repeats, leading to stronger probe binding and fluorescence intensity. After the hybridization of telomere-specific probes, images are taken with z-stack to ensure that all telomere foci are captured. The quantification process involves three steps: image correction, image segmentation, and signal extraction (Figure 1B). First, maximum intensity projection (MIP) is applied to optimize the visualization of telomere signals within the cell. The resulting images are corrected for camera dark noise, illumination bias, and extracellular background (see Supplementary Materials and methods). Cellular background is further subtracted using the morphological image opening operation. This process enhances the clarity of the foreground and enables subsequent quantitative analysis. Second, for cell nuclei segmentation, a well-established method is employed (Schmidt et al., 2018). For telomere segmentation, since telomere signals appear as bright puncta with well-defined boundaries, thresholding can be applied to binarize the image and isolate the telomere signals (Figure 1C). The threshold can be determined using the Otsu method (Otsu, 1979). Each telomere is then assigned to a nucleus based on the spatial overlap of their masks, enabling the quantification of telomere signals at the single-cell level.

To evaluate the accuracy and reliability of the telomere signals extracted using this method, we performed multiple verification steps. Initial validation confirmed that our quantitative data faithfully reproduced the raw FISH staining patterns, clearly showing lower signal intensities in Cell-2 and Cell-3 compared to Cell-1 and Cell-4 (Figure 1D and E). The four cells represent those derived from the same lineage, hinting that mES cells may asymmetrically segregate more damaged telomeres into one daughter cell lineage—similar to the asymmetric partitioning of damaged components in yeast (Aguilaniu et al., 2003), which can be further explored using this method. Furthermore, biological validation using human airway basal cells demonstrated that early passage cells exhibited significantly brighter telomeric foci than late passage cells, consistent with the knowledge that primary cells progressively shorten telomere over passages (Figure 1F). Moreover, telomere intensity was enhanced in basal cells overexpressing human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT) (Figure 1G), consistent with the telomere lengthening role of hTERT (Ulaner et al., 2001). These results are consistent with telomere length measured by qPCR-based methods (Figure 1H and I), demonstrating the reliability of LiHt-QFISH.

We further applied LiHt-QFISH to mES cells for interrogating telomere dynamics in the two-cell-like (2C-like) state. Approximately 1% of mES cells cultured in vitro transiently enter this state, which resembles the two-cell stage embryos and is marked by the expression of MERVL and Zscan4 (Macfarlan et al., 2012). Previous studies suggest that telomere extension occurs upon entering this state (Zalzman et al., 2010). While we also observed overall telomere elongation in the 2C-like cells (Figure 1J and K), the precise timing remained unclear. Through the live-cell imaging of 2C-like reporter followed by telomere FISH, we find that telomeres are longer only in cells exiting from the 2C-like state. Conversely, cells entering the 2C-like state exhibit shorter telomeres compared to MERVL– mES cells. These results indicate that telomere elongation occurs specifically during exit from, rather than entry into, the 2C-like state (Figure 1L and M). This conclusion is unattainable with other high-throughput methods, as the exit from the 2C-like state is a dynamic process characterized by gradual attenuation of 2C-state marker fluorescence, which cannot be resolved by snapshots. This result also suggests that cells with shorter telomeres are prone to enter the 2C-like state, potentially as a mechanism for telomere repair. Supporting this notion, chemically induced DNA damage is known to promote the transition into the 2C-like state (Atashpaz et al., 2020; Canat et al., 2023). Thus, telomere shortening may trigger the entry into a 2C-like state by activating a DNA damage response, where exposed chromosome ends are recognized as DNA damage sites (Blasco, 2005).

In summary, we have established a novel platform that integrates live-cell imaging with high-throughput single-cell telomere analysis. Validation of this approach confirms its ability to accurately quantify relative telomere lengths at the single-cell level from FISH images. Moreover, the method maintains practical accessibility, requiring only a 20× objective lens for reliable imaging while achieving remarkable throughput. Most notably, the method demonstrates a throughput of quantifying 3000 cells per minute on a single CPU core. This combination of single-cell resolution, technical accessibility, and high throughput makes our approach particularly valuable for investigating telomere dynamics in diverse biological contexts.
