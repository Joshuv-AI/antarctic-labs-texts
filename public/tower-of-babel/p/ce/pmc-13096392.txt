Porphyromonas gingivalis produces a functional HemH ferrochelatase important for its survival in a heme-limited environment
Authors: Michał Śmiga, Ewa Roszkiewicz, Natalia Wojtal, Teresa Olczak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPorphyromonas gingivalis, the keystone pathogen responsible for dysbiosis in the oral microbiome, the development of periodontal diseases, and the contribution to systemic comorbidities, is a heme auxotroph. It encodes only four enzymes in the heme biosynthesis pathway (HemD, HemN, HemG, and HemH). Comparative genomic analyses demonstrated that, while many Bacteroidota members have lost most of the canonical heme biosynthetic enzymes, Porphyromonas species uniquely retained a ferrochelatase (HemH) homolog. This study aimed to functionally characterize the P. gingivalis HemH protein to prove its hypothesized role in heme formation. HemH can bind heme and PPIX and insert iron or manganese ions into the PPIX ring. The deletion of the hemH gene triggers a heme-starvation response characterized by transcriptional activation of heme uptake systems and virulence-associated genes, coupled with repression of transport and surface proteins preferentially expressed in heme-rich conditions. Therefore, it is proposed that HemH may play a role in maintaining proper heme homeostasis. Introduction

Porphyromonas gingivalis is recognized as the keystone pathogen responsible for dysbiosis in the human oral microbiome and the development of periodontal diseases1–3. Periodontal diseases belong to a group of infectious inflammatory diseases that destroy tooth-supporting tissues, cause gum bleeding, and eventually may lead to tooth loss2–6. Importantly, the presence of P. gingivalis, its outer membrane vesicles, or DNA have been detected in host niches other than the oral cavity (e.g., plasma, synovial fluid, atherosclerotic plaque, brain)7–9 contributes to the development and progression of systemic comorbidities8,10–12. P. gingivalis can also infect host cells (e.g., gingival epithelial cells or cells of the immune system), and can survive and multiply within them13–17.

Iron and heme are indispensable for all living organisms, as they serve as crucial cofactors in fundamental biochemical processes18. However, like many members of the Bacteroidota phylum, P. gingivalis neither synthesizes siderophores nor utilizes xenosiderophores19; therefore, it relies mainly on heme as an iron source. Most bacteria synthesize heme from glutamate using the protoporphyrin IX (PPIX)-dependent pathway, the coproporphyrin III-dependent pathway, or alternative pathways20–23. In the last step of the PPIX-dependent heme biosynthesis pathway, due to the ferrochelatase activity, iron is inserted into PPIX to produce heme b (protoheme IX). However, many members of the Bacteroidota phylum, including P. gingivalis, are heme auxotrophs lacking the full functional heme biosynthesis pathway19,24–26. In most of these bacteria, some genes encoding homologs of the enzymes engaged in heme biosynthesis are conserved27. In P. gingivalis, four genes encoding homologous proteins to those involved in the final steps of the PPIX-dependent heme biosynthesis pathway, namely uroporphyrinogen III synthase (HemD), oxygen-independent coproporphyrinogen III oxidase (HemN), oxygen-independent protoporphyrinogen IX dehydrogenase (HemG), and ferrochelatase (HemH), are encoded.

As is typical for many host-associated pathogenic bacteria, P. gingivalis acquires heme in vitro from culture media and in vivo from host hemoproteins28,29. In the periodontal pocket, heme availability changes during the progression of periodontal disease. Heme is extremely limited in healthy individuals and at the early stage of periodontal disease. Therefore, the main source of heme comes from the lysis of bacteria residing in the periodontal pocket30–32. At this stage of infection, P. gingivalis also utilizes heme from the gingival crevicular fluid, a complex mixture comprising hemoproteins derived from serum (mainly albumin-heme complex and hemopexin), and from leukocytes and structural cells of the periodontium33. With the development of periodontal disease, hemoglobin becomes the main source of heme, which is associated with the deepening of the periodontal pockets, weakening of the tooth-supporting tissues, and bleeding34–37.

The main and best-characterized heme acquisition mechanism in P. gingivalis is the Hmu system (HmuYRSTUV) encoded on the hmu operon, which is characteristic of the Bacteroidota phylum28,29,38,39. Uptaken heme is then incorporated into respective proteins and may also serve as an iron source. P. gingivalis can also grow in culture media without added heme but supplemented with PPIX and inorganic iron17,40,41. Therefore, it was hypothesized that heme can be formed in the P. gingivalis cell from PPIX and iron due to the HemH ferrochelatase activity. However, studies on the partial heme biosynthesis pathway in P. gingivalis are limited, and the role of the preserved proteins has not been elucidated. This study aimed to functionally characterize the P. gingivalis HemH protein to prove its hypothesized role in heme formation.

Materials and methods

Porphyromonas gingivalis strains and growth conditions

Porphyromonas gingivalis wild-type (W83), mutant (ΔhemH), and complemented (ΔhemH+hemH) strains (Table 1) were grown at 37℃ in anaerobic conditions (80% N2, 10% CO2, 10% H2) in a Whitley A35 anaerobic workstation (Bingley, UK) on Schaedler anaerobe blood agar plates (ABA, Argenta, Poznań, Poland) composed of 10.0 g/L tryptone soya broth, 5.0 g/L peptone, 5.0 g/L yeast extract, 5.0 g/L glucose, 4.0 g/L NaCl, 0.4 g/L cysteine HCl, 10 mg/L hemin, 0.75 g/L Tris, 2 mg/L vitamin K1, 13.5 g/L agar supplemented with 5% sheep blood for 3–5 days, and then in a liquid basal medium (BM) composed of 30.0 g/L trypticase soy broth (Becton Dickinson, Sparks, MD, USA), 5.0 g/L yeast extract (Biomaxima) 0.05 mg/L vitamin K3 (Fluka, Munich, Germany), and 0.5 g/L L-cysteine (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). To grow bacteria in iron and heme-rich conditions, BM was supplemented with 7.7 µM hemin chloride (Pol-Aura, Morąg, Poland) (BM + Hm). To analyze the influence of heme starvation, bacteria were first grown on BM agar plates composed of BM + Hm medium, supplemented with 15.0 g/L Bacteriological LAB-AGAR (Biomaxima) for 4 days, and then inoculated into liquid BM supplemented with 2 µM hemin chloride (Pol-Aura) for one 24-h passage, and for 2 passages in BM without added heme. Alternatively, bacteria were grown for an additional passage in BM or BM supplemented with 10 µM hemin chloride (BM+heme) or 10 µM PPIX (BM+PPIX). In every passage, the initial optical density of the culture at 600 nm (OD600) was 0.2. To monitor P. gingivalis growth rate, bacteria were inoculated into fresh medium, and OD600 was measured over time using the Stratus plate reader (Cerillo, Charlottesville, VA, USA).

Construction of P. gingivalis mutant and complemented strains

The inactivation of the hemH gene was carried out in the wild-type W83 P. gingivalis strain by replacing the entire open reading frame encoding the HemH protein in the hemH gene (gene locus ID: PG_RS00590/PG0127) (Fig. S1A) with the Bacteroides fragilis erythromycin resistance cassette (ermF; GenBank ID: NG_047825), lacking its native promoter sequence (Fig. S1B), resulting in the hemH deletion mutant strain (ΔhemH). The flanking regions of the hemH gene were PCR-amplified using genomic DNA, and ermF was amplified using the pTIO-1 plasmid42. All primers used in PCR are listed in Table S1. All fragments were ligated using the NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and subsequently amplified using PCR, resulting in the linear fragment used for electroporation. The ΔhemH mutant strain was selected on ABA plates supplemented with 3 µg/ml erythromycin.

To generate the complemented strain (ΔhemH+hemH), a modified pTIO-tetQ plasmid43 was constructed. The hemH gene sequence with an additional 500 bp located upstream of the hemH gene, containing its native promoter, was PCR-amplified from genomic DNA using primers listed in Table S1. In the first step, during amplification of the hemH gene, the sequence encoding the HA tag at the C-terminus of the HemH protein was added. The second step allowed for amplification of the DNA sequence with the flanking regions and cloning it into the XhoI and BamHI restriction sites of the pTIO-tetQ plasmid using NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Kit (New England Biolabs), resulting in the pTIO-hemH plasmid. The correctness of the sequence was verified by DNA sequencing (Microsynth Seqlab GmbH, Gottingen, Germany). pTIO-hemH plasmid was introduced into the ΔhemH mutant strain by electroporation. The ΔhemH+hemH complemented strain was selected on ABA plates supplemented with 1 µg/ml tetracycline.

Homologous recombination of the linear construct with the genomic DNA or the presence of the plasmid was verified by PCR (Fig. S1C) and DNA sequencing (Microsynth Seqlab GmbH). RT-PCR and Western blotting were used to verify the introduced mutations at the transcript and protein level, respectively (Fig. S1D and S1E).

Overexpression and purification of recombinant proteins

The sequence encoding the HemH protein was PCR-amplified and cloned into the XmnI and BamHI restriction sites of the modified pMAL-c5x plasmid43, resulting in the pMAL-c5x_HemH plasmid, enabling overexpression of the recombinant HemH protein (in N-terminal fusion with 8×His-maltose binding protein; His-MBP) (Fig. S2A) in Escherichia coli BL21-CodonPlus (DE3)-RIL strain (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The correctness of the pMAL-c5x_HemH plasmid nucleotide sequence was verified by DNA sequencing (Microsynth Seqlab GmbH).

E. coli carrying the pMAL c5x_HemH plasmid was cultured in Terrific Broth (1.2% tryptone, 2.4% yeast extract, 0.5% glycerol, 17 mM KH₂PO₄, 72 mM K₂HPO₄; MP Biomedicals, LLC, Solon, OH, USA) supplemented with ampicillin (100 µg/mL) and chloramphenicol (35 µg/mL) at 37 °C and shaking at 220 rpm, until OD₆₀₀ reached 0.8. Protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM IPTG for 16 h at 16 °C and shaking at 220 rpm. Cells were harvested by centrifugation (6,000×g, 20 min, 4 °C). The pellet was resuspended in 25 mM HEPES, pH 7.7, with 300 mM NaCl and 0.2% Tween-20 (starting buffer) and sonicated (Sonopuls HD 4100; Bandelin, Berlin, Germany). Samples were centrifuged (20,000×g, 20 min, 4 °C), and an equal volume of the starting buffer without Tween-20 was added to the supernatant. The recombinant HemH protein was purified from the soluble fraction using amylose resin (New England Biolabs). After washing of unbound proteins with the starting buffer without Tween-20 and subsequently with 25 mM HEPES, pH 7.7, containing 1 M NaCl, the protein was eluted with 25 mM Tris/HCl, pH 7.5, containing 80 mM NaCl and 25 mM maltose. The purification process was monitored by SDS-PAGE of collected samples (Fig. S2B). The protein was concentrated using Amicon Ultra-4 Centrifugal Ultracel-10 kDa filter units (Millipore), and its concentration was determined using Roti Nanoquant (Carl Roth). The protein was stored with 10% glycerol at -20 °C. The His-MBP protein was overexpressed and purified analogously to the recombinant HemH protein; however, Tween-20 was omitted in the buffers used.

RNA isolation

Total RNA was extracted from ~ 0.5–1.5 × 109 bacteria using the Total RNA Mini Kit (A&A Biotechnology, Gdańsk, Poland), followed by treatment with the Clean-up RNA Concentrator Kit (A&A Biotechnology) to eliminate residual genomic DNA contamination. RNA purity and integrity were evaluated using spectrophotometry and agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. RNA samples were stored at − 80 °C or in dry ice until further processing.

Gene expression analysis using RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) and reverse transcriptase-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

P. gingivalis strains were cultured on BM agar plates supplemented with 7.7 µM heme for 4 days. Subsequently, the bacteria were transferred to liquid BM containing 2 µM heme, inoculated at an initial OD₆₀₀ of 0.2, and cultured for 24 h. Then, two 24-h passages in BM lacking heme, each initiated at an OD₆₀₀ equal to 0.2, were carried out. In the final step, a culture was established in heme-free BM at OD₆₀₀ 0.2 and incubated for 20 h, reaching an OD₆₀₀ of approximately 1–1.5. Alternatively, bacteria were cultured on ABA plates and then for 3 passages in the liquid BM + Hm medium. In the final step, a culture was established in BM medium supplemented with 7.7 µM hemin at OD₆₀₀ 0.2, and bacteria were cultured for ~ 6 h, reaching an OD₆₀₀ of approximately 0.6–0.7. After cultivation, cells were harvested from 2-mL cultures by centrifugation. RNA-seq and data analyses were carried out by Novogene (Cambridge, United Kingdom), following the company’s standard protocols. Differential gene expression was analyzed using three independent biological replicates. Changes in gene expression were considered significant if the fold change (FC) was greater than 1.5 with a p-value < 0.05.

For gene expression analysis using RT-qPCR, bacteria were cultured analogously to the RNA-seq analysis. RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and qPCR was carried out as reported previously44.

Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and Western blotting

Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE on 12% polyacrylamide gels and visualized by staining with Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 (CBB G-250) or were transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were probed with rabbit anti-HmuY polyclonal antibodies (1:10,000) (GenScript USA Inc., Piscataway, NJ, USA), rabbit anti-IhtB polyclonal antibodies (1:10,000) (ProteoGenix, Schiltigheim, France), or rabbit anti-HA monoclonal antibodies (0.5–1.0 µg/ml; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and subsequently with goat anti-rabbit IgG antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (1:10,000) (Sigma-Aldrich). The procedure was carried out as described previously45. Chemiluminescence staining (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA) and ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) were used to visualize proteins. Before antibody incubation, the membrane was cut to reduce membrane area and antibody consumption, removing only regions where no signal was expected. Densitometric analysis was performed using Image Lab 6.0.1 software (Bio-Rad).

Preparation of porphyrin solutions

Porphyrin solutions were prepared as previously reported44,46,47. Briefly, heme (FePPIX; hemin chloride; Pol-Aura, Morąg, Poland) and MnPPIX (Mn(III)Protoporphyrin IX chloride; Frontier Scientific) were prepared in 0.1 M NaOH, whereas PPIX (Sigma-Aldrich) was prepared in DMSO. The porphyrin concentration was determined by weight or using the empirical molar absorption coefficients for heme (ε385 = 58.5 mM− 1 cm− 1)48 or PPIX (ε405 = 150 mM− 1 cm− 1)49.

Protein-porphyrin binding assay and determination of ferrochelatase activity

Protein-porphyrin binding ability was examined by mixing 5 µM recombinant HemH protein with porphyrins at a 1:1 molar ratio in 25 mM Tris-HCl buffer, pH 7.4, containing 140 mM NaCl (TBS). The UV-visible spectra of the samples were then recorded using a double-beam Jasco V-750 spectrophotometer (Jasco Corporation, Hachioji, Tokyo, Japan) and cuvettes with 10 mm path length.

To demonstrate ferrochelatase activity, mixtures containing 5 µM purified recombinant HemH protein, 5–10 µM PPIX, and varying concentrations of iron ions (Fe²⁺), supplied as (NH₄)₂Fe(SO₄)₂, or manganese ions (Mn2+) supplied as MnSO4 were used. The samples were incubated in the presence of 0.05% ascorbic acid in anaerobic conditions (80% N2, 10% H2, and 10% CO2) at 37 °C for 90 min in the dark. The reaction was monitored by measuring the UV-visible spectra of the substrate (PPIX) and the products (heme or MnPPIX). The control reaction contained His-MBP protein, or the protein was not added.

Theoretical analyses

Protein amino acid sequences were obtained from the Protein database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein). The search for protein homologs was performed using PSI-BLAST, and the sequences were selected manually. Amino acid sequences were compared using Clustal Omega (the Multiple Sequence Alignment tool)50. The phylogenetic tree was generated using Simple Phylogeny50 with the Neighbour-joining clustering method, Clustal Tree format, and distance correction. Subsequently, it was visualized using iTOL51. The three-dimensional structure of the P. gingivalis HemH protein was modeled using the AlphaFold 3.0 server (https://alphafold.com)52,53. Protein structures were visualized with the UCSF ChimeraX program (https://www.cgl.ucsf.edu/chimera)54.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) using an unpaired Student’s t-test or an unpaired t-test with Welch’s correction. Growth curves were evaluated by two-way ANOVA to assess the effects of the strain type and time on bacterial growth. All experiments were performed at least three times with a minimum of two biological replicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

The conservation of heme biosynthesis pathway proteins varies among Bacteroidota species

Many bacteria from the Bacteroidota phylum encode a few proteins involved in the oxygen-independent PPIX-dependent heme biosynthesis pathway (Fig. 1A and B), but conserved genes differ between bacterial species. For example, Porphyromonas canoris and Porphyromonas cangingivalis, both canine-inhabiting species, possess a fully conserved heme biosynthesis pathway (Fig. 1B). Porphyromonas pogonae, a human pathogen, apart from uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase (HemE), possesses all the other genes (Fig. 1B), although it has not been experimentally demonstrated whether this bacterium does not synthesize heme. Most of the analyzed bacteria do not encode proteins involved in the initial steps of the heme biosynthesis (HemA, HemL, HemB, and HemC). However, all of them encode homologs of uroporphyrinogen III synthase (HemD) (Fig. 1B), which is the last common enzyme of the heme and cobalamin biosynthetic pathways. In addition, although a homolog of coproporphyrinogen III oxidase (HemN) was also identified in these bacteria (Fig. 1B), in some species, including P. gingivalis, it may function more similarly to E. coli HemW heme chaperone due to higher homology to this protein (Fig. S3). Nevertheless, the exact function of those proteins in Porphyromonas species should be determined experimentally. Interestingly, all of the analyzed Porphyromonas species encode the HemH ferrochelatase (Fig. 1B). In contrast, other members of the Bacteroidota phylum, including Prevotella intermedia, Tannerella forsythia, and Bacteroides species, lack the hemH gene (Fig. 1B).

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that Porphyromonas HemH proteins form a distinct subgroup, clearly separated from classical ferrochelatases (Fig. 2A). Consistently, P. gingivalis HemH and its homologs in other Porphyromonas species exhibit only low to moderate amino acid sequence identity with classical HemH proteins, such as those from Bacillus subtilis (~ 18–22%) and E. coli (~ 28–36%) (Fig. 2A). The similarity of HemH proteins among Porphyromonas species is also highly variable. For instance, HemH from P. gingivalis exhibits the lowest sequence identity with HemH from Porphyromonas gingivicanis (~ 27%) and Porphyromonas endodontalis (~ 29%), while showing the highest identity to HemH from Porphyromonas loveana (~ 65%), P. pogonae (~ 46%) (Fig. 2A), and Porphyromonas gulae (~ 95%). Furthermore, when considering the host species or the ecological niches occupied by Porphyromonas species (Table S2), no correlation is observed between either the proteins of the heme biosynthesis pathway produced or the similarity of the HemH protein (Fig. 2A).

P. gingivalis encodes a functional HemH protein

Despite the observed amino acid sequence diversity among HemH proteins of Porphyromonas species and classical bacterial ferrochelatases, they retain critical conserved amino acid residues (histidine and glutamate) found in the active center (Fig. 2B). Moreover, P. gingivalis HemH structure modelling showed that its overall fold closely resembles that of known HemH structures, including B. subtilis HemH. Notably, P. gingivalis H199 and E302 residues align with H183 and E264 residues of B. subtilis HemH55, forming the enzyme’s catalytic site (Fig. 2C). Therefore, the theoretical analysis indicates that the P. gingivalis HemH protein may retain ferrochelatase activity.

To confirm the suggested function of P. gingivalis HemH, the recombinant HemH protein was overexpressed and purified. The protein was produced with an N-terminal 6×His-tag; however, it was hardly soluble in this form. Therefore, to facilitate its solubility and stability, the protein was produced with an N-terminal 8×His-MBP tag (Fig. S2A and S2B). Since the tag might contribute to heme binding, the overexpressed and purified His-MBP protein was used as a control.

First, the binding of selected porphyrins to the recombinant HemH protein was examined by analyzing the spectral changes in the Soret (~ 350–450 nm) and Q bands (~ 500–700 nm) regions of the free porphyrins and protein-porphyrin complexes (Fig. 3A). After mixing heme or PPIX with HemH protein, the changes in the Soret and Q bands were observed. This effect was mostly visible in the case of PPIX binding (the appearance of the distinct Soret band maximum at 426 nm). In the case of heme binding, the red shift in the Soret band maximum was visible (from 385 nm to 408 nm). Analogously, changes in the visible spectra indicated that the recombinant HemH protein bound MnPPIX (Fig. 3A). While heme was slightly bound to the control His-MBP protein (observed as a red shift in the Soret band maximum from 385 nm to 408 nm), PPIX and MnPPIX did not bind to the His-MBP.

To analyze HemH chelatase activity, the protein was incubated with PPIX and Fe2+ in anaerobic conditions. A decrease in the Soret band maximum and changes in the Q bands of the visible spectrum were observed (Fig. 3B and S4A). The spectrum shifted from that characteristic of the HemH–PPIX complex to one resembling the HemH–heme complex (Fig. 3A and B), mostly visible as the decrease in the Soret band maximum at 426 nm, indicating the insertion of Fe²⁺ into PPIX and the subsequent formation of the heme molecule. Similarly, the chelation reaction was carried out with manganese ions, as Mn²⁺ is commonly used for in vitro analyses due to the susceptibility of Fe²⁺ to oxidation in aqueous solutions. Also in this case, the changes in the spectrum, visible as the decrease in the Soret band maximum at 426 nm and the formation of the maxima at 377 and 473 nm, indicated the insertion of Mn2+ into the PPIX ring (Fig. 3C and S4B).

The control reactions carried out with His-MBP instead of the recombinant HemH protein confirmed the specificity of the reaction carried out with ions because no changes in the visible spectra were observed (Fig. S4).

HemH protein is important for P. gingivalis survival during heme deficiency

To assess the importance of the hemH gene for the growth of P. gingivalis, its expression in bacteria cultured in different conditions was determined. When the P. gingivalis wild-type strain was grown in BM medium supplemented with heme or PPIX, hemH gene expression decreased 3 times, as compared to bacteria grown in BM without added porphyrins (Fig. 4A). These observations indicated that HemH production may be regulated by heme and PPIX levels and that HemH is important for P. gingivalis in heme/PPIX-deficient conditions.

To investigate the impact of HemH on the P. gingivalis phenotype, a ΔhemH mutant strain was generated (Fig. S1B). First, the effect of the lack of HemH on bacterial growth was assessed. The most pronounced difference was observed when bacteria were cultured in medium lacking heme (BM). In these conditions, the ΔhemH strain exhibited the poorest growth in liquid media and showed almost no growth on solid media (Fig. 4B and C). In contrast, when bacteria were grown in media supplemented with heme or PPIX, the mutant strain displayed growth comparable to that of the wild-type strain (Fig. 4B and C). The complementation of the ΔhemH mutant strain with a plasmid carrying the hemH gene restored the wild-type phenotype (Fig. 4B). A Similar effect was observed when bacteria were cultured on agar plates (Fig. 4C). This phenotype correlated with higher hemH expression in the complemented strain compared to the wild-type strain (~ 18-fold) in bacteria cultured in BM medium without heme or PPIX supplementation (Fig. 4D).

To further understand the importance of HemH for P. gingivalis and determine changes influenced by hemH gene inactivation, the global gene expression analysis was carried out using RNA-seq analysis after growing bacteria in medium without (BM) or with heme supplementation (BM + Hm). When bacteria were cultured in BM + Hm medium, only two genes showed altered expression: PG0128 (PG_RS00595) and PG0129 (PG_RS00600), with their expression levels decreased by 1.95- and 2.89-fold, respectively. Notably, these two genes encode a putative lipopolysaccharide kinase (PG0128) and a glycosyltransferase (PG0129) and may be located in the same operon as the hemH gene (Fig. S1A). In contrast, when bacteria were grown in BM medium lacking heme, inactivation of the hemH gene led to significant changes in the expression of approximately 120 genes (Table S3 and Fig. 5A). Among 76 up-regulated genes are those encoding proteins involved in heme acquisition (including P. gingivalis’ main heme uptake Hmu system), genes encoding proteins important for bacterial virulence (such as hemagglutinin B, HagB, and fimbrial Fim proteins), and genes encoding proteins forming uncharacterized transport systems (PG1019 and PG1020 encoding a lipoprotein and TonB-dependent receptor; PG1178, PG1179, and PG1180 encoding porin, outer membrane lipoprotein-sorting protein, and membrane protein) (Table 2, Table S3, Table S4, and Fig. 5A).

Complementation of the hemH inactivation did not fully restore expression of the PG0128 gene and PG0129 genes to the level of the wild-type (Fig. S5). However, since the hemH inactivation effect was phenotypically restored in the complemented strain, we assumed that the decreased expression of the hemH downstream genes (observed in both culture conditions) does not affect the phenotype. Moreover, it was shown that the PG0129 mutant strain is non-pigmented56. In our studies, we did not observe differences in pigment formation between the wild-type and ΔhemH mutant strains.

At the protein level, no noticeable difference in HmuY abundance between the ΔhemH mutant and the wild-type strain was observed (Fig. 5C). However, it should be noted that in this experiment, only the membrane-associated protein fraction was analyzed, and the level of soluble HmuY protein, released from the bacterial cell surface to and accumulated in the culture medium, was not examined. In contrast, although no significant change was detected at the transcript level in the case of the ihtB gene (Table 2), encoding a putative CbiK cobalt chelatase homolog proposed to function as a reverse ferrochelatase, a slight increase in IhtB protein abundance was observed (Fig. 5C).

Discussion

P. gingivalis lost the ability to synthesize heme de novo, likely as a result of adaptation to the host environment and the niches it occupies, characterized by the potential availability of heme. As P. gingivalis encodes the last three proteins of the heme biosynthesis pathway (HemN, HemG, and HemH)29,41, it has been suggested that it could synthesize heme from coproporphyrinogen III or coproporphyrin III57, heme precursors present in the cytoplasm of the host cells. However, theoretical analyses showed that this might not be the case. Instead of HemN, P. gingivalis encodes the HemW protein homolog. Both HemN and HemW are members of the radical SAM (S-adenosylmethionine) enzyme superfamily, exhibiting relatively high amino acid similarity and sharing a highly conserved three-dimensional structure. In addition, HemW proteins are considered heme chaperons and do not possess coproporphyrinogen III oxidase activity58. Although the P. gingivalis hemG gene restored the phenotype of an E. coli hemG deletion mutant strain26, its deletion in P. gingivalis did not influence the phenotype59.

Since heme is available from host hemoproteins, P. gingivalis developed a sophisticated array of heme acquisition mechanisms28,29,39. In laboratory conditions, P. gingivalis can also satisfy its heme requirements when provided with PPIX and an inorganic iron source17,40,41. Comparative genomic analyses demonstrated that, while many Bacteroidota species have lost most of the canonical heme biosynthetic enzymes, Porphyromonas species uniquely retained a HemH homolog. It may maintain at least partial ferrochelatase activity, potentially enabling P. gingivalis limited endogenous heme synthesis or metal chelation. Therefore, the preservation of the last gene in the heme biosynthesis pathway, enabling ferrochelatase activity, may serve as an alternative mechanism for acquiring heme in an environment with limited heme availability.

To support this hypothesis, we demonstrated that HemH can insert iron or manganese ions into the PPIX ring. This capability may be important when P. gingivalis grows in healthy periodontium or during the early stage of periodontal disease, including the host’s intracellular environment, where heme availability is limited. This assumption is supported by higher hemH expression in heme/PPIX-limited conditions. Based on our results, we conclude that the absence of heme/PPIX induces the hemH gene expression, but the regulatory mechanism is unknown. P. gingivalis produces many regulatory proteins related to the regulation of heme/iron uptake and homeostasis. For example, expression of the hmu operon (including hmuY) is regulated by PgFur, a homolog of the ferric uptake protein produced by P. gingivalis43. However, we did not identify a Fur-box sequence recognized by this protein within the hemH promoter; therefore, we do not believe that HemH production is PgFur-dependent. It has also been shown that the regulation of the expression of genes encoding proteins responsible for heme acquisition may occur via HaeSR and PgRsp60,61. Nevertheless, it has never been shown that those proteins regulate the expression of the hemH gene. We cannot exclude other, unknown regulatory mechanisms, forming components of a multi-level regulatory network responding to iron and heme levels.

To assess the HemH function, several questions must be answered. The first question is the source of iron-free porphyrins. In vivo, PPIX may be present in the cytoplasm of host cells, and its amount may be elevated in some human disorders62–65, leading to the disruption of homeostasis of heme precursors. Excess PPIX may not only be accumulated inside host cells, where P. gingivalis can survive and multiply, but also be released into the intercellular space, and therefore present in the serum, mainly as a complex with serum albumin66. Since gingival crevicular fluid is composed of serum components, the albumin-PPIX complex might be the main source of PPIX in the peridontal pocket. The second question is the route of PPIX delivery into the bacterial cell. It has been hypothesized that the P. gingivalis Hus system (HusABCD) may be involved in the acquisition of PPIX and other heme precursors41. In periods of heme deficiency, PPIX could be acquired by the Hus system and used as a substrate for the proteins of the final steps of the PPIX-dependent heme biosynthesis pathway preserved in P. gingivalis. Interestingly, P. gingivalis may also grow in the presence of non-iron metalloporphyrins40,41,67, suggesting the possibility of converting them into heme. The bacterium may be using an as-yet-unidentified mechanism to remove metal ions from non-iron metalloporphyrins and then use HemH to insert iron into the PPIX ring. This might explain the low anti-bacterial activity of several non-iron metalloporphyrins40,67. The third question is the source of iron. P. gingivalis does not encode iron chelators (siderophores) and proteins transferring iron across the outer membrane (designated siderophore TonB-dependent receptors). Moreover, a classical system transporting iron derived from transferrin or lactoferrin across the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria68 was not identified in P. gingivalis. We can only assume that the Iht system28 may be used to transport heme or, under certain circumstances, iron. Another source could be intracellular iron reserves stored in ferritin69.

Some bacteria from the Bacteroidota phylum, such as B. fragilis, lack a HemH homolog but still can grow when PPIX and iron are available instead of a heme source, suggesting alternative heme-formation pathways25. Likewise, as shown in this study, P. gingivalis can also grow in the presence of PPIX when its hemH gene is inactivated, indicating that it may possess a mechanism similar to that utilized by B. fragilis, allowing for heme production from PPIX and iron despite the absence of HemH. In addition, P. gingivalis produces HmuS and IhtB proteins, putative reverse ferrochelatases functioning in the periplasmic space and in the extracellular environment, respectively. In this study, we demonstrated that both proteins are produced in the ΔhemH mutant strain at higher transcript and protein levels, which may support our hypothesis. However, the identity of a cytoplasmic enzyme that inserts iron into the PPIX ring in the absence of the hemH gene in P. gingivalis and other Bacteroidota species should be identified.

The deletion of the hemH gene triggers a heme-starvation response characterized by transcriptional activation of heme uptake systems (mainly the Hmu system), as well as virulence-associated genes, coupled with repression of transport and surface proteins preferentially expressed in heme-rich conditions. Significantly increased expression of hmuY and hmuR genes suggests heme starvation and less efficient utilization of systems required for heme delivery, including heme synthesis from iron and PPIX. Increased expression of hagB and fim genes, which are important for bacterial virulence, may indicate a need for greater adherence to and invasion of host cells to acquire intracellular ﻿heme. It is worth noting that the W83 strain of P. gingivalis is generally considered afimbriated. Although it encodes the fim gene cluster, expression of fimbrial proteins that form long fimbriae is significantly decreased due to the defect in the FimS histidine kinase, a component of the FimR-FimS regulatory complex70,71. Increased expression of fim genes may indirectly explain the disturbances in heme metabolism in the mutant strain. Previous studies have shown that under iron/heme limitation, P. gingivalis, including the W83 strain, produces higher amounts of Fim proteins and forms more extensive biofilm structures72–74. A similar effect may occur in the ΔhemH mutant strain, where impaired heme biosynthesis in the absence of hemH could even further increase heme-starvation conditions. It can also be hypothesized that increased fim genes expression may be mediated by potential regulatory proteins encoded by PG2161, PG1535, or PG1181 genes, whose expression is upregulated in the ΔhemH mutant strain.

Up-regulation of the expression of genes encoding proteins of other potential transport systems may point to other putative mechanisms used to compensate for the lack of heme delivery through classical uptake routes. Decreased expression of hagA and trp genes, whose products are engaged in heme acquisition mainly in heme-rich conditions, where hemoglobin is the main heme source, and therefore in bacterial virulence, suggests that they may play a minor function when heme availability is limited. Lower expression of other genes encoding proteins potentially involved in transport through the inner membrane may suggest a change in homeostasis of ions or other molecules, such as PPIX.

Based on the results presented in this study and existing data on heme and iron acquisition mechanisms in P. gingivalis28,29,39, it seems that, under heme limitations, HemH may play a role in maintaining proper heme homeostasis in this bacterium. During evolution, resulting in the loss of several genes of the heme biosynthesis pathway, P. gingivalis preserved a functional HemH protein with ferrochelatase activity. In a heme-limited host environment, P. gingivalis may use intracellular iron and PPIX reserves to synthesize heme by HemH, thereby contributing to heme supply.
