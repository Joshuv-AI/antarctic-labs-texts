Design optimization of wearable antennas using an adaptive network-based fuzzy inference system
Authors: Asrafuzzaman Khan Nahin, Waleed M Hamanah, Alaa Hussein, Ali Ahmed Salem, Mohamed Ali Abido
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents a data-driven optimization framework for wearable microstrip patch antennas operating near the 2.4 GHz ISM band using an Adaptive Network-Based Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS). The proposed approach predicts key antenna geometry parameters—patch width, patch length, and feed point coordinates—from target electromagnetic performance metrics, including resonant frequency, reflection coefficient, bandwidth, and substrate thickness. A dataset of 500 samples was extracted from parametric HFSS simulations, and the ANFIS model was trained using a 70/30 training-testing split. Four membership function types “triangular, trapezoidal, Gaussian, and generalized bell” were systematically evaluated to analyze their influence on prediction accuracy and convergence behavior. Results demonstrate strong agreement between ANFIS predictions and HFSS simulations, with sub-millimeter mean absolute errors for patch dimensions and high regression coefficients across parameters. Among the tested membership functions, the generalized bell function showed the most stable convergence and the lowest overall prediction error. The proposed approach reduces reliance on repeated full-wave simulations by providing rapid estimation of antenna geometry from performance specifications, enabling faster prototyping of wearable antennas. The results highlight the importance of membership function selection in neuro-fuzzy modeling for antenna design and demonstrate the feasibility of ANFIS-based surrogate modeling for wearable communication devices.Keywords: ANFIS, Membership function optimization, Microstrip patch antenna, Wearable devices, Antenna design, Performance metricsSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

WIRELESS communication has become a cornerstone of modern technological advancements, revolutionizing various industries, particularly healthcare, where biomedical telemetry has emerged as a critical application1. The development of wireless technology has enabled real-time patient monitoring, offering a transformative approach to medical care. Continuous 24 × 7 monitoring through biomedical antennas has proven instrumental in the early detection and diagnosis of life-threatening conditions, significantly reducing risk factors and improving patient outcomes2. However, as demand for high-speed, reliable wireless connectivity continues to grow, wireless operators face the challenge of delivering high efficiency and enhanced data rates within limited spectral resources while ensuring Quality of Service (QoS)3.

Microstrip patch antennas (MPAs) are ideal for wearable biomedical devices due to their compact size, lightweight design, and low-profile structure4, 5, 6. These antennas are particularly suited for health-monitoring applications, as they can conform to the body’s contours while maintaining reliable performance7. A major challenge in wearable antenna design is achieving miniaturization without compromising essential performance characteristics, such as impedance matching, low return loss, and radiation efficiency. Overcoming these challenges is critical for the successful integration of MPAs into wearable and implantable systems8.

Recent studies have demonstrated the growing use of machine learning and optimization techniques in antenna design to accelerate parameter tuning and improve prediction accuracy. For instance, studies reported in9 employed tree-based machine learning models such as random forest and gradient boosting to predict antenna characteristics including reflection coefficient (S11), achieving prediction accuracies above 90%. While these models provide high prediction performance, they often behave as black-box predictors and offer limited interpretability regarding the relationship between antenna geometry and electromagnetic performance. In contrast9, combine neural learning with fuzzy rule extraction, enabling both high predictive capability and interpretability of nonlinear relationships between design variables and antenna responses. Previous investigations have shown that ANFIS models can achieve geometry prediction errors as low as MAE ≈ 0.02–2.5 mm, indicating strong potential for accurate antenna parameter estimation. Similarly, recent ML-based wearable antenna research demonstrates that ensemble regression models can effectively predict reflection coefficient behavior while reducing computational time compared with conventional full-wave electromagnetic simulations10. However, most existing studies focus primarily on prediction accuracy rather than investigating the influence of membership function (MF) selection in neuro-fuzzy models, which plays a crucial role in determining ANFIS modeling performance.

In addition to ML-driven antenna modeling, wearable and IoT-enabled antenna technologies have attracted increasing research attention due to their applications in wireless body area networks (WBAN), biomedical monitoring, and smart wearable systems. The study in11 highlights that compact antenna designed for IoT and wearable systems must address challenges such as miniaturization, performance trade-offs, and reliable communication when operating near the human body. Furthermore, wearable antenna systems experience strong electromagnetic interaction with human tissues, which may degrade gain, detune resonance frequency, and increase the specific absorption rate (SAR)12, 13. To overcome these limitations, researchers have introduced various design strategies including metamaterial-based structures such as artificial magnetic conductors (AMC), electromagnetic bandgap (EBG) surfaces, and high-impedance surfaces to suppress surface waves and reduce backward radiation toward the body14. Recent developments also show that machine learning methods are being applied across different antenna types and frequency ranges. For example, ML-assisted optimization has been explored for terahertz antennas in15 and ultra-wideband antennas in16, demonstrating the growing role of data-driven techniques in modern antenna engineering15, 16. Nevertheless, despite the broad adoption of ML in antenna optimization, limited research has examined the role of membership function optimization in ANFIS-based antenna modeling, particularly for widely used antenna structures. Therefore, investigating MF behavior within neuro-fuzzy frameworks can provide deeper insights into parameter interactions and improve both the interpretability and reliability of ML-based antenna design methodologies.

This study focuses on optimizing the design of a microstrip patch antenna for wearable devices operating near the ISM band (2.4 GHz) to enhance its flexibility, performance, and signal integrity. While various optimization techniques have been employed for antenna design, including Evolutionary Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Fuzzy Logic, and Evolutionary Algorithms (EA)14, this work proposes the use of the ANFIS as a hybrid soft computing technique. ANFIS facilitates the training and testing of key antenna parameters, such as substrate thickness and patch dimensions, resulting in improved performance metrics, including return loss and radiation patterns17.

In this paper, the design and simulation of the microstrip patch antenna are carried out using Ansys HFSS, with optimization achieved through fuzzy logic to balance trade-offs between flexibility, signal strength, and return loss. The structure of this paper is organized as follows: Section II reviews existing research on antenna design and optimization; Section III outlines the proposed design methodology; Section IV discusses the ANFIS-based optimization approach; Section V presents the simulation results; and Section VI concludes with remarks on the antenna’s potential for wearable biomedical applications7.

While machine learning techniques have increasingly been applied in antenna design, many existing studies focus primarily on predicting electromagnetic responses such as return loss or resonant frequency. In contrast, fewer works attempt to predict antenna geometric parameters directly from performance targets, which is essential for practical design automation. Furthermore, most studies employ a single machine learning architecture without systematically investigating the effect of model structure or membership function selection on prediction accuracy.

Overview of microstrip patch antennas

Microstrip patch antennas (MSAs) are widely valued for their lightweight and low-profile structure, making them indispensable components in modern communication systems (Fig. 1). An MSA typically consists of a radiating patch on one side of a dielectric substrate and a ground plane on the other. The radiating patch, often constructed from copper or other conductive materials, is engineered to resonate at specific frequencies, while the dielectric substrate significantly influences the antenna’s impedance and radiation characteristics8. The performance of MSAs is determined by critical parameters such as gain, bandwidth, directivity, and efficiency. Additionally, MSAs provide multiple polarization options, including linear (horizontal or vertical) and circular polarization. Circular polarization, achieved through structural modifications or specialized feeding techniques, is particularly advantageous in applications like satellite communications due to its resilience against signal fading and multipath effects21.

Probe feeding microstrip patch antenna.

Various feeding methods are used in MSAs, broadly categorized into contacting and non-contacting techniques. Contacting methods, such as microstrip line and coaxial probe feeds, directly connect to the radiating patch, whereas non-contacting methods, including aperture coupling and proximity coupling, rely on electromagnetic coupling22, 23 Coaxial probe feed is a popular choice for its simplicity, effective impedance matching, and ability to minimize spurious radiation, making it suitable for compact and integrated systems2.

Recent innovations in MSA design have focused on improving their performance for advanced applications. For example, in7, the authors introduced a wideband antenna optimized using an ANFIS, achieving significant improvements in efficiency and gain for 5G millimeter-wave applications. Similarly, the author of24 proposed the Uniform Cross-Entropy (UCE) optimization technique for multiband MSA design, demonstrating superior computational efficiency and enhanced bandwidth performance. Material and structural modifications have further driven MSA capabilities. For instance, the improved gain and return loss through slot-based structural designs, resulting in compact, high-performance antennas suitable for mm-wave applications, is shown in25. These advancements highlight the adaptability and scalability of MSAs in meeting the demands of next-generation wireless communication technologies.

MSAs have a broad range of applications in fields such as 5G, IoT, and biomedical systems. For instance, Soni et al. (2024) developed wearable MSAs for real-time tumor detection, showcasing the potential of MSAs in healthcare1. Additionally, miniaturized antennas are optimized for multiband operations using Genetic Algorithms, making MSAs ideal for compact and portable devices26.

As MSAs continue to evolve, advancements in computational optimization, material science, and innovative design methodologies ensure their relevance in addressing the challenges of next-generation communication systems.

Despite their advantages, designing microstrip patch antennas typically requires multiple iterations of full-wave electromagnetic simulations to achieve desired performance characteristics such as impedance matching, bandwidth, and radiation efficiency. These iterative processes can significantly increase design time and computational cost. As a result, data-driven modeling approaches have recently emerged as promising tools to accelerate antenna design by predicting optimal geometric parameters directly from performance requirements.

Optimization techniques for microstrip patch antennas

Early optimizations of microstrip patch antennas relied on metaheuristic methods, including Genetic Algorithms (GA) and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO). These techniques were later enhanced through hybrid approaches, combining ANN or ANFIS for improved efficiency. For example, GA was utilized to triple antenna bandwidth27, while Abbas Ali H. and Abolfazl Dadgarnia combined ANFIS and GA to optimize circularly polarized antennas28.

To address computational complexity, advanced optimization techniques such as Chaotic PSO, Accelerated PSO (APSO), Genetic Algorithm (GA), Neural Network-Based PSO (NN-PSO), and various soft computation techniques have been introduced, demonstrating faster convergence and improved performance29, 30, 31, 32, 33. Additionally, specialized PSO variants, including Discrete PSO (DPSO) and Microstrip Radiator PSO, have been employed for enhanced optimization efficiency in antenna design and related applications34, 35. Differential Evolution (DE)36 has also been utilized as a robust alternative, offering strong global search capabilities and effective convergence properties for complex optimization problems34. Furthermore, Adaptive Network-Based Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS) optimization has been applied to multi-band microstrip patch antennas to enhance design flexibility and efficiency, while Genetic Algorithms (GA) continue to be refined for miniaturized antenna structures with improved performance37, 38.

Recent research has leveraged machine learning, as seen in those who used the Uniform Cross-Entropy (UCE) method for multi-band antenna optimization with reduced computational costs24. Further advancements focused on compact, high-performance designs for modern applications. El-Hakim achieved multiband performance (25–32 GHz and 40–60 GHz) using GA21, while R. Guttula introduced Elephant Herding Optimization (EHO-NSF), achieving a gain of 17.2 dB at 77 GHz. Material engineering also played a role, as26 optimized a thin-film antenna using GA, reducing its size by 60% while improving bandwidth and return loss.

Recent studies emphasize antenna miniaturization for biomedical and IoT applications, combining structural modifications, advanced algorithms, and high-permittivity materials to achieve compact designs with high efficiency. This work leverages ANFIS to optimize patch dimensions, achieving size reduction and gain improvements suitable for compact biomedical systems.

Recent studies have explored machine learning approaches for antenna design and optimization. For example, several works have applied ensemble learning models such as Random Forest, Decision Trees, and Gradient Boosting to predict antenna return loss and other electromagnetic characteristics. These approaches have demonstrated high prediction accuracy and reduced simulation requirements. However, most existing studies focus on predicting performance metrics such as S-parameters rather than directly estimating antenna geometry parameters. In contrast, the present work employs an Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS) to predict geometric design parameters (including patch dimensions and feed point locations) based on target performance characteristics. Additionally, this study systematically evaluates the impact of different membership function types on model accuracy and convergence, which is often overlooked in machine learning-based antenna design studies.

Architecture of ANFIS

The Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS) combines the strengths of fuzzy logic and artificial neural networks to model complex and nonlinear relationships effectively. Similar to traditional fuzzy systems, ANFIS consists of two main components: the antecedent (fuzzification) and the consequent (defuzzification). These components are connected through fuzzy rules, enabling ANFIS to map inputs to outputs accurately.

The architecture of ANFIS is organized into five layers: the fuzzy layer, product layer, normalized layer, defuzzification layer, and summation layer. Each layer plays a distinct role in processing inputs and generating outputs, as depicted in Fig. 2. Circular nodes represent fixed functions, while square nodes are adaptive42, 43, 44.

Utilizing ANFIS for optimized microstrip patch antenna design

ANFIS has emerged as a powerful tool for optimizing the design parameters of microstrip patch antennas. By combining the adaptive learning capability of neural networks with the interpretability of fuzzy logic, ANFIS can model nonlinear relationships between input parameters and antenna characteristics with remarkable precision. This integration significantly reduces the iterative design process traditionally required for antenna optimization.

Dataset preparation

The dataset used for training and testing the proposed model was generated through parametric electromagnetic simulations conducted in Ansys HFSS. The input parameter space consisted of key antenna design variables that influence the geometry and performance of a microstrip patch antenna. The dataset used in this study was divided into two segments: 70% for training and 30% for testing. The training dataset was utilized to train the ANFIS model to predict the patch antenna’s dimensions and feed point locations based on the input parameters. The testing dataset validated the model’s performance, providing feedback for refinement.

The dataset used in this study was primarily generated using full-wave electromagnetic simulations performed in Ansys HFSS. Training algorithm

ANFIS uses a hybrid learning algorithm that combines the Least Squares Method (LSM) and Backpropagation (BP) to iteratively minimize prediction errors.

The premise parameters define the shape and location of the fuzzy membership functions (e.g., mean, width, slope) and are updated using gradient descent via BP.

The consequent parameters determine the linear functions in the rule outputs and are estimated using LSM in a closed-form solution at each epoch.

This hybrid approach allows rapid convergence with high accuracy while maintaining the interpretability of the fuzzy inference system.

This hybrid approach allows rapid convergence with high accuracy while maintaining the interpretability of the fuzzy inference system.

For example, consider the hybrid learning update in the training cycle: during each epoch, the ANFIS model first fixes the premise parameters (which define the shape of the membership functions) and uses the Least Squares Method (LSM) to compute optimal consequent parameters for all rules based on current training data. Once these consequent parameters are estimated, the model switches to a backpropagation (BP) phase, where the error between the ANFIS output and the target value is propagated backward to update the premise parameters. This two-step process, LSM for fast linear optimization and BP for nonlinear tuning is repeated iteratively until the model converges to a minimal overall error. This hybrid mechanism significantly improves convergence efficiency and model accuracy compared to using either approach in isolation.

Design optimization stages

The optimization process was carried out in three key stages. In the first stage, Patch Width (PatchX) Optimization, the input parameters included the minimum reflection coefficient (S₁₁), resonant frequency (fr), bandwidth, and substrate height (h). The output from this stage was the optimized value for PatchX. Next, in the Patch Length (PatchY) Optimization stage, the input parameters expanded to include the previously optimized PatchX, along with S₁₁, fr, bandwidth, and substrate height (h). This stage produced the optimized PatchY.

Finally, the Feed Point Optimization stage utilized the resonant frequency, PatchX, PatchY, and substrate height as input parameters. The output from this stage were the optimized coordinates for the feed point: FeedX and FeedY.

Advantages of ANFIS for antenna design

The application of ANFIS in the design of microstrip patch antennas offers several key advantages. First, it reduces complexity by minimizing the need for iterative manual optimization, which accelerates the design process. Additionally, ANFIS provides high accuracy, as evidenced by the close agreement between the predicted results and HFSS simulations. Another advantage is its generalizability, as the trained model can accommodate a wide range of input parameters, making it flexible and adaptable for various design requirements. Overall, this systematic approach demonstrates the effectiveness of ANFIS in optimizing critical antenna design parameters, offering a robust framework for microstrip patch antenna development. A key contribution of this study is the systematic evaluation of multiple membership functions and their impact on training stability and prediction accuracy. This level of comparative analysis is often overlooked in antenna design literature, yet it significantly influences model reliability and generalizability.

Discussion

The comparison between the ANFIS-predicted values and the HFSS simulation results demonstrates the effectiveness of the ANFIS model in predicting the key design parameters. It also highlights the significant impact of membership function selection on the accuracy of the predictions.

For PatchX, the triangular membership function achieved the lowest MAE, demonstrating its effectiveness in predicting patch width. The simplicity and balanced nature of this function make it particularly well-suited for this parameter, allowing for accurate and efficient modeling. For PatchY, the Gaussian membership function provided the best performance, thanks to its smooth and localized nature.

To enhance the evaluation of ANFIS prediction accuracy, we also computed Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) for each output parameter across all membership functions. These metrics complement MAE by capturing both the magnitude and variance of prediction errors. For example, using the generalized bell membership function, RMSE for FeedY was found to be 0.022, indicating a strong correlation between ANFIS predictions and HFSS results. Across all parameters, the generalized bell function consistently achieved the lowest RMSE, confirming its superiority in modeling nonlinear relationships in antenna design.

Table 6 shows RMSE values for each membership function. To further validate the regression quality of ANFIS predictions, we also calculated the Coefficient of Determination (R²) for each output parameter across all membership functions. Table 7 shows, R² values closer to 1.0 indicate strong correlation between predicted and simulated values. As shown in Table 5, the triangular and Gaussian membership functions achieved near-perfect R² for all parameters. Notably, Gaussian outperformed others on FeedX and FeedY, confirming its consistency in modeling smooth nonlinear transitions. This characteristic makes it ideal for modeling the patch length, enabling precise predictions even with variations in input parameters. The trapezoidal membership function excelled in predicting FeedX, showcasing its ability to handle broader input ranges effectively. Its versatility in accommodating a wide range of input-output relationships highlights its suitability for this parameter.

Computational efficiency comparison with existing optimization methods

Table 8 compares the proposed ANFIS-based optimization approach with several machine-learning-based antenna optimization methods reported in the literature. Traditional antenna optimization techniques typically rely on repeated full-wave electromagnetic (EM) simulations using tools such as CST Microwave Studio or HFSS. These approaches are computationally intensive because each geometric modification requires a new simulation run, leading to long optimization times, especially for complex antenna structures. In contrast, machine-learning-based models learn the nonlinear relationship between antenna parameters and performance metrics from a dataset generated through simulations. Once trained, these models can predict antenna responses almost instantly without requiring additional EM simulations. As shown in Table X, previous studies have reported computational time reductions ranging from approximately 70 to 80% compared with conventional simulation-based methods. The proposed ANFIS model demonstrates very high prediction accuracy with R² values close to 1 and extremely low RMSE values, indicating reliable performance prediction while significantly reducing computational overhead. Consequently, the proposed method offers an efficient and scalable alternative for optimizing antenna parameters.

Conclusion

This study confirms the effectiveness of the Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS) in optimizing microstrip patch antenna designs, demonstrating its ability to achieve high precision with minimal computational effort. By analyzing four membership function types “triangular, trapezoidal, Gaussian, and generalized bell” the research emphasizes the significant role of membership function selection in enhancing optimization accuracy and efficiency. Among the membership functions evaluated, the generalized bell function emerged as the top performer, achieving an MAE of 1.0261 mm for FeedY, the most accurate prediction across all parameters. Similarly, the triangular membership function excelled for PatchX with a MAE of 0.0182 mm, while the Gaussian function delivered superior results for PatchY with a MAE of 0.1117 mm, and the trapezoidal function showed strength for FeedX with an MAE of 2.5367 mm. Overall, the generalized bell function exhibited 15–25% lower errors compared to other membership functions, proving its superior adaptability for complex, nonlinear relationships. The training process stabilized efficiently within approximately 150 epochs, ensuring convergence without excessive computational demands. The streamlined ANFIS approach reduced design complexity and eliminated the need for manual iterative adjustments, improving the scalability and robustness of the optimization framework.

These results highlight the potential of ANFIS for advancing microstrip patch antenna designs, with applications in wearable devices, biomedical systems, and next-generation communication technologies. Future research should investigate additional membership function types and extend the framework to more diverse datasets and real-world scenarios. This study lays the groundwork for further advancements, demonstrating how a systematic, data-driven approach can transform the design and optimization of cutting-edge antennas. Although this work focuses on single-band antenna optimization, the ANFIS model can be extended to multiband and reconfigurable antenna systems by incorporating additional input parameters and output targets. This presents a promising direction for future research, particularly in adaptive wireless systems.
