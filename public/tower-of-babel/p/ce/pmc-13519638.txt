Increased VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio is associated with large cerebral white matter hyperintensities
Authors: Charlotte M Story, Lisa Yanek, Dhananjay Vaidya, Jenna Brown, Ivo M B Francischetti, Paul Nyquist, Shruti Chaturvedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Increased VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio is associated with large WMH on brain magnetic resonance imaging.•Disruption of the VWF-ADAMTS13 axis is implicated in vascular disease pathogenesis and may be a biomarker of cerebrovascular disease risk.

Introduction

ADAMTS13 (a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with a thrombospondin motif repeats 13) cleaves von Willebrand factor (VWF), a large multimeric glycoprotein with multiple roles in hemostasis. Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency causes thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), a life-threatening disorder in which microthrombus formation leads to catastrophic outcomes, including stroke and death.1 In non-TTP populations, lower levels of ADAMTS13 have been implicated in the development of cardiovascular disease (CVD), ischemic stroke, and increased mortality.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 In addition, multiple prospective studies have demonstrated an association between elevated VWF levels and vascular sequelae such as myocardial infarction (MI) and stroke.2,3,8, 9, 10, 11, 12 Although perturbations in the VWF-ADAMTS13 axis are implicated in the risk of overt stroke, the impact on cerebral ischemic white matter disease such as white matter hyperintensities (WMH), which are considered precursor lesions on the spectrum of cerebrovascular disease, has not been previously studied.13, 14, 15

WMH are areas of abnormal signal intensity often found in the periventricular and subcortical white matter observed on brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).16 WMH increase in frequency with age and have been shown to be an independent risk factor for cognitive and motor impairment, stroke, and dementia.17, 18, 19, 20 Multiple studies have found increased WMH in patients with vascular risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and smoking.21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26 Although WMH were previously treated as imaging artefacts or expected indicators of aging, they are now widely recognized as a variety of small vessel cerebrovascular disease.18

In this study, we tested the hypothesis that an increase in the ratio of VWF antigen to ADAMTS13 activity (VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio) is an independent risk factor for the presence of WMH in a cohort enriched for genetic and environmental factors associated with earlier vascular disease. We separately examined the association of VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio with large WMH (≥3 mm), which are more strongly associated with cognitive impairment, and ischemic stroke.18, 19, 20

Methods

Data source and study population

The Genetic Study of Atherosclerosis Risk (GeneSTAR) is an ongoing prospective cohort study at the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine initiated in 1982 to characterize genetic and environmental risk factors for CVD in European and African American families with early-onset (age < 60 years) coronary artery disease.27, 28, 29 The GeneSTAR research center is active within the Division of General Internal Medicine, Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine. Briefly, probands hospitalized with MI or unstable angina with coronary revascularization or acute angina with flow-limiting stenosis of >50% diameter in at least 1 coronary artery at the age of <60 years were identified in any of 10 Baltimore area hospitals and then their initially healthy siblings aged <60 years were recruited. Starting in 2002, adult offspring of both the probands and siblings along with the coparent of the offspring were also enrolled.30 Prospective enrollees were excluded if they had a history of stroke, transient ischemic attack, or MI or symptoms consistent with angina at the time of enrollment. Healthy siblings and offspring were invited to return for an MRI study31,32 between 2009 and 2013 if they remained free of MI; CVD; stroke/transient ischemic attack; serious chronic illness such as systemic autoimmune disease, chronic kidney disease, or neurologic diseases (dementia, Parkinson disease, multiple sclerosis, or life-threatening comorbidity such as acquired immunodeficiency syndrome or cancer); had no implanted metal precluding MRI; and were not claustrophobic. The GeneSTAR cohort includes 3949 participants from 894 families and continues to be followed for incident cardiovascular events.

All enrolled participants underwent a comprehensive screening, with all testing and laboratory draws performed at initial enrollment and all subsequent study visits. A study physician took a detailed medical history, recorded active medication use, and performed a physical examination. Height was measured in inches and weight in kilograms; body mass index was calculated as kilograms per meters squared. Current cigarette smoking was assessed via a standardized questionnaire and/or by expired carbon monoxide levels. Blood pressure was recorded 3 times over an 8-hour screening visit, and hypertension was defined as an average blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg systolic or ≥90 mm Hg diastolic and/or use of an antihypertensive drug. Blood was drawn for fasting measurement of total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, triglycerides, and glucose; glucose concentration was measured using the glucose oxidase method. Type 2 diabetes was defined by either a documented physician diagnosis, a fasting plasma glucose concentration of ≥126 mg/dL, or the use of glucose-lowering pharmacologic therapy. Further information can be found regarding concurrent study measurements in prior publication.32

For this analysis, among 808 participants who provided consent and completed MRI, 287 had measured VWF antigen levels from a prior substudy33 and stored citrated plasma available for ADAMTS13 testing (Figure 1). The Johns Hopkins Institutional Review Board has reviewed and approved this protocol.

Case selection.

Cerebrovascular imaging and WMH determination

Participants underwent cerebrovascular imaging with brain MRI on a Philips 3.0-Tesla MRI scanner between 2009 and 2013. The MRI protocol included axial T1-weighted MPRAGE sequences with fluid-attentuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) contiguous slices (field of view: 240 mm; matrix size: 256 × 256 × 160 mm) and axial turbo spin echo FLAIR sequences, also with contiguous slices (field of view: 240 mm; matrix: 256 × 256 mm). A board-certified neuroradiologist evaluated all images. Additional details regarding MRI protocol are available in previous GeneSTAR publications.31 WMH volume was measured using the Medical Image Processing, Analysis, and Visualization software package as previously described.34 Total intracranial volume was also computed using Medical Image Processing, Analysis, and Visualization, and WMH burden in different areas was identified with Lesion-Topology-preserving Anatomical Segmentation (TOADS). Segmented brain volumes and the WMH volume were quantified automatically using a multichannel classifier based on a support vector machine approach.

ADAMTS13 and VWF testing

VWF antigen was determined using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; DiaPharma, West Chester, OH) at the University of Maryland Cytokine Laboratory from samples collected at a study visit between 2003 and 2006. ADAMTS13 activity was measured using fluorescence resonance energy transfer methods at the Johns Hopkins Hospital Special Coagulation Lab, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (ADAMTS-13 Activity Assay; Werfen) using a Synergy HTX multimode microplate reader, interfaced with Gen5 2.09 Software (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT) using stored citrated plasma from routine study follow-up.

Inflammatory and endothelial marker testing

Markers of endothelial inflammation including interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1), intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), and vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1) were measured at the Cytokine Core Laboratory, The University of Maryland, Baltimore using 2-antibody ELISA with commercial antibody pairs (IL-6: Pierce Endogen; MCP-1, ICAM-1, VCAM-1, and TNF-α: R&D Systems) using biotin-streptavidin-peroxidase detection. Additional information, including the ELISA protocol, is available in the supplemental Materials.

Statistical methods

Data were summarized as counts (and percentages) and medians (interquartile range [IQR], representing Q1-Q3) for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. The primary outcomes of interest were the presence of any WMH and large WMH, defined as WMH lesions ≥3 mm in any dimension; larger WMH are associated with a higher risk of cognitive impairment, ischemic stroke, and mortality.16,18, 19, 20 Additional exploratory outcomes evaluated were the total WMH lesion volume as well as subcortical, periventricular, brainstem, cerebellar, frontal, insular, occipital, parietal, temporal, and globoid lesion volumes, each taken as a log to account for nonnormal distribution. We used mixed-effects logistic regression to evaluate factors associated with the dependent variable (WMH and large WMH) while accounting for family structure. The primary exposure of interest was the VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio, which was log-transformed before regression to ensure a better fit to the model assumption of a linear relationship with the log-odds of WMH and large WMH presence. Therefore, the odds ratio (OR) for the logarithmically transformed variable are interpreted as the change in the dependent variable (WMH or large WMH) per 2.87 times increase in the VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio. Other covariates were selected based on biologic plausibility and included age, sex, race, and the presence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, smoking, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, and aspirin use, with random effects for family relatedness. Covariate definitions are summarized in Table 1. Separate models were developed for all WMH, large WMH, deep WMH, whole-brain WMH lesion volume, and WMH lesion volume by anatomical area (subcortical, periventricular, brainstem, cerebellum, frontal, insular, occipital, parietal, temporal, and globoid volumes) as dependent variables. The association between VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio and IL-6 levels, as well as age and IL-6 levels, were assessed using Spearman rank correlation due to nonnormal distribution of biomarker levels. We repeated the multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression analysis to incorporate log-transformed IL-6. A P value of <.05 was considered significant. All analyses were performed using Stata version 18.

Results

Cohort demographics and clinical characteristics

A total of 287 participants met the inclusion criteria including availability of VWF measurement, ADAMTS13 activity results, and completed brain MRI (Figure 1). Of the 287 participants, 155 were related to at least one other participant in a total of 63 families; 132 participants were unrelated to all others. Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 2. Most (60.6%) were female and median age at enrollment was 50 years (IQR, 41-59). Most self-identified as White (55.1%) or Black (43.9%) individuals. Vascular risk factors were common; 40.4% had hypertension, 12.9% had diabetes, and 20.9% reported active smoking. The median ADAMTS13 activity was 109.14% (IQR, 93.31%-128%), and the median VWF antigen level was 64.29% (44.07%-97.34%).

Demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with and without WMH

There was a trend toward lower ADAMTS13 activity in those with WMH (median, 106.9%; IQR, 93.2%-127.0%) than in those without WMH (median, 112.6%; IQR, 94.0%-130.6%; P = .322). Median (IQR) VWF antigen levels were higher in those with WMH than in those without WMH (74.5% [44.1%-105.7%] vs 57.2% [43.9%-86.7%]; P = .010). The VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio was also significantly higher in those with WMH (median, 0.623; IQR, 0.434-0.926) than in those without WMH (median, 0.509; IQR, 0.373-0.804) (P = .013).

On multivariable adjusted analysis, the strength of the association of log of the VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio with WMH was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (OR, 1.49; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.95-2.35; P = .082). Age was the only significant predictor of WMH (OR, 1.11; 95% CI, 1.07-1.15; P ≤ .001) in the mixed-effects logistic regression model that accounted for family structure and included age, sex, race, diabetes mellitus, smoking, total and HDL cholesterol, hypertension, aspirin use, and the log of the VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio (Table 4).

Prevalence and predictors of large WMH

Large (≥3 mm) WMH were present in 91 (31.7%) participants. Demographic and clinical characteristics of participants with and without large WMH are summarized in Table 5. Compared with participants without large WMH, those with large WMH were older (median age: 58 years vs 46 years; P ≤ .001), more likely to be hypertensive (49.5% vs 36.2%; P = .034), and had a higher rate of active aspirin use (40.7% vs 18.9%; P ≤ .001). Those with large WMH also had lower median (IQR) ADAMTS13 activity (103.15% [91.00%-122.00%] vs 112.59% [94.13%-130.32%]; P = .046), higher VWF antigen levels (76.65% [IQR, 49.42%-111.68%] vs 60.88% [IQR, 42.89%-88.49%]; P = .010), and higher VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratios (0.702 [IQR, 0.468-1.083] vs 0.514 [IQR, 0.373-0.836]; P = .003) than those without large WMH. The prevalence of large WMH was higher in participants in the highest quartile of VWF-to-ADAMTS13 activity (42.8%) than those in the lowest quartile (21.9%) (Figure 2).

Univariate analysis by presence or absence of large WMH

Large WMH prevalence increases in dose-dependent manner with increasing VWF/ADAMTS13 quartile.

The log of the VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio (OR, 1.93; 95% CI, 1.21-3.07; P = .006) and age (OR, 1.11; 95% CI, 1.07-1.15; P ≤ .001) were significantly associated with large WMH in the multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression model adjusted for sex, race, diabetes mellitus, smoking, total and HDL cholesterol, hypertension, aspirin use, and accounting for family structure (Table 6). Therefore, for every 2.72-fold increase in the VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio, the odds of large WMH increase by 92%, and every 10% increase in the VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio is associated with an ∼6% increase in the odds of large WMH.

Separate multilevel mixed-effects linear regression analyses were performed to evaluate the predictors of total WMH lesion volume as well as WMH lesion volume in different anatomic areas (subcortical, periventricular, frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital, brainstem, cerebellum, insular regions, etc). Age, race, and smoking were significantly associated with lesion volumes in some models of specific anatomic areas (supplemental Tables 1-10). The VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio was significantly associated with WMH volume only in the globoid WMH model. Given the negative β-coefficient and absence of similar findings in other models, this result was attributed to likely type I error. The model examining cerebellar volume is not included because most participants had no cerebellar WMH, precluding log transformation.

Markers of inflammation and endothelial activation

We performed Spearman correlations between log(VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio) and other log-transformed inflammatory markers including IL-6, TNF-α, MCP-1, ICAM-1, and VCAM-1. There was a weak positive correlation between log-transformed IL-6 and log-transformed VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio (Spearman’s rs, 0.1292; P = .034); the other markers were not statistically significant (supplemental Table 12). None of these markers were associated with large WMH on univariate analysis (supplemental Table 13). We repeated the multilevel mixed logistic regression analysis to incorporate the log-transformed IL-6 variable to examine whether the effect of the VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio on large WMH would be abrogated. The impact of log(VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio) on large WMH remained significant (OR, 2.04; 95% CI, 1.25-3.32) (supplemental Table 15).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, we show, to our knowledge, for the first time an association between increased VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio with large WMH, an imaging manifestation of cerebral small vessel disease, which is associated with a slew of adverse outcomes including cognitive decline, ischemic stroke, and mortality.17, 18, 19, 20 The role of the VWF-ADAMTS13 axis is increasingly recognized in the pathogenesis of thrombosis and vascular disease. VWF is synthesized in endothelial cells and megakaryocytes as large multimers with high hemostatic activity. VWF facilitates platelet adhesion to exposed subendothelial collagen at sites of vascular injury via platelet glycoprotein Ib-V-IX (GPIb-V-IX) complex binding to the VWF A1 domain and subsequently promotes platelet aggregation.35 It also functions as the carrier protein for FVIII, prolonging its half-life by protecting it from proteolytic degradation.36 ADAMTS13 exerts an antithrombotic effect by cleaving the highly thrombotic ultralarge VWF multimers into smaller, less prothrombotic units.36 Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency causes TTP, a life-threatening disorder in which microthrombus formation leads to catastrophic outcomes, including stroke. In survivors of immune TTP, low ADAMTS13 activity during clinical remission is associated with stroke as well as relapse.37, 38, 39, 40 Even during remission from immune TTP, the rate of silent cerebral infarction (defined as a focus of T2 and FLAIR hyperintensity, visible on 2 planes with no clinical neurologic deficit) is 50%.41 Further supporting the premise that the disruption of the ADAMTS13-VWF homeostasis can tip the balance toward stroke even in the absence of frank TTP comes from congenital TTP cohorts characterized by constitutional severe ADAMTS13 deficiency. The prevalence of stroke is reported at 100% by the age of 50 years among participants in the International Hereditary TTP Registry.42 In both immune and congenital forms of TTP, stroke may occur in the absence of thrombocytopenia.38,39,42,43

In the general (non-TTP) population, low ADAMTS13 activity and elevated VWF levels have also been associated with adverse vascular outcomes. Low ADAMTS13 activity has been associated with the development of CVD, cognitive decline, and ischemic stroke in the general population.3, 4, 5, 6,44 In 2015, Sonneveld et al published a report investigating the role of ADAMTS13 and VWF in stroke risk among 5941 older adults enrolled in the prospective Rotterdam cohort.4 They found that those with ADAMTS13 activity in the lowest quartile (median activity, 70.3%; 95% CI, 69.8%-70.8%) had about double the absolute risk of stroke (11%) after controlling for cardiovascular risk factors compared with that of those with ADAMTS13 activity in the highest quartile (median activity, 114.4%; 95% CI, 113.8%-114.9%). The Rotterdam study also demonstrated that high VWF and low ADAMTS13 activity are both associated with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, independent of cardiovascular risk factors.5 Notably, even patients in this cohort in the lowest quartile of ADAMTS13 activity had levels in the normal range. Multiple prospective studies have shown an association between elevated VWF levels and vascular sequelae such as MI11,12 and stroke.2,3,8, 9, 10,45

Our study examined the association between the VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio, reflecting an imbalance in the VWF and ADAMTS13 axis, and the presence of any or large WMH in a population highly enriched for vascular risk factors. The VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio was slightly higher in those with any WMH (median, 0.623; IQR, 0.434-0.926) than in those without WMH (median, 0.509; IQR, 0.373-0.804) (P = .013), although the VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio did not remain a significant predictor of any WMH after adjusting for other risk factors. Similarly, the VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio was higher in those with large WMH (median, 0.702; IQR, 0.468-1.083) than in those without large WMH (median, 0.514; IQR, 0.373-0.836) (P = .003) and remained significantly associated with large WMH in multivariable analysis adjusted for age, sex, race, diabetes mellitus, smoking, total and high-density cholesterol, hypertension, and aspirin use. There appeared to be a dose-dependent relationship between the VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio and large WMH prevalence, with the highest quartile having nearly double the prevalence of large WMH compared with that of the lowest quartile (42.9% vs 21.9%), as shown in Figure 2. Because IL-6 can downregulate ADAMTS13, we attempted to examine whether IL-6 was associated with the VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio. Interestingly, we found there was a weak positive correlation between log-transformed IL-6 and the VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio, but this did not significantly affect the primary multivariable analyses (supplemental Tables 14 and 15), suggesting that additional drivers affect the VWF-ADAMTS13 axis.

The strengths of this study include its multiethnic, highly diverse makeup. Although the sample size is limited, selecting a population enriched for environmental and genetic risk factors for early-onset CVD likely improved the power to identify a significant relationship between VWF-to-ADAMTS13 and the outcomes. The limited sample size is also likely why diabetes, high cholesterol, and hypertension do not emerge as risk factors for WMH, although they have been shown to be risk factors for WMH in studies with larger sample sizes that can detect smaller effect sizes.16,18,21,22,24,25,31,46 This suggests that underpowering may be a limitation for this study and may explain why an effect is observed with large WMH, but not any WMH. A Japanese study showed that the VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio tends to increase with age, which was also observed in the Rotterdam cohort.47,48 The authors postulated that perhaps this played a part in the increasing prothrombotic risk observed in older adults.47 In our analysis, the VWF-to-ADAMTS13 ratio was a predictor for large WMH after adjusting for age and other risk factors, indicating that this effect is independent of age. Our results in a cohort highly enriched for family and environmental risk factors of vascular disease may not be generalizable to populations with a low incidence of vascular disease, but provide an enhanced opportunity to identify relevant risk factors. As a cross-sectional study, our observation does not establish causality.

We attempted to assess whether there were differences between cases with and without plasma samples for inclusion in this study. Although included and nonincluded participants had similar distributions of sex, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, aspirin use, total and HDL cholesterol, and WMH, included participants were significantly younger with larger WM lesion volume, more likely to be Black individuals, and more likely to have large WMH (supplemental Table 11). The difference in age is statistically significant, but authors do not believe it to be clinically significant (difference in the median age was <2 years). Although patients of Black race were more likely to be included in than study, race was accounted for in the multivariable analysis. Finally, there was a small difference in the percentage of patients with WMH included in the study (31.93% vs 25.34%). These findings indicate that inclusion was not completely random and could introduce bias into the study design.

Another possible limitation is that we are unable to assess for the impact of ABO blood group. It is well documented that the type O blood group is associated with lower circulating VWF levels.49 However, any potential association between ABO type and arterial thrombosis or cerebrovascular disease would be expected to be primarily mediated through VWF.50 Because we directly measured plasma VWF, we assessed the substrate most relevant to thrombosis risk. Therefore, although residual confounding by ABO blood group cannot be entirely excluded, we believe that its principal mechanistic contribution is captured through the measured VWF levels included in our analyses.

It remains unknown whether VWF and ADAMTS13 are markers of inflammation and endothelial dysfunction or are themselves causative agents in stroke pathogenesis in the non-TTP setting: a bidirectional relationship is likely. Complete ADAMTS13 deficiency in mice leads to markedly accelerated atherosclerosis and increased VWF-mediated vascular leukocyte adhesion.51,52 ADAMTS13-deficient mice have increased infarct size when cerebral arteries are occluded, whereas VWF deficiency is protective.53, 54, 55, 56 The regulation of VWF during hypoxia is heterogeneous between endothelial cells of different organs.57 In a mouse model, it has been shown that there is increased secretion of Weibel-Palade bodies from cerebral endothelial cells exposed to hypoxia.58 Inflammatory signals such as IL-6 and IL-1β can downregulate ADAMTS13 in endothelial cells.59,60 The role of turbulent flow and shear stress has also been studied. IL-6 can inhibit the cleavage of ultralarge VWF by ADAMTS13 under flow (but not static) conditions.61 Exposing platelets to shear stress leads to the clustering of GPIbα and thereby enhanced affinity for VWF.62 VWF itself undergoes a conformational change at high shear rates, leading to unfolded conformation with exposure of A1 and A2 domains, which both increases GPIbα-mediated binding with platelets, but also increases its susceptibility to ADAMTS13.63 It appears that during chronic inflammatory states, a relative decrease in ADAMTS13 activity with unchanged or increased VWF-platelet interactions tips the balance toward thrombosis. In the cerebral microvasculature of the patient with inflammation, turbulent flow leads to increased activation and aggregation of platelets with high affinity for VWF, abnormally decreased ADAMTS13 activity secondary to IL-6 and other cytokines, increased VWF secretion from Weibel-Palade bodies, and proliferation of ultralarge-VWF into the already turbulent microvasculature, and increased shear-induced unfolding of VWF and exposure of the A1 domain: a perfect storm for thrombosis.

Our study cannot distinguish whether the trend in increased ADAMTS13:VWF axis is a proxy for endothelial pathology and/or inflammation or is itself a primary driver of arterial thrombosis independent of inflammatory disease. Our limited exploratory investigation of markers of endothelial activation and inflammation did not find a clear association between these markers and large WMH but is likely to be underpowered. Future study should include markers of endothelial activation and inflammation (such as IL-6) to further explore the impact of inflammatory signaling pathways on the VWF-ADAMTS13 axis and vascular pathogenesis. Nonetheless, our findings are hypothesis-generating, particularly given the easy measurability and targetability of VWF and ADAMTS13. Ongoing study is needed to investigate how these markers can aid in identification of non-TTP patients who are at high-risk for the development of stroke. A recent prospective study found an association between the ADAMTS13:VWF ratio and modified Rankin disability scores at discharge for patients hospitalized with ischemic stroke.15 Further work is needed to determine whether ADAMTS13 and VWF are useful for predicting first or recurrent stroke and whether they can be used to improve preexisting stroke prediction tools such as the CHA2DS2-VASc score for predicting stroke risk in atrial fibrillation.64

To conclude, elevated VWF levels and low ADAMTS13 activity are associated with stroke in observational and prospective studies in the literature, but this is the first study to our knowledge that investigates an association between these 2 variables with an earlier lesion, which supports the role of the VWF-ADAMTS13 axis in incipient cerebrovascular disease.
