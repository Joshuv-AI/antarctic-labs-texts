Origin and diversity of leaf vein patterns
Authors: Janlo M Robil, Enrico Scarpella, Mary Byrne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe have all felt at the touch the vein stripes in a grass leaf or have seen against the sun the vein mesh in a fallen leaf. Those veins distribute water to and collect sugars from all leaf areas. How vein networks evolved and develop is therefore a key question in biology. Here we address that question for vascular plants, in whose leaves veins are thought to be arranged in either grids or webs. We will see that those are just two instances of the natural diversity of vein patterns, which evolved independently multiple times. We will also see that diversity may result not from different vein patterning mechanisms but from different leaf growth patterns acting on a single underlying mechanism. Vein patterning mechanisms have been inferred from the patterns with which vascular precursor cells appear in the leaf. Because vascular tissues predated leaves, vein precursor cells should share defining features across all vascular plants. We will see that the identification of vein precursor cells is instead arbitrary, depending on which features are emphasized. Identifying features that are not only shared by vein precursor cells across all vascular plants but that are also required for vein formation will be essential to understand how veins are patterned.Keywords: Leaf evolution, leaf venation, procambium differentiation, vascular pattern, vascular tissues, vein pattern classification, vein pattern diversity, vein pattern evolution, vein patterning, vein precursor cell

Introduction

Most multicellular organisms form networks of tissues to solve the problem of long-distance transport of water, nutrients, and signals. How those tissue networks evolved and developed is therefore a key question in biology. In animals, this essential transport function is distributed over separate tissue networks such as the nervous, circulatory, and respiratory systems. By contrast, in plants, all transport functions are performed by a single tissue network. In vascular plants, that network is composed of interconnected bundles of vascular tissues. Some non-vascular plants have vascular-like tissues (reviewed in Ligrone et al., 2000), but we do not consider these tissues here because they are non-homologous to the vascular tissues of vascular plants.

In leaves, vascular bundles are called veins. Veins are commonly thought to be arranged in either one of two patterns (reviewed in Nelson and Dengler, 1997). In the parallel or striate pattern, longitudinal veins run parallel to one another along the length of the leaf and are bridged by cross-veins. In the reticulate or netted pattern, lateral veins branch off a midvein and are connected by a mesh of minor veins. However, in the ‘Mature vein patterns’ and ‘Vein pattern classification’ sections, we will see that parallel and reticulate patterns are just two examples of the staggering diversity of vein patterns in nature and of how these patterns can be classified.

Parallel and reticulate vein patterns are usually considered hallmarks of monocots and dicots, respectively, and, as such, fundamentally different (reviewed in Nelson and Dengler, 1997). In the ‘Mature vein patterns’ and ‘Leaf and vein pattern evolution’ sections, we will instead see that both patterns evolved independently in all groups of plants, including dicots and monocots, and that the two patterns are simply variations of a common motif.

All the mature vascular cells in the plant are thought to derive from a single precursor tissue: the procambium (reviewed in Esau, 1943b). Procambial cells were first identified by following the reproducible course of vascular bundles in stem longitudinal sections. Closer to the shoot tip, the mature vascular cells in those bundles were gradually replaced by cells with features that operationally defined procambial cells. In leaves, however, the precise course of the veins is variable (reviewed in Scarpella and Berleth, 2013), so it is impossible to follow their course with certainty to identify the youngest procambial cells. Moreover, even at late stages of leaf development, cells with features such as those of stem procambial cells cannot always be discriminated from surrounding non-procambial cells (e.g. Pray, 1955b). As such, in the ‘Leaf procambium differentiation’ section, we will see that the identification of the youngest procambial cells in the leaf is arbitrary and depends on which features are used to define these cells.

Below we will focus on the diversity of vein patterns, how these patterns develop, and how they evolved. Mature vein patterns

Here we will follow a simplified classification of extant land plants based on Judd et al. (2016) and Simpson (2019) (Fig. 1). We will use terms such as ‘enter’, ‘run’, ‘extend’, ‘curve’, ‘fork’, ‘branch’, ‘end’, ‘join’, ‘merge’, and ‘stretch’ descriptively, with no intention to suggest developmental processes. We will use ‘open’ to describe vein patterns in which all the veins connect to other veins only at one of their ends and ‘closed’ to describe vein patterns in which at least one of the veins connects to other veins at both of their ends (reviewed in Roth-Nebelsick et al., 2001). Vein pattern features and their formation

In their simplest form, vein patterns are defined by three features: (i) one or more midveins (MVs) that run the length of the leaf blade; (ii) lateral veins (LVs) that branch off the midvein and that can join distal veins to form closed vein loops (VLs); and (iii) minor veins (HVs) that branch off the midvein and lateral veins, and that can either join other veins or end freely in the leaf tissue. In some vein pattern classifications, midveins are referred to as primary veins or veins of the first order, lateral veins as secondary veins or veins of the second order, and minor veins as veins of the third and higher order. Mature veins differentiate from procambial strands (PSs), which are bundles of files of procambial cells (PCs). Procambial cells differentiate from ground cells (GCs), which are the polygonal, isodiametric, inner cells of the developing leaf that also give rise to the mesophyll cells—the photosynthetic cells of the leaf.

Lycophytes

The usually small sporophytic leaves of extant club mosses, quillworts, and spike mosses are called microphylls. Most microphylls only have a midvein that runs the length of the leaf (Fig. 2A), but some spike moss leaves have more complex vein patterns. In some of them, lateral veins branch off the midvein, but the vein patterns remain open (Mukherjee and Sen, 1981) (Fig. 2B). In some others, minor veins branch off the midvein and lateral veins, and either end freely in the leaf or connect to other veins at both ends, giving rise to closed vein patterns (Wagner et al., 1982) (Fig. 2C).

Euphyllophytes

The usually large sporophytic leaves of extant ferns and seed plants are called megaphylls.

Ferns

Leptosporangiatae

The leaves of a few leptosporangiate ferns only have a midvein, like the leaves of most lycophytes (Benedict, 1911) (Fig. 2D). However, the leaves of most leptosporangiate ferns have more complex vein patterns, some of which are open and some of which are closed (Benedict, 1911; Troll, 1939; Saha, 1958; Nayar, 1966) (Fig. 2E–H). Some of those vein patterns have a clear midvein, but some others lack it.

Seed plants

Gymnosperms

Cycads

The leaves of most cycads lack a midvein, and the veins that enter those leaves fork repeatedly until the last veins end freely near the leaf margin (Stevenson et al., 1996) (Fig. 2I). The leaves of the few remaining cycads have a midvein. In most of those cycads, lateral veins branch off the midvein. In some of those cycads, the lateral veins end freely at the margin, leaving the vein pattern open; in the others, the lateral veins fork and merge with one another near the leaf margin, closing the vein pattern (Fig. 2J).

Ginkgos

Two veins enter ginkgo leaves (Arnott, 1959) (Fig. 2K). Each of those veins forks, and each of the resulting veins forks repeatedly until the last veins end freely near the leaf margin.

Conifers

Conifer leaves have a single vein or multiple parallel veins that end freely near the leaf tip, leaving the vein pattern open (Kausik and Bhattacharya, 1977; Chambers et al., 1998) (Fig. 2L).

Gnetophytes

Ephedra leaves have two or three parallel veins that run the length of the leaf and either merge or end freely near the leaf tip (Foster, 1972) (Fig. 2M). Welwitschia leaves have many such parallel veins (Rodin, 1958) (Fig. 2N). Minor veins branch off neighboring parallel veins, run towards the leaf tip, and merge into single veins, which end freely in the leaf or join the next distal pair of minor veins. In Gnetum leaves, lateral veins branch off the midvein and join distal veins to form closed loops (Rodin, 1966) (Fig. 2O). Minor veins branch off the midvein and lateral veins, and either join other veins or end freely in the leaf.

Flowering plants

Dicots

Amborella and Austrobaileyales

The leaves of both Amborella and Austrobaileyales have a midvein and lateral veins that branch off the midvein and join distal veins to form closed loops (Bailey and Swamy, 1948; Brodribb et al., 2010) (Fig. 2P). Minor veins branch off the midvein and lateral veins, and either join other veins or end freely in the leaf.

Eudicots

The leaves of most eudicots have a midvein; lateral veins that branch off the midvein and join distal veins to form closed loops; and minor veins that branch off the midvein and lateral veins, and either join other veins or end freely in the leaf (reviewed in, for example, Nelson and Dengler, 1997) (Fig. 2Q). In a few of those eudicots, lateral veins branch off the midvein near the leaf base, run parallel to one another along the whole length of the leaf, and merge near the leaf tip to form stretched-out loops (Goebel, 1905; Troll, 1939; Klucking, 1988; Dickison and Weitzman, 1996) (Fig. 2R). The midvein and stretched-out loops are connected transversely by minor veins. Irrespective of whether vein loops are stretched out or flattened along the whole length of the leaf, all those vein patterns are closed. In a few eudicots, however, the veins that enter the leaf fork repeatedly end freely near the leaf margin, leaving the vein pattern open (Foster and Arnott, 1960; Foster, 1966; Subramanyam and Yoganarasimhan, 1979) (Fig. 2S).

Monocots

The leaves of most monocots have a midvein; lateral veins that branch off the midvein near the leaf base, run parallel to one another along the whole length of the leaf, and join distal veins near the leaf tip to form closed stretched-out loops; and minor veins that connect transversely the neighboring, parallel veins (reviewed in, for example, Nelson and Dengler, 1997) (Fig. 2T). However, in a few monocots, lateral veins branch off along the whole length of the midvein as they do in most eudicots (Goebel, 1905; Von Ertl, 1932; Troll, 1939; Conover, 1983; Inamdar et al., 1983; Kores, 1995; Cameron and Dickison, 1998; Boyce, 2000) (Fig. 2U). In a few of those monocots, lateral veins fail to join other veins, leaving the vein pattern open (e.g. Sargent and Wangermann, 1959) (Fig. 2V).

Vein pattern classification

Vein patterns are usually classified according to the relative thickness of the veins and their distribution in the leaf. Most classifications are based on the number of primary veins, which include lateral veins as thick as the midvein, and attributes of secondary veins such as their number, their course, and the positions and angles of their branching from primary veins.

The first of such classifications was published by von Ettingshausen in 1861 (von Ettingshausen, 1861). von Ettingshausen's classification of dicot vein patterns was updated and expanded in 1973 by Hickey, who included leaf features such as shape and margin configurations (Hickey, 1973). In 1979, Hickey revised his original classification of dicot vein patterns to include compound leaves and a more detailed classification of margin configurations (Hickey, 1979). Spicer's 1986 classification of dicot vein patterns modifies Hickey's by avoiding the distinction between primary and secondary veins, which commonly intergrade, but retains leaf features (Spicer, 1986).

The only consideration for leaf features in Melville's 1976 classification of vein patterns in flowering plants is the inclusion of compound leaves (Melville, 1976). However, unlike previous classifications, Melville's in part reflects the evolution of vein patterns and as such includes the systems of branches containing a single vein from which megaphylls are thought to have evolved (see ‘Leaf and vein pattern evolution’ below). Melville's classification was updated and expanded in 1995 and 1997 by Klucking, who in his classification of vein patterns in vascular plants included leaves that have no obvious midvein, such as those of ginkgos and water lilies (Klucking, 1995, 1997) (Fig. 2K).

Finally, in his 1991 classification of vein patterns in flowering plants, Pole considered leaf growth and development (Pole, 1991). As such, in Pole's classification, whether a vein is, for example, a vein of the third or fourth order is derived from when and how during leaf development that vein appears and not from the thickness of the vein at its point of origin as in previous classifications. Pole's insight has been confirmed in time-lapse imaging of vein formation, which has revealed that what in a mature leaf seems to be a fourth-order vein branching off a third-order vein may in fact be the product of the merging of two third-order veins (Sawchuk et al., 2007; Marcos and Berleth, 2014) (Fig. 3A–F). Moreover, in Pole's classification, vein pattern features that seem different from one another in mature leaves may arise simply because of different leaf growth patterns that modify initially identical features. An example of that is minor veins and lateral veins, which can remain recognizable as distinct in mature leaves but whose separate identities can be confounded by leaf growth such that they appear as single vein loops at leaf maturity (Kang and Dengler, 2004; Scarpella et al., 2004, 2006) (Fig. 3G–K).

Leaf procambium differentiation

All the mature vascular cells in the plant are thought to derive from the same precursor tissue—the procambium—whose cells are typically organized in files or bundles of files called strands (Esau, 1965). In the leaf, procambial cells differentiate from the same isodiametric, polygonal ground cells that give rise to the mesophyll (Foster, 1935a). Two features have been reported to set apart leaf procambial cells from surrounding ground cells (reviewed in Esau, 1943b): (i) procambial cells are elongated, whereas ground cells are isodiametric; and (ii) in tissue sections, the cytoplasm of procambial cells stains darker than that of surrounding ground cells, possibly because of fewer, smaller vacuoles. However, at least in some cases, the darker staining of the cytoplasm of procambial cells is due to the vacuolation of the surrounding ground cells (Esau, 1942; Majumdar, 1942; Reeve, 1942; Vaughan, 1952). Furthermore, the cytoplasm of procambial cells may seem to stain darker because, given their smaller diameter, more of these cells occur in longitudinal sections (Esau, 1942). Finally, because of the internal position of procambial cells, their cytoplasm is more difficult to fix, shrinks more, and stains darker than that of the surrounding ground cells (Esau, 1942).

Leaf procambial cells have not been reported to dedifferentiate into ground cells or transdifferentiate into mesophyll cells. Therefore, by the time procambial strands differentiate, veins are patterned. As such, vein patterning mechanisms have long been inferred from the patterns with which procambial strands differentiate (reviewed in, for example, Esau, 1943b; Nelson and Dengler, 1997; Scarpella and Meijer, 2004; Sawchuk et al., 2008; Scarpella et al., 2010; Biedroń and Banasiak, 2018). In turn, inferring patterns of procambial strand differentiation requires that the youngest procambial cells can be unambiguously identified. However, we found that which cells in the literature are interpreted as the first procambial cells is arbitrary.

In a few cases, no evidence was presented to support how the youngest procambial cells were identified and how the patterns of procambial strand differentiation were inferred (Langdon, 1927; Hara, 1962; Ramji, 1967; Franck, 1979). In a few other cases, the evidence was only presented as drawings (Langdon, 1931; Cross, 1937a; Johnson, 1943; Slade, 1959; Ramji, 1961b). In some of the cases in which the evidence was presented in part or in full as photographs, the shape and size of procambial cells were no different from those of ground cells, and the cytoplasm of the youngest procambial cells stained no different from that of surrounding ground cells (Boke, 1940; Cross and Johnson, 1941; Satina and Blakeslee, 1941; Esau, 1942, 1943a; Vaughan, 1952; Girolami, 1954; Denne, 1960; Arnott, 1962; Pray, 1963, Ramji, 1967; Hitch and Sharman, 1968) (Fig. 4A).

In some other cases in which the evidence was presented in part or in full as photographs, continuity or direction of procambial strand differentiation was inferred from transverse leaf sections (e.g. Sharman and Hitch, 1967; Merrill, 1979; Isebrands and Larson, 1980). However, if procambial cells are not oriented perpendicular to the section plane, they will be cut on a slant; their small diameter will be obscured; and they will not be recognized as procambial (Esau, 1942) (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, sectioning, whether transversely or longitudinally, procambial strands whose course is meandering could lead to the conclusion that procambial strands differentiate discontinuously (Esau, 1942; Crafts, 1943; Vaughan, 1952) (Fig. 4C, D), and it often mistakenly did lead to that conclusion (e.g. Langdon, 1927; Foster, 1935a, b; Cross, 1937a, b; Hsü, 1944; Hitch and Sharman, 1968) (Fig. 4E).

Finally, even when leaves were sectioned longitudinally and the meandering course of procambial strands was followed across serial sections, in most cases, patterns of procambium differentiation were inferred from single developmental stages (e.g. Vaughan, 1954; Pray, 1955a; Ramji, 1961a, b; Hara, 1962; Franck, 1979; Merrill, 1979). From those single stages, the existence of procambial strands that were connected to other veins on one end only was taken as evidence that procambial strands differentiate progressively along individual lines; that is, ground cells that are directly in contact with pre-existing veins differentiate first into procambial cells, and differentiation extends away from those initiation points. Likewise, the existence of procambial strands that were connected to other veins on both ends was taken as evidence that procambial strands differentiate simultaneously along individual lines; that is, all the ground cells in a given file differentiate simultaneously into procambial cells. In other words, evidence of partial procambial strand differentiation was assumed to be evidence of progressive procambial strand differentiation, and evidence of complete procambial strand differentiation was assumed to be evidence of simultaneous procambial strand differentiation. Below we will review evidence that addressed all those shortcomings.

Euphyllophytes

Ferns

Leptosporangiatae

In the leaves of a few leptosporangiate ferns, the youngest procambial cells stain lighter than surrounding ground cells (Gaudet, 1964; Croxdale, 1976; Qiu et al., 1995) (Fig. 4F), but in the leaves of most leptosporangiate ferns, the cytoplasm of the youngest procambial cells stains no different from that of surrounding ground cells (Wardlaw, 1944; Saha, 1958; Pray, 1962; Kuehnert and Larson, 1983; Zurakowski and Gifford, 1988). However, because of the narrow shape of those procambial cells, their elongated nuclei are more frequently visible in tissue sections than the nuclei of surrounding, larger ground cells. Therefore, because of their higher nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio, the youngest procambial cells seem to stain darker than surrounding ground cells (Fig. 4G). Eventually, ground cells are set apart from procambial cells because ground cells vacuolate or accumulate darkly stained material (Pray, 1960, 1962).

Procambial cells differentiate first near the leaf margin, and procambial strands extend from the margin of the leaf to its base (Wardlaw, 1944; Saha, 1958). Procambial strands can curve towards each other and seemingly join by running next to each other (Saha, 1963; Nair and Das, 1973; Korn, 1998). Extension of procambial strands can be terminated before they join other strands by differentiation of the mesophyll between the extending strands and the existing ones, eventually giving rise to vein fragments (Saha, 1958).

Seed plants

Gymnosperms

Conifers

In conifer leaves, the cytoplasm of the youngest procambial cells stains no different from that of surrounding ground cells, but the youngest procambial cells have an elongated nucleus and shape (Crafts, 1943).

In the leaves of seed plants, it is generally accepted that the median procambial strand first differentiates near the base of the leaf primordium and then extends towards the tip of the primordium (reviewed in, for example, Nelson and Dengler, 1997). However, in the leaves of the conifers in which the differentiation of the median procambial strand has been investigated, the distance between the tip of the median procambial strand and the epidermis at the tip of the leaf primordium fails to decrease over time as the primordium lengthens (Cross, 1942) (Fig. 4H), failing to support that the median procambial strand extends toward the tip of the leaf primordium.

Flowering plants

Dicots

Magnoliids

In magnoliid leaves, the cytoplasm of the youngest procambial cells stains no different from that of surrounding ground cells, but the youngest procambial cells have an elongated nucleus and shape, which is thought to result from cell divisions parallel to the differentiating procambial strand (Pray, 1955a, 1963; Ramji, 1961a).

Chloranthaceae

In Chloranthaceae leaves, the cytoplasm of the youngest procambial cells stains darker than that of surrounding ground cells. Eventually procambial cells also acquire their elongated shape and nucleus (Ramji, 1961b) (Fig. 4I).

Eudicots

In the leaves of some eudicots, the first sign of procambial differentiation is cell elongation (Vaughan, 1952; Pray, 1963; Isebrands and Larson, 1980; Deschamp and Cooke, 1985). In the leaves of some other eudicots, the first sign of procambium differentiation is the division of ground cells parallel to the differentiating procambial strand (Ramji, 1967; Herbst, 1972). Furthermore, in the leaves of yet other eudicots, the first sign of procambial differentiation is the darker staining of the cytoplasm (Ramji, 1967) (Fig. 4J).

In the leaves of those eudicots in which it is the darker staining of the cytoplasm of procambial cells that sets them apart from surrounding ground cells, procambial cells differentiate simultaneously with one another along the whole length of the differentiating procambial strand (Ramji, 1967). Likewise, the median procambial strand seems to differentiate simultaneously along the length of the whole leaf primordium, as opposed to extending towards the tip of the leaf primordium, because the distance between the tip of the median procambial strand and the epidermis at the tip of the leaf primordium fails to decrease over time as the primordium lengthens (Foster, 1935a; Boke, 1940; Girolami, 1954; Merrill, 1979; Deschamp and Cooke, 1985) (Fig. 4K). Moreover, stem procambial strands do not seem to extend towards the leaf primordium, and the median procambial strand of the leaf primordium does not seem to extend towards the stem vascular strands, suggesting that the median procambial strand of the leaf primordium is always continuous with stem vascular strands from early on (Boke, 1940, 1941; Cross and Johnson, 1941; Majumdar, 1942; Reeve, 1942; Vaughan, 1952).

Monocots

In the leaves of some monocots, the first sign of procambial differentiation is the darker staining of the nucleus (Sharman, 1942) or of the cytoplasm (Esau, 1943a; Pray, 1955b; Arnott, 1962) (Fig. 4L). In the leaves of some other monocots, the first sign of procambial differentiation is the elongation of the cell or of the nucleus (Hsü, 1944; Pray, 1955b, 1963). In the leaves of yet other monocots, the youngest procambial cells are set apart by the vacuolation of the ground cells (Denne, 1960). Finally, in the leaves of the remaining monocots, the first sign of procambium differentiation is the division of ground cells parallel to the differentiating procambial strand (Sharman, 1942; Pray, 1955a, b; Arnott, 1962; Blackman, 1971) (Fig. 4M).

In the leaves of those monocots in which the first sign of procambium differentiation is the division of ground cells parallel to the differentiating procambial strand, these cell divisions do not appear to be initiated in one specific position, and there is no indication of progressive differentiation of the procambial strand, suggesting that ground cells divide either simultaneously or randomly along the length of the differentiating procambial strand (Pray, 1955b; Blackman, 1971). In the leaves of those monocots in which it is the vacuolation of the ground cells that sets them apart from procambial cells, procambial strands do not differentiate progressively but are blocked out as a whole (Denne, 1960). Likewise, it seems that major procambial strands—the median and lateral procambial strands—are blocked out as a whole along the length of the entire leaf primordium, as opposed to extending towards the tip of the leaf primordium, because the distance between the tip of the major procambial strands and the epidermis at the tip of the leaf primordium fails to decrease over time as the primordium lengthens (Hitch and Sharman, 1968) (Fig. 4N). Moreover, stem procambial strands do not seem to extend towards the developing leaf primordium, and major procambial strands of the leaf primordium do not seem to extend toward the stem vascular strands, suggesting that major procambial strands are always continuous with stem vascular strands from early on (Priestley et al., 1935; Esau, 1943a; Denne, 1960; Dengler et al., 1997). In some monocots, however, it does seem that at least some minor procambial strands differentiate progressively (Pray, 1955a, b; Arnott, 1962).

Leaf and vein pattern evolution

The body of the earliest known vascular plants consisted of naked aerial axes that repeatedly branched into two equal axes oriented in three dimensions (Stewart and Rothwell, 1993; Kenrick and Crane, 1997) (Fig. 5A–C). From those leafless plants, microphylls are thought to have evolved once and megaphylls several times (reviewed in Tomescu, 2009) (Fig. 1). In each of those several times, megaphylls are thought to have evolved through unequal branching to give rise to a dominant axis and non-dominant axes, branching of the non-dominant axes in only two dimensions, and formation of tissue to fill the space between those non-dominant axes (Zimmermann, 1952) (Fig. 5D–G). By contrast, microphylls are thought to have evolved from either axis reduction or vascularization of axis outgrowths (Bower, 1935; Zimmermann, 1952; Kenrick and Crane, 1997) (Fig. 5H, I).

Irrespective of their origin, the first microphylls were probably single veined, and microphylls with more complex vein patterns were likely to have evolved from single-vein microphylls (Liu et al., 2022). The first megaphylls were probably leaves with very limited tissue growth near the base of single-vein branches (Boyce and Knoll, 2002) (Fig. 5J–M). More pronounced tissue growth between those branches may have led to leaves with veins that forked, ran towards, and ended near the leaf margin. It is from those leaves with open vein patterns that leaves with closed vein patterns are thought to have evolved. And it is from those leaves with open or closed vein patterns that a conspicuous midvein may have evolved by aggregation of longitudinal veins in the middle region of the leaf (Srivastava, 1991) (Fig. 5N–Q). That aggregation may at first have been limited to the lower parts of the leaf but may later have extended to the whole length of the leaf.

One such midvein was already present in both primitive dicots and monocots (reviewed in Doyle, 1973, 1978; Doyle and Hickey, 1976). In primitive dicots, lateral veins branched off the midvein throughout the length of the midvein and either ended near the leaf margin or curved and joined distal veins to form loops (Fig. 5R, S). Minor veins branched off the midvein and lateral veins, and either joined other veins or ended in the leaf. In primitive monocots, lateral veins branched off the base of the midvein and formed concentric arches that merged with one another and the midvein near the leaf tip (Fig. 5T). Midvein and lateral veins were often joined by chevron-like minor veins. In both primitive dicots and monocots, veins were irregular in their course, their thickness, and the position and angles of their branching and merging. However, the vein patterns of both primitive dicots and monocots were already closed, suggesting that the open vein patterns of extant monocots and dicots (e.g. Foster and Arnott, 1960; Foster, 1966; Subramanyam and Yoganarasimhan, 1979) are derived.

Conclusion

The diversity of vein patterns in nature is truly astounding, but that diversity may result not from different vein patterning mechanisms but from different leaf growth patterns superimposed on a single vein patterning mechanism (Pole, 1991). Indeed, applying different leaf growth models to the same vein patterning algorithm simulates leaf shapes and vein patterns that are surprisingly close to those found in nature (Runions et al., 2005, 2017; Fujita and Mochizuki, 2006). That modeling evidence is consistent with imaging of vein patterning in monocots with parallel vein patterns and eudicots with reticulate vein patterns, which shows that those vein patterns are more similar to each other at emergence than would be expected from their mature products (Scarpella et al., 2006; Wenzel et al., 2007; O’Connor, 2012; Marcos and Berleth, 2014; Johnston et al., 2015; Robil and McSteen, 2022). It will therefore be interesting to test whether modifying patterns of leaf growth indeed leads to different vein patterns.

Whereas computational simulations can easily turn reticulate vein patterns into parallel vein patterns and vice versa by simply modifying the growth pattern of the field in which the vein patterning mechanism takes place, biologically motivated models of vein patterning struggle to simulate the formation of closed vein patterns (e.g. Mitchison, 1980; Rolland-Lagan and Prusinkiewicz, 2005; Feugier and Iwasa, 2006). Yet closed vein patterns can be opened by simply changing growth conditions (Scarpella et al., 2004). Furthermore, even leaves with usually open vein patterns can form, though rarely, closed loops (Arnott, 1959; Foster and Arnott, 1960; Foster, 1966; Subramanyam and Yoganarasimhan, 1979), suggesting that the gap between the two patterns is not unbridgeable. Indeed, mutation in a single gene of the eudicot Arabidopsis—FORKED 1 (FKD1)—is sufficient to open closed vein patterns (Steynen and Schultz, 2003), which could explain the ease with which closed vein patterns revert to open vein patterns in extant dicots and monocots (e.g. Sargent and Wangermann, 1959; Foster and Arnott, 1960; Foster, 1966; Subramanyam and Yoganarasimhan, 1979). Interestingly, FKD1 is absent in conifers and lycophytes with open vein patterns and appears for the first time in the basal flowering plant Amborella (Prabhakaran Mariyamma et al., 2018), suggesting that its appearance may have been key for the evolution of closed vein patterns. It will therefore be interesting to see whether the presence of FKD1 is always associated with closed vein patterns and its absence with open vein patterns.

Vascular tissues evolved before the evolution of leaves, so we would expect the leaf procambium—the tissue precursor to all the mature vascular cells in the leaf—to have similar features in all vascular plants. However, that does not seem to be the case. Indeed, we were unable to find a single feature that allows the youngest procambial cells to be discriminated from surrounding ground cells in the leaf of all vascular plants. The darker staining of the cytoplasm of leaf procambial cells because of their fewer and smaller vacuoles may be an artifact of tissue processing for histology (Esau, 1942). It will therefore be interesting to see whether leaf procambial cells indeed have fewer and smaller vacuoles by live imaging of fluorescent dyes or reporters for the vacuole (Hunter et al., 2007; Stefano et al., 2018).

Unlike the staining properties of the cytoplasm of leaf procambial cells, the elongated shape of these cells seems overall more reproducible across vascular plants. Even so, the elongated shape of leaf procambial cells is not always easy to follow in tissue sections because of the often-meandering course of leaf procambial strands. That difficulty could be overcome by 3D imaging of fluorescent dyes or reporters for the cell wall or plasma membrane (e.g. Wang et al., 2025). However, mutants in which procambial cells are misshapen still seem to form veins (e.g. Torres-Ruiz and Jurgens, 1994), suggesting that cell shape may not be a requirement for vein formation.

Leaf procambial cells are thought to attain their elongated shape by divisions parallel to the differentiating procambial strand (Sharman, 1942; Pray, 1955a, b, 1963; Ramji, 1961a; Arnott, 1962; Blackman, 1971; Herbst, 1972). Therefore, it is possible that, as in embryo vascular development (Yoshida et al., 2014), it is those divisions that characterize the ground cells in the leaf that will differentiate into procambial cells, whereas the elongated shape of leaf procambial cells is just a coincidental outcome of those divisions. It will therefore be interesting to compare the division planes of the leaf ground cells that will differentiate into procambial cells and of those that will differentiate into mesophyll cells.

On the other hand, that no single cellular feature allows us to distinguish the youngest procambial cells from surrounding ground cells in the leaf of all vascular plants suggests that we may need a definition of leaf procambial cells that is based on molecular features such as the onset of expression of specific genes. However, genes whose expression is initiated in leaf procambial cells have not been identified—though PHLOEM INTERCALATED WITH XYLEM/TRACHEARY ELEMENT DIFFERENTIATION INHIBITORY FACTOR RECEPTOR and WUSCHEL-RELATED HOMEOBOX 4 are possible candidates (Hirakawa et al., 2008, 2010; Eeda and Werr, 2020). The only expression markers so far unambiguously associated with the appearance of leaf procambial cells are two enhancer traps—ET1335 and Q0990—in which reporter expression is an artifact of promoter interruption by the trap construct. Therefore, reporter expression in those enhancer traps does not recapitulate the expression of the trapped genes (Lynn et al., 1999; Scarpella et al., 2004; Sawchuk et al., 2007; Radoeva et al., 2016). In the end, however, whether leaf procambial cells have fewer and smaller vacuoles, whether they have an elongated shape, whether they divide according to distinctive planes, or whether they initiate expression of specific genes, it will be important to test whether any of those features are required or sufficient for vein formation or whether they are simply coincidental to it.
