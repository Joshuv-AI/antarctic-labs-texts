Pre-treatment of p-coumaric acid reduced chilling injury by increasing some antioxidant and enzyme activity in loquat fruit to maintain post-harvest quality
Authors: Huifen Zhang, Yunting Xu, Jing Pu, Miao Wang, Jie Niu, Hongxia Wang, Xiaofu Tang, Ying Du, Xian Luo, Xiaoai Zhang, Yongqing Wang, Qunxian Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLoquat (Eriobotrya japonica Lindl.) fruits are popular for their distinctive flavor and numerous health benefits. However, their short shelf life limits commercial viability. In this study, we sought to evaluate the effect of p-coumaric acid (p-CA) treatment at a concentration of 300 mg·L−1 on the quality of loquat fruits during storage.ResultsThe results indicated that the p-CA treatment reduced fruit chilling injury, prevented fruit rot, and retarded the decomposition of soluble sugar, titratable acid, and phenolic substances. It also reduced accumulation of malondialdehyde, lignin, and other substances to maintain fruit quality. Additionally, p-CA treatment improved antioxidant activity of the fruit by promoting the synthesis of enzymatic antioxidants and activities of antioxidant enzymes.ConclusionsIn summary, p-CA application preserved the quality and enhanced the antioxidant capacity of post-harvest loquat fruits.Graphical Abstract

Background

Loquat (Eriobotrya japonica Lindl.), a native evergreen fruit tree of China, is highly valued for its economic value in the southern regions of the country. Ripening in the late spring and early summer [1], loquat fruits are appreciated by consumers because of their unique flavour and nutritional and health values. However, owing to the thin skin, loquat fruits are susceptible to mechanical damage and prone to water loss during post-harvest. Furthermore, owing to the high temperature and rain during their ripening stage (late spring and early summer), loquat fruit are more susceptible to decay and have a short preservation period.

Previous studies on loquat fruit storage have mainly focused on improving loquat fruit tolerance to chilling injury by 2,4-epibrassinolide treatment [2], and Zein-Lecithin-EGCG((−)-epigallocatechin gallate) nanoparticle coating treatment [3]. These approaches inhibit the production of harmful metabolites that affect fruit quality, thereby effectively delaying fruit senescence, reducing the degree of chilling injury, and preserving the post-harvest quality of loquat fruits. In practical applications, low-temperature storage and cold-chain transportation methods are used to extend the shelf life of loquats, but low-temperature storage may lead to chilling injuries, such as tissue browning and lignification of loquat fruit [4, 5]. In the actual production of cold storage, a temperature of 4 ℃ is commonly used. Previous research has shown that when stored at 4 ℃ for an extended period, there will be noticeable chilling injuries [6].

p-Coumaric acid (p-CA) is a hydroxyl derivative of cinnamic acid, a naturally occurring, small-molecule phenolic acid substances that benefit human health [7]. It is mainly synthesized through the shikimate pathway and enhances plant defense against biological or abiotic stresses [8]. In recent years, p-CA has been used as a novel and safe antibacterial compound and browning inhibitor for plant preservation [9]. It can inhibit bacterial activities by changing cell-membrane permeability and DNA binding [10], as well as change fungal membrane targeting and organic acid metabolism, leading to fungal death [11], thus improving fruit disease resistance. p-CA can be used as an antioxidant to delay the post-harvest deterioration of fruit quality at the cost of its own oxidation. The molecular chains of p-CA can become intertwined and pile up on the benzene ring of the antioxidant components, thereby preventing the breakdown of the latter [12]. Studies on the post-harvest preservation of peach [9] and cherry [13] fruits showed that p-CA treatment exhibited a substantial inhibitory impact on fruit lignification and bacterial and fungal infections.

Chilling injury lignification is a common problem that affects the deterioration of loquat fruit quality during refrigerated storage. However, the effect of p-CA on the quality of loquat fruit storage remains unclear. Thus, this study aimed to investigate the influence of p-CA on fruit quality, antioxidant properties, and resistance to low temperatures and develop a safe and non-toxic fruit preservation method. Our findings can provide a theoretical basis and guidance for improved storage of loquat fruits. This is also a new study in which p-CA alone has been applied to increase cold tolerance in any fruit species.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and p-CA treatments

Loquat fruits (Eriobotrya japonica Lindl. cv. “Chunhua No.5”) were harvested from a germplasm resource nursery in Sichuan Agricultural University (Chengdu, Sichuan, China). Fruit without damage and disease were selected and randomly divided into two groups: p-CA and water (control) treatment groups. Fruits in the control group were dipped in water, whereas those in the treatment group were dipped in 300 mg·L−1 p-CA solution (this concentration was determined in a pre-experiment) for 20 min (only once immersion times). After air drying, the fruits were placed in polyethylene film bags and stored at 4 ± 0.5 ℃ with 80–90% relative humidity for 35 days. Fruits from all groups were taken every 7 days for 5 weeks (0, 7, 14, 21, 28, and 35 d) to simulate the shelf-life period for 3 days at room temperature. For each sampling date, three replicates of 10 fruits in each treatment group were taken. The flesh (F) and the peduncle flesh (P) (0.5 cm × 0.5 cm flesh between the seed and the peduncle) (Fig. 1) were sampled at the end of the simulated shelf-life period. Collected flesh samples were chilled in liquid nitrogen and stored at −40 ℃ for further analysis.Fig. 1Longitudinal section of loquat fruit

Weight loss, browning index, and decay index

Weight loss, browning index, and decay index were assessed every 7 days for 5 weeks. For each measurement date, three biological replicates (each consisting of 10 fruits) were sampled from each treatment group. Record the initial weight (before storage) and final weight (each sampling day) of the fruit, and the weight loss was calculated as follows: weight loss (%) = [(initial weight – final weight) × 100%]/initial weight. The browning and decay indices were categorized on a scale ranging from 0 to 4 based on the proportion of brown and decayed tissue areas relative to the total area (where Level 0 corresponds to 0%, Level 1 to 25%, Level 2 to 50%, Level 3 to 75%, and Level 4 to 100%) and were calculated in compliance with the approach of Zhang et al. [6].

Firmness, lignin content and malondialdehyde content

Firmness was evaluated utilizing a WDGY-4 hardness tester, equipped with an 8-mm diameter probe. For each measurement, three biological replicates (each consisting of 10 fruits) were tested per treatment group. Lignin content was measured at 280 nm according to Zhang et al. [14]. The frozen flesh was homogenized with 100 g·L−1 TCA solution, and Malondialdehyde content (MDA) was determined according to Banin et al.’s method [15].

Soluble sugar content and titratable acid

Soluble sugar content (SSC) was quantified by anthrone colorimetry [16]. Titratable acid (TA) was assessed through the sodium hydroxide titration method, as elaborated by Lin et al. [17].

Vitamin C content and carotenoid content

Vitamin C (Vc) content was assessed through the Fe3+ reduction method by Zhang et al. [18]. Carotenoid content was detected at 440 nm, 645 nm, and 663 nm by spectrophotometry [6].

Total phenolic, flavonoid, and flavanol contents and total antioxidant activity

0.1 g samples were added to 1.5 mL pre-cooled methanol solution (prepared by mixing methanol, formic acid, and anhydrous ethanol at a ratio of 70:28:2, respectively) for homogenization, sonicated for 30 min, and shaken at 250 rpm for 2 h. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation for further analysis of the total phenolic content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC), total flavanol content (TFAC) and total antioxidant activity.

The determination of phenolic substances was performed according to Zhang et al. [18]. The absorbance of TPC, TFC, and TFAC were measured at 765 nm, 510 nm, and 640 nm and expressed in terms of gallic acid (50–1000 mg·L−1), rutin (20–100 mg·L−1), and catechin (6.25–200 mg·L−1) equivalents, respectively. The 2,2,diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay was carried out as the method described by Brand-Williams et al. [19]. Following the protocol of Benzie & Strain [20], the ferric-reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay was performed. Trolox was used as the standard for the DPPH and FRAP assays.

Antioxidant enzymes

Frozen fruit samples (~ 0.2 g) were added to 1.5 mL ice-cold phosphate buffer (containing 1 g PVP, 3.1 mg DTT, and 0.1 M PBS at pH 7.8) for homogenization. The upper phase was meticulously collected after centrifugation and used as a crude enzyme extract to accurately assess enzyme activity.

The detection of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and peroxidase (POD) was carried out as per [6]. Catalase (CAT) and polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activities were determined as previously reported with slight modifications [21, 22]. SOD activity was expressed by measuring the inhibition of NBT photochemical reduction by 50% at 560 nm. Changes in absorbance readings of POD, CAT, and PPO were monitored at 470 nm, 240 nm, and 420 nm, respectively. Data was recorded at regular intervals of 1 min, with at least 6 data points recorded. Enzyme activity was quantified by measuring the change in absorbance per minute, with a unit of U·(g · min)−1.

Phenylalanine amidase activity

Phenylalanine amidase (PAL) activity was detected by the catechol method [23]. The extraction of PAL was carried out from frozen fruit samples (~ 0.2 g) using an ice-cold borate buffer solution. One unit of PAL was defined as a 0.01 increase in absorbance at 290 nm per minute.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 19.0 and Origin 2024 were used for data analysis and visualization, and results were presented as the mean ± standard error (SE) of three independent biological experiments (n = 3). The statistical tests used included one-way ANOVA, Duncan’s multiple range test, principal component analysis (PCA) and correlation analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Effect of p-CA treatment on sensory quality

During storage, the weight loss of fruits in both treatment groups showed a gradually increasing trend (Fig. 2A). Fruits in the p-CA treatment (T) had a significantly lower weight loss than those in the control (CK) at 35 days (end of the observation period). As shown in Fig. 2B, after 14 days of low-temperature storage, the loquat fruits showed visible browning. The browning index of the fruits in the p-CA group was significantly lower than that of the control from 14–35 days. At 21 days, fruits in the control group began to decay, and the decay index increased gradually from 21–35 days with a range of 1.39% to 9.72% (Fig. 2C). Discussion

Browning and rotting are important indicators that reflect the quality and commercial value of loquats during storage. Previous studies have demonstrated that p-CA effectively suppressed the browning of mushrooms [24] and yellowing of broccoli [25], while also enhancing antioxidant activity and inhibited bacteria in cherries [13]. Wu et al. found that salicylic acid is another small-molecule phenolic acid in plants [26], which showed good resistance and could effectively delay the decay caused by flesh browning and pathogen infection during storage. Consistent with these studies, we found that p-CA pre-treatment (300 mg·L−1) maintained the quality attributes of loquat fruit by delaying senescence and lignification and inhibiting disease decay.

The contents of SS, TA, vitamin C, and carotenoid are crucial indexes for assessing the flavor and quality of loquat fruit. We found that p-CA treatment delayed the decrease in soluble solids and inhibited the decomposition of glycoacids, which was consistent with previous studies on other phenolic acids in loquat [26], nectarine [27], and kiwifruit [28]. In addition, we found that p-CA can increase the antioxidant content in loquat fruits. As reactive oxygen species were produced during storage, fruits treated with p-CA responded by increasing the contents of vitamin C and carotenoid, thus inhibiting the ongoing generation of reactive oxygen species, such as hydrogen peroxide and oxygen anions. This is consistent with the effects of p-CA treatment in kiwifruit [29].

During low-temperature storage, loquat fruit is susceptible to lignification, accompanied by the flesh becoming rough and less juicy, with a gradual increase in firmness. Lignification of loquat fruit is a manifestation of chilling injury due to cold storage [30]. During storage, the lignin content increases, promoting cell lignification [31, 32]. Wang et al. [33] found that melatonin treatment could effectively inhibit the lignification of cells and quality deterioration of loquat fruit. Here, we found that p-CA treatment effectively slowed the increase in firmness and lignin content of post-harvest loquat fruits, thereby mitigating the quality decline due to lignification.

MDA is generated through the peroxidation of membrane lipids and is mainly affected by the accumulation of peroxides, which cause damage to cell membranes, leading to changes in membrane permeability and post-harvest deterioration of fruit quality [34]. MDA content can indicate the extent of cell-membrane damage. In nectarine fruit, chlorogenic acid treatment effectively inhibited the increase in MDA content [27]. In this study, p-CA treatment reduced the MDA content. We propose this effect stems from the combined action of p-CA’s inherent reducing capacity as a phenolic compound and its induction of elevated antioxidant levels (e.g., Vc). This synergy enhances peroxide scavenging capacity, thereby mitigating membrane lipid peroxidation and reducing chilling injury incidence in cold-stored loquats by suppressing oxidative damage.

SOD, POD, and CAT are indispensable oxidoreductases that regulate the generation of reactive oxygen species in plants. Enhanced SOD and POD activities can promote the conversion of superoxide to O2− and hydrogen peroxide. CAT can promote the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide. The p-CA treatment slowed down the decline in SOD, POD, and CAT activities during broccoli yellowing, thereby inhibiting the decrease in quality [25]. It also can induce antioxidant enzyme activity in sweet cherries to resist the invasion of fungal pathogens [13]. In a previous study of disease resistance in loquat, SOD, POD, and CAT were found to be crucial in neutralizing reactive oxygen species through their reaction with them. The increased activity of these enzymes was associated with decreased cell damage resulting from reduced reactive oxygen species [35]. In this study, following p-CA treatment, antioxidant enzymes responded rapidly to the increase in the generation of reactive oxygen species, thus improving the antioxidant capacity of cold-storage fruits. This result aligns with the findings of Yuan et al. [36], who used p-CA in jujube fruits.

TPC, TFC, TFAC, and other phenolic acids affect the antioxidant capacity of fruits during storage. The higher the concentration of phenolic compounds, the stronger the antioxidant capacity of fruits [14]. Cao et al. [29] found that p-CA could effectively improve the antioxidant performance of kiwifruit post-harvest by regulating the phenolic anabolism pathway. In lychee [37] and peach fruits [9], p-CA increased the total phenol and flavonoid contents, thereby improving their antioxidant capacity. Previous studies on fresh-cut lotus root [38] and dried apricots [39] demonstrated that suppressed PAL activity reduces enzymatic browning. Consistent with this, our study revealed lower PAL activity in p-CA-treated loquats compared to controls during storage, with PAL inhibition strongly correlating with delayed fruit browning. Notably, an opposing trend was observed in broccoli storage [25], where p-CA treatment up regulated PAL activity, potentially enhancing disease resistance in the tissue. These contrasting results underscore the tissue-specific roles of PAL in postharvest systems, warranting further investigation into its dual functions in browning regulation and defense responses.

In peach [40] and apple [41], gene expression, material synthesis, and storage differed across flesh parts. After evaluating the quality of different flesh parts during loquat storage, we found that the TPC, TFC, and FRAP had varying trends in the flesh and peduncle flesh. The content of phenolic substances in the flesh showed an overall increasing then decreasing trend, whereas that in the peduncle flesh had a consistently increasing trend. Similar to Zhang et al. [6], we found that the change in FRAP in the flesh and peduncle flesh corresponded to that in phenolic compounds, such as total phenols and flavonoids. Although there were variations in the changes in phenolic compounds and antioxidant capacity across different flesh parts, the FRAP was primarily affected by the phenolic content. Further research should be conducted to investigate the differences in phenolic content in the two flesh parts.

Conclusions

Overall, p-CA treatment improved the post-harvest quality of loquat fruits during storage by reducing chilling injury and decay and inhibiting the decomposition of SSC, TA, and phenols and the production of MDA, lignin, and other substances, which are not conducive to maintaining fruit quality. Moreover, p-CA treatment could promote the synthesis of non-enzymatic antioxidants (TPC, TFC, and TFAC) and enhance the activities of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, POD, and CAT), resulting in a stable antioxidant capacity. This study provides a reference for prolonging shelf life and improving the commercial quality of loquat fruits.
