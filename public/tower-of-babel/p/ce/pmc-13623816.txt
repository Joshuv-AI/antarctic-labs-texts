Comparative Efficacy of Short-Term Spinal Cord Stimulation for Subacute Zoster-Associated Pain: A Three-Group Propensity Score-Matched Analysis
Authors: Wenxing Zhao, Liangliang He, Liqiang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSpinal cord stimulation (SCS) has emerged as a promising modality for managing zoster-associated pain (ZAP). This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of short-term spinal cord stimulation (SCS) for treating ZAP during the subacute phase.MethodsRecords of patients undergoing transforaminal epidural injection (TFEI), pulsed radiofrequency (PRF), and short-term SCS were retrospectively reviewed. A 1:2:2 propensity-score matching was performed. The primary endpoint was Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) pain scores at 3 months after rash onset. Secondary endpoints included postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) incidence, Hospital Anxiety and Depression (HADS) scores, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) scores, quality-adjusted life years (QALY) scores, and adverse events.ResultsAt 3 months post-rash onset, mean NRS pain score was 1.59 ± 1.18 in SCS cohort, which was lower than that of 4.80 ± 1.41 in TFEI cohort and 4.54 ± 1.40 in PRF cohort, with the difference exceeding the prespecified minimal clinically important difference of three points (p < 0.001). PHN occurred in 8.9% of patients in SCS cohort at 3 months and 5.9% at 6 months following rash episode, which was lower than the other two cohorts (both p < 0.001). The proportion of patients requesting weak opioids for rescue pain relief was lower in SCS cohort, with less usages, at these time points. SCS cohort had better improvements in terms of HADS, PSQI and QALY scores over time compared to both TFEI and PRF cohorts (all p < 0.05). No severe adverse events were observed.ConclusionsAmong patients with subacute ZAP, short-term SCS was more effective than TFEI or PRF, resulting in greater improvements in PHN occurrence, psychological function, sleep quality, and quality of life.

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Zoster-associated pain (ZAP) can become chronic and debilitating, progressing to postherpetic neuralgia (PHN).While both transforaminal epidural injection (TFEI) and pulsed radiofrequency (PRF) have been utilized for subacute ZAP, a need existed to directly compare their effectiveness with another promising modality, short-term spinal cord stimulation (SCS), in a real-world context.This study aimed to investigate the comparative effectiveness of short-term SCS versus both TFEI and PRF for treating subacute ZAP using a propensity score-matched analysis. The hypothesis was that short-term SCS would demonstrate better effectiveness.What was learned from the study?Our results indicated that short-term SCS provided more effective pain relief and higher safety, leading to greater improvements in PHN incidence, psychological well-being, sleep quality, and overall quality of life compared to TFEI and PRF.The study provided initial evidence that short-term SCS might be a more effective therapeutic strategy for subacute ZAP, potentially offering multi-dimensional benefits beyond pain control.These positive findings, if confirmed by larger-scale studies, could influence future clinical treatment algorithms for managing subacute ZAP.

Introduction

Herpes zoster (HZ) occurs when the dormant varicella-zoster virus in the dorsal root ganglion (DRG) is reactivated due to the failure of the immune system to control viral replication [1]. According to a nationwide population-based surgery in China in 2024, the adjusted annual incidence of HZ ranges was 5.85 per 1000 persons per year [2]. The acute eruptive phase is characterized by painful vesicles on the skin innervated by the affected ganglion. Although the rashes are self-limiting, the zoster-associated pain (ZAP) can progress to become chronic and debilitating, leading to the development of postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) [3]. It has been reported that PHN is associated with a heavy burden of illness in urban China, affecting daily activities, sleep, and quality of life [4]. The transition from ZAP to chronic pain states indicates the contribution of neuropathic events in the reactivated ganglion. Furthermore, the neural tissue damage can extend along the ascending sensory pathway to the dorsal horn of the spinal cord [5]. Based on the European consensus-based (S2k) guideline, antiviral therapy combined with analgesics serves as the first-line treatment for HZ, and peripheral nerve root block is effective for moderate-to-severe ZAP in the acute phase [6]. For disabling pain that lasts more than 30 days following rash onset, transforaminal epidural injection (TFEI), pulsed radiofrequency (PRF) on DRG and short-term spinal cord stimulation (SCS) have been widely utilized as promising modalities to prevent PHN in general clinical practice, supported by clinical evidence [7, 8]. Nevertheless, no studies have examined all three treatments simultaneously.

The hypothesis of this study is that the short-term SCS was more effective for the treatment of subacute herpes zoster-associated pain compared to both the transforaminal epidural injection (TFEI) and PRF on DRG under fluoroscopic guidance. It can also prevent the transition of subacute pain to PHN.

Methods

Study Design and Participant

The matched retrospective observational study was conducted at a large medical university-affiliated hospital. The ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Capital Medical University-affiliated Beijing Xuanwu Hospital (xw-clincal-2026120) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki principles. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Results were reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for a retrospective case–control study [9].

Between January 1, 2021, and December 31, 2025, patients who underwent minimally invasive intervention for HZ were retrospectively reviewed from the electronic medical records (EMRs). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) meeting the diagnosis criteria of HZ according to the International Classification of Diseases 10th revision (ICD-10) codes [10]; (2) the course of disease within 30 to 90 days after rash onset; (3) pain resistant to antiviral therapy combined with analgesics; (4) having a Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) pain score ≥ 4 points; (5) aged > 18 years. Patients were excluded if they had bilateral HZ, active malignancies, other conditions using immunodeficiency, uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, coagulation dysfunction, severe systemic disorders, active psychiatry disease, alcohol addiction, opioid abuse, history of epidural injections within the past 3 months, pregnancy or lactation, and incomplete medical records.

After consulting with their pain specialists, patients chose the most suitable option among TFEI, PRF on DRG and short-term SCS, considering their preferences, values, and possible outcomes through a shared decision-making procress. To mitigate selection bias and unbalanced covariates, a 1:1:1 propensity score-matched (PSM) analysis was performed among three treatment modalities. Logit-transformed propensity scores were obtained by utilizing the nearest neighbor approach with a caliper of 0.1. Clinical variables including: age, gender, body mass index, affected side/location/dermatomes, hemorrhagic lesion, disease duration, baseline NRS pain score, analgesic history, and other treatments during acute phase, as well as comorbidities were selected as matching factors. A standardized mean difference (SMD) less than 0.1 confirmed balance.

Spinal Cord Stimulation Procedure

All procedures were performed by the same two pain specialists who had over 20 years of experience in minimally invasive pain interventions. Patients were placed in the prone position and monitored using electrocardiography, non-invasive blood pressure, and pulse oximetry in the operating room. A Tuohy needle was inserted into the epidural space under fluoroscopic guidance. An eight-contact stimulation electrode (Model 977A2, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was then implanted into the epidural space through the needle until its tip reached the targeted anatomical location at the dorsal column, with the aim of producing a comfortable paresthesia sensation that covered at least 50% of the painful area as reported by patients (Fig. 2). The short-term stimulation was administered for 14 days.

Rescue Analgesics

According to the World Health Organization (WHO) analgesic ladder, rescue analgesics were prescribed upon request after procedures. Celecoxib was administered at a dose of 200 mg per tablet, up to twice a day, if patients reported an NRS pain score ranging from 1 to 3 points. Tramadol hydrochloride was provided at a dose of 100 mg per tablet, up to twice daily, for patients with an NRS pain score ≥ 4 points [11].

Outcome Measurement

Patients’ demographic and clinical characteristics were retrieved from the EMRs. Follow-up data were collected from medical records by two specially trained investigators. Intensity of ZAP was measured using the NRS with an 11-point scale ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain) [12]. The Chinese version of the Douleur Neuropathique 4 (DN4) questionnaire was utilized to identify neuropathic pain (NeP). This instrument included ten items: seven sensory descriptors, including burning, painful cold, electric shock, tingling, pins and needles, numbness and itching, as well as three signs assessed through a sensory physical examination, including tactile hypesthesia, pinprick hypesthesia, and allodynia in the affected area. Each positive response was scored as 1 and each negative response as 0, yielding a total score between 0 and 10. A score of ≥ 4 points was considered, as the presence of NeP [13]. PHN was defined as a ZAP pain score ≥ 3 points on the NRS, persisting for at least 90 days after rash onset [14]. Patients’ anxiety and depression levels over the past week were evaluated by the self-reported Hospital Anxiety and Depression (HADS) score, which included two subscales: anxiety (HADS-A) and depression (HADS-D). Each subscale contained seven items, and responses were rated on a four-point scale ranging from 0 (do not apply to me at all) to 3 (applied to me very much). The total score for each subscale ranged from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms. Patients were categorized as follows: no significant symptoms (0–7 points), mild symptoms (8–10 points), moderate symptoms (11–14 points) and severe symptoms (15–21 points) [15]. Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), which included 18 items across seven dimensions: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of medication and daytime dysfunction. Each item was scored on a four-point Likert scale between 0 and 3, with the total score ranging from 0 to 21. Higher scores indicated worse sleep quality [16]. The five-dimension EuroQoL (EQ-5D) 3L index score was used to measure patients’ health-related quality of life (HR-QoL). Responses to different hypothetical choices were converted into an index score on a scale from 0 (representing death) to 1 (indicating perfect health). This index score was then multiplied by the duration spent in the health state to calculate a quality-adjusted life years (QALY) metric, yielding a total score ranging from − 0.594 to 1 [17].

The primary endpoint was the NRS pain scores at 3 months after rash onset among different cohorts. Secondary endpoints included PHN incidence, HADS scores, PSQI scores, QALY scores and adverse events.

Sample Size Calculation

PASS software, version 22 (NCSS, LLC., Kaysville, UT, USA) was utilized for sample size calculation. The present study was designed to compare the means of three cohorts using the Tukey–Kramer pairwise multiple comparison test with a significance level of 0.05. Previous research indicated that the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for acute pain management was three points in patients with HZ [18, 19]. The researchers wanted a sample size large enough to detect a minimum three-point difference in NRS scores at 1 month following treatments, with a 1:2:2 ratio. To achieve a 90% power, a total of 230 patients were required, assuming a standard deviation (SD) for NRS pain scores was between 1 and 4. Considering a potential 20% data loss, the final sample size was set at 288 cases (58 for ESI cohort, and 115 for both PRF and SCS cohorts).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software, version 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) with a significance level set at p < 0.05. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov Z-test was applied to evaluate the normality of the data. Continuous data with a normal distribution were described as mean ± SD and percentages, while categorical data were presented as percentages. Differences among cohorts were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the post hoc Bonferroni correction (adjusted Bonferroni p = 0.017) for continuous data and chi-square test for categorial data, respectively.

Following treatments, patients across all three cohorts showed significant pain relief at both 3 and 6 months after rash onset compared to their baseline values (all adjusted p < 0.05). According to ANOVA analysis, patients who underwent SCS for ZAP during the subacute phase had a significantly lower mean NRS pain score than those treated with TFEI or PRF at 3 months after rash onset (1.59 ± 1.18 vs. 4.80 ± 1.41 vs. 4.54 ± 1.40, p < 0.001), with the difference exceeding the prespecified MCID of three points. At 6 months post-rash onset, the mean NRS pain score was 1.23 ± 0.96 in the SCS cohort, which was also significantly lower than that of 3.90 ± 1.39 in the TFEI cohort and that of 3.81 ± 1.48 in the PRF cohort (p < 0.001). However, no significant differences between the TEFI or PRF cohorts were observed over time (all adjusted Bonferroni p > 0.05). PHN occurred in 8.9% of patients in the SCS cohort at 3 months following the rash episode, which was significantly lower than that of 25.5% in the TFEI cohort and that of 23.7% in the PRF cohort (p < 0.001). As shown in Table 2, the proportion of patients requesting tramadol hydrochloride for rescue pain relief was significantly lower in the SCS cohort compared to the other two cohorts at both 3 and 6 months (both p < 0.05). Additionally, the mean usage of both celecoxib and tramadol hydrochloride was significantly lower in the SCS cohort at these time points (all p < 0.05). Discussion

With the utilization of a propensity score-matched (PSM) analysis, the present study is the first to investigate the effectiveness of the short-term SCS for the treatment of ZAP during the subacute phase as compared to both the TFEI and PRF methods. It proved to be more effective than the other two treatments and mediated significantly better improvements in outcomes related to PHN incidence, psychological function, sleep quality and patients’ HR-QoL.

Following the implantation of the SCS electrode, the pulse generator delivered stimulations with various parameters such as tonic stimulation, high-frequency stimulation, high-frequency burst stimulation and dorsal root ganglion stimulation [20]. Then, these stimulations suppressed the segmental ascending nociceptive transmission by the release of neurotransmitter including gamma aminobutyric acid (GABA), endocannabinoids, and endogenous opioids in the spinal dorsal horn. Meanwhile, the stimulations facilitated the descending inhibition to promote the release of noradrenaline and serotonin, which modulated the wide dynamic range in the spinal cord. This process also played an important role in the antinociceptive mechanism of SCS [21]. Furthermore, SCS downregulated the levels of pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, TNF-α and NF-κB and inhibited sustained activation of microglia and astrocytes in the dorsal horn, leading to improvements in the neuroimmune condition [22].

Although SCS is usually implanted permanently for treating chronic neuropathic pain such as PHN, complex regional pain syndrome, and failed surgical pain syndrome, the temporary SCS has been widely used in patients who presented with acute and subacute ZAP that failed to conventional therapy [23, 24]. Dao-Song Dong et al. evaluated the efficacy of short-term SCS in 46 patients with refractory acute/subacute ZAP. Their results showed that the ten-point visual analog scale pain scores significantly decreased from the baseline value of 7.28 ± 0.93 to 3.59 ± 2.40 just before being discharged, with 69.6% of patients obtaining a favorable MCID of three points. At 6 months post-treatment, a total of 67.4% of cases achieved excellent pain relief with VAS pain scores ≤ 2 (p < 0.01) [18]. A recent study retrospectively investigated the efficacy of temporary SCS treatment and prevention of PHN among patients with ZAP during acute (n = 42), subacute (n = 34), and chronic phases (n = 23). They found that patients with herpetic pain in both the acute phase and subacute phase were clinically and statistically improved in ten-point VAS pain scores compared to those in chronic phase (p < 0.001). The incidence of PHN (VAS pain score ≥ 3) was observed in 2.38% in acute cohort, 16.0% in the subacute cohort and 68.42% in the chronic cohort at the 6-month follow-up [25]. Consistent with prior evidence, our results demonstrated that the mean NRS pain scores significantly declined over time, with the reductions exceeding the prespecified MCID of three points compared to the baseline values in patients undergoing short-term SCS to alleviate subacute herpetic pain. PHN defined as NRS pain score ≥ 3 occurred in 5.9% of patients at 6 months after rash onset, indicating that this method effectively prevented the development of PHN.

Epidural administration of local anesthetic and steroid into the affected DRG has been reported to be effectively block the nociceptive signals to the spinal dorsal horn in cases of ZAP beyond the acute phase. It may also help prevent further progression to PHN [26]. PRF utilizes radiofrequency waves to generate an electric field and produce intermittent pulsed currents at the electrode tip to control electric stimulation. This effect influences cytokine expression, cellular responses and gene regulation pathways to provide relief for ZAP [27]. According to a recent systematic review and meta-analysis involving 6 trials with 509 patients, patients undergoing short-term SCS to alleviate ZAP showed a better analgesic effect with lower pian intensity than cases receiving PRF (standardized mean difference (MD) = − 0.83, P = 0.002) [28]. A more recent study retrospectively evaluated the comparative efficacy of short-term SCS and PRF therapy in 275 patients with thoracic ZAP in the acute phase, subacute phase, and postherpetic phase. Their findings suggested that the SCS group demonstrated superior pain relief at 3, 6, and 12 months post-treatment compared to the PRF group, particularly in the subacute and postherpetic phases (p < 0.05). Aligned with previous findings, our results showed that patients in the SCS cohort exhibited lower NRS pain scores at all follow-up time points compared to those in both the TEFI and PRF cohorts, indicating that the short-term SCS provided more effective and sustained pain relief.

In addition to analgesic effects, SCS of the dorsal column can activate a variety of supraspinal brain regions, such as thalamus, insular cortex, limbic network, and hippocampus, which are associated with emotion. As a result, can alleviate not only pain but also anxiety and depression [21]. Evidence from recent research indicated that both short-term SCS and PRF for treating thoracic ZAP significantly reduced HADS-A and HADS-D scores across all time points following treatments (p < 0.05), with SCS method demonstrating superior long-term improvement in depression scores (p < 0.05) [29]. The temporary SCS had better effects in improving sleep quality and quality of life as compared to the conventional nerve block therapy in 135 patients with ZAP [30]. Additionally, patients receiving short-term SCS for ZAP also reported less incidence of adverse events (risk ratio = 0.32, p < 0.05) compared to the PRF modality [28]. In line with earlier findings, our results indicated that at 3 and 6 months after the rash episode, the SCS cohort reported lower HADS scores and PSQI scores, as well as fewer adverse events, resulting in greater improves in QALY scores compared to both the other cohorts.

There were several limitations. Firstly, as a retrospective observational study, our findings were subject to potential selection bias. Secondly, although propensity score matching was employed to balance baseline characteristics among cohorts, it cannot account for unmeasured or unknown confounders that were not captured in the dataset. Thirdly, the single-center design limited its external validity. The patient population and clinical practices at our institution might differ from those in other settings, restricting the generalizability of our findings to broader or different demographic cohorts. Lastly, the retrospective design precluded the establishment of causal relationships, limiting our conclusions to associations rather than definitive cause-and-effect outcomes. Therefore, our findings should be validated by a well-designed randomized controlled trial in the future.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the short-term SCS provided more effective pain relief for patients with subacute ZAP than both TFEI and PRF methods. It also resulted in a lower incidence of PHN and fewer adverse events, along with greater improvements in psychological well-being, sleep quality, and overall quality of life.
