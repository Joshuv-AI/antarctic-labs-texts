Navigating digital health: Development and validation of the online health information seeking scale (OHISS)
Authors: Gursheel Kaur, Dinesh Chhabra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing reliance on online platforms for health information establishes the need to understand people’s engagement and attitude towards Online Health Information Seeking (OHIS). The current study aims to develop and validate a comprehensive multidimensional Online Health Information Seeking Scale (OHISS). Items were generated using both inductive and deductive approaches, followed by multiple content and face validity screenings that incorporated qualitative and quantitative evaluations. Data were collected in two stages: Study 1 (N=364) for item development and identifying the preliminary factor structure using Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Study 2 (N=236) for validating the obtained factor structure using Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). Following the descriptive analysis, EFA using Promax rotation yielded a 20-item scale comprising three dimensions: Perceived Benefits and Engagement (11 items), Perceived Risks (6 items), and Social Influences on OHIS (3 items), collectively accounting for 51.1% of the total variance. This three-factor structure was subsequently confirmed through CFA with DWLS estimator (χ²(167)=271.55, χ²/df=1.63, CFI=0.986, TLI=0.984, RMSEA=0.052, SRMR=0.071, and GFI=0.978). The scale has good internal consistency as demonstrated by Cronbach's alpha (α=.833), McDonald's omega (⍵=.824), Spearman brown split-half (rxx=.886), Guttman's lambda (λ=.885) and test-retest (ICC2,1=.728) coefficient. Construct validity was supported by significant correlations with the Attitude towards Online Health Information and Information-Seeking subscales, as well as Average Variance explained, Composite Reliability, and the Heterotrait-Monotrait Ratio. The scale demonstrated measurement invariance across male and female groups. The results conclude that the OHISS is a psychometrically standardized measure. The OHISS may be used by health practitioners to assess individuals’ attitudes toward online health information, identify barriers in accessing digital health content, and support both health-related communication and research.Keywords: Digital health, Online health information seeking, Online health information benefits, Online health information risks, Scale construction, Psychometric validation

Introduction

The Internet has become an essential need, with 5.5 billion users worldwide in 2024, representing 68% of the global population (Petrosyan, 2025c). Asia marks the largest market with 2.9 billion users (Petrosyan, 2025b), including 1.1 billion in China and 806 million in India. While the United States has approximately 322 million internet users (Petrosyan, 2025a). The Internet fulfils various purposes, including social networking, knowledge seeking, work, entertainment, shopping, and expressing views. It is also being widely used for health promotion, prevention, and treatment information (Annadurai, 2023). The tendency to seek Online health information (OHI) is widespread, ranging from 35–54% in low and middle-income countries (Lim et al., 2022; Nangsangna & Vroom, 2019) to 68-87% in high-income countries (Finney Rutten et al., 2019; Moreland et al., 2015; Wong & Cheung, 2019). People seek health information online to fill knowledge gaps and alleviate stress from uncertainty (Wilson, 2006), aiding in forming a complete and clear understanding of their health (Chu et al., 2017). A report published by The Telegraph revealed that there are nearly a billion health-related questions asked on Google each day across the world, accounting for 7% of daily searches (Murphy, 2019). It’s thus essential to study online health information seeking behavior because it influences the decisions made by individuals to interact with healthcare professionals, seeking assistance, coping with acute or chronic health conditions, as well as how they view their health (White & Horvitz, 2009). It may result in perception of common symptoms as severe, can cause intense worry and create a predisposition for anxiety disorders, and on the contrary, may also help detect early symptoms and stay informed about one's health status.

Conceptualization of online health information seeking (OHIS)

As internet usage for health has increased, it becomes pertinent to know why individuals seek health information using online platforms. Based on the conceptual analysis of Health Information Seeking, Lambert and Loiselle (2007) define it as “acquiring health information purposefully to bridge the specific information gaps from selected information sources.” This definition relies on information gaps, often triggered by a threat to oneself or loved ones, making its applicability limited. Further, conventional offline ways of information seeking were intentional and required greater effort to look for information, as also conveyed in the definition given by Niederdeppe et al. (2007): "purposeful and active attempts to acquire particular information triggered by a relevant circumstance" (p. 54).

Health information seeking in the online context has been poorly conceptualized, with limited operational definitions, unlike conventional health information seeking. Traces of this concept can be found in a related term, ‘Interactive Health Communication’, which is defined as “the interaction of an individual—consumer, patient, caregiver, or professional—with or through an electronic device or communication technology to access or transmit health information or to receive guidance and support on a health-related issue. (Robinson et al., 1998)” This construct focuses on the communication and mutual interactive relationship. While others have defined health related information which has been looked for through online sources as “anything regarding the symptoms, diagnoses, and treatments of different diseases or simply general information about weight loss, healthy diets or wellness tips (Ghahramani & Wang, 2020).”

Based on existing related constructs and dimensions of health given by the World Health Organization (1995), an operational definition was formulated and agreed upon by three Subject matter experts in the field of health psychology and test construction. The definition of Online Health Information Seeking (OHIS) for the current study is as follows:

“Seeking any information related to physical, mental, and/or social well-being that an individual avails through internet sources (including websites, blogs, social media, etc.), whether intentionally or unintentionally.”

This definition attempts to capture health as a complete state of well-being and not just the absence of disease, expanding OHIS to also include information searches about fitness, wellness, and optimal functioning as well. The information can be sought via any internet platform. Although contemporarily ‘seeking’ as a term denotes intentionality, in today’s time, with the influence of Artificial Intelligence algorithms, information seeking may also be unintentional and pops up in the form of watching suggestive content on Google and social media pages, advertisements, etc. A lot of contemporary media websites offer their online users material recommendations based on their interests and collective community patterns, such as more news articles, videos, posts, etc. (Elahi et al., 2022). Studies have also shown that individuals absent-mindedly scroll on social media platforms often to the extent of losing track of time (Eid, 2022), and these patterns are also true while consuming health-related content. The decision-making power of users is weakened while consumer preferences are strengthened by AI-moderated recommendations, often also resulting in confirmation bias (Chen et al., 2022). Thus, OHIS may be intentional as well as unintentional.

Potential drivers and barriers for online health information seeking

The internet enables the dissemination of vast amounts of information with a single click, which has significant implications. It provides convenient access to health-related topics, existing research, treatment options, and drug information, enhancing patient knowledge and improving healthcare delivery (Levy & Strombeck, 2002). This supports the holistic promotion of wellness and disease prevention. According to motivated information management theory, uncertainty or insufficient knowledge about a risk motivates people to seek information (Afifi & Weiner, 2004). The most common reasons for accessing health information online are the ease of obtaining relevant information (80.9%), free consultation (41.3%), and privacy protection (31.1%) (Parija et al., 2020). Płaciszewski et al. (2022) found that participants sought online health information for drug information (69.9%), locating medical facilities or doctors (67%), checking doctor reviews (55.2%), and purchasing drugs (43.5%).

Individuals with stigmatized health conditions seek more online health information to better communicate and manage their illness compared to those without such conditions (Berger et al., 2005). Online health information seeking (OHIS) also provides social support in the form of reassurance and community participation alongside convenience and lower costs (Grandinetti, 2000). Further increased OHIS among older adults is associated with social encouragement from family, friends, and doctors (Ma et al., 2023).

While online health information offers benefits such as increasing knowledge about health and illness, social support (Popovac & Roomaney, 2022), early symptom detection, and improved communication with healthcare professionals (Win et al., 2015), it also presents significant challenges and negative effects. Access to a vast amount of clinical and health information often leads to self-diagnosis and self-treatment, which can have severe consequences, including potential fatalities (White & Horvitz, 2009). Medical professionals, especially nurses, report that patients frequently bring flawed information from unreliable sources like Wikipedia (Scantlebury et al., 2017). Additionally, OHIS contributes to confirmation bias, where individuals favor information that aligns with their existing beliefs and this tendency is more pronounced in those with higher health literacy (Meppelink et al., 2019).

OHIS is also linked to emotional and behavioral issues such as cyberchondria, which involves negative emotional states from excessive health information searching (Zheng et al., 2023). McMullan et al. (2019) found significant correlations between OHIS and cyberchondria (r = 0.34, p < .0001) and between cyberchondria and health anxiety (r = 0.62, p < .0001), suggesting that those worried about their health are more prone to cyberchondria and continue seeking online health information for reassurance. Digital web searches can escalate medical concerns, with harmless symptoms often linked to serious illnesses (White & Horvitz, 2009). Privacy and confidentiality issues are also a concern, as many health-related websites contain third-party elements and tracking mechanisms that could be used for targeted advertising based on users' search patterns (Huesch, 2013).

Existing measures related to online health information seeking (OHIS)

Existing measures have focused on different aspects of online health information, such as the Online Health Seeking Behavior (OHSB) scale developed by Popovac and Roomaney (2022) to assess the outcomes and driving forces (like gaining insight about their illness, seeking social and emotional support, connecting with others, diagnosing, etc.). Another measure is the e-Health Impact Questionnaire (Kelly et al., 2015), which assesses the outcomes of online health information seeking as well as the experiences of users, particularly while accessing health and medical information from websites. Part 1 comprises 2 subscales, catering to attitudes towards OHI (5 items, α=0.77) and attitudes towards sharing experiences (6 items, α=0.89), while eHIQ-Part 2 has 3 subscales, identification & confidence (9 items, α=0.92), presentation & information content (8 items, α=0.89), and motivation & understanding (9 items, α=0.90). Although useful for comparing websites, its application is limited to a single source of OHI as reported by the study.

Online Health Information‑Seeking Skill Scale (Izadi-Avanji et al., 2021), comprising 20 items, assesses the skills that are required for seeking online health information. E-Health literacy scale (Norman & Skinner, 2006) is another instrument that outlines and measures six skills that form the core of e-Health literacy, including traditional literacy, literacy regarding computers, media, information, health, and scientific literacy. While many studies examining OHIS rely on single items, ad hoc questions(Raudenská, 2026) or unstandardized survey items (Nölke et al., 2015; Powell et al., 2011).

These existing tools, although few in number, have focused on understanding and measuring isolated facets such as motivations for OHIS, attitudes towards OHIS, prerequisite skills, literacy about health, media, information, etc. The extent of engagement in OHIS remains largely unexamined. The present study aims to construct a standardized tool that assesses the attitudinal and behavioral aspects of OHIS engagement in terms of frequency and direction, alongside the motivations for engaging in OHIS.

The lack of a comprehensive standardized tool hinders accurate measurement of OHIS prevalence and individual OHIS patterns, which have immense applications for healthcare professionals, such as to gauge their patients' level of understanding as well as their tendency to engage in OHIS. Based on individuals' OHIS scores, they may be made aware of the detrimental impacts (such as self-treatment and self-diagnosis, cyberchondria, etc.) and also be guided towards reliable sources for enhancing awareness and educating them. Identifying the appropriate OHI sources is contingent on the assessment of individuals’ attitudes and motivations towards it.

This scale will also extend its utility to specific internet sources like websites, social media, etc., or for broadly understanding online health information seeking behaviors related to disease, wellbeing, illness, etc. Exploring the barriers can aid in improving the quality and accessibility of these sources. The current scale will be among the first standardized tool that measures different facets of OHIS holistically in one scale.

Method

Sample

Inclusion criteria required participants to have internet access and the ability to surf the internet. The age eligibility criteria were set to be above 18 years of age. Recent trends have also shown the decreasing age for onset and rise of physical (Ng et al., 2014) and mental illnesses (Mcgrath et al., 2023). Medical physicians and medical health professionals were excluded as their responses might contaminate the data due to their knowledge, awareness and expertise about health information. The Departmental Council Dissertation Sub-Committee, Department of Psychology, University of Delhi examined the study protocol, and the research complied with the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association, 2025) and the American Psychological Association (APA, 2017) ethical guidelines.

The scale was shared approximately with 450 and 350 participants for Study 1 and 2 (Table 1), respectively residing in urban and semi-urban areas. Minimum sample sizes were determined following responses to an item ratio of at least 10:1 (Nunnally, 1978). An informed consent was taken mentioning the research objectives, inclusion criteria, rights of the respondents including voluntary participation and withdrawing at any point without any consequences (American Psychological Association, 2017). All questions were mandatory, so incomplete responses could not be submitted. Neither coercion nor rewards were utilized to get the data from participants.

Demographics of study 1

The scale was filled by 370 respondents however 6 responses were eliminated as three participants did not give consent while the other three did not meet the age criteria of >18 years (Supplementary Figure 1). Participants belonged to the age range of 18-73 years with mean age as 33.19 ± 12.9 years. The sample predominantly comprised females (65.11%) and participants belonging to middle class households (84.06%). Major segments of the sample were graduates (43.68%) and postgraduates (35.72%). Nearly half the participants had someone in their immediate family with a chronic illness including themself. Online websites (70.88%) and social media (65.11%) were the most prevalent internet sources for health information. These findings are consistent with other studies (AlMuammar et al., 2021; Ashkanani et al., 2019).

Demographics of study 2

In the second study of data collection, 243 responses were received. Following the exclusion of duplicates, responses with false data and responses from participants who did not give consent, 236 responses were considered for Confirmatory factor analysis (Supplementary Figure 2). The sample ranged from 18 to 75 years with a mean age of 28.82, primarily females (69.5%) and belonging to the middle class (83%). More than half the participants (59.32%) had a diagnosed chronic illness in their family including themselves. Similar to study 1, websites (79.6%) and social media (59.4%) were the most prevalent sources for OHIS.

Measures

Newly constructed Online Health Information Seeking scale (OHISS): OHISS was constructed in this study to comprehensively assess individual’s attitude and behavioral engagement associated with OHIS. After content validity, face validity and item level analysis, the scale comprised 26 items which were subjected to EFA resulting in 20 item scale with 3 factors, which were also affirmed using CFA. The scale uses a 4-point response pattern (1=Almost Never, 2=Sometimes, 3=Often, 4=Almost Always). Standardization indices are reported in the reliability and validity sections.

Attitudes towards online health information subscale (Kelly et al., 2015): This is a subscale of e-Health Impact Questionnaire part 1 which assesses the experiences and potential impact of engaging with health-related websites. Attitudes towards OHI had 5 items which were measured on a 5-point scale (1=Strongly Disagree, 5=Strongly Agree) with higher scores indicating favorable attitudes. It had good reliability established through Cronbach's alpha (α=0.77) and test-retest reliability (ICC=0.85). In the current study, Cronbach's alpha was found to be .70.

Information-seeking subscale (Popovac & Roomaney, 2022): This is a subscale of the 37-item Online Health-Seeking Behavior (OHSB) scale designed to measure motivation and outcomes related to OHSB. The information seeking subscale had 8 items with a 5-point scale (1=Never; 5=Very Often). Cronbach's alpha was 0.89 and .91, for this subscale as reported in the original study and the current study, respectively.

Procedure

Item generation and item writing

Items were generated using both inductive and deductive approaches, a proven effective method (Boateng et al., 2018). Research on online health information seeking (Deng & Liu, 2017; Li & Wang, 2018; Zhao et al., 2022), the Health Information Orientation Scale (DuBenske et al., 2009), and other related scales to OHIS were considered. Comprehensive Information Seeking Model (Johnson et al., 1995; Johnson & Meischke, 1993) and Health Belief Model (HBM) (Rosenstock, 1974) were also referred for item development. The HBM posits that behavior is guided by beliefs about vulnerability, severity of disease, benefits of engaging in a behavior, barriers that may be faced, cues and self-efficacy (Champion & Skinner, 2008) while the Comprehensive Model of Information Seeking (CMIS) is an integrative theoretical framework that combines gratification and utility research, health belief model and appraisal and media exposure models (Johnson & Meischke, 1993). It proposes that information carrier characteristics perception and information utility are affected by four factors: salience, direct experience (personal or social), beliefs and demographic variables (insurance, income, education). The inductive process included brainstorming sessions, discussions, and qualitative interviews with subject matter experts and researchers about OHIS.

The theoretical models were not strictly adhered to impose a priori factor structure, rather they served as reference points to guide the content of items and ensure representativeness. Initially, items were kept broad (Boateng et al., 2018) to ensure comprehensive coverage, with the expectation that some would be eliminated in successive screenings. The number of items generated was at least twice the desired scale length (Boateng et al., 2018) and were framed following the BRUSO principle (Peterson, 2000) to ensure the items were brief, relevant, unambiguous, objective, and not double-barreled. The initial 66 items were chiseled in 3 preliminary screening sessions by the researchers on the basis of length, clarity, uniqueness, objectivity, neutrality and comprehensibility resulting in 35 items.

Selection of response format

A 4-point rating scale was carefully adopted, consisting of options ‘almost never’, ‘sometimes’, ‘often’ and ‘almost never’. The response set was chosen to examine frequency and directionality. An attempt was made to prevent social desirability (Chyung et al., 2017; Garland, 1991) and middle responding bias by making the response set forced choice so that direction could be known. Another reason for using a 4-point response over an odd-numbered format was that neutral or meaningful midpoint response was not appropriate for many items (Krosnick & Fabrigar, 1997) in this scale. Further extreme response options (‘Never’ and ‘Always’) were not adopted to avoid floor/ceiling effects, acknowledging that behavior is not absolute and producing greater variability in responses (Wyatt & Meyers, 1987). Despite the advantages of 4-point response categories, it is acknowledged that it may limit participant’s genuine ambivalence, reduce the nuanced responding (Krosnick & Fabrigar, 1997) and may make it difficult to compare results with other studies as odd numbered Likert are more commonly used (Kusmaryono et al., 2022).

Content validity

Content validity was then established qualitatively with the help of three subject matter experts (SMEs) working in the area of health psychology and psychometrics. The SMEs converged on the overall satisfaction and relevance of items. They emphasized retaining only the most appropriate response to reduce redundancy, thereby enhancing relevance and representativeness. Specifically, the reviewers recommended excluding items with an excessive focus on self-efficacy as it undermined the main construct. Another feedback was to tap only the general perception of disease vulnerability rather than situation specific scenarios (for instance, ‘I am susceptible to health issues that run in my family’; ‘I am susceptible to illnesses based on my age’, were removed and replaced with ‘I consider myself prone to illnesses’). Based on the SME feedback, 5 items were excluded and 6 were modified to enhance the clarity.

After elimination of 5 items, a quantitative Content Validity Index (CVI) was also computed by taking ratings from 6 SMEs for the remaining 30 items based on two criteria: (i) relevance to the construct, (ii) language clarity, using a 4-point Likert scale (1 = irrelevant or unclear and 4 = relevant or very clear) (Yusoff, 2019). Scale-level Content Validity Index average (S-CVI/Ave) and the Item-level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) were computed. All I-CVI were above the acceptable standards ranging from 0.83 to 1, except for one item (I consider myself prone to illnesses) which was eliminated. Strong content validity was indicated with obtained values of 0.95 and 0.97 for relevance and clarity, respectively, which satisfied the accepted criteria of S-CVI/Ave > 0.90 (Polit & Beck, 2006; Yusoff, 2019).

Face validity

Face validity was assessed qualitatively from 30 respondents meeting the eligibility criteria. They were asked to give detailed feedback on response set, understanding and relevance, coverage of construct, and overall scale. For some items, modifications were made to help respondents to better comprehend such as replacing the word ‘wellbeing’ with ‘health’, the word ‘navigate’ was replaced by "browse" etc. Definition of the term “chronic illness”, was added along with some examples of chronic illness to enhance the understanding and obtain an accurate response. Some items having minute distinctions were perceived as repetitive so they were removed (example: ‘The possible outcomes of health conditions mentioned on online platforms worry me’ and ‘Online health information leads to excessive worry.’).

Items were also quantitatively reviewed by another independent set of 25 participants from the target population on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not clear and understandable to 4 = very clear and understandable) to compute Face Validity Index (FVI) (Yusoff, 2019). Only 1 item was eliminated as it did not meet the acceptable threshold of 0.7 for Item-level Face Validity Index (I-FVI). The Scale-Level Average Face Validity Index (S-FVI/Ave) was 0.91, which surpassed the widely accepted threshold of 0.90 for robust face validity (Yusoff, 2019).

Exploratory factor analysis

Item-level analysis, exploratory factor analysis, confirmatory factor analysis, various reliability and validity indices have been computed using JASP (2024) version 0.19.1.0. Before carrying out the factor analysis, descriptives such as the mean, standard deviations, kurtosis and skewness were determined for each item (Supplementary Table 1). Items that were falling outside the range of -1 to +1 (Item 9) in skewness were removed (George & Mallery, 2019; Singh et al., 2019). All items were acceptable based on the kurtosis criteria of -2 to +2 (George & Mallery, 2019). With a final sample size of 364, and a significant KMO (.914), adequacy of sample for factor analysis was established. Conventionally, a KMO value of 0.5 is acceptable, while those greater than 0.9 are deemed Excellent (Kaiser, 1974). The Bartlett’s test for sphericity was also significant, χ2(325) =5086.79, p<.001, implying that the correlation coefficients were strong enough to detect a suitable structure in factor analysis. Mardia’s test of multivariate normality was carried out, which indicated that normality was not met. Specifically, skewness was significant, χ2(3276) =5412.99, p < .001, and kurtosis was also significant, z=27.59, p<.001.

Principal Axis Factoring with Promax rotation and polychoric correlations was performed on 26 items to find the factors. This extraction was adopted considering the ordinal nature of response points (Holgado-Tello et al., 2010) and non-normal distribution. The number of factors to be retained was based on multiple criteria, including parallel analysis, eigenvalues greater than 1, scree plot, model-fit indices and total variance explained to be at least 50%. Factors were retained if they had at least three adequately loaded, non-cross-loading items (Samuels, 2017). Items having communalities below .20 were omitted (Child, 2006; Samuels, 2017).

Stevens (2002) suggests factor loading above 0.40 while Hair et al. (2019) considers factor loading above 0.30 as acceptable for a sample with 350 participants, so the more stringent cutoff of .40 was used to retain the items. In case of multiple factors, existing literature does not enumerate a fixed procedure for item elimination (Güvendir & Özkan, 2022). In the present study, exploratory factor analysis was carried out sequentially until there was no item remaining with cross-loading (Samuels, 2017) and at least three items loaded adequately on a factor.

In the first iteration, parallel analysis (Horn, 1965), the Kaiser–Guttman criterion (Kaiser, 1960), and inspection of the scree plot (Cattell, 1966) suggested a 4-factor solution, however the last factor had only a single item. After removing items with loadings <.40 (Item 11 and Item 13), cross-loading (Item 17) and single item in fourth factor (Item 15), model fit improved, the Kaiser–Guttman rule, the scree plot (Fig. 1) and parallel analysis (Supplementary Figure 3) consistently indicated a three-factor solution. In the next iteration, Item 18 did not load adequately and was removed, while Item 22 showed cross-loadings and was subsequently excluded. The final solution continued to yield a three-factor structure across the three criteria referred. Stepwise item exclusion is shown in Supplementary Figure 4.

Scree plot indicating Eigen values from factor reduction analysis.

The model demonstrated a clean and interpretable structure with an acceptable fit to the EFA data, χ² (133) = 318.805, RMSEA = .062, 90% CI [.053, .071], and SRMR = .030. Incremental fit indices also supported the model, with TLI = .922 and CFI = .946. Moreover, the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) decreased with each iteration, indicating progressive model improvement. The final three-factor model demonstrated superior fit compared to the one and two-factor alternatives. The twenty items retained after EFA are shown in Table 2.

Assessment of factor wise normality and floor-ceiling effects

For each factor, skewness and kurtosis were assessed as per the permissible ranges of ±1 and ±2, respectively. The floor-ceiling effect for each factor was computed by dividing the number of respondents who scored the minimum and maximum possible score, respectively, by the total number of respondents (Table 4). Factor wise Floor-ceiling effects show whether a factor as a whole can distinguish between participants. The presence of floor ceiling effect can reduce variance, validity, reliability and cause inaccuracies in measurement (Terwee et al., 2007). The widely used threshold of >15% was adopted to mark the floor-ceiling effect in this study (McHorney & Tarlov, 1995).

Discussion

With increasing technological advancements worldwide, the majority of the population uses the internet to search for health-related information. Link et al. (2021) found that nearly 72% in Germany, 80% in Austria, and 84% in the Netherlands sought OHI. Similar trends were evident in studies conducted in the United States (Ajayi et al., 2023), China (Liu, 2022), and India (Parija et al., 2020). The present study aimed to construct and validate a scale that measures Online health information seeking in terms of extent and valence. This tool was premised in bridging the gap in previous research by establishing a standardized measure that not only focuses on attitudes, motivations, and impact but also taps the behavioral aspect directly.

Item writing was governed by inductive and deductive approach, followed by a thorough qualitative and quantitative assessment of content and face validity. Participants who declined consent and/ or did not meet the eligibility criteria, gave duplicate responses or incomplete demographic data, were filtered resulting in a final sample of 364 (study 1) and 236 (study 2) considered for analysis. The low non-response rate in both studies served as a preventative measure against potential response rate bias (Kelley et al., 2003).

Descriptive level item reduction was employed on 27 items assessing mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis. Only ‘item 9’ was eliminated on the basis of skewness. After satisfying the acceptable thresholds for Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin and Bartlett’s sphericity test, the subsequent 26 items were subjected to Exploratory factor analysis with Promax rotation. Items with factor loading <.40 (Stevens, 2002), cross loadings, and fewer than three items in a factor were excluded sequentially (Samuels, 2017) in four rounds, resulting in a 3-factor structure with 20 items accounting for 51.1% variance and appropriate fit indices. The structure was also validated through a CFA with DWLS estimator, where χ2/df, CFI, TLI, GFI, RMSEA, and SRMR were found to be excellent.

The items in each of the three factors were independently interpreted by both researchers and one independent subject matter expert to unravel underlying commonality for naming them. Factor 1 with 11 items was labeled as ‘Perceived Benefits and Engagement’, which included individual-related factors such as positive attitude, perceived advantages, belief in one’s ability to navigate OHIS, health severity and active existing or prior engagement with internet sources for obtaining health information. This factor is consistent with previous literature, which suggests attitude towards OHI has a positive relationship with intention (Kim et al., 2012) and online health information's usefulness, quality, and trustworthiness are the main determinants of OHIS (Wang et al., 2021). Win et al. (2015) noted that using Online health information increases patient knowledge and understanding as well as facilitates enhanced communication between patient and medical professionals. Further, one’s belief about self-efficacy and perceived severity were linked positively to OHIS using multiple sources (Jiang et al., 2021).

The second factor was identified as ‘Perceived Risks’, comprising 6 items assessing the barriers and negative consequences of OHIS including difficulty in finding trustworthy information, excessive worry, contradictory information, unnecessary self-treatment and diagnosis as well as exaggerated severity of ordinary health conditions. The escalation of common symptoms being portrayed as rare and severe has been linked to negative affective states such as anxiety (White & Horvitz, 2009) and excessive worrying. Previous literature also highlights concerns around the credibility of online health information found on common websites (Scantlebury et al., 2017) and unsupervised content culminating in erroneous information dissemination. Perceived Risks are often considered to adversely influence OHIS (Augustaitis et al., 2021; Wei et al., 2016); however, they may also increase OHIS. McMullan et al. (2019) concluded that despite individuals experiencing negative consequences, such as worry while searching information online, they still repeatedly engage in OHIS for assurance benefits. The negative emotions may contribute to increased OHIS as these individuals need unceasing reassurance, while in others, it may reduce it due to these challenges which explains the insignificant correlation with other two factors.

Factor 3 was named as ‘Social influences’, comprising 3 items. These included items assessing social influences such as seeking online health information due to family influences such as discussion with members, feedback from healthcare professionals (Ma et al., 2023), or availing it for social support benefits (Ren et al., 2019). OHIS helps individuals to connect with others experiencing the same illness often through online community groups (Nambisan, 2011). Popovac and Roomaney’s (2022) scale on online health-seeking behavior has also identified support seeking to be a potential motivation for seeking health information. Cline and Haynes (2001) have explored increased interpersonal communication and interactivity as a consequence of OHIS.

The item clustering in three factors is also supported by theoretical frameworks. The roots of first factor ‘Perceived Benefits and Engagement’ and second factor, ‘Perceived Risks’ can be traced to the Health Belief Model (Rosenstock, 1974). The first factor has items assessing perceived benefits (e.g. Item 24: Suggestions from online sources can improve my health.), perceived threat (e.g. Item 3: I seek information from online health sources when I have a serious health condition.), cues to action (e.g. Item 6: I check online health information when I have an upcoming medical consultation.) and self-efficacy (e.g. Item 5: I can browse through different online health information sources to find what I need.) which are likely to increase OHIS as also found in other researches (Jiang et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2019). As positive attitude towards OHIS is associated with OHIS behavior, some items assessing behavioral engagement (e.g. Item 1: I have viewed health-related information (such as treatment, symptoms, or prevention on the internet.)) are also found in this factor. Since CMIS integrates HBM with other models, the items in this factor also reflect the ‘antecedent component’ of CMIS namely, ‘direct experiences’ (e.g. Item 2: Online health information has improved my understanding of medical conditions.) ‘beliefs’ (e.g. Item 10: Online health information is reliable.) and ‘salience’ (e.g. Item 25: I seek online health information when I experience new or unusual symptoms.) component.

The second factor (Perceived Risks) is parallel to ‘Perceived barriers’ in HBM that tends to decrease OHIS with items tapping on unnecessary diagnosis (Item 26: Referring to online health information results in unnecessary diagnosis), worrying (Item 20: Online health information leads to excessive worry.), difficulty in finding trustworthy sources (Item 27: I have difficulty in finding trustworthy sources of health information online.), etc. While the third factor ‘Social influences’ draws from External cues (e.g. Item 21: Feedback from healthcare professionals encourages me to seek additional information.) in the HBM model and Antecedents specifically ‘Personal experience’ component of CMIS model (e.g. Item 16: I tend to look for online health information when someone in my social circle shares their health experiences) that contains direct experience as well as experience through others (Johnson et al., 1995). Based on the statistical analyses as well as support from existing literature, these three factors together offer a comprehensive assessment of OHIS.

The psychometric properties of the scale were also examined. Internal consistency was examined as a measure of reliability using Cronbach's alpha, McDonald's omega, Split-Half method, Guttman's lambda and Test-retest reliability coefficient which was found to be above 0.7 and considered good (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994). Internal consistency for each factor was also computed and found to be adequate. The third factor, had slightly lower reliability (α=0.68) in comparison to other factors owing to only three items in this sub-scale as reliability estimates are strongly influenced by length of scale (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994). However, it still represents moderate and satisfactory internal consistency (Janssens et al., 2008).

For construct validity, Pearson's correlation showed a positive relationship between Perceived Benefits and Engagement, the Social Influences factor, and Attitude towards OHI, and the Information seeking subscale. No significant correlations were found between Perceived Risks and Attitude towards OHI and Information seeking subscale, as some adverse effects, such as potential worry, may increase OHIS (Augustaitis et al., 2021), while others suggest a decrease in OHIS (McMullan et al., 2019) thereby highlighting lack of consistent directional influence. Convergent validity was also affirmed by AVE, CR, while HTMT provided support for discriminant validity.

Further testing for measurement invariance across sex demonstrated that the configural model had good fit (Table 8). Constraining the factor loadings and item intercepts to equality across male and female group resulted in permissible minor changes in fit supporting metric and scalar invariance respectively. The ΔCFI=.006 in current study, was marginally above stringent criteria of ΔCFI=.005 used by some researchers, however it remained within the widely used criterion of ΔCFI ≤ .010 (Chen, 2007; Cheung & Rensvold, 2002) indicating acceptable measurement invariance for sex. Taken together these findings suggest that OHIS scale is a multidimensional, standardized and reliable measure for both males and females.

Limitations

Despite the strengths of a comprehensive scale constructed to be validated in the Indian population, the study has certain limitations. Firstly, the sample consisted of predominantly female participants belonging to middle-class socio-economic status resulting in a limited generalizability. Previous studies have shown differences informed by gender and SES whereby females tend to engage more proactively in OHIS as well as refer to a greater number of sources as compared with males (Płaciszewski et al., 2022; Rowley et al., 2017). Even Higher SES predicted more OHIS (Jacobs et al., 2017). Secondly, distinct items in demographic or the scale specifically targeting engagement in Online health information seeking on behalf of significant others, such as family or friends, were not addressed. Thirdly, the impact of existing Clinical health conditions and personality variables may differentially affect Online health information-seeking behavior and influence the results. Fourth, although the scale assesses self-efficacy in few items, the actual awareness about Online health information sources and skills required for optimally utilizing OHI was not explored. Lastly, while the sample size for test-retest (N=69) was deemed appropriate (Terwee et al., 2007), it was still relatively small and should be interpreted cautiously.

Implications and suggestions for future research

This scale would be among the first to comprehensively cover attitudes, engagement, social influences, and risks faced while engaging in OHIS. By utilizing response points measuring the extent of Online health information seeking, it taps on the behavioral and attitudinal aspect as opposed to just attitudinal favorableness assessed by previously existing measures.

The scale can be used by healthcare practitioners to gain insights about individual’s Online health information seeking which can positively or negatively influence healthcare professional-patient relationship (Luo et al., 2022; Win et al., 2015) as well as patient cooperation. Excessive Online health information seeking may pose an antecedent for anxiety and mental illnesses (Swar et al., 2017), misconceptions, or even heightened insight, thus highlighting the significance of its assessment. Therefore, future studies can assess OHIS in clinical samples and for individuals who seek healthcare advise more frequently. The high reliance on websites and social media platforms for seeking health information emphasizes the need for regulatory frameworks and guidelines. Efforts should be made for the identification and dissemination of reliable information sources to expand patient knowledge.

This scale also provides a standardized alternative to existing questionnaires that use single or few items (Raudenská, 2026) to assess Online health information seeking. The scale can be used in its totality to measure positive attitudes and engagement (Factor 1 and 2) and risks (Factor 2), or may be used to examine specific aspects contingent on the research purpose by utilizing only the subscales. However, it must also be acknowledged that the third factor ‘social influence’ had only 3 items, so further refinements can be done to capture this domain even more comprehensively by adding relevant items which can also potentially increase the reliability of this sub-scale. While the total variance explained meets the acceptable criteria (Hair et al., 2019), complexity and multifactorial nature of OHIS construct may contribute to the unexplained variance which can be explored in future studies. Related constructs like Concern about Health (Ahadzadeh et al., 2018), Health literacy, etc., are linked with greater OHIS (Jia et al., 2021), but were not explicitly captured in this scale, which may potentially account for part of the residual variance.

Lastly, the scale can be used in cross-sectional and longitudinal studies to compare and validate Online health information seeking patterns across different populations (e.g. varied demographics) and time, respectively. It is also important to assess cross-cultural and cross-national validation as place of origin affects OHIS, with greater OHIS in developed countries (Jia et al., 2021).
