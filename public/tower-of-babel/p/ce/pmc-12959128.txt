Investigation of the Optimal Length of Discharge Summaries in Care Transitions: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Taku Harada, Shintaro Kosaka, Aki Morishima, Seitaro Iguchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDischarge summaries are essential documents that support continuity of care during patient transitions between inpatient and outpatient settings. They help ensure that crucial clinical information is conveyed accurately and completely. However, while their clinical significance is well established, there is little consensus on the optimal format, particularly in terms of length and structural clarity. Excessively brief summaries may omit key details, while overly lengthy ones can hinder efficient information transfer. Despite varied suggestions in the literature—ranging from half a page to four pages—empirical data on physician preferences are limited.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional, web-based survey among physicians who attended a medical symposium focused on transitional care. Participants were asked to indicate their preferred length for A4-sized (approximately 210 × 297 mm) structured discharge summary document. Additional data on demographics and clinical practice settings were also collected. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize responses.ResultsA total of 46 physicians completed the survey. The majority (91.3 %) had over 10 years of clinical experience and primarily practiced in outpatient hospital or clinic settings. Two A4 pages was the most preferred length (67.4 %), followed by one page (17.4 %) and three pages (10.9 %).ConclusionA structured discharge summary of approximately two A4 pages is generally preferred for care transitions, although the optimal length may vary depending on patient complexity and clinical context. Future research should investigate the ideal format, taking into account provider needs, patient engagement, and the integration of artificial intelligence.

Introduction

The advancement of regional healthcare planning in Japan has led to a clear division of roles, such as advanced acute care, acute care, recovery, and chronic care, among medical institutions. Consequently, the transition from hospital-based to region-based healthcare has been aggressively promoted. This shift has created a patient journey wherein individuals move between various medical institutions, welfare facilities, and nursing homes during recovery, resulting in multiple care transitions. The loss of information during these transitions, known as care fragmentation, can lead to readmissions, emergency visits, and adverse events. Therefore, improving the quality of care via patient education at discharge, medication reconciliation, and referral letters is crucial.1 The discharge summary is a key factor in this context.

Discharge summaries requiring more than 3 days to complete have been associated with readmission (odds ratio [OR] 1.09), and the odds of readmission reportedly increased by 1 % for every 3-day delay.2 Furthermore, improving the content of the discharge summary can enhance the quality of outpatient care3 and prevent medication errors.4 However, the optimal content and length of discharge summaries remain unclear.5 Some studies suggest that exceeding 2 pages may reduce quality,6 whereas others advocate summaries that are as concise as half a page.7 Others have suggested that the optimal length is approximately 3–4 pages.8 Additionally, recent changes in healthcare systems and medical information infrastructure, along with growing attention to post-discharge care transitions, have also been reported to contribute to an increase in the length of discharge summaries over time.9

While a certain amount of structured content is necessary for the discharge summary, a shorter length is preferable. Herein, we conducted a survey during a presentation on care transitions at the Regional Medicine Symposium 2024, held on 13 April 2024 at the Japanese Society of Internal Medicine Annual Meeting to investigate the optimal length of discharge summaries.

Methods

A “Google Forms” survey was completed by physicians who agreed to participate during a presentation on care transitions at the Regional Medicine Symposium 2024 held on 13 April 2024, as part of the Japanese Society of Internal Medicine Annual Meeting. The survey was also made available in conjunction with on-demand lecture distribution until the end of May 2024. Data collected included the number of years since the physician’s graduation, sex, type of institution where the physician practiced (hospital or clinic), and their perceived optimal discharge summary length. Responses regarding the optimal length were based on the assumption of an “A4-sized structured discharge summary”.

Statistical analyses were performed using Easy R software.10 Nominal variables such as sex and type of institution were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. T-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used for continuous variables such as years since graduation depending on data distribution. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. There were no missing data, so no imputation methods were necessary. The study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines, 11 and it was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nerima Hikarigaoka Hospital (approval number 24020801). All respondents indicated their consent to participate through an online consent form at the start of the survey.

Results

Of the approximately 2000 attendees, including those who participated in person, 1971 viewed the on-demand lecture, and 46 physicians responded to the survey. Of these, 42 (91.3 %) had >10 years of postgraduate experience, 3 (6.5 %) had 6–9 years, and 1 (2.2 %) had 3–5 years. Twenty-eight (60.9 %) were male, 17 (37.0 %) were female, and 1 (2.2 %) did not specify their gender. The respondents provided outpatient care in hospitals (24 [52.2 %]), clinics (12 [26.1 %]), home care settings (7 [15.2 %]), and others (3 [6.5 %]). The median optimal length of discharge summaries was two pages. Of the 46 respondents, 31 (67.4 %) preferred 2 pages, 8 (17.4 %) preferred 1 page, 5 (10.9 %) preferred 3 pages, 1 (2.2 %) preferred 4 pages and 1 (2.2 %) preferred 5 pages.

Discussion

In the current cross-sectional study, under the assumption of a structured discharge summary, Japanese internists generally preferred a length of approximately two A4-size pages. This result does not significantly deviate from clinical practice and is consistent with the findings of Walraven et al.,6,7 who suggested that the optimal length was 0.5–2.0 pages.

The strengths of this study lie in its novelty. Few studies have examined the optimal length of discharge summaries, which are key to care transitions and frequently used in daily clinical practice. This is the first study of its kind in Japan.

There are some limitations. First, the response rate was low (2.3 % of 1971 viewers), raising the possibility of selection bias. As participants were recruited during a symposium held by the Japanese Society of Internal Medicine, the sample may be skewed toward physicians particularly interested in care transitions. Indeed, many respondents had over 10 years of postgraduate experience. Second, the study did not define the contents of the “structured” discharge summary. As recommendations for structure vary,12,13 the ideal format may depend on patient complexity, institutional norms, and the clinical setting. For some straightforward cases, two pages may be excessive, while for complex patients, critical information may be omitted. Ultimately, the primary aim of a discharge summary is to ensure clear, complete communication. This study demonstrated that, assuming a structured format, a discharge summary of approximately two A4-sized pages is generally considered desirable during care transitions. However, the optimal length may vary depending on the patient and clinical context, and it is essential that the summary includes the necessary information to support effective care transitions. Recent reports indicate that generative AI can enhance discharge summaries’ quality, clarity, and efficiency, contributing to future improvements in care transitions.14 Future research should explore the optimal format of discharge summaries by incorporating qualitative elements such as not only the needs of the receiving providers but also perspectives on the use of generative AI, patient engagement, key content components, and layout preferences (e.g., narrative vs. bullet points).
