Lorenz curves of urban and rural health care expenditure in Colombia: analysis of a cohort of 4.6 million patients
Authors: Oscar Espinosa, Giancarlo Buitrago, Valeria Bejarano, Ramon Castaño
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In Colombia, health expenditure is highly concentrated, with a small portion of the population incurring most costs, particularly in rural areas. Men show greater inequality than women. The study suggests that current risk-adjustment models have opportunities for improvement, emphasizing the need for better health premium definitions and stronger health policies.

Background

In low- and middle-income countries, resources available for health care are often limited, which requires a careful and strategic allocation of funds. The inequities in rural areas, including insufficient investment in hospital infrastructure, shortage of human capital and limited access to quality health services, are well known. Recent global challenges, such as the pressure of technological innovation in medicines, increasing migrant population and demographic transitions, pose a major challenge to the sustainability of health systems [1].

Colombia has a health system based on a managed competition model, where each year the government defines a per capita payment for health insurers to manage the health risk of their members and ensure adequate provision of medical services within the regulated benefit package [2]. The actuarial calculation of this risk premium is based on age1, sex and geographic area2. While these risk categories are relevant in estimating medical care costs, they fail to fully capture the complexity of the population’s health status and the diverse health care needs that may exist within specific groups. For instance, age and sex alone do not reflect differences in the risk of certain conditions, such as chronic diseases, mental disorders or disabilities, which can have a significant impact on health care costs and patients’ quality of life [3].

Main text

Under this context, we constructed Lorenz curves (proportion of total health expenditure allocated to different percentiles of the population) and calculated Gini coefficients (quantifying the inequality in expenditure distribution) for urban and rural areas (to shed light on access barriers that are not visible in the zonal actuarial model), disaggregated by sex and life cycles as defined by the Colombian Ministry of Health and Social Protection (see Fig. 1). This analysis used a cohort of 4,654,297 patients belonging to the contributory scheme, followed from 2016 to 20193 (i.e., the total health expenditure over four years was calculated for each patient). The aim was to demonstrate that the current risk adjusters can improve, and to highlight disparities in the concentration of health spending between rural and urban areas. Monetary values are at 2019 prices and are expressed in US dollars (USD) (taking the annual average of the market's representative exchange rate). The definition of rurality follows that established by the Mission for the Transformation of the Countryside led by the National Planning Department [4].

At both the national level and across different age groups, it can be observed that expenditure is highly concentrated. Less than 10% of the population accounts for over 50% of the expenditure across all urban, rural, and sex-based categories, with per capita health spending exceeding USD 2,243. In contrast, the bottom 50% of the population accounts for only 7.76% of the expenditure on average, with per capita spending below USD 702.

At the national level men exhibit higher concentration of expenditures, similar for adulthood and even higher for the youth, with similar patterns observed in urban and rural areas. The top 20% of the population with the highest per capita expenditure represents 74.04, demonstrating the famous Pareto principle. Similarly, 1% of the population accounts for 21.80% of the expenditure, with an average per capita health spending of USD 38,082, reaching USD 95,485 for the top 0.1% of the population. Women demonstrate a lower concentration of expenditure, even lower in urban areas, as reflected in their lower Gini coefficients (compared to men). For women, the top 1% of the population accounts for an average of 21.35% of the expenditure with per capita spending exceeding USD 12,315.

For individuals aged 11 years and younger and those aged 60 years and older, expenditure patterns are similar across all zones and sexes. However, childhood shows higher concentration levels for rural males, with Gini coefficient exceeding those for early childhood and old age. In terms of per capita health expenditure, the top 1% of the population spends an average of USD 24,579 in early childhood, USD 21,695 in childhood, and USD 47,728 in old age, accounting for 24.46%, 34.03% and 14.94% of total expenditure, respectively.

These results highlight the marked concentration of individual health spending across all population categories analyzed. In summary, we found that individual health spending at the national level is more concentrated in rural areas than in urban areas, and that men experience greater concentration in the distribution of health spending compared to women. In rural areas, people have less access to low or medium complexity health services and, when they have high complexity needs, the probability of reaching a tertiary hospital in an urban center is much lower than for those residing in urban centers. For this reason, people who reach these tertiary hospitals end up concentrating on spending [5].

In this way, deficiencies in risk adjustment may overlook significant variability, potentially resulting in inadequate compensation for insurers covering complex populations, thereby limiting their ability to ensure access to and continuity of care. Accurate risk adjusters are essential for setting equitable health insurance premiums, as they account for differences in patient health status and expected costs [6–8].

Conclusions

The inclusion of health conditions in the statistical-actuarial estimation of the premium could improve the allocation of health resources by better capturing the actual risk profile of individuals. Unlike current risk groups based only on age, sex, and geographic area -which overlook important within-group heterogeneity- health conditions provide direct insight into care needs. For instance, two adult rural men may be classified identically under the current model, yet have vastly different health risks and expected costs due to chronic conditions, disabilities, or mental health disorders. Incorporating these variables could help align financial transfers more closely with real health needs, thereby improving coverage effectiveness and reducing inequities, especially in structurally underserved rural populations.

While incorporating health conditions into risk adjustment formulas may improve equity, it also introduces the risk of perverse incentives, such as excluding high-cost individuals or overcoding diagnoses. Therefore, any reform in this direction must be accompanied by strong national regulation and mandatory oversight mechanisms to ensure transparency and accountability.

Although the integration of social determinants such as employment status, housing, education level, or ethnicity into risk adjustment formulas has been proposed in various contexts, we believe their inclusion in actuarial premium estimation should be approached with caution. These variables, although strongly associated with health outcomes, may not reflect direct or predictable health care costs at the individual level. Moreover, their incorporation could introduce significant complexity, data quality issues, and privacy concerns, especially in countries with heterogeneous information systems. Instead, we argue that social determinants should guide complementary public health interventions and territorial planning, rather than serve as direct input for financial compensation formulas.

Several countries have adopted more refined risk adjustment models that incorporate health conditions to improve equity and efficiency. For example, the Medicare Advantage program in the United States uses diagnostic-based models to adjust payments based on chronic diseases and past health service utilization [9]. Similarly, the Netherlands includes pharmaceutical cost groups and prior diagnoses in its competitive insurance market to prevent undercompensation for high-risk individuals [10, 11]. These international experiences demonstrate that, when paired with robust regulation and monitoring, the inclusion of clinical variables can enhance the precision of risk adjustment without compromising system sustainability.

Finally, we would like to reiterate that robust defining the premium is only one of the challenges facing the Colombian health system and would not be the sole solution to financial sustainability pressures. We advocate for strengthening health technology assessment, conducting technology obsolescence studies, and applying clear criteria in institutional processes for designing and updating the health benefit plan, among other measures. These strategies, at least initially, will be essential to maximize the benefits of each monetary unit invested, ensuring that resources are directed toward interventions that have a high impact in terms of public health for the entire Colombian population.
