Feasibility and tolerability of electrical muscle stimulation during rehabilitation in older adults with osteoporotic vertebral fractures
Authors: Yuko Fujiwara, Toshifumi Fujiwara, Seiichi Kamo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesIn the context of Japan's rapidly aging population, osteoporotic vertebral fractures are becoming increasingly prevalent, creating greater demands for rehabilitation amid healthcare labor shortages. This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility and potential efficacy of abdominal electrical muscle stimulation (EMS) as an adjunct to rehabilitation in older adults with osteoporotic vertebral fractures, focusing on muscle thickness and bowel function.MethodsThirty-four elderly patients (mean age: 85 years) hospitalized for osteoporotic vertebral fractures underwent conventional physical and occupational therapy combined with abdominal EMS four times per week. Rectus abdominis muscle thickness was assessed by ultrasound, and bowel function was evaluated using bowel movement frequency and laxative usage. Interrater reliability for ultrasound measurement was high (ICC = 0.97; 95% CI: 0.90–0.99).ResultsRectus abdominis muscle thickness increased significantly by 0.5 mm (P = 0.001). A non-significant trend toward increased spontaneous bowel movements was observed (5–6 times/week; P = 0.058), and laxative use decreased significantly (P = 0.0172). No EMS-related adverse events were reported, and all participants completed the intervention as scheduled.ConclusionsAbdominal EMS is a feasible, safe, and potentially effective adjunct to conventional rehabilitation for older adults with osteoporotic vertebral fractures, even during periods of bed rest. It may contribute to muscle maintenance and support bowel function. These findings support further investigation in larger prospective studies to confirm efficacy and explore long-term outcomes.Keywords: Vertebral fracture, Electrical muscle stimulation, Rehabilitation in older adults, Muscle strengthening, Constipation management

Introduction

Japan is rapidly becoming a super-aging society, with a steadily increasing proportion of older individuals requiring medical care [1]. As the number of older patients continues to rise, managing age-related pathologies and injuries across all medical disciplines is becoming increasingly complex [2,3]. In orthopaedics in particular, there has been a marked rise in fragility fractures among older adults [4,5], leading to a growing demand for rehabilitation services due to prolonged reductions in home-dwelling rates and overall declines in quality of life [6]. Vertebral fractures are among the most common injuries in the older. While conservative treatments such as corset use and rehabilitation are effective, many older adults struggle to return to independent living and often require prolonged inpatient rehabilitation [7,8]. However, rehabilitation time and staffing are frequently limited, highlighting the need for programs and tools that enable bedridden patients to continue rehabilitation effectively [9].

In response to these challenges, we focused on electrical muscle stimulation (EMS) as a novel adjunctive rehabilitation method for older patients with vertebral fractures [10]. EMS offers several advantages: it can be applied during the acute phase when movement is limited, it requires no change in posture, it is painless, and it reduces the physical burden on both caregivers and patients [11,12]. Additionally, EMS can be administered individually, which aligns well with infection prevention strategies [13,14]. EMS works by electrically stimulating skeletal muscles and motor nerves, thereby enhancing muscle contraction, promoting re-education of movement, and improving local circulation, which may help reduce pain [15,16]. Its effectiveness in muscle strengthening has been reported not only in athletes but also in patients with musculoskeletal disorders, chronic heart failure, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and in intensive care unit (ICU) care [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. Furthermore, EMS has been used to support rehabilitation increasing muscle quality in patients with motor impairments due to conditions such as hemiplegia [22] and spinal cord injury [[23], [24], [25]]. Older adults who are hospitalized may experience interruptions in rehabilitation due to medical complications such as fever. However, EMS offers the advantage of enabling continued rehabilitation during bed rest. To date, however, there have been no reports on the use of EMS in patients hospitalized with spinal vertebral fractures. - Interestingly, EMS is also utilized internationally as a first-line therapy for managing chronic constipation in pediatric populations [26]. Constipation is highly prevalent among the older, typically due to factors such as reduced physical activity, lower fiber and fluid intake, atonic bowel, and the side effects of medications [27]. Older patients with vertebral fractures are particularly susceptible to severe constipation, owing to reduced mobility and the frequent use of oral analgesics following injury. Constipation is also a common complication during conservative treatment of vertebral fractures, particularly when prolonged bed rest is required. A study from Korea comparing one-week versus two-week bed rest after vertebral fractures reported that extended immobilization led to gastrointestinal complications [28,29], including constipation, highlighting the clinical relevance of this issue. Currently, the primary treatment for chronic constipation in the older consists of oral laxatives, with suppositories and enemas recommended in refractory cases [30]. However, these approaches can be labor-intensive and burdensome for caregivers, and as Japanese older population continues to expand, the development of alternative, more efficient management strategies is urgently needed, such as EMS. Indeed, abdominal EMS may improve bowel function by increasing intra-abdominal pressure through abdominal muscle contractions and activating enteric nervous system pathways [31,32], and has been reported to be effective in children, patients with spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis, and women with chronic constipation [28,[31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36]].

In this study, we evaluated the feasibility and tolerability of EMS as part of a rehabilitation program for older patients with vertebral compression fractures. We also explored the potential of EMS to improve secondary complications such as constipation. The effects of rehabilitation, including EMS, were assessed by measuring rectus abdominis muscle thickness using ultrasound imaging, monitoring for adverse events, and evaluating constipation based on bowel movement frequency and laxative use.

Methods

Ethical approval and study design

This prospective interventional study was conducted at Haradoi Hospital between May 2021 and December 2021. The study protocol was approved by the Haradoi Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval Number: 2020–06). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants received verbal and written explanations regarding the purpose, procedures, and potential risks of the study. Written informed consent was obtained prior to participation, and patients were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without consequence.

Participants

The study consecutively enrolled 34 older patients (mean age: 85 years; range: 62–101 years) who were admitted with acute vertebral compression fractures between May and December 2021. As this was an exploratory feasibility study, no a priori sample size calculation was performed; instead, all eligible patients who provided informed consent during the study period were included in the analysis. The diagnosis of vertebral compression fracture was made by plain radiographs in all patients. Acute fractures were defined as those requiring hospitalization due to impaired activities of daily living (ADL), based on the reported time from injury onset or the sudden onset of severe back pain to admission. MRI was not obtained in all cases, but when available, bone marrow edema was considered supportive of acuity. Patients were admitted based on the presence of vertebral compression fractures combined with limitations in ADL that made home care difficult. Admission criteria included severe pain, inability to perform self-care, or posterior wall involvement. All patients received conventional physical therapy (PT) and occupational therapy (OT) during hospitalization. The inclusion criteria were: a diagnosis of vertebral compression fracture confirmed by imaging; age ≥ 65 years; and no contraindications to EMS (eg, pacemakers). To evaluate the feasibility and tolerability of EMS, all patients received EMS in addition to inpatient rehabilitation. Patients who were unable to participate due to significant medical complications or declined EMS intervention were excluded.

Rehabilitation protocol

All participants received a standardized daily PT and OT program, which consisted of range of motion (ROM) exercises, muscle strengthening exercises, and training in ADL. The rehabilitation content was individually tailored based on each patient's functional status and clinical condition. However, there were instances when certain participants were unable to fully engage in therapy sessions or complete assessments due to temporary health issues such as fever, hypertension, headache, or joint pain. Conventional rehabilitation was generally provided on weekdays, approximately four sessions per week, although the exact number of sessions varied depending on weekends and individual conditions. The rehabilitation program was structured as follows: Days 1–3: Bed-based muscle strengthening exercises were initiated to maintain physical function while patients remained on bed rest. Days 4–7 (Week 1): Once the corset was fitted, patients began seated posture training to improve endurance. Gait training was initiated using parallel bars, alongside trunk muscle strengthening exercises including abdominal contractions and the draw-in maneuver. Weeks 2–3: Patients worked toward regaining their pre-injury level of ambulation, either independently or with the use of a cane, depending on their baseline function. Weeks 4–6: Training focused on movement strategies to prevent recurrence of vertebral fractures. Prior to discharge, the home environment was assessed and modified as needed to reduce fall risk.

In addition to conventional rehabilitation, participants underwent abdominal EMS using the ESPURGE® device (ITO Co., Ltd.), a portable EMS system (Fig. 1A). Electrodes coated with a low-impedance, oxidation-resistant silver-carbon gel were placed by the physiotherapist at the motor points on either side of the rectus abdominis muscle approximately at the level of the umbilicus (Fig. 1B). The cost of EMS for each patient primarily involves disposable electrode pads, which cost approximately JPY 850 per set and can be used throughout the treatment course. The EMS device itself represents a one-time investment for the rehabilitation facility. Given that the study population included bedridden individuals and patients with multiple comorbidities, a basic EMS mode was chosen to evaluate its feasibility and tolerability through the simple program outlined below. The EMS parameters were set to a frequency of approximately 50 Hz, with a pulse width ranging from 100 to 200 μs. The intensity was adjusted to induce visible muscle contractions without causing discomfort. EMS was applied concurrently with standard rehabilitation sessions. Each session lasted 20 min, but because stimulation was performed during ongoing therapy, the total rehabilitation time was not substantially extended, with only a brief additional time required for electrode placement. This concurrent application allows efficient muscle stimulation without increasing overall therapy duration. Rehabilitation following vertebral compression fractures requires strengthening of both the abdominal and lumbar muscles [37]. EMS was applied to the abdominal muscles rather than the lumbar muscles because patients with acute vertebral fractures often experienced severe pain and could not tolerate position changes. Abdominal stimulation can be performed safely in the supine position, allowing early initiation without causing additional discomfort.

Body weight, grip strength, and functional independence measure (FIM) [38] were assessed at admission and at discharge. The FIM is an 18-item global measure of independence in ADL, consisting of two subscales: the motor subscale (13 items, including self-care, sphincter control, transfers, and locomotion) and the cognitive subscale (5 items, including communication and social cognition). Each item is scored on a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (total dependence) to 7 (complete independence), with a maximum total score of 126 points.

Outcome measures

To assess the effectiveness of the intervention, two primary outcomes were evaluated: changes in abdominal muscle morphology and the degree of improvement in constipation. Morphological changes in the rectus abdominis muscle were assessed using ultrasonography (Toshiba Aplio 400, Canon Medical Systems, Otawara, Japan) [39]. Measurements were taken bilaterally below the level of the umbilicus within one week of admission and again within one week prior to discharge (Fig. 2A and B). The average thickness of the left and right rectus abdominis muscles was used for analysis (Fig. 2C). Three trained examiners conducted the ultrasound assessments, and inter-rater reliability was confirmed to ensure measurement consistency. Interrater reliability of muscle thickness measurements was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way random-effects model. The ICC among the three ultrasound technicians was 0.97 (95% CI: 0.90–0.99), indicating excellent agreement.

Constipation was evaluated objectively, as some participants were unable to complete subjective questionnaires due to cognitive or physical limitations. The evaluation was based on the number of bowel movements recorded during the first week after admission and the final week prior to discharge. Additionally, the frequency and types of laxatives and enemas administered during these periods were recorded and compared to assess changes in bowel function.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using JMP Pro 17.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean, median, and range. Normality of the distribution for continuous variables was visually assessed using histograms. Depending on the distribution, paired t-tests were used for normally distributed variables, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical data (eg, frequency of defecation, laxative use) were compared using Chi-square or Fisher's exact test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the study participants. During the study period, all eligible patients admitted with acute vertebral compression fractures were screened. One patient was excluded because of persistent unexplained fever after admission, and no patients refused participation. A total of 34 older patients (3 men and 31 women) were included in the study. All participants had been diagnosed with vertebral compression fractures and were admitted to our hospital for rest and rehabilitation. The interval from injury to admission ranged from 0 to 19 days (median 0 days, mean 4 days), with 4 patients for whom the interval was unknown. As shown in Table 1, patients received EMS on a mean of 48 days (median 51, range 20–68). EMS was not applied on a mean of 4 days (median 1, range 0–33), mainly due to temporary conditions such as fever, headache, or joint pain. Table 2 presents data collected at admission and discharge. At discharge, left-hand grip strength showed a statistically significant improvement (P = 0.0280), and the total FIM score also improved significantly (P < 0.0001). The thickness of the rectus abdominis muscle was measured using ultrasound within one week of admission and again during the final week before discharge. On average, the rectus abdominis muscle thickness significantly increased by 0.5 mm over the course of hospitalization (P = 0.0025) (Fig. 3). Bowel movement frequency was recorded during the first week after admission and compared to the final week before discharge. The number of spontaneous bowel movements tended to increase (from a median of 5–6 times per week), though the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.0580). However, the number of laxatives used per day significantly decreased, from a median of 2 to 1 (P = 0.0172). No adverse events related to EMS were observed during the study period.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the feasibility and tolerability of EMS of the rectus abdominis muscle on muscle thickness and bowel function in older patients undergoing inpatient rehabilitation for vertebral compression fractures. Our findings demonstrated a significant increase in rectus abdominis muscle thickness, as assessed by ultrasound, over the course of hospitalization. Additionally, bowel movement frequency showed a tendency to improve, and the number of laxatives used significantly decreased. There were no adverse events attributable to EMS. These results suggest that EMS may be a beneficial adjunct to conventional rehabilitation, supporting both muscular and gastrointestinal function during the recovery period.

Our findings are consistent with prior studies reporting the benefits of EMS in various patient populations. EMS applied to the abdominal muscles has previously been shown to shorten the duration of mechanical ventilation and ICU stays in critically ill patients [40]. It has also been associated with improved cough reflex and trunk stability in post-stroke rehabilitation [41]. In geriatric populations, Miura et al. reported improvements in motor function with abdominal EMS [42], supporting its utility in increasing physical activity and preventing muscle atrophy even during the acute phase of injury. Given that muscle strength in elderly individuals tends to decline rapidly during hospitalization [43,44], the observed average increase of 0.5 mm in the rectus abdominis muscle thickness in this study may be considered a clinically meaningful effect. An increase in rectus abdominis muscle thickness in older adults is considered clinically significant, as it is associated with reduced risk of urinary incontinence and improvements in walking speed and balance [[45], [46], [47]]. In addition, EMS is non-invasive and painless, and it can be safely integrated with conventional physical and occupational therapy programs [[48], [49], [50], [51]]. In fact, all patients in this study completed the EMS sessions as scheduled, and no adverse events were observed.

In this study, the use of laxatives was significantly reduced at discharge compared to admission, and there was a non-significant trend toward an increased frequency of bowel movements. While the absence of a control group limits our ability to determine causality, these findings suggest that EMS may have contributed to improvements in bowel function. Constipation is a well-recognized complication during conservative treatment of vertebral fractures, particularly when prolonged bed rest is required, as extended immobilization can lead to gastrointestinal issues including constipation [31,32]. Previous studies have reported that abdominal EMS can promote intestinal motility and relieve symptoms of constipation, particularly in populations with limited mobility, such as children with chronic constipation or individuals with neurological disorders [[52], [53], [54]]. The mechanism is thought to involve abdominal muscle contractions that increase intra-abdominal pressure, combined with activation of the enteric nervous system, which may enhance intestinal smooth muscle activity [32,55,56]. Previous research using a chicken gut model demonstrated that smooth muscle contractions are modulated by pressure changes through mechanosensitive pathways involving L-type calcium channels and IP3 receptors, highlighting the physiological role of intraluminal pressure in regulating intestinal motility [57]. Although the precise effect of EMS in the present patient population remains unclear, our findings raise the possibility that EMS could support both muscle strengthening and improved bowel patterns in older patients with vertebral compression fractures. Given that EMS can be performed safely in the supine position, it may also be a practical adjunct for rehabilitation in bedridden patients and could potentially be applied in home-based settings, offering a non-pharmacological option to help prevent constipation in this vulnerable population.

Regarding cost, in the present study EMS was provided as part of the inpatient rehabilitation program, and therefore no separate billing was made to individual patients. The only additional material required is disposable pads, which cost approximately 850 JPY per set. If EMS becomes more widely implemented, patients could purchase their own pads, enabling continued use of EMS not only during hospitalization but also in outpatient and home-visit rehabilitation settings. This represents both an opportunity and a future challenge for broader dissemination of EMS in clinical practice.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a prospective study without a control group, focusing primarily on the feasibility and tolerability of EMS rather than establishing causal effects. So, it cannot be proven whether the results were directly attributable to EMS. Second, it was conducted in a single facility with a relatively small sample size, and the marked gender imbalance (3 men and 31 women) further restricts the generalizability of the findings. Thus, this study was exploratory in nature, and larger-scale randomized controlled trials are necessary to validate the findings in the future. Third, the assessment of constipation relied on objective parameters such as bowel movement frequency and laxative use, but did not include validated patient-reported outcomes such as the Bristol Stool Form Scale or constipation severity indices. This was partly because half of the patients in this study had dementia, which made the use of such self-reported measures impractical. Fourth, although the FIM score significantly improved between admission and discharge, exploratory subgroup analyses stratified by abdominal muscle thickness did not show a significant association with FIM improvement (data not shown). Moreover, detailed gait and balance assessments were not performed. Therefore, the clinical significance of the observed increase in rectus abdominis thickness in relation to functional recovery remains unclear. Fifth, standardized pain assessments (eg, VAS) at baseline and discharge were not systematically collected. Half of patients in this study had dementia, making self-reported pain scales impractical. Acute vertebral fracture pain is an important factor influencing functional decline and rehabilitation progress; therefore, the absence of these data limits our ability to evaluate the relationship between pain, rehabilitation performance, and EMS tolerance. Nonetheless, EMS was well tolerated, and no EMS-related adverse events or worsening of pain were reported. Sixth, EMS was applied only to the abdominal muscles and back muscle strength was not assessed; future studies should investigate the effects of EMS on back muscles to better understand its potential impact on overall trunk function. Seventh, although EMS may improve bowel function, not all patients had constipation at baseline, which limits the ability to evaluate its effect specifically in constipated patients. In addition, dietary intake and analgesic use were not systematically recorded, which may have influenced bowel function. Therefore, improvements cannot be solely attributed to EMS. Finally, although ultrasound assessment of muscle thickness is subject to operator dependency and day-to-day variability, measurements in this study were conducted by three trained examiners. The intrarater reliability for rectus abdominis thickness was high (ICC = 0.97), indicating good reproducibility; nevertheless, potential measurement error due to multiple examiners cannot be completely excluded. In addition, while no patients discontinued EMS and no adverse events were observed, feasibility and tolerability were not systematically assessed using standardized tools such as VAS for discomfort or structured skin evaluations. Furthermore, given the long hospitalization period and lack of a control group, it remains difficult to distinguish the specific effects of EMS from natural recovery or conventional rehabilitation.

Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths. It is one of the first to demonstrate that EMS can be safely and feasibly implemented during the acute phase of vertebral compression fractures in older adults, even in patients with comorbidities or limited mobility. Importantly, no adverse events occurred, and adherence to EMS therapy was excellent. These findings support the potential of EMS as a practical, low-burden adjunct to conventional rehabilitation, especially in bedridden or frail elderly patients. Future randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these preliminary findings, assess long-term outcomes such as muscle preservation, bowel function sustainability, and quality of life, and determine the optimal stimulation parameters and implementation strategies for EMS in both clinical and home-based settings.

Conclusions

EMS showed potential benefits in enhancing abdominal muscle strength and alleviating constipation among older adults with vertebral compression fractures. Its non-invasive nature, ease of application, and excellent safety profile make it a promising adjunct to conventional rehabilitation, even in the acute phase when patients may experience severe pain or limited mobility. EMS may offer a practical solution for maintaining physical function and supporting gastrointestinal health in frail or bedridden individuals. Future large-scale, prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and to further explore the long-term effectiveness and broader applicability of EMS in both clinical and home-based rehabilitation settings.
