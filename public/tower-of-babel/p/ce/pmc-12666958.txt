An audit of complications from non physician-led implantable cardiac monitor procedures: A 5-year single-center experience
Authors: Adam Marzetti, Iain Thorp
Published: 2025

Introduction

In 2019, East Kent Hospitals University NHS Foundation Trust launched a nonphysician-led service for implantable cardiac monitor (ICM) implantation, initially led by 2 cardiac scientists. Cardiac scientists in the United Kingdom are responsible for programming cardiac implantable electronic devices such as pacemakers and defibrillators. As clinical scientists, cardiac scientists are registered professionals governed by the Health and Care Professions Council. This service was introduced to address an increasing waiting list for patients awaiting ICM implantation, which at the time exceeded 6 months.

Over the following 5 years, the team expanded to include 2 nurses and 2 additional cardiac scientists. Existing literature supports the safety of ICM implants by trained nonmedical staff1 and demonstrates a positive impact on workforce capacity and retention.2 Careful monitoring through audit of the East Kent Hospitals University NHS Foundation Trust nonphysician-led ICM implant service prioritized patient safety.

The audit aimed to evaluate the safety of the nonphysician-led ICM implant service by accurately documenting any procedure-related complications.

ICM implantation procedures performed by physicians were excluded from the audit.

Methods

Retrospective audit data were collected over a 5-year period (2019–2024) from an internal shared database tracking all ICM implantation procedures performed by nonmedical staff, including all procedure-related complications.

This audit focused on 4 key complications:1.Bleeding requiring treatment: Postprocedural bleeding managed with clinical intervention, such as suturing or pressure dressing, that did not result in an extended stay in hospital2.Erosion: Device exposure due to wound dehiscence in the absence of clinical infection3.Infection: Clinically suspected or confirmed infection based on microbiological evidence (positive cultures) or elevated inflammatory markers (eg, C-reactive protein)4.Wound problems requiring treatment: Any wound-related complication that necessitated a return to hospital but did not meet criteria for bleeding, erosion, or infection.

Results

Data from 1588 nonphysician-led ICM implants were analyzed. Thirty-nine complications were recorded, corresponding to an overall complication rate of 2.4%.

Of the complications that resulted in premature explantation of the ICM (1.45%, n = 23), infection was confirmed in 0.19% (n = 3) and erosion in 1.26% (n = 20) of cases. Incomplete microbiological testing means that subclinical infection cannot be excluded as a potential cause of erosion; thus, the overall infection rate may be underreported.

The 16 remaining complications were managed with minor interventions, such as sutures and pressure dressings, and did not require premature explantation of the ICM. These findings reflect a low incidence of serious complications, with infections representing a minority. Conclusion

This 5-year audit demonstrates a low complication rate for ICM implantation procedures performed by trained nonphysician staff at a single UK center. The findings support the safety and viability of a nonphysician-led ICM implant service and reinforce existing evidence for role expansion to cardiac scientists and nurses. Further development to include nonphysician-led ICM explant services has also been shown to be safe3 and represents a logical progression in this clinical model.
