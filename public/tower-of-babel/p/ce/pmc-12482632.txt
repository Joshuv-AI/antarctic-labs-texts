The impact of disordered eating and eating behavior on weight loss after sleeve gastrectomy in Egyptian adolescents
Authors: Mohey Elbanna, Amany Falah, Ahmed Magdy Hafez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBariatric surgery is the most effective treatment for obesity in Adolescents. However, disordered eating is frequent in this population. Several studies found that disordered eating affects weight loss after bariatric surgery. The aim of this study was to find out the impact of disordered eating on weight loss in Egyptian adolescents two years after sleeve gastrectomy.MethodsThis is a retrospective cohort study conducted at Ain Shams University Hospitals from March 2020 to March 2024. It included 47 adolescents with obesity who underwent laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG) two years before. Their age was 16.2 ± 0.67 years (range 14.5 – 17.8 years old). Their body mass indices were above 99th percentile for age and sex with or without comorbidities. They had a mean BMI of 39.9 kg/m² (range: 36.9 – 54.3 kg/m²). Patients with psychiatric illnesses and eating disorders were excluded. Patients were recruited after completion of two years of follow up for weight loss. Weight loss was evaluated by total weight loss % (TWL%). Disordered eating was detected by the Arabic version of EDE-Q v.6.0, and a global score of > 2.5 was considered positive. TWL% was correlated with EDE-Q score.ResultsAfter two years, the mean TWL% was 27.5% ± 7.7%. Nineteen patients (40.4%) had EDE-Q score more than 2.5. The EDE-Q positive patients had a mean %TWL of 25.7% ± 4.8%, while EDE-Q negative patients had a mean %TWL of 30.5% ± 6.2%. The difference is statistically significant.ConclusionWe conclude that the outcome of sleeve gastrectomy in adolescents is affected by their eating behavior as soon as the restrictive effect of the operations subsides.

Introduction

The World Health Organization considers adolescence between 10 and19 years [1]. The definition of the American Association of Pediatrics of adolescence is ages between 10 and 21years [2]. In this stage, the adolescent undergoes physical, social, and cognitive transition towards adulthood [3]. Adolescents with obesity face unique challenges. Some of them suffer from obesity-related diseases. The majority of adolescents spend years struggling with obesity management, and its social and academic consequences [4]. Many adolescents with obesity have concomitant psychiatric diseases, and disordered eating [5]. In Egypt, healthcare is divided into pediatric care and adult care, with lack of special care to meet the needs for adolescents [6]. Metabolic and bariatric surgery (MBS) has emerged as the most effective treatment for adolescents with severe obesity, with effective long term effect [7].

Disordered eating is abnormal eating behavior between normal eating and eating disorders and may include symptoms and behaviors of eating disorders, but at a lesser frequency or lower level of severity. Disordered eating may include loss of control eating, night time eating, restrictive eating, compulsive eating, or irregular or inflexible eating patterns [8]. Disordered eating behavior is prevalent among adolescents [9]. Several studies have shown that the prevalence of disordered eating among Arab adolescents is comparable to that in Western countries [10]. Specially in bariatric patients disordered eating behaviors are common. In the LABS-3 study 40.4 % reported loss-of-control (LOC) eating, 30.5 % reported binge eating, 16.5 % reported night eating, and 6.4 % reported compensatory behaviors. These behaviors may improve after bariatric surgery. Yet disordered eating behaviors may persist after bariatric surgery, recur, or may even develop de novo [11].

Several studies found that the presence of preoperative disordered eating does not affect weight loss after bariatric surgery [11,12]. On the other side, disordered eating after bariatric surgery is often associated with inadequate weight loss, and weight regain, as well as psychological disorders, as depression, low self-esteem, and cognitive impairment [13].

The aim of this study was to find out the impact of eating behavior on weight loss in Egyptian adolescents two years after sleeve gastrectomy.

Methods

Type of study and patients

This is a retrospective cohort study conducted at Ain Shams University Hospitals from March 2020 to March 2024. Convenience sampling was used to recruit patients.

The study was conducted according to STROCSS guidelines [14]. It included 47 adolescents with obesity who underwent laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG) two years before. They were 30 females and 17 males (63.8 %, and 36.2 %). They had a mean age of 16.2 ± 0.67 (range 14.5–17.8 years old). Their body mass indices were above 99th percentile for age and sex with or without comorbidities. They had a mean weight of 112.7 ± 17.2 (range from 88 - 172kg). They had a mean BMI of 39.9 kg/m2 (range: 36.9–54.3 kg/m2). All the patients had BMI ≥35 kg/m2. Study procedures

All patients underwent laparoscopic Sleeve Gastrectomy. They were followed up for two years, for weight loss, and resolution of comorbidities. Weight loss was evaluated by total weight loss % (TWL%). During follow up they were given nutritional instructions, that included gradual meal progression from clear liquids to normal diet, with instructions on eating in small bites, chew food well and eat slowly, with adequate time between eating and drinking.

The Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q)

At the end of the two-year period, the Arabic version of Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q v.6.0) was used to evaluate the patients for disordered eating in the last 28 days. The EDE-Q consists of 28-items and is self-administered. It generates one global score and four subscale scores. The four subscale scores are restraint, eating concern, weight concern, and shape concern [[15], [16], [17]]. The Arabic version of EDE-Q was validated by Melisse et al. and Aldubayan et al. [16,17].

The scores range from 0 to 6, and higher scores indicate greater psychopathology.

The EDE-Q evaluates three types of binge eating: objective bulimic episodes, characterized by the consumption of a substantial amount of food accompanied by a sense of loss of control; subjective bulimic episodes, characterized by the consumption of a relatively small amount of food accompanied by a sense of loss of control; and objective overeating, defined by the consumption of a substantial quantity of food without a sense of diminished control. EDE-Q also includes questions about self-induced vomiting and intensive exercise as methods for shape or weight control. We considered total score values of 2.5 to discriminate between individuals with low risk and high risk of eating disorders [18]. The risk of disordered eating was correlated with the total weight loss%.

Statistical analysis

Recorded data were analyzed using the statistical package for social sciences, version 23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). The quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation and ranges. Data were explored for normality using Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk Test. Independent-samplest-test of significance was used when comparing between two means. The confidence interval was set to 95 % and the margin of error set to 5 %, and the P-value < 0.05 was considered significant. Logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate the correlation between disordered eating and weight loss outcome of LSG after 2 years.

Results

Surgical procedure and postoperative management

All procedures were completed laparoscopically with no conversion. The operative time was 62 ± 17min (range 42–110min). The hospital stay was 2.1 ± 1.4 days (range 1–6 days). Postoperatively, two patients suffered from intra-abdominal bleeding. In both cases, re-laparoscopy was carried out. Hematomas at the operative site were found and evacuated. The bleeding points on the staple line were clipped and sutured. Two more patients suffered from superficial wound infection at the site of extraction of the stomach. We found that, on average, 40.4 % (19/47) of the patients engaged in episodes of overeating approximately 5.38 ± 8.25 times over the past month. About 36.2 % of the patients felt loss of control (14/47). Instances of self-induced vomiting and laxative use were infrequent but present in about 14.9 % of the patients (7/47). A significant majority (68.1 %) reported no days of secretive eating (32/47), indicating that most patients are not engaging in this behavior. However, 12.8 % of the patients admitted to eating in secret every day (6/47), indicating that some patients struggle with food-related shame or discomfort.

Additionally, 3 patients had a daily feeling of guilt over eating (6.4 %), while 17 patients (36.2 %) reported feeling guilty "a few of the times" when they indulge in large volume eating, highlighting the persistent psychological burden of weight management post-surgery.

Interestingly, 22 patients (46.8 %) were not concerned about others seeing them eat, suggesting a level of confidence or acceptance in public settings. Nevertheless, 25.5 % of the patients were moderately concerned, 3 (6.4 %) patients were markedly concerned and 3 patients (6.4 %) were always concerned, indicating that for some, the fear of judgment remains a significant issue.

A good percentage of patients (42.6 %; 20/47) reported that their weight markedly influenced how they judge themselves as individuals. Dissatisfaction with shape was reported by 11 patients (23.4 %), and 20 patients (42.6 %) felt that their body shape had a significant impact on their self-judgment (Table 4).

Discussion

This study included 47 Egyptian adolescents with obesity who underwent laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy two years earlier. Disordered eating was tested in this group of patients applying the Arabic version of Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q) [16,17]. Patients were considered EDE-Q positive if the score of EDE-Q is more than 2.5. EDE-Q positive patients were considered having disordered eating or high risk for disordered eating (DE/DEHR). Total weight loss percentage (TWL%) two years after LSG was correlated with DE/DEHR. Nineteen patients (40.4 %) had EDE-Q score more than 2.5. These patients had a mean %TWL of 25.7 % ± 4.8 % (range 19.5 %–29.1 %), while EDE-Q negative patients had a mean %TWL of 30.5 % ± 6.2 % (range: 24.4 %–37.3 %). The difference is statistically significant. This proves the correlation between post-bariatric eating behavior and weight loss in adolescents 2 years after Sleeve Gastrectomy, as the early restrictive effect of LSG fades away. These findings are consistent with those of several previous studies [18,19]. Correlation between each of the four subscales of EDE-Q and TWL% was statistically significant, indicating that postoperative restraint, eating concern, shape concern, and weight concern all correlate with weight loss two years postoperatively. This is consistent with most studies on the impact of eating behavior after surgery on weight loss [11,20,22].

We found that, on average, 40.4 % (19/47) of the patients engaged in episodes of overeating approximately 5.38 ± 8.25 times over the previous month. About 36.2 % of the patients felt loss of control eating (14/47). This is higher than reported by other studies [20,21]. This may indicate that there is a high prevalence of disordered eating among Egyptian adolescents undergoing Bariatric Surgery. The correlation between loss of control eating and weight loss has been proved in previous studies. The findings of several studies support ours that disordered eating results in less post-bariatric weight loss [22,23].

Instances of self-induced vomiting and laxative use were rare but present in about 14.9 % of the patients (7/47). A significant majority (68.1 %) reported no days of secretive eating (32/47), indicating that most patients are not engaging in this behavior. However, 12.8 % of the patients admitted to eating in secret every day (6/47), pointing to possible ongoing struggles with food-related shame or discomfort. Secretive eating after bariatric surgery is relatively common in patients experiencing loss of control eating. Lydecker et al. (2019) found secretive eating in 37 % of post-bariatric patients seeking treatment of loss of control eating [24]. They associated secretive eating with body dissatisfaction than to weight loss after surgery. Our patients had less incidence of secretive eating probably because they were adolescents, and could not hide from their adult parents, who have more control on their daughters and sons than western societies.

In our study, 22 patients (46.8 %) did not have eating concern, while 25.5 % of the patients were moderately concerned, 6.4 % were markedly concerned and 3 patients (6.4 %) always had eating concern, indicating that for 38.9 % the fear of judgment remains a significant issue. These results are consistent with the findings of previous research [23,24].

Only 3 patients had a daily feeling of guilt over eating (6.4 %), while 17 patients (36.2 %) reported feeling guilty "a few of the times" when they indulge in large volume eating. This may indicate the persistence of weight concern after surgery.

A good percentage of patients (42.6 %; 20/47) reported that their weight markedly influenced self-judgement, reflecting the deep rooted psychological challenges associated with obesity that persist after surgery. Shape concern was reported by 11 patients (23.4 %), and 20 patients (42.6 %) felt that their body shape had a significant impact on their self-judgment. Shape and weight concern in adolescents undergoing bariatric surgery were studied and discussed by several authors. The relationship between eating, weight and shape concern on one side and bariatric surgery outcome in adolescents on the other side have been established [[23], [24], [25]].

Laparoscopic Sleeve Gastrectomy (LSG) has proved itself as a safe effective procedure for weight loss among adolescents. Yet, the outcome is affected by eating behavior and the presence or absence of eating psychopathology. Several studies found that the outcome of bariatric surgery in adolescents is affected by their eating behavior after surgery. Our results are aligned with these findings [21,24,26].

The study strength lies in the fact that it is one of a few studies on the relationship between the weight loss outcome of LSG and postoperative eating behavior in Egyptian adolescents. It demonstrates the impact of abnormal eating behavior on weight loss in adolescents 2 years after surgery.

Study limitations

It still has some limitations. The limitation of the study is that it is a single center study. Data were collected at two years after LSG. The retrospective nature of the study is a limitation. Collection of data at different postoperative time points would give more insight on the impact of eating behavior and weight loss outcome after bariatric surgery. Other psychological characteristics of the study population were not studied, considering that adolescents with obesity and psychiatric illness were excluded from the study. Female adolescents constituted the majority of our study population (63.8 %). This is a study limitation. EDE-Q is a self-reported assessment tool, adding to the study limitations. Finally, the study is limited by dichotomizing the outcome.

Conclusion

We conclude that the outcome of sleeve gastrectomy in adolescents is affected by the eating behavior of the patient as soon as the restrictive effect of the operation subsides.

In the post-bariatric surgery period, behavioral intervention to diagnose and treat disordered eating is required to maintain the weight loss achieved by the operation.
