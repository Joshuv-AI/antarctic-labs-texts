Protocol for identification of NEMF-mediated C-terminal extensions on mitochondrial nonstop proteins via customized MS/MS spectra database searching
Authors: Leijie Chen, Jinyou Mo, Yuyong Tan, Liang Lv, Jia Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe ribosome-associated protein quality control (RQC) core factor nuclear export mediator factor (NEMF) appends C-terminal extended sequences (CESs) to ribosome-stalled nascent chains (NCs). Specific CESs compositions could be directly recognized by enzymes and facilitate NC degradation. Yet, NEMF-mediated CESs remains largely unidentified. Here, we present a protocol for identifying and characterizing NEMF-mediated C-terminal modifications on mitochondrial NCs (mitoNCs) via tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) analysis. We describe strategies aimed at constructing a customized MS/MS spectra database for unknown CESs and detail the steps for CES-modified sample preparation.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Lv et al.1

Before you begin

Ribosome-associated protein quality control (RQC) provides translational surveillance for aberrant ribosome stalling, and its dysregulation may lead to neurological diseases.2,3,4 The RQC core factor Rqc2/NEMF recruits E3 ligase Ltn1/Listerin for the degradation of nascent polypeptide chains (NCs) arising from ribosome stalling. Rqc2/NEMF could modify NCs with a C-terminal Ala and Thr extension (CAT tail; Rqc2) or CAT tailing-like extensions (NEMF) by recruiting aminoacyl-tRNA to the large ribosomal subunit A-site independently of the mRNA template.5,6 Appending C-terminal extended sequences (CESs) to NCs help in pushing NCs through ribosome exit tunnel to potentially expose lysine for ubiquitination via Ltn1/Listerin. Specific forms of CESs mediated by NEMF could serve as degrons to promote NCs degradation via Listerin-independent proteolysis pathway.1,7 Compared to well-identified Rqc2-mediated CAT-tails, NEMF-mediated CESs in mammal were heterogeneous yet not-fully characterized.1,4,7,8 Identification of NEMF-mediated CESs may help in gaining a better understanding of the NCs elimination mechanism and identifying unknown downstream C-end recognition molecules, and fundamental for better understanding of the role of NEMF in diseases.

Mitochondria-targeted RQC substrates may undergo co-translational translocation during protein synthesis upon ribosome stalling and thereby are more prone to be marked for CESs. Here, we introduce a protocol for customizing the MS/MS spectra database to analyze NEMF-mediated CESs appended to mitochondria-targeted nonstop proteins. The strategies utilized to construct the MS/MS database could be modified according to the experimental purpose, and this protocol may also be used to identify the CESs to RQC substrates targeted to different cellular compartments or in different cell types or species.

Customized MS/MS spectra database construction

Timing: 3–4 days

Timing: 3–4 days

This section describes the construction of the customized MS/MS database, the design principles should be based on the formats of the targeted peptides. Putative mitoGFPns sequences include mitochondrial targeting sequences (MSVLTPLLLRGLTGSARRLPVPRAKIHSLGPTGTG), GFP protein sequences, linker peptide before poly(A) signal (GPSFLVQSGGKPIPNPLLGLDSTRTGHHHHHHRLCLLVASHLLFAPPPCLPSPWKVPLPLSFPNKMRKLHR), putative lysis repeats generated by poly(A) tail (from 1 to 10 K repeats), and potential candidates of extended C-terminal extensions composed by reported amino acids (AAs; A, T, G, N, Q, P, C, W, S, D, Y).4 The principle of database construction depends on the research purpose. E.g., Replace the organelle target sequence of plasmid, or expand amino acids combinations like poly-Gly tract focused database (i.e., reported homopolymeric Gly repeats and its relatively frequent incorporation in previous study).41.A schematic of the strategy of putative mitoGFPns sequences of Ala-library is presented in Figure 2, the strategies for CESs design are:a.All 11 amino acids homopolymeric repeats from 1 to 30.b.Dipeptides arranged by all 11 amino acids repeats from 1 to 15.c.Tripeptides randomly arranged by A, T, G, N, Q, Y repeats from 1 to 10.d.Tetrapeptides randomly arranged by A, T, G, N repeats from 1 to 8.e.Poly-Ala (Alanine number is 5 to 30) with single incorporation with the other 10 AAs except alanine at different positions from the first to fifth counting from the C-end.f.Poly-Ala tract at C-terminus with N-terminal peptides composed with diverse amino acid combinations; the putative peptides include:i.Homopolymeric amino acids (except alanine) repeats from 3 to 8 plus poly-Ala (alanine number is 3 to 20).ii.Amino acids except alanine randomly arranged to dipeptides repeats from 1 to 10 plus poly-Ala (alanine number is 3 to 15).iii.6 amino acids (A, T, G, N, Q, Y) randomly arranged to tripeptides repeats from 1 to 6 plus poly-Ala (alanine number is 3 to 10).g.Use the same composition criteria as in step 1f to create various amino acid compositions at C-terminus of the CESs, with the poly-Ala tract at N-terminus. Day 1

4.Perform reverse transfection of Listerin siRNA:a.For each cell line, around 5 × 90% confluency 100-mm dish cells (around 4 × 107 cells per dish) were used for knocking-down.b.Add 5 × 130 pmol siRNA (6.5 μL of 20 μM siRNA per 100-mm dish) into 15 mL centrifuge tube containing 5 × 1 mL Opti-MEM Medium.c.Add 5 × 32.5 μL RNAiMAX (the ratio of siRNA and RNAiMAX is 1 pmol : 0.25 μL) into another 15 mL centrifuge tube containing 5 × 1 mL Opti-MEM Medium.d.Add siRNA mixture into RNAiMAX mixture at a 1:1 ratio (v/v).e.For each cell line, aliquot 5 × 2 mL siRNA-RNAiMAX mixture into 5 × 100-mm dishes.f.Incubate the siRNA-RNAiMAX mixture at 25°C for 15 min; process cell preparation during that time:i.Discard supernatant medium from 100-mm dishes containing 4 × 107 cells each and wash cells twice by pre-warmed 1 × PBS.ii.Use trypsin-EDTA (0.25%) to detach cells.iii.When ≥ 90% of the cells have detached, tilt the dishes and add pre-warmed DMEM complete medium at a 2.5:1 ratio to trypsin-EDTA.iv.Disperse the medium and collect cells suspension into 50 mL centrifuge tubes and centrifuge at 25°C, 500 g for 5 min.v.Discard supernatant medium and resuspend the cells with DMEM complete medium, add the cell suspension into 5 × 100-mm dishes containing 2 mL siRNA-RNAiMAX mixture each.vi.Incubate the cells at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 24 h. Expected outcomes

Different identification outcomes could result from the different ways that the MS/MS database is constructed. Here we constructed two databases based on poly-Ala and poly-Thr tract respectively. Through searching the customized poly-Ala-focused database, we identified 165, 468, and 91 putative peptides in ‘p’, ‘m’, and ‘c’ samples obtained from HEK293T_ WT cells, compared to 55, 72, and 14 from HEK293T NEMF_KO cells. For the poly-Thr-focused database, 5, 29, and 4 putative peptides in ‘p’, ‘m’, and ‘c’ samples from HEK293T_ WT cells, and 5, 26, and 4 in HEK293T NEMF_KO cells were identified. The analysis of data could also be performed according to experimental purposes. We are interested in the percentage of C-end poly-Ala tails, which may serve as a potential degron to promote NCs for degradation. The comparison of identified peptides with poly-Ala tract at N-terminus, C-terminus or no poly-Ala tract of CESs was shown in Figure 5A. Since the quantity of alanine residues is essential for enzyme recognition, the analysis regarding the number of consecutive alanine residues was performed. Figure 5C showed the CESs from the HEK293T_WT cell ‘m’ samples consisted of a higher concentration of 5–9 consecutive Ala residues. We also identified the incorporation of other amino acids; the details were shown in Table S2 in Lv et al.1

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantitative analysis of different C-terminal tails of mammalian nonstop proteins was conducted using column tables. These tables have one grouping variable, with each group defined by a column. The analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software (Dotmatics, USA).

Limitations

This protocol may be potentially affected by several limitations. First, although we constructed a substantial amount of putative CESs, the principles that were utilized for database construction could not account for all possible CES formats. Second, we identified discrepancies between the CESs in some ‘p,’ ‘m,’ and ‘c’ slices from the same sample, which may be attributed to the pyramid varieties of tail formats and sample concentrations. Thus, data obtained using mass spectrometry may need further validation via other approaches, such as tRNA-binding experiments and loss-of-function of specific aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases to confirm corresponding amino acids incorporation in the CESs.8 Besides, other methods may be used to identify the assignments of CESs. Edman sequencing is a database-independent, phenylisothiocyanate-mediated, and routine automated method, that has been developed to determine amino-acid sequences in peptides.15,16 Although the accuracy of Edman sequencing is higher than that of mass spectrometry, the amount and quality of protein samples required are far higher than those required for mass spectrometry. In this case, for the lack of relatively pure protein and the big variations in length between tails in the detection of CESs, updated technologies aimed at eliminating current limitations are felt to be warranted.
