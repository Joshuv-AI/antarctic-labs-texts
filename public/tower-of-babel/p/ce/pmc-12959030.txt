Querying Q fever: spatiotemporal patterns and environmental drivers in French Guiana
Authors: Anissa Desmoulin, Amanda Esparon, Fabrice Quet, Claire Teillet, Pauline Thill, Mathieu Nacher, Emmanuel Roux, Thibault Catry, Loïc Epelboin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundQ fever is a zoonotic infection caused by the bacterium Coxiella burnetii. French Guiana, largely covered by the Amazon rainforest, is considered a hyper-endemic region. While ruminants are the primary reservoirs worldwide, the reservoir in French Guiana remains debated, likely relying on wild fauna. This study aimed to identify spatiotemporal clusters of human Q fever in the Cayenne area and investigate their relationships with environmental factors using remote sensing data.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted on acute Q fever human cases from January 2007 to December 2017. Cases were aggregated into regular grids, and explanatory variables derived from remote sensing data or local sources. Clusters were identified using spatial autocorrelation and spatiotemporal scanning. A generalized Poisson additive model was applied for explanatory modelling.FindingsA total of 513 cases of acute Q fever were aggregated within 1205 analysis units. Spatial and spatiotemporal analyses identified six clusters, all classified as hotspots. An epicentre was detected at the base of ‘Mont Rémire’ in the municipality of ‘Rémire-Montjoly’. Several risk factors were associated with the occurrence of acute Q fever cases: proximity to forests (edf: 4.05), wild live mammals watching (edf: 1.00), slaughterhouse (edf: 6.11), density of potentially unfit housing (edf: 6.53) and spatial distribution (edf: 2.00).InterpretationThis study identifies priority areas where public health actions and research efforts should be focused, including slaughterhouses, farms and the surrounding wildlife.Keywords: Public Health; Epidemiology; Infections, diseases, disorders, injuries; Environmental health; Cross-sectional survey

Introduction

Q fever is a global zoonotic disease caused by Coxiella burnetii, an intracellular bacterium affecting humans, livestock and wildlife.1 Its burden lies in both health and economic impacts, including abortions in animals and chronic forms in humans.2

In French Guiana, a French territory in the Amazon region, Q fever is ‘hyperendemic’ and exhibits unusual features.3 The incidence there is among the highest globally, reaching 25–40 cases per 100 000 annually—likely an underestimation due to uneven healthcare access, limited diagnostic testing in remote areas (reachable by river or air), the presence of pauci-symptomatic cases and the non-specific nature of clinical presentation.4 A distinctive trait is the predominant pulmonary form, linked to the MST17 genotype found only in French Guiana, which may be more virulent than reference strains.5 Unlike elsewhere, where domestic ruminants are recognised as key reservoirs, the origin of the disease here remains unclear.1 4 6 7 Hence, C. burnetii has been detected in diverse wildlife species including sloths (Bradypus tridactylus), capybaras (Hydrochoerus hydrochaeris), ticks, bats and small mammals, therefore suggesting a sylvatic cycle.8,11 However, recent seroprevalence studies suggest that sheep and goat farms may also be likely sources.4 12 These apparent discrepancies highlight that the epidemiology of Q fever in French Guiana remains incompletely understood.

Furthermore, the peculiar spatial distribution of the clinical cases, most of them being reported along the coastlines of Cayenne, the main city in French Guiana, and surroundings (Rémire-Montjoly and Matoury municipalities) while very few cases are reported in the rest of the territory, is still an enigmatic piece of the puzzle.3 4 13 However, the seroprevalence study did find seropositive persons for C. burnetii in the other parts of the territory, ranging from 5%–10% in the towns of Saint-Laurent du Maroni, Apatou, Saint-Élie, Saül, Camopi, Sinnamary and Roura, and 15%–20% in the Cacao region, particularly among populations born in Haiti (adjusted incidence relative risk 1.91 (1.09–3.36)).4 There again, our understanding of the epidemiology of Q fever in French Guiana seems incomplete.

Aerosolisation of contaminated particles, carried by wind over distances up to 10 km², is the main transmission route of C. burnetii—making spatial and temporal patterns valuable clues to Q fever dynamics.12 14 15 In French Guiana, other environmental factors, including soil humidity and vegetation index, also appear to have an influence on the disease risk.4 16 The seasonal nature of the disease is consistent with these observations—cases occurring several months after the rainy season.17

Beyond the above factors, the potential interplay between animal reservoirs and the environment can further complicate the understanding of the emergence of outbreaks.12 In fact, some studies suggested a relationship between the density of competent wildlife, the rainy season and the incidence of cases.18 Although still underused in epidemiological studies, spatial data hold significant potential for enhancing public health by enabling (1) the characterisation of environmental determinants and their dynamics, (2) the identification of clusters, and (3) the study of the relationship between clusters and the particular features of their environmental context.19 20 Q fever in French Guiana is still incompletely understood. The persistent areas of uncertainty (geographical distribution and reservoir identification) highlight the crucial need for an integrated One Health approach to understand the disease’s complex dynamics.

We hypothesise that identifying statistical regularities or spatiotemporal patterns could help improve understanding of transmission dynamics and enhance predictive capacity. This study thus aimed to retrospectively explore the spatiotemporal clustering of acute Q fever in Cayenne area from 2007 to 2017 as well as its biogeographical and environmental factors, using remote sensing data.

Material and methods

Study area

French Guiana located between Suriname and Brazil and covered by 95% of Amazon rainforest, covers approximately 83,534 km² and has a population of about 300,000 inhabitants. Ninety percent of the population lives on the coastal strip, where the main healthcare facilities are located. The healthcare system is organised around three hospitals and 17 remote Centers for Prevention and Care (CDPS) and proximity hospitals, including three newly established community hospitals, affiliated to the Cayenne Hospital. Private healthcare services remain limited across the region. The three hospitals in the territory are located in the three main cities: Cayenne, Kourou and Saint-Laurent du Maroni. All electronic medical records of patients and the results of biological examinations have been available since 2007. The study area, hereafter referred to as Cayenne area, encompasses the municipalities of Cayenne, Rémire-Montjoly and Matoury (figure 1).

Number of cases of acute Q fever per 300 m grid cell in the Cayenne area from 2007 to 2017.EPSG: European Petroleum Survey Group.

Case screening process and case definition

This work was a retrospective observational study based on data from an existing database of acute and chronic Q fever cases in French Guiana from 1 January 2007 to 31 December 2017.21 Acute Q fever cases were identified from the four laboratories conducting diagnostic tests of Q fever in France until 2017: the Institut Pasteur in French Guiana, the French National Reference Center for Q fever in Marseille, and two private laboratories, Cerba SA and Eurofins Biomnis in Paris. Diagnostic tests were then based on serology results, and the PCR was not routinely performed. The case selection process is fully detailed in the publication by Thill et al.21 21 Only cases of acute Q fever residing in Cayenne area between 1 January 2007 and 31 December 2017 were included in this study.

Geospatial data sources and management

The datasets detailed below were extracted from open access data or on request from national databases or non-profit organisations. These data were managed and processed in QGIS free and open source software, version 3.36.2, to generate explanatory variables necessary for statistical analyses.

Geocoding of acute Q fever cases

The locations of acute Q fever cases were obtained from medical records. The addresses were first converted into geographic coordinates (longitude and latitude).22 A verification phase was conducted in the field to ensure the accuracy and precision of the geocoding for unclear addresses. These latest were addresses for which the street number was not provided, in which case the street and district were used. The geocoding was performed using Python software (GeoPy package) and two geocoders: Nominatim OpenStreetMap from the WHO and the French National Address Database (BAN).23 24 When neither source provided relevant results, manual geocoding was performed using MapCarta and Google Maps.

Population and housing

The data provided by the Global Human Settlement Layer (GHSL, 2010) allowed for the extraction of 5 years population density data at 1 km resolution.25

Data from the study conducted by the ‘Agence d'Urbanisme et de Développement de la Guyane’ (AUDeG), the ‘Direction Générale des Territoires et de la Mer’ (DGTM) and the ‘Plan Intercommunal de Lutte contre l'Habitat Indigne’ (PILHI) from the ‘Communauté d'Agglomération du Centre Littoral de Guyane’ (CACL) in 2012 and 2017 were used to identify potentially unfit housing.26

Environmental data

Multiple environmental variables were included, selected based on available data across the study area and on known factors reported in the literature that could influence the transmission or dispersal of C. burnetii (online supplemental table S1).

Data on monthly average of daily rainfall and temperatures 2 months before the Q fever case occurred (maximum incubation period reported) were obtained from the Climate station of Cayenne Matoury.27

Remarkable forest (defined as evergreen rainforests on ferrallitic soils of hills, plateaus and the coastal plain) and agricultural areas were determined using data provided by the National Forestry Organization (ONF) for the year 2015 and satellite data from the European Space Agency (ESA) Sentinel 2A images (10 m resolution) in 2017. The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI) and Brightness Index (BI) were calculated using data provided by the National Institutional Device for Mutual Access to Satellite Imagery (DINAMIS) using SPOT-5 (2.5 m resolution) and SPOT-7 (1.5 m resolution) satellite images for the years 2007, 2008, 2011 and 2015 and ESA Sentinel 2A satellite images (10 m resolution) for the year 2017.

The ground elevation was determined using data provided by The Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (STRM) in 2017 spearheaded by the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency (NGA) and the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA).

Information on the road network distribution and coastline on Cayenne region was determined using the BD-TOPO database provided by the National Institute of Geographic and Forestry Information (IGN) for the year 2015.

Wild and domesticated wildlife data

A census of live terrestrial mammal watching and mammalian roadkills for the period 1 January 2007 to 31 December 2017 was obtained using the database of the Faune-Guyane participatory science project, managed by the Study and Protection Group of Birds in French Guiana (GEPOG, 2024, Faune-Guyane, www.faune-guyane.fr). These are declarative data, transmitted by volunteer naturalists, scientists and animal watchers through a digital interface.

The distribution of 43 specific and industrial livestock farms (goats, swine, cattle, ovine and poultry) was provided by the DGTM as well as the Directorate of Environment, Agriculture, Food and Forestry (DAAF) of French Guiana for the years 2014 and 2016. The only slaughterhouse in Cayenne was geo-referenced based on its address.

Statistical analyses

Spatial data partitioning

The Cayenne area was partitioned into regular grids of 100 m, 300 m, 500 m, 700 m and 1 km. All variables of interest were resampled to these spatial resolutions. Autocorrelation and cluster identification analyses were carried out at all mesh scales. For the regression analyses, only the 300 m × 300 m grid was retained based on the analysis of variance and field relevance.

Acute Q fever cluster identification

The Potthoff and Whittinghill spatial dispersion test was conducted by comparing the incidence rates with expected regional incidence rates and determining if incidence rates are spatially uniformly or non-uniformly distributed.28 Once the non-uniform distribution was established, two methods were applied to identify clusters in the study area: the global and local spatial autocorrelation index (Moran’s Index) and the spatial sweep method.

Moran’s Index, a global spatial autocorrelation index, was calculated considering a queen-type neighbourhood for each spatial unit, denoted as Imoran. Disaggregation of the Imoran was carried out in the event of a statistically significant value (threshold set at 0.05) of Imoran in order to identify hot spot (High-High), cold spot (Low-Low) or outlier (High-Low and Low-High) location using the local indicators of spatial association (LISA), local Imoran.29 A p value was calculated using 999 Monte Carlo permutations. A more restrictive threshold of significance of 0.01 was used for the LISA to take account of the increased risk of error due to multiple tests. Analyses were performed using RStudio software 4.3.3, spdep and sf packages.30

Spatiotemporal clusters were identified using spatial sweep method (SaTScan v10.2.2 software).31 We defined a cluster as two or more cases and defined the maximum spatial cluster size so that it includes 25.0% of the population at risk. The time aggregation was 2 months, based on the maximum incubation period reported. The geographical unit of analysis was the centroid of each spatial unit. Space-time analysis was conducted using an elliptic scanning window with a strong non-compactness penalty, and a retrospective space-time discrete Poisson probability model was applied to estimate relative risk. Both high and low rates were scanned, and a standard Monte Carlo test with 999 permutations was performed to generate a p value.

Determinants of acute Q fever: variables and statistical models

The explanatory variables tested as determinants of acute Q fever cases at the 300 m mesh resolution were altitude of residence, mean monthly temperature and precipitation, mean NDVI, BI and NDWI values, number and density of vegetation patches, density of patch edges, road length, distance to nearest sighted live mammals, distance to nearest dead mammals, distance to nearest various types of livestock farms, distance to nearest slaughterhouses, roads, the coast, forests and agricultural areas (online supplemental table S2).

Two models were applied to explore factors associated with case occurrence and cluster membership. First, a Poisson regression model was used to investigate factors associated with the number of cases, adjusting for the population at risk. Variables were categorised either based on objective distribution criteria (quartiles) or according to data from the literature. Second, Poisson generalised additive models (GAMs) were applied to explore the spatial contribution of the data through the coordinates of the centroids (X, Y) and the potential non-linear relationships of the variables, using the mgcv package.32 Principal component analysis was used to assess collinearity among continuous variables. In both models, a univariate analysis was conducted, and variables with a p value ≤0.25 were selected for inclusion in the multivariate model. All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio software 4.3.3. All tests were two-tailed and a p value below 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in this study.

Results

A total of 513 cases of acute Q fever were included in the study (online supplemental figure S1). After aggregation of the data by regular grids, the total number of spatial units (SU) per mesh was 395 (over 6753 cells in total) for the 100 m resolution, 282 SU (over 1205) for the 300 m resolution, 162 (over 390) for the 500 m resolution, 142 (over 285) for the 700 m resolution, and 92 (over 168) for the 1 km resolution. Over our study period, the incidence rate of acute Q fever in Cayenne area was estimated at 42.5 cases/100 000 inhabitants per year, 95% CI 38.9 to 46.1 (online supplemental figure S2). The distribution of the number of cases at a resolution of 300 m in the Cayenne region is shown in figure 1, and that of incidence rates by SU in online supplemental figure S3.

Acute Q fever cluster identification

The Potthoff-Whittinghill test revealed heterogeneity in the spatial distribution of cases over the period 2007 to 2017 based on 300 m mesh (p value <0.01). The Moran’s Index showed a positive overall autocorrelation of incident cases over the study period (p value=0.031). Local analyses identified 3.48% of spatial units as statistically significant at the 1% threshold (42/1205 SU) (figure 2), all showing positive autocorrelation. Seven units were classified as ‘High-High’ and 35 as ‘Low-Low.’

Identification of spatiotemporal epicentres by applying an epicentre score to the 100 m grid. We have created a new variable called epicentre index, which for each spatial unit (SU), and in each layer (100 m, 300 m and 500 m), takes the value 1 whenever the spatial unit is included in a spatiotemporal cluster, and the value 0 otherwise. The sum of these epicentre indexes gives us an epicentre score in the layer with the highest resolution (100 m). The maximum value of this epicentre score is therefore the sum of clusters detected for the three layers of interest (14). So the higher a spatial unit’s score, the more spatial clusters it is involved in. This epicentre score for each spatial unit reflects both the permanence of a signal and the possible dilution processes as the layer scale increases. Drivers of Q fever

The spatial distribution of all variables of interest is shown in online supplemental figure S9. Positive and statistically significant spatial autocorrelation (Imoran) was observed for most variables, except for case sex, age, birthplace in France or Europe, temperature, rainfall 2 months before case occurrence and reported live mammals. The distribution parameters of the variables of interest are presented in online supplemental table S2, and their linear correlations in online supplemental figure S10.

Univariate and multivariate analyses of the risks associated with the incidence of acute Q fever in Cayenne and the surrounding area are presented in online supplemental table S5.

In the multivariate analysis, the incidence of acute Q fever increased with proximity to live mammal sights: adjusted OR (aOR) 1.55 (0.96–2.52) for distances between 500 m and 1 km, and aOR 1.81 (1.12–2.93) for distances less than 500 m compared with those more than 1 km away (p=0.029). However, neither the number of live wild mammal sightings in the SU nor proximity to roadkill was significantly statistically associated with Q fever incidence. No proximity to any type of farm increased incidence, and proximity to sheep farms was linked to a lower incidence (p<0.001). Proximity to the slaughterhouse increased incidence: aOR 1.24 (0.93–1.66) for distances of 5–10 km, and aOR 3.18 (2.28–4.45) for distances less than 5 km (p<0.001). Distance from the nearest forest was also associated with case occurrence: aOR 1.11 (0.89–1.38) for distances of 50–500 m, and aOR 2.25 (1.67–3.05) for distances less than 50 m, compared with distances greater than 500 m (p<0.001). There was a significant association between patch density and incidence: aOR 1.12 (0.92–1.36) for 50–100 patches per SU, and aOR 1.65 (1.16–2.35) for more than 100 patches per SU, compared with fewer than 50 patches (p=0.027). Distance to the coast was negatively associated with case occurrence (p=0.005). No other variable was significantly associated with case occurrence in the multivariate analysis. In GAM analysis, distance to sheep farms (edf: 4.05), proximity to forests (edf: 4.05), wild live mammals watching (edf: 1.00), slaughterhouse (edf: 6.11), density of potentially unfit housing (edf: 6.53) and spatial distribution (edf: 2.00) are associated with the occurrence of cases (figure 5).

Drivers of belonging to clusters

The second multivariate regression analysis revealed that cluster membership was negatively associated with population density: aOR 0.64 (0.49–0.83) for population density >90 inhabitants/km² compared with those with less than five inhabitants/km², online supplemental table S7. Additionally, proximity to reported live mammal sights within 1 km (p=0.019) and to the slaughterhouse (p<0.001) within 10 km were positively associated with cluster membership. Distance to poultry farms showed a strong association: aOR 7.17 (5.26–9.78) for distances between 2 and 5 km, and aOR 6.83 (4.76–9.80) for distances less than 2 km. Proximity to the nearest forest and the coast also appeared to be linked to cluster membership, p<0.001. In the GAM analysis, only population density (edf: 1.001), distance to poultry farms (edf: 5.337), distance to the forest (edf: 1.830) and distance to the slaughterhouse (edf: 6.887) were statistically significant at the threshold of 5% (online supplemental figure S12).

Discussion

Here, we found that Q fever in French Guiana presented in spatiotemporal clusters near patches of forest but that proximity to sights of wild mammals and slaughterhouses was associated with an increased risk of disease. It thus reconciles the conflicting hypotheses of a wild versus a domestic source of infection—both seem supported by the data.

While less sensitive, methods relying exclusively on spatial data, such as Moran’s Index and LISA, have the benefit of delivering quick results as soon as case geolocation is available. This allows for prompt prioritisation of interventions, especially in the context of emerging threats.33

Temporal data, on the other hand, provide insight into the dynamic evolution of clusters. The absence of detected clusters before 2010 likely reflects underdiagnosis (already mentioned in the first article (Thill et al.)) rather than a true lack of cases during that period.21 Although not classified as a neglected tropical disease, Q fever remained relatively unknown to healthcare professionals at the time.34 35 This, combined with disparities in healthcare access, cultural perceptions of the disease and its often mild and non-specific presentation, likely contributed to its underdiagnosis.113 36,38 The identification of high-incidence areas in the Cayenne region as early as the late 1990s could be interpreted in light of these hypotheses, supporting the idea of C. burnetii circulation over time.19 Although definitive conclusions cannot be drawn, cases appear to be more concentrated in the municipality of ‘Remire-Montjoly’. The spatial evolution of these clusters in subsequent years could not be studied, as Q fever is not a mandatorily reported disease, and cluster detection requires a labour-intensive data collection and treatment process. Implementing passive, georeferenced surveillance of cases could provide a solution to systematise this approach and enhance our understanding of the infection’s dynamics.

Cluster 3 (‘Camp du Tigre’) was identified through both spatial and spatiotemporal analyses, consistent with previous findings among military families, confirming the robustness of our methods.9 Among the six clusters, it is the most well-documented, with Q fever cases reported for nearly two decades. Other clusters—such as those near the ‘Route des Plages’ and ‘Dégrad des Cannes’—have been linked to familial cases reported by local health authorities, though their sources remain unknown.39 In contrast, the clusters at the foot of ‘Caïa, Cabassou’ and ‘Paramana Hills’ appear to have gone unnoticed, despite being areas that have reported cases since the early 2000s.19 The absence of mandatory notification for Q fever is one of the main explanations for why these phenomena may have been overlooked. Capturing the full epidemic process remains challenging, but analysis of epidemic curves suggests a common, potentially persistent point source across clusters. This profile aligns with prolonged bacterial shedding by animals or environmental persistence, both of which could sustain human transmission over time. Moreover, cluster dynamics did not clearly follow the expected seasonal pattern, although most cases occurred during months with the lowest rainfall.18

While the human epidemiology of Q fever in French Guiana is increasingly well characterised, the animal reservoir remains uncertain. Ruminants were initially considered to play a minor role, but recent studies challenge this view.8 40 41 One reported 66.3% seropositivity among sampled livestock, with cattle herds showing up to 14.2% prevalence.7 Bailly et al. also suggested an association between sheep farm proximity and human seroprevalence, though without a clear dose–response effect.4 At the scale of the Cayenne area, our data indicate a complex, non-linear relationship with proximity to sheep farms, whereas no association was observed for cattle or goat farms. However, proximity to poultry farms was strongly associated with membership in various clusters. The potential role of bird species and/or poultry farming in this epidemiology remains uncertain and has yet to be demonstrated in French Guiana. Gardon et al. highlighted the possible exposure of swallow species (Progne chalybea and Progne tapera) to C. burnetii (one positive species out of 69 tested).8 Moreover, the grey-breasted martin (P. chalybea) was considered as a possible protagonist during the various outbreaks at the ‘Rémire-Montjoly’ prison between 2010 and 2021.17 It is worth noting that the period of these described outbreaks significantly overlapped with our study period. However, only the years 2019, 2020 and 2021 reported at least two cases, which met the minimum requirement to define a cluster in our study.42 43

Citizen-reported wildlife observations, while subject to reporting bias, remain a valuable proxy for animal presence and contribute meaningfully to territorial knowledge.44 In this study, the recording of wild mammals, whether alive or as roadkill, can lead to overestimation or underestimation of local wild animal densities due to inherent high observation and reporting biases of the data collection method. However, it still serves as a valuable indicator of their presence. The involvement of wildlife in the epidemiology of Q fever has been supported by various studies, without any particular species standing out: rodents, marsupials, three-toed sloths, capybaras, wild suids and birds.48,11 37 A recent study, in process of publication, involving 2014 samples from wild animals identified 34 positive cases, including new potential players: 27 mammal species distributed across 7 orders (Epelboin et al.).45 It is noteworthy that the epicentre of the clusters identified in the Cayenne area is located at the foot of the ‘Remire Hill’, an area known to host many of these species. Examining the spatial distribution of these positive species may provide useful insights to substantiate this hypothesis.

The potential role of the slaughterhouse in the epidemiology of Q fever in French Guiana remains to be explored and represents a novel finding; it could reflect either a reservoir within livestock or, as there is only one slaughterhouse, its location could be a confounding factor, being by chance close to a large number of cases.18 20 Studying the seroprevalence among slaughterhouse workers, along with local sampling, may help clarify these uncertainties. It is noteworthy that the two first human cases described in French Guiana were both butchers who had worked in the slaughterhouse located in Cayenne area.46

Although it was not initially tested, it is intriguing to note that the Cayenne landfill, where garbage is disposed of, is located less than 5 km from clusters No. 1, No. 3 and No. 6. Similar to slaughterhouses, the accumulation of waste in this area could be particularly attractive to potential reservoirs, such as rodent species. The resuspension of particles contaminated by these reservoirs during the near-daily turning of waste could be an interesting avenue for further investigation.

Our findings underscore the complex influence of coastal proximity. Ignoring the temporal dimension, it appears to exert little influence on case distribution. Yet, spatiotemporal patterns reveal clusters consistently near the coast, particularly in areas exposed to high wind levels, suggesting that wind may facilitate the rapid dispersal of particles driving these clustered cases. Although our data could not account for wind patterns at the required spatial resolution, the Cayenne area is shaped by trade winds, predominantly from the northeast during the rainy season and from the southeast during the dry season.47 Since acute Q fever cases mainly occur during the dry season, and clusters are concentrated along the coast in areas experiencing winds of 50–200 km/hour, it is plausible that wind contributes to the local epidemiology, warranting further investigation.8 12 17

The choice of analysis scale remains a crucial step in interpreting the results, as varying scales can result in varying degrees of information loss. Unlike the administrative breakdown provided by the grouped plots for statistical information—named IRIS and based on population density—the use of regular grids enables a multiscale segmentation, providing an objective framework for analysis across varying levels of resolution. Identifying the optimal analysis scale remains complex and lacks consensus, but it seems essential to consider both local terrain specificities and objective, discriminating criteria.48 Spatiotemporal clusters were identified at all mesh scales, but the regression analysis was conducted only at the 300 m × 300 m scale, which provided the best variance of incidence between spatial units, the highest capability for detecting known real clusters, and most accurately reflected the epicentre phenomenon while maintaining clinical relevance for the analysis of environmental factors.

Furthermore, our approach had some limitations. This study used the address of the cases’ residences, operating under the strong assumption that the site of contamination is likely to be nearby home rather than a risk-related activity. Aside from the availability of data, our results are nonetheless consistent with findings from recent seroprevalence studies in humans, indicating high exposure levels among populations living in the municipalities of ‘Rémire-Montjoly’ and ‘Matoury’.4 Furthermore, the role of informal settlements in French Guiana—slums—is a crucial factor to consider, as they can serve as hotspots for emerging infectious diseases.49 They are often overlooked in field studies, and using population modelling methods based on remote sensing data that rely on built environment information offers an interesting way to address this bias, especially considering that they accounted for 11% of the built environment in the Cayenne region in 2015.50 51

Moreover, the explanatory variables used in this study exhibited a temporal misalignment with the occurrence of acute Q fever cases. However, these were the only data available for the study period, and their collection was conducted as exhaustively as possible. Despite this limitation, the data provide a valuable basis for formulating potential hypotheses and exploring future research directions.

Another limitation is that the approach adopted in this study struggled to fully capture the space as a support for social and cultural practices related to the environment, as well as for the movement of populations or species.52 Spatialising qualitative and interaction data would provide the most comprehensive model. Nevertheless, the negative associations observed with both population density and substandard housing support the well-established absence of meaningful human-to-human transmission in Q fever epidemiology. Furthermore, and in contrast to what is observed for other infectious diseases such as cholera or leptospirosis, housing sanitation conditions do not appear to play a significant role in the emergence of Q fever.53 In French Guiana, the spread of C. burnetii appears to be driven more by conditions and activities that enable population interactions with high-risk environments, creating specific ecosystems that support transmission.

Furthermore, the study by Bailly et al. revealed that individuals born in Haiti were more frequently exposed, whereas acute Q fever cases predominantly affected individuals born in Europe.4 21 This disparity, coupled with the high exposure levels detected in the Cacao region (located south of Cayenne area)—despite the absence of acute cases notified—raises critical considerations about potential differences in host susceptibility, the presence of diverse circulating strains, or varying patterns of exposure.5 54 55

Conclusion

This study, within the One Health framework, provides new insights into the persistent uncertainties surrounding the epidemiology of Q fever in French Guiana. By identifying clusters with spatial precision of just hundreds of metres in the Cayenne area, this study has highlighted specific locations that require focused attention for prevention and further research. The valuable role of remote sensing data in advancing human health, driving not only surveillance efforts but also the development of explanatory and predictive models, is underscored by this work. The present work reconciles conflicting hypotheses about the wild mammal versus farm reservoir of Q fever—both seem to be plausibly involved.

Furthermore, prioritising serological surveys of high-risk groups—such as slaughterhouse workers, farmers and veterinarians—along with targeted PCR testing in slaughterhouses, farms, veterinary clinics and among diverse bird species, as well as exploring the role of winds, should be key avenues for future investigations. Taken together, these results, highlighting the under-recognition of Q fever epidemic dynamics, provide a strong rationale for advocating mandatory notification of Q fever, if not at the national level, at least in French Guiana.
