Therapeutic potential of mesenchymal stem cell-derived apoptotic vesicles in liver fibrosis: targeting the IGFBP3/PI3K-AKT axis via miR-409-3p
Authors: Bin Lu, Xingzhi Chen, Hui Wang, Mengyue Li, Yuhui Shi, Yongheng Huang, Jie Ding, Yi Wang, Ling Li, Jing Luo, Changchang Jia, Nan Lin, Yuan Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatic fibrosis is a progressive pathology driven by hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation and excessive extracellular matrix deposition. While mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) therapies show promise, their clinical translation is hindered by the inherent safety constraints of live cell transplantation. MSC-derived apoptotic vesicles (MSC-ApoVs), a structurally distinct class of extracellular vesicles, emerge as a compelling cell-free alternative. However, their specific therapeutic efficacy and mechanistic targets in hepatic fibrosis remain elusive.MethodsMSC-ApoVs were isolated from human umbilical cord MSCs and stringently characterized to exclude non-vesicular contaminants. Their anti-fibrotic efficacy was evaluated in vitro using TGF-β1-activated LX-2 cells and in vivo within a CCl₄-induced murine fibrosis model. To elucidate and functionally validate the underlying mechanisms, we integrated transcriptomic profiling (RNA-seq), dual-luciferase reporter assays, and dual-directional pharmacological interventions. Furthermore, human clinical liver biopsies were analyzed to determine the clinico-pathological relevance of the identified targets.ResultsWe demonstrated that MSC-ApoVs were efficiently internalized by HSCs, significantly blunting TGF-β1-induced activation, proliferation, and migration, alongside a marked reduction in fibrotic markers (α-SMA, MMP2, and Collagen-I). Transcriptomic screening identified Insulin-like Growth Factor Binding Protein 3 (IGFBP3) as a crucial pro-fibrotic mediator. Mechanistically, MSC-ApoV-enriched miR-409-3p directly targeted and downregulated IGFBP3, thereby restricting the PI3K-AKT signaling cascade—a dependency unequivocally validated by targeted rescue and blockade assays. In vivo, systemic MSC-ApoV administration robustly ameliorated CCl₄-induced hepatic fibrosis and restored liver function. Crucially, clinical analyses revealed a strong positive correlation between elevated IGFBP3 expression and the severity of human fibrotic progression.ConclusionsThis study delineates a novel cell-free therapeutic paradigm, demonstrating that MSC-ApoVs mitigate hepatic fibrosis largely through the miR-409-3p-mediated silencing of the IGFBP3/PI3K-AKT axis. By integrating robust in vitro, in vivo, and clinical evidence, these findings highlight MSC-ApoVs as a highly efficacious and translatable alternative to traditional live stem cell therapies for hepatic fibrosis.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13287-026-05143-w.

Introduction

Hepatic fibrosis (HF) is driven singly or in combination by multiple etiologies, including viral hepatitis (e.g., hepatitis B and C virus infection), non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), excessive alcohol consumption leading to alcoholic liver disease (ALD), and metabolic-associated disorders (e.g., diabetes, obesity-related metabolic abnormalities) [1]. It represents a progressive pathological response of the liver to chronic injury, primarily characterized by hepatocyte apoptosis and necrosis, along with the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs). A hallmark of this process is the imbalance between synthesis and degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM) [1, 2]. With the persistent activation of HSCs, they transform into proliferative and migratory myofibroblasts, which excessively deposit ECM components [3, 4]. This pathological process is progressive; without effective long-term intervention, it can ultimately advance to cirrhosis, severe liver failure, and in some cases, further develop into hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [5]. Globally, chronic liver diseases affect 840 million people, causing 2 million annual deaths, making hepatic fibrosis a significant medical burden [6].Previous research targeting HSCs to mitigate hepatic fibrosis has largely focused on modulating inflammatory factor expression and other related biochemical mechanisms [7–10], with some attention on biomechanics [11, 12]. Although some progress has been made, therapeutic efficacy remains uncertain. Consequently, effective anti-fibrotic therapies for hepatic fibrosis represent a major unmet clinical need.

In recent years, mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-based therapy, owing to its potent immunomodulatory and tissue repair capabilities, has been considered a promising strategy for treating hepatic fibrosis [13–16]. However, therapies based on live cells face significant challenges, such as low cell survival rates, potential tumorigenic risks, immune rejection, and difficulties in standardized production [17, 18]. These limitations have shifted the research focus towards the paracrine effects of MSCs, suggesting that MSCs exert their therapeutic actions primarily through the release of bioactive molecules, particularly extracellular vesicles (EVs) [19, 20]. EVs derived from various MSC sources, such as adipose tissue, offer advantages including lower immunogenicity, higher safety profile, and greater potential for engineering, representing a new direction for next-generation “cell-free” therapies [21].Among various types of EVs, apoptotic vesicles (ApoVs) are a class of double-membrane vesicles generated during programmed cell death [22]. For a long time, ApoVs were regarded as inert “corpses” after apoptosis. However, recent groundbreaking studies have revealed that ApoVs are not merely cellular debris but are enriched with specific bioactive cargo (e.g., proteins, nucleic acids) and play an active, indispensable signaling role in intercellular communication, immunomodulation, and tissue regeneration [23]. Unlike exosomes or microvesicles actively secreted by live cells, the biogenesis and cargo packaging mechanisms of ApoVs are unique, implying distinct biological functions and therapeutic potential [24]. Although the anti-fibrotic effects of MSC-derived exosomes (MSC-Exo) have been reported [25–31], and ApoVs have demonstrated therapeutic potential in other tissue-injury contexts [32], the function, mechanisms of action, and potential advantages of MSC-derived ApoVs (MSC-ApoV), which are triggered by the critical physiological/pathological process of apoptosis, in the context of hepatic fibrosis remain entirely unexplored.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate whether MSC-ApoVs can serve as an efficient cell-free therapeutic agent for hepatic fibrosis. We provide evidence demonstrating that MSC-ApoVs alleviate hepatic fibrosis by primarily delivering miR-409-3p, which targets and downregulates IGFBP3 expression, consequently inhibiting the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. Our findings offer valuable insights and identify novel therapeutic targets and concepts for the treatment of hepatic fibrosis.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The human hepatic stellate cell line (LX2) and primary hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) used in this study was provided by the Laboratory of Hepatology Center, The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University. The cells were cultured in complete medium consisting of DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and maintained in a constant temperature incubator at 37 °C. LX2 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were induced with TGF-β1 for 24 h for subsequent experiments.

Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) used in this study were isolated using the tissue explant adherence method. Sterile umbilical cord tissues were collected from three independent healthy donors during cesarean section from normal full-term pregnancies after obtaining informed consent. To ensure the reproducibility of our findings and minimize donor-specific bias, MSCs from these three biological replicates were processed and validated independently. After washing with PBS and treatment with 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S; Invitrogen), the amniotic membrane and umbilical arteries/vein were removed. The Wharton’s jelly of the umbilical cord was cut into small pieces (3–5 mm in edge length) and placed in 10 cm culture dishes. The culture medium was changed every 3–5 days using Mesenchymal Stem Cell Medium (Yocon, NC0103.S). After 12–14 days, the adherent cells were digested and collected. The harvested MSCs were then cultured and maintained in Mesenchymal Stem Cell Medium (Yocon, NC0105.S).

Sample source

A total of 10 normal liver tissue controls (CTRL group) and 24 liver fibrosis tissues (LF group) were collected. The liver fibrosis group (LF group) consisted of patients diagnosed with hepatic fibrosis by pathological biopsy. The normal control group (CTRL group) consisted of patients pathologically confirmed to have no fibrosis, inflammation, or tumor infiltration. This study was conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines for the use of human samples and was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University.

The clinical characteristics of the patients, including etiology, fibrosis stages, and biochemical indices, are summarized in supplementary Table 1. Patients with active hepatic malignancies or significant systemic comorbidities were excluded to minimize potential confounding factors affecting IGFBP3 expression.

Cell transfection

LX2 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells/mL in the wells of 6-well plates. Cell transfection was performed using Lipofectamine™ 3000 transfection reagent (L3000-015, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) in Opti-MEM medium. The cells were transfected with si-IGFBP3, si-TMEM219, or the corresponding si-NC (40 nM, 10 pmol/mL), respectively. The oligonucleotides used in this study were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., and are listed in Table 1. At 48 h post-transfection, the cells were harvested for subsequent experiments, including quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and protein analysis.

Flow cytometry

MSCs in the logarithmic growth phase were digested, collected, and resuspended in pre-cooled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The cell density was adjusted to 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. Then, 100 µL of the cell suspension (approximately 1 × 10⁵ cells) was aliquoted into each flow cytometry tube. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, corresponding fluorescently labeled antibodies were added to the respective tubes. The markers and antibodies (or dyes) used in this experiment included: FITC-conjugated anti-human CD24, CD19, CD45, CD116, CD34 antibodies; PE-conjugated anti-human CD105, CD44, CD73, CD90 antibodies; and PE-conjugated anti-human HLA-DR antibody. The cells and antibodies were incubated at 4 °C in the dark for 30 min. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with 2 mL of PBS to remove unbound antibodies. Finally, the cells were resuspended in 300 µL of PBS and immediately analyzed using a flow cytometer. The resulting data were analyzed using FlowJo V10 data analysis software.

Isolation and Identification of ApoVs

To induce apoptosis, MSCs were washed twice with filtered PBS and cultured in basic DMEM containing 500 nM staurosporine (STS; Enzo Life Sciences) for 6 h. The culture supernatant was collected and sequentially centrifuged at 800 × g for 10 min and 2000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. The resulting supernatant was then subjected to two rounds of centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The pelleted ApoVs were finally resuspended in filtered PBS.

For characterization, ApoVs were stained with CellMask Deep Red Plasma Membrane Stain (Thermo Fisher) for 5 min at 37 °C, followed by incubation with FITC-Annexin V (BD Biosciences) in 1× binding buffer for 15 min at 4 °C. Vesicles were quantified using nano-flow cytometry (U30, China). Structural characteristics of the fixed ApoVs were observed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) following negative staining. Particle concentration and size distribution were assessed using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA; Particle Metrix, Germany). To maintain inter-batch consistency, ApoVs purity was evaluated using the particle-to-protein ratio (total particle count via NTA divided by total protein mass via BCA assay). The dosage for subsequent experiments was standardized based on protein concentration (corresponding to a particle density of approximately 8 × 10⁸ particles/µg). MSC-ApoVs were administered at 2.5 µg/mL for in vitro assays. For in vivo interventions, a fixed dose of 5 µg (suspended in 200 µL of PBS) was administered per mouse.

To generate ApoVs with modulated miR-409-3p levels, parent MSCs were transfected with miR-409-3p inhibitors, mimics, or their corresponding negative controls (GenePharma, China) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher). Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were subjected to the identical STS-induced apoptosis and ApoVs isolation protocol described above. The efficiency of miR-409-3p modulation in both parental cells and derived ApoVs was validated by qPCR (Fig. S5).

Cellular uptake of ApoVs

ApoVs were labeled using PKH26 (MINI26, Sigma). Subsequently, the labeled ApoVs were isolated by centrifugation and washed twice with filtered PBS to remove any unbound PKH26 dye. LX2 cells were then incubated with the PKH26-labeled ApoVs for 12 h. Following incubation, the cell cytoplasm was stained using Maleimide (MedChemExpress, USA), and the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a confocal microscope (LSM880, Zeiss, Germany).

MSC differentiation assay

The differentiation potential of MSCs into osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic lineages was induced using commercial kits (Cyagen Biosciences, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Osteogenic differentiation was assessed by Alizarin Red S staining, adipogenic differentiation was evaluated by Oil Red O staining, and chondrogenic differentiation was determined by Alcian Blue staining. The staining intensity was analyzed to evaluate the differentiation potential.

CCK-8 assay

For the CCK-8 assay, approximately 1 × 10⁴ cells were seeded per well in a 96-well plate. Following the incubation period, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, CCK-8 reagent (APExBIO, USA) was added to the cells to assess cell viability. The absorbance at 450 nm was then measured using a microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek, USA).

Wound healing assay

LX2 cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well in a 12-well plate and cultured until they reached full confluence. A sterile 200 µL pipette tip was used to create a straight “wound” by scratching the cell monolayer. The detached cells were removed by washing with PBS. Images of the wound gaps were captured at 0 h and 24 h using an optical microscope (Zeiss, Germany). The wound widths were measured using ImageJ software. The wound healing percentage was calculated using the following formula: Wound healing rate (%) = [1 - (Width at 24 h/Initial width)] × 100%.

TUNEL staining

Cells were fixed with fixative for 30 min and then treated with a permeabilization buffer for 5 min. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture for 1 h using a commercial TUNEL apoptosis detection kit (Beyotime, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The stained cells were analyzed using a fluorescence microscope (Axio Observer 3, Carl Zeiss Microscopy, Germany).

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and bioinformatics analysis

Total RNA was extracted from LX2 using TRIzol reagent (Sigma, USA). The integrity of the RNA was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, USA). The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina PE150 platform.For bioinformatics analysis, raw sequencing reads were first subjected to quality control and filtering using Trimmomatic. The clean reads were then aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2. Gene expression levels were quantified using featureCounts. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package in R. Genes with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 and an absolute log₂ fold change greater than 1 (|log₂(fold change)| > 1) were considered differentially expressed. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses for the differentially expressed genes were conducted using the clusterProfiler package. The P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR).

Luciferase assay

The dual-luciferase reporter plasmids containing the wild-type (IGFBP3-WT) or mutant (IGFBP3-MUT) sequences were constructed by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. HEK293T cells were seeded in 24-well plates at 40% confluence. Using Lipofectamine 2000 (52,887, Invitrogen), the cells were co-transfected with 500 ng of either the IGFBP3-WT or IGFBP3-MUT plasmid, along with 50 nM of miR-409-3p mimic or a control mimic (GenePharma, Shanghai). At 24 h post-transfection, luciferase activity was measured following the manufacturer’s protocol using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (E1910, Promega). The firefly luciferase activity was normalized to the Renilla luciferase activity for internal reference. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation from three independent experiments.

Animal experiments

The animal experiments in this study were reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Wild-type C57BL/6 mice (5–6 weeks old, male, 20–25 g) were purchased from the Animal Center of Sun Yat-Sen University. All animal studies were approved by the Animal Protection and Utilization Committee of Sun Yat-Sen University and conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. The mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at a temperature of 22± 2 °C and a relative humidity of 55 ± 10%, with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, and provided with standard food and water ad libitum. All mice were acclimatized for one week prior to experimentation. The sample size of n = 5 per group was justified a priori by a prospective power analysis based on our preliminary experimental data. Using hepatic hydroxyproline content and Ishak fibrosis scoring as primary readouts, this sample size provided a statistical power of > 80% to detect significant differences with an effect size of 1.5 at a significance level (α) of 0.05. This allocation rigorously adheres to the “3Rs” principles to minimize animal usage while maintaining sufficient statistical stringency. Extra mice were initially allocated to the CCl₄ modeling cohorts to account for anticipated model-related mortality. Pre-specified exclusion criteria dictated that any mice dying prematurely or reaching humane endpoints prior to the scheduled termination were strictly excluded from the study. To maintain a balanced experimental design and prevent selection bias, exactly five viable mice (n = 5) were randomly selected from the surviving animals in each group for all final downstream histological and molecular evaluations. Prior to the interventions, mice were randomly assigned to different experimental groups using a computer-generated random number sequence via the RAND function in Microsoft Excel. Subsequently, during the 10-week intervention period, the first batch of mice was randomly divided into three groups (n = 5 per group): (1) Sham group: received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of an equal volume of corn oil; (2) CCl₄ group: received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of a 20% CCl₄ corn oil solution at a dose of 5µL/g, twice a week for 10 weeks; (3) CCl₄ + ApoV group: received i.p. injections of the 20% CCl₄ corn oil solution (5µL/g, twice weekly for 10 weeks) plus 5 µg of MSC-ApoV administered via tail vein injection once a week for 4 weeks, specifically during weeks 7 to 10 of the modeling period. A second batch of mice was randomly divided into four groups (n = 5 per group): CCl₄ group, CCl₄ +ApoV group, CCl₄ + ApoVmiR−409−3p inhibitor group, and CCl₄ + ApoVmiR−409−3p mimic group. All groups in the second batch received i.p. injections of the 20% CCl₄ corn oil solution (5µL/g, twice weekly for 10 weeks). During weeks 7 to 10, each group received weekly tail vein injections as follows: the CCl₄ group received an equal volume of PBS; the CCl₄ + ApoV group received MSC-ApoV; the CCl₄ + ApoVmiR−409−3p inhibitor group received ApoV loaded with the miR-409-3p inhibitor; and the CCl₄ + ApoVmiR−409−3p mimic group received ApoV loaded with the miR-409-3p mimic. Three days after the final intervention, mice were deeply anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg). While under deep anesthesia, blood was collected via retro-orbital bleeding for serum biochemical analysis. Immediately following blood collection, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. The liver tissues were then promptly harvested. The harvested livers were rinsed with ice-cold PBS and divided into two parts. One part was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent western blot analysis. The remaining part was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and embedded in paraffin for subsequent hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunofluorescence experiments. All procedures were performed to minimize pain and distress, ensuring that tissue and blood collection occurred only after the animal was completely unresponsive. Throughout the experimental period, the diet, activity, fur condition, and mortality of the mice in each group were monitored and recorded. Body weight was measured weekly to track changes.

To prevent observer bias, all histological evaluations and quantitative analyses of immunohistochemistry (IHC) positive areas were performed in a strictly blinded manner. Tissue sections and digital images were coded by an independent researcher, and assessments were conducted by two researchers who were unaware of the experimental group assignments. In cases of discrepancy, a third investigator was consulted to reach a consensus.

Serum and liver tissue biochemistry

For whole blood samples, centrifugation (3,000 rpm, 15 min, 2–8 °C) was performed after the samples were kept at room temperature (RT) for 2 h. To avoid degradation caused by multiple freeze-thaw cycles, the supernatant was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C before further processing. The hydroxyproline (HYP) content in the liver homogenates was quantified using a Hydroxyproline (HYP) Content Assay Kit (AC10115-50T/48S, Acmec, China).

The results are presented as fold changes.For miRNA quantification, qRT-PCR was carried out using SYBR Green Master Mix, miRNA-specific forward primers, and a universal reverse primer. Western blot analysis

Total protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (KGB2101, Key-GEN). The Western blotting procedure was performed as described below. Briefly, protein samples were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 1 h, followed by an overnight incubation at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies (all diluted at 1:1000): anti-α-SMA (bm0002, Boster, USA), anti-MMP2 (10373-2-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-Collagen-I (14695-1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-IGFBP3 (10189-2-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-CD63 (ab59479, Abcam, UK), anti-CD9 (ab307085, Abcam, UK), anti-H3 (9715 S, CST, UK), anti-Calnexin(10427-2-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-caspase-3 (ab184787, Abcam, USA), anti-β-tubulin (2128 S, CST, UK), anti-mTOR (2983 S, CST, UK), anti-phospho-mTOR (5536 S, CST, UK), anti-PI3K (4292 S, CST, UK), anti-phospho-PI3K (4228 S, CST, UK), anti-AKT (9272 S, CST, UK), anti-phospho-AKT (13038 S, CST, UK), and anti-GAPDH (81640-5-RR, Proteintech, China).After incubation, the membranes were washed with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) and subsequently incubated with species-appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using the Signal Fire Elite ECL Reagent (12757, CST) and an imaging system (Tanon 5200CE). The band intensity was quantified and analyzed using ImageJ software.

H&E, masson, and sirius red staining

Mouse liver tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining, Masson’s trichrome staining, and Sirius Red staining were performed to evaluate tissue morphology and collagen fiber deposition, respectively. The morphological changes and collagen content were observed and analyzed using a microscope (Nikon 80i, Tochigi, Japan).

Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining

Mouse liver tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Paraffin-embedded Sect. (5 μm thick) were prepared for staining. The routine procedure included deparaffinization and tissue permeabilization using xylene, followed by dehydration and xylene removal through a graded alcohol series. The tissue specimens were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibody probes overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used included: anti-α-SMA (bm0002, Boster, USA), anti-MMP2 (10373-2-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-Collagen-I (14695-1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-CD63 (GB115694, Servicebio, China), and anti-cleaved-Caspase-3 (GB11532, Servicebio, China).After primary antibody incubation, the sections and cells were thoroughly washed and then incubated with a secondary antibody (K5007, DAKO) for 1 h at room temperature. For immunohistochemical staining, the immunoreaction was visualized using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) (G1212-200T, Servicebio, China), followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin.For immunofluorescence staining, after washing the sections and cells, they were incubated with appropriate Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. The sections and cells were washed again, and the nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were captured using a confocal microscope (LSM 880, Zeiss) and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). All data processing and statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.2) and GraphPad Prism 9.5 (GraphPad Software, USA). Statistical comparisons between two groups were conducted using the independent Student’s t-test. For multiple group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) multiple comparison test for pairwise comparisons. For variables that were not normally distributed, the Mann-Whitney U test (i.e., the Wilcoxon rank-sum test) was employed. Unless otherwise specified, Pearson correlation analysis was used to calculate correlation coefficients between different molecules. Exact p-values were reported where applicable, and a p-value of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) was considered statistically significant.

Results

Increased apoptosis and apoptotic vesicles in human liver fibrosis tissues

To elucidate the characteristics of apoptosis and apoptotic vesicles in human fibrosis livers, we collected samples of normal liver tissue (CTRL) and liver fibrosis tissue (LF). First, cell apoptosis was assessed by TUNEL and DAPI immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 1A). The results showed that almost no TUNEL-positive signals were observed in the CTRL group. In contrast, the number of TUNEL-positive apoptotic cells was significantly increased in the LF group, indicating a marked upregulation of apoptosis in cirrhotic tissues.Furthermore, apoptotic vesicles were detected by triple immunofluorescence co-staining for CD63, cleaved-Caspase-3, and DAPI (Fig. 1B). In the CTRL group, both cleaved-Caspase-3-positive apoptotic signals and CD63-positive vesicle signals remained at low levels. However, in the LF group, cleaved-Caspase-3-positive apoptotic signals were significantly enhanced, accompanied by a notable increase in CD63-positive vesicle signals. Clear co-localization signals (orange overlapping areas) were observed, suggesting the generation of apoptotic vesicles was significantly increased in fibrosis tissues.To further elucidate the functional niche of these vesicles, we evaluated the spatial relationship between ApoVs and activated HSCs across different clinical stages of fibrosis using double immunofluorescence staining for CD63 and α-SMA (Fig. S1). In normal liver tissues (CTRL), the expression of both markers was minimal. However, as the disease progressed to moderate fibrosis (Stage F2) and advanced cirrhosis (Stage F4), we observed a striking synchronous upregulation of CD63 and α-SMA. Identification of umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and purification and characterization of MSC-derived apoptotic vesicles (MSC-ApoVs)

Based on the clinical question “What role do apoptotic vesicles play in the progression of hepatic fibrosis?”, we explored this using MSC-ApoVs. We isolated and cultured MSCs from human umbilical cord tissue. MSC apoptosis was induced using Staurosporine (STS). After induction, the supernatant was collected, and cell debris was removed by low-speed centrifugation. MSC-ApoVs were then pelleted by high-speed centrifugation and resuspended in PBS to obtain the final MSC-ApoV preparation (Fig. 2A).

Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the MSCs expressed the characteristic surface markers CD44, CD73, CD90, and CD105, but showed minimal expression of CD19, CD24, CD45, and CD116 (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, the MSCs demonstrated multilineage differentiation potential, successfully differentiating into adipocytes (stained with Oil Red O), osteoblasts (positive for Alizarin Red staining), and chondrocytes (stained with Alcian Blue) (Fig. 2C). Observation under phase-contrast microscopy showed that untreated MSCs exhibited the typical spindle-shaped, fibroblast-like morphology. In contrast, after induction of apoptosis with 500 nM STS for 6 h, the cells displayed obvious apoptotic features, such as cell shrinkage and nuclear pyknosis (Fig. 2D). TUNEL staining results further confirmed significant apoptosis in MSCs at 6 h post-induction (Fig. 2E).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images demonstrated that the MSC-ApoVs were spherical vesicles with a lipid bilayer membrane structure and a diameter of approximately 100–200 nm, consistent with the typical morphology of vesicles (Fig. 2F). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) indicated that the MSC-ApoVs had an average diameter of 178.9 nm and a concentration of 1.45 × 10⁸ particles/mL (Fig. 2G). Importantly, evaluating the particle-to-protein ratio derived from these measurements confirmed the high purity of our ApoVs isolates, effectively excluding massive contamination by soluble protein aggregates. Furthermore, to rigorously distinguish ApoVs from passive necrotic debris or non-vesicular contaminants, a comprehensive marker panel was assessed. Western blot analysis confirmed that MSC-ApoVs expressed the vesicle-specific markers CD63 and CD9. Crucially, the robust enrichment of the active executioner cleaved-Caspase-3 unequivocally verified the active apoptotic origin of these vesicles. Meanwhile, the vesicles did not express the nuclear marker Histone H3 and the endoplasmic reticulum marker Calnexin (Fig. 2H), further ruling out substantial contamination by internal cellular organelles or apoptotic debris. Transcriptome sequencing identifies IGFBP3 as a key mediator in MSC-ApoV-mediated attenuation of LX2 cell fibrosis

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the anti-fibrotic effects of MSC-ApoVs, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on LX2 cells from three groups: the control group (C), the TGF-β1-treated group (T), and the TGF-β1 + MSC-ApoV co-treated group (T + A). A multi-group clustering heatmap visually displayed the distinct gene expression patterns among these groups, where red indicates high expression and blue indicates low expression, demonstrating clear separation of the transcriptomic profiles across the three conditions (Fig. 4A).Differential expression analysis revealed 565 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the C group and the T group (Fig. 4B), and 247 DEGs between the T group and the T + A group (Fig. 4C). The intersection of these two DEG sets, comprising 68 genes, was identified as the core set of mediators responsible for the anti-fibrotic effects of MSC-ApoVs (Fig. 4D).KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that the DEGs between the C and T groups were primarily enriched in classical fibrotic pathways such as the “PI3K-AKT signaling pathway,” “focal adhesion,” and “ECM-receptor interaction” (Fig. 4E). The DEGs between the T group and the T + A group were also significantly enriched in pathways like “focal adhesion” and “ECM-receptor interaction” (Fig. 4F). miR-409-3p in MSC-ApoVs directly targets IGFBP3 to suppress PI3K-AKT pathway activation

Extracellular vesicles can mediate intercellular communication via their cargo miRNAs. This study aimed to identify miRNAs within MSC-ApoVs that target IGFBP3 and validate their functional roles. miRNA sequencing revealed that MSCs contained 830 miRNAs, while MSC-ApoVs contained 454 miRNAs. Using the ENCORI database, 99 miRNAs were predicted to target IGFBP3. Taking the intersection of miRNAs present in MSCs, MSC-ApoVs, and those predicted to target IGFBP3 yielded 63 candidate miRNAs (Fig. 7A). Among the top 20 most abundant miRNAs in the MSC-ApoVs sequencing data, miR-409-3p, miR-93-5p, miR-103a-3p, miR-34a-5p, and miR-26b-5p were predicted to target IGFBP3 (Fig. 7B). qPCR validation showed that the expression level of miR-409-3p was significantly higher in MSC-ApoVs than in the parent MSCs (Fig. 7C), leading us to hypothesize that miR-409-3p might be the key miRNA responsible for MSC-ApoV-mediated suppression of IGFBP3.Subsequently, the binding site between miR-409-3p and IGFBP3 was predicted using miRBase (Fig. 7D). A dual-luciferase reporter assay demonstrated that co-transfection of the miR-409-3p mimic with the wild-type (WT) IGFBP3 3’UTR vector significantly reduced luciferase activity. In contrast, co-transfection with the mutant (MUT) IGFBP3 3’UTR vector showed no significant change in luciferase activity (Fig. 7E), confirming that miR-409-3p can directly bind the IGFBP3 3’UTR and inhibit its expression.When MSCs were transfected with a miR-409-3p inhibitor, the resulting ApoVmiR−409−3p inhibitor lost their ability to downregulate the expression of IGFBP3, α-SMA, MMP2, and Collagen-I in TGF-β1-treated LX2 cells compared to ApoVs derived from MSCs transfected with NC inhibitor (Fig. 7F, G). Furthermore, these ApoVs also failed to inhibit the activation of the PI3K-AKT pathway (Fig. 7H, I).These results confirm that MSC-ApoVs deliver miR-409-3p, which directly targets and inhibits IGFBP3 expression, thereby suppressing the activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway.

To confirm the successful modulation of miR-409-3p, we first validated the transfection efficiency in parental MSCs and the subsequent encapsulation efficiency in ApoVs. As shown in Fig. S5A, transfection with miR-409-3p inhibitor significantly knocked down its expression in MSCs, whereas the mimic significantly overexpressed the miRNA. Crucially, these changes were faithfully reflected in the derived ApoVs (Fig. S5B), confirming the successful generation of engineered ApoVs for subsequent functional assays.

While our previous data demonstrated that exogenous IGFBP3 could bypass the ApoV-mediated suppression and promote fibrogenesis, the concurrent addition of LY294002 substantially abrogated this pro-fibrotic effect. The inhibition of PI3K significantly reduced the phosphorylation of downstream AKT and mTOR, leading to a concurrent decrease in α-SMA, MMP2, and Collagen-I expressions (Fig. 8D; Fig. S6D). Immunofluorescence assays further supported that blocking the PI3K cascade attenuated the fibrotic signals induced by exogenous IGFBP3 (Fig. 8E, F; Fig. S6E, F). Collectively, these dual-directional pharmacological interventions suggest that the anti-fibrotic properties of MSC-ApoVs are mediated through the modulation of the IGFBP3/PI3K-AKT signaling.

Importantly, to further investigate whether the pro-fibrotic effect of extracellular IGFBP3 relies on its specific membrane receptor, TMEM219 [36], we performed a targeted siRNA-mediated knockdown of TMEM219 in activated LX-2 cells. To ensure experimental stringency, three siRNA sequences were initially screened via quantitative real-time PCR. Because Si-TMEM219-2 exhibited the highest knockdown efficiency at the mRNA level (Fig. S7A), it was selected for all subsequent experiments. The robust depletion of TMEM219 by this specific sequence was further confirmed at the protein level (Fig. S7B). Following this validation, we assessed the downstream fibrotic markers. As depicted in Fig. S7C, the exogenous supplementation of IGFBP3 successfully counteracted the anti-fibrotic effects of MSC-ApoVs, rescuing the protein expression of typical fibrotic markers (Collagen-I, MMP2, and α-SMA). However, this pro-fibrotic reactivation was substantially reversed following the targeted silencing of TMEM219. These data strongly support the notion that the pro-fibrotic signaling cascade driven by exogenous IGFBP3 is largely dependent on the TMEM219 receptor to relay pathogenic signals into hepatic stellate cells.

Discussion

Hepatic fibrosis is associated with an aberrant liver microenvironment, characterized by the release of inflammatory factors and persistent activation of HSCs [37, 38]. TGF-β1 is recognized as a potent pro-fibrotic cytokine that mediates the activation of quiescent HSCs [39, 40], a pivotal event in fibrogenesis [41] leading to excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition [38]. While inhibiting HSC activation is a theoretical consensus [42], translational challenges remain. This study was propelled by a key clinical observation (Fig. 1): apoptotic signals and the resulting ApoVs are significantly enhanced in human fibrotic liver tissues. In the pathological microenvironment, the endogenous increase of ApoVs predominantly reflects the widespread apoptosis of damaged parenchymal cells (e.g., hepatocytes). These endogenous ApoVs typically encapsulate damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and pro-inflammatory signals that activate local cells and exacerbate the fibrotic niche [24, 43]. A critical qualitative distinction must be made here: the biological function and cargo of ApoVs are strictly dictated by their cellular origin [44]. In stark contrast to hepatocyte-derived ApoVs, MSC-ApoVs inherit the intrinsic immunomodulatory and regenerative properties of their parent mesenchymal stem cells. Rather than delivering pro-inflammatory DAMPs, MSC-ApoVs are enriched with specific therapeutic payloads. As demonstrated by our extensive in vitro and in vivo functional assays, MSC-ApoVs act as targeted therapeutic vehicles that actively reprogram the microenvironment, rather than serving as passive pathogenic indicators of tissue injury.

Over the past two decades, MSCs have held considerable potential in regenerative medicine due to their robust immunomodulatory capabilities [45]. Accumulating evidence suggests that MSC-derived exosomes are highly efficacious [46]. Among EVs, ApoVs possess distinct advantages in high yield and scalable production, exhibiting significant efficacy in bone regeneration [47], ovarian repair [48], and hemophilia [22]. Building upon this robust cell-free foundation, our characterization (Fig. 2) confirms the successful enrichment of highly pure MSC-ApoVs. It is important to precisely define the subpopulation of apoptotic vesicles utilized in this study. Apoptotic cell-derived extracellular vesicles are highly heterogeneous. Classical apoptotic bodies are generally large vesicles (1–5 μm) containing intact organelles and nuclear fragments, which typically pellet at low-speed centrifugation (e.g., 2,000–3,000 x g) [49]. In contrast, the MSC-ApoVs in our study were isolated at 16,000 x g. Consistent with this isolation protocol, our NTA analysis demonstrated an exact size distribution with a mean diameter of 178.9 nm. Consequently, our isolated vesicles naturally lack large chromatin fragments (Histone H3-negative) and endoplasmic reticulum debris (Calnexin-negative), while sharing typical membrane features (CD63/CD9) with other functional vesicles [43, 50]. Therefore, based on current extracellular vesicle nomenclature guidelines [51], the MSC-ApoV population evaluated in this study predominantly overlaps with apoptotic microvesicles or large extracellular vesicles (lEVs) released during apoptosis. Moreover, accumulating evidence indicates that extracellular vesicles can functionally reprogram recipient-cell behavior through their bioactive cargo, supporting the biological plausibility of MSC-ApoV-mediated HSC reprogramming [52]. Importantly, our data explicitly demonstrate (Fig. 3C) that these MSC-ApoVs are efficiently internalized by activated LX2 cells and significantly inhibit TGF-β1-induced HSC activation, proliferation, and migration.

To unravel the underlying mechanism, our transcriptome sequencing (Fig. 4) identified IGFBP3 as a key node. The role of IGFBP3 in liver fibrosis has been controversial in the literature, with some studies reporting a pro-fibrotic role [33, 34] while others suggest a protective effect [35]. By integrating transcriptomic data with both loss-of-function (Fig. 5) and gain-of-function (Fig. 6) experiments, our results unequivocally resolve this controversy within our model, clearly establishing that IGFBP3 functions as a pro-fibrotic driver in activated LX2 cells, and that MSC-ApoVs exert their effects primarily by downregulating this specific target.

The cargo of ApoVs plays a crucial role in intercellular communication [53–55], with miRNAs serving as vital informational bridges [56–59]. Through dual-luciferase reporter assays (Fig. 7E), we directly confirmed the specific binding of miR-409-3p to the 3’UTR of IGFBP3. Furthermore, our comprehensive rescue experiments demonstrate that the delivery of miR-409-3p is the central factor in MSC-ApoV function. Regarding the downstream cascade, established literature identifies TMEM219 as a high-affinity receptor for IGFBP3[33]. Crucially, our targeted siRNA knockdown experiments directly validated this interaction in our model, demonstrating that the pro-fibrotic effects of exogenous IGFBP3 are largely mediated through this specific membrane receptor. Building upon this receptor-ligand axis, our newly incorporated pharmacological rescue and blockade assays (Fig. 8) strongly indicate that the IGFBP3-mediated pro-fibrotic effect is primarily driven by the downstream activation of the PI3K-AKT cascade. Consistent with this, our animal studies (Figs. 9 and 10) demonstrate a synchronized inhibition of the PI3K-AKT pathway following the targeted suppression of IGFBP3. Consistent with this, our animal studies (Figs. 9 and 10) demonstrate that systemic administration of MSC-ApoVs effectively downregulates hepatic IGFBP3 expression, accompanied by a synchronized inhibition of the PI3K-AKT pathway.

The dynamic balance of ECM turnover is also reflected in our results. Although MMPs are traditionally associated with ECM degradation, the pathological upregulation of MMP2 during active fibrogenesis is a hallmark of activated HSCs. The subsequent downregulation of MMP2 following MSC-ApoV treatment, alongside the reduction in Collagen I and α-SMA (Figs. 3 and 8), reflects a profound suppression of the myofibroblast activation state. Furthermore, miR-409-3p is part of the DLK1-DIO3 imprinted cluster, which is highly active in stem cells [60–62]. This molecular background provides a logical explanation for its specific enrichment in MSC-ApoVs.

Collectively, this study possesses several notable strengths. First, it represents a pioneering investigation of MSC-ApoVs as a therapeutic platform for hepatic fibrosis. Second, it follows a rigorous “clinical-to-bench-and-back” approach, originating from clinical observations to ensure translational relevance. Furthermore, the elucidation of the miR-409-3p/IGFBP3/PI3K-AKT axis provides a robust, data-driven mechanistic framework validated across multiple models.

Naturally, there are several limitations. Firstly, MSC-ApoVs contain a highly diverse cargo [63]. While our data establish miR-409-3p as a pivotal mediator, multi-omics profiling is imperative to decipher the synergistic contributions of other vesicular components. Simultaneously, miRNAs inherently possess a “one-to-many” biological regulatory property [64]; thus, parallel regulatory networks cannot be completely ruled out. Secondly, while our in vitro siRNA knockdown experiments suggest that the pro-fibrotic effect of exogenous IGFBP3 is largely mediated through the TMEM219 receptor to activate the PI3K-AKT cascade in HSCs, the complex multicellular microenvironment in vivo presents additional variables. Future studies employing HSC-specific conditional TMEM219 knockout murine models are warranted to further elucidate this receptor-dependent axis within a physiological whole-body context. Furthermore, as the current mechanistic study utilized a specific well-characterized MSC line to ensure baseline molecular consistency, addressing potential inter-donor variability and validating the generalizability of these findings across alternative MSC sources (e.g., adipose or bone marrow) require further comprehensive cross-source investigations [44, 47]. Finally, while our mechanistic framework was constructed using the LX2 cell line and supported by validation in primary HSCs, future studies employing 3D human liver organoids or precision-cut liver slices will be vital to further bridge the translational gap and mimic human pathology.

Looking toward future clinical translation, several pharmacological and manufacturing milestones must be achieved. While MSC-ApoVs present a promising cell-free alternative, standardized protocols for the scalability of ApoVs production and stringent quality control criteria remain to be established [43, 47]. Furthermore, determining a robust clinical dosing rationale requires comprehensive pharmacokinetic modeling to accurately translate the murine effective dose into human equivalent doses [22]. A detailed profiling of ApoVs biodistribution in vivo is also critical to verify their preferential liver tropism [27] and evaluate off-target clearance. Finally, before advancing to early-phase clinical trials, comprehensive long-term safety profiling—specifically monitoring for potential systemic toxicity or immunogenicity following repeated administrations—must be rigorously conducted [24].

In conclusion, this study identifies MSC-ApoVs as a potent “cell-free” therapeutic agent for hepatic fibrosis, offering a safer and standardizable alternative to live MSC transplantation. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that MSC-ApoVs reprogram activated hepatic stellate cells by primarily delivering miR-409-3p, which modulates the IGFBP3/PI3K-AKT axis. These insights, reinforced by clinical correlation analysis, not only validate IGFBP3 as a critical therapeutic target but also position MSC-ApoVs as a promising translational strategy for liver regenerative medicine.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identifies MSC-ApoVs as a potent “cell-free” therapeutic agent for hepatic fibrosis, offering a safer and standardizable alternative to live MSC transplantation. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that MSC-ApoVs reprogram activated hepatic stellate cells by primarily delivering miR-409-3p, which specifically silences the IGFBP3/PI3K-AKT axis. These insights, reinforced by clinical correlation analysis, not only validate IGFBP3 as a critical therapeutic target but also position MSC-ApoVs as a promising translational strategy for liver regenerative medicine.
