Going into the final chapters of a nursing career: nurses’ lived experiences and perspectives on pre-retirement in South Korea
Authors: Jeong Sook Lee, Haejin Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRetirement is a critical life transition that requires emotional, psychological, and social adjustments. For nurses in South Korea, who typically spend over three decades in clinical practice, little is known about their pre-retirement experiences and perspectives. This study aimed to explore how nurses nearing retirement reflect on their careers, respond emotionally, and envision life after retirement.MethodsAn exploratory qualitative design was employed. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 10 nurses working at four general hospitals in South Korea, all within five years of mandatory retirement. Data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis.ResultsFive themes were identified: Reflection in a life like late autumn, Rest at the end of passion, Worries about having to fill in the blank, Expectations for strengthening family and social relationships, and Confidence in challenges and self-realization. Participants described mixed emotions of loss, relief, and pride, along with anxiety stemming from insufficient preparation. At the same time, they expressed hopes for strengthened family ties, new social affiliations, and self-realization, albeit often without concrete plans.ConclusionsThis study highlights that nurses approaching retirement undergo psychological and emotional transitions while facing limited practical preparation. Tailored counseling, education, and systematic retirement programs are needed to support this transition. At organizational and community levels, mechanisms to sustain professional identity and utilize retirees’ expertise in mentoring and community health can positively influence both nurses’ post-retirement well-being and healthcare resources.Keywords: Nurse, Retirement, Pre-retirement, Retirement preparation, Psychological transition, Interview, Qualitative research

Background

Retirement is a major life event that marks the transition from an individual’s long-term professional role into a new stage of life. Traditionally viewed as a simple withdrawal from the workforce, retirement is increasingly recognized as a complex and transformative process involving emotional, psychological, and social adjustments [1, 2]. In rapidly aging societies such as South Korea, the average life expectancy continues to rise, leading to an extended post-retirement period and highlighting the growing importance of adequate retirement planning and support systems [3, 4]. Retirement has thus evolved into a pivotal life phase, often referred to as a “second step life,” in which individuals pursue leisure, self-realization, and new social identities [5, 6].

However, retirement is not a uniformly positive experience. It is frequently accompanied by a complex interplay of emotions, including relief, loss, anticipation, and anxiety. Research shows that while retirement brings new freedom, it can also be a crisis situation that causes a variety of stresses due to the loss of financial security, social status, and structure in daily life, especially if it is not properly planned [7, 8]. Therefore, to adapt well to life after retirement, it is necessary to actively prepare in various areas, such as income changes, role changes, shifts in social relationships, awareness of environmental changes, and identity formation.

These emotional and existential challenges are especially salient for professionals with strong occupational identities, such as nurses [3]. As core personnel in the healthcare system, nurses are accustomed to high levels of engagement, responsibility, and relational labor. Their work is often intertwined with their identity and sense of purpose [3, 9]. In South Korea, nurses typically begin their careers in their early twenties, and the legal retirement age is approximately 60 [9]. This means many retiring nurses have accumulated over three decades of clinical experience. As a result, the decision to step away from the nursing role is not only a career shift but also a profound detachment from a lifelong vocation. Consequently, leaving the nursing profession may evoke feelings of emptiness or disorientation, particularly when the transition is not accompanied by a clear vision for life after retirement. In South Korea, where traditional expectations about aging, family, and social contribution persist, older nurses may also experience uncertainty about their value and role in society after leaving the formal workforce.

Although prior studies have examined retirement-related variables such as attitudes, preparation, and anxiety among Korean nurses [9, 10], the majority of these investigations have relied on quantitative approaches using standardized measures, which cannot fully capture the complexity of nurses’ lived experiences. Furthermore, most research has focused on post-retirement outcomes, such as difficulties with re-employment or loss of social recognition [11, 12], while neglecting the crucial pre-retirement stage. To date, the literature has largely emphasized post-retirement adjustment, leaving the pre-retirement stage relatively underexplored.

However, the pre-retirement period represents a pivotal window during which emotional, identity, and planning processes actively unfold, directly influencing how well individuals adapt after retirement. Alavi et al. [13] demonstrated that pre-retirement planning positively affects post-retirement well-being and quality of life, while La Rue et al. [14] reported that programmatic interventions during the preparation stage—particularly those supporting adaptation to identity changes—can enhance retirement adjustment and satisfaction. Moreover, Guo et al. [15] suggested that retirement planning intention plays a mediating role in linking pre-retirement preparation to individual happiness and successful adaptation. Therefore, deeper insights into pre-retirement experiences are essential for identifying intervention points, reducing transition-related distress, and informing timely support systems.

Despite these findings, a significant gap remains in understanding how Korean nurses nearing retirement make sense of this transition, what emotional and psychological challenges they encounter, and how they envision their post-retirement roles. Given the emotionally charged and identity-redefining nature of retirement [16, 17], there is a need to examine how they reflect on their careers, reinterpret their sense of self, and prepare mentally and emotionally for the future. To address this gap, the present study qualitatively explores the pre-retirement experiences and perspectives of Korean nurses. By examining these experiences, this study is expected to provide practical insights for the development of retirement preparation programs tailored to nurses and to highlight the support needed to ensure a stable post-retirement transition. Beyond programs, the findings may also promote psychological readiness and personal growth at the individual level and enable retired nurses to share their expertise and engage in meaningful social activities at the community level.

The aim of this study is to explore the pre-retirement experiences and perspectives of nurses in South Korea. Accordingly, the research question is: “What are the experiences and perspectives of nurses approaching retirement?”

Methods

Study design

This study explored nurses’ pre-retirement experiences and perspectives, focusing on their thoughts, feelings, and expectations, to provide foundational data to support a successful transition to retirement. An exploratory qualitative design was adopted, appropriate for examining underexplored phenomena and capturing participants’ subjective perspectives in depth [18]. Data were collected through individual semi-structured interviews and analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis approach [19]. This study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines [20].

Setting and participants

The participants were 10 nurses working at four general hospitals in South Korea, all of whom were within five years of the mandatory retirement age of 60. The five-year inclusion window was selected because retirement planning in Korea typically begins around this time. Specifically, the Korean government provides a basic retirement preparation education course for those scheduled to retire within the next five years, based on its “Support Measures for Civil Servants Expected to Retire” [21]. Those further from retirement (e.g., more than five years) may lack a realistic sense of immediacy, whereas those very close to retirement (e.g., within one year) may focus only on short-term, practical matters. Thus, a five-year range was chosen to capture diverse yet meaningful reflections and preparations during this transitional stage.

Snowball sampling was used to recruit participants through referrals from those nearing retirement. To mitigate potential bias associated with snowball sampling, participants were recruited from four different general hospitals. After explaining the study’s purpose and interview procedures, interviews were conducted with participants who provided written informed consent. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached, that is, when recurring patterns and categories consistently emerged and no new themes were identified. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) nurses expected to retire within five years, (2) currently employed in general hospitals, and (3) provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) history of diagnosed mental illness that could affect participation, and (2) involuntary or short-term retirement (e.g., temporary unemployment, sick leave).

Data collection

Data were collected from July 1 to August 29, 2024, through one-on-one in-depth interviews. With the participants’ consent, interview content was recorded on a smartphone, and gestures, facial expressions, and other nonverbal cues were noted in an interview notebook. After each interview, the researcher repeatedly listened to the recordings and transcribed the content verbatim. Interview locations were chosen for participant convenience, including hospital lounges or quiet study cafés near their homes. Each interview began with a greeting and then progressed naturally into the discussion. The researcher refrained from influencing the conversation, aiming to maintain a neutral stance. Interview durations ranged from approximately 50 to 90 min.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis is a flexible method for identifying, analyzing, and reporting patterns within qualitative data, enabling researchers to interpret both manifest and latent meanings. In line with the study’s purpose of exploring the experiences and perspectives of nurses in the pre-retirement stage, Braun and Clarke’s [19] six-phase thematic analysis was employed.

Researchers first familiarized themselves with the transcripts through repeated readings and highlighted passages that reflected participants’ perceptions, feelings, and expectations related to retirement. Initial codes were then generated by extracting phrases that captured these experiences. Codes were compared and organized into broader categories that represented different aspects of pre-retirement experiences, such as emotional responses, reflections on career, and anticipated future roles. Themes and subthemes were refined through iterative discussion among the research team to ensure coherence and distinctiveness. Finally, the analysis was synthesized into five main themes, each of which addressed how nurses nearing retirement made sense of this transition and envisioned their future.

This approach ensured that the data analysis was directly aligned with the research aim, moving beyond surface-level descriptions to highlight recurring patterns in nurses’ pre-retirement experiences and perspectives.

Rigor and reflexivity

This study applied Lincoln and Guba’s four criteria for trustworthiness [23], namely, credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability, along with researcher reflexivity. Credibility was secured by recruiting nurses within five years of retirement, transcribing interviews verbatim, and repeatedly reviewing the audio files. To further enhance credibility, we conducted member checking by sharing the summarized thematic findings with two participants for validation. Both confirmed that the analysis accurately reflected their experiences and perspectives. Transferability was addressed by providing detailed descriptions of the study context and participants, and by sharing the findings with five non-participant nurses who affirmed their applicability to similar settings. Dependability was enhanced by recording field notes on non-verbal cues and emotions during interviews, and two qualitative research experts reviewed the data collection and analysis. Confirmability was maintained by keeping an audit trail and ensuring that interpretations were grounded in participants’ narratives.

Researcher reflexivity was maintained throughout all stages of the study by continuously reflecting on our conceptual lenses, explicit and implicit assumptions, preconceptions, and values, and by considering how these might have influenced interpretations.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB NO: 7001066-202404-HR-025). Participants were informed of the study’s purpose, procedures, confidentiality measures, and their right to withdraw at any time. They were also informed that the data would be used solely for research purposes and destroyed three years after the study’s completion. Freedom of expression was guaranteed, and written informed consent was obtained.

Results

Participant characteristics

Table 2 provides general information about the 10 nurses who participated in this study. Their ages ranged from 56 to 60 years, placing them close to the mandatory retirement age of 60 in Korea. They had worked for 32 to 38 years, spending more than half of their lives in the nursing profession. All participants held administrative positions (four head nurses, four department heads, and two nursing directors). This reflects the career progression system in Korea, where long-serving nurses typically move into management roles. Given their extensive clinical and managerial experience, the participants were able to provide clear, realistic, and in-depth reflections on retirement.

By analyzing the experiences and perspectives of nurses with fewer than five years remaining until retirement, 10 subthemes and 5 overarching themes were derived from a total of 208 initial codes. Thematic results

First theme: Reflection in a life like late autumn

The first theme illustrates how participants, as they neared retirement, engaged in deep reflection on their lives and careers, directly addressing the research question regarding how nurses prepare for retirement both practically and emotionally. This theme captures a duality of emotions: sadness and regret over aging and parting with the workplace to which they had devoted decades, alongside a sense of liberation and anticipation for the freedom that retirement might bring. Unlike Theme 3, which emphasizes uncertainty about the future and the anxiety of filling a blank page, this theme is rooted in retrospection—looking back on one’s life and work with mixed emotions while acknowledging retirement as a major turning point.

Sub-theme 1–1: Calmly accepting the sadness and regret of aging

The participants described retirement as entering the “late autumn” of life, where conflicting emotions of freedom and emptiness coexisted. They recognized retirement as inevitable but processed it through reflection on both what they had lost and what awaited them.

It’s already time for retirement. I think it feels like something is falling off my body, even though it has supported me my whole life. (Participant 5)

Well, I’m retiring at 60. After retirement, I still have about 40 years ahead of me, so I see it as a major turning point in life, something everyone has to go through at least once. (Participant 3)

Sub-theme 1–2: Pride in a life well worked

Despite feelings of loss, participants expressed pride and satisfaction in their careers, viewing themselves as having contributed meaningfully in a profession where life and death coexist. This sense of pride distinguished their reflections from later themes, which focused more on planning and envisioning the future.

I rarely thought about how hard it was. I never thought about quitting my job. (Omitted) I had fun and worked hard while working. (Participant 7)

I never thought retirement would come so quickly. (Omitted) When I think about it myself, I’m sure that I did my best as a nurse. (Participant 1)

Second theme: Rest at the end of passion

The second theme highlights the sense of relief and liberation that participants experienced after decades of professional dedication and responsibility. This theme addresses the research question by illustrating how nurses nearing retirement reinterpret their lives, shifting from a career defined by duty to a future envisioned as freer and more balanced. Unlike Theme 1, which centered on retrospective reflection marked by mixed emotions of pride and regret, this theme emphasizes the transition into rest and tranquility, underscoring participants’ aspirations for a simple and self-directed life.

Sub-theme 2 − 1: Relief from rules and responsibilities

Participants described retirement as a release from the rigid schedules and accountability that had defined their working lives. While grounded in pride for their long-standing service, they anticipated liberation from constraints and looked forward to enjoying new possibilities.

I think I will feel very relieved. I don’t think I will have any regrets. Even when that time comes, I don’t think I will regret it and I think it will be so refreshing and really liberating. (Participant 4)

It’s like being tied down for 40 years. Being free from work itself means being able to do whatever I want and not being tied down, so it feels good. (Participant 5)

Sub-theme 2–2: Desire for personal time and peace

Beyond relief, participants longed for a life characterized by simplicity, harmony, and self-care. They envisioned stepping away from the demands of their careers to embrace new rhythms of leisure, part-time work, and inner peace. This distinguished the theme from later ones (e.g., Theme 5 on self-realization), as the focus here was not on new achievements but on rest and balance.

I have 40 more years to live, so I need to do something, but rather than doing something passionate like I do now, I want to do something that I can do leisurely, something new. (Participant 4)

No matter what I do, I want to work part-time. If I can use my spare time freely, I will have more free time, and I don’t have to work hard from the beginning, so I think it would be good to do that for about 5 years. (Participant 2)

Third theme: Worries about having to fill in the blank

The third theme reflects participants’ recognition of their inadequate preparation for retirement, which generated feelings of anxiety and concern. At the same time, their narratives revealed a vague but emerging awareness that some form of preparation would be necessary. This theme therefore highlights the tension between anxiety about unpreparedness and a tentative, modest willingness to begin considering retirement preparation. Unlike Theme 1 (focused on reflection on the past) and Theme 2 (focused on relief and rest), this theme emphasizes the uncertainty of facing an undefined future.

Sub-theme 3 − 1: Anxiety over unplanned retirement

Participants expressed worry that, despite their imminent retirement, they were ill-prepared for the changes to come. Their concerns extended beyond financial stability to broader uncertainties regarding daily life and the balance between work and leisure in retirement.

Since I have a job, I’m enrolled in a pension and have insurance, but I worry a lot about whether that will be enough. Just because I retire doesn’t mean my living expenses will decrease. (Participant 8)

We are licensed professional nurses. Because I thought I could do anything with my nursing license, I think I have been living without much thought. Currently, I am not prepared for anything. (Participant 4)

Sub-theme 3 − 2: Vague but willing to prepare for retirement

Although participants had not yet formed specific plans, they began to recognize that retirement would require some form of preparation. Their willingness was expressed not as concrete action but as a modest belief that it was “not too late” to start, however vaguely. This tentative stance distinguished the theme from being solely about anxiety, revealing a faint orientation toward constructive adjustment.

My friends told me that I should start preparing for retirement 5 years before. My husband also said the same thing a lot. But I hardly ever thought about it. I think I should start preparing now, even though it is late. (Participant 6)

I used to just think vaguely that I should relax and do nothing, but now I feel like I need to do something. (Participant 2)

Fourth theme: Expectations for strengthening family and social relationships

This theme highlights participants’ aspirations to restore and enhance family bonds that had been overshadowed by decades of professional dedication, while also cultivating new social connections after retirement. In contrast to Theme 3, which emphasized uncertainty and anxiety about insufficient preparation, this theme represents a more future-oriented and relational outlook, demonstrating how participants sought meaning through family affection and broader social belonging.

Sub-theme 4 − 1: Longing for family time and making amends

Participants expressed regret over not having spent enough time with their families because of their long careers as nurses, along with a strong desire to spend more time with their families after retirement. In particular, they sought to compensate for feelings of guilt toward their children by caring for their grandchildren or through family trips. The participants regarded the restoration of family bonds after retirement as an important life goal.

I think I didn’t raise my children properly. So, when my children get married, I plan to help raise my grandchildren until they are three or four years old. Then I will feel good, and I will feel less sorry toward my children. (Participant 3)

The first thing I want to do after retirement is a family trip. I want to go anywhere, domestically or internationally. I have been so busy that my whole family has not been able to go on an overseas trip. (Participant 8)

Sub-theme 4 − 2: Desire for new social affiliations

Beyond family, participants sought opportunities to remain socially engaged and to cultivate a sense of belonging through new affiliations aligned with their interests and values. Volunteer activities, cultural participation, and hobby clubs were perceived as meaningful avenues for sustaining social connectedness and maintaining a sense of usefulness after retirement. This sub-theme illustrates how participants envisioned retirement not as social withdrawal but as re-engagement in new relational contexts.

I want to belong somewhere—do a lot of volunteer work, enjoy myself, meet friends, make new connections, cook delicious meals for others… things like that. That’s what I want to do. (Participant 9)

I want to work as a guide at a museum and do activities related to history. I am very interested in history. Just thinking about it makes me feel good. When I retire, I want to do things I have never done before. (Participant 10)

Fifth theme: Confidence in challenges and self-realization

This theme reflects how participants reframed retirement as a beginning rather than an ending, envisioning it as an opportunity for renewed growth and self-realization. Unlike Theme 3, which was marked by anxiety and uncertainty about insufficient preparation, this theme emphasized constructive confidence and future orientation. Participants drew upon their resilience and professional experience, expressing belief in their ability to face new challenges and create meaning beyond nursing.

Sub-theme 5 − 1: Willingness to go beyond nursing

Participants demonstrated openness to moving beyond their long-standing professional identity as nurses and engaging in new activities rooted in their personal interests and values. Retirement was viewed as a chance to pursue unfulfilled aspirations, explore leisure or learning opportunities, and reestablish a sense of achievement independent of their careers.

I like traveling. I have never done backpacking or independent travel, but I think I will try it after I retire. I haven’t thought about it because I didn’t have time, but I think it will be a good opportunity because I have a lot of time. I am scared, but I have to try it. (Participant 6)

I think I am not old yet at my age. If there is something I can learn at my age, I will try it. I think I should try learning something that suits me. (Participant 1)

Sub-theme 5 − 2: Confidence in taking on new challenges

Anchored in decades of demanding clinical practice, participants expressed confidence and courage to take on new endeavors. Their professional resilience fostered a conviction that they were capable of pursuing novel goals, framing retirement as a stage of re-engagement rather than decline.

I have been working in a difficult hospital for over 30 years, so what can I not do? It takes courage to try something new again, and I have that courage. (Participant 5)

I think I will start something new, and I can still do it. I think that is my strength. I am confident in anything I do. I am retired, but I still think I am young. (Participant 10)

Discussion

This study explored in depth the experiences and perspectives of nurses approaching the end of their careers. Based on the emergent themes, we discuss how nurses perceive and prepare for retirement, as well as the psychological and social meanings embedded in these experiences.

First, reflecting the first theme, nurses nearing retirement naturally engaged in self-reflection as they faced the life transition that retirement represents. The participants expressed mixed emotions while looking back on their lives and parting with a profession to which they had long devoted themselves. They felt both a sense of loss over the decline in their professional roles and pride in the paths they had taken. Rather than simply reminiscing about the past, they interpreted and reconstructed their lives and professional experiences in meaningful ways. This theme shows that reflection was not merely backward-looking but functioned as an adaptive strategy, aligning with the research question by revealing how nurses experience retirement emotionally and psychologically. This kind of reflection serves as an important internal resource, helping individuals become more aware of who they are, what they value, and the kind of life they want to lead post-retirement [24]. Reflection, as part of the transitional process, can facilitate adaptive coping and identity reconstruction, which may, in turn, foster personal growth, strengthen coherence, and enhance readiness for life changes [25, 26]. Therefore, strategies are needed to help nurses nearing retirement engage in constructive reflection. For example, a “life reflection program” targeting soon-to-retire nurses could support positive interpretations of their careers and life value. At the organizational level, strategies could include opportunities for these nurses to share their experiences and pass on their wisdom to junior nurses. Such practices help expand personal reflection into meaningful social activity.

Second, consistent with the second theme, the participants expressed expectations of leading more leisurely lives after retirement. For them, retirement signified an escape from the intense environment of hospitals, where life, aging, illness, death, joy, and sorrow coexist. It also represented freedom from a daily life confined within a set framework and a rigid schedule. They looked forward to a life free from time constraints and rules, one that would allow them to release accumulated tension and burdens. This theme extends prior research by showing how expectations of rest and liberation are tied not only to relief from work but also to nurses’ redefinition of meaningful time use. This expectation aligns with previous research showing that retired nurses initially experience comfort with their newfound time [27] and express a desire for liberation from the pressures of work [28]. To fully enjoy meaningful rest, systematic preparation is indispensable, helping soon-to-retire nurses adjust to shifts in daily rhythms and foster plans for leisure and lifestyle. Hutchinson et al. [29] emphasize that lifestyle planning, including goal-setting for time use, is a critical component of retirement readiness and significantly predicts satisfaction with retirement. Similarly, Halama et al. [26] demonstrate that meaning-making processes during transition involve reconstructing daily routines and roles, not merely disengaging from work. Thus, prior education in time management, hobby cultivation, health maintenance, and financial planning may be beneficial. Furthermore, providing counseling or programs that support emotional and identity transitions may help nurses embrace retirement as a meaningful new stage rather than simply an endpoint [14, 30].

Third, in line with the third theme, the participants generally lacked specific plans for retirement. Unlike the first two themes, which revealed psychological reflection and expectations of rest, this theme highlights the absence of concrete preparation and its implications for retirement adjustment. Many expressed uncertainty about what to do after retirement or were unsure how to begin planning. Confidence in having a basic pension, insurance coverage, and a nursing license contributed to their tendency to delay concrete preparation. However, inadequate preparation can lead to loss, isolation, weakened social connections, and negative emotions such as anxiety, depression, and helplessness [9, 12]. Given nurses’ strong professional identities, the absence of a plan for their future social roles may intensify psychological anxiety. Thus, systematic life planning education and retirement preparation programs are essential. Research has shown that the more thoroughly individuals prepare for life transitions, the better their post-retirement adaptation and life satisfaction [31]. Because professionals who lead job-centered lives often struggle to conceptualize life after retirement [32], a multi-layered approach integrating psychological, social, and organizational support is required to help retiring nurses form new identities and rebuild their social networks [13, 14].

Fourth, aligned with the fourth theme, the participants demonstrated a willingness to reconstruct their lives after retirement. They particularly emphasized the importance of family relationships and the desire to establish new roles through volunteer work or social engagement. This theme differed from prior themes by shifting the focus from personal emotions and preparation gaps to relational and community-oriented expectations, suggesting that retirement preparation also involves redefining social connectedness. This reflects previous findings that female retirees often seek to redefine life meaning around family [33]. Other studies report that professional women tend to maintain interest in interactions related to their former occupations, rather than shifting entirely to private relationships [34, 35]. To bridge this gap, retirees attempt to maintain or restore social connections post-employment. Strengthening family ties and social networks can significantly enhance emotional stability and life satisfaction. Therefore, programs that support reestablishing family connections and meaningful community roles are essential. Opportunities such as peer community building, volunteer work, or mentoring activities can help job-centered individuals develop a renewed sense of social belonging. Empirical research supports this, showing that volunteering fosters social connectedness and elevates well-being in older adults [36, 37], while peer-led interventions have been demonstrated to positively influence social integration and mental health [38].

Finally, reflecting the fifth theme, the participants were considering activities for self-actualization after retirement and expressed strong confidence. Their internal motivation was fueled by a desire to form a new identity based on accumulated professional experience. This theme underscores the constructive and forward-looking dimension of perspectives on retirement, in contrast to the earlier anxiety-laden themes, showing how confidence and self-realization become central to envisioning life beyond work. This reflects a drive to remain socially useful and aligns with the concept of retirement as a transitional phase in which individuals pursue autonomy and meaningful living [39, 40]. Although the participants lacked specific roadmaps, they demonstrated flexible and courageous attitudes toward new challenges. Therefore, a customized preparatory program that includes re-examining capabilities, exploring resources, and designing meaningful activities is needed from the pre-retirement stage so that they can realize their expectations. In addition, it may be worth considering establishing a policy so that nurses’ expertise and experience can be utilized in community health promotion activities, nursing education, and mentoring of junior nurses even after retirement [41, 42]. This process can be an important foundation for proactively facing retirement as a “life transition period” rather than simply preparing for a job change or old age.

Through these results, this study expands the in-depth understanding of the psychological changes and difficulties experienced by nurses nearing retirement and how they prepare for retirement. By explicitly aligning the discussion with the emergent themes, this study clarifies how each theme contributes uniquely to understanding nurses’ pre-retirement experiences and perspectives. Before retirement, nurses are not simply ending their careers, but are going through an important period of contemplation to find a new identity and direction in life. These results highlight the need for policy and educational interventions to support the successful transition of nurses to retirement. Ultimately, this insight can contribute to improving the quality of life of nurses after retirement.

Limitations and strenghts

Although this study provides meaningful insights into the experiences of nurses approaching retirement, it has some limitations. First, because all participants had extensive clinical experience of more than 30 years, the perspectives of nurses with relatively shorter careers or those working in other nursing fields (e.g., psychiatric or community nursing) may not have been captured. In addition, because all participants held administrative positions, the perspectives of nurses in direct clinical or specialized care roles may not have been fully reflected. This reflects the structural characteristics of the Korean nursing system, where nurses with long careers typically transition into administrative roles as they approach retirement. While this context provides valuable insight into the retirement experiences of administrative nurses, it also limits the applicability of the findings to those in non-administrative positions. Future studies should compare experiences by role to examine whether managerial versus direct-care positions differentially shape pre-retirement perceptions and preparations. Furthermore, since the study included only nurses within five years of retirement, the findings may not fully represent the experiences of those at earlier stages of their careers or those in the very final phase (e.g., within one year). Differences in timing are likely to influence how nurses perceive and prepare for retirement, suggesting that future studies should investigate such variations in a more systematic manner.

This study employed thematic analysis to identify patterns and perspectives in nurses’ pre-retirement experiences, rather than a phenomenological approach that seeks to uncover the universal essence of lived experiences. While this design was appropriate for exploring diverse viewpoints, it may not fully capture the deeper ontological meanings of retirement as a life transition. Future studies using phenomenological methods could provide complementary insights into the essence of this experience.

Nevertheless, the strength of this study lies in its exploration of the complex and internal experiences of nurses approaching an important turning point in their lives–retirement–using qualitative methods. Unlike previous studies that have mainly focused on life after retirement or reemployment, this study provides practical and timely insights by focusing on emotional and psychological changes and the process of life reconstruction before retirement. These results can be used as important data for the development of policies and organizational programs to support nurses’ retirement preparations.

Implications for practice

This study showed that nurses nearing retirement experience various emotions and expectations at a turning point in their lives and express a willingness to reconstruct the meaning of life after retirement. Accordingly, nursing organizations need to prepare counseling and education programs to help nurses navigate psychological and emotional transitions. Additionally, a systematic retirement preparation program that supports career transitions, financial planning, and maintenance of social relationships from the pre-retirement stage is required. At the organizational level, structures can be established to facilitate the transfer of knowledge and experience from nurses nearing retirement to junior colleagues, thereby allowing senior nurses to continue contributing their expertise and maintaining their professional identity. Furthermore, at the community level, mechanisms may be developed to utilize retired nurses’ expertise in education, mentoring, or volunteer activities, thereby extending their professional contributions beyond the workplace.

Evidence from international studies shows that structured pre-retirement planning enhances post-retirement well-being and quality of life [13], and that program interventions supporting identity adaptation during this stage can significantly improve adjustment and satisfaction [14]. In Korea, while specific retirement preparation programs for nurses have not yet been developed, studies assessing nurses’ retirement preparation attitudes and needs underscore the importance of designing contextually appropriate interventions [9, 27]. Such practical support is likely to positively affect nurses’ healthy retirement, contribute to sustainable human resource management within organizations, and enrich community health resources.

Conclusions

This exploratory qualitative study used thematic analysis to examine the pre-retirement experiences of nurses approaching retirement. The results revealed five themes: “Reflection in a life like late autumn,” “Rest at the end of passion,” “Worries about having to fill in the blank,” “Expectations for strengthening family and social relationships,” and “Confidence in challenges and self-realization.” The findings extend prior research by showing that, for nurses, retirement involves not only financial and practical preparation but also a profound process of reflection and meaning reconstruction. Unlike previous studies that primarily examined retirement attitudes or post-retirement adjustment, this study highlights the transitional stage before retirement as a unique period in which nurses reinterpret their professional identity, confront anxieties over insufficient preparation, and envision new life directions. In particular, the study demonstrates that nurses, despite their confidence and strong professional foundations, often lack concrete and realistic plans, revealing a critical gap between perception and action in retirement preparation. This insight underscores the need for systematic interventions prior to retirement, including counseling and education programs to support psychological transitions, institutional structures to facilitate life planning and resource access, and organizational initiatives to pass on expertise to younger generations of nurses. Such measures may help ensure a healthier and more meaningful transition, benefiting not only individual nurses but also the sustainability of the nursing workforce and the communities they continue to engage with after retirement.
