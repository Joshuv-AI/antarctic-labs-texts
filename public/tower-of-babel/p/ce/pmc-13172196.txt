Prognostic factors for young adult colorectal cancer patients with liver metastases based on the SEER database
Authors: Yinghua Wu, Rui Liu, Ping Wang, Yan Xiong, Xiaoling Jia, Xiaoyan Wu, Aiying Cao, Jia Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) with liver metastases poses significant challenges, particularly in young adults. This study aims to identify and evaluate the prognostic factors associated with overall survival (OS) in young adult patients with colorectal cancer and liver metastases using data from the SEER database.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 3,188 young adults aged 18 to 45 years, diagnosed with stage IV colorectal cancer and liver metastases between 2010 and 2015, using the SEER database. Patients were categorized based on surgical interventions, including primary tumor resection, metastasectomy, and combinations thereof. Kaplan-Meier survival curves and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate survival outcomes and associated prognostic factors.ResultsThe median overall survival (OS) varied significantly based on the type of surgical intervention. Patients undergoing both primary tumor resection and metastasectomy had the longest median OS of 60 months. The multivariable Cox model confirmed that these patients had the lowest hazard ratio (HR) for mortality (HR 0.4, 95% CI: 0.334–0.479, p < 0.001) compared to those who did not undergo surgery. Additionally, chemotherapy and radiotherapy were associated with improved survival outcomes. Subgroup analyses for colon and rectal cancer confirmed consistent results, with combined surgery showing significant survival benefits.ConclusionSurgical intervention, particularly the combination of primary tumor resection and metastasectomy, plays a crucial role in improving survival outcomes in young adults with colorectal cancer and liver metastases. Chemotherapy and radiotherapy further enhance survival, underscoring the importance of comprehensive treatment strategies in this population.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Liver metastases, Young adults, Prognostic factors, Overall survival

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a major global health concern, ranking as one of the most commonly diagnosed cancers and a leading cause of cancer-related deaths [1, 2]. Traditionally, CRC has been seen as a disease affecting older adults, with most cases occurring in those over 50 [3–6]. However, recent trends have shown a troubling rise in CRC incidence among young adults, particularly those aged 18 to 45. This shift has sparked increased interest in understanding the distinct clinical and biological characteristics of CRC in younger patients [7, 8].

Young adults diagnosed with CRC often present with more advanced stages of disease, and their tumors may exhibit distinct biological and molecular profiles compared to those in older adults. This group is more likely to be diagnosed with aggressive histological subtypes, such as signet ring cell carcinoma and mucinous adenocarcinoma, which are associated with poorer prognoses [9, 10]. Additionally, young adults with CRC tend to have a higher likelihood of developing synchronous or metachronous metastases, particularly to the liver, which is the most common site of distant spread in CRC [11]. Liver metastasis in CRC significantly worsens the prognosis, with survival rates dropping markedly when the liver is involved [12]. The management of CRC with liver metastases in young adults is complex and multifaceted, involving a combination of surgical resection, systemic chemotherapy, and radiotherapy [13, 14]. However, the optimal treatment strategy remains controversial, particularly in the context of younger patients who may have different tolerances and responses to treatment compared to older populations [5, 8].

This study aims to address this gap by analyzing data from the SEER (Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results) database, a comprehensive source of cancer statistics that covers a significant portion of the U.S. population [15]. By examining the outcomes of young adults diagnosed with CRC and liver metastases, this study seeks to uncover the key factors that influence survival in this group. In particular, the study will explore the impact of different treatment modalities, such as surgical resection of the primary tumor and metastases, as well as the role of systemic therapies. Ultimately, the goal of this research is to provide insights that can guide clinical decision-making and improve outcomes for young adults facing this challenging diagnosis. By understanding the prognostic factors that matter most in this population, we can help tailor treatment approaches to better meet the needs of young CRC patients with liver metastases.

Materials and methods

Data sources and study cohort

We identified cases of primary colorectal cancer diagnosed between 2010 and 2015 (excluding autopsy cases) from the National Cancer Institute’s (NCI) Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) registry data (https://seer.cancer.gov/) [16]. SEER 18 cancer registry is a recognized data source for cancer epidemiological studies, covering 34.6% of the U.S. population. The SEER 18 custom data, with additional treatment fields that we used, provides detailed information on tumor characteristics, demographics, treatments received, and survival rates.

We restricted the sample to young adults aged 18 to 45 who were diagnosed with stage IV disease according to the 6th edition of the AJCC with liver metastases. Cases lacking treatment and survival information were excluded. We considered variables such as patient age at diagnosis, gender, race (White, Black, etc.), marital status (single, married, and others), histology (adenocarcinoma NOS, mucinous adenocarcinoma, signet ring cell carcinoma, etc.), year of diagnosis (2010–2011, 2012–2013, and 2014–2015), surgical treatment (primary tumor resection (PTR) with or without metastasectomy, no metastasectomy with primary tumor resection, no resection of primary tumor or metastasis), chemotherapy (yes/unknown), radiation therapy (yes/no/unknown), and overall survival.

Statistical analysis

We provided descriptive statistics for the study cohort. Kaplan-Meier estimates and log-rank tests were used to examine survival differences among treatment groups. Additionally, multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to investigate the association between receiving treatment and overall survival while controlling for other patient characteristics. Hazard ratios (HR), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p-values were reported. All statistical tests were two-sided, and results were considered statistically significant when p ≤ 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Characteristics of patients

A total of 3,188 young adults aged 18 to 45 years who were diagnosed with CRC with liver metastases from 2010 to 2015 were identified from the SEER database and included in this study. The median age at diagnosis was 37.0 years. Among them, 1,475 patients (46.3%) were male, and 1,713 patients (53.7%) were female. A total of 781 patients (24.5%) were non-White. 1,259 patients (39.5%) underwent primary tumor resection alone, 248 patients (7.78%) underwent both primary tumor resection and metastasectomy, 596 patients (18.7%) underwent metastasectomy without primary tumor resection, and 521 patients (16.3%) did not undergo any surgical resection. A total of 2,234 patients (70.1%) received systemic chemotherapy, and 1,850 patients (58.0%) received radiotherapy. Histologically, 1,902 patients (59.7%) had adenocarcinoma NOS, 573 patients (18.0%) had mucinous adenocarcinoma, and 303 patients (9.5%) had signet ring cell adenocarcinoma (Table 1).

Prognostic factors for CRC patients

Figures 1 and 2, and 3 present the results of the overall survival (OS) analysis. The Kaplan-Meier (KM) curve in Fig. 1 shows a significant difference in OS between patients who underwent primary tumor resection and those who did not (log-rank test, p < 0.001). The median OS for patients who underwent primary tumor resection was 35 months, compared to 20 months for those who did not undergo primary tumor resection (log-rank test, p < 0.001). When we compared patients who underwent non-primary site resection to those who did not undergo non-primary site resection, we again observed a significant difference in OS (log-rank test, p = 0.05). The median OS for patients who underwent non-primary site resection was 36 months, whereas the median OS for those who did not undergo resection was 25 months (log-rank test, p = 0.05).

When comparing the four groups of patients (those who underwent primary tumor resection only, those who underwent both primary tumor resection and metastasectomy, those who underwent metastasectomy only, and those who did not undergo any surgical intervention), the KM curves in Fig. 3 again show significant differences in survival (Log-rank test, p < 0.0001). Patients who underwent both primary tumor resection and metastasectomy had the longest median overall survival at 60 months, followed by those who underwent primary tumor resection only, with a median overall survival of 30 months. Patients who underwent metastasectomy only had a median overall survival of 23 months, while those who did not undergo any surgical intervention had the shortest median overall survival of 15 months.

Table 2 includes the results of the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model. The multivariable Cox model confirmed that, compared to patients who did not undergo any surgical resection, those who underwent both primary tumor resection and metastasectomy had the lowest HR of 0.4 (95% CI: 0.334–0.479, p < 0.001), followed by patients who underwent primary tumor resection only (HR 0.536, 95% CI: 0.475–0.604, p < 0.001), and lastly, patients who underwent metastasectomy only (HR 0.668, 95% CI: 0.577–0.772, p < 0.001). We also found that receiving chemotherapy and radiotherapy was significantly associated with better survival outcomes (HR 0.909, 95% CI: 0.826–0.989, p = 0.001 and HR 0.861, 95% CI: 0.788–0.942, p = 0.001, respectively). Additionally, there was an observed improvement in overall survival among patients diagnosed in more recent years (HR 0.907, 95% CI: 0.834–0.979, p = 0.004 for 2012–2013; HR 0.834, 95% CI: 0.756–0.892, p < 0.001 for 2014–2015 compared to 2010–2011). Patients with colorectal cancer liver metastasis combined with bone metastasis had a higher risk compared to those with liver metastasis without bone metastasis (HR 1.45, 95% CI: 1.064–1.977, p = 0.019). Similarly, patients with colorectal cancer liver metastasis combined with lung metastasis had a higher risk compared to those without lung metastasis (HR 1.739, 95% CI: 1.492–2.025, p < 0.001). Tumor size greater than 45 mm was also associated with a higher risk compared to those with a tumor size of 45 mm or less (HR 1.857, 95% CI: 1.69–2.04, p < 0.001).

Discussion

The findings of this study provide crucial insights into the prognostic factors influencing OS in young adults with CRC and liver metastases, using data from the SEER database. The results clearly indicate that surgical interventions, particularly the combination of primary tumor resection and metastasectomy, play a pivotal role in enhancing survival outcomes in this population. Patients who underwent both primary tumor resection and metastasectomy exhibited the longest median OS, underscoring the potential benefits of aggressive surgical management in cases of advanced CRC.

The substantial survival advantage observed in patients who received comprehensive surgical treatment suggests that even in the presence of metastatic disease, complete resection of both the primary tumor and metastases can offer significant clinical benefits. This aligns with existing evidence supporting the role of metastasectomy in extending survival for metastatic CRC patients [17, 18]. However, it is important to approach these findings with caution, as the decision to pursue such aggressive surgical interventions must be individualized, taking into account each patient’s overall health status, tumor burden, and the extent of metastasis.

In addition to surgery, this study highlights the importance of systemic therapies in improving survival outcomes. Chemotherapy has long been a cornerstone of CRC treatment, particularly in metastatic settings, and its association with improved OS in this study reinforces its critical role in managing the disease. Similarly, radiotherapy, although less commonly emphasized in the treatment of metastatic CRC, was also associated with better survival outcomes, suggesting that it may be beneficial in selected cases, especially when used in combination with surgery and chemotherapy. In the subgroup analyses, we observed variations in the magnitude of effect compared with the overall cohort, while the direction of association remained consistent across all models. Notably, mucinous adenocarcinoma demonstrated a consistent protective effect across the overall cohort and subgroup analyses, although the magnitude of the association differed. Similarly, chemotherapy was associated with improved survival across all models, despite variations in the hazard ratio estimates between subgroups. These differences may reflect underlying biological heterogeneity between colon and rectal cancers, differences in treatment selection patterns, or sample size variations. In addition, these findings may be subject to confounding by indication, as treatment allocation in retrospective studies may be influenced by baseline patient characteristics and disease severity. Further prospective studies are warranted to clarify whether these differences represent true biological variation or are influenced by unmeasured confounders.

The analysis also revealed several patient characteristics associated with poorer survival outcomes. The presence of additional metastatic sites, such as bone and lung metastases, was also linked to significantly worse survival, highlighting the need for a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach to treatment that considers both the tumor characteristics and the broader context of the patient’s overall health and social circumstances.

Another important finding of this study is the observed improvement in survival among patients diagnosed in more recent years. This trend likely reflects advancements in medical technology, treatment protocols, and early detection, as well as the increased use of targeted therapies and personalized medicine, which have been shown to improve outcomes in certain CRC subgroups. However, despite these improvements, the overall prognosis for young adults with metastatic CRC remains challenging, underscoring the ongoing need for research and innovation in treatment strategies.

While this study provides valuable insights, several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective nature of the SEER database analysis introduces the possibility of selection bias, and the lack of detailed information on specific treatment regimens, such as chemotherapy types and doses or radiotherapy protocols, limits the ability to fully assess the impact of these therapies. Moreover, the absence of genetic and molecular data in the SEER database restricts our understanding of how biological differences in tumors might influence treatment response and survival in this population. In addition, confounding by indication represents an important limitation, particularly for surgical interventions and chemotherapy. As treatment allocation is not randomized, patients who received these treatments may have had more favorable baseline characteristics, such as better performance status, lower tumor burden, or fewer comorbidities, which could bias the observed survival benefits. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, and prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the crucial role of surgical intervention, particularly the combination of primary tumor resection and metastasectomy, in improving overall survival in young adults with colorectal cancer and liver metastases. The findings support the integration of surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy in the treatment strategy for this patient population. Further prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings and explore the underlying mechanisms driving the observed survival benefits. Understanding the unique clinical characteristics and treatment responses in young adult CRC patients with liver metastases will be essential for optimizing therapeutic approaches and improving patient outcomes.
