Carcinoid heart disease (CHD) is a fibrotic complication of metastatic neuroendocrine tumors, most commonly of small intestinal origin, primarily driven by chronic serotonin exposure. This results in right-sided valvular dysfunction and right-sided heart failure.1,2 While medical therapy—including somatostatin analogs and symptom-directed agents—can control tumor progression and carcinoid syndrome, no approved pharmacologic interventions reverse established cardiac fibrosis.3 Surgical valve replacement is the definitive treatment for severe symptomatic CHD.1,2

Case Presentation

A 46-year-old woman presented with a 1-year history of progressive exertional dyspnea and signs of right heart failure. Echocardiography demonstrated right heart dilation with severe tricuspid regurgitation. While evaluating for connective tissue disease, computed tomography incidentally revealed a large hepatic mass. Subsequent evaluation showed progression to severe tricuspid stenosis and regurgitation, severe pulmonary regurgitation, and marked right heart enlargement. Elevated 24-hour urinary 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA) (121.88 mg/d) confirmed CHD.

In June 2007, the patient underwent tricuspid (27 mm) and pulmonary (21 mm) bioprosthetic replacements (Carpentier-Edwards), right ventricular outflow tract reconstruction with pulmonary annulus augmentation (Hemashield patch), and patent foramen ovale closure. Intraoperative findings revealed diffuse pearly white plaques on the right atrial endocardium and a narrowed pulmonary annulus. The tricuspid valve was thickened, retracted, and with commissural fusion (Figure 1). Postoperatively, the patient reached NYHA functional class I status. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated well-functioning bioprosthetic valves with residual mild tricuspid stenosis, reduced right heart dimensions, and preserved biventricular function; these findings remained stable at the 3-month follow-up.

In October 2007, the patient underwent total right hepatic lobectomy, cholecystectomy, and right hemicolectomy. Histopathology confirmed a 12 × 12 × 6 cm malignant carcinoid tumor in the right hepatic lobe with mesenteric lymph nodes metastasis. Although the cecum was the suspected primary site, the definitive origin remained undetermined, as the resected colon specimen was negative for malignancy.

The patient was monitored regularly with serial 24-hour urinary 5-HIAA levels and annual abdominal computed tomography. After 8 disease-free years, recurrence was detected in 2015 (elevated 5-HIAA, new hepatic lesion). After surgical refusal, a 2016 carcinoid crisis prompted monthly octreotide therapy (Sandostatin long-acting release [LAR]; Novartis). In 2017, a recurrent tumor was resected via laparoscopic partial hepatectomy; pathology confirmed metastatic carcinoid, and Sandostatin LAR was resumed.

The patient remained stable under regular surveillance until June 2025. The patient's course was further managed with radiofrequency ablations for recurrent atrial flutter in 2017 and 2019, attributed to chronic right atrial remodeling. Cardiac follow-up was also maintained through regular visits at the cardiovascular surgery outpatient clinic and serial transthoracic echocardiography to assess prosthetic valve function (Videos 1 and 2). To date, the patient remains clinically stable with preserved right heart function and is at NYHA functional class I-II status.

Discussion

CHD is a paraneoplastic complication of neuroendocrine tumors, most commonly originating from the small intestine, and is characterized by right-sided valvular fibrosis due to chronic exposure to vasoactive substances, particularly serotonin. This leads to progressive tricuspid and pulmonary valve dysfunction and right heart failure, which is a major source of morbidity and mortality in affected patients.1,2

Valve replacement surgery is the only definitive therapy for severe CHD, and it has been shown to improve symptoms and functional status as well as survival, especially when performed in experienced centers with a multidisciplinary approach.4 Bioprosthetic valves are generally preferred given the limited life expectancy and the avoidance of long-term anticoagulation, although structural valve deterioration remains a concern, particularly in patients with persistently high serotonin levels.5

Long-term outcomes after valve replacement are influenced by preoperative cardiac status, tumor burden, and extent of metastatic disease. Patients with extensive liver or bone metastases, severe right ventricular dysfunction, or advanced heart failure have poorer survival rates.6 Perioperative management should include somatostatin analogs to reduce the risk of carcinoid crisis, and careful timing of surgery is critical to balance cardiac and oncologic factors.2

Key long-term outcome studies and case series consistently show that the median survival after right heart double-bioprosthetic valve replacement for CHD is approximately 2 to 3 years, with rare cases surviving beyond 1 decade. In a cohort of 60 patients, the maximum reported survival was 21 years, but the median was 2.2 years; combined tricuspid and pulmonary valve replacement yielded better survival than tricuspid alone (median: 3.0 vs 0.9 years).7 Larger series and meta-analyses have reported similar findings, with median postoperative survival ranging from 2.9 to 3 years and 5-year survival rates around 34%.6,8 Ten-year survival is uncommon, and most deaths are related to tumor progression rather than valve dysfunction.4,8

Bioprosthetic valve durability is generally adequate relative to the limited oncologic life expectancy, with structural valve deterioration occurring in a minority of cases, typically within 1 to 2 years in patients with persistently high serotonin levels.5,7 Reoperation for bioprosthetic degeneration is rare, and most patients experience significant improvement in functional status postoperatively.4,7

Bioprosthetic valve degeneration in CHD is primarily driven by persistent exposure to high-circulating serotonin, leading to accelerated fibrosis and structural valve deterioration. The risk of degeneration is highest in patients with elevated urinary 5-HIAA and serum serotonin levels, especially when tumor burden is uncontrolled.5,7,9 Clinical series have reported that bioprosthetic valves may fail within 1 to 2 years in patients with ongoing serotonin excess, with freedom from structural valve deterioration at 4 years as low as 43%.5

Serotonin control is critical for bioprosthetic valve durability. Perioperative and long-term use of somatostatin analogs is standard to suppress serotonin secretion and reduce the risk of carcinoid crisis and valve degeneration.3,4,10

The North American Neuroendocrine Tumor Society recommends individualized prosthesis selection, favoring bioprosthetic valves for most patients owing to the avoidance of anticoagulation, but mechanical valves may be considered in those with persistently high serotonin levels unresponsive to medical therapy.10 Early and aggressive serotonin control, combined with multidisciplinary management, is associated with improved bioprosthetic valve durability and long-term outcomes.3,7,10

A 17-year follow-up after right heart double-bioprosthetic valve replacement is exceptionally long and exceeds typical published survival data, highlighting both the durability of bioprosthetic valves and the importance of careful patient selection and multidisciplinary care.

Conclusions

We report a rare case of carcinoid heart disease with right heart double-bioprosthetic valve replacement in a patient with metastatic primary ileal neuroendocrine tumor. Multidisciplinary management, including surgery, somatostatin therapy, and vigilant follow-up, enabled 17 years of stable cardiac and oncologic outcomes.
