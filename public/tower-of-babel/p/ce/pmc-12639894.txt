An evidence-informed Delphi study of ambulatory care sensitive conditions in China: a policy tool to assess primary care performance

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We conducted a systematic literature search to identify studies on the development of the ACSCs lists, covering MEDLINE (via PubMed), Web of Science, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) from inception to September 15, 2025, without language restrictions. Search terms included “ambulatory care sensitive”, “primary care sensitive”, “avoidable hospitali∗” and “preventable hospitali∗”. We supplemented the search through manual reference screening. Existing evidence indicates that multiple countries and institutions — including the United States, Spain, the United Kingdom, Australia, Brazil, Canada, New Zealand, Germany, Switzerland, Portugal and OECD member states — have developed ACSCs lists, typically comprising approximately 20 conditions. These lists are widely utilized as healthcare quality indicators to inform policy. However, China currently lacks a standardized ACSCs list adapted to its unique healthcare context and evolving medical needs.

Added value of this study

Our study develops the first ACSCs list specifically tailored to China, providing a tool for healthcare performance evaluation and policy development. By combining evidence-based medicine with a modified Delphi approach, we systematically evaluated existing international lists to create this policy tool. The resulting ACSCs list features two versions (core and general conditions) to optimize both international comparability and local adaptability across varying primary care capabilities in China. Unlike most international ACSCs lists that rely primarily on expert consensus, this study innovatively incorporates evidence evaluation prior to expert consultation, thereby enhancing scientific validity and practical applicability while reducing reliance on extensive expert panels. This experience provides a transferable model for other health systems facing similar challenges.

Implications of all the available evidence

The ACSCs list developed in this study provides Chinese policymakers with a standardized tool for healthcare quality improvement and system optimization. It specifically assesses primary care performance in delivering timely and effective services, establishing foundations for evidence-based policymaking. By replacing reliance on foreign ACSCs lists (e.g., AHRQ/OECD) with China-specific criteria, this tool could help to generate locally relevant evidence to inform decision-making. Given China's current hospitalization rates exceeding 20%, it enables identify avoidable hospitalizations to guide targeted service improvements by healthcare providers and policymakers.

Introduction

The concept of ambulatory care sensitive conditions (ACSCs), defined as health conditions that are preventable or manageable through timely and effective ambulatory care to reduce the risks of hospitalizations,1, 2, 3 has gained global attention in healthcare research and policy evaluation. ACSCs serve as an important policy tool by providing standardized indicators to assess healthcare accessibility and primary care effectiveness. Internationally, ACSCs hospitalization rates are widely adopted as healthcare performance measures in many developed countries. This is evidenced by their inclusion within measurement frameworks, such as the U.S. Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ)'s Prevention Quality Indicators (PQIs) and the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)'s Health Care Quality Indicators (HCQI).4,5

Initially, ACSCs were used to identify community and population healthcare needs, often conceptualized as issues of service accessibility due to challenges in resource allocation. The U.S. Institute of Medicine (IOM) subsequently recommended using hospitalization for ACSCs to measure primary care accessibility and reflect health system performance.6 Unlike in the US, in many European countries (e.g., the UK, Germany), where primary care is universally accessible and free, ACSCs hospitalization rates are primarily used to assess service quality.7,8 The rising hospitalization rates for ACSCs typically reflect systemic barriers to healthcare access, particularly affecting vulnerable populations. This phenomenon often stems from imbalanced resource distribution, where over-reliance on costly inpatient services coexists with underfunded ambulatory care. Strategic management of ACSCs through enhanced ambulatory interventions offers a crucial opportunity to optimize resource allocation. By shifting focus to preventive ambulatory care, health systems may reduce avoidable hospitalizations while improving efficiency, reducing unnecessary inpatient expenditures, and alleviating resource strain.9,10

From the healthcare-quality and patient-outcome perspectives, effective management of ACSCs is critical for improving patient experiences and health recovery. Timely and accurate ambulatory care can alleviate patient suffering, shorten illness duration, and reduce complication risks, thus significantly enhancing the quality of life. Beyond direct clinical benefits, optimized ACSCs management enhances healthcare system performance by fostering care continuity and strengthening service coordination. This approach facilitates seamless integration between ambulatory and inpatient care pathways, ultimately creating a more efficient and patient-centered healthcare delivery model.11,12

Current research and implementation of ACSCs in China remain in early developmental stages.13 While international organizations including the AHRQ and the OECD have developed standardized ACSCs lists (comprising 16 and five conditions respectively) that are widely adopted globally,5, 6, 7,14,15 these lists demonstrate limited applicability to China's unique healthcare context. Significant variations in healthcare delivery systems, disease burden, and sociocultural factors necessitate the development of a tailored ACSCs list in China.16, 17, 18 Specifically, China's tiered referral system struggles with under-resourced primary care: most facilities lack diagnostic capabilities and comprehensive services for common physical and mental conditions,19 while 84% of patients bypass them for hospitals due to distrust or limited understanding.20 Additionally, primary facilities may lack medications prescribed by higher-level hospitals,21 and conditions like pneumonia generally require hospital care given limited treatment capability.22 In response to this need, our study applies the World Health Organization (WHO) evidence-based clinical practice guideline methodology,23 incorporating multiple research strategies to develop a scientifically validated and operationally feasible ACSCs list specifically designed for China's healthcare system.

Methods

This study employed an integrated approach combining evidence-based medicine and a modified Delphi method, following our pre-published protocol.18 Based on the protocol, we established a 69-member multidisciplinary ACSCs working group (Supplementary Materials 1) comprising clinical specialists, general practitioners, and health policy experts to ensure comprehensive perspective integration. While adhering to the original protocol, we incorporated context-specific adaptations during implementation, particularly expanding working group sizes to strengthen validity while preserving scientific objectives. The research process comprised three sequential phases: (i) Scoping Review: We conducted a scoping review to systematically map existing international ACSCs lists and identify potential conditions. (ii) Systematic Evidence Synthesis: We performed a systematic review to evaluate and synthesize available evidence for these potential conditions within China's healthcare context. (iii) Delphi Consensus Process: We implemented a two-round Delphi survey to achieve consensus on the final ACSCs list that best aligns with China's healthcare priorities.

The ACSCs selection process followed established criteria, primarily based on the Solberg and Weissman framework, which requires: (i) Evidence-based inclusion rationale; (ii) Precise disease definition and coding standards; (iii) Public health relevance (≥1 hospitalization per 10,000 population); (iv) Preventability through ambulatory care interventions; and (v) Clear hospitalization criteria when necessary.24,25 For China's ACSCs list, we implemented two guiding principles: (i) Clinical significance: We prioritized conditions with high prevalence, substantial disease burden, and significant quality-of-life impact, particularly those requiring prompt ambulatory management. (ii) Management rationality: We selected conditions demonstrating both (a) ambulatory-manageable progression in the context of incurability, and (b) mandatory 48-h hospitalization referral via China's tiered healthcare system upon meeting inpatient criteria during exacerbation.

Scoping review

We systematically searched MEDLINE (via PubMed), Scopus, and Web of Science databases for studies published between 1990 and 2022 that related to the development of ACSCs lists. To check for newly published studies, a supplemental search was performed in 2025. Search terms were combinations of terms related to ACSCs (e.g., “ambulatory care sensitive”, “primary care sensitive”, “nursing home sensitive”, “avoidable hospitali∗”, “preventable hospitali∗”, “avoidable hospital admission∗”, “preventable hospital admission∗”). The search strategy is shown in Supplementary Materials 2. We also conducted supplementary searches of websites from leading healthcare quality organizations (e.g., WHO, OECD, AHRQ, National Institute for Health and Care Excellence [NICE], and Canadian Institute for Health Information [CIHI]), Google Scholar (top 100 records reviewed), and reference lists of included publications to identify additional relevant studies.

Studies and reports on ACSCs list development published in English were included. From these publications, we systematically extracted the following information: publication year, country or institution of origin, listed disease names, condition counts, and corresponding ICD-10 codes. Following data extraction, we consolidated the disease information and applied a frequency threshold, primarily excluding conditions that appeared in fewer than three different lists, to generate a preliminary ACSCs list.

Systematic evidence synthesis

For the preliminary ACSCs list, all potential conditions were systematically evaluated through the application of the “6S model”.26 To gather supporting evidence, we conducted comprehensive searches in MEDLINE (via PubMed), China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Wanfang Data databases, focusing on clinical practice guidelines, consensus statements, and review articles published within the last five years. Priority was given to high-quality Chinese guidelines and consensus statements, particularly those designed specifically for primary care settings.27 When there was insufficient Chinese evidence, international guidelines and consensus statements were included, supplemented by relevant high-quality reviews.

Evidence synthesis focused on disease definitions, classifications, as well as epidemiological characteristics, disease burden, clinical characteristics, and management policies. To better inform the expert consultation, we integrated data from the China Health Statistics Yearbook,28 including hospitalization rates, average length of stay, and average cost per hospitalization. These data were synthesized into comprehensive evidence summary tables for each potential condition, thereby facilitating informed decision-making during the Delphi consensus process.

Delphi consensus process

Prior to the formal survey, we conducted a pilot phase using convenience sampling to recruit frontline healthcare professionals. This pilot phase aimed to screen out conditions clearly unsuitable for China's healthcare context from the potential ACSCs list based on predefined guiding principles. It ensured the final list aligned with China's clinical realities and streamlined subsequent expert consultations.

We convened a multidisciplinary panel of experts representing diverse geographic regions and healthcare institutions across China to evaluate the preliminary ACSCs list. The consultation employed a hybrid online-offline format, conducting one-on-one discussions either via Tencent Meeting for virtual sessions or in-person for offline interviews. Prior to the evaluation, all participants received detailed information regarding the study's background, research objectives, and scientific significance. For each potential condition, experts were requested to indicate their opinions (“agree”, “disagree”, or “uncertain”), assess its clinical significance within China's healthcare context, and evaluate its feasibility for management in ambulatory care settings. Additionally, experts were encouraged to propose any other new conditions that met the criteria. Following the consultation, we systematically analyzed the response data and documented expert commentaries. Applying established consensus criteria, conditions receiving ≤50% agreement were excluded from further consideration. For any newly suggested conditions, those achieving ≥80% agreement were advanced to the second-round evaluation.

We conducted an online survey of primary care experts from the Chinese Medical Association's General Practice Branch using the Wenjuanxing platform (https://www.wjx.cn/app/survey.aspx). These experts possessed extensive experience in primary care and common disease management. The refined questionnaire specifically assessed both retained and newly proposed conditions, with experts providing definitive opinions on each condition's inclusion. Following data aggregation, we finalized the ACSCs list by incorporating expert feedback. Conditions achieving ≥80% agreement across both consultation rounds were included in the final list and further classified as either “core conditions” (≥90% agreement) or “general conditions” (80%–89% agreement). Should conditions exhibit significant disagreement between rounds (e.g., low agreement in the first-round but high agreement in the second), an additional targeted survey would be conducted as needed to resolve these discrepancies. Core conditions, reflecting high consensus, are critical to China's ACSCs list, characterized by high incidence, significant burden, and major impact on quality of life. They are prioritized in ambulatory care for disease progression control and complication prevention. General conditions, while meeting basic inclusion criteria, demonstrate relatively lower consensus among experts.

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Fourth Hospital and West China School of Public Health, Sichuan University (Grant No. Gwll2023120). Informed consent was obtained from all participants, with written or verbal consent secured prior to the first-round interviews and implied consent confirmed upon completion of the online survey.

Role of funding source

The funders had no involvement in the study design, data collection, data analysis, interpretation, or writing.

Results

This study developed the first evidence-based China's ACSCs list through a rigorous three-phase methodology involving scoping review, systematic evidence synthesis, and Delphi consensus process. Scoping review

Our scoping review of international ACSCs literature and reports identified 16 representative lists encompassing over 60 distinct conditions. The internationally representative ACSCs lists provided in Supplementary Materials 3 (Tables S1–S17), with development methods detailed in Supplementary Materials 4 (Table S18). Through rigorous analysis and application of predefined selection criteria, we primarily excluded conditions cited fewer than three times across these sources, resulting in a preliminary ACSCs list of 38 potential conditions.

Systematic review

The 38 potential conditions underwent rigorous evaluation through a systematic review of more than 700 high-quality clinical guidelines, consensus statements, and review articles. Subsequently, a comprehensive evidence summary table was created, covering disease definitions, classifications, epidemiological characteristics, disease burden, clinical characteristics, and management policies (e.g., hypertension in Supplementary Materials 5). To strengthen our assessment, we integrated critical health statistics from the China Health Statistics Yearbook, including hospitalization rates, average length of stay, and average cost per hospitalization (Supplementary Materials 6, Tables S19–S21). These quantitative indicators provided robust, data-driven insights into disease burden and healthcare resource utilization, forming an evidence-based foundation for condition screening and prioritization.

Delphi consensus process

From April to May 2024, we conducted a pilot survey employing convenience sampling methodology. Eight healthcare professionals evaluated the 38 potential conditions, excluding 17 conditions unsuitable for China's ACSCs list. Exclusions included: seven conditions that typically require hospitalization under China's current clinical practices standards (i.e., pneumonia, ulcer perforation, pyelonephritis, gangrene, appendiceal perforation, hip fracture, and convulsions); three conditions that rarely necessitate hospitalization (i.e., constipation, migraine/acute headache, and oral diseases); three conditions that infrequently present as primary diagnoses (i.e., malnutrition, alcohol-related disorders, and hypokalemia); and four conditions with unique clinical characteristics (i.e., dementia, low birth weight, cerebrovascular disease, and peripheral vascular disease). Following the pilot survey, 21 potential conditions were retained for further evaluation (Table 1).

The first Delphi round (from July to October 2024) enlisted 24 multidisciplinary experts from diverse Chinese regions. Participants demonstrated balanced gender distribution, geographic representation, and professional seniority (83.3% clinical/general medicine, 16.7% public health). The characteristics are detailed in Supplementary Materials 7 (Table S22). The first-round response analysis excluded five conditions with low consensus: angina pectoris (50.0%), rheumatic heart disease (33.3%), pelvic inflammatory disease (50.0%), cellulitis (33.3%), and otolaryngologic infections (50.0%). Conversely, two additional conditions—primary osteoporosis and chronic kidney disease—were incorporated based on expert recommendations (Table 1).

The second Delphi round (from November to December 2024) engaged 29 general practice experts and achieved a 100% response rate through diligent follow-up procedures. Participants maintained balanced gender distribution, geographic representation, and professional seniority (79.3% general hospitals, 20.7% community health centers). The characteristics are detailed in Supplementary Materials 7 (Table S23). From the 18 potential conditions retained after the first Delphi round, four failing to reach consensus (<80% agreement in both rounds) were excluded, resulting in 14 conditions for the China's ACSCs list: five core conditions (i.e., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD], bronchial asthma, hypertension, chronic kidney disease [CKD], and diabetes mellitus) and nine general conditions (Table 1). Following the prevailing classification framework in academia, the final list was categorized into 12 chronic ACSCs, one acute ACSC, and one infectious ACSC. Discussion

Characteristics of China's ACSCs list

This study developed the first evidence-based ACSCs list in China comprising 14 conditions. Internationally, ACSCs lists typically include approximately 20 conditions tailored to national demographic structures, socioeconomic factors, and healthcare system characteristics. Western countries with aging populations and well-established chronic disease management systems often prioritize geriatric conditions. For instance, Canada's 2009 elderly-focused list adds fall-related injuries and sepsis,32 while Portugal's 2020 adult-oriented version incorporates cervical and colorectal cancers, thromboembolism, and elective pregnancy termination.37 Historically, earlier ACSCs lists often included a greater emphasis on infectious diseases and acute conditions, reflecting contemporary medical priorities.6,14,25 Notably, China's current ACSCs list includes approximately 30% fewer conditions than most international lists, with exclusions such as pneumonia and pyelonephritis.

Among the 14 conditions included in China's ACSCs list, twelve (85.7%) are categorized as chronic diseases. This composition reflects the nation's growing chronic disease burden driven by population aging and lifestyle transitions. Prevalent conditions such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and COPD appear consistently across international and China's ACSCs lists. This convergence reflects shared global health challenges where long-term ambulatory management is essential to delay progression and prevent complications. Notably, the ACSCs list in China incorporates CKD and primary osteoporosis.39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45 The 78th World Health Assembly officially recognized CKD as a global priority non-communicable disease in 2025.46 Globally, CKD prevalence reached 9.1% in 2017,47 comparable to China's adult prevalence of 8.2% (approximately 82 million cases based on national census data).48 Projected to become a top-five cause of years of life lost by 2040,49 CKD exhibits insidious onset, complex progression, and poor prognosis, compounded by low patient awareness.47 However, early intervention enables effective prevention and treatment. A tiered healthcare system with primary care managing stable patients and hospitals treating complex cases facilitates early detection, prevention, and standardized management. Similarly, osteoporosis affects 13.6%–29.5% of China's population,50 with significant gender disparity (women 20.6% vs men 5.0% in ≥40-year-olds).51 This growing public health challenge, exacerbated by population aging, is a leading cause of disability and mortality in the elderly, with substantial economic burdens. Primary care institutions, with their chronic disease management experience, serve as the frontline for osteoporosis prevention and collaborate with hospitals in coordinated services. These two conditions were prioritized based on strong evidence for preventability and manageability, fully meeting ACSCs inclusion criteria.24,25

Among the 12 chronic ACSCs, the five core conditions—COPD, asthma, hypertension, CKD, and diabetes—exhibit particularly high prevalence and substantial diseases burden in China. Significantly, these conditions achieved over 90% agreement in both Delphi rounds, underscoring their critical importance for ambulatory care optimization. For instance, COPD requires regular outpatient monitoring (e.g., pulmonary function tests and inhaler technique education) to prevent exacerbations and reduce hospitalization risks.52,53 Similarly, hypertension demands continuous blood pressure control and lifestyle counseling.54, 55, 56 Both conditions typically depend on well-organized ambulatory care systems.

Gastroenteritis, as a representative of acute ACSCs in China, demonstrates the need for strengthened ambulatory care capabilities to ensure timely diagnosis and effective treatment during disease outbreaks. Meanwhile, influenza (classified as an infectious ACSC) highlights the vital surveillance role of ambulatory care in public health systems. Notably, the core conditions in China's ACSCs list show substantial alignment with those in OECD and major international lists, ensuring comparability across regions and health systems. Although our final list contains fewer conditions (14 ACSCs) than most international counterparts (typically about 20 ACSCs), this reflects a strategic methodological prioritization of diagnostic specificity over coverage sensitivity during this initial development phase.

Application of China's ACSCs list

Given the current absence of standardized policy tools to evaluate timely and effective healthcare delivery in China, ACSCs offer a viable measurement approach. This approach is grounded in the principle that hospitalization for ACSCs can reveal primary care deficiencies in ambulatory services, emphasizing preventable admissions through early interventions.57,58 ACSCs thus represent a paradigm shift from disease treatment to prevention and from medical services to health promotion.16 Elevated regional ACSCs hospitalization rates may indicate: (i) Insufficient primary care access or quality; (ii) Inequitable resource allocation; and/or (iii) Suboptimal system efficiency as evidenced by inappropriate hospitalizations.59,60 Particularly relevant to China's ongoing healthcare system reform. In this context, where primary care capacity is developing but requires strengthening, ACSCs hospitalization may provide evidence-based guidance for targeted policy improvements.61

At the healthcare delivery level, ACSCs hospitalization rates serve as key performance indicators for health systems, comprehensively evaluating core dimensions of accessibility, quality, coordination, and efficiency.3,57,62 The implementation of the ACSCs list promotes tiered healthcare delivery by clarifying clinical responsibilities across institutional levels and establishing standardized referral pathways to enhance care continuity. Primary care enhancement strategies, such as clinician training, technological upgrades, and process optimization can improve ACSCs management and reduce avoidable hospitalizations. Furthermore, developing integrated ambulatory services and closed-loop care models for ACSCs may enhance treatment efficiency, patient adherence, and clinical outcomes.

Currently, insufficient public trust in primary care frequently results in patient bypass to higher-level hospitals. Effectively reducing hospitalization for ACSCs requires standardized admission criteria, enhanced outpatient services across all hospital levels, and insurance payment reforms to incentivize preventive care. China is presently advancing the integration of medical care and disease prevention. In this context, the ACSCs list could guide payment reforms by incentivizing healthcare delivery to optimize payment systems and strengthen fund supervision. Given primary care's strength in managing initial ACSCs cases, piloting capitation in selected regions may encourage comprehensive health management. This would be achieved by monitoring hospitalization changes for ACSCs to precisely calculate fund surpluses under the “retained savings” mechanism, thereby incentivizing proactive health management. Such measures would enhance prevention effectiveness and optimize fund utilization. Simultaneously, ACSCs-performance evaluation would promote standardized clinical processes and rational resource allocation in hospitals, supporting evidence-based, cost-effective care. These measures collectively improve healthcare value while meeting insurance demands for high-quality services. Therefore, the development of ACSCs in China is a key step toward value-based healthcare.63

However, calculating ACSCs hospitalization requires careful attention. Given asymmetric hospitalization distribution (with some conditions predominating), relying solely on overall hospitalization rates may lead to representational bias. This occurs when overall rates primarily reflect high-prevalence conditions while masking patterns of less common but important diseases or underlying primary care issues. We suggest analyzing major contributing conditions and their proportional impacts, especially through subgroup analyses. When comparing overall rates across regions or time periods, caution is essential as differences in disease composition and relative weights may significantly affect interpretation, even with similar or different overall rates.

In addition, diseases can be classified into four categories based on their current ACSCs status and local primary care management capacity (Supplementary Materials 8, Figure S2). For policymakers, providers, or researchers prioritizing sensitivity (systematically identifying potential issues), broader ACSCs inclusion may be preferable. Conversely, adopting a more focused ACSCs scope improves specificity, allowing more accurate detection of severe and confirmed problems.

Strengths, limitations, and considerations

China's ACSCs list developed in this study comprises two versions (core and general conditions), balancing international comparability with adaptability to regional variations in primary care capacity across China. For policymakers and researchers, the comprehensive general list optimizes sensitivity in problem detection, while the targeted core list enhances specificity for identifying critical systemic issues. Unlike existing international ACSCs lists mainly developed through the Delphi method, modified Delphi method, and nominal group techniques,6,8,14,29,31, 32, 33,37 with some studies lacking clear documentation of expert consensus process. This study adapted an evidence-based guideline methodology, integrating localized evidence with expert consensus. By synthesizing the best available evidence for each potential disease prior to expert consensus, this approach maximizes scientific validity and practical applicability while minimizing reliance on extensive expert resources. It is particularly suitable for large countries with significant inter-regional disparities, such as China. In the first Delphi round, detailed one-on-one interviews were conducted for each potential disease, which was very beneficial for subsequent clarification of the inclusion and exclusion of diseases and codes. And the deliberate inclusion of general practitioners in the second Delphi round guaranteed the list's relevance to China's primary care realities. Their expertise in primary care delivery, integrated disease management, and resource coordination was critical to this alignment. Although a small-scale pilot survey using convenience sampling was conducted before these two formal rounds, which may present certain limitations, it was crucial for enhancing the efficiency of the following formal investigation. Of particular importance is that this study exhibits strong methodological and empirical generalizability. China's experience provides a transferable model for health systems in Asia and other developing countries.

However, this study also has limitations. Our ACSCs list primarily uses 2- to 3-digit ICD-10 codes to ensure nationwide applicability across regions of varying diagnostic coding practices, while retaining potential for finer-grained adaptation as needed. In addition, hospitalization for ACSCs requires careful interpretation as health system performance measures. Evidence indicates that these hospitalizations reflect multidimensional factors extending beyond care quality alone.64 Administrative data sources (e.g., insurance claims, hospitalization records) provide advantages including large samples, wide coverage, and longitudinal tracking. However, they often lack clinical and contextual details such as disease severity, social determinants, and patient preferences, which are important for accurate ACSCs hospitalization evaluation.64,65 Extensive evidence confirms that ACSCs hospitalizations are influenced by complex interactions between individual, environmental, and health system factors. Therefore, appropriate data interpretation must avoid directly equating hospitalization rates with healthcare quality unless population-level analyses and confounder adjustment are performed. It also needs to consider inherent surveillance lag, since measurement after hospitalization may delay timely ambulatory interventions.

For future ACSCs management, two priorities should be emphasized: (i) Intelligent utilization of hospitalization data through advanced analytics to develop early warning systems for high-risk patient identification and targeted ambulatory interventions; and (ii) Dynamic updating mechanisms for ACSCs lists involving periodic revisions based on evolving disease patterns and therapeutic advances, while maintaining diagnostic code alignment with international standards.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study represents the first evidence-based development of China's ACSCs list, filling a critical research gap in health services research. It provides policymakers with a scientifically grounded tool to enhance healthcare quality and system efficiency, while also laying foundation for subsequent policy efforts. Meanwhile, the experience offers a transferable model for other health systems facing similar challenges. Future research will validate the list's real-world effectiveness and implement periodic updates based on practical application and medical advances, thereby facilitating continuous improvement in China's healthcare system.
