Distinct Genetic Profiles Associated With Subretinal Drusenoid Deposits and Cardiovascular Risk in Age-Related Macular Degeneration
Authors: Natan Lishinsky-Fischer, Shlomit Jaskoll, Adi Kramer, Michelle Grunin, Sarah Elbaz-Hayoun, Batya Rinsky, Itay Chowers, Jaime Levy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeSubretinal drusenoid deposits (SDDs) are increasingly recognized as a distinct phenotype in age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and may be linked to cardiovascular disease (CVD). This study aims to explore whether AMD patients with SDDs carry a distinct genetic profile related to CVD risk compared to those without SDDs.MethodsIn a retrospective cohort of 459 AMD patients with genotyping and spectral-domain OCT data, we annotated SDD status and extracted cardiovascular diagnoses and procedures from electronic medical records. Fifty-two AMD-associated single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were used to calculate weighted genetic risk scores (GRSs), and minor allele frequencies (MAFs) were compared between groups. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to assess clustering by SDD status within CVD subgroups.ResultsPatients with SDDs exhibited distinct MAFs in several SNPs, including CFH, ARMS2, COL4A3, and ARHGAP21. Certain variants were more strongly associated with cardiovascular subtypes in patients with SDDs compared to those without. GRSs related to lipid metabolism and complement pathways were higher among SDD-positive patients within selected CVD subgroups. PCA suggested modest but significant separation between genetic profiles of patients with and without SDDs.ConclusionsAMD patients with SDDs show distinct genetic signatures potentially relevant to cardiovascular health. These findings suggest that SDDs may represent a genetically and systemically unique AMD phenotype. Further investigation is warranted to understand the shared pathophysiology and potential for systemic risk stratification.Translational RelevanceIntegrating OCT phenotyping and genomic profiling in AMD may uncover systemic disease associations with implications for precision medicine.

Introduction

Subretinal drusenoid deposits (SDDs) and drusen are two clinically distinguishable manifestations of age-related macular degeneration (AMD).1 SDDs, also known as retinal pseudodrusen, differ from drusen with respect to their anatomical location and biological composition, and their presence is commonly considered a risk factor for late-stage AMD.2

Recent studies suggest a possible link between the presence of SDDs and cardiovascular disease (CVD). For example, Ledesma-Gil et al.3 found that 41.2% of patients with AMD and SDDs had high-risk vascular disease (HRVD) compared to only 6.8% of patients with AMD but without SDDs. In addition, we found that patients with SDDs have a higher incidence of undergoing percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty or a coronary atherectomy procedure compared to patients without SDDs.4 Moreover, patients with SDDs were reported to have a higher prevalence of CVD or stroke,5 as well as several valvular heart diseases and decreased systemic perfusion.6 Beyond epidemiologic observations, physiological data support a hypoperfusion mechanism. A dose-response relationship between decreasing cardiac index, valve insufficiency, and SDD prevalence has been demonstrated.6 Moreover, SDDs occur only downstream of moderate or greater internal carotid artery stenosis, highlighting a paired-organ model in which impaired perfusion on one side produces SDDs ipsilaterally.7 Together, these observations strongly implicate systemic and ocular hypoperfusion in SDD pathogenesis.

To date, ARMS2 (age-related maculopathy susceptibility 2) and HTRA1, both of which in complete linkage disequilibrium, is the only locus shown to be associated independently with both the manifestation of SDDs in patients with AMD and the development of certain forms of CVD.5,8 However, mounting evidence supports a model in which genetic risk and vascular dysfunction act as independent contributors. In multivariate analyses, only HRVD and homozygous ARMS2 genotype remain independent predictors of SDDs.5 A third physiological factor, hypoxemia, particularly in obstructive sleep apnea, has also been associated with SDDs.9 Both hypoperfusion and hypoxemia converge on photoreceptor hypoxia and mitochondrial damage, and thinning of the ellipsoid zone has been correlated with SDDs.10

In this study, we investigate the relationship between genetic risk, SDDs, and the presence of CVD in AMD. Unlike previously published questionnaire-based studies involving relatively small cohorts,3,5 we performed a retrospective cohort study involving patients drawn from a large patient pool. In addition, we integrated the patients’ electronic medical records (EMRs) data with their genetic data, providing a more detailed perspective regarding the potential underlying basis of CVD in patients with SDDs.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the ethics committee at Hadassah Medical Center, Jerusalem, Israel (HMO 382-19), and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The genetic data were obtained with consent and was also approved by the ethics committee at Hadassah Medical Center (22-03.08.07).

Inclusion Criteria

Patients with AMD, EMRs and genetic data. A dataset comprising volumetric OCT scans (SD-OCT Spectralis; Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) and corresponding EMRs was collected from the Department of Ophthalmology at Hadassah Medical Center. The data includes information from AMD patients aged 50 and older, gathered over a 13-year span from 2009 to 2022. All diagnoses and procedures in the EMRs were recorded using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9).

Exclusion Criteria

Patients with AMD who also presented with other ophthalmologic conditions were excluded from the study. These conditions included diabetic retinopathy, subretinal hemorrhage, pattern dystrophy, polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy, central serous chorioretinopathy, high myopia, or perifoveal exudative vascular anomalous complex.

OCT Annotations and Grading

OCT scans were annotated and graded as previously described.4 In brief, OCT scans of a 6 × 6 mm retinal area centered on the macula, averaging 37 B-scans per eye (768 × 512 × 1 pixels), were analyzed by two certified graders following a standardized protocol. Discrepancies were resolved by a senior retina specialist. Graders identified AMD biomarkers (e.g., drusen, SDDs, PED, SHRM, IRF, SRF, iRORA, and cRORA) and classified disease stages. Based on the quantity and distribution of these biomarkers, disease stages were classified according to the Beckman criteria and OCT findings. The classification included no AMD, early AMD, intermediate AMD, and late AMD (neovascular AMD or geographic atrophy).11–14

Genomic Analysis

Lastly, genetic samples obtained from the patients were analyzed using two concurrent platforms. The first platform was a custom Illumina chip designed by the International AMD Genomics Consortium (IAMDGC)15 at the Center for Inherited Disease Research (Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine, Baltimore, MD, USA), which included approximately 250,000 tagging markers for imputation and approximately 250,000 custom markers specific to AMD. The second platform was the Infinium Global Screening Array v3.0 (GSA) at the genomics core facility at the Technion–Israel Institute of Technology (Haifa, Israel), which has been extensively documented for its reliability regarding polygenic scores and genome imputation.16 The genetic data from each platform were submitted separately to the TOPMed Imputation Server for TOPMed v2 imputation17,18 using Eagle v2.4 for phasing and to Minimac4 for imputation.19 Imputation was completed for all autosomal chromosomes and for the pseudo-autosomal regions in the X chromosome, with no missing data blocks (10-MB chunks). After imputation, the datasets were merged into a single set with a consistent number of markers and identical matches to strand/allele for reference. A cross-chip comparison showed uniform variant imputation, confirming successful imputation on both platforms.

To test the hypothesis that patients with AMD and SDDs present with a different cardiovascular profile than patients with AMD but without SDDs, we divided our cohort to two groups: patients with AMD and SDDs and patients with AMD without SDDs. We then created a set of all diagnoses and procedures that were reported in the EMRs and extracted the CVD-related diagnoses procedures; These diagnoses and procedures are summarized in Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Table S2, respectively.

Data Preprocessing

We used the Python programming language (version 3.10; Python Software Foundation, Beaverton, OR, USA) and Jupyter Notebook (version 7.0.6; NumFOCUS, Austin, TX, USA) to process the data and perform calculations. Microsoft Excel 2016 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) was used to organize the data, while GraphPad Prism (version 9, GraphPad, San Diego, CA) was utilized for statistical analysis and graph generation. Using the Python Pandas package, we categorized the patients into two groups as previously described: those with AMD and SDDs, and those with AMD but without SDDs. Each patient was then assigned their corresponding diagnoses and procedures as recorded in their medical files.

Minor Allele Frequency and Genetic Risk Score Calculation

The minor allele frequency (MAF) for each variant was obtained for each patient group using the following equation: MAF=Totalnumberoftheminoralleleinthegroup2×Numberofpatientsinthegroup

In this equation, homozygous and heterozygous patients were addressed equally.20

The genetic risk score (GRS) was calculated as follows: GRSj=∑Giβi,where Gi is the log(odds ratio) for each allele based on the effect size that was identified in the fully conditioned analysis conducted in the previous study via the IAMDGC,15 and βi is the number of each risk allele coded as 0, 1, or 2.21

A GRS was calculated for each subject (j = 1, ..., n) based on a set of 52 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). This approach allowed us to account for the varying degrees of the contribution of genetic variants related to various molecular pathways to overall genetic risk, providing a more accurate representation of genetic predisposition in our subsequent genetic analyses.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square test and Fisher's exact test, and continuous variables were analyzed using the Student's t-test. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Principal Component Analysis

To explore genetic variation across patients and assess whether individuals with both SDD and a history of a cardiovascular disease exhibit a distinct genetic profile, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) on 52 SNPs using Python's built-in PCA package.22 SNP data were standardized before PCA using z-score normalization to ensure comparability across features. PCA was conducted to reduce dimensionality and summarize the major axes of genetic variation. We examined the proportion of variance explained by each principal component (PC) and used the cumulative variance to determine how many components captured most of the genetic variation in the dataset.

T-tests were conducted on each PC to assess statistical differences between individuals with SDDs and patients without SDDs. A logistic regression model was trained and evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation. Performance metrics included area under curve (AUC), F1-score, and accuracy, calculated from predicted probabilities and class labels.

Results

Descriptive Statistics and Statistical Analysis

A total of 554 subjects with AMD were sourced from the Department of Ophthalmology at Hadassah Medical Center. All patients had corresponding EMRs, SD-OCT annotations and MAF of the 52 SNPs based on a previously published genome-wide association study (GWAS). 95 patients were excluded according to the exclusion criteria. Among them nine with diabetic retinopathy, 28 with pattern dystrophy, 16 with subretinal hemorrhage, 35 with polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy, and seven with high myopia.

Our final cohort consisted of 459 patients with AMD; 62% of these patients (n = 285; 173 females and 112 males; mean age: 83.5 ± 7.4 years) had AMD with SDDs, and 38% (n = 174; 102 females and 72 males; mean age: 81.72 ± 8.0 years) had AMD without SDDs. Among patients with SDDs, 143 had unilateral and 142 had bilateral involvement. The number of patients with early, intermediate and late AMD was 24, 87, and 348, respectively. The number of patients with SDDs at each stage of AMD was 16 (67%), 64 (74%), and 205 (59%), respectively. There were no patients with SDDs and without drusen. Patients in the SDDs group were generally older (P = 0.02). We found no significant difference between groups with respect to gender, hypertension, hyperlipidemia and type 2 diabetes mellitus (P = 0.73, 0.21, 0.54, and 0.55, respectively). The precise ICD-9 diagnoses with their respective codes for hypertension, hyperlipidemia and type 2 diabetes are summarized in Supplementary Table S3.

The cohort was further stratified according to the cardiovascular-related diagnoses and procedures that were performed. The diagnoses were categorized into the following subgroups according to the ICD-9 classification: Acute rheumatic fever (codes 390–392), Chronic rheumatic heart disease (codes 393–398), Hypertensive disease (codes 401–405), Ischemic heart disease (codes 410–414), Diseases of pulmonary circulation (codes 415–417), Other forms of heart disease (codes 420–429), Cerebrovascular disease (codes 430-438), Diseases of arteries, arterioles, and capillaries (codes 440–449). The procedures were categorized into the following subgroups according to the ICD-9 classification: Operations on valves and septa of heart (code 35), Operations on vessels of heart (code 36), Other operations on heart and pericardium (code 37), Incision, excision, and occlusion of vessels (code 38), and Other operations on vessels (code 39). In addition, we included certain categories from the subgroup Procedures and Interventions, Not Elsewhere Classified (code 00), specifically Procedures on Blood Vessels (code 00.6). MAFs

For each patient, we extracted information regarding 52 SNPs based on a previously published GWAS.15 The MAFs for the two groups—AMD with SDDs and AMD without SDDs—are presented in Figure 1, revealing a significant difference in MAFs for the CFH rs570618, CFH rs61818925, RDH5/CD63 rs3138141, COL4A3 rs11884770, and PILRB/PILRA rs7803454. Table 2 summarizes the significantly different MAFs between patients with SDDs from each subgroup in Table 1 and those without SDDs. We then calculated odds ratios for each of the genetic variants that showed significant differences between AMD patients with SDDs and those without SDDs. These odds ratios were examined across a series of cardiovascular-related diagnostic and procedural subgroups defined by ICD-9 codes (Fig. 2). This approach allowed us to determine whether specific AMD-associated genetic variants were more strongly linked to categories of CVD. In practical terms, for every variant that distinguished SDD from non-SDD AMD, we evaluated how its frequency varied in patient groups who had ICD-9 codes for different groups of CVD. The resulting odds ratios indicate the strength and direction of association between each genetic variant and each cardiovascular subgroup. While we recorded AMD stage (early, intermediate, late) for all subjects, further stratification by AMD stage together with SDD status and cardiovascular diagnoses yielded subgroups with insufficient sample sizes for reliable statistical comparison; therefore stage-specific CVD associations were not evaluated.

Principal Component Exploratory Analysis

Groups such as “Operations on vessels of heart,” “Diseases of arteries, arterioles, and capillaries,” and “Cerebrovascular disease” showed modest classification performance and significant associations with several principal components, suggesting potential differences between individuals with and without SDDs. The top 10 principal components (PC1–PC10) summarize different patterns of correlated genetic variation among the analyzed SNPs. PC1 captures the largest proportion of variance, whereas subsequent PCs reflect progressively smaller, orthogonal patterns. Supplementary Table S4 presents logistic regression performance metrics (AUC, F1-score, accuracy) for predicting the presence of a diagnosis from each CVD group, alongside P-values from t-tests comparing PC1-PC10 between SDD and non-SDD individuals. For each cardiovascular subgroup, we evaluated whether the distribution of each PC differed between individuals with and without SDDs. Significant PCs therefore represent dimensions of genetic variation that discriminate SDD status within that subgroup, whereas non-significant components do not. The observation that different PCs emerge as significant across diagnostic groups suggests heterogeneity in the genetic patterns associated with SDDs in the context of different cardiovascular conditions. Supplementary Figure S1 illustrates the AUC and explained variance for “Cerebrovascular disease” and “Diseases of arteries, arterioles, and capillaries.”

Discussion

Here, we investigated the genetic profiles of AMD patients with SDDs and patients with AMD without SDDs, with respect to cardiovascular-related diagnoses and procedures. In our analysis, we focused on 52 genetic variants that were previously reported to be correlated with AMD.

A Genetic Correlation Between SDDs and CVD?

The genetic profile of AMD has been studied in detail. Notably, a 2016 GWAS from the IAMDGC that included 16,144 patients with AMD identified 52 independent risk variants for AMD in 34 loci.15 We consider that these 52 variants are relevant to our cohort due to the participation of Israeli patients with AMD, particularly patients at the Hadassah Medical Center, in the GWAS. Furthermore, a recent study involving Israeli patients with AMD confirmed that 31 of the 34 loci reported in the previous GWAS are significantly associated with AMD in this population.23

The notion that the presence of SDDs and the risk of CVD was recently addressed by Smith et al.24 and Ledesma-Gil et al.3 The authors proposed that patients with AMD with SDDs have an increased risk of insufficient ophthalmic artery perfusion, which can coexist with certain cardiovascular pathologies.3,24 The hypoperfusion theory suggests SDDs may signal not only local retinal changes but also broader disturbances in ocular and systemic microcirculation, and chronic reductions in blood flow may lead to low-grade ischemia and hypoxia in these tissues. Under such conditions, photoreceptor outer segment turnover becomes disrupted, as these cells require high levels of oxygen for continuous renewal,24 leading to metabolic waste products accumulation in the subretinal space and promoting the formation of SDDs. Persistent hypoxia can lead to cellular dysfunction and apoptosis of photoreceptors, contributing to AMD progression.

However, an alternative interpretation of the strong association between SDDs and HRVDs is that these relationships may reflect shared genetic susceptibility rather than a purely hemodynamic or hypoperfusion-driven mechanism.3 Under this model, common genetic risk factors could predispose individuals to both SDDs and specific HRVDs, potentially explaining the epidemiologic links reported in prior studies. This possibility does not exclude hypoperfusion as a contributory mechanism but highlights that genetic risk may be a primary or complementary pathway in the etiology of SDDs, particularly where HRVDs do not share a unified genetic etiology.

Direct physiological evidence further supports hypoperfusion as a major driver of SDDs. Fei et al.6 demonstrated a clear dose effect of decreased cardiac index and valve insufficiency on SDD prevalence. Similarly, Mordechaev et al.7 showed that SDDs occur only downstream of moderate or greater ICA stenosis, providing a highly persuasive within-patient control where the two paired ICAs serve as identical test beds except for perfusion differences. Such dose-dependent effects make a causal contribution of systemic hypoperfusion highly plausible. A more complete model of SDDs etiology must also include hypoxemia, as obstructive sleep apnea has been shown to be an independent risk for SDDs.9 Both hypoperfusion and hypoxemia ultimately converge on photoreceptor hypoxia and mitochondrial damage, consistent with reports of thinning of the ellipsoid zone in eyes with SDDs.10 If it becomes clear that hypoperfusion and genetics are driving the SDDs phenotype, the next step could be definitive treatment of hypoperfusion, as is under investigation for advanced AMD with ophthalmic artery angioplasty.25

The three HRVDs identified by Ledesma-Gil et al.,3 myocardial infarction, severe valve disease, and ischemic stroke with ICA stenosis, do not share a single established common genetic mechanism. Atherosclerotic disease is the major etiology for myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke but is not a primary driver of severe cardiac valve disease. This lack of a unified genetic etiology across HRVDs makes a purely genetic explanation less likely and maintains hypoperfusion as a plausible mechanistic link in many patients.

Our study used an ICD-9 category combining cardiac valve disease with congenital septal defects. Because these conditions have different pathophysiology, this classification limits our ability to isolate whether any identified genetic signals are specifically driven by valve disease, the most relevant HRVD in the hypoperfusion model. Future studies must disaggregate valve disease from septal defects to test whether genetic risk for SDDs is specifically linked to valve pathology or whether hypoperfusion remains the dominant pathway. A more complete model of SDDs etiology is therefore likely multifactorial: both genetic susceptibility and hypoperfusion may contribute to SDDs, and the relative contribution of each mechanism may differ across HRVDs, particularly for conditions without a shared genetic basis.

Importantly, Ledesma-Gil et al.3 suggested that the association between HRVDs and the presence of SDDs are clinically meaningful even without a proven mechanistic link. That is, the presence of SDDs alone can guide screening for serious vascular disease, and conversely, patients with HRVDs can be monitored for SDDs and AMD risk. While hypoperfusion may underlie SDD formation, this mechanistic uncertainty does not diminish the potential of retinal imaging and artificial intelligence (AI)–based models as tools for early detection and public health interventions.

Nevertheless, possible genetic links between SDDs and CVD risk are poorly understood. The ARMS2/HTRA1 locus is the most strongly associated with SDDs but is not known to drive CVD risk,5,8,24,26 and this finding is somewhat supported by our results. Specifically, we found that the ARMS2 rs3750846 variant was present in SDDs patients with a disease of the pulmonary circulation.

Some studies suggest that variants in the CFH (complement factor H) gene are linked to the development of both SDDs and coronary artery disease.26,27 In our cohort, patients with SDDs were more likely to carry the CFH rs61818925 variant compared to patients without SDDs. Particularly, variant CFH rs61818925 was more prevalent in patients with SDDs compared to patients without SDDs, with respect to diseases of the arteries, arterioles, and capillaries. It is worth noting that this variant is not among the most common or well-established AMD-associated CFH risk SNPs, such as Y402H (rs1061170) and I62V (rs800292).28 For the Y402H allele, the literature reported a reduced SDDs risk.29,30 In our cohort, the minor allele frequency of CFH rs61818925 was 0.78 in patients with SDD versus 0.50 in patients without SDD, with respect to patients with “Diseases of arteries, arterioles, and capillaries.” The elevated frequency in the SDD group may suggest that complement pathway involvement may be relevant for both SDDs and the vascular disease category examined, although the precise functional effects of these variants remain unclear.

A deeper observation of the MAFs in each subgroup of cardiovascular-related diagnoses and procedures revealed some interesting results. For instance, the ARHGAP21 rs12357257 variant was more frequent in AMD patients with SDDs compared to AMD patients without SDDs, with respect to the following subgroups: “Ischemic heart disease”, “Operations on vessels of heart” and “Other operations on heart and pericardium.” Although studies on ARHGAP21 are limited, the gene is highly expressed in cardiac tissue, and emerging data suggest that it may play a role in cardiac stress response,31 providing a biologically plausible link that warrants further study.

The reported associations between the presence of absence of SDDs with certain major forms of CVD raises the idea that SDDs and typical drusen may have distinct molecular pathological mechanisms. Thus, SDDs may represent a distinct retinal disease, rather than simply a manifestation of progressive AMD. This concept was first proposed by Thomson et al.,5 who argued that SDD and drusen pathways represent separate disease entities with different systemic, genetic, and lipid signatures. This model, in which homozygous ARMS2 and systemic hypoperfusion emerge as the only two independent risk factors, remains consistent with our findings and provides a framework for interpreting the genetic signals we observed.

Identifying the clinical and molecular characteristics of SDDs may represent an opportunity for cardiovascular risk stratification in AMD patients. If the apparent association between SDDs and CVD is confirmed, OCT-based identification of SDDs could potentially serve as a noninvasive marker for systemic vascular dysfunction. Conversely, given that any direction of causality remains uncertain, patients who experience major cardiovascular events may benefit from ophthalmic evaluation to detect or monitor AMD.

Study Limitations

Our study has several limitations that warrant discussion. First, we focused on 52 SNPs based on previously reported AMD-associated variants; therefore, a more comprehensive genome-wide approach could identify additional relevant loci. Second, we considered homozygous and heterozygous genetic variants equally in our analysis; however, from a molecular perspective, this decision is debatable. To this point, Thomson et al.,5 demonstrated that only homozygous ARMS2 and high-risk vascular disease remain significant predictors of SDDs in multivariate models. Third, although multiple testing correction methods such as Bonferroni or false discovery rate (FDR) are typically applied in genetic association studies, the combination of 52 SNPs and several stratifications across CVD subgroups would result in extreme conservatism and eliminate all signals; therefore, our findings should be viewed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Fourth, we were unable to assess whether the association between SDDs and cardiovascular disease varies by AMD stage (dry vs. neovascular or early/intermediate vs. late AMD) because cell sizes become too small after stratification, preventing stable estimation. Future studies with larger, balanced cohorts are needed to address this question. Last, although both ICD-9 procedures and ICD-9 diagnoses were analyzed, ICD-9 procedures may better reflect true disease burden, because diagnostic codes can be inconsistent or incomplete.

Conclusions

Here, we analyzed SD-OCT annotations, EMR-derived data, and genetic profiles for a large cohort of patients with AMD and identified genetic variants that differ between patients with SDDs who underwent a cardiovascular-related procedure and those without SDDs. who underwent the same procedure. These findings provide new insight into potential genetic and physiologic differences between AMD patients with and without SDDs and may help guide future studies examining links between retinal disease and cardiovascular health.
