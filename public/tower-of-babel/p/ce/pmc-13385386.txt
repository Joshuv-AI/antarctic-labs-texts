Multiple Genetic Shifts at the Crossroads of the Eastern Steppe and Loess Plateau Over 4,000 Years
Authors: Xin Chang, Yu Xu, Hongying Zhang, Qiang Ma, Hailiang Meng, Tingting Bai, Xiaomin Yang, Kun Wang, Jianxue Xiong, Panxin Du, Tianyou Bai, Hao Ma, Xiaoying Ren, Bangyan Wang, Chenshuang Sun, Baoshuai Zhang, Hetong Wen, Ke Wang, Kongyang Zhu, Rui Wang, Jiajing Zheng, Huangzhen Huang, Feng Luo, Li Jin, Chuan-Chao Wang, Shaoqin Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNingxia, located at a key crossroads between the Eurasian Steppe and East Asia, was an important corridor for cultural exchange and human migration, yet its genetic history has remained poorly understood. In this study, we sequenced 89 individuals from 23 archeological sites in Ningxia, dating from 4245 to 301 BP. Our findings show that late Neolithic inhabitants of Ningxia possessed ancestral components from the Yellow River, Ancient Northeast Asia, and the Tibetan Plateau. Over time, the region experienced major genetic shifts associated with historical migrations and political change. During the Western Zhou and Han periods (3.0-1.7kBP), expansions of Central Plains polities promoted the movement of Yellow River populations into Ningxia, influencing Ningxia’s genetic structure up to the Ming-Qing period (582-301BP). Individuals associated with the Eastern Zhou Northern Bronze Complex (2.7-2.0kBP) show mixture between Eurasian Steppe and Yellow River populations. In the Northern Dynasties and Sui-Tang periods (1.6-1.0kBP), Western Eurasian ancestry increased markedly, likely reflecting Silk Road mobility. Tangut groups of the Western Xia show close genetic ties to Di-Qiang-related populations from the upper Yellow River. Reconstructing the genetic history of Ningxia from the late Neolithic to Qing dynasty (4.2k-301BP), evidencing complex genetic dynamics shaped by historical processes.Subject terms: Population genetics, Archaeology, Biological anthropology

Introduction

Interactions and cultural exchanges among populations across Eurasia have been pivotal in shaping the region's civilizational landscape1,2. Recent archaeogenomic studies have revealed that crossroads regions—such as ancient Rome3, the Caucasus4,5, Xinjiang6, and the Hexi Corridor7—which geographically link distinct ecological and cultural units, acted as crucibles of human migration and admixture. The genetic histories of these areas depict a dynamic and complex picture, characterized by multiple waves and diverse sources of population integration or replacement. Furthermore, such processes of mixture may have been associated with specific migratory routes or exhibited sex-biased patterns7.

Ningxia, located in the inland northwest of China, lies at the convergence of the Eurasian Steppe and the Central Plains, as well as the Tibetan Plateau and the Mongolian Plateau. Throughout history, it has served as a vital corridor for cultural and population interactions between East and West8,9. Its unique geographical position has made it one of the crossroads in the civilizational framework of the Eurasia. How Ningxia responded to and integrated into broader waves of human migration across historical periods remains a subject for further investigation using genetic evidence.

The significance of Ningxia as a crucial East-West connection in Eurasia can be traced to the Shuidonggou culture (46–10 k BP) of the late Paleolithic period. Stone tools unearthed at its sites display characteristics of the Levallois technique, which are also observable in contemporaneous regions across Eurasia (e.g., Mongolia, Siberia, and areas further west). This implies trans-regional population movement or cultural exchange as early as tens of thousands of years ago10–13. By the late Neolithic period, the locally developed Caiyuan culture (a Neolithic culture indigenous to Ningxia, characterized by settled agriculture), which emerged around 4800 years ago14, and the subsequent Qijia culture (a transitional culture in northwestern China between the Neolithic and Early Bronze Age, renowned for its plain pottery and early copper metallurgy, 4.2–3.4k BP)15 that developed under its influence, further demonstrate the integration and evolution of regional cultures.

The historical trajectory of Ningxia during the Zhou dynasty (Western Zhou, 2996–2721 BP; Eastern Zhou, 2720–2206 BP, an early dynasty originating from the Central Plains of China) took place within the context of the interaction between Eastern and Western Eurasian civilizations16. In the early Western Zhou period, the discovery of the Yaoheyuan site in Ningxia represents the first powerful northwestward expansion of Central Plains agricultural civilization in the form of a political entity, facilitated by the enfeoffment system17. However, from the mid-to-late Western Zhou period onwards, in the context of climatic changes, the expansion of pastoralist groups across the Eurasian Steppe led to the arrival of people from the steppe and its periphery into agricultural areas18,19. Ningxia was situated at the crossroads where the region of nomadic pastoralism and settled agriculture converged. Archeologists excavated burials of the Eastern Zhou Dynasty that contained horse, cattle, and sheep heads and hooves as grave offerings, a distinctive characteristic of nomadic mortuary traditions in northern Asia20,21. This custom is found across regions such as the northern and western Mongolian Plateau and the Ili River valley in the Tianshan Mountains20,22. Accompanying burial items, such as animal-motif plaques, belt ornaments, and pickaxes, also exhibit strong steppe cultural characteristics, which are referred to as the Northern Bronze Complex23. During the Eastern Zhou period, the decline of the Yaoheyuan site and the rise of the Northern Bronze Complex reflect Ningxia’s transformation from an agricultural frontier outpost into an exchange hub integrated within the steppe cultural system and connecting the inner Eurasian landmass.

During the Han dynasty (2152–1730 BP), the Central Plains dynasty exerted strong governance over the northwest, establishing commanderies and counties in Ningxia24. Previous archaeogenomic studies on the Hexi Corridor region have revealed that Han-era migration policies toward the frontier led to shifts in ancestral composition7,25. Meanwhile, the Eurasian trade/travel routes, namely the Silk Road, were gradually thrived, transforming Ningxia into a key stronghold along its eastern section26,27. Nevertheless, the genetic landscape shaped by the large-scale influx of agricultural populations from the Central Plains and by the movement of people along trade routes remains underexplored.

During the Northern Dynasties and Sui-Tang periods (1564–1043 BP), the existence of Sogdian communities in southern Ningxia, as exemplified by the cemetery sites associated with the “Shi” surname families28, provides direct evidence that this trade-specialized ethnic group from the Amu Darya and Syr Darya River basins of Central Asia migrated eastward along the Eurasian trade/travel routes and established immigrant settlements29,30. These sustained east–west population movements might have reshaped the cultural landscape and genetic structure of Ningxia and the broader northwestern region of China31–33.

In 912 BP, the Western Xia dynasty, established by the Tangut people—an ethnic minority active in the upper Yellow River region—designated Xingqing Prefecture (present-day Yinchuan) as its capital and governed the Ningxia area for nearly two centuries34,35. The Tangut incorporated various cultural elements from Han, Tubo, Xianbei, and other groups during their formation and development34. Regarding the ethnic origins of Tangut, clear evidence in historical documents or genomic research is lacking to support the existing hypotheses tracing their origin to the Xianbei or the Qiang36,37.

Each historical period in Ningxia might be associated with a significant demographic event. This study generated and analyzed genome-wide data from 89 ancient humans, covering 23 archeological sites throughout Ningxia, with sample dates ranging from the late Neolithic to the Ming-Qing periods. It aims to answer the following questions: 1) the genetic profile of early populations in Ningxia, and 2) the impacts of historical cultural and population expansion events on Ningxia’s gene pool.

Results

Samples and Ancient DNA Generation

Eighty-nine ancient samples from the Ningxia region as following: Late Neolithic-Chalcolithic transition (Caiyuan Culture, n = 3; Qijia Culture, n = 4); Bronze to Iron Age (Western Zhou, n = 6; Eastern Zhou, n = 11); Han Dynasty (n = 27); Northern Dynasties to Sui-Tang periods (n = 25); Western Xia (n = 2); and later historical periods (Song 990-674 BP, n = 1; Yuan 679–582 BP, n = 3; Ming and Qing 582-38 BP, n = 7) (Supplementary Data 1A and 1D). Samples in this study yielded an average of 542,936 SNP sites using the 1240k panel (Supplementary Data 1A). Initially, we considered samples with more than 40,000 targeted SNPs from the 1,240k panel and modern human contamination levels below 3%, as determined by mitochondrial-based methods (Schmutzi and ContamMix) and X chromosome-based methods (ANGSD). Five samples were excluded due to insufficient SNP coverage (<40,000 targeted SNPs), and nine samples were identified as potentially contaminated (Supplementary Data 1A). For these contaminated samples, we limited the analysis to fragments displaying characteristics typical of ancient DNA damage. After this additional processing, one restricted sample met the contamination thresholds and was included in further analysis (Supplementary Data 1B).

We successfully obtained high-quality genetic data from 76 individuals. To assess genetic kinship, we employed three methods—READ, TKGWV2, and lcMLkin—and identified 10 pairs of individuals with relationships closer than second degree, detected by at least one method (Supplementary Data 1C). After filtering out related individuals, we retained 69 high-quality, unrelated ancient genomes (Fig. 1). This dataset represents the collection of ancient genomes from the Ningxia region. Based on radiocarbon dating and cultural affiliations, we categorized the samples into five sub-datasets: Neolithic, Zhou Dynasty, Han Dynasty, Northern Dynasties and Sui-Tang Dynasty, and post-Sui-Tang Dynasty (Supplementary Data 1D).

We initiated our analysis with principal component analysis (PCA) to examine the overall genomic structure of populations in the Ningxia region across different periods (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1A). The first principal component (PC1) clearly distinguished between eastern and western populations. Within the eastern cluster along PC1, the second principal component (PC2) further separated populations from northern and southern East Asia. The primary genetic structure of Ningxia populations exhibited eastern Eurasian characteristics, as indicated by their position on the right side of the PCA plot along PC1 (Fig. 2). Two distinct eastern populations—Yellow River farmers and Ancient Northeast Asian (ANA)-related groups—coexisted in this region since the Neolithic period and were differentiated along the PC2 component in the PCA plot (Fig. 2A). Additionally, during the Han Dynasty, an individual (I42418) deviated from the eastern cluster along PC1 (Fig. 2C), showing increased genetic affinity with western Eurasians. This suggests a potential integration of non-local ancestry during this period, a trend that intensified further during the Northern Dynasties and Sui-Tang Dynasty (Fig. 2D). These observations were corroborated by unsupervised ADMIXTURE analysis (Supplementary Figs. 1B and 2C). The genetic structure of Ningxia populations primarily consisted of four ancestral components: the Yellow River farmer-related dark green, the southern East Asian-related light green, the ANA-related dark blue, and the western Eurasian-related pink (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Different periods of the Ningxia populations exhibited varying combinations and proportions of these components. Alongside the PCA results, where populations from different periods occupied distinct positions in the PCA plot (Fig. 2), this evidence indicates that Ningxia populations were highly differentiated over time, reflecting the dynamic genetic history of Northwest China shaped by demographic influences from surrounding populations.

The Genetic Influence of Yellow River Farmers, Ancient Northeast Asians, and Tibetan Plateau Populations on Neolithic Ningxia

In this study, we examined the ancient genomes of five individuals from two Neolithic archeological sites in Northwest China, representing the Caiyuan and Qijia cultures (Supplementary Data 1D). By comparing these genomes with previously published data from ancient Eurasian populations, we found that the genetic structure of Neolithic populations in Ningxia predominantly exhibited Eastern Eurasian characteristics (Fig. 2A). To further dissect this characteristic, we conducted an additional PCA analysis featuring a triangular pattern, incorporating clines related to the Yellow River, Ancient Northeast Asian (ANA), and Southern East Asian populations (Supplementary Fig. 2A). This analysis revealed significant genetic differences between the two archeological sites and among individuals within the same Qijia culture site.

Individual Wutai_Caiyuan exhibited a distinct genetic structure. It was positioned between the Yellow River-related cline and the ANA-related cline in the PCA plot (Supplementary Fig. 2A) and displayed both dark green and dark blue components in the ADMIXTURE plot (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Wutai_Caiyuan could be modeled as deriving the majority of its ancestry from ANA populations (~80%), with an additional contribution from Tibetan Plateau populations (~20%) (Supplementary Data 2A). Remarkably, this individual also carried the Tibetan Plateau-specific adaptive EPAS1 (endothelial PAS domain protein 1) haplotype, which is associated with Tibetan Plateau adaptation (Supplementary Data 1F)38. This represents the first documented case of the adaptive EPAS1 variant in Late Neolithic individuals from the Yellow River Basin. Our findings support the qpAdm model, indicating it was not driven by overfitting, and suggest that Tibetan Plateau populations have a history of returning to the lowlands and mixing with local populations.

At the Shatang_Beiyuan site, associated with the Qijia culture, we identified two distinct genetic structures (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 2A, and Supplementary Data 1E). One group (Shatang_Beiyuan_Qijia), comprising three individuals, clustered along the genetic cline of Yellow River farmers and displayed a dark-green genetic component associated with Yellow River farmers in the ADMIXTURE plot. The other group (Shatang_Beiyuan_Qijia_2), comprising a single individual, was most closely related to the ANA populations in the PCA and ADMIXTURE plots. The quantitative cluster-based f3 analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3A), f4 analysis in the format of f4(Mbuti, References; Shatang_Beiyuan_Qijia_2/Shatang_Beiyuan_Qijia, China_YR_MN) (Supplementary Fig. 3B), and the estimation of ancestry proportions by qpAdm (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Data 2A) supported these qualitative results. The Shatang_Beiyuan_Qijia group was derived from Yellow River farmers, while the Shatang_Beiyuan_Qijia_2 individual was derived from ANA populations. These findings suggest that ANA populations had spread southward into the Yellow River Basin by at least ~4,000 years ago, coexisting with local Yellow River farmers, which may explain the previously observed admixture patterns between Yellow River farmers and ANA populations in the Yellow River Basin (China_Shimao_LN and China_Upper_YR_LN)39.

In summary, as a critical crossroads for population exchanges, the Central Plains, Mongolian Plateau, and Tibetan Plateau populations spread into the Ningxia region during the Neolithic period. Their interactions collectively contributed to the genetic pool of Neolithic populations in Ningxia.

The Emergence of YR_LN-Related Ancestry and the ANE-Related Ancestry Influx During the Zhou Dynasty

During the Zhou Dynasty, the Ningxia region experienced cultural influences from both steppe populations and those from the central plains, creating uncertainty about its role in demographic movements along the northwest frontier. To explore this, we analyzed four ancient genomes from the Yaoheyuan site dating to the Western Zhou Dynasty and nine ancient genomes from four other archeological sites from the Eastern Zhou Dynasty (Supplementary Data 1D and 1E).

Our analysis revealed that the Yaoheyuan_Western_Zhou group occupied a genetic cline associated with Yellow River farmers. However, they clustered with post-Middle Neolithic Middle Yellow River farmers (China_YR_LN and China_YR_LBIA) rather than with Middle Neolithic Middle Yellow River farmers (China_YR_MN) or local Late Neolithic populations, as seen in the PCA plot (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. 2B). In the ADMIXTURE analysis, Yaoheyuan_Western_Zhou exhibited a genetic profile similar to China_YR_LN and China_YR_LBIA, notably featuring an additional light-green component associated with southern East Asians, which was absent in China_YR_MN (Supplementary Fig. 1C). This was further supported by a significant negative Z value (Z < − 3) in the f4(Mbuti, Taiwan_Hanben_IA; Yaoheyuan_Western_Zhou, China_YR_MN) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3B). These findings suggest that the Yaoheyuan elite individuals derived their ancestry either directly from China_YR_LN or through a two-way admixture of China_YR_MN and a southern East Asian-related ancestral component (~10%, similar to the genetic makeup of China_YR_LN) (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Data 2B). This indicates that the elite populations in the high-level burial area of the Yaoheyuan site were linked to the migration of Late Neolithic Yellow River farmers, which played a significant role in the Zhou Dynasty’s expansion and control over the northwest frontier regions.

As the Western Zhou transitioned to the Eastern Zhou Dynasty, a decline in central administrative power coincided with an influx of new genetic ancestry into the region. Populations from the four Eastern Zhou archeological sites clustered closely with ANA populations in the PCA plot (Fig. 2B) and in cluster-based outgroup-f3 analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3A). However, a distinct genetic component associated with Ancient North Eurasian (ANE) ancestry, represented by a red component, emerged in the ADMIXTURE analysis. This observation was further corroborated by negative Z values in the f4(Mbuti, China_Xinjiang_Xiaohe_BA.SG; Eastern Zhou Ningxia populations, Shatang_Beiyuan_Qijia_2) analysis, ranging from −0.71 to −3.96, with an average of −2.39. The ANE-related ancestry component was estimated to constitute approximately 4% of their genomes (Supplementary Data 2B).

We proposed two hypotheses to explain the influx of ANE-related ancestry into the Eastern Zhou populations: (1) Direct Expansion Hypothesis: ANE-related populations expanded directly into the Ningxia region and mixed with local populations; (2) Mediated Expansion Hypothesis: The ANE-related genetic influence was introduced indirectly through pre-admixed populations, which subsequently expanded into the Ningxia region and mixed with local groups.

To test these hypotheses, we employed rotation-based qpAdm modeling to evaluate competing sources of ancestry: (1) direct contributions from ANE populations and (2) indirect contributions from pre-admixed populations with both ANE and ANA ancestry. Using Russia_Shamanka_EBA as a proxy for the pre-admixed population40, we found that models incorporating the pre-admixed population as one of the sources provided a better fit for most Eastern Zhou Ningxia populations compared to models relying on direct ANE ancestry (Supplementary Data 2 C). These populations were best modeled using three-way admixture models, reflecting varying ancestry proportions from the central plain Yellow River, ANA, and pre-admixed populations with both ANE and ANA ancestry. Specifically, their genetic makeup comprised 10.4%-25% China_YR_MN-related ancestry, 62.8%-73.4% China_AR_EN-related ancestry, and 12.1%-18.3% Russia_Shamanka_EBA-related ancestry (Supplementary Data 2 C).

Based on the superior fit of the qpAdm models, we present the Mediated Expansion Hypothesis as a plausible scenario for the introduction of ANE-related ancestry into Eastern Zhou Ningxia. However, we acknowledge that a rotation-based qpAdm framework alone cannot provide definitive evidence to distinguish between a direct contribution of ANE ancestry and an indirect contribution mediated through pre-admixed groups. What can be stated with greater confidence is that new gene flow, represented by ANE-related ancestry, entered these Eastern Zhou populations. Considering published ancient genomic data from surrounding regions, northern populations from the Neolithic to the Bronze Age exhibit increasing levels of ANE-related ancestry. Therefore, the ANE ancestry observed in Ningxia was plausibly mediated by the expansion of these northern populations southward during the Bronze to Iron Age transition. We emphasize that this interpretation should be regarded as a plausible scenario, partially supported by regional aDNA studies and consistent with our qpAdm modeling, rather than as a definitive conclusion or proven mechanism.

In summary, our findings reveal two distinct waves of population migrations to Ningxia during the Zhou Dynasty. It reflects the dynamic shifts in the gene pool of the northwest frontier against the historical backdrop of the rise and fall of the Zhou regime.

The Emergence of Steppe Pastoralists and BMAC-Related Ancestry and Increased YR_LN-Related Ancestry During the Han Dynasty

To explore the genetic compositions of the population under the frontier management policies of the Han Dynasty, we analyzed 22 ancient genomes from the Han Dynasty, collected from nine archeological sites (Supplementary Data 1D and 1E), examining their genetic structures and comparing them with the pre-existing genetic composition of the local population.

With the exception of Yangjia_Zhuang_Han_2, all Han individuals from the Ningxia region clustered closely in the PCA plot, occupying a genetic position similar to Yaoheyuan_Western_Zhou, China_YR_LN, and China_YR_LBIA (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. 2C). This suggests a shared genetic affinity among these groups. ADMIXTURE analysis further supported this, revealing higher proportions of dark green and light green components co-existing in these populations (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Additionally, cluster-based outgroup-f3 analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3A) and f4 analysis (f4(Mbuti, References; Han Ningxia populations, China_YR_MN)) (Supplementary Fig. 3B) quantitatively confirmed their close genetic relationship, highlighting shared common ancestry and additional genetic affinities with Southern East Asian populations compared to China_YR_MN. Moreover, these populations could be modeled as deriving the majority of their ancestry from China_YR_LN-related groups (74.1% − 100%, average: 95.2%), with minor contributions from Southern East Asian populations (average: 9.7%) and ANA populations (average: 11.2%) (Supplementary Data 2D and Fig. 3). This suggests a broader pattern of population movement from the central plains to northwest China during this period.

In contrast, Yangjia_Zhuang_Han_2 was positioned between the Eastern and Western clines in the PCA plot (Fig. 2C) and displayed additional genetic components associated with ANA populations and Western Eurasians in the ADMIXTURE analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Quantitative cluster-based outgroup-f3 and f4 analyses (Supplementary Fig. 3) demonstrated that the genetic affinities of Yangjia_Zhuang_Han_2 were distinct from the East Asian lineage. This individual could be modeled as a four-way admixture, comprising 42.1% China_YR_LN-related ancestry, 32.1% ANA-related ancestry, 19.8% Steppe_MLBA-related ancestry, and 7% BMAC-related ancestry (Supplementary Data 2D and Fig. 3). Archeological evidence indicates that the pottery unearthed from this burial site bears traces of nomadic influences. Specifically, we found a significant female-biased contribution of Steppe-related ancestry for Yangjia_Zhuang_Han_2 (Supplementary Data 4B and Supplementary Note 2). A similar sex-bias pattern was observed in the Hexi Corridor, the Khitan and Mongol empires. Eurasian trade/travel routes may facilitate marriages between Han males and non-Han females.

To estimate the admixture dates of Yangjia_Zhuang_Han_2, we applied DATES (Distribution of Ancestry Tracts of Evolutionary Signals), a method designed to date admixture events in a single individual. Our analysis revealed that the influx of Steppe_MLBA-related and BMAC-related ancestry into Yangjia_Zhuang_Han_2 occurred around the same time, approximately 14 and 12 generations (Z value > 2 and NRMSD < 0.7), respectively, before the individual’s death (Supplementary Data 3 A). This suggests that these two components were introduced simultaneously by a pre-admixed population, rather than through separate admixture events. Assuming 28 years per generation, the admixture likely occurred within a 400-year period. Yangjia_Zhuang_Han_2 radiocarbon dating ranges from 2107 to 1942 BP. These results indicate that the influx of Steppe pastoralists and BMAC-related ancestry into northwest China may occurred before the Han Dynasty(2152-1730BP).

The Increased Steppe Pastoralist and BMAC-related Ancestry in the Northern Dynasties and Sui-Tang Dynasty

In this study, we present 18 ancient genomes from seven archeological sites dating to the Northern Dynasties and Sui-Tang Dynasty (Supplementary Data 1 D and 1E), representing the most flourishing periods of the Eurasian trade/travel routes.

Our analyses revealed that most individuals from this period exhibited strong genetic affinities with Western Eurasian populations, as demonstrated by PCA and ADMIXTURE analyses (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. 1C). Notably, two individuals (H81507 and H91502) clustered closely with Western Eurasian groups, with over 80% of their genetic component attributable to Western Eurasian ancestry (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. 1C). This suggests that these individuals may represent entirely incoming Western Eurasian populations to the Ningxia region during the Sui-Tang Dynasty. Quantitatively, we conducted cluster-based outgroup-f3 and f4 analyses to compare the overall genetic affinities of Sui-Tang Dynasty Ningxia populations with those of other dynasties and published Eurasian populations. We found that at least eight groups (Jiulongshan_Sui_Tang_1, Jiulongshan_Sui_Tang_2, Jiulongshan_Sui_Tang_3, Nanjiao_Sui_Tang, Nanyuan_Sui_Tang_2, Nanyuan_Sui_Tang_3, Nanyuan_Sui_Tang_4, and Nanyuan_Sui_Tang_5) exhibited genetic profiles that significantly deviated from typical East Asian genetic patterns (Supplementary Fig. 3). These groups showed strong genetic affinities with Western populations, as evidenced by significantly negative Z values (Z < − 3) in the f4(Mbuti, Luxembourg_Loschbour/Russia_MA1_HG/ Russia_MLBA_Sintashta / Turkmenistan_Gonur_BA_1; Eight deviated populations, China_YR_MN) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Three of these eight populations—Jiulongshan_Sui_Tang_1, Jiulongshan_Sui_Tang_2, and Nanyuan_Sui_Tang_5—could be modeled without any Yellow River-related ancestry. The other five populations exhibited varying degrees of ancestry from China_YR_LN, ranging from 13.4% to 60.6%, along with additional ancestry components from ANA, Steppe_MLBA, and BMAC (Supplementary Data 2E and Fig. 3). Furthermore, the individual (H81506) from Jiulongshan_Sui_Tang_2 exhibited lactase persistence (genotype CT at rs182549), which is common among people of European ancestry.

At the Nanyuan site, we observed distinct genetic patterns among individuals. Nanyuan_Sui_Tang_1 derived its ancestry directly from China_YR_LN, while Nanyuan_Sui_Tang_5 showed no detectable ancestry from Yellow River farmers and could be modeled as a mixture of 13.9% ANA-related ancestry, 40.4% Steppe_MLBA-related ancestry, and 45.7% BMAC-related ancestry (Supplementary Data 2E and Fig. 3). Groups 2 to 4 exhibited varying proportions of admixture between Yellow River farmers and Western Eurasian populations, while Nanyuan_Sui_Tang_2 and Nanyuan_Sui_Tang_4 showed a mixed pattern of incoming West Eurasian-derived males and local Yellow River-derived females (Supplementary Data 4 C). The admixture events are estimated to have occurred 4 to 14 generations before the individuals’ deaths (Supplementary Data 3B). These findings suggest a period of coexistence and intense interaction between Eastern and Western populations, indicating that the admixture likely occurred relatively recently rather than being inherited from earlier Han Dynasty populations. This pattern is further supported by admixture estimates for other contemporaneous sites, where admixture events for these individuals occurred, on average, approximately 10 generations before their deaths (Supplementary Data 3 C).

In summary, among the 14 groups from seven Ningxia Northern Dynasties and Sui-Tang Dynasty archeological sites, 12 exhibited additional ancestry components from Steppe_MLBA-related or BMAC-related populations (Supplementary Data 2E and Fig. 3). Only two groups derived their ancestry entirely from China_YR_LN. These findings provide direct DNA evidence that the establishment and flourishing of the Eurasian trade/travel routes indeed promoted a broader pattern of population movement and genetic admixture between Eastern and Western populations.

Genetic Diversity and Ancestry Dynamics in Post-Sui-Tang Dynasty Ningxia

Our analysis of Han and Sui-Tang Dynasty populations from Ningxia highlights significant population mobility and genetic interactions between Eastern and Western groups across Northwest China. However, it remains uncertain whether the previously established ancestry persisted or if new genetic influxes occurred in the post-Sui-Tang periods, particularly given the fluctuating governance of the Ningxia region during this time.

To address this question, we examined 10 ancient genomes from seven post-Sui-Tang-Dynasty archeological sites (Supplementary Data 1D and 1E). These individuals displayed diverse genetic structures, occupying distinct positions in the PCA plot (Fig. 2E). Unlike most Sui-Tang Dynasty populations, which deviated significantly from typical East Asian genetic patterns, the genetic affinities of post-Sui-Tang Dynasty populations closely clustered with previously identified groups enriched in Yellow River farmer ancestry. This was supported by cluster-based outgroup-f3 analysis, indicating that the predominant ancestry components in post-Sui-Tang Dynasty Ningxia populations were derived from Yellow River farmers (Supplementary Fig. 3A). However, these populations did not derive their ancestry solely from Yellow River farmers; they also exhibited genetic affinities with groups including Tibetan Plateau, ANA, and Steppe-related populations. This was corroborated by cluster-based f4 analysis in the format of f4(Mbuti, References; post-Sui-Tang Ningxia populations, China_YR_MN), revealing that 7 out of 9 populations formed another East Asia-related group (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Genetic composition estimates showed that all these populations contained Yellow River-related ancestry, with the predominant ancestry from China_YR_LN. The proportion of China_YR_LN-related ancestry ranged from 50.6% to 100%, with an average of 79.5% (Supplementary Data 2 F and Fig. 3), consistent with cluster-based f-tests.

In addition to the China_YR_LN-related ancestry, we identified five additional ancestry components in these Ningxia populations: ANA-related (12.2% to 26.2%, average 19.0%), Steppe-related (2.7% to 14.6%, average 7.6%), southern East Asian-related (14.6%), Tibetan Plateau population-related (49.4%), and ANE-related (7.3%) ancestry (Supplementary Data 2 F and Fig. 3). Compared to the admixture observed during the Han to Sui-Tang periods between Eastern and Western populations, the admixture dates for these later-period populations were estimated to have occurred at least 40 generations earlier (Supplementary Data 3D). This suggests that the Steppe-related ancestry components were not introduced through recent admixture but rather represent a genetic legacy already present in the genomes of these post-Sui-Tang Ningxia populations.

Intriguingly, during the Western Xia period in the Ningxia region, the genetic makeup consisted of 50.6% China_YR_LN-related ancestry and 49.4% Tibetan Plateau population-related ancestry (Supplementary Data 2 F and Fig. 3). To test whether Tibetan Plateau-related ancestry is indeed present in this individual irrespective of the specific Tibetan Plateau proxy used, we expanded the source panel to include additional ancient Tibetan Plateau populations from different subregions (populations and regional representation follow Hongru Wang et al.’s definitions)38: Ngari2.3k (west), Shigatse2.6k (southwest), Shannan3k (south), Nagqu2.7k (central), and Chamdo2.8k_1 (southeast), in addition to Zongri5.1k and Yushu2.8k representing the northeast. Across these tests, the best-fitting models (highest P values and nestedp <0.05) for all Tibetan Plateau source locations required a Tibetan Plateau-related component, indicating that Tibetan Plateau ancestry was a consistent contributor to this individual (Supplementary Data 2H). However, some ancient Tibetan Plateau populations (excluding Yushu2.8k and Zongri5.1k) required an additional ANA component for the optimal genetic modeling. This was likely because Yushu2.8k and Zongri5.1k already contain a degree of ANA-related ancestry7. Consequently, a Tibetan Plateau ancestry signal was robust in this individual, evidence for an additional ANA contribution was inconsistent across different Tibetan Plateau sources. One plausible explanation was that the geographic location of the northeastern Tibetan Plateau provided opportunities for interaction with ANA populations. Genetic origins of this individual may have then formed through the interaction of these already admixed northeastern Tibetan Plateau populations with populations from the Yellow River region.

This unique genetic composition appeared only during the Western Xia period and disappeared thereafter. This pattern might be linked to the Dangxiang, a minority group that supplanted the rulers of the Central Plains and established the Western Xia state (912-723 BP) in Ningxia, potentially contributing to ongoing efforts to uncover the origins of the Dangxiang tribes.

Thus, despite multiple influences from surrounding populations from the Neolithic to the Ming-Qing Dynasty—particularly the significant impact of Western populations on local populations during the Sui-Tang period—we find that the primary genetic component of these Ningxia populations remained closely related to Yellow River farmers.

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the genomes of 89 ancient human individuals from the Ningxia region of China. By examining genomic data across historical periods, we reveal that the population history of Ningxia did not follow a linear trajectory but unfolded as a layered history shaped by successive waves of population admixture from diverse origins (Fig. 4).

Synthesis of ancestry dynamics and gene flow in Ningxia over four millennia.

Temporal changes in population structure and ancestry composition in Ningxia from the Neolithic to historical periods. Genetic diversity in Ningxia was shaped by multiple waves of admixture and sustained population interactions.

During the Neolithic period, the genetic profile of early populations in Ningxia was shaped by three ancestral sources: Tibetan Plateau populations (Zongri5.1k), ANA, and YR_MN populations. Genetically, they exhibited high homogeneity with Neolithic populations (approximately 6231–4860 years BP) from the middle to upper reaches of the Yellow River41. This genetic continuity represents a comprehensive phenomenon jointly shaped by genes, geography, and culture. Investigations into the genetic structure of mid-Neolithic populations along the Yellow River and its adjacent regions indicate that the YR_MN ancestry expanded significantly outward under the dual drivers of archeological cultural expansion and the rise of millet agriculture. This expansion formed distinct genetic substructures across different regions of the Yellow River basin41. Against this backdrop, the Caiyuan Culture in the Ningxia region emerged and subsequently influenced the cultural essence of the Qijia Culture, a pivotal archeological culture that marks the transition from the Neolithic to the Bronze Age in northwestern China14,15. Our findings provide crucial biological evidence for understanding population interactions, cultural diffusion, and the trajectory of civilization in prehistoric northern China.

The high-status Western Zhou tomb individuals from the Yaoheyuan site in Ningxia exhibit significant genetic affinity with populations from the Yellow River basin. Given the context of the Western Zhou enfeoffment system and the military outpost nature of the Yaoheyuan settlement, the genetic profile of the elite reflects the entry of a new, politically organized group with close ties to eastern Eurasian populations into the Ningxia region. Whether the genetic impact of this population movement remained limited, whether endogamous practices or marriage strategies aimed at maintaining bloodline identity existed among the elite, and whether social stratification based on genetic background emerged are questions that await further systematic investigation through additional archaeogenetic data.

The genetic profile of populations associated with the Northern Bronze Complex in the Ningxia region during the Eastern Zhou period displays an admixture of three ancestral components: ANA, YR_MN, and ANE. Notably, the pre-admixture of the ANA and ANE components prior to their arrival in Ningxia provides insights into the dynamic mechanisms behind early pastoralist cultural diffusion and its localization process along the eastern edge of Eurasia. The genomes of Eastern Zhou populations in Ningxia largely reflect two critical admixture events: the first occurred on the northern steppe, where the ANA and ANE components intermixed; the second occurred locally in Ningxia, leading to the final genetic integration with agricultural populations from the south. This positioned Ningxia as a crucial zone for the southward movement of eastern Eurasian steppe populations, against the backdrop of climatic changes, and their deep integration with local groups. These southward-moving steppe populations likely engaged in cooperation, trade, conflict, and intermarriage with local groups carrying Yellow River-related ancestry, collectively contributing to the complex historical entity referred to in Central Plains textual traditions as the “Xirong”.

The Han frontier defense system served as a crucial military guarantee for imperial rule, with the Ningxia region successively incorporated into the administrative jurisdictions known as the Beidi and Anding Commanderies24. The garrison and land reclamation policies implemented along the Han borders facilitated a second large-scale influx of Central Plains ancestry into Ningxia, significantly increasing the proportion of this component in the local genetic structure. Within the socio-political context shaped by state power, we observed instances of genetic integration between Eastern and Western lineages at the individual level. Notably, the detection of a female-biased genetic contribution associated with steppe and Central Asian-related ancestry in Han-period individuals, which genetic mix pattern was also discovered in contemporaneous individuals from the Hexi corridor7,25, demonstrates that Eurasian trade/travel routes served as corridors for human mobility and biological admixture (Supplementary Fig. 4).

During the Sui-Tang periods, the genetic structure of the Ningxia population exhibited an unprecedented level of trans-Eurasian admixture, with most individuals showing strong genetic affinities to populations from western Eurasia. A particularly notable pattern was the genetic admixture between males of western Eurasian ancestry and females of local Yellow River basin ancestry. This likely corresponds to social practices in which male‑dominated commercial, military, or artisan groups established long‑term communities in Ningxia and intermarried with local women. This pattern is consistent with historical records describing the settlement of Central Asian migrant groups, such as the Sogdians, along Eurasian trade routes30. In the Sui-Tang era, Ningxia not only served as a military stronghold on the northwestern frontier but also became a pivotal hub for transportation and commerce, linking Chang’an with the Western Regions.

The unique genetic composition of an individual from the Western Xia (Minning_Western_Xia_G122115, M7 male in Supplementary Data 1), characterized by admixture between the Tibetan Plateau and YR_MN, aligns with the scholarly hypothesis of a “Qiang origin” for the Tangut. The detection of an ancient, locally derived “Zongri-like” ancestry component in a Tangut individual from the Western Xia period is significant, as it demonstrates the deep local roots and long-term continuity of this component in the Qinghai-Gansu-Ningxia region. However, a deeper investigation into the origins of the Tanguts requires further investigation based on additional sample data in the future. Following the unification under the Yuan dynasty, the gene pool of Ningxia returned to a stable genetic profile dominated by East Asian ancestry in subsequent historical periods, yet it retains traces of earlier Eurasian interactions. The genetic dynamics in the Ningxia region over millennia reflect the interplay among its geographical role as a crossroad, political power shifts, and broader Eurasian exchange networks.

While this study generated 89 ancient genomes spanning nearly 3,500 years and represented a significant advance in reconstructing the dynamic population history of Ningxia, temporal sampling within Ningxia remained uneven. Several key cultural phases were represented by only a few individuals, which limited our ability to capture the full spectrum of genetic diversity and demographic processes. For example, only a single individual associated with the Caiyuan culture met quality thresholds; this individual exhibited a distinctive genetic profile, likely underestimating the culture’s internal substructure. Likewise, only one Western Xia period (Tangut) individual was available for downstream analysis, which might bias inferences about Tangut origins. Future research incorporating broader spatiotemporal sampling across Ningxia will be essential for reconstructing a more comprehensive and accurate population history and for more robustly assessing the genetic origins of groups recorded in historical documents.

Methods

Ethics and permissions

Samples were collected in collaboration with Ningxia Institute of Cultural Relics and Archeology from human skeletal remains excavated at local archeological sites. The project was conducted under the framework of the Ningxia Science and Technology Department key research and development plan (2020BFG02008), with all necessary permits and approvals obtained. The background of archeological sites were provided in Supplementary Notes 1. This study was conducted with the approval and oversight of the Ethics Committee for Biological Research at Fudan University (BE1921).

Radiocarbon dating of sample materials

Of the 89 individuals reported in this study, 15 samples were directly dated using Accelerator Mass Spectrometry (AMS) at Lanzhou University (Lanzhou, China), and the Institute of Geochemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Guiyang, China)42. The calibration of the dated samples was performed by the program Calib 8.20 (http://calib.org/calib/calib.html) and the IntCal.20 calibration curve43.

Ancient DNA extraction and library preparation

We extracted DNA from 89 samples in the Molecular Archeology Laboratory at Fudan University, following established precautions for working with ancient human DNA44,45. Sampling sites were the teeth and the temporal bone. Sample surfaces were cleaned with 5% sodium hypochlorite and 50% ethanol, then ground into powder. 50 mg of bone powder was used to extract DNA46. The lysis step included the addition of 1 ml extraction buffer to each 50 mg sample, containing 0.45 M EDTA (PH 8.0), 0.25 mg/ml Proteinase K (Vetec, Germany), 0.05% (vol/vol) Tween-20 (Sigma Aldrich, Germany). The sample was incubated overnight (15–24 h) at 37 °C with continuous rotation. Then we mixed 17.5 ml magnetic beads (G-Biosciences, USA) with 2.5 ml binding buffer containing 5 M GuHCl, 40% (vol/vol) Isopropanol, 0.12 M sodium acetate, 0.05% (vol/vol) Tween-20 (Sigma Aldrich, Germany). After centrifugation, we transfer the lysate supernatant(500 ml) to the binding buffer/bead mixture, followed by an extraction using a robot (Enlighten Biotech, China) procedure. Finally, the DNA was eluted with 50 μl TET buffer (QIAGEN, Germany). We prepared initial double-stranded and double-indexing libraries basically following Meyer’s protocol47 with minor modification1,48. The end-repair step was performed in 25 ml reactions using 20 ml of DNA extract. Reaction mixture was incubated for 20 mins at 12 °C and 15 mins at 37 °C, purified using a standard MinElute (Qiagen, Germany) purification step, and eluted in 15 μl TET (Qiagen, Germany). Next, Illumina-specific adapters were ligated to the end-repaired DNA in 25 μl reactions. The reaction was incubated for 15 mins at 20 °C and purified with another MinElute purification step before being eluted in 20 μl EB Buffer (Qiagen, Germany). The adapter fill-in reaction was performed in a final volume of 25 μl and incubated for 20 mins at 37°C followed by 20 mins at 80 °C to inactivate the Bst enzyme (NEB, USA). Libraries were amplified with indexing primers in two parallel PCRs using Q5 High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (NEB, USA). The clean-up libraries were qualified using Qubit 2.0 (Thermo Fisher, USA).

Nuclear SNPs capture and sequencing

We used the Twist Ancient DNA panel enrich double-stranded shotgun libraries, following the protocol developed by David Reich’s lab (short for twist 1240 K)49. The sequencing of the double-stranded library was performed using the Illumina Nova6000 platform at the Mingma Technologies Company, China, in the 150-bp paired-end sequencing design.

Ancient DNA sequence data processing and contamination assessment

AdapterRemoval v2.3.350 was used to trim adapter sequences, filter low-quality reads (<20), filter short reads (<30) and merge paired-end reads into single sequences. The merged reads were then mapped to the human reference genome hs37d5 (GRCh37 with decoy sequences) using BWA v0.7.1751 with the parameters “aln -l 1024 -n 0.01”. PCR duplicates were removed using DeDup v0.12.852 and reads with low mapping quality (MAPQ < 30) were filtered out using SAMtools v1.1853.

Contamination rates for each sample were evaluated using Schmutzi54, ContamMix55 and ANGSD v0.94056. Samples with contamination rates exceeding 3% were further processed by restricting the analysis to fragments exhibiting typical ancient DNA damage patterns using PMDtools v0.6057 with the “--threshold 3” parameter.

BamUtil v1.0.1558 was employed to trim the ends of each mapped read in the BAM files based on DNA damage patterns, ensuring that transition rates at the read ends were below 3% or 6%, depending on whether the sample was filtered to retain only sequences with typical ancient DNA damage signals. These trimmed BAM files were used to generate pseudo-haploid genotype calls for the “1240k” and “HO” panels using pileupCaller v1.5.3 (available at https://github.com/stschiff/sequenceTools) with the “—randomHaploid -q30 -Q30” parameter.

Genetic sex determination, haplogroup assignment and kinship analysis

Genetic sex was determined for each sample using Rx59 and Ry60 calculations. Sex determination was considered accurate only when consistent results were obtained from both methods. Mitochondrial haplogroups were assigned using HaploGrep2 v2.4.061, and Y-chromosome haplogroups were determined using YLeaf v2.262 (Supplementary Fig. 4).

To assess genetic kinship among ancient individuals, we employed READ63, TKGWV264 and lcMLkin65. Ten pairs of individuals were identified as being closer than second-degree relatives.

Data collection

Our dataset was merged with the Allen Ancient DNA Resource (AADR) v54.1.p166, which includes the “Human Origin” (HO) and “1240k” datasets, using mergeit v2450 implemented in EIGENSOFT67. The following filtering criteria were applied to the published datasets: (1) Only data marked as “PASS” in column 34 of the.anno file were retained; (2) Low-coverage data with fewer than 15,000 SNP sites were excluded; (3) Data from individuals identified as relatives or duplicates within the datasets were removed.

Additionally, we merged a separate dataset related to Tibetan Plateau populations for further analysis. The “HO” dataset was used for Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and ADMIXTURE analysis, while the “1240k” dataset was utilized for f-statistics-based analyses.

Principal component analysis and ADMIXTURE analysis

PCA was performed using smartpca v1814068 with the “HO” dataset, which includes a broader representation of modern populations. The parameters “lsqproject: YES, altnormstyle: NO, and numoutlieriter: 0” were applied. Modern populations were used to calculate the principal components (PCs), and ancient individuals were projected onto these PCs.

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning was conducted on the “HO” dataset using plink v1.9069 with the parameters “--indep-pairwise 200 25 0.4 --allow-no-sex”. The pruned dataset was then used for unsupervised ADMIXTURE analysis with ADMIXTURE v1.3.070, testing K values ranging from 2 to 13 and applying the parameters “-B100 –cv”.

Quantitative analysis

We performed outgroup-f3 statistics by qp3Pop v6567 with “inbreed: YES” parameters and f4 statistics using qpDstat v98067 with the parameters “f4mode: YES, printsd: YES, and inbreed: YES”.

For qpWave and qpAdm analyses, we used qpWave v410 and qpAdm v810, respectively, both implemented in ADMIXTOOLS67. The parameters “details: YES, allsnps: YES, and inbreed: YES” were applied.

For rotation-based qpWave analyses, we used qpWave to assess whether individuals from the same archeological site could be treated as a homogeneous group for downstream analyses. qpWave tests the minimum number of independent ancestry waves required to explain a set of “left” populations relative to a set of “right” reference populations67. For example, for four individuals (A, B, C, D), we performed qpWave in four rotations - treating each individual in turn as the first-listed left population (ABCD, BACD, CABD, DABC) while keeping the same right set. If all four rotation tests returned p > 0.05, we considered the four individuals homogeneous and merged them into a single group. If one or more rotations failed to meet this criterion, we removed the individual whose exclusion most improved model fit and repeated the rotation procedure to identify a homogeneous core group; the remaining individuals were assigned to secondary subgroups (e.g., group_2, group_3).

In qpAdm analysis (one- through four-way admixture models), we employed two distinct approaches: (1) Combination Method: Potential source populations were grouped and target populations were modeled as combinations of these groups. (2) Rotation Method: Populations not involved in modeling were included as outgroups. For both methods, we defined a base outgroup set: “Mbuti.DG + Luxembourg_Loschbour.DG + Russia_MA1_HG.SG + Iran_GanjDareh_N + Russia_Caucasus_EBA_Yamnaya + China_Tianyuan + Russia_Shamanka_Eneolithic.SG + China_NEastAsia_Coastal_EN + China_Guangxi_Longlin_Epipaleolithic + China_Fujian_Qihe_Epipaleolithic + Taiwan_Hanben_IA”.

Model selection was based on the following criteria: (1) P values: Models with Pvalues greater than 0.01 were considered statistically plausible; (2) Admixture Proportions and Standard Errors: The estimated admixture proportions and their standard errors (SE) were required to fall within the range of 0 to 1; (3) Admixture Proportion Threshold: The lower bound of the admixture proportion (estimated proportion minus SE) had to be greater than 0. In addition, model selection also incorporated the results of p-nest values and f4 statistics to identify the best-fitting model.

Time of admixture

Admixture times for Ningxia populations were estimated using the DATES v4010 (Distribution of Ancestry Tracts of Evolutionary Signals)71 on the “1240k” dataset. The parameters “maxdis: 1.0, binsize:0.001, jackknife: YES, qbin: 10, runfit: YES, afffit: YES, and lovalfit: 0.45” were applied. To convert the DATES output (in generations) to years B.P., we multiplied the mean admixture date by a generation time of 28 years.
