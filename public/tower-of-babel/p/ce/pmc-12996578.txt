Thymic epithelial tumors at the crossroads of immunity, autoimmunity, and immunotherapy
Authors: Kübra Canaslan, Fatemeh Moeini Nia, Maria Baez, Hassan Abolhassani, Natalie Ridge, Maroun Bou Zerdan, Jennifer Marks, Hussain Rangoonwala, Behnaz Jahanbin, Amir Hossein Emami, Katie O’Reilly, Yesim Gökmen-Polar, Sunil S. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thymic epithelial tumors (TETs) sit at a unique intersection of cancer, central tolerance, and autoimmunity. These tumors arise in the thymus, an immune-rich organ responsible for T-cell education, and despite their low tumor mutational burden (TMB) and microsatellite stability, they display paradoxical behavior under immune checkpoint inhibition. This review synthesizes data on thymic biology, paraneoplastic autoimmunity, molecular profiling, and clinical trials of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in thymoma and thymic carcinoma. We describe how disruption of autoimmune regulator (AIRE)/Fez family zinc finger 2 (FEZF2)-driven tissue-restricted antigen expression and impaired regulatory T-cell generation collectively permit the escape of autoreactive T cells and foster classic paraneoplastic syndromes such as myasthenia gravis, pure red cell aplasia, and Good syndrome. We then link these baseline defects to the high frequency and severity of immune-related adverse events observed in TET patients treated with ICIs, despite only modest response rates. Emerging “harm axis” biomarkers, including autoantibody profiles and composite T- and B-cell perturbations, may help stratify risk and guide treatment selection. Finally, we review rational therapeutic strategies such as vascular endothelial growth factor-tyrosine kinase inhibitors–ICI combinations, radiotherapy–ICI approaches, perioperative immunotherapy, and emphasize the need for biomarker-enriched trial design. TETs provide a powerful model to understand how failure of central tolerance can simultaneously sensitize tumors to immune attack and prime patients for catastrophic toxicity, with implications that extend to other low-TMB, immune-infiltrated malignancies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-026-04311-4.

Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy has been historically anchored to a narrow set of biomarkers. Tumor mutational burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI), and programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression have served as valuable, yet imperfect, predictors of response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [1]. A large population of patients, particularly those with tumors having low-TMB, microsatellite-stable (MSS) without high PD-L1 expression, often have a modest response to treatment [2–4]. Thymic epithelial tumors (TETs) comprised of thymomas, thymic carcinomas (TC), and thymic neuroendocrine tumors are characterized by a low TMB due to lower tumor mutation rate, and moderate to high PD-L1 expression within an immune-rich organ [5]. TET biology creates a treatment outcome paradox: their immune-rich tumor microenvironment (TME) suggests treatment susceptibility; yet, their disrupted tolerance mechanisms predispose patients to severe autoimmune toxicity [6–8]. This review demonstrates how the failure of central tolerance in TETs provides a blueprint for therapeutic strategies and novel biomarker discovery that move beyond conventional paradigms.

Immunobiology of the thymus: the foundation of central tolerance

T-cell precursors travel from the bone marrow to the thymus medulla as immature double-positive (DP T cells: CD4+/CD8+) T cells. By traveling from the medulla to the cortex of the thymus, DP T cells differentiate into effector T cells (either CD4+ or CD8+) and T regulatory cells. (Tregs, CD25 +). This process, known as “positive selection,” exclusively occurs in the cortex of the thymus [9]. T-cells with low or moderate affinity for self-peptides undergo positive selection and exit the thymus as naïve CD4⁺ T cells, while strong and extended signaling with medullary thymic epithelial cells (mTECS) causes T-cells to undergo clonal deletion called “negative selection”. Importantly, thymocytes with intermediate- to high-affinity T-cell receptor (TCR) engagement, insufficient to trigger clonal deletion but strong enough to activate nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NFκB) pathways and are diverted toward the Treg lineage [10, 11].

Negative selection occurs in both the cortex and medulla of thymus. In the cortex, cortical thymic epithelial cells (cTECs) serve as antigen-presenting cells (APCs), presenting self-peptides to thymocytes to facilitate recognition of self-antigens [12]. In the medulla, mTECs are key to establishing tolerance, where strong signaling leads to clonal deletion and prevents autoimmunity. Both cTECs and mTECs express a diverse array of tissue-restricted antigens (TRA), regulated by autoimmune regulator (AIRE) and Fez family zinc finger 2 (FEZF2) [13–15]. Single-cell sequencing reveals that this “self-representation” encompasses ≥ 18,000 protein-coding genes in mature mTECs, with an approximate 4,000 directly regulated by AIRE [16, 17]. This comprehensive antigen display is further diversified by alternative splicing and ribonucleic acid (RNA) editing, with presentation occurring both directly by mTECs and indirectly via dendritic cells through cooperative antigen transfer [16].

The integrity of negative selection depends on transcriptomic signaling Forkhead box N1 (FOXN1), Homeobox A3/Paired box protein 1 (HOXA3/PAX1), and Fibroblast Growth Factor (FGF) 10–FGFR2-IIIb [13, 18, 19]. In thymoma, the disruption of this delicate machinery, caused by downregulation of AIRE, FEZF2, and major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II, coupled with ectopic germinal centers, represents a catastrophic failure of the medullary compartment. This failure permits the escape of autoreactive T cells into the periphery and is thought to contribute to the well-established association between thymic pathology, clinical autoimmunity, and the disproportionately high risk of severe ICI-related toxicities [20–23].

The pathological, genomic, and immune landscape of TETs

Pathology and genomic landscape

TETs are anatomically staged using the tumor, node, and metastasis (TNM) system, which is increasingly replacing the Masaoka–Koga system due to its prognostic significance supported by large survival data [24, 25]. Histologically, the World Health Organization (WHO) classification distinguishes thymoma subtypes based on epithelial morphology and lymphocyte content [26]. TETs display a histopathological spectrum that ranges from indolent thymomas (types A, AB, B1-B3) to aggressive TCs, including rare thymic neuroendocrine tumors [26, 27].

Type A thymomas are primarily epithelial with few lymphocytes, while type B thymomas exhibit increasing lymphocytic infiltration as they progress from B1 to B3 [5]. Thymoma is characterized by the presence of terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT +) and TC with CD5 and CD117 (C-Kit). B3 thymoma often expresses β5, a marker of cortical thymic epithelial differentiation [27]. The WHO-2021 classification recognizes a POU2F3-positive “tuft-cell–like” subset within a squamous TC [26, 28]. The WHO subtypes are also associated with paraneoplastic autoimmunity. Myasthenia gravis (MG) and related autoimmune syndromes are most prevalent in B2–B3 thymomas, intermediate in B1, and rare in types A and AB [29], indicating that more lymphocyte-rich tumors are more prone to breakdown of central tolerance and subsequent export of autoreactive T cells [19, 30]. Type A and AB thymomas, though often considered benign, can show invasive, recurrent, and metastatic behavior [31].

The cancer genome atlas (TCGA)’s integrated multi-omics atlas established robust molecular subtypes linked to clinical behavior, clarified differences between thymoma and the more genomically unstable TC and uncovered mechanistic ties between thymoma and autoimmunity. In particular, MG was mechanistically linked to antigen overexpression and increased aneuploidy, consistent with “false-positive” selection [32]. Other studies have confirmed that thymoma has a uniquely low TMB, dominated by the highly recurrent general transcription factor II-I (GTF2I) L424H mutation [33]. Subsequent sequencing efforts have confirmed the importance of GTF2I, HRAS, and TP53 mutations in thymoma, while expanding the catalog of clinically relevant alterations in TC to include KIT, ERBB2 (HER2), PDGFRA, SETD2, BAP1, and TET2 [34–36]. Pathway analyses indicate involvement of the PI3K/ AKT/ mTOR, EGFR/RAS, and cell cycle/DNA repair pathways, justifying targeted therapy exploration [37, 38]. Association of distinct molecular clusters with different histological subtypes have has also been reported [39], which that ultimately lead to the development of a nine gene expression signature (DecisionDX® Thymoma) that predicts the likelihood of metastasis [40].

Immune landscape of TETs

Unlike most carcinomas, TETs develop within a pre-existing immune-rich TME, exhibiting a spectrum from lymphocyte-rich thymomas with active thymopoiesis to cytotoxic T-cell skewed type B3 thymomas and TC, which show increased Epithelial cell and macrophage infiltration [38, 41]. Neoplastic thymic epithelium inadequately expresses AIRE, FEZF2, and MHC class II, leading to disrupted self-reactive T-cell deletion [15, 21, 42, 43]. The GTF2I mutation hinders mTEC differentiation in vivo, skewing the T-cell repertoire toward self-reactivity without elevating TMB [44]. While immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-1 and PD-L1 are highly expressed in this lymphoid-rich milieu, the underlying biology indicates impaired tolerance rather than effective anti-tumor immunity [41].

PD-L1 expression often appears higher in TC than thymoma (e.g., 70% vs 23%) but does not consistently correlate with T-cell infiltrates or overall survival (OS) [8]. In specific subgroups such as TCs, high PD-L1 expression has been associated with improved survivals [45, 46] while PD-L1 expression level and TMB did not predict survival benefit in the CAVEATT trial. In contrast, genomic alterations such as mutation in in TP53, PIK3CA, and PBRM1 correlated with higher response rates [47], highlighting the importance of genomics-driven selection. Pembrolizumab responses were observed across PD-L1 strata in TC, with a post-hoc progression free survival (PFS) signal noted at the ≥ 50% cutoff [48]. These inconsistencies suggest that PD-L1 expression may reflect inflammatory signals or variability in the regulation of genomic alterations (e.g., Cylindromatosis protein (CYLD) loss) rather than susceptibility to ICIs [8]. Additionally, PDL-1 expression variability in different assays (SP263, SP142, 22C3) limits its utility as a stand-alone biomarker [7].

Beyond PD-1/PD-L1, the tumor TME expresses various alternative checkpoints, including cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3), lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG-3), T-cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT), and V-domain Ig suppressor of T-cell activation (VISTA). Co-regulatory molecules are also expressed, including inducible T-cell co-stimulator (ICOS) and glucocorticoid-induced TNF receptor–related protein (GITR). These molecules are often co-expressed on CD8⁺ T cells and forkhead box P3 (FOXP3)⁺ Tregs [41, 49, 50]. Prognostically, higher densities of CD3⁺ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and “immunotype A” signatures are linked to favorable outcomes, while CD163⁺ M2 macrophage polarization is associated with poorer prognosis in thymoma B3 [41].

Autoimmunity as a window into biology and a predictor of toxicity

Paraneoplastic autoimmunity represents the most striking clinical manifestation of thymic tolerance failure. MG occurs in 30–50% of thymoma patients, with incidence rising from 15% in type A/AB to 40–50% in B1-B3 tumors [51]. Other syndromes, including pure red cell aplasia, Good syndrome, and various neurologic disorders, are more prevalent in WHO B2-B3 thymomas, while thymic carcinomas rarely are associated with autoimmunity [43, 52, 53].

Three complementary models explain thymoma-associated autoimmunity: (i) an escape model in which thymocytes bypass medullary negative selection; (ii) a genetic model in which neoplastic TEC alterations impair selection cues and favor self-reactivity; and (iii) an AIRE-deficiency model in which an AIRE-poor milieu drives both failed deletion and active autoimmunization against thymic/extrathymic antigens, including cytokines [42]. Immunotherapy toxicities and “Harm Axis” in TETs

The immunological imbalance in thymoma extends beyond defective Treg development [21] and closely parallels the B cell abnormalities observed in lipopolysaccharide-responsive and beige-like anchor protein (LRBA) and CTLA-4 deficiency syndromes, involving both B cell abnormalities and defective Tregs [55]. This combination results in “autoimmune immunodeficiency,” a state of coexisting tolerance failure and immune incompetence [55]. In this primmed immune TME, blocking PD-1/PD-L1 alongside abundant intratumoral thymocytes leads to rapid autoreactive expansion and early severe immune-related adverse events (irAEs), such as myositis, myocarditis, hepatitis, and MG, within 1–6 weeks [56, 57] (Grade 3–5 irAEs 26.4%, with 17.1% in TC, 58.3% in thymoma [58]). The overlap between thymic and striated muscle antigens may explain the frequent occurrence of myositis, myocarditis, and MG, termed ‘Triple M Syndrome’ [32, 59]. ICI-induced myocarditis has been more common in TET compared to other cancers (up to 9.1% vs < 1% in lung cancer [58]). In a recent meta-analysis, 26% of patients with TET experienced grade 3 and higher toxicity, while 3.3% had grade 4 hepatitis and 4.7% had myocarditis [58]. Although myocarditis remains a frequent toxicity in thymoma compared to other solid tumors, post ICI treatment, most patient have response to immunosuppression and only one death reported, after intensive immunosuppressive management [58, 60].

Identifying biomarkers to predict ICI toxicity in TETs is critical. The pathological subtypes of thymic tumors provide important guidance and hold significant value in predicting and interpreting immunotherapy-related adverse events with higher risk of immunotherapy related toxicity in thymoma B type than thymic carcinoma. Baseline anti-acetylcholine receptor (AChR)/striational antibodies provide a potential quantifiable measure of severe irAE risk [49, 50]. Therefore, pre-treatment assessment of “Harm Axis” with anti-AChR screening, surveillance for B-cell/T-cell receptor abnormalities, and early cardiac/neurologic monitoring are recommended (Supplementary Table-S1).

Immunotherapy efficacy in TETs

Historically, systemic treatment of TETs has relied on multi-agent cytotoxic chemotherapy (most commonly platinum–anthracycline or platinum/etoposide–based regimens), which achieves meaningful but often transient tumor shrinkage at the cost of cumulative toxicity [61, 62]. This paradigm prompted a shift in therapeutic development toward immune-based strategies.

In TC, single agent ICI demonstrates modest activity; pembrolizumab shows an objective response rate (ORR) of 19–23%, while nivolumab failed to achieve responses, indicating variability [48, 63, 64]. The MARBLE trial found that upfront chemo-immunotherapy with atezolizumab, carboplatin, and paclitaxel is feasible, achieving a 56% ORR but associated with significant grade ≥ 3 toxicity [65]. Dual checkpoint blockade, such as ipilimumab-nivolumab [60] or bispecific antibodies (KN046 47 [66]), shows additional activity but is linked to high-grade toxicity. The phase II EORTC–ETOP NIVOTHYM trial indicated that adding ipilimumab to nivolumab did not improve OS or PFS in B3 thymoma (n = 8) or TC (n = 48), with an ORR of 17.7%. Grade ≥ 3 irAEs occurred in 29% of patients, primarily colitis, myocarditis, and dermatologic reactions, reflecting increased toxicity without corresponding efficacy gains [60].

Rational combinations to overcome resistance

Emerging strategies for TC treatment involve combining vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibitors with chemotherapy and immunotherapy [67]. Anti-angiogenic tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) can normalize tumor vasculature, reduce hypoxia-driven immunosuppression, and decrease Tregs and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [68]. These effects may enhance responses to ICIs by inducing tumor apoptosis and antigen release.

Multi-kinase inhibitors like sunitinib and lenvatinib have shown efficacy in chemotherapy-refractory thymomas, with an ORR of 26–38% [69, 70]. VEGFR-2 expression can reach 65% in TETs, particularly in aggressive forms like B3 thymoma and TC [71]. Phase II trials such as S1701 and RELEVENT demonstrated that adding ramucirumab to carboplatin-paclitaxel increased response rates (ORR 58–88%), though 48% experienced grade ≥ 3 toxicity [72, 73]. Combinations of VEGF inhibitors and ICIs are promising; the CAVEATT trial (avelumab plus axitinib) reported a 34% ORR [47], while the PECATI trial (lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab) showed a 23% ORR with prolonged PFS in pre-treated B3/TC [74]. Both studies had a 12%-14% incidence of grade ≥ 3 irAEs, with no treatment-related deaths observed.

Harmonizing immune activation: rational for radiotherapy-ICI combinations in TETs

An emerging area of interest is integrating radiotherapy (RT) with ICI to overcome immune resistance. Unlike conventional systemic approaches, which either activate or ablate the immune system, radiation serves as a middle ground: at immunogenic doses, it converts tumors into localized antigen-releasing organs, enhancing dendritic cell priming and T-cell recruitment. This “in situ vaccination” effect has been observed in thoracic malignancies, producing local control and systemic immune responses that can facilitate tumor rejection [75, 76]. These principles are particularly relevant for TETs, where the unique TME enhances responsiveness.

Ionizing radiation induces dose-dependent immunogenic cell death, releasing tumor antigens and damage-associated molecular patterns that promote dendritic activation and cytotoxic T-cell infiltration [77, 78]. Additionally, RT upregulates PD-L1 and MHC class I expression, creating opportunities for PD-1/PD-L1 blockade to enhance T-cell activity [79, 80]. RT-ICI regimens confine the initial immune priming event, reducing the risk of widespread autoimmune reactions. Hypofractionated dosing, particularly stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), shows greater immunogenic potential by amplifying type I interferon-driven chemokine gradients and expanding the TCR repertoire, making it compatible with ICIs [81]. The translational implication is clear: RT may act as an “immune metronome” for TETs, synchronizing antigen release, T-cell activation, and immune restraint, thereby addressing key barriers to immunotherapy. Future trials should explore RT as a harmonizing tool to recalibrate the immune landscape of TETs.

Rational of peri-operative immunotherapy in TETs

Recent data suggests a potential role for ICIs in the perioperative management of TETs, especially in locally advanced or borderline resectable cases. The modest efficacy of ICIs in metastatic settings and the need for improved cytoreduction support their evaluation in the perioperative context. A Korean phase II trial of perioperative pembrolizumab combined with neoadjuvant chemotherapy showed a 46.4% major pathological response (MPR) and a 17.9% pathological complete response (pCR), with a median disease-free survival of 49.3 months (95% CI, 25.3-NR). R0 resection was achieved in 71.4% of surgical patients, and MPR and pCR rates did not significantly vary based on PD-L1 tumor proportion score (TPS). While 52.5% of adverse events were low grade, 35.0% were grade 3–4 [82].

These findings indicate that neoadjuvant ICIs may improve R0 resection, MPR, and pCR rates in select patients. However, potential benefits should be weighed against the high risk of irAEs, particularly in thymomas. Larger, randomized biomarker-driven trials are needed to refine patient selection.

Early patient education and impact of quality of life (QoL)

A significant number of irAEs underscoring the need for vigilant monitoring to facilitate early detection of ICI toxicities [6]. These toxicities can affect patients’ QoL due to the stress of treatment interruptions and the potential need for corticosteroids [83]. Prompt recognition of irAEs is a shared responsibility between patients and their care teams. Education on irAEs and their management should be provided to patients and caregivers before treatment initiation [84]. Studies indicate that various forms of education (oral, written, and electronic) enhance patients’ self-efficacy, leading to better self-management and increased reporting of symptoms [83]. One trial demonstrated that electronic patient-reported outcomes improved health-related QoL, while pharmacist-led follow-up calls reduced discontinuation rates of ICIs [85, 86]. Early recognition and reporting of irAEs by patient facilitate timely management, emphasizing the critical role of patient education in treatment initiation.

The path forward—novel strategies

The current pipeline for TETs includes several mechanistic classes beyond VEGF-ICI combinations. Immunocytokines, such as bintrafusp alfa (PD-L1/TGF-β trap), target dual immunosuppressive pathways (NCT04417660, [87]). Inducers of immunogenic cell death like PT-112 trigger calreticulin exposure and HMGB1 release, demonstrating high disease control rates with manageable safety [88]. Additional approaches include antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) and novel cell surface-targeting therapies like anti-mesothelin CAR-T, though data remain limited [50, 66, 89–91]. Cancer vaccines, such as Wilms’ tumor gene 1 (WT1) peptide, show safety and antigen-specific responses, indicating potential utility in minimal residual disease settings [92]. A call for new trial design strategies

Future studies could advance beyond conventional frameworks by incorporating several critical elements: 1) Including baseline autoimmunity screening and biomarker strata in eligibility criteria for risk-adapted analyses. 2) Developing comprehensive correlatives to perform single-cell and spatial tumor bed analyses, TCR sequencing, and multi-omic analysis. 3) Considering dose adjustment as lower doses of ICI can be as effective as the maximum tolerated dose and, in some cases, even more effective, due to lower irAEs [93, 94]. 4) Assessment of normal thymus storage before starting treatment. Thymic health correlates with increased TCR diversity and adaptive immune signaling, with improvements linked to better responses to ICIs across various tumor types [95, 96]. This concept aligns with TET biology, indicating that preserving thymic function may enhance immune response after checkpoint release. 5) Assessment of upfront tumor genomic testing is supported by data showing DNA-repair variants enhance immunotherapy response [5]. 6) Incorporating integrative germline-somatic sequencing with immunophenotyping to uncover underlying errors of immunity [34, 55].

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) in thymic tumors

In TETs, ctDNA is explored as a biomarker for tumor volume, offering real-time insights into tumor burden and molecular changes, critical due to TETs’ unique immunobiology and high irAE risk from ICIs [49, 67, 97, 98]. Kinetics of ctDNA, such as early declines in ctDNA levels, may act as surrogates for ICI response, potentially preceding radiographic changes and distinguishing true progression from pseudoprogression [99–101]. Additionally, ctDNA profiling can identify actionable mutations and assess TMB and MSI, although TETs generally display low TMB [102–104]. While ctDNA is not yet validated for managing immunotherapy in thymic cancers, ongoing studies are evaluating its utility for real-time monitoring, treatment duration guidance, and resistance identification [67, 103].

Artificial intelligence in TET

Artificial intelligence (AI) has strong potential to accelerate progress in TETs, which rarity and biologic heterogeneity limit conventional evidence generation. CT-based radiomics can help differentiate low risk TETs (A, B, B1 thymoma) from high-risk disease (B2-3, TC) [105], while machine learning models trained on PET-CT features can predict recurrence risk and patterns [106]. These approaches could support clinical decision-making yet need standardization. Beyond imaging, AI can facilitate the identification of aberrant proteins and candidate biomarkers to guide the development of targeted antibodies [107, 108], and AI-assisted digital pathology (pathomic) may improve risk stratification by predicting treatment response and survival [109].

Conclusion

TETs exemplify a unique facet of immuno-oncology, where central tolerance mechanisms are co-opted by oncogenesis, creating a paradox of an immune-rich environment that is also prone to autoimmune complications. This results in modest therapeutic efficacy and disproportionate toxicity, underscoring the limitations of conventional immunotherapy biomarkers. Insights from TETs extend beyond this rare cancer, providing a paradigm for immunotherapy in low-TMB, immune-rich malignancies and highlighting the necessity of understanding both immune tolerance and activation signals to fully harness the potential of immuno-oncology. There is an opportunity to use image-based AI modeling such as radiomics/pathomics biomarkers that could predict immunotherapy benefits and adverse events.
