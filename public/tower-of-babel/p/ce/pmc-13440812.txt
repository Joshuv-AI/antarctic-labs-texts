Dural-based metastasis mimicking meningioma: A rare case report
Authors: B. Paul Sudhakar John, Hershdeep Singh, Moloti Kichu, Parul Gupta, Pallavi Chaudhary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: The incidence of dural-based metastasis (DBM) is 4% of all patients diagnosed with metastatic cancers. DBM most commonly arises from primary cancers of the lung, breast, and prostate. Most cerebral metastasis lesions are intra axial tumors, whereas a dural base metastasis mimicking meningioma is uncommon.Case Description: Here, we are presenting a case report of a 64-year-old woman with a history of treated left breast cancer 14 years ago, who presented to us in the casualty with left lower limb weakness for 1 month and dizziness for 7 days. INTRODUCTION

Incidence of dural-based metastasis (DBM) is 4% of all patients diagnosed with metastatic cancers and 9–14% of patients with intracranial tumors.[1] DBM most commonly arises from primary cancers of the lung, breast, and prostate.[6] Most cerebral metastasis lesions are intra axial, whereas a DBM mimicking meningiomas are uncommon.[4] DBM poses a diagnostic challenge, especially because they can also present as an isolated, intracranial, and subdural mass. DBM may exhibit radiological and gross pathological features that are classically associated with meningiomas, thereby potentially leading to misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment.[1]

Even though the diagnostic challenges and radiological presentation in DBM are relatively well studied, the epidemiology, locations, and therapeutic outcomes are poorly studied.[5] Studies of cerebral metastasis have shown that combined surgical resection and radiotherapy provide superior local disease control and increased survival time, as compared to radiotherapy alone.[8]

CASE PRESENTATION

In July 2024, a 64-year-old woman presented to the emergency room with complaints of a history of left lower limb weakness for 1 month and dizziness for 7 days, headache, and vomiting for 1 day. The patient had a history of left-sided mastectomy for breast cancer (stage IIB, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)/neu positive, estrogen receptor and progesterone receptor positive) 14 years ago. She subsequently received adjuvant chemotherapy with docetaxel and cyclophosphamide (administered every 3 weeks for six cycles). It was followed by external beam radiotherapy to a total dose of 50 Gy in 25 fractions over 5 weeks. This was followed by hormonal therapy with letrozole (2.5 mg once daily) for 5 years after surgery. She remained on regular follow-up at another hospital until 2020, after which she discontinued follow-up. There is no history of any other comorbidity.

On examination, the patient was conscious, oriented to time, place and person, vitals were stable, Glasgow coma scale (GCS) 15/15 (E4V5M6), cranial nerve examination was normal, motor examination showed left lower limb power of 4/5 and rest of the limbs were 5/5, sensory dermatomes examination was normal, deep tendon reflexes at knee, ankle and heel of both lower limbs were normal. No sign of cerebellar dysfunction was elicited. No signs of autonomic dysfunction were present. On local examination, the mastectomy scar was noted on the left chest wall and left axillary region. No clinically palpable lump on the right breast or lymphadenopathy. There was no clinically palpable axillary or supraclavicular lymphadenopathy. Spinal examination was normal.

Blood routine investigations were within normal limits, plain and contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (CE-MRI) of the brain showed a large 4 × 4.8 × 5.1 cm well-defined heterogeneously enhancing mass involving the right parieto-occipital lesion, suggestive of a dural-based lesion without any underlying bony changes [Figures 1 and 2]. A differential diagnosis of dural-based lesions like meningioma or brain metastasis was made.

After pre-anesthetic evaluation, the patient’s consent was taken, and she was taken up for surgery. The patient was placed in a supine position with head tilted to the left side with slight flexion, and a right parietal parasagittal craniotomy was performed. The overlying dura just above the space-occupying lesion (SOL) was adherent to it, and also the medial aspect of the tumor was seen adherent to the posterior end of the superior sagittal sinus. A durotomy was done, leaving a part of the unhealthy dura with the SOL. A firm, yellow to red-looking, highly vascular mass with a dimension of approximately 3.9 × 3.3 × 4.3 cm was identified over the right parietal occipital region [Figure 3]. A near-total excision of the tumor was achieved with excision of the overlying dura. Using bipolar, cauterization of the dural sinus was done. Hemostasis was achieved throughout the procedure. Tissue was sent for histopathological examination (HPE). The patient was shifted to the neurosurgery intensive care unit after extubation. The patient postoperatively had a GCS of 15/15 (E4V5M6), pupils were bilaterally equal and reactive to light. Subgaleal drain was removed on postoperative day 2, and the patient was mobilized in a wheelchair. Postoperative period was uneventful, and the patient was discharged after a week of hospital stay, but her right lower limb weakness persisted. The rest of the neurological examination was normal.

Intra-operative finding shows a well-defined, firm space occupying lesion (marked with white) in the right parieto-occipital region with attachment to the posterior part of the superior sagittal sinus (marked with black arrow).

HPE of the tissue revealed an infiltrating malignant epithelial tumor arranged in solid sheets, small nests, trabeculae, and occasional cribriform patterns. Mitotic activity was brisk, and scattered apoptotic bodies were observed. Marked desmoplastic changes were noted in the stroma, and the background also showed normal brain parenchyma. Lymphovascular invasion was present, and large areas of hemorrhage were identified. The HPE findings were suggestive of metastatic ductal carcinoma, and immunohistochemistry (IHC) supported a metastatic lesion with a primary origin in the breast. Follow up

After suture removal on the 10th postoperative day, the patient was referred to the radiotherapy department with the HPE and IHC reports. The patient received radiotherapy at a dose of 30 Gy in 10 fractions over 2 weeks. She was also started on letrozole (2.5 mg once daily) after a dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry scan, which did not show any evidence of osteoporosis. She is currently on regular follow-up visits to the radiotherapy and neurosurgery outpatient departments. Patient’s GCS is 15/15 (E4V5M6) with persistent left lower limb weakness (motor power: 4/5), rest of the neurological examination was normal.

DISCUSSION

This case report highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by rare dural-based lesions, which can mimic more common dural pathologies in both their clinical presentation and imaging characteristics. Cerebral metastases are the most common brain tumors in adults, and they may occasionally present as isolated meningeal lesions that closely mimic meningiomas. This resemblance underscores the prognostic and therapeutic importance of accurately distinguishing between these two entities. Thus, DBMs can pose a significant diagnostic challenge and may lead to potential misdiagnosis.[11]

It is estimated that 30–50% of patients with metastatic breast cancer (MBC) will develop brain metastases (BM).[2,8,9] In the Unicancer Epidemiological Strategy and Medical Economics MBC database (n = 16,701), 24.6% of patients developed BM, with a higher risk observed in those with HER2-positive/ hormone receptor (HR)-negative and triple-negative breast cancer.[9]

In an individual patient data meta-analysis (n = 9,524) conducted by the International Breast Cancer Study Group, which included patients with early breast cancer who had received no prior systemic therapy, the 10-year cumulative incidence of BM was higher among women with HER2-positive disease (6.8% vs. 3.5%; p < 0.01).[10] Other factors associated with the development of BM include age >70 years, the presence of more than two metastatic sites at the time of MBC diagnosis, HR negativity, and a more advanced stage of the primary tumor.[9,13]

Despite this compelling evidence, routine screening for BM is currently not recommended due to a lack of data demonstrating a clear benefit in terms of overall survival.[3]

Female predominance is observed in both meningiomas and DBMs. Both are also associated with breast carcinoma. Even classic radiological features of meningioma may be mimicked by DBM, potentially leading to delays in surgical intervention and postoperative management. This underscores the importance of understanding the unique epidemiological and diagnostic features of DBM.[11]

Lung cancer represents the most common primary malignancy associated with DBMs, accounting for approximately 57% of cases, followed by breast cancer, which contributes around 22%. Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the seeding of tumor cells within the dura mater. One of the most well-recognized pathways is direct extension from adjacent calvarial metastases into the dura, a process that is most frequently observed in malignancies such as breast and prostate cancers (PCa’s), which have a known predilection for bone involvement.[6]

However, in our case, there was no radiological or intraoperative evidence of calvarial or skull involvement, which is the most well-recognized pathway, thereby making direct extension most unlikely. This finding suggests that hematogenous dissemination is the most plausible mechanism of dural involvement in our patient, wherein tumor cells spread via the systemic circulation and subsequently seed the dural vasculature. Such a route highlights the aggressive biological behavior of the primary malignancy and its capacity for distant metastatic spread.

In a study by Tagle et al., four cases of isolated meningeal metastasis were presented, in which meningioma was considered the most probable preoperative diagnosis.[14] The primary tumors identified after histopathological evaluation were thyroid cancer, breast adenocarcinoma, prostate adenocarcinoma, and renal cell carcinoma. DBMs are widely recognized as indicators of advanced systemic malignancy, reflecting hematogenous or direct spread of cancer to the central nervous system. Their presence often signifies a high tumor burden and an aggressive disease course, thereby underscoring the importance of optimal and timely management of the primary malignancy to potentially reduce the risk of such metastatic dissemination. In terms of management, surgical resection followed by adjuvant radiotherapy has been shown to significantly improve overall survival, enhance local tumor control, and alleviate neurological symptoms. This combined approach is particularly beneficial in carefully selected patients with accessible lesions and good functional status.[5]

Notably, DBMs share several clinicopathological and radiological features with intra-parenchymal BM. Both represent manifestations of advanced systemic cancer within the central nervous system and commonly originate from similar primary malignancies, such as breast, lung, and melanoma. Clinically, they often present with comparable neurological signs and symptoms, largely attributable to mass effect, surrounding edema, or increased intracranial pressure. Furthermore, both entities demonstrate a similar therapeutic response pattern, with the best outcomes generally achieved through maximal safe surgical resection followed by adjuvant radiotherapy.[5]

Meningiomas are the most common primary brain tumors, accounting for approximately 30% of all brain tumors. They are often encountered as incidental findings in patients undergoing whole-body prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) positron emission tomography (PET) for PCa evaluation. Meningiomas are neoplasms arising from arachnoid cap cells and typically demonstrate characteristic imaging features, such as being extra-axial, dural-based lesions with homogeneous enhancement, associated hyperostosis, and with or without edema in the adjacent brain parenchyma. Both meningiomas and dural metastases may present as extra-axial masses with contrast enhancement on T1-weighted post-contrast images and are typically associated with a broad-based dural attachment. Hypervascularity can be demonstrated using dynamic CE-MRI, which may help distinguish between different grades of meningiomas.[15] [68Ga]-DOTATATE PET (1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane-tetraacetic acid 0-Tyr3-Octreotate) targets somatostatin receptor 2 on the cell surface of meningiomas with high specificity and provides improved delineation of tumor extent, particularly in post-surgical and post-radiotherapy settings, as well as in cases with osseous and parenchymal invasion.[15]

Incidentally increased PSMA avidity in the brain has also been reported in meningiomas. Meningiomas have been shown to exhibit increased vascularity, which is related to higher levels of vascular endothelial growth factor expression and microvessel density, both surrogate markers of angiogenesis. PSMA avidity in meningiomas is thought to be due to endothelial PSMA expression. However, PSMA PET findings in meningiomas have not been extensively studied. Several cases have been documented in the literature in which dural metastases were misdiagnosed as meningiomas on conventional imaging. Accurate differentiation between these two entities is crucial, as it has significant implications for prognosis and influences treatment strategies.[15]

A case report by Mankada et al. described a 67-year-old female patient, previously treated for breast cancer 22 years earlier, who was later diagnosed with a dural-based metastasis in the absence of any skeletal lesions. The patient underwent surgical excision, and HPE confirmed a dural-based metastasis of breast origin. Following surgery, she received whole-brain radiotherapy and was placed on palliative chemotherapy.[7] In our study, we were unable to perform a PET scan to rule out any distant primary lesions elsewhere in the body.

For an isolated dural lesion, meningioma is an important differential diagnosis of dural metastasis (DM), particularly in women with breast cancer, who have an increased incidence of meningiomas.[7] The presence of overlying skull metastases and bony erosion can help support the diagnosis of DM. In the absence of these features, surgical resection of the dural lesion is often required for pathological confirmation.[7]

Given these overlapping characteristics, accurate diagnosis and differentiation remain essential, as they carry important implications for prognosis, treatment planning, and overall patient management.

CONCLUSION

Isolated DM can occur in a treated case of breast cancer and should be confirmed pathologically in the absence of other skeletal metastases. DBM mimicking meningiomas are rare, but they should be considered in the differential diagnosis of extraaxial lesions. In this report, we discuss the clinical aspects of the case we observed where a DBS mimicking a meningioma was confirmed following HPE in a previously treated case of breast cancer.
