Novel Observations on the Precursor Arrhythmias of Ventricular Fibrillation in the Ambulatory Setting

Introduction

Ventricular fibrillation (VF) is a life‐threatening cardiac arrhythmia characterized by disorganized ventricular electrical activity, resulting in inadequate blood flow to vital organs and hemodynamic collapse. VF requires immediate medical intervention, cardiopulmonary resuscitation and defibrillation, to restore a viable rhythm and prevent death [1, 2, 3]. The onset of VF is unpredictable but is commonly associated with cardiac conditions, like ischemic heart disease or cardiomyopathies [4, 5, 6], that alter the electrical properties of the myocardium to allow susceptibility to VF; VF can also occur in individuals without significant structural heart disease, highlighting the complexity of its etiology [7, 8].

VF onset caused by specific clinical conditions display distinct electrical patterns, some of which have been well defined (long QT syndromes, ischemic heart disease, genetic channelopathies, exercise‐induced, idiopathic) [9, 10, 11, 12]. The importance of identifying distinct electrical patterns on telemetry, either precursor arrhythmia or an ECG change that precedes the onset of VF, is paramount to understanding why VF may have occurred in an individual patient. VF onset is commonly preceded by monomorphic ventricular tachycardia (VT) [6], with the organized electrical activity characteristic of VT subsequently degenerating into the electrical chaos of VF. While extensively studied in the inpatient setting, depiction of arrhythmogenic VF precursors in an ambulatory setting has not fully been explored. Short‐term outpatient telemetry studies have focused on the characteristics of non‐sustained VT as predictors for serious cardiac events or ablation targets [13, 14, 15, 16], or sparse data from Holter studies have been included as part of a VF study cohort [10]. However, no prior analysis has focused upon VF occurring in the ambulatory setting. Data collected from outpatient telemetry studies allows researchers to gain a deeper understanding of patterns of arrhythmia preceding VF in the ambulatory setting, this knowledge may serve as a foundation to develop interventions that limit arrhythmia progression to VF [17]. This study focused upon analysis of VF events captured by the Philips Mobile Cardiac Outpatient Telemetry (MCOT®), with the goal of categorizing the precursor arrhythmia (PA) culminating in VF.

Methods

Device Description

We performed a retrospective analysis of patients in the United States prescribed Philips MCOT (Philips, Malvern, PA, USA) in 2021–2022. Philips MCOT is a remote, ambulatory telemetry monitoring system comprised of a recording sensor attached to an adhesive patch(es), and a cellular‐enabled monitor (phone) used to analyze ECG data. Three ECG adhesive patch configurations are currently approved for use with MCOT: a patch‐based ECG electrode, a flexible ECG adapter utilizing standard “coin“ electrodes, and a 3‐lead, wired ECG configuration utilizing standard “coin” electrodes. In the current analysis, it could not be determined which configuration was worn, however, all three configurations are FDA approved for up to 3‐lead ECG analysis. MCOT monitors a patient's cardiac rhythm for up to 30 days and automatically detects and categorizes rhythms such as atrial fibrillation, tachycardia, bradycardia, pause, VT, and VF. Detected arrhythmic events are transmitted to technicians for review, preliminary interpretation, and potential clinician notification. ECG tracings are presented upon daily and end‐of‐service reports for physician review and signoff. Ethics approval was provided by WCG Institutional Review Board (IRB). A waiver of authorization for use and disclosure of protected health information was granted as the study met the criteria for exemption under 45 CFR § 46.104(d)(4). All patient data were retrospectively analyzed and de‐identified to ensure confidentiality and compliance with HIPAA regulations. The study complied with all US ethical standards and federal regulations.

Study Design

A discrete data query for technician‐reported preliminary findings of “ventricular fibrillation” was performed to identify potential patients with VF noted during MCOT monitoring. High‐level review of the events interpreted as VF was performed to adjudicate the validity of the interpretation and remove false positives. In the adjudicated subset of patients who suffered VF during MCOT, the following data were recorded: baseline rhythm, including the presence or absence of intraventricular conduction delay (IVCD) (defined as a QRS > 120 ms), basic demographics (age, sex, referral diagnosis code, prescribed prescription length), and time from baseline rhythm to VF onset. The time of VF onset was recorded as either daytime or nocturnal; daytime as 6 a.m. to 10 p.m. and nocturnal as 10 p.m. to 6 a.m., local time.

Once the onset of the VF event for each patient was identified, we retrospectively reviewed all available ECG tracings going back to the last tracings demonstrating the patient's baseline rhythm. From this point, we reviewed all subsequent tracings recorded prior to the onset of VF to determine the PA. PA was defined as the arrythmia or ECG change noted as the initial change from the baseline rhythm, irrespective of the time preceding the VF event or any subsequent dysrhythmias identified prior to the onset of VF. Although multiple arrhythmia‐types may have been observed in the period leading up to the occurrence of VF, the purpose of this analysis was to identify the first change from “baseline rhythm,” the PA. PA were categorized as: rate dependent, intraventricular conduction, re‐polarization changes, and Ectopy. Rate dependent changes were defined as either Bradycardia (Sinus Arrest > 2 s or AV conduction disturbances) or Tachycardia (heart rate > 100 BPM); AV conduction disturbances refer to 2nd degree type 2 or higher AV block. Changes in intraventricular conduction were defined as QRS widening to > 120 ms compared with baseline, or any increased widening of baseline IVCD. Re‐polarization changes were defined as ST‐depression or ST‐elevation, and prolonged QTc, defined as a QTc measurement > 500 ms via Bazzett's formula. Ectopy refers to patients whose rhythm demonstrated a single premature ventricular contraction (PVC) immediately preceding the onset of VF in the setting of a normal QTc. If the Ectopy (PVC) was observed in the setting of QT prolongation, we categorized the precursor arrhythmia as “prolonged QTc.” Monomorphic VT degrading into VF was recorded. Data available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Results

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics

During 2021–2022, 271 MCOT patients were identified with at least one ECG tracing with technician‐labeled VF; following event adjudication, 71 (26%) patients were identified as having true VF, and 74% of technician‐labeled VF were determined not to be true VF. The patient population of true VF were 52% men with a mean (± SD) age of 71.3 ± 9.7 years, and 48% women with a mean (± SD) age of 75.8 ± 10.6 years (Table 1). The most common MCOT referral diagnosis was syncope and collapse (34%), followed by palpitations (18%). The average prescribed study duration was 25 days (70% of all studies had a prescribed duration of 30 days). Sinus rhythm was identified at baseline in 65 (92%) patients, with sinus bradycardia (rate < 60 BPM) in 12 (19%), normal sinus rhythm (rate 60–100 BPM) in 49 (75%), and sinus tachycardia (rate > 100 BPM) in 4 (6%); atrial fibrillation was seen at baseline in six (8%) patients. When baseline sinus rhythm was observed, eight (12%) patients noted a first‐degree atrioventricular conduction delay, with an average PR interval of 266 ms. At baseline, 14 (20%) patients were noted to have a QRS duration > 120 ms (Table 1).

Detection of Precursor Arrythmias

The most common PA identified was Ectopy in 26 (37%) patients (Figures 1 and 2); all ectopic events were attributed to PVCs. Re‐polarization changes were the second most common PA, identified in 18 (25%) patients, with ST‐depression seen in 9 (13%) patients, ST‐elevation in 6 (8%) patients (Figure 3), and prolonged QTc measurement greater than 500 ms, by Bazett's formula, noted in 3 (4%) patients (Figure 4). Widening of the QRS complex > 120 ms, when compared with baseline, or an increased duration of baseline IVCD, was observed in 10 (14%) patients. Rate dependent changes were noted in 17 (24%) patients, with bradycardia in 15 (21%) patients and tachycardia, due to AF with rapid ventricular response, in 2 (3%) patients. Sinus arrest (P–P interval > 2 s, with or without an escape rhythm) was identified in 7 (10%) patients (Figure 5) and AV conduction disturbances prior to the onset of VF were observed in eight (11%) patients, all adjudicated as 3rd degree AV block (Figure 6).

Incidence of VF

The average time to VF onset from the start of monitoring was 9.7 days in men and 7.7 days in women. Daytime VF events were similar in men (n = 24; 34%) compared with women (n = 21; 30%), both groups had an equal prevalence for nocturnal VF (18%). Monomorphic VT was observed prior to degradation to VF in 27 (38%) studies (Figure 7). Within this group demonstrating monomorphic VT, the PA was Ectopy in 15 (56%) studies, IVCD in 5 (19%) studies, re‐polarization changes in 4 (15%) studies, and bradycardia including both sinus arrest and 3rd degree AV block in 3 (11%) studies.

Discussion

Although VF is well recognized as a fatal dysrhythmia, there are sparse data available on how patients progress to VF in the ambulatory setting, with early studies limited to short periods of monitoring using a Holter [10, 18, 19]. In an attempt to explore potential mechanisms of VF in the outpatient setting, this analysis sought to identify the precursor arrhythmia/changes in ECG preceding VF in patients wearing Philips MCOT.

It is well recognized from data in patients with implantable cardioverter defibrillators that monomorphic VT is often present before VF, which is the reason that anti‐tachycardia pacing can often prevent shock therapy [20, 21]. However, our data showed that PVCs in the setting of a normal QTc were the most common events preceding VF, the second most common was re‐polarization changes. The re‐polarization changes likely reflected an ischemic or metabolic change in the ventricular milieu or a long QT syndrome, whereas the PVCs likely reflected ischemic, idiopathic or channelopathy VF substrates [10]. We suspect that the sudden slowing of sinus rate, AV conduction delay, and QRS prolongation seen as PA may reflect hypoxia, possibly related to ischemia, but other inciting causes in our patients cannot be excluded. Ideally, we would correlate the electrical PA findings noted in this analysis with patient level data to link the PA with a clinical condition. However, we did not have access to patient clinical data, so our study is limited to describing the frequency and characteristics of electrical findings noted prior to VF during ambulatory monitoring. While a limitation of our study, the value of characterizing and quantifying the electrical presentations preceding VF that are described in this analysis, the largest dataset to date focusing on the ambulatory setting, cannot be underscored. Knowing the presentation of PA for VF will allow future research to analyze ECG patterns during monitoring, likely with the help of artificial intelligence. The hope will be to identify patients at high risk for VF early during the monitoring period in order to prevent sudden death.

Although it is well known that isolated PVCs can initiate runs of VT, even when Ectopy was identified as the PA monomorphic VT was noted to precede VF in only 27 (38%) patients. This is much different than the findings reported in the sudden death holter studies which reported higher rates of VT as a precursor to VF (62.4%–75.5%) [19, 20]. The Leclercq et al. Holter study also noted a notable increase in atrial arrhythmias in the hour before sudden death caused by VF [19]. These findings underscore the complex mechanism of VF induction and highlight the need for a broad clinical focus that extends beyond monitoring for VT, particularly in those at risk for VF.

Study Limitations

This analysis was undertaken retrospectively and thus carries limitations inherent to a retrospective analysis.

Perhaps the biggest limitation is the lack of specific patient information regarding underlying clinical conditions, comorbidities, and medications available for review. While sudden changes in repolarization are suspected to be due to hypoxia, it is unknown what event or condition caused or may have contributed to such a change, and thus categorization of the PA should be considered objective and not etiologic.

As ECG tracings transmitted from the MCOT monitor will only be routine daily ECG tracings and triggered arrhythmic events, this analysis only included these transmitted ECG tracings. Categorization and distribution of the PA could have been affected by the lack of full disclosure recordings available for review and analysis.

This study was derived from a Philips MCOT hardware and software offering utilizing specific triggering criteria. The triggering criteria could have impacted ECG strips available for review, and thus impacted the distribution of PA. As MCOT is prescribed for specific clinical indications, the findings of this analysis may only be representative of patients prescribed MCOT and not extensible to either the general population or to ambulatory patients receiving different forms of telemetry monitoring. Additionally, non‐arrhythmic ECG findings, like ST segment variation or QRS duration, may have been impacted by differences in the ECG recording electrodes worn during the MCOT study (i.e., patch‐based ECG electrode, vs. a flexible ECG adapter utilizing standard “coin” electrodes, vs. a three‐lead, wired ECG configuration utilizing standard “coin” electrodes) as the configurations may have varied in the recorded vector and/or signal amplitude. Future studies may benefit from standardization of the recording ECG utilized.

Adjudication of VF events demonstrated that most of the technician‐labeled VF were not actually VF. This is not surprising as technicians are trained to overdiagnose VF in order to not miss these events, and since differentiation from artifact can be challenging. Conversely, it is possible that true VF events were not labeled as such by technicians, and hence these events and their associated PA would not have been included in our dataset.

Conclusion

This study reveals that Ectopy (PVCs) and Re‐polarization changes, most commonly ST‐depression, are the most common precursor arrhythmia or ECG change prior to the onset of VF in an ambulatory population of patients prescribed Philips MCOT. Monomorphic VT preceding VF was identified in only 38% of studies (Central Illustration 1).

Patients receiving ambulatory ECG monitoring most commonly exhibited Ectopy, re‐polarization changes, and rate dependent changes prior to VF. Monomorphic VT was detected in only 38% of patients.
