HTA Evidence in Rare Diseases: Just Rare or Also Special?
Authors: Anirban Basu, Simu K Thomas, Richard H Chapman, Jason Spangler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractManufacturers of orphan drugs face several obstacles in meeting health technology assessment requirements because of poor availability of natural history data, small sample sizes, single-arm trials, and a paucity of established disease-specific endpoints. There is a need for specific considerations and modified approaches in health technology assessments that would account for the challenges in orphan drug development. Multistakeholder collaborations can benefit patients, their families, and the broader society and reduce the inequity faced by patients with rare diseases.

Key Points for Decision Makers

Challenges associated with a health technology assessment of orphan drugs span multiple areas, including the availability of natural history data and disease-specific endpoints, clinical trial design, and small sample sizes.Current health technology assessment processes and methods often fail to adequately accommodate the evidence complexities associated with assessing interventions for rare diseases.Health technology assessment processes and requirements should be modified to account for the evidence challenges in rare diseases, ultimately improving access to innovative drugs for patients.

The Unique Nature of Rare Disease (RD) in a Health Technology Assessment (HTA)

There are tremendous unmet needs in the treatment of rare diseases (RDs). An RD is defined as a disease or condition that affects relatively few individuals, for example, < 200,000 in the USA, 1:2000 in the European Union (EU) [1, 2]; however, alternative RD definitions exist, taking into account aspects such as the severe and life-threatening nature of the condition and the inability to recoup the costs of developing treatment through future sales [3]. While >400 million people worldwide live with an RD [4], there is an approximately 5-year delay to receiving an RD diagnosis [5], and 95% of RDs lack approved treatments [6], resulting in inequity of access and care. Many RDs are life-threatening or life-limiting genetic conditions, and roughly half of affected patients are children [7, 8].

The societal impact of RD is immense, spanning the costs of lost productivity, travel to medical appointments, and family impacts (e.g., childcare) [9]. A 2019 review of the national economic burden of RD in the USA found that 55% of the burden was attributable to costs other than direct medical costs (e.g., productivity loss, home adaptations) [10]. Although the economic burden of an RD on the patient and their family may vary between healthcare and social support systems, the societal impact persists. Intangible impacts of an RD include uncertainty about the future and inequity in access to diagnosis and treatment experienced by individuals with RD, their families, and their caregivers. Families of individuals with an RD have been found to experience psychosocial impacts that are unique to or made worse by the rare nature of the condition [11].

Orphan drugs, defined as medicines used to treat, prevent, or diagnose an RD, are unlikely to generate sufficient returns to justify their development because of low sales volumes secondary to limited RD prevalence [12, 13]. Several legislative initiatives have been introduced across the world to encourage innovation in the area of orphan drug development [14], notable examples being the US Orphan Drug Act and the EU Regulation (EC) No. 141/2000 [15, 16]. However, patient access to these drugs can sometimes be delayed or restricted because of the challenges associated with a health technology assessment (HTA), which is generally designed for therapies for more prevalent diseases. Indeed, for orphan drugs, HTA requirements may be stricter than those imposed by regulatory agencies. Health technology assessments typically evaluate evidence on the effects, costs, and additional outcomes associated with health interventions (e.g., drugs, medical devices, or vaccines) to inform decision making on the allocation of scarce resources across the covered population [17]. The evidence packages expected by HTA agencies generally include results from large, controlled, randomized clinical trials encompassing quality-of-life data and subgroup analyses, and indirect comparisons against any relevant comparators to which the assessed intervention was not compared directly [18]. However, the use of a single HTA framework for both common diseases and RDs could hamper scientific innovations in RDs and negatively impact patient health and societal well-being. Several HTA agencies have already taken steps to account for disease severity, rarity, a lack of alternative therapies, and other unique challenges associated with RDs [18], whether through modified HTA requirements applied to orphan drugs within the existing processes or, in the case of the Highly Specialised Technologies program of the National Institute of Health and Care Excellence (NICE) in the UK, a separate process dedicated to the assessment of orphan drugs [19].

Developing innovative treatments in RDs is challenging (with an estimated 6% clinical trial success rate) [20], which poses a substantial risk for biomedical investors concerned about the return on their investments. In many markets, access to innovative orphan drugs is limited by the need to meet typical cost-effectiveness thresholds used in common diseases and/or concerns around the budget impact. However, such a system may adversely affect innovation and does not align with public views on healthcare priorities. Like every other health intervention, orphan drugs are associated with opportunity costs, i.e., the costs associated with forgoing another intervention because of spending that portion of a limited healthcare budget on high-cost orphan drugs. On the one hand, if cost-effectiveness thresholds are derived from the supply side, they reflect the opportunity costs only within the healthcare sector, overlooking the significant value generated by RD treatments outside the healthcare sector. On the other hand, if these are demand-based thresholds, they reflect the societal opportunity costs, and hence value calculations should take a full societal perspective to make them comparable to these thresholds. With regard to public views on healthcare, comprehensive systematic reviews indicate that while disease rarity in itself is not associated with a social preference, there is a willingness to pay for other aspects generally associated with RDs, such as disease severity, equity of access to therapy, unmet need (a lack of alternative effective therapies), and the opportunity for a greater proportional advantage compared with more common diseases (i.e., treatment helping a greater proportion of affected patients) [21–23].

The paragraphs below describe the specific challenges that orphan drug manufacturers face when addressing HTA requirements. Difficulties in fulfilling these requirements may result in HTA agencies considering the evidence package to be insufficient to adequately quantify the value of an orphan drug, ultimately resulting in a negative recommendation.

HTA Evidence Challenges for RDs

Lack of Natural History Data and/or International Classification of Diseases Codes

In general, RDs often have poorly understood disease pathophysiology, large variations in disease manifestations, and high unpredictability [24, 25]. Furthermore, a dearth of International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision diagnosis codes and natural history cohorts makes it difficult to characterize RDs, understand real-world treatment patterns, and establish a standard of care. Without the relevant data, this information is often derived from descriptions within case reports, physician interviews, and, if available, electronic medical records. The paucity of natural history data and International Classification of Diseases codes poses several issues for HTAs. It hinders accurate estimation of the size of the treatment-eligible population, adding substantial uncertainty to a budget impact assessment. Long-term extrapolation of treatment effect is difficult without reliable natural history data, potentially impacting survival and quality-adjusted life-year estimates and, ultimately, the outcome of a cost-effectiveness assessment for an orphan drug.

The lack of established treatments compounds the problem. Rare disease clinicians and patients are left to trial-and-error approaches [26], resulting in a highly variable care pathway. From the HTA perspective, this results in substantial variability between various pieces of (often scarce) evidence and complicates the selection of appropriate comparators. For example, among 78 research questions for 20 orphan drugs undergoing a full HTA in Germany between 2011 and 2021, active comparators were available for 58% of the research questions, with the remaining assessments using best supportive care or watchful waiting only as a comparator [27]. This poses an issue for both cost-effectiveness and budget impact assessments, as the cost of supportive care or symptomatic-only treatment is often relatively low compared with the costs of active treatment, particularly with novel agents.

Small Sample Sizes

In RDs, a clinical trial with 100–150 patients can be considered large. Within such a trial, randomization may be 2:1, meaning that one third of patients receive placebo or, if available, an active comparator. Subgroup analyses may contain cohorts of < 30 patients, which often necessitate non-parametric statistical tests. As an example, in a sickle cell disease gene therapy trial, only 25 of 35 total patients could be evaluated for an event rate analysis [28]. Even if trial results appear compelling, small numbers can prevent the detection of statistically significant differences and can lead to a judgment that the results are inconclusive. Symptom heterogeneity in RDs can magnify these difficulties. Measuring a symptom benefit may require the assessment of several endpoints in one trial, compounding the challenge of adequate statistical power in small trial populations.

As the trial data often directly inform economic modelling, challenges in demonstrating a treatment effect because of the small sample size are likely to translate into uncertainty around survival and quality-adjusted life-year estimates in the model. This issue is particularly relevant for subgroups, which may include very few patients in an RD trial.

Use of Non-Traditional Study Designs

Health technology assessment agencies have made it clear that they consider randomized clinical trials to provide the highest level of evidence on the efficacy and safety of a drug [29–31]. While we agree, it should be noted that Ethics Review Boards often reject the use of a placebo arm for severe RDs, especially those that are rapidly progressing or that lack approved treatments. Although effect size estimations may be calculated using a naturalistic or pre-specified historical control (external control arm), these techniques can be viewed as less valid compared with randomized clinical trials. Furthermore, generation of comparative evidence via a network meta-analysis is often impossible because of the paucity of studies and heterogeneity of populations and trial designs. This often necessitates the use of alternative indirect comparison methodologies, such as simulated treatment comparisons or matching-adjusted indirect comparisons [32]. The latter method is often more practical for use in HTAs that list few comparators but require an assessment of multiple outcomes of interest. However, in the setting of a limited sample size in many orphan drug trials, the effective sample sizes in a matching-adjusted indirect comparison may be very low and the resulting evidence may be deemed inconclusive and inappropriate to inform decision making.

Lack of Robust and Disease-Specific Study Endpoints

A positive regulatory decision is uncertain for most orphan drugs in development, partly because of disagreement on trial endpoints. If established endpoints exist in an RD, they often “borrow” assessments from more common diseases, even though these insufficiently capture symptoms or treatment benefit in RDs. For example, physicians treating neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder typically have a background in multiple sclerosis, where the Expanded Disability Status Scale [33] is commonly employed to measure disability. However, the Expanded Disability Status Scale fails to capture the full range of vision disability in neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, as vision is only one of six parameters that it assesses. This makes it more difficult to measure treatment benefit in neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder trials using the Expanded Disability Status Scale.

Furthermore, disease-specific patient-reported outcome measures do not exist for most RDs, so trials commonly employ generic instruments such as the 36-item Short Form Survey [34–36]. The lack of patient-reported outcome measures that adequately capture outcomes important to patients with RDs and their caregivers might lead to an underestimation of (or the complete omission of) the effects of an intervention. This issue emerged for example during the recent NICE appraisal of a gene therapy for transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia, where concerns were raised about the ability of the non-disease specific EQ-5D questionnaire to adequately capture the symptom burden and treatment effect [37].

While the generation of new endpoints in an RD is a possible solution, it takes years to establish and validate an endpoint and, because of the small size of the affected population, validation of the robustness of a novel endpoint in an RD is particularly challenging. An example of such challenges is well illustrated by the pivotal trial of an enzyme replacement therapy for mucopolysaccharidosis type VII, an ultra-RD with a global prevalence of less than 1 in 1 million individuals [38]. The trial, which enrolled 12 patients, assessed clinical efficacy based on the Multi-Domain Responder Index, combining several independent, clinically relevant assessments [38]. However, because of the heterogeneity of the disease presentations (including its cognitive impact), not all components of this endpoint could be completed by all patients, resulting in a substantial proportion of missing data [38]. Consequently, the regulatory assessment for this orphan drug primarily considered only a single component of the Multi-Domain Responder Index, the 6-Minute Walk Test [39, 40], which is a non-disease specific measure of walking capacity and endurance, commonly used in clinical trials across multiple therapeutic areas.

Another approach used in RD trials is to combine a disease-specific measure and a broad generic measure into a composite endpoint, as exemplified by a recent trial in Rett syndrome, which combined the Rett Syndrome Behaviour Questionnaire and the Clinical Global Impression-Improvement scale into a composite co-primary endpoint [41, 42]. However, this is not a one-size-fits-all solution, as it requires the availability of a disease-specific endpoint with some established validity (such as the Rett Syndrome Behaviour Questionnaire [43]), which may not be an option for ultra-orphan diseases or scenarios in which the regulatory pathway, where endpoint selection plays a key role, is highly uncertain.

Time is also of the essence when considering endpoint development in RDs. Many RDs have no approved therapies and, as patients with RDs desperately await treatment options, manufacturers usually face a trade-off between demonstrating the ability of an intervention to mitigate disease progression over the shorter term versus potentially longer drug development timelines that would enable demonstrating the full and longer term benefits of the intervention. This is particularly important in RDs with nascent and borrowed endpoints that may fail to capture the full treatment benefit to patients and their families. Furthermore, despite the severe nature of many RDs and the fact that affected patients are often children, the disease impact or treatment benefit on caregivers and family is not routinely considered in HTAs [44].

Are Current HTA Solutions Enough?

Recognizing the quandaries described above, some countries have adopted special considerations for RDs in their HTA processes. Although these considerations vary substantially across countries, many stipulate that disease rarity alone is insufficient to warrant a distinct HTA process and pose additional requirements that an orphan drug must meet. For example, to qualify for NICE’s aforementioned Highly Specialised Technologies program, a disease must be both very rare (1:50,000 in England, with no more than 500 people eligible for the treatment across all indications) and severe (reduced life expectancy or severely impaired quality of life) [19]. Furthermore, there must be either no alternative treatments available or the new treatment must demonstrate a significant additional benefit [19]. In the aforementioned gene therapy example, the Highly Specialised Technologies criteria were not met and the HTA followed the standard single technology appraisal process, despite the prevalent transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia population being estimated at only 920 patients across the UK [37]. In Germany, there is no dedicated HTA process for orphan drugs; however, a budget impact cap is used to determine whether an orphan drug is required to undergo a full HTA. The G-BA automatically assigns an “added benefit” to orphan drugs at the time of approval but mandates a full value assessment once combined annual sales exceed €30 million and this assessment forms the basis for price negotiations [45]. An analysis of orphan drug prices published by the Institute for Quality and Efficiency in Health Care reported that, among 23 full assessments conducted between 2011 and mid-2022, 78% resulted in a price reduction relative to the launch price [46]. The mean price reduction was higher for 13 orphan drugs for which no added benefit was demonstrated in any sub-population considered (−19.5% with a range of −40.3% to −5%) compared with the ten drugs for which an added benefit was demonstrated in at least one sub-population (−3.7% with a range of −31.9% to +14.2%) [46].

The issue of price reductions secondary to an HTA raises concerns related to achieving dynamic efficiency in the RD space. Dynamic efficiency is a well-established economic concept describing the ability to optimize resource allocation and innovate over time to maximize growth [47]. In the setting of healthcare funding, dynamic efficiency refers to balancing affordability of care for the payers with providing sufficient incentives for the manufacturer to continue investing in innovation. Several pricing and financing models have been discussed in the context of achieving dynamic efficiency in pharmaceutical innovation [48, 49]. For ultra-RDs, however, even if a perfect cure becomes available and investors are allowed to appropriate the full surplus of these cures, that may be insufficient to recoup the costs of investments, making investing in RDs with particularly small population sizes economically unviable. Consequently, valuing and pricing such cures using traditional benchmarks commonly used in HTAs is likely to compromise future innovations.

Finally, current HTA methods result in long delays from approval to launch for orphan drugs across European countries, apart from Germany (Fig. 1) [50]. This is despite initial discussions between manufacturers and HTA agencies often taking place well ahead of the approval and generally reflects the scrupulous nature of the HTA process rather than administrative delays. However, it should be noted that the HTA landscape is continuously evolving and efforts to accelerate the assessment are ongoing. One such example is the Joint Clinical Assessment, established by EU Regulation No. 2021/2282, which provides an assessment of the health benefits of a technology to HTA agencies across member states [51]. Such efforts have already successfully been undertaken on a smaller scale through the EUnetHTA collaboration, the Beneluxa initiative, and the Joint Nordic HTA Bodies collaboration, with the latter two also conducting joint economic assessments [52–55]. The Joint Clinical Assessment will become applicable to all orphan drugs as of January 2028 [51] and will, hopefully, reduce the duplication of efforts for both HTA agencies and the industry and, ultimately, accelerate the availability of novel orphan drugs for patients across the EU. It should, however, be noted that the Joint Clinical Assessment is restricted to a clinical benefit, while the assessment of cost effectiveness and/or budget impact and the ultimate decision to recommend for reimbursement remain country specific.

What Can Be Done?

We recommend the following approaches to address the HTA challenges with RDs:

(1) Broaden the perspective used to define value for orphan drugs by comprehensively considering their potential benefits and cost offsets, including the widely taken into account direct medical costs (e.g., the costs associated with a hospitalization) as well as the less commonly considered direct non-medical costs (out-of-pocket costs associated with medical appointments, e.g., travel and accommodation), and indirect costs (e.g., the costs of lost income as a result of caring for a child with an RD). The EveryLife Foundation for Rare Disease has demonstrated that half of the disease burden for RDs falls outside of the healthcare sector (e.g., ability to work, perform everyday tasks, and see children grow up) [56], suggesting that the inclusion of only direct medical costs substantially underestimates the true value of orphan drugs. However, broadening of the assessment perspective requires widely accepted methods for considering patient, caregiver, and family perspectives and lived experiences outside of clinical trials in HTAs. While these may take a while to establish, the potential for scientific spillover effects resulting from RD research and treatments should be evaluated, including advancements that could impact common outcomes across RD areas and potential applications in more common diseases.

(2) Develop an extended evaluation framework for RDs that not only describes what outcomes should be considered, but also how those outcomes are ascertained during the HTA process, and how their assessment may impact access to treatment. Transparent guidance on when and how decision making can proceed with the additional uncertainty could help reduce the duration of the HTA process. An example of a framework enabling patient access despite uncertainty would be the NICE managed access process which, while not specific to orphan drugs, enables an intervention to be reimbursed despite substantial uncertainty and mandates an additional data collection (usually over a period of several years) followed by a reassessment [57]. In addition to tackling uncertainty around the evidence base, it is important for HTA agencies to adopt clear and socially acceptable approaches for overcoming inequality and inequity because of the rarity and severity of RDs and to incorporate these considerations when valuing outcomes in an RD. An example of acting on equity principles are the changes to funding for the aforementioned managed access agreements in England. The initiative of funding an innovative medication while further evidence is collected started with the Cancer Drugs Fund [58], restricted to oncology medications. Under the Innovative Medicines Fund, it was subsequently broadened to other therapeutic areas [59], including potential application in RD.

(3) Given the diversity and heterogeneity of RDs, we recommend considering outcomes beyond aggregated cost effectiveness, such as a cost-consequence or multi-criteria decision analysis, with all important outcomes depicted through an Impact Inventory as recommended by the Second Panel on Cost-Effectiveness in Health and Medicine [60]. Grand et al. have comprehensively described the challenges associated with economic evaluations of orphan drugs and highlighted the paucity of epidemiological, natural history, and humanistic and economic burden evidence that could inform economic models, as well as the limitations of clinical data in RDs [61]. These data limitations mean that the inputs for cost-effectiveness and budget impact models in RDs are associated with substantial uncertainty and may need to be sourced, for example, from more common diseases with some overlap in symptoms or health consequences, or from expert opinion, which may be difficult to obtain owing to the rarity of the condition [61]. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no RD-specific guidance on informing economic models exists, posing potential challenges of very diverse input preferences between different HTA agencies, ultimately resulting in prolonged access times for patients.

These issues may be partly overcome by expanding the value assessment criteria beyond a traditional cost-effectiveness analysis using a multicriteria decision analysis, an emerging approach that uses a multistage framework to support complex decision making and has been comprehensively reviewed in the context of HTAs of orphan drugs by Lasalvia et al. [62]. Where sourcing appropriate utility and/or epidemiological data is the main issue, adopting a more flexible cost-consequence framework may also facilitate the HTA process.

(4) Adopt a partnership approach, including sharing risks of costs and consequences for treatment. While some of the HTA challenges for RDs could conceivably be addressed by manufacturers (e.g., more research and development/outcomes research spending or earlier multistakeholder engagement with clinical experts, patient organizations, and physician organizations), others cannot be solved by manufacturers alone. Regardless of these issues, HTAs need to proceed even when evidence is limited to ensure fair access to patients with an RD. Collaboration between researchers, physician organizations, patient advocacy groups, RD organizations, and the drug development industry is vital to address the needs of patients with an RD.

In turn, better collaboration between regulatory and HTA agencies is needed to create an environment that nurtures scientific innovation. Early multistakeholder engagement, development and validation of endpoints, and conducting natural history studies (which in RDs are often multinational efforts involving multiple centers with only a handful patients each) are examples of considerable early investments that manufacturers make despite an uncertain future of a drug in terms of regulatory and HTA approvals. A welcome step in the direction of facilitating innovation are the joint scientific consultations from the European Medicines Agency and European HTA bodies that will provide manufacturers with scientific advice on generating evidence that satisfies both regulatory and HTA requirements [51].

(5) Bring concepts of dynamic efficiency into the HTA field. Fair pricing of orphan drugs is crucial for maintaining an interest in innovation in the RD area. If transferring the entire social surplus generated by an innovation in RD to the manufacturer is insufficient to recoup development costs of innovations, alternatives should be considered to ensure patient access, including patent buyouts, relaxed cost-effectiveness thresholds, and dedicated budgets for funding orphan drugs.

We firmly believe that HTA evidence in RD is not just rare, but also special. The particular challenges associated with HTA in RDs warrant special considerations for orphan drugs, independent of whether these are weaved into existing HTA processes for more common conditions, or a dedicated process is established. By working together to systematically address the HTA challenges outlined above, cross-functional stakeholders can meaningfully move the needle on ensuring patients access to life-changing interventions and overcoming the health inequities faced by the RD community.
