Inflammatory Bowel Disease in Jordanian Children: A Tertiary Center Experience
Authors: Hala Almomani, Ayah Alsmadi, Nicole Issi, Maria AlShurman, Eyad Altamimi
Published: 2025

INTRODUCTION

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic inflammatory condition of the gastrointestinal tract that includes Crohn’s disease (CD), ulcerative colitis (UC), and indeterminate colitis (IC). These disorders are characterized by periods of relapse and remission and can profoundly affect quality of life, particularly when diagnosed in pediatric patients [1]. The pathogenesis of IBD is multifactorial and involves genetic susceptibility, environmental influences, immunological dysregulation, and alterations in the gut microbiota (dysbiosis) [2].

Approximately 10% of all IBD cases have pediatric onset [3]. The incidence and prevalence of pediatric IBD are increasing globally, especially in high-income countries. Studies show that the highest rates occur in North America and Europe, with substantial increases now being reported in Asia and other developing regions [4]. This trend represents a growing global health concern and underscores the importance of early detection and management strategies tailored to the pediatric population.

Data on pediatric IBD in Arab populations remain limited. However, recent reports indicate a rising frequency in countries such as Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and Egypt [5]. The clinical manifestations of IBD include both intestinal and extraintestinal symptoms, and pediatric patients are particularly susceptible to growth impairment and nutritional deficiencies [6,7]. Although the clinical features and disease progression among Arab children appear similar to those reported in Western populations, region-specific genetic and environmental factors may influence disease behavior [8].

Research on IBD, particularly pediatric IBD, in Jordan is scarce, with few studies describing its epidemiology and clinical characteristics. This lack of data highlights the need for comprehensive studies to improve understanding of IBD within the Jordanian context. The present study aimed to address this gap by providing an in-depth analysis of the clinical features, management, and outcomes of pediatric IBD cases treated at King Abdullah University Hospital (KAUH). It also compared findings with data from other regions to identify distinguishing trends and shared characteristics among Arab pediatric patients with IBD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We conducted a retrospective review of the medical records of patients diagnosed with IBD before the age of 18 years who were followed up at the Pediatric Gastroenterology Clinic at Jordan University Hospital between January 2015 and December 2022. The diagnoses were established according to the revised Porto criteria.

Study population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary Pediatric Gastroenterology Unit in Irbid, North Jordan. All enrolled patients were diagnosed between 2013 and 2023 and attended at least two follow-up visits. Patients diagnosed after 18 years of age were excluded, as were those who did not complete two follow-up visits after diagnosis.

Data acquisition, clinical characterization, and outcome assessment

Electronic hospital records and endoscopy databases were reviewed. Data on patient demographics, clinical manifestations, endoscopic findings, disease location, disease behavior, and growth impairment (as defined by the Paris classification) [9] were collected. Laboratory results and treatment data were also obtained. In addition, patient outcomes and the occurrence of complications were documented.

Definitions

The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) [10] was calculated by dividing absolute neutrophil count by absolute lymphocyte count. NLR >2.26 considered elevated.

Platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) [10] was calculated by dividing the absolute platelet count by the absolute lymphocyte count. PLR>179.8 considered elevated

Treatment Escalation: switching from a less potent immunosuppressant or relapse occurring during established maintenance therapy [11].

Fecal calprotectin test: A non-invasive biomarker assay to assess intestinal inflammation. Its concentration in the stool correlates with neutrophil migration to the intestinal mucosa, making it a sensitive indicator of inflammatory activity in pediatric IBD (The test was not available in our facility and was only occasionally obtained externally).

Growth Impairment was defined as a height or weight measurement consistently below the 3rd percentile for age and sex or a significant decrease in growth velocity crossing two major percentile lines on standardized growth charts (we used CDC growth charts).

Statistical analysis

The data were collected and entered into an Excel spreadsheet. The means and standard deviations of the data were calculated. The frequencies of events and their percentages are presented.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Research Committee of the Faculty of Medicine at the Jordan University of Science and Technology (20230094). As the patient data were anonymous, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

RESULTS

A total of 31 patients (61.3% male) were included in this study. The mean age at diagnosis was 8.7±4.75 years. Very early-onset IBD (VEO-IBD) accounted for 38.7% of all cases. Crohn’s disease (51.6%) was more common than UC (29.0%) or unclassified IBD (19.4%). Diarrhea and abdominal pain were the most frequent presenting symptoms. Steroids were used for remission induction in 90.3% of cases, while %-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA) was the most commonly prescribed maintenance therapy (48.4%).

Demographics and clinical characteristics

During the study period, 31 pediatric patients were diagnosed and followed-up at our Pediatric Gastroenterology Clinic. The cohort showed a slight predominance of females (n=18; 58.1%). The mean age at diagnosis was 8.71±4.75 years, and the average duration of follow-up was 3.7±0.5 years.

Crohn’s disease was diagnosed in 17 patients (54.8%). The most common disease location was ileocolonic (L3) (n=5; 29.4%). Most patients with CD had non-fistulizing, non-stricturing disease (B1) (n=10; 58.8%). Growth impairment (G1) was observed in seven patients (41.2%), with females more frequently affected than males (five [45.5%] vs. two [33.3%]) (Fig. 1).

Management and patients’ outcomes

Steroids were the most frequently used medication for remission induction, administered to 28 patients (90.3%). Two patients (6.5%) underwent exclusive enteral therapy for induction and both responded favorably to a polymeric diet. Biologic therapies were not readily available during the study period; therefore, none of the patients received these agents initially (Table 4).

Thiopurines and 5-ASA compounds were the most commonly prescribed maintenance therapies, used in nine (29.0%) and 15 (48.4%) patients, respectively. DISCUSSION

This retrospective study analyzed a cohort of pediatric patients with IBD diagnosed and followed at a tertiary hospital in North Jordan. Despite the small sample size, this study highlights pediatric IBD as an emerging concern in the regional pediatric population. The age distribution and sex ratio were consistent with findings reported in the literature. Similarly, the clinical presentation and disease behavior were aligned with those observed in larger international cohorts.

Benchimol et al. [12] reported that the incidence and prevalence of pediatric-onset IBD are increasing globally, with the highest rates observed in Northern Europe and North America. Regions such as the Middle East, including ours, have also reported a rising trend in pediatric IBD. A systematic review by Mosli et al. [5] concluded that IBD incidence in Arab countries is growing, though patients in this region may exhibit distinct clinical characteristics compared with Western populations. Although our study did not estimate incidence or prevalence, the increasing number of diagnosed patients supports this emerging trend.

Gender difference

Shah et al. [13] found in a pooled analysis of population-based studies that males were more frequently affected by Crohn’s disease during childhood, while UC showed no significant sex difference. Similarly, Ye et al. [14] reported a higher prevalence of all IBD types in boys during childhood in the United States, with the trend reversing in adulthood, showing female predominance. Comparable male predominance was also observed in small cohorts of Australian [15] and Saudi [16] pediatric patients of Middle Eastern descent. The female predominance observed in our study likely reflects sampling variation due to the limited cohort size.

The mean age at diagnosis for pediatric IBD varies across regions but generally ranges between 8 and 14 years. A U.S. study reported median ages of 12.5 years for UC and 13 years for CD [17]. A 13-year national study from the Czech Republic found median ages of 14.5 years for UC and 13.6 years for CD, with increasing pediatric IBD incidence over time [18]. In Saudi Arabia, a multicenter national study of 352 pediatric patients (ages 0–14 years) reported mean ages at diagnosis of 8.12±3.77 years for UC and 12±3.6 years for CD, with 21.6% diagnosed before age six [19]. The mean age at diagnosis in our cohort (8.71 years) aligns with these findings, and the younger age of patients with CD may be attributed to the relatively high proportion (25%) of very early-onset IBD cases.

In our cohort, Crohn’s disease (54.8%) was more common than UC, consistent with global trends showing higher CD prevalence among pediatric patients in Western countries [20]. Historically, UC predominated in regions such as South Asia; however, recent studies indicate a global shift toward increasing CD incidence [21]. In the Arabian Gulf, a similar pattern of higher CD incidence in children has been documented [22].

Among our patients, ileocolonic (L3) involvement was most common (29.4%), mirroring findings from multicenter studies in Saudi Arabia [23], Japan [24], and Scotland [15]. Disease progression monitoring remains essential in CD. van Limbergen et al. [25] reported that although most patients initially presented with inflammatory disease (B1), 25% developed stricturing or fistulizing disease (B2, B3) over four years. In our study, over half of the patients presented with B1 disease, and approximately 20% progressed to more complicated forms despite treatment—findings comparable to international reports.

Abdominal pain, diarrhea, and weight loss are common presenting symptoms of pediatric IBD, with regional variation in frequency [22]. In a Saudi cohort, diarrhea (89.4%), rectal bleeding (75.8%), and abdominal pain (62%) were most frequent [16]. Our results similarly showed diarrhea (77.4%) as the most common presenting symptom, followed by abdominal pain (64.5%) and weight loss or poor weight gain (35.5%), consistent with findings from a Pakistani cohort [26].

The small size of our UC subgroup limits detailed comparison, but pancolitis was among the predominant presentations, as also reported in Saudi [16] and Pakistani [26] cohorts.

Growth failure at the time of pediatric IBD diagnosis—especially in CD—is a major concern and is associated with delayed puberty, reduced adult height, and decreased bone mineral density [18,27,28]. Reported prevalence varies by region and is influenced by disease severity, nutritional status, and healthcare access. A multicenter study from developing countries reported growth impairment in 50% of cases [29], with CD patients more commonly affected. Global estimates range between 15–40%. The rate of growth impairment in our CD cohort (41.2%) aligns closely with these figures.

Serum inflammatory markers are essential in the diagnostic workup of pediatric IBD. Their diagnostic value in differentiating IBD from non-IBD conditions is well established. With the advent of fecal biomarkers such as calprotectin, diagnostic algorithms have evolved, and fecal calprotectin is now recommended for triaging children with suspected IBD [30].

In our cohort, CRP was elevated in a greater proportion of patients than ESR (61.3% vs. 51.6%). These values were somewhat lower than those reported by Tanpowpong et al. [29] and in U.S. and Canadian cohorts [31]. Thrombocytosis rates (48.4%) were similar to those of Western cohorts (approximately 50%). Anemia was identified in 58% of our patients, consistent with global reports ranging from 44-74% [32]. Although we calculated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte and PLRs [10], our sample size was too small to draw firm conclusions.

Induction therapy for pediatric IBD aims to achieve rapid remission, relieve symptoms, and promote mucosal healing. According to ECCO and ESPGHAN guidelines, treatment should be tailored to disease severity and site, with exclusive enteral nutrition (EEN) recommended as the preferred induction therapy for pediatric CD [33]. In our cohort, only 6.5% of patients received EEN—much lower than rates in high-income countries. Data from the CEDATA-GPGE registry in Germany and Austria showed that 88.5% of pediatric centers recommended EEN and 71.8% initiated therapy [34]. In contrast, a Saudi survey found that only 14.5% of gastroenterologists routinely prescribed EEN, citing barriers such as poor acceptance (73.8%), poor adherence (65%), limited dietitian support, and lack of standardized protocols or insurance coverage [35].

Sociocultural factors—including the unpalatability of formula diets and disruption of traditional eating patterns—further limit EEN acceptance, while systemic challenges such as cost and access contribute to low adoption. Culturally sensitive education, improved nutritional support, and expanded insurance coverage may enhance EEN utilization in similar contexts.

Corticosteroid use for induction therapy has declined globally, reflecting a shift toward alternative strategies. Data from the ImproveCareNow Network (27,321 pediatric IBD patients) showed corticosteroid use fell from 28% in 2007 to 12% in 2018 [36]. In our cohort, however, steroid use remained high, primarily due to the limited availability of biologics and the low acceptance of EEN, leaving steroids as the main induction option.

Recent guidelines advocate early initiation of biologic therapy in high-risk patients to prevent complications, reduce corticosteroid dependence, and improve long-term outcomes [33]. In our cohort, anti-TNF biologics were used only as step-up therapy after immunomodulator failure, with approximately one-quarter of patients receiving them. ECCO-ESPGHAN guidelines recommend early biologic use for pediatric Crohn’s disease with high-risk features—such as severe growth retardation, stricturing or penetrating disease, or perianal involvement—to minimize cumulative bowel damage and improve outcomes [37,38].

Evidence supports that early biologic therapy improves growth and bone health. In one study, early infliximab normalized height z-scores within three years, whereas delayed treatment led to persistently lower stature [39]. Thus, delayed access to biologics in our setting may have contributed to ongoing inflammation, growth impairment, and higher complication rates. Facilitating earlier biologic initiation in high-risk patients could help mitigate these outcomes.

Observed complications such as pancreatitis and dural venous thrombosis are rare adverse effects of IBD therapy.

This study has limitations. As a retrospective analysis, some data were incomplete, and the small sample size limits generalizability. Biologic and enteral nutrition therapies were administered to few patients, restricting subgroup comparisons. Furthermore, the absence of fecal calprotectin testing represents a significant limitation. As a sensitive, noninvasive biomarker of intestinal inflammation, fecal calprotectin is valuable for monitoring disease activity and mucosal healing without repeated endoscopy [40]. Its unavailability in our setting increases reliance on symptoms, serum markers, and imaging, which are less specific and may result in over- or under-treatment. This limitation could delay the detection of subclinical inflammation, hinder timely therapeutic adjustment, and elevate the risk of disease progression or relapse.

Conclusion

Our findings are largely consistent with the global patterns in pediatric IBD, indicating similar disease behaviors and challenges. Regional discrepancies, particularly in the use of biologics and the specific complications observed, underscore the need for localized healthcare strategies and further research to optimize the care for pediatric patients with IBD in Jordan and similar settings.
