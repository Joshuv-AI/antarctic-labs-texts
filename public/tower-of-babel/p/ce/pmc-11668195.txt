A 10/14 French silicone urinary catheter with inflatable balloon used as novel expansion device in lid reconstruction surgery
Authors: Salil K Mandal, Santosh G Honavar, Asrik Mukhopadhyay, Anwesha Maitra, Oishik Sarkar, Mausree Gayen, Nazibul H Mallick
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To describe the surgical technique using a 10/14 French silicone urinary catheter as a novel tissue expander for repair of defects after removal of eyelid tumors. This device recruits additional tissue by tissue expansion for repair of large eyelid defects.Methods:A prospective noncomparative, interventional case study was conducted over a period of 3 years in which 30 patients were enrolled. All the 30 patients were selected for upper or lower lid Tenzel rotational flap after removal of large malignant tumor, using the tissue expander.Results:A 10/14-French silicone urinary catheter was placed in the periocular region and expanded by graded inflation with normal saline to a total volume of 10 or 14 ml, respectively, over a period of 3 days. On the fourth day before the planned surgery, the catheter balloon was deflated and a modified Tenzel rotational flap was performed to close the defect. Postoperative blink reflex was preserved. There were no cases of wound dehiscence. There was a significant reduction of incision length in upper and lower lid reconstruction with modified Tenzel flap reconstruction.Conclusion:This approach allows for temporary tissue expansion, permitting the closure of large eyelid defects, typically not possible with a conventional Tenzel flap. This also provides excellent functional outcomes without closing patients’ eyelids (which limits vision for weeks to months) as is typical of other reconstructive modalities (e.g. Cutler Beard procedure). The device is cost-effective and readily available. The authors believe it is an excellent alternative in the developing world where access to more expensive options is limited.

The eyelids are flexible tissue screens placed in front of the eyeballs. These act as shutters protecting the anterior surface of the eyeballs from any form of injury or trauma and excessive light. They also perform other important functions: spreading the tear film over the cornea and the conjunctiva by blinking and tear drainage into the lacrimal sac by maintaining the lacrimal pump mechanism.[1] The large eyelid defects caused by trauma, coloboma, or after tumor excision need to be covered by performing lid reconstruction surgeries, for example, Tenzel flap,[2] Fricke's flap,[34] Mustarde cheek rotational flap.[5] In a full-thickness eyelid defect, the posterior lamella can be replaced by ipsilateral tarsoconjunctival flap, free tarsal graft from the contralateral lid, hard palate graft,[6] nasal[7] or auricular cartilage graft,[8] or donor sclera.[9] The anterior lamella can be reconstructed with the help of local myocutaneous flaps or free skin grafts.

One of the methods to recruit more skin in lid reconstruction is graded tissue expansion. The tissue adjacent to the skin defect is expanded over many weeks with the help of an expander placed subcutaneously, while its reservoir is inflated by injection of fluid. This implant is deflated and removed after adequate expansion is achieved. The expanded tissue is then fashioned to close the adjacent defect. This principle has been commonly used in postmastectomy breast reconstruction to generate enough local tissue to cover a permanent silicone prosthesis[1011] and to cover skin defects of the scalp,[121314] nose,[7] ear,[15] head, neck,[1617] and extremities.[18]

It is hypothesized that the skin's stress relaxation property is the basis of tissue expander. The term “stress relaxation” refers to the property that once a force has been used to stretch the skin, a decreasing load is needed to maintain that stretch over time. “Creep” refers to the increasing rate of skin expansion when a constant load is applied over time.[1920]

Aims

We aim to evaluate the efficacy of the 10/14 French silicone inflatable balloon urinary catheter, used as both soft tissue and skin–muscle complex expander to repair created upper and lower lid defects with modified Tenzel rotational flap, to appraise the cosmetic, anatomic, and functional outcomes of lid reconstruction.

Methods

This is a prospective, noncomparative, interventional study conducted over 3 years in 30 different patients. Institutional Ethical Committee Clearance was obtained with Ref no. NC/KOL/IEC/NONSPON/235/01-2019, and informed written consent was obtained from each patient in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki before surgery. The inclusion criterion was all upper and lower lid malignant tumors with peroperative defect of 40%–100% after tumor excision. The exclusion criteria were involvement of both eyelids, gross corneal infiltration, malignancies with local lymph node involvement, distant metastases in the liver, lung, or brain, and intraorbital extension. Pre- and postoperative serial photographs were taken for comparison, and any complication, for example, wound site infection, necrosis, tumor recurrence, or graft rejection, was observed [Figs. 1-3]. In our study, the surface area of tissue expansion was calculated by the formula for area of ellipse: [S (surface area) = π(3.14) × A (long axis or semi-major) × B (short axis or semi-minor)] expressed in square millimeters.[2122]

Surgical procedure

Lid reconstruction surgery was conducted in two stages.

First stage: A “proposed incision” is marked and measured based on the lid defect that is to be created. This is a semicircular skin mark extending from the lateral canthus up to the tragus with convexity downward for the upper lid and convexity upward for the lower lid. A large ellipse is drawn over the proposed area of expansion, such that the highest point of proposed incision crosses the center of the ellipse, marked with a cross sign. All necessary markings are done and measurements are taken before expansion. Long or semi-major and short or semi-minor axes are measured by a metal scale with millimeter unit [Fig. 4a]. The catheter is then introduced and expanded in such a way that the balloon expansion coincides with the marked area [Fig. 5]. Second stage: On the fourth day, the balloon of the catheter is deflated, thus relaxing the expanded area and generating extra tissue. Wide local excision of tumor mass is made with 4-mm healthy margin all around (for margin control) and sent for frozen section biopsy [Fig. 4d]. The skin–muscle complex is dissected out and mobilized from over the periosteum. A modified Tenzel flap is fashioned from the superior and inferior crus of the lateral canthal tendon in lower and upper lid defects, respectively [Fig. 4e]. The temporarily expanded tissue is easily mobilized without tissue tension. The tarsus is exposed along the margin of the lid defect area. The posterior lamella is reconstructed with a periosteal strip mobilized from the lateral orbital rim, which is reflected medially and then joined with the cut margin of the tarsus, thus forming the inner smooth surface of the lid. The anterior lamella is formed by the thick myocutaneous flap which covers the periosteal strip. A full-thickness (5-0) polyglactin suture is then placed through the anterior surface of the tarsus and the created myocutaneous flap. Another (4-0) reinforcing silk suture is applied for final closure.

The rotational flap is anchored to the underlying periosteum of the zygomatic bone with (4-0) Prolene to prevent suture line tension and de-rotation of flap. The canthotomy incision is closed with interrupted (4-0) silk sutures to re-form the lateral canthus. The skin is closed with (4-0) silk sutures [Fig. 4f]. At the end of the operation, length of incision is measured by a wet cotton thread which is placed along the whole length of the curvilinear incision. It is straightened over a metal scale and measured. This final measurement is the “actual incision.”

Statistical data analysis was done using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences statistics, version 25. Descriptive statistical analysis was performed to calculate the mean with standard deviation. Assuming non-normal distribution of data, we used Wilcoxon signed-rank test (a nonparametric test) to compare the sixth-month postoperative outcome with the preoperative finding. P <0.05 was taken to be statistically significant. MS Excel 2010 was used to calculate the mean, median, and standard deviation for each postoperative parameter at every follow-up.

Results

This study included 30 patients in total. All the 30 patients underwent curative resection with upper or lower eyelid reconstruction by a modified Tenzel flap with 10/14 French silicone inflatable balloon catheter used as a novel tissue expander in two stages. Preoperatively, patients with upper lid tumors had mechanical ptosis, canthal deformity, decreased vertical palpebral fissure height (PFH), margin reflex distance 1 (MRD-1), and Levator palpebrae superioris (LPS) action. These parameters were compared at the immediate postoperative period and during the follow-up visits at 1 and 6 months, respectively. They were also compared to those of the fellow unaffected eye.

In this study, none of the cases showed any color changes, ulceration, or inflammatory signs in the region of the expanded skin–muscle complex.

The total length of the created defect was [N (edge-to-edge length of tumor)] + [4 mm (medial margin control)] + [4 mm (lateral margin control)]. Considering the horizontal lid length to be 30 mm (on average) in each case, the percentage of defect is given by the formula: [(N + 4 + 4)/30] ×100.

For lower lid tumors, the mean ± standard deviation of intraoperative surgically created defect was 71.13 ± 7.73%. The mean preoperative vertical PFH was 8.38 ± 0.50 mm, whereas the mean postoperative vertical PFH was 10.50 ± 0.34 mm at 6 months and 10.75 ± 0.25 mm in the other eye [Table 1]. The mean preoperative MRD-1 was 3.27 ± 0.35 mm, whereas the mean postoperative MRD-1 was 4.16 ± 2.42 mm at 6 months and 4.55 ± 0.29 mm in the other eye [Table 1].

Discussion

A larger eyelid defect due to trauma or surgical excision of tumor is always a challenge to the oculoplastic surgeon. Soft tissue expansion is needed for reconstruction either by flap or skin grafting, where there is a definite shortage of local tissue. The temporary expansion of the tissue helps to cover the defective area either by an expanded flap or by skin grafting, where the color and texture are similar to those of the surrounding area. Without expansion of the tissue, there are alternative methods like locoregional or distal flap mobilization, which may cause recipient site deformities.[18]

In the advanced concept for reconstructive surgery, graded tissue expansion is a perfect choice for planning the area of the defect to be covered adequately with flap or graft. It enhances the vascularity of the skin–muscle flap. The probable explanation is that the physical force applied for multidimensional expansion generates a stimulus for local angiogenesis and neovascularization. This increased vascularity of the skin–muscle flap may help in long-term flap survival and less chances of flap necrosis. Moreover, due to rapid vascularization of the host bed, free graft acceptance is very high.[2324]

In the present study, after mass excision, the average lower lid defect was 58.5 ± 6.8% and for the upper lid, it was 55.9 ± 7.8% [Table 2]. The lid reconstruction was performed by the modified Tenzel rotational flap, and a 10/14 French silicone inflatable balloon catheter was used as the expansion device.

No previous report of tissue expansion as an adjunct procedure to close a large eyelid defect exists. Garber and Lukash[24] performed subtotal reconstruction of both the upper and lower lids in cases of severe chemical lye burn with the help of an eyelid tissue expander. Well-controlled graded tissue expansion in the particular region produces highly vascularized, pliable, non–hair-bearing flap that is useful in the anterior lamellar graft in eyelid reconstruction. It is also used as advancement or rotational Myo cutaneous flap or for full-thickness skin grafts.[25] In our study, we performed graded expansion of tissue in the periocular area, following which modified Tenzel flap was used to fill up the created defect. Here the anterior lamellar reconstruction was done by thick myocutaneous flap and posterior lamella was formed by periosteal strip mobilized from lateral orbital rim.

In this study, the mean elliptical area marked for upper lid defects was 4250.77 ± 239.19 mm2 and for lower lid defects was 4251.90 ± 302.89 mm2. The mean incision length for lower lid modified Tenzel flap with the use of expander was 62.27 ± 1.36 mm, whereas without expander, it was 99.83 ± 10.8 mm [Table 2]. The reduction of the incision length was 62.37%. Use of expander generated extra tissue, which required a smaller length of the incision and covered greater area of the created defect. The mean incision length of modified Tenzel flap in upper lid reconstruction with the use of expander was 60.58 ± 2.02 mm, whereas without expander, it was 81.08 ± 1.44 mm [Table 2]. The result was 73.28% reduction in incision length and reduced flap size [Figs. 3 and 8].

A 10/14 French silicone urinary balloon catheter has been used as the expansion device to apply in the periocular region or under the lateral orbital rim against the bony surface. It causes temporary tissue expansion with inflation of the catheter balloon [Fig. 2]. This application should be skillfully done, so that any major vessel or nerve, for example, the superficial temporal artery or the facial nerve, is not damaged. This device expands in a multidimensional way; therefore, it must never be applied over the eyelid as it may cause rise of pressure on the eyeball and subsequent damage to the optic nerve. However, this expander requires one hard surface, allowing it to expand in three directions. In this study, the shape of the expander is ellipsoid, but conventional expanders may have varying sizes or shapes (triangular, rectangular, or circular). Ellipsoid expansion is much more suitable when a wider area is required for expansion, as in flap surgery or skin grafting,[2627]as it recruits more tissue from the temporal side. The wider the surface area for expansion, more tissue is likely to be relaxed and generated. We observed that rapid expansion within a short duration causes more angiogenesis and increased vascularity of the flap that helps in good reconstruction and promotes wound healing.

In controlled tissue expansion, surface area is roughly estimated, but ranges from about 35% to 60%. There is a change in dermis and subcutaneous layer, whereby a decreasing thickness provides flexible and a more contoured skin flap.[2829] In our study, surface area was calculated by the formula for area of ellipse [S (surface area) = π (3.14) × A (long or semi-major axis) × B (short or semi-minor axis)] expressed in square millimeters. For the upper lid, it was 4250.77 ± 239.19 mm2 and for the lower lid, it was 4251.90 ± 302.89 mm2.[21223031] This temporary tissue expansion causes more vascularity by stimulating angiogenesis and helps in long-term flap survival. Due to rapid vascularization, free graft acceptance in the host area markedly increases.[3233] In this study, no instance of flap necrosis has been noted till now.

The plan for skin incision for placement of the implant is done in such a way that it can be hidden in the scalp or the pretragal region. The incision should not interfere with flap transfer or blood supply, and should not be positioned at 90° axis to the line of expansion.[3435]

In this procedure, we take the advantage of temporary expansion of the skin–muscle complex to easily rotate the heavy musculocutaneous flap with minimal dragging force. Thus, suture line tension is markedly reduced and incision length is also reduced in comparison to conventional Tenzel. The surgical scars associated with this technique are relatively inconspicuous because the incision length is much smaller.

In this study, no instance of ectropion, entropion, epiphora, or distal flap necrosis was noted. Complications include major blood vessel injury leading to hematoma formation (in one case), catheter balloon rupture due to overinflation (in one case), lack of perfect planning regarding the area of inflation for rotational flap (in one case), and temporary disfigurement of the face during the initial postoperative period (30 cases), which might lead to psychosocial problems. Another limitation is the increased hospital stay-in period.

Various authors like Brobmann and Huber[36] discussed about the shape of the implant for periocular reconstruction: rectangular and crescentic expander may generate more surface area than the round expander. Roughly, the expander base size should be 2–5 times greater than the created defect. This study showed that the expanded ellipsoid area is the largest area by geometry. Larger area of expansion means increased axial values that shall cause easier rotation and mobilization of the flap for reconstruction.[20]

There are few limitations of this procedure. Firstly, it is a two-stage surgery. In the first stage, the prosthesis for tissue expansion is implanted and in the second stage, the prosthesis is removed, followed by reconstruction. Secondly, multiple outpatient department visits or hospital stays are required for graded inflation of expander. A major disadvantage is the temporary bodily or facial disfigurement between the surgical stages due to tissue expansion.[3537]

Conclusion

In this study, a 10/14 French silicone urinary catheter was used as a novel tissue expander in rotational flap (modified Tenzel) to repair upper and lower lid defects. In this procedure, less amount of tissue needs to be dissected and the incision length is significantly reduced with respect to conventional Tenzel flap reconstruction. Use of tissue expander allows repair of 100% lower lid defects and cosmetically causes much less scarring with respect to conventional surgical procedure. Early visual rehabilitation is possible as we are not closing the eyelid (no bridge or switch flaps are used).

The catheter balloon's inflation property is used as an expansion device in this study. It is a safe material and is widely available and cost-effective, unlike conventional tissue expanders costing $150–$200. Therefore, 10-14 French silicone urinary catheter can be used as an excellent tissue expander in developing countries where access to other expensive options is limited.
