Neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade: a new treatment option for patients with early-stage mismatch repair deficient solid tumors
Authors: Kristin G Anderson, Antoni Ribas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDNA mismatch repair deficiency has been identified as a biomarker that strongly correlates with tumor response to anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) checkpoint blockade therapy in the metastatic setting. In a promising Phase 2 study published in the New England Journal of Medicine, Cercek et al demonstrate that PD-1 checkpoint blockade also has potential in the neoadjuvant setting and may eliminate the need for surgical intervention. These results offer great hope to patients with a variety of solid malignancies because those who experience complete clinical responses may be able to safely choose non-operative management to preserve organ function and minimize side effects associated with surgery.Keywords: Biomarker, Circulating tumor DNA - ctDNA, Colorectal Cancer, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, Mismatch repair - MMR

Commentary

Checkpoint blockade immunotherapy has revolutionized cancer treatment1; however, only a fraction of patients experience durable clinical benefit.2 Thus, there is an urgent need to identify reliable biomarkers that can predict which tumors are likely to respond to individual or combination treatment strategies and inform clinical decision-making.3 Excitingly, in 2015, the pivotal Phase 2 KEYNOTE-016 study demonstrated that programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) checkpoint blockade treatment in patients with mismatch repair deficient (dMMR) metastatic colorectal cancer could achieve clinical responses and that dMMR status predicted clinical benefit.4 Subsequently, dMMR, defined as the loss of functional DNA mismatch repair enzymes encoded by MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and/or PMS2, has been accepted as a highly predictive biomarker of clinical response to PD-1/programmed death-ligand 1 checkpoint blockade,5 and the US Food and Drug Administration approved anti-PD-1 checkpoint blocking agents for all patients with metastatic or unresectable dMMR or microsatellite-instability high solid tumors in 2017.6 However, concerns remained regarding whether checkpoint blockade in the neoadjuvant setting could promote clinical responses in early-stage tumors before the critical surgical “window of opportunity” closes. A recent Phase 2 study by Cercek et al aimed to address this question by exploring if patients with stage I, II, or III dMMR solid tumors could achieve complete clinical responses and opt for non-surgical management, regardless of tumor site.7 The team evaluated overall responses and sustained clinical complete responses after 12 months as primary endpoints and explored safety, recurrence-free survival, genomic alterations after treatment, and which assessment strategies had the highest correlation with clinical responses.

All patients presented with tumors that were amenable to curative-intent surgery and were treated with neoadjuvant dostarlimab, a PD-1 blocking agent, every 3 weeks for 6 months. Patients were assigned to one of two cohorts based on disease site: locally advanced rectal cancer (Cohort 1) or non-rectal solid tumors (Cohort 2). Of 124 patients who enrolled, 103 completed the study and 14 were still on treatment. Promisingly, neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade enabled a non-operative management strategy in a high proportion of patients, and the option to pursue curative resection was not compromised by treatment. Of the 117 patients included in the analysis, recurrence-free survival at 2 years was 92%. Most patients experienced either no (35%) or low-grade, reversible adverse events (60%), and only five Grade 3 events and one Grade 4 event were reported. The results observed in patients with rectal cancer (Cohort 1) were consistent with the prior study: all 49 patients who completed treatment experienced clinical complete responses and chose non-operative management. In the second cohort, 65% (35/54) of patients experienced clinical complete responses. Of the 19 patients who experienced incomplete responses, 16 underwent surgery and 81% (13 of 16) showed evidence of tumor regression in the resected lesion; the remaining three patients showed evidence of a pathological complete response in the primary tumor but exhibited tumor dissemination to the lymph node. Genomic alterations in the resected tumors revealed high similarity with the primary tumors at baseline, except in two patients, and the investigators speculated that the residual tumors in these two patients may have been new primary lesions because they were not dMMR. Encouragingly, only five patients across both cohorts experienced recurrences, four of whom achieved complete responses with additional doses of dostarlimab or lymph node removal.

These results offer significant hope for patients with dMMR tumors. Indeed, the opportunity for front-line immunotherapy treatment rather than surgery, chemotherapy or radiation may significantly transform clinical practice, although a larger randomized study measuring if overall survival is improved is still needed. Notably, all patients in the Cercek et al study were determined to have loss of at least one of the DNA mismatch repair enzymes. However, dMMR incidence is only approximately 3% across solid tumors,8 and immunotherapy has not improved outcomes to the same degree for patients with mismatch repair proficient (pMMR) tumors. Thus, additional studies are urgently needed to identify opportunities to improve tumor control in patients with pMMR disease.

A variety of immune-suppressive mechanisms actively restrain responses to checkpoint blockade, many of which are present concurrently in the solid tumor microenvironment. Immune cell exclusion, low antigenicity, poor tumor antigen recognition, immune cell dysfunction/death, a preponderance of suppressive immune cells, cytokines and soluble factors, the microbiome, and a hypoxic and hostile metabolic milieu all can contribute to immune resistance.1 9 Moreover, the contribution of each immune-evasion mechanism can be impacted by tumor type, location, treatment exposure, host and environmental factors, and intratumoral heterogeneity. Thus, multiple high-dimensional technologies may need to be used together to comprehensively profile the suppressive pathways most active in different tumor types, define additional biomarkers correlated with response or resistance, and rationally design trials implementing therapeutic combinations that address multiple resistance pathways simultaneously.1 10

Another challenge facing the field has been to identify the least invasive and most informative strategies to monitor therapeutic responses during and after treatment. While imaging modalities are non-invasive, their utility can be limited because microscopic lesions are typically not detectable. Even when lesions are visible, it can be challenging to distinguish between true disease progression and pseudoprogression caused by immune infiltration in tumor masses. Biopsies may be invasive and are not always feasible, complicating their use for ongoing monitoring. To investigate which clinical assessment strategy was most strongly correlated with anti-PD-1 response in the neoadjuvant setting, the authors correlated the time to complete response with tumor-specific imaging (MRI and/or Positron Emission Tomography (PET)/CT), endoscopy (Cohort 1) or disease-specific examinations (Cohort 2), tumor biopsies (performed when feasible), and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA). In this study, imaging modalities and endoscopic assessments demonstrated similar time to complete response (median of approximately 6 months), which was longer than time to negative biopsy or ctDNA clearance (approximately 1.5 months). ctDNA was evaluated at baseline and two times during treatment by measuring up to 50 patient-specific genomic regions of interest, defined using whole exome sequencing of matched tumor samples. Almost all patients (>90%) had detectable ctDNA at baseline. Critically, ctDNA became undetectable during treatment in patients who experienced a clinical complete response and remained negative at the end of treatment, whereas it remained detectable during treatment in patients who experienced incomplete responses or recurrences (figure 1). Thus, ctDNA may be a high-priority biomarker for determining response rates during neoadjuvant immunotherapy and informing if additional interventions are needed.

In conclusion, the work by Cercek et al has revealed that a subset of patients can likely receive neoadjuvant immunotherapy and completely avoid surgical resections that risk permanent negative impacts on quality of life. While additional studies are needed, these results are groundbreaking and have great promise for treating patients with dMMR tumors.
