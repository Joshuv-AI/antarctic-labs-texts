AMOC slowdown amplifies North Atlantic salinity variability to unprecedented levels
Authors: Tomoki Iwakiri, Jong-Seong Kug, Soon-Il An, Yechul Shin, Ji-Hoon Oh, Sunhee Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC), a key component of the Earth system, is projected to weaken under anthropogenic warming, but how this change affects oceanic variability remains unclear. Here we show, using a sustained global warming scenario, that AMOC weakening triggers extreme upper-ocean salinity variability in the North Atlantic, with amplitudes far exceeding historical levels. The salinity variability amplifies as it travels across the North Atlantic Ocean and is linked to a slowdown of the Gulf Stream and strengthened spatial salinity contrasts. An idealized model demonstrates that these changes in the mean ocean state promote salinity extremes, with their amplification critically dependent on the salinity–temperature coupled feedback. Once the AMOC slowdown is underway, enhanced variability can arise even under climate mitigation because ocean circulation recovers slowly. Emergent compound extremes of salinity and sea level increase the risks to coastal ecosystems and societies in Europe.

Introduction

Ocean salinity, alongside temperature, is a principal variable of ocean state1. While widely treated as a passive tracer of water masses and a fingerprint of the global water cycle2, salinity also exerts active control over ocean dynamics, which modulates seawater density and plays a central role in deep overturning circulation. Beyond its dynamical role, salinity influences nutrient supply, habitat conditions3, and also affects human health4, highlighting its far-reaching importance across biological and societal systems. Anthropogenic global warming is causing sea level rise, which intensifies the risk of saltwater intrusion in coastal regions5. Furthermore, it disrupts marine and coastal ecosystems as well as environmental stability6. In addition to mean salinity change, internal salinity variability also influences oceanic processes. The Great Salinity Anomaly (GSA), a large freshening event driven by Arctic freshwater input, Greenland’s meltwater, and/or atmospheric forcings, circulates through the subpolar North Atlantic and modulates deep ocean convection on the multi-decadal timescales7–9. Therefore, understanding how salinity and its variability respond to climate change is essential for anticipating its multifaceted impacts.

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) redistributes heat, carbon, and nutrients globally, making it a crucial regulator of the Earth system10–13. In this context, ocean salinity also plays a key role in sustaining the AMOC, particularly through mechanisms such as the salt-advection feedback14. The weakening of the AMOC raises concerns about this feedback, which could further reduce northward salinity transport in the Atlantic and accelerate its collapse under global warming. In fact, observations and model projections indicate that the AMOC has weakened in recent decades, and may approach a tipping point in the near future15–19. Although the AMOC is stabilized by global ocean circulation, which may preclude a complete collapse20, substantial weakening of the AMOC21 could trigger widespread climate disruptions, such as a slowdown of the Gulf Stream22, which transports warm, salty water northward and contributes to the mild climate over Europe23–25. The observed weakening of the AMOC over the past several decades has substantially altered the mean state of the North Atlantic, and changes in ocean circulation have been associated with the emergence of warming hole and a strengthening trend in the meridional salinity and temperature gradients26,27. In addition to regulating the mean climate28–31, the AMOC modulates interdecadal–multidecadal climate variability over the Atlantic, as demonstrated by theoretical and modeling studies32–39. This implies that future AMOC weakening could intensify or give rise to internal variability and ocean extremes40. Such emergent climate variability under future warming remains largely unknown and warrants further attention, particularly given its potential but poorly understood impacts on the Earth system41.

Here, we show changes in salinity variability under a substantially weakened AMOC using eight state-of-the-art climate models from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) output, including two large ensemble experiments, extending to the end of the 23rd century under a fossil-fueled development pathway (known as extended SSP5-8.5). We find an unprecedented intensification of salinity variability, characterized by an emergent salinity mode, over the North Atlantic across models. This large-scale salinity variability affects sea surface height (SSH) variability through density modulation. Furthermore, considering that the extended global warming scenario represents the worst-case pathway, we also examine the impacts of our climate mitigation efforts. Under an idealized CO2 mitigation scenario, this intensified variability occurs, indicating potentially unavoidable coastal impacts especially for European countries and requiring integrated mitigation efforts and adaptation strategies.

Results

Emergence of North Atlantic Salinity extremes under future warming

Under the high-emissions SSP5-8.5 scenario, in which atmospheric CO2 concentrations continue to rise, global-mean surface temperature (GMST) increases steadily and reaches a global warming level of about 5°C by the end of the 21st century and about 10°C by the end of the 23rd century (Fig. 1). Historical simulations suggest that the AMOC has begun to weaken under present-day conditions, and it is projected to decline across all models during the 21st century in response to anthropogenic warming. Under continued global warming beyond the year 2100, the AMOC is expected to weaken substantially, reaching ~5 Sv level by the mid-22nd century and remaining at that level through the end of the 23rd century. In the preindustrial climate experiment, denoted as piControl, the strongest salinity variability in the upper-ocean (0-200 m) across the Atlantic basin is found over the Gulf Stream and its extension, where the large fluctuations arise in association with the meandering of the western boundary current (Fig. 1a). By contrast, variability in the central and eastern North Atlantic is relatively small. This spatial pattern remains largely unchanged throughout the 20th century (Fig. 1b) and climate models reasonably capture the observed pattern in the present climate (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, from the 21st century onward, a marked increase in salinity variability is projected over the central to eastern North Atlantic. By the 22nd and 23rd centuries, this increase becomes more pronounced, with the amplitude in the central-eastern basin exceeding that of the western boundary region. This shift indicates a west-to-east reversal in the dominant region of salinity variability.

For simplicity, this study adopts the monthly-mean time series of area-averaged salinity anomaly over the central North Atlantic as the definition of variability (black rectangle in Fig. 1a,b). The central North Atlantic salinity shows pronounced oscillatory behavior on interannual to decadal timescales (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). By the end of the 23rd century, the multi-model ensemble-mean amplitude of salinity variability increases by 527% relative to the piControl, with 95% CI from 282% to 811% (Fig. 1e). This projects a level of variability that is unprecedented compared to the present-day conditions. As a more objective detection, the Hilbert empirical orthogonal function analysis42 can be applied by considering the oscillatory nature of the salinity variability. It clearly shows that the dominant variability is confined to the western boundary current in the piControl, whereas the spatial pattern is substantially altered from the 22nd century onward, with the center shifting to the central North Atlantic (Supplementary Fig. 4). Although similar increases in the amplitude are faintly seen in upper-ocean temperature variability, their magnitudes are smaller and exhibit greater inter-model spread, likely due to the thermal damping and stronger influence from atmospheric noise (Supplementary Fig. 5). Furthermore, this pronounced salinity variability substantially amplifies SSH variability and could potentially threaten European coasts (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 5d,f).

These eastward-propagating signals are also visible in the salinity anomaly time series averaged over the central North Atlantic, which show alternating positive and negative peaks (Fig. 2b). This propagation is primarily explained by horizontal advection solely in the salinity budget analysis (green curve in Fig. 2c; see Methods), whereas the contribution from surface freshwater flux is negligible (Supplementary Fig. 7). In particular, the dominant contribution arises from anomalous meridional advection of the background meridional salinity gradient (magenta curve). This process reflects anomalous zonal density gradients that induce anomalous meridional geostrophic currents, which in turn advect the background salinity. This mechanism can be referred to as a salinity-driven Rossby wave, in which the salinity gradient acts as the restoring force (see Supplementary Fig. 8a and Supplementary Discussion). In other words, the salinity-driven Rossby wave propagates through isohaline lines as waveguides. A sensitivity test, which replaces the mean salinity gradient with that from piControl, shows reduced amplitude of this process to about one-fourth, highlighting the critical role of the mean-state changes in modulating the variability (blue curve). These results imply that the extreme enhancement of salinity variability is associated with the emergence of a distinct salinity mode. Note that the density anomaly (i.e., SSH), which drives the geostrophic current, is determined by variations in both salinity and temperature. Although density variability in the present climate over the North Atlantic is dominated primarily by thermosteric effect, salinity-driven density variability becomes dominant under future climate owing to the extreme salinity anomalies (Supplementary Fig. 9).

These salinity modes are accompanied by temperature anomalies (Supplementary Fig. 10) and meandering of the Gulf Stream, resembling characteristics of GSA over the subpolar North Atlantic. However, whereas GSAs are primarily forced by episodic freshwater input and advected by mean currents, the salinity anomalies found here exhibit oscillatory behavior with both positive and negative phases and propagate eastward along midlatitude as salinity-driven Rossby waves. Covariability between salinity and temperature may modulate their dynamical characteristics through coupled interactions39, as examined using a simplified model in the next section. In contrast to temperature anomalies, which are more strongly regulated by surface flux damping and atmospheric noise, salinity anomalies have the potential to reach extreme magnitudes in response to changes in the mean states. Also, this variability can influence the meridional overturning circulation, but in this study, we focus on the upper-ocean variation.

Idealized model for extreme salinity mode

Figure 3a shows a Hovmöller diagram of the composite salinity anomalies along the midlatitude band. Both positive and negative anomalies emerge near the western boundary and propagate eastward, with their amplitudes increasing as they reach the central basin. To gain deeper insight into the propagation and amplification of the extreme salinity mode, we construct an idealized one-dimensional linear model, which is derived from the linearized budget equations for salinity and temperature. It incorporates advection by the climatological mean flow, the salinity-driven Rossby waves, and the interaction between salinity and temperature (Methods). Crucially, all model coefficients in the linear operator can be obtained from the mean state of climate model outputs, allowing us to diagnose how changes in the background ocean state affect the evolution of variability. Theoretical expectations from the model operator indicate that both the phase speed and growth of the coupled mode are highly sensitive to the mean current and horizontal gradients of salinity and temperature.Fig. 3The idealized model for conceptual understanding of salinity mode.a Hovmöller diagram of MME-mean composited upper-ocean salinity anomaly along 35°-40°N mean in 2100-2299. The idealized model reasonably reproduces the eastward propagation and its amplification in the central basin under post-2100 mean-state conditions (Fig. 3a,b), when periodic forcing is imposed at the western boundary to mimic Gulf Stream meanders on decadal timescales43,44. Phase speed consistently decreases near the eastern boundary in both the multi-model ensemble and the simple model. Although the amplitude is slightly underestimated compared to the CMIP6 composite, the pattern and its evolution are qualitatively reproduced, indicating the simplified framework well captures fundamental mechanisms of the mode. Considering that salinity-driven Rossby waves explain only wave propagation, other processes, such as salinity–temperature interaction, should be validated to account for the amplification. We conduct a sensitivity experiment in which the coupling term is turned off. In this case, the phase speed of the salinity wave increases, but no amplification can be found in the eastern basin (Fig. 3c). This result indicates that the interaction between salinity and temperature plays a crucial role in amplifying the amplitude, which can be referred to as salinity-temperature coupled feedback. Here, as for the salinity-driven Rossby wave, we illustrate the salinity-temperature coupled feedback using a schematic diagram (Supplementary Fig. 8b). Given a positive salinity anomaly in the midlatitude, a northward geostrophic current anomaly is induced on the eastern flank of the anomaly through the associated zonal density gradient. This northward flow advects climatological warm water from the tropics, leading to the formation of a positive temperature anomaly to the east. The resulting temperature anomaly further modifies the density via the thermosteric effect, thereby generating an additional northward geostrophic current anomaly on the western flank of temperature anomaly. This transports climatological salty water, which in turn amplifies the salinity anomaly through salinity-temperature coupling. Without this coupling, the salinity mode does not amplify, emphasizing the fundamentally coupled nature of the phenomenon. This mechanism is supported by the lead-lag relationship between salinity and temperature. Indeed, both the climate models and the idealized model show that temperature anomaly leads to salinity anomaly (Supplementary Fig. 11). It should be noted, however, that the accompanying temperature variability is largely constrained by the thermal damping, primarily the strong restoring influence of air-sea heat fluxes on surface temperature anomaly, which may suppress its amplitude and persistence. As a result, temperature variations are not equivalent to salinity variations and instead play a supportive role in the salinity mode.

Figure 3d shows the growth rate, defined as the ratio of amplitude in the eastern basin to the initial perturbation at the western boundary, under different background states. Current climatology does not excite amplification, and thus small variabilities are likely masked by other climate signals and noise. In contrast, a clear amplification emerges after substantial global warming (post-2100). Because the phase speed and coupling strength are theoretically linked to the mean flow and gradients, this amplification is attributed to the background-state changes. While salinity variability strengthens even along the western basin (Fig. 1b), the eastward-propagating mode amplifies it further in the eastern basin. Thus, the idealized model explains how the dominant region of salinity variability shifts from the western to the eastern basin under global warming, capturing the reversal pattern over the North Atlantic.

Salinity variability linked to mean state changes under AMOC weakening

The North Atlantic climate is projected to undergo dramatic changes in future warming. The Gulf Stream weakens and spatial salinity contrasts become more pronounced (Supplementary Fig. 12), both of which are key to explaining the emergence and amplification of salinity mode, as demonstrated in the idealized model. These mean state changes are consequences of the AMOC weakening15,26. Multi-model comparison reveals that models exhibiting a stronger AMOC decline tend to show a significant weakening of upper-ocean current speed over the Gulf Stream (Fig. 4a). Also, the AMOC weakening enhances the negative meridional gradient of climatological salinity, especially along the midlatitude (Fig. 4b). This effect is most pronounced in the central North Atlantic, where the amplitude of salinity variability also becomes largest. The mechanism is physically consistent: as the weakened Gulf Stream reduces the northward transport of warm and salty subtropical waters, both the spatial contrasts in salinity and temperature are intensified. In agreement with the idealized model’s implications, these changes in the mean state are closely associated with enhanced salinity variability over the central North Atlantic (Fig. 4d,e). The amplification of salinity extremes is significantly correlated with background-state features such as reduced Gulf Stream velocity and strengthened meridional salinity gradient. These results support the interpretation that the emergence of extreme salinity variability is fundamentally linked to changes in the mean ocean circulation associated with AMOC dynamics, rather than to purely stochastic forcing. We further examine the relationship across models between future salinity variability and mean state biases of Gulf Stream velocity and salinity gradient in the present climate (Supplementary Fig. 13). Changes in future salinity variability exhibit only a weak and statistically insignificant correlation with Gulf Stream velocity, whereas they show a strong positive correlation with the meridional salinity gradient in the present climate. This indicates that models with a weaker salinity gradient in the present climate tend to project stronger future salinity variability. Since seven out of eight models overestimate the mean salinity gradient, present climate salinity variability may be overestimated (Supplementary Fig. 1), which could in turn lead to an underestimation of future salinity variability in CMIP6 models.

Emerging salinity variability threatens the European coast despite mitigation efforts

The extended SSP5-8.5 scenario represents the worst-case pathway in which global warming continues unabated. To evaluate the possibility of avoiding the emerging salinity extremes by CO2 mitigation, a set of idealized CO₂ ramp-up and ramp-down experiments are conducted (black curve in Fig. 5a; see Methods). While this mitigation successfully cools global mean surface temperature after the CO₂ peak, it fails to halt the amplification of salinity variability over the central North Atlantic (red and magenta curves in Fig. 5a). This is due to the slow response of ocean adjustment: the AMOC continues to weaken during the CO₂ removal phase45, reaching its minimum approximately 50 years after CO₂ level peak (blue curve in Fig. 5a). Consequently, the background ocean states stimulate the amplitude of salinity variability, which is further enhanced over the central North Atlantic and remains robust even under global cooling. Of note is that the amplitude increase is smaller compared to the case without climate mitigation efforts, such as the extended SSP5-8.5 scenario. Also, the AMOC exhibits overshoot behavior after CO2 level returns to present-day level, yet the salinity variability does not return to historical values, indicating that these responses exhibit hysteresis rather than a simple linear response to the AMOC strength.

As also seen under the extended SSP5-8.5 scenario, SSH variability becomes more pronounced during the CO2 mitigation phase along coastal Europe, particularly in association with salinity variation (Fig. 5b). Such strong SSH variability, when superimposed on the background global sea level rise, may increase the risk of coastal flooding and storm surge46. Even more concerning, salinity extremes coupled with SSH may disrupt the balance of saltwater intrusion, posing significant risks to coastal regions—particularly to salt marsh ecosystems47. We demonstrate compound events as the co-occurrence of extreme salinity and SSH anomalies (Red curve in Fig. 5b; see Methods). In the present climate, compound events of salinity and SSH extremes are quite rare, whereas their occurrence increases substantially during the CO₂ mitigation phase. Therefore, our findings highlight that unless mitigation efforts are initiated before warming worsens, certain aspects of climate variability—particularly salinity extremes and associated coastal risks may increase markedly and turn irreversible on centennial timescales due to the AMOC’s slow response.

Discussion

An emerging extreme salinity variability has been identified over the North Atlantic under projected AMOC weakening induced by anthropogenic greenhouse gases. This emerging variability reverses the dominant region of salinity variability from western boundary current to eastern basin. The emergent salinity mode and key climate changes are schematically summarized in Fig. 6. This salinity mode exhibits pronounced interannual to decadal variability and propagates eastward, with its amplification strongly dependent on coupling with temperature mode. Propagation dynamics and strength of salinity-temperature coupled feedback are governed by the evolving background fields of ocean circulation and their horizontal patterns. In the present climate, AMOC transports warm, salty upper-ocean water poleward via the North Atlantic current, resulting in weak salinity and temperature gradients–that is, weak salinity–temperature coupling. However, once the AMOC weakens, the salt-advection feedback intensifies salinity and temperature contrasts between the midlatitude and subpolar North Atlantic due to the slowdown of the Gulf Stream, enabling salinity extremes to emerge. In light of its spatiotemporal characteristics, we propose the name North Atlantic Salinity Oscillation for this mode. In the future climate, this oscillatory mode emerges as the leading mode, exceeding the western boundary variability associated with the Gulf Stream that dominates in the present climate (Supplementary Fig. 4). Notably, its timescale broadly coincides with the subdecadal to decadal-scale meandering of the Gulf Stream43. This coincidence raises the possibility that initial anomalies generated at the western boundary current by such meanders may become amplified as they propagate eastward. However, because the eastward transit time of these anomalies is comparable to the mode’s intrinsic period, it remains unclear whether this oscillation is primarily a boundary-currents-forced response or an internal mode of the system. Resolving this distinction and unraveling the origin of periodicity are key open questions.

The salinity mode identified in this study is controlled by large-scale ocean dynamics, so key features of the salinity mode may not be resolution-dependent. Nevertheless, eddy-permitting and eddy-resolving ocean models generally provide a more realistic representation of western boundary currents, including sharper temperature and density fronts, more realistic meanders, and stronger mesoscale eddy activity49,50. If meandering of the western boundary current is indeed important for initiating the salinity mode, higher-resolution models may produce a more energetic mode with a larger amplitude and may also reduce uncertainty in its periodicity.

Salinity-temperature coupled mode may not be unique to future warming. Paleoclimate records suggest that abrupt climate events like the Younger Dryas and Heinrich events51,52, linked to the freshwater input and AMOC weakening, were also accompanied by large-scale salinity changes. Our findings suggest that the salinity variability identified in this study may reflect an intrinsic dynamical behavior of the climate system, rather than being solely a consequence of anthropogenic forcing. This hypothesis could be further evaluated using paleoclimate model simulations, particularly during past AMOC transition periods.

Methods

Metrics and preprocessing

Upper-ocean anomaly is defined as 0-200 m mean of anomaly. The central North Atlantic region, as defined in this study, refers to the black rectangle (40°−20°W, 30°−45°N) shown in Fig. 1a,b, and is selected as the area exhibiting the largest amplitude change in salinity. AMOC index is defined as the maximum of the oceanic meridional stream function below 500 m at 26.5°N. Upper-ocean Gulf Stream current speed is measured as area-averaged index over the 70°−50°W, 35°−45°N. Throughout the datasets, anomaly is calculated relative to a 31-year moving-window climatology (apply to each ensemble member, where applicable). This approach is effective in separating the signal from the nonmonotonic nature of both the global warming trend and the AMOC response. For the first and last 15 years of datasets, we use the first and last 31 years, respectively, to compute the climatology. The results are not sensitive to the moving-average window length, as the conclusions are unchanged when using a 61-year moving-average climatology.

Composite analysis

The salinity index is calculated as the monthly mean upper-ocean salinity anomaly averaged over the central North Atlantic. For event detection, the monthly mean index is first smoothed using a 4-year lowpass filter and then converted to an annual mean. A salinity extreme event is defined as an event in which the index exceeds ±1.5 standard deviations relative to the piControl of each model. Because the positive and negative events are nearly symmetric, negative events are sign-reversed and combined with positive events for the composite analysis. Also, extreme events of the same sign occurring within four years before or after the strongest case are excluded, as salinity variability exhibits interannual persistence. Note that no lowpass filter is applied to the composite variables themselves.

Compound events

To examine the linkage between salinity extremes and SSH variability, we define compound events as instances where both variables simultaneously exceed their respective extreme thresholds. Specifically, high-salinity events are expected to be associated with lower SSH, while low-salinity events are expected to correspond to higher SSH. Therefore, we considered both cases as compound events and combined their occurrences using a 61-year moving window. Extreme events for salinity and SSH are identified annually as anomalies exceeding ±1.5 standard deviations relative to piControl variability. The spatial domain used for calculating frequency of compound events is shown in Fig. 5b.

CMIP6 model outputs and observation

Climate change under future global warming is evaluated using the fully coupled simulation outputs from the CMIP656. Since sufficient integration time is required for the substantial AMOC weakening due to global warming, we utilize all eight available models that provide simulation periods up to the end of the 23rd century–representing the so-called extended SSP5-8.5 or extended global warming scenario. Two of the eight models have ten ensemble members each (ACCESS-ESM1-5 and GISS-E2−1G), while the remaining six models offer only a single member (ACCESS-CM2, CanESM5, CESM2-WACCM, IPSL-CM6A-LR, MIROC-ES2L, and MRI-ESM2-0). The boundary forcings are the historical simulation for 1850-2014 and SSP5-8.5 for 2015-229957. We also analyze the last 500 years of data from the preindustrial control (piControl) run. The models and ensemble members use in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1. All model outputs are re-grided to a 1°×1° horizontal resolution prior to analysis. Although few models lack specific variables, all available data are utilized in the analysis, as described in the Supplementary Discussion. For models with ensemble members, the ensemble means are computed before the multi-model mean, ensuring equal weighting across models. Note that large-ensemble models are expected to detect climate change signals of internal variability more clearly than others.

Observation-based gridded ocean salinity is taken from the quality controlled monthly mean EN4 dataset version 4.258. Ocean velocities and sea surface height are taken from the ECCO ocean state estimate version 4. The analysis uses data covering the period from 1950 to 2024 for EN4.4.2 and 1992 to 2017 for ECCO59.

Experimental design of CO2 removal

For the idealized CO2 mitigation scenario, we use the Community Earth System Model version 1.2 (CESM1.2)60, which consists of the atmosphere (Community Atmospheric Model version 5), ocean (Parallel Ocean Program version 2), ice (Community Ice Code version 4), and land models (Community Land Model version 4). The atmosphere and land models operate with approximately 1° resolution, with 30 vertical levels. The ocean and ice models use a nominal 1° resolution, with 60 vertical levels.

The idealized CO2 mitigation scenario consists of CO2 concentration-driven ramp-up and ramp-down experiments. We perform a 900-year control simulation with the present-day CO2 level (367 ppm), and then CO2 level increases by 1% per year to quadrupled level, designed to mimic global warming. A CO₂ ramp-down experiment is conducted, in which atmospheric CO₂ is decreased by 1% per year from a quadrupled-CO₂ state. The model is further integrated for 220 years after the CO2 levels return to the present-day. Ramp-up experiments branch from a randomly selected initial condition of present-day simulation, generating 28 large ensemble members. This large ensemble experiment allows for the detection of robust climate change signals. The experimental design basically follows the irreversibility experiment from Carbon Dioxide Removal Intercomparison Project61.

Significance test

The statistical significance and confidence interval are evaluated using bootstrap tests with 1000 resampling iterations. For models with multiple ensemble members, the ensemble-mean of each metric is first calculated. Then, bootstrapping is applied across the multi-model ensemble, treating each model equally. The statistical significance of the correlation analysis is assessed using a two-tailed t-test. Also, the statistical significance of composite analysis is assessed using a two-tailed t-test62, and p-values are calculated for each individual model. A combined p-value for the multi-model ensemble-mean composite is derived from the Stouffer Z-score method63.
