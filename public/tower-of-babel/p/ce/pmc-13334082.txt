A second life
Authors: Irini Topalidou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Genetics Society of America Mentorship Award recognizes members of the scientific community for exceptional mentoring of geneticists at any career stage. Irini Topalidou is the inaugural recipient in the nonfaculty category, honored for her outstanding contributions to mentoring within the C. elegans community and beyond. She has mentored numerous trainees who have gone on to pursue graduate and medical degrees, postdoctoral or faculty positions, and careers in biotech. The award further acknowledges her published work on STEM education and career choice, as well as her commitment to understanding and addressing trainee challenges and to improving mentorship practices. In the essay below, Irini reflects on her journey as an immigrant in the United States and how these experiences have shaped her life, career, and approach to mentorship. She also highlights how recent scientific and cultural shifts are challenging the principles of openness, diversity, and discovery in the United States, placing one of its greatest strengths at risk.

The moment I remember most vividly from my last day in Greece, before leaving for New York for my post doc, is sitting in my parents’ bedroom late at night, crying uncontrollably on the phone with a friend. He was convinced of the incredible luck I had in going to New York for postdoctoral research. How could I make him understand that what looked like a dream to him felt to me like an irrational decision I did not understand myself? I kept thinking that I must have decided it in a moment of madness or emotional absence, when some vague ambition, or a sense of obligation to follow the research path I had chosen, overpowered my love for my family and my home.

I felt desperate at the thought of moving to a country I did not love. But the feeling that truly tore me apart was the unbearable guilt of leaving my family behind. I felt as if I had committed a terrible crime against them. At the same time, I deeply regretted that I had never been a diligent student of English and now had to navigate daily life speaking my broken English, with a dreadful Greek accent! How had I, a good student, ended up in such a state? I felt that everything I had done and chosen had been wrong. My sadness and confusion were so overwhelming that my memory erased most of the good farewell moments I had shared with my family and friends.

The next thing I remember is sitting in a café in New York, dizzy from jet lag and overwhelmed by the crowds, the colors of people's clothes, and the diversity of faces around me. I felt as if I had suddenly landed in the Tower of Babel and yet, at the same time, as if I had always belonged there. Although I had traveled to New York before, I now believe that this was the first time I felt what this city had to offer: the chance to experience and appreciate the diversity of human existence, the beauty of difference, and, at the same time, to feel part of it. The fact that I was now a resident of this city opened a window to the world for me. I felt a sense of belonging.

That absolute euphoria lasted for a few months, until the day I woke up and felt Greece as a tiny, fragile dot, betrayed and abandoned. I felt like a spouse who had repeatedly cheated on the person they loved most. How had I managed to live happily away from my home and people for so many months? Nostalgia began to slowly eat away at me. I forced myself to recall images of my favorite neighborhoods, my loved ones, familiar smells, and every time details escaped me, I was overcome with guilt. I could not picture the shape of my mother's eyes, could not recall how the geraniums on our balcony smelled…

Until I boarded the plane to return to Greece for Christmas, I was convinced my memory had erased every important detail, leaving a knot in my throat. Yet, the anticipation and overwhelming joy of returning home suddenly felt like sufficient justification for my migration to a foreign country. That intense excitement of returning, the feeling of nostos (Ancient Greek: νόστος), of homecoming, was entirely new to me, and it suddenly gave a new meaning to my decision to live far away.

Looking back, I do not think I would have dared to leave Greece if the lab where I worked during my post doc had not felt so welcoming from the start. It was winter when I interviewed at Columbia, and Martin Chalfie, my future Principal Investigator, greeted me in a furry brown hat that for some reason endeared him to me. His lab felt both like a museum of biological research and a global commune, where people somehow fit perfectly together. Marty had a talent for bringing like-minded people together and gave me the opportunity to become an integral member of the lab through both mentoring and collaboration. Years later, Marty won the Nobel Prize for green fluorescence protein, but what I remember most is that when I fell ill and underwent surgery, he stood beside my mother in the waiting room.

This experience in Marty's lab shaped me as a mentor and offered the first clear indication that being surrounded by trainees feels both natural and deeply fulfilling. Trainees often gravitated toward me, not only because of my experience, but also because I tried to be an empathic listener who encouraged them and provided a supportive space for their concerns. Here I realized that mentorship is not only about training in technical and conceptual skills, but also about providing encouragement, recognizing achievements, and showing genuine care for each individual. This has become central to my approach, from celebrating small wins in the lab to supporting trainees through personal and professional challenges.

Every year that passed, I told myself it would be my last in the United States. I had developed a way of deceiving myself, setting “just one more year” as a deadline to ease the guilt of living far away from home. This reassured me that what felt like a betrayal of my homeland and my family would one day end. When the economic crisis in Greece took shape, I found a convincing justification for not returning. “I did not choose this; I was forced to stay,” I told myself. Unable to choose between the country I loved and the one where I had built my daily life, I avoided the decision by convincing myself that circumstances had made it for me. And yet, deep down, I knew my mind and heart were always in Greece. So why was not I trying to return?

The United States, with its vastness and plethora of opportunities, compared with my small country of Greece, gave me the sense that my possibilities were endless. As a relatively young country, America has traditionally focused on the present and the future, rather than being weighed down by the heaviness of its history. This forward-looking spirit made America feel more modern, innovative, open-minded, and daring to take on challenges, characteristics especially appealing to young, eager minds. In the United States, I also felt freer to choose my own path and make decisions without fear of judgment. I met people who started PhDs at sixty and musicians who became scientists. I saw a flexibility often missing from Greece, and perhaps from Europe, which gave me both a sense of freedom and a romantic, probably naïve, belief that anything is possible. Recent shifts that challenge this spirit of openness in the United States risk undermining one of America's greatest strengths.

Experiencing this openness and flexibility reinforced my belief in promoting freedom and independence in trainees’ decisions. I encourage them to explore their interests fully, take intellectual risks, pause when needed, and pursue their goals without hesitation while providing guidance and support to help them navigate challenges and make difficult decisions. I aim to use a Socratic method of mentoring where instead of telling my students what to do, I ask questions that prompt them to think deeply about their problems, concepts, and interests, fostering confidence in their own reasoning and judgement.

Having been fortunate to live in bigger cities, first New York and later Seattle, I loved being surrounded by people who were Asian, Hispanic, Black, queer, and trans; people who wore their pajamas to the supermarket and others who wore high heels for morning coffee. At first, I observed this diversity with curiosity; later, I came to appreciate it and realize that I was part of it. I often notice that I miss it when I fly to Greece, where life can feel more predictable and set in its ways. America's multiculturalism, built and shaped largely by immigrants, is an important reason why life here often seems vibrant and dynamic. It is difficult to watch the principles of diversity and inclusion face increasing challenges, particularly for immigrants, whose opportunities and sense of belonging tend to become more uncertain.

This diversity gave me the opportunity to train and grow close to students from all walks of life, something that would never have happened had I stayed in Greece. This experience has shaped my approach to mentorship, encouraging me to be more understanding, patient, and attentive to the diverse perspectives that trainees bring. It has also reinforced my interest to learn from this diversity and to recognize that rigidity is the enemy not only of science but also of effective mentorship.

I have been very fortunate throughout my career in the United States to consistently find employment as a Caenorhabditis elegans experimentalist, having realized early on that this was the path that made me happiest. Knowing that being in the lab, surrounded by worms and students, is where I belong, I found ways, sometimes not easily, to make this possible. First at Columbia, then at the University of Washington, and finally at the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center in Seattle, I have felt immensely grateful to have found communities that I am happy to be part of. At Fred Hutch in particular, I have recently found a dynamic community of scientists guided by curiosity, committed to basic research, and passionate about working with model systems, like the little worm. Here, I have found a scientific home and colleagues who go out of their way to show appreciation for me both as a scientist and as a mentor, something I have never taken for granted. Trainees from different labs seek me out for help, guidance, or collaboration, creating a sense of community unlike anything I have experienced before. Unfortunately, such thoughtful and innovative groups of basic scientists are often among the first to feel the effects of recent funding cuts, which could irreversibly harm scientific progress in America.

Despite all this, nostalgia for Greece, especially for my loved ones, is always present in my life. My life has unfolded through countless transatlantic phone calls, long flights and layovers, suitcases filled with gifts for Greece, olive oil and chocolates for friends in America. My gratitude for Skype, FaceTime, and Viber, which make daily communication with my loved ones possible is immense. I fear airplanes, I hate suitcases, and I dread the sound of the phone. One Monday morning, my worst fear was realized when I learned that my father had passed away unexpectedly. Being far from home during such a profoundly devastating moment for my family was unbearable. And yet, despite everything, I am still grateful to live in a time that allows me to bridge that distance and remain present for the people I love, even from afar. I remain torn between two countries and two lives, but I have come to accept that this is the reality for many of us who live abroad.

Life in America has given me extraordinary experiences and a sense that I have lived more than one life; I would not change a single second of it. Expatriation, especially in a vast country full of opportunities, is a profound life experience; it broadens one's horizons in unthinkable ways and slowly turns you into a citizen of the world. However, it is inseparable from a constant nostalgia for the country and people left behind. I know that I will always feel a longing for my homeland and, knowing my nature, I will always wonder how different my life might have been had I never left. I was drawn to basic research by intense curiosity, and still, if I were given a second life, on that morning in May 2004, I would still choose to board the plane to New York. This country has given me so much—possibility, diversity, appreciation, the chance to pursue my passion for experimental biological research, and the opportunity to mentor dozens of unique mentees—that I could never turn any of it away.
