Streets ahead: Neighborhood safety and active outdoor play in early childhood using a nationally representative sample of 5‐year‐olds
Authors: Suzanne M. Egan, Jennifer Pope
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractUsing data from a nationally representative sample of 5‐year‐olds in the Growing Up in Ireland study (N = 9001, 51% male), this research investigated the role of factors in the neighborhood environment on levels of active outdoor play in young children. Primary caregivers (98% mothers; 81% Irish) responded to questions regarding their child's levels of active outdoor play (e.g., chasing) and their perceptions of their neighborhood (e.g., social cohesion and antisocial behavior). Hierarchical regression analyses indicated that levels of active outdoor play were associated with parental perceptions of neighborhood safety and traffic levels, even after other factors were controlled for (i.e., family income). The results are discussed from a bioecological systems perspective. Implications for policy makers and parents are considered.

The benefits of outdoor play for young children across a range of physical and psychological indicators have been well documented (e.g., Kemple et al., 2016; Richardson et al., 2017). Playing outdoors affords opportunities for unstructured play (Sharma‐Brymer & Bland, 2016), freedom of movement and exploration involving natural resources (Bento & Dias, 2017), increased physical activity (Dankiw et al., 2020), and risky outdoor play also supports healthy child development (Brussoni et al., 2017). Cognitively, unstructured play outdoors affords opportunities for risk assessment (Brussoni et al., 2012), spatial awareness, rule formation and opportunities for negotiation (Gill, 2007), developing social skills, dramatic play (Dankiw et al., 2020), independence, and resilience (McArdle et al., 2013). Physically, the outdoor environment can provide opportunities to develop gross motor movements; affordances for running, jumping, and climbing are extended compared to the indoors (Gray et al., 2015; Sando, 2019; Vanderloo et al., 2013). Children are more active when playing outdoors compared to indoor play (Tandon et al., 2018). Hinkley et al. (2014) also identified that physical activity is positively, and sedentary behavior is inversely, associated with psychosocial well‐being.

Beyond the more obvious links to physical activity, outdoor play and engaging in nature‐based activities also have wider benefits for children's health and well‐being (Herrington & Brussoni, 2015). Outdoor play and access to green spaces have beneficial effects on children's mental health and well‐being (McCormick, 2017; Richardson et al., 2017). While childhood obesity is a complex and multifaceted issue, opportunities for outdoor play and activity may be a contributing factor (Zhang et al., 2020). However, despite the many benefits of outdoor play and exposure to nature, there is a growing concern regarding the declining and lack of time that children are spending outdoors (e.g., Gray, 2011; Louv, 2005). Mullan (2019) and others have also highlighted an apparent lack of opportunities for outdoor play for children (e.g., Singer et al., 2009), particularly unstructured and risky outdoor play (Brussoni et al., 2015; Malone, 2007). Given the many benefits of outdoor play, it is important therefore to understand the factors that can act as supports or barriers to outdoor play, and how different factors may interact with each other.

Supports and barriers to outdoor play

Previous research has identified a number of different types of supports and barriers relating to outdoor play, such as parental encouragement, neighborhood safety, social cohesion, and traffic levels. Parent's encouragement of outdoor play is one important factor in supporting outdoor play (Cleland et al., 2010). For example, Ferrao and Janssen (2015) reported that parental use of verbal cues to encourage their children to play outdoors was independently associated with outdoor active play among 7‐ to 12‐year‐olds. Parental perceptions of neighborhood safety have also been identified as a factor linked to levels of outdoor play and physical activity in young children (Carver et al., 2008; Veitch et al., 2010). For example, in an Australian study, Xu et al. (2017) reported a positive association with children's outdoor play if mothers perceived that the neighborhood was identified as a good place to bring up children, safe to play outside, and had good parks or playgrounds in the locality. In their study on low‐income families, Nicksic et al. (2018) also reported that parental‐perceived neighborhood safety was significantly associated with encouraging outdoor physical activity. However, based on a representative sample of parents of 3‐year‐olds in the United States, Burdette and Whitaker (2005) reported a link between mothers' perception of neighborhood safety and their children's TV viewing time but not with outdoor play time or BMI, although this was possibly due to the young age of the children included in the study.

Social cohesion or “the glue that holds society together” (Janmaat, 2011, p. 61) has also been identified as a key indicator of children's capacity to play outdoors and their independent mobility. In British Columbia, Parent et al. (2021) observed that children of parents who perceived strong trust in their neighbors were twice as likely to play outdoors in their neighborhood every day compared to those whose parents had less trust in their neighbors. Schoeppe et al. (2015) identified that women, parents of younger children, adults with lower education, and those who perceived neighborhood social cohesion as being lower were less willing to let children independently travel further away from home. In terms of parental‐rated neighborhood social cohesion, Loebach et al. (2021) also reported a significant positive relationship with the reported time children spent outdoors per week. In Brockman's study involving children aged 10–11, children themselves identified fear of older teenagers as a barrier to their outdoor active play (Brockman et al., 2011) while identifying access to green spaces as a motivator for outdoor play.

In terms of promoting children's mobility and independent travel, traffic safety has been identified as a limiting factor. However, this has also been identified as a factor when it comes to outdoor play. For example, Bringolf‐Isler et al. (2010) reported that parental concern about traffic safety was associated with less time playing outdoors in primary school children. In a qualitative study in Scotland, parents identified traffic issues as a key concern (Teedon et al., 2014), along with drug and alcohol misuse. In a longitudinal study in Quebec, Barnett et al. (2022) characterized neighborhoods using a range of built environment features (based on in‐person neighborhood assessments and geocoded administrative data) and reported that neighborhoods with lower traffic safety appeared to be more obesogenic to children. In their systematic review of the literature, Zougheibe et al. (2021) reported a correlation between increased concerns regarding road danger and a decrease in children's active behavior (including active travel, play, and exercise). Although the correlations were influenced by other factors such as child's sex/gender, age, car ownership, neighborhood types, across time, and proximity to destination.

Previous research has identified a number of neighborhood factors that play a role in levels of outdoor play in young children, such as neighborhood safety, neighborhood cohesion, neighborhood facilities, and antisocial behavior. In their systematic review, Boxberger and Reimers (2019) also identified a number of parental correlates in the amount of time children (aged 0–12) spent outdoors such as mothers' ethnicity, mothers' employment status, parents' education level, the importance parents assign to outdoor play, and perceived social cohesion in the neighborhood. A recent systematic literature review on the influences of outdoor free play in 0‐ to 6‐year‐olds, Gemmell et al. (2023) identified neighborhood space for play, green and natural environments, traffic and pedestrian environments, and neighborhood designs that facilitate social connection with neighbors as factors that support young children's outdoor play.

In interpreting the findings of previous studies on outdoor play in young children, it is important to consider parental perceptions of neighborhoods and traffic compared to objectively measured characteristics of neighborhoods and traffic. For example, Rothman et al. (2015) note that parental perceptions of traffic danger may not always concur with objective measures of traffic danger. Their perceptions, however, may influence their parenting decisions and may depend on the age of the child (van den Berg et al., 2020).

The current study

From a children's rights perspective, the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UN General Assembly, 1989; UNCRC, 2013) states that every child has the right to grow up in an environment where they feel safe and secure and they also have the right to develop, to play and to learn in an environment that is free from harm. Ireland ratified the UNCRC in 1992 and must ensure that all children's rights are available to all children without discrimination. Therefore, from a rights‐based and equity perspective, understanding the factors that act as supports or barriers to outdoor play for young children is very important to ensure equality of opportunity. The Growing Up in Ireland study was established so that children's lives in Ireland could be better understood and could inform future policy and practice.

While previous research has identified a number of neighborhood factors that play a role in levels of outdoor play in young children, fewer studies, however, have looked at how these factors combine and play a role in levels of outdoor play. In Lee et al.'s (2021) systematic review of the literature, while identifying a range of correlates of outdoor play and/or outdoor time across individual, parental level, and wider factors at a macro level, the authors also highlight a gap in the literature in terms of the influence of family systems on children's outdoor play. Based on their findings, they recommend a more holistic approach toward identifying influencing factors and examining interactions and processes between factors, for example, and how the sociocultural environment may influence parents' practices and concerns.

It is evident that there are both facilitators and barriers that impact children's level of outdoor play. These factors can be contextualized using Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory (Bronfenbrenner, 2005) which maintains that children's development is affected by factors across many different systems that work concurrently and are interconnected. The microsystem, the child's immediate environment (e.g., their home, their neighborhood, or school), is influenced by relationships between wider systems including the mesosystem (where microsystems affecting the child interact such as the interaction between the home and neighborhood environment). The macrosystem is a distant system from the child but still influences the child, and includes cultural factors, legislation, and governing policies (e.g., these may relate to the provision of parks). The exosystem includes factors that affect the child such as the parent's work environment or arrangements. The final system is the chronosystem which includes significant times, events, or changes across the life of the child and society (see Pope et al., 2021, for wider discussion; Bronfenbrenner, 2005).

Adopting an ecological systems lens, the aim of this paper was to explore how a combination of neighborhood‐related factors (i.e., parental perceptions of the neighborhood environment, social cohesion, antisocial behavior, and access to good neighborhood facilities and amenities) are associated with levels of active outdoor play in young children in a nationally representative sample. Additionally, we also sought to explore if these neighborhood factors were still associated with levels of outdoor play, even after other factors were statistically controlled for, such as parents' education and income levels, and their employment.

It may be the case that parents with particular education or income levels live in particular neighborhoods and have particular beliefs about outdoor play (see Boxberger & Reimers, 2019; Lee et al., 2021 for further reviews of the factors that may influence outdoor play). Therefore, controlling for these potential confounding factors allows us to explore whether neighborhood factors are significantly associated with levels of outdoor play by themselves or if the neighborhood factors are underscored by these other variables. Therefore, the study reported below was exploratory in nature.

METHOD

The data reported in this paper were collected as part of the Growing Up in Ireland study, an ongoing national cohort longitudinal study of children. The current study involved a secondary analysis of the data gathered when the children were 5 years old. This wave of data includes information related to the variables of interest, both active outdoor play and measures of parental perceptions of the neighborhood environment. This information was not collected together in other waves of data collection. The data analyzed in the current study were mainly gathered as part of the primary caregiver's main questionnaire, as only the mother was asked questions in relation to the child's active outdoor play and detailed questions about the neighborhood environment. The questionnaire for the secondary caregiver at this age was shorter and did not include any questions related to the child's active outdoor play and only one question related to the neighborhood (i.e., how do you feel about your neighborhood as a place for bringing up children).

The Growing Up in Ireland study is a nationally representative study that commenced in 2006 and is funded by the Government of Ireland. See https://www.growingup.gov.ie for more information, including complete questionnaires, ethical approval for the study, and participant consent. Data were initially gathered from over 11,000 nine‐month‐old infants (born between December 1, 2007, and June 30, 2008) and 9000 nine‐year‐olds, along with their families, caregivers, teachers, and school principals with subsequent waves of data gathered approximately every 2 to 4 years. The data are available by request from the Irish Social Science Data Archive (https://www.ucd.ie/issda/).

Participants

The sample consisted of 9001 Irish children when they were 5 years of age (51% male), along with their parent(s). The majority of primary caregivers (98% female, average age 36 years) (hereafter also referred to as the child's mother) indicated that they were of an Irish ethnicity (81%), of a Roman Catholic or other Christian religious denomination (97.7%), and that their child's first language was English (90%). In 98% of two‐parent families, the secondary caregiver was the 5‐year‐old's biological father (average age of 39 years; see Table 1). The children were initially selected to take part in the Growing Up in Ireland study when they were 9‐month‐old infants (N = 11,134) using a stratified sampling technique in order to obtain a nationally representative sample. Complete details of the sample characteristics are available in Murray et al. (2019). No children were excluded from the current analysis.

Sample design

The Child Benefit Register was used as the sampling frame to originally select potential participants for the Growing Up in Ireland study when the children were 9 months old to obtain a nationally representative sample. Child Benefit is a social welfare payment to all families with children in Ireland and has almost universal uptake. It, therefore, provided a complete list of all infants born in the target age range during the sampling period, from which to draw an appropriate sample. Of the 41,185 children in total born in Ireland between December 1, 2007, and June 30, 2008, 11,134 participated in the study. The sample for the Growing Up in Ireland study was selected on a systematic basis and was stratified by a number of factors, including the county of residence, the number of children in the family, the marital status of the parents, and the nationality of the payee.

No new participants were added to the sample as time progressed so the sample at age 5 represents those families who chose to continue their participation in the Growing Up in Ireland study, having first participated when the infant was 9 months old. The 9001 children who participated at age 5 represent 81% of the 11,134 that originally participated in the study at age 9 months (at age 3 years, 9793 families took part or 91% of the original sample) (see Murray et al., 2019). To account for the attrition of participants over time, a weighting variable was constructed by the Growing Up in Ireland study team based on the most recent Census and Child Benefit Register data to account for differential response and attrition rates according to background family characteristics (Williams et al., 2019). This weighting variable was applied to the current analysis and ensures that the data are representative of the population under study, in line with best international practice (Williams et al., 2019).

Procedure

As part of the Growing Up in Ireland study, initial contact with the family about the data collection was made via a letter from the Growing Up in Ireland study team, to provide information about the study and invite them to participate. At age 5, both the primary caregiver (98% of whom were the child's mother) and the secondary giver (98% of whom were the child's father) were asked to provide data. Translated questionnaires were available to non‐English‐speaking participants. Once participants gave their informed consent to participate in the research, trained interviewers for the Growing Up in Ireland study subsequently visited each family in their home in order to gather the data for the study. The Growing Up in Ireland study questions were read aloud to parents by the trained interviewer who noted parent's responses on a laptop. The questionnaires included questions related to the lives of the individual parents (e.g., education, work, and health), many aspects of family life (e.g., school, childcare, hobbies, and stress), as well as questions related to outdoor play and neighborhoods, which are analyzed in this study.

Materials

Questions related to the target variables of interest (i.e., active outdoor play and parent's perceptions of the neighborhood) were included among the many questions parents answered as part of the Growing Up in Ireland study. These measures are described below. In designing the questionnaires and scales for use in the Growing Up in Ireland study, the study team aimed to synchronize instruments with other contemporary longitudinal child cohort studies to enable international comparisons where possible (e.g., Murray et al., 2019).

Active outdoor play

The questions relating to the frequency of active outdoor play activities were included on the questionnaire for the primary caregiver, in a section relating to the child's play and activities that the parent may carry out with the child. This section also asked questions about how often the parent would engage in other activities with the child (e.g., play computer games with the child), how often the child engaged in various types of activities at home (e.g., painting or drawing), and how many books the child had access to in the home. Other separate sections on the primary caregiver questionnaire asked about school and early years settings, but these sections did not include questions on outdoor play activities.

The primary caregiver, in most cases, the child's mother, responded to a series of five questions relating to how often their child engages in various types of active outdoor play activities. These activities were (i) “climbing trees, climbing frame, wall bars, etc.,” (ii) “plays with a ball,” (iii) “plays chasing,” (iv) “rides a bike, tricycle or scooter,” and (v) “skates.” The response options were as follows: 1—never; 2—less than once per week; 3—one or two times per week; 4—three to six times per week; and 5—everyday. Parents also had the option of responding “Don't know.” An overall outdoor play score was calculated for each child (M = 17.2, SD = 3.2) by summing the score on each of the individual five activities parents were asked about, such that a score of 5 indicated that a child never engaged in any of the outdoor activities and a score of 25 indicated that a child engaged in each of the activities every day. Parent report measures of outdoor play frequency have been reported to significantly correlate with direct measures of physical activity in children, suggesting they are a valid way to measure outdoor play (Burdette et al., 2004).

Parental perceptions of the neighborhood environment

The primary caregiver also responded to a series of questions about their neighborhood and community, asked in the final section of the primary caregiver's main questionnaire. Participants were asked to indicate their agreement (1—strongly agree; 2—agree; 3—disagree; and 4—strongly disagree) with a series of 13 statements about their neighborhood. These statements have been used in other studies previously (e.g., Lochner et al., 1999; Sampson et al., 1997; Xu et al., 2017), including the Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children (Fraser et al., 2013; Yakubovich et al., 2020). Studies that have used statements such as those used in the current study to explore neighborhood environments have reported both good face validity and good construct validity (e.g., Young et al., 2004).

The statements in the current study related to neighborhood safety (e.g., this is a safe neighborhood; it is safe for children to play outside during the day; and there is heavy traffic on my street or road), social cohesion (e.g., people around here are willing to help their neighbors; most people in your neighborhood can be trusted; you feel a strong sense of identity with your neighborhood; and you are well informed about local affairs), and access to good local facilities and amenities (e.g., there are good parks, playgrounds, and play spaces; the state of the footpaths, roads, and street lighting is good; there is access to basic shopping facilities; there is access to basic services such as banks, medical clinics, etc.; there is access to close affordable regular public transport; and if you need information about local services, you know where to find that information).

Antisocial behavior in the neighborhood was measured by the primary caregiver indicating how common (1—very common; 2—fairly common; 3—not very common; and 4—not at all common) four aspects of antisocial behavior were in their area (e.g., rubbish and litter lying about; homes and gardens in bad condition, vandalism, and deliberate damage to property; and people being drunk or taking drugs in public). Definitions of antisocial behaviors range from common sense understandings of violations of social norms to the inclusion of criminal acts, with no one standard agreed‐upon definition in literature or legislation (see Vaughan et al., 2022, for a discussion). Finally, both the primary and the secondary caregiver were asked to provide an overall rating of how they felt about their neighborhood as a place for bringing up children (1—excellent; 2—good; 3—average; 4—poor; and 5—very poor; parents could also indicate “don't know”). This final overall rating was provided by each parent separately. This is the only measure of the neighborhood environment provided by the secondary caregiver on their questionnaire.

Analytical approach

In order to explore the association of the various neighborhood environmental factors with levels of active outdoor play in young children, an overall outdoor play score was calculated for each child as described above. Hierarchical multiple regression was then used to determine which different neighborhood variables were associated with levels of active outdoor play (Gelman & Hill, 2006). This type of analysis was chosen because it can determine how much variance in the outcome variable is explained by independent variables. The researcher decides the order in which independent variables, or groups of independent variables, are entered into the regression model, typically on the basis of practical or theoretical grounds (Howitt & Cramer, 2017). Drawing on Bronfenbrenner's ecological system model, the neighborhood‐related variables were entered in blocks to the regression model, in a manner consistent with the different types of potentially influential neighborhood factors. These analyses were exploratory in nature.

Parent perceptions of neighborhood safety were entered in Block 1, as this related to the safety of the local environment (i.e., the microsystem in which a young child plays outdoors). Parent perceptions of neighborhood social cohesion were entered in Block 2, as this related to the relationships and connections between the parent and their neighbors and local community (i.e., the mesosystem connections). Parent perceptions of antisocial behavior in the neighborhood were entered in Block 3, as this related to both people in the local community (e.g., those being drunk or taking drugs in public) as well as the physical state of the area (e.g., litter lying around). Parent perceptions of neighborhood facilities were entered in Block 4, as these represent macrosystem factors related to the provision of physical and social infrastructure such as paths, lighting, public parks, transport, shops, and banks.

A final block was then added to the hierarchical regression model to account for the influence of family circumstances relating to number of hours worked per week by each parent, the highest education level achieved by each parent, and the equivalized household annual income. These family variables (which include exosystem factors such as parental work time) allow us to determine if the various neighborhood‐related variables (e.g., traffic and social cohesion) make a unique contribution to the final regression model, independent of the family circumstances and also of other neighborhood factors.

Individual items were entered into the regression model in the aforementioned blocks in order to obtain a detailed understanding of the exact factors that are associated with levels of outdoor play in young children. For example, while rubbish and litter lying about, homes and gardens being in bad condition and people bring drunk or taking drugs in public are all aspects of antisocial behavior, they may not all be associated with levels of outdoor play in young children. Understanding which, if any, aspects of antisocial behavior act as a barrier to outdoor play is potentially useful in terms of how financial and human resources at the local or national level can be best used to support outdoor play (e.g., increasing policing or drug addiction facilities to address taking drugs in public or multiple strategies to address issues with littering).

The hierarchical regression model enables an understanding of the role of each of the individual factors that may potentially influence outdoor play levels, as well as the collective role of the groups of variables entered in each block (i.e., by examining the additional variance accounted for by the model, when each block of variables is added to the hierarchal regression) (Howitt & Cramer, 2017).

RESULTS

The findings of the study indicated that most children engaged in active outdoor play multiple times per week with 64% playing chasing every day, and a further 21% playing chasing 3–6 times per week. Nearly half of the parents indicated also that their child rode a bike, tricycle, or scooter (49%) or played with a ball (47%) every day, with more children engaged in these play behaviors 3–6 times per week (24% for riding a bike and 22% for playing with a ball) (see Figure 1). In relation to the neighborhood environment, most parents agreed or strongly agreed that their neighborhood is safe (96.1%) and that it is safe for children to play outside (83%). Furthermore, 88% indicated that their neighborhood was a good or excellent place to bring up children, with 72% agreeing that there are good parks, playgrounds, and play spaces. However, a substantial minority of 17% of parents in the sample disagreed or strongly disagreed that it is safe for children to play outside.

Percentage of parents indicating how often their 5‐year‐old child engages in various active outdoor play activities.

As regards the factors that may be concerning parents about their neighborhood, 30% of parents in the study indicated that there was heavy traffic on their road or street, 29% disagreed there were good parks, playgrounds, and play spaces, 30% disagreed that the state of the footpaths, roads, and street lighting was good, while 24% indicated that rubbish and litter lying about was fairly or very common. In terms of social cohesion and antisocial behavior, 8% of parents disagreed that most people in their neighborhood could be trusted. A similar percentage of parents also reported that was fairly or very common for there to be vandalism and deliberate damage to property (9%) and people being drunk or taking drugs in public (7%).

What factors are associated with levels of active outdoor play in young children?

Block 1 of the regression model showed that parental perceptions of neighborhood safety factors accounted for 1.5% of the variance in outdoor play scores, Radj2 = .015, F(5, 3397) = 11.26, p < .001. An examination of the standardized beta values indicated that the mothers overall rating of how safe it is for children to play outside during the day made a unique contribution to the model in Block 1, β = −.113, p < .001, as did the mother's feelings about the neighborhood as a place to bring up children, β = −.061, p = .007. The father's feelings about the neighborhood as a place to bring up children did not make a significant contribution to the model, β = −.113, p = .39, nor did the mother's overall rating of neighborhood safety, β = .017, p = .40, or the level of heavy traffic, β = −.032, p = .072. The same pattern was evident in the final model after all variables were entered including antisocial behaviors and family factors (see Table 1). This suggests that mothers' perceptions of neighborhood safety make a unique contribution to the regression model, even when factors such as family income and education levels are accounted for.

When mothers' perceptions of neighborhood cohesion were added in Block 2, the model accounted for 2.1% of the variance in outdoor play scores, Radj2 = .021, F(9, 3393) = 9.09, p < .001. In the final model, both a sense of identity with the neighborhood, β = −.062, p < .008, and how well informed the mother was about local affairs, β = −.050, p = .035, made significant unique contributions to the model. After antisocial behaviors and crime were added in Block 3 and neighborhood facilities were added in Block 4, the model accounted for 2.6% of the variance in active outdoor play scores, Radj2 = .025, F(19, 3383) = 5.81, p < .001.

Parent factors related to work, education, and income were added in Block 5 and the final model accounted for 4% of the variance in active outdoor play scores, Radj2 = .04, F(24, 3378) = 6.93, p < .001. In the final model (see Table 1), the neighborhood factors that made a significant unique contribution to the model were the mothers' overall rating of how safe it is for children to play outside during the day, β = −.088, the mother's feelings about the neighborhood as a place to bring up children, β = −.058, her sense of identity with the neighborhood, β = −.062, how well informed the mother was about local affairs, β = −.050, and people being drunk or taking drugs in public, β = −.092. This suggests that even after parental income and education levels are accounted for, multiple aspects of the neighborhood environment related to parental perceptions of safety, social cohesion, and antisocial behaviors are associated with levels of active outdoor play in young children.

The findings of the regression model indicate that there are significant associations between levels of active outdoor play and the mother's perceptions of people being drunk or taking drugs in public, and the mother's overall ratings of how safe it is for children to play outside during the day. Public drink or drug taking had a negative association with levels of active outdoor play, as might be anticipated such that the more common it was, the less frequently the child engaged in active outdoor play. In contrast, mother's perceptions of how safe it is for their child to play outside during the day had a positive association with levels of active outdoor play. However, while it might be expected that parents who think it is safe for their children to play outside during the day would have children with higher levels of active outdoor play, the question remains as to what is contributing to parent perceptions of their neighborhood being safe or not for their child to play outside? In order to answer this question, we conducted another hierarchical regression, using instead this time as a criterion variable the mother's perception of their neighborhood being safe or not for their child to play outside, to explore what neighborhood factors are influencing this (e.g., traffic, trusting neighbors, and antisocial behavior).

What factors are associated with parents' perceptions of neighborhood safety for active outdoor play?

Hierarchical regression was used to determine the extent to which each of the categories of independent variables was associated with parents' rating of how safe it is for children to play outside during the day. Similar to the previous regression model, parents' perceptions of neighborhood safety were entered in Block 1, with their perceptions of neighborhood cohesion entered in Block 2. Parents' perceptions of antisocial behavior in the neighborhood were entered in Block 3 while their perceptions of neighborhood facilities were entered in Block 4. Similar to the previous hierarchical regression, in Block 5, the control variables were entered into the model relating to the parent's work hours, education level, and income level.

Block 1 of the regression showed that parents perceptions of various aspects of neighborhood safety accounted for 23% of the variance in their ratings of how safe it is for children to play outside, Radj2 = .23, F(4, 3398) = 254.50, p < .001. An examination of the standardized beta values indicated all of the factors made a significant unique contribution in this block including the level of heavy traffic, β = −.239, p < .001, the overall rating of neighborhood safety, β = .208, p < .001, the father's feelings about the neighborhood as a place to bring up children, β = .194, p < .001, and the mother's feelings about the neighborhood as a place to bring up children, β = .066, p < .001. These factors all continued to make significant unique contributions to the model in Block 5 when all other variables were entered.

Parent perceptions of neighborhood cohesion were included in Block 2 of the model and they added 5.2% to the variance accounted for how safe it is for children to play outside, Radj2 = .281, F(8, 3394) = 167.60, p < .001. In the final model, the mother's belief that most people in the neighborhood can be trusted, β = .115, p < .001, and that people are willing to help their neighbors, β = .14, p < .001, made significant unique contributions to the model. After antisocial behavior and crime were added in Block 3, and neighborhood facilities were added in Block 4, the model accounted for an additional 1.1% of the variance in how safe it is for children to play outside, Radj2 = .292, F(18, 3384) = 78.90, p < .001.

Parent factors related to work, education, and income were added in Block 5 and the final model accounted for an additional 1.6% of the variance in parental perceptions of how safe it is for children to play outside, Radj2 = .307, F(23, 3379) = 66.45, p < .001. The final model with all variables entered accounted for 30.7% of the variance in mothers' perceptions of how safe it is for children to play outside during the day (see Table 2). In the final model, the neighborhood factors that made significant unique contributions to the model were the mothers overall rating of how safe the neighborhood is, β = .114, the father's feelings about the neighborhood as a place to bring up children, β = .096, and also the mother's feelings, β = .044, how willing people are to help their neighbors, β = .144, and how well people in the neighborhood can be trusted, β = .115. A significant unique contribution was also made by parents' perceptions of how much heavy traffic there is on the street or road, β = −.211, which indicated that the higher the rating of heavy traffic the lower the rating of safety for children to play outside. Another significant factor that also acted as a barrier to outdoor play in the model was parental perceptions of people being drunk or taking drugs in public, β = −.068, p = .001. Interestingly, parental perceptions of other elements of antisocial behavior such as rubbish and litter lying about, homes and gardens in bad condition or vandalism, and deliberate damage to property did not make significant unique contributions to the final model, all p's greater than or equal to .662. However, it may be the case that these factors were already accounted for by parents' ratings of how safe they perceive the neighborhood to be overall (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

The findings of this study provide some initial evidence of the interplay of neighborhood factors that may be associated with levels of active outdoor play in early childhood and parental perceptions of neighborhood safety. The findings indicate that levels of active outdoor play were associated with a number of different factors, including parental perceptions of neighborhood safety, particularly, parent's perceived safety of their neighborhood for children to play outside during the day. Parental perceptions of neighborhood safety continued to be associated with levels of active outdoor play even after other factors were controlled for, such as the income and education levels of the family. These findings, based on children living in Ireland, are in keeping with international findings.

For example, based on their sample of low‐income families, Nicksic et al. (2018) also reported that parental‐perceived neighborhood safety was significantly associated with encouraging outdoor physical activity. In their younger‐aged Australian sample (2‐ and 3.5‐year‐olds), Xu et al. (2017) observed a positive association in young children's outdoor play with mother's perceptions of the neighborhood environment if they rated it as a good place to bring up children and that it was safe to play outdoors and if there were good parks or playgrounds in neighborhood, although other neighborhood environment factors, such as traffic, was not associated with children's outdoor play at 2 and 3.5 years. However, they did report the percentage of mothers who thought it was not safe for children to play outdoors and there was heavy traffic in the neighborhood significantly increased from 26% to 42% and from 19% to 26%, respectively, across the two data waves. Therefore, the age of the child may be an influencing factor.

The findings from this current study also highlighted the detrimental impact of heavy traffic on parental perceptions of safety in line with some other studies (Gemmell et al., 2023). While Zougheibe et al. (2021) did report an association with increased concerns regarding road danger and a decrease in children's active play, other demographic factors did impact this relationship. In the current study, these factors were included in the regression model but the relationship remained significant. Bringolf‐Isler et al. (2010) reported that traffic safety was associated with less time playing outdoors, and several studies have highlighted the link between perceived traffic safety and independent mobility, for example, although not all studies have reported the association with outdoor play levels. However, in a Canadian study of older children, parental perceptions of traffic were not found to be associated with levels of outdoor play (Nguyen et al., 2018). These differences in findings across studies and countries potentially highlight the benefits of adopting an ecological systems approach when interpreting findings, as consideration of macrosystem (e.g., culture) and chronosystem (e.g., age of the child) factors may play a role.

Factors relating to neighborhood cohesion, neighborhood facilities, and antisocial behavior were also associated with levels of active outdoor play in young children and parental perceptions of how safe the neighborhood was. The findings suggest that people taking drink or drugs in public was a significant negative factor associated with active outdoor play levels and parental perceptions. While the current study used a quantitative approach to understanding the role of various factors, qualitative research has also found this was identified by parents as a key issue (Teedon et al., 2014). Interestingly, none of the neighborhood facilities, such as good paths, light, and roads, or close and affordable public transport were associated with levels of outdoor play, however, they did influence parental perceptions of how safe it is for their child to play outside during the day. This was also true of people in the neighborhood helping and trusting their neighbors.

In their framework to characterize social cohesion, Fonseca et al. (2019) highlight the interconnectedness and dependency across individuals, communities, and institutions. They maintain that individual beliefs and perceptions of an environment relate directly to individual experiences that people are able to have within that environment. Cohesion within a group is possible if there are compatible norms and values. Children's experiences may therefore be strongly influenced by parental perceptions of social cohesion, and this was observed in this study in terms of levels of engagement with the outdoors in the local environment.

Therefore, in order to address parental perceptions around neighborhood cohesion and safety, there is no “quick fix” solution and issues need to be addressed from an ecological approach (Bronfenbrenner, 2005), mindful of how multiple systems interact (Bronfenbrenner, 1979, 1994). Within this theory, factors interact within and across different systems, for example, what happens at an individual (or microsystem level) such as a parent restricting their child from playing outside is influenced by wider neighborhood factors and decisions made at a macrosystem level. Examples could include policies around traffic calming measures or legislation around speeding, planning laws in relation to access to green spaces in urban or suburban areas, or policing and tolerance in terms of antisocial behavior. Within the exosystem, parental work commitments, long working hours, etc. may limit time in the neighborhood which may impact levels of social cohesion and connection or capacity to supervise children outside. Income levels, housing, and wider sociocultural factors at a chronosystem level may also impact. The environments that young children have to live, play, and grow up in matter. The Center on the Developing Child at Harvard (2010) highlights the importance of safe and supportive physical, chemical (free from toxins), and built environments as a core foundation for lifelong health that is laid in early childhood.

There are many factors and relationships that can influence young children's health and development both directly and indirectly through different interconnected systems within an ecological systems theory (Bronfenbrenner, 2005). Due to the extensive data collected in the Growing Up in Ireland study, and through the analysis modeling process in the current study, the neighborhood factors that were associated with outdoor play in young children were able to be considered through an ecological lens. For example, it was possible to control for factors within the exosystem such as the number of hours parents work, as well as microsystem factors such as parents' perception of the quality of the neighborhood environment.

A key strength of this study is that it was possible to statistically control for factors from different levels of Bronfenbrenner's model, including family circumstances in the microsystem, in order to identify the neighborhood factors that were associated with levels of active outdoor play and parental perceptions of neighborhood safety. From an ecological perspective, this is an example of how factors within the macrosystem such as policies and local or national governance decisions in terms of density of traffic or antisocial behavior in an area can impact parents' perceptions of safety, and potentially affect children's play outdoors. Additionally, by examining multiple factors in one study, it is possible to identify which factors play an important role and which factors may matter less.

By identifying the factors that are important in shaping parental perceptions of neighborhood safety and levels of active outdoor play, government policies and resources can be directed toward those elements of the neighborhood environment that matter most. At this macrosystem level, in line with findings by Gemmell et al. (2023), neighborhood planning should move to create safe networks and infrastructure for young children and their families that prioritize affordances for meaningful interactions with local nature and the community in order to promote well‐being and build social cohesion.

There are many educational initiatives to promote road safety with children directly through early childhood settings and schools. In Ireland, for example, the Road Safety Authority provides high‐visibility equipment and teaching resources for children. However, there is also a need for a collective responsibility to ensure safe streets and environments for children at a macro level such as through traffic calming measures, enforced speed restrictions, and safe streets for cycling and walking. Some jurisdictions are engaging in initiatives such as “play streets” that involve neighborhood‐led street closures to facilitate play opportunities for children and adults.

Previous research suggests measures such as street closures for “play streets” support local communities in various ways. For example, D'Haese et al. (2015) reported an increase in physical activity and a decrease in sedentary activity when a play street intervention was introduced in an urban environment when children were on school vacation. There can also be wider benefits beyond physical activity and play opportunities. Umstattd Meyer et al. (2021) reported increased social cohesion through play streets initiatives by “fostering community connections” and building “perceptions of safety and trust in the community.” In their systematic review of play streets in 2019, Umstattd Meyer et al. also highlighted the positive impacts overall in terms of physical activity and the potential to promote community involvement, although they did acknowledge the need for further rigorous evaluations.

Limitations and future research

While the findings of this study give insight into the factors that are associated with perceptions of neighborhood safety and levels of outdoor play in young children, there are a number of limitations that should be noted when considering the findings. A limitation of this study is the focus on one wave of cross‐sectional data collected as part of this longitudinal study when children were aged 5 years. Future research might examine how neighborhood safety at age 5 might be associated with levels of outdoor play in later childhood, where this is measured. However, while it should be noted that the current research was cross‐sectional in nature, a strength of the study is that it is based on a large, nationally representative sample.

Other limitations also relate to the measures used in the Growing Up in Ireland study. For example, the activities measured for active outdoor play were limited in scope and may not capture all of the different activities that children engage in when playing outside. Future research should include additional measures of outdoor play, such as through time use diaries, for example, and other outdoor play activities. Across the literature, there is ambiguity around definitions of outdoor play/active play/risky play/outdoor time and Lee et al. (2021) call for a standardized approach to terminology and measures of outdoor play/time to facilitate cross‐comparative analysis. One limitation of the measures used in the Growing Up in Ireland study is that they rely on parental recollections and perceptions in relation to active outdoor play and the neighborhood environment. Future studies should consider the use of more refined measures of data collection including objective trackers of physical activity and outdoor play (e.g., Burdette et al., 2004), as well as questions about the precise locations that the active outdoor play took place in (e.g., local park, the street, etc.; Veitch et al., 2009) in order to improve the reliability and validity of measures.

Additionally, these were parental report measures rather than gathered through child observation or child reporting. While parents of 5‐year‐old children might be expected to have a reasonably high level of supervision of their young child when playing outside, future research should consider using alternative measures that would complement and extend the methods used in this study. Increasingly, studies are adopting child‐centered, participatory data collection techniques such as a mosaic approach to gain young children's own perspectives on their experiences (Clark & Moss, 2011) including methods such as listening to children and discussing their visual representations, artwork, and photo elicitation. For example, Castonguay and Jutras (2009) asked children to photograph outdoor places in their neighborhood and subsequent interviews discussed the places they liked and disliked.

Another potential limitation related to the measures used is the parental report of the neighborhood factors such as safety, antisocial behaviors, neighborhood cohesion, and neighborhood facilities. Parents self‐reported their perceptions of these factors and it is reasonable to consider how accurate parental perceptions of neighborhood safety and knowledge of the local environment may be (e.g., Fernandez et al., 2023). For example, different parents may have different standards of what constitutes helping behavior from neighbors, what constitutes a garden in poor condition, or what is a “good” playground, park, or play space. It may be the case that what a parent considers “heavy traffic” may not be heavy traffic according to local or national norms but to the standard of what might be “heavy” for a 5‐year‐old child to safely play nearby. Future research may be able to include an objective way to quantify these factors in further studies, although it is also important to acknowledge the validity of parental subjective perceptions and their impact on behavior (Yakubovich et al., 2020). Parents' perceptions are in most cases going to be subjective regarding what they consider to be safe in their neighborhood and for each parent, some factors will be more important than others in supporting, or acting as a barrier to, outdoor play for each young child.

Despite these limitations, however, the findings do shed light on the factors influencing parents' perceptions of neighborhood safety and potentially their decisions to allow their young children outside to play. Evidence would suggest that having access to green spaces, nature play, and private gardens has positive impacts on children's physical and mental health and well‐being across a range of indicators (Armstrong et al., 2019; Brockman et al., 2011). From a children's rights perspective, under the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child, children have the right to play. Article 31, for example, stresses that “every child has the right to rest and leisure, to engage in play and recreation activities appropriate to the age of the child, and to participate freely in cultural life and the arts” (UN General Assembly, 1989). Ireland ratified this convention in 1992 so, as with the vast majority of other countries, there is a national responsibility to ensure that children have such appropriate opportunities to play, as is their right.

However, the findings from this current study would suggest that not all children have access to such amenities and are not getting such opportunities which may potentially perpetuate existing social inequalities in health and well‐being (Williams et al., 2016). Adopting an ecological approach is required to implement change (Bronfenbrenner, 1979, 2005). Therefore, parents, early years professionals, schoolteachers, after schools, local communities, and policy makers and legislators, in conjunction with children themselves, all need to consider how outdoor play can be encouraged and facilitated. The infrastructure needs to be put in place to support child‐friendly cities and local environments, ensure equality of access, and nurture social cohesion within communities.

Internationally, these findings are consistent with findings from other studies highlighting the importance of the local environment and the impact it can have on parents' and young children's play opportunities and their developmental trajectories (Villanueva et al., 2016). The findings of a recently published systematic review of 53 studies (Gemmell et al., 2023) considered the influence of neighborhood‐built environments on outdoor free play in young children and highlighted that there are few previous studies on the topic that have drawn on nationally representative samples of young children. This current study, based on a nationally representative sample with a large sample size, has reported important insights into the lives of young children in terms of their active play outdoors, their parent's perceptions of their localities, and how they are associated. Similarly, few previous studies have been able to explore the combination of neighborhood factors examined in the current study, while also accounting for family factors such as the education and income levels of the parents. Therefore, this study offers important insight into the lives of young children in Ireland, as well as the role of various ecological system factors in active outdoor play and parental perceptions of the neighborhood environment.

CONCLUSION

The findings from this large nationally representative sample indicate that levels of active outdoor play were associated with measures of neighborhood safety, particularly parents' perceived safety of their neighborhood for children to play outside during the day. Factors relating to neighborhood cohesion, neighborhood facilities, and antisocial behavior also were associated with levels of active outdoor play in young children, even when other family‐related factors were controlled for. The level of heavy traffic also made a contribution to parents' perceptions of how safe it was for their children to play outside. This is valuable empirical evidence to support the view that children's local outdoor environment and how safe it is perceived to be affect the level of outdoor play and engagement with the outdoors. From a children's rights perspective, it is important that all those involved in the lives of children, including children themselves, their families, communities, and policy makers at local and macrosystem levels put the necessary supports in place to ensure that all children have the opportunity to grow up in safe, supportive, and stimulating environments.
