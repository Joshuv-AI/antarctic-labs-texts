Multifunctional signal probe-based immunochromatographic test strip for rapid detection of aflatoxin B1 in complex matrix samples
Authors: Wenjie Ren, Ting Wang, Jiarui Pang, Yifan Li, Yuxiu Han, Baoshan He, Renyong Zhao, Min Wei, Wenhong Zhao, Zhiguang Suo, Yiwei Xu, Zhui Tu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmunochromatographic test strips (ICTS) are widely used for the immediate and rapid detection of toxins; however, they are prone to false-positive results and insufficient sensitivity when applied to complex sample matrices. To overcome these limitations, herein, a multifunctional signal probe-based ICTS was developed for the rapid detection of aflatoxin B1 (AFB1) in complex matrix samples by conjugating biotinylated nanobodies (G8-Bio) with streptavidin-modified magnetic beads (MB-SA) as the probes. The constructed ICTS achieved a limit of detection (LOD) of 0.063 ng/mL for AFB1. In corn spiked experiments, the recovery rates ranged from 98 % to 102 %, with coefficients of variation (CV) between 1.4 % and 6.86 %, confirming the reliability of this method. Furthermore, the IC10 value in soy sauce samples was 0.069 ng/mL, indicating its suitability for detecting AFB1 in dark-colored matrices. This approach provides a novel strategy for the rapid and accurate detection of trace toxins in complex matrix samples.Keywords: Magnetic beads, Streptavidin-biotin system, Nanobody, Immunochromatographic test strip, Complex matrix sample

Introduction

Aflatoxin B1 (AFB1), classified as a Group I carcinogen (Ostry et al., 2016), is frequently detected in various agricultural products and food items (Rushing & Selim, 2019). Prolonged consumption of AFB1-contaminated foods poses significant risks to human health (Eneroth et al., 2017). Therefore, the rapid and precise accurate detection of trace levels of AFB1 contamination is essential for implementing early warning systems and source control measures. In this context, immunochromatographic test strips (ICTS) have increasingly gained favor among researchers due to their advantages, which include ease of use, short detection times, low costs, and the absence of specialized instrumentation requirements. ICTS represent a widely used rapid detection technology that relies on the specific binding interactions between antibodies and antigens (Zhang et al., 2024). Compared with traditional laboratory-based precision large-scale instrumental methods such as high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Wang et al., 2022) and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) (Romero-Sánchez et al., 2022), ICTS presents several advantages, including lower costs, rapid response times, high stability, and suitability for mass production. These benefits have facilitated its widespread application in various fields, including medical diagnostics (Dong et al., 2025), food safety control (Ma et al., 2024), and environmental monitoring (Jia et al., 2025). In traditional ICTS, monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) are used for target recognition, while colloidal gold acts as a colorimetric signal label to indicate the presence of the analyte (Zeng et al., 2021). However, the detection performance of conventional ICTS is significantly compromised as sample matrices become increasingly complex. For instance, when analyzing milk and jam, components such as proteins, polysaccharides, and fats may induce non-specific binding with mAbs, colloidal gold particles, or nitrocellulose membrane (NC membrane), leading to false-positive results. Moreover, the coloration color of dark samples like soy sauce and red wine can interfere with ICTS detection outcomes, resulting in erroneous outcomes. In summary, these limitations compromise the reliability of ICTS for accurate detection of target analytes in complex matrix samples.

In response to these limitations, a multitude of studies have been conducted to enhance the performance of ICTS, which can be classified into three primary research directions. The most prevalent strategy involves replacing traditional colloidal gold with novel labeling materials that generate stronger signals, including quantum dots (QDs) (Zhao et al., 2025), latex microspheres (LMs) (Hu et al., 2025), time-resolved fluorescent microspheres (TRFM) (Lu et al., 2025), and upconversion nanoparticles (UCNPs) (Shen et al., 2024), among others. Another strategy focuses on introducing signal amplification systems, such as enzymatic amplification (Liang et al., 2025), nucleic acid amplification (Cui et al., 2024), streptavidin-biotin system (SABS) (Li et al., 2025) and clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats/CRISPR-associated proteins system (CRISPR-Cas) (Zuo et al., 2025). The third direction aims to improve resistance to matrix interference. Specifically, sample pretreatment techniques such as enzymatic hydrolysis (Ang et al., 2022), immunomagnetic magnetic beads (Mbs) capture (Chai & Bi, 2022), and microfluidic integrated enrichment (Wang et al., 2024) are employed to remove interfering components like proteins, pigments, and lipids from samples. Collectively, these studies can effectively enhance detection sensitivity, laying a solid foundation for the advancement of ICTS technology.

The mAbs, frequently employed as recognition elements in ICTS, present several limitations including high production costs, inadequate stability, challenges in recognizing complex antigens, and significant batch-to-batch variability. Consequently, alternative recognition elements have been developed, such as polyclonal antibodies (pAbs) (Chen et al., 2025), genetically engineered antibodies (GEAbs) (Yuan et al., 2025) and nanobodies (Nbs) (Guo et al., 2024). Notably, Nbs are the minimal functional antigen-binding fragments derived from immunizing camelids and are approximately 15‐17 kDa in size, which are significantly smaller than traditional antibodies (Muyldermans, 2021). Their compact structure and high content of disulfide bonds confer several advantages such as high affinity, enhanced stability, rapid response time, reduced production costs, and ease of modification (Jin et al., 2023). Owing to these favorable properties, Nbs have demonstrated superior performance over other recognition elements, highlighting their considerable potential for practical applications (Bi et al., 2024). Nb-based ICTS exhibits high sensitivity and remarkable tolerance to matrix interferences in various samples (Pang et al., 2025). They have been successfully utilized in virus detection (Qin et al., 2022), food safety assessment (Pang et al., 2025), environmental monitoring (Zhou et al., 2022), and point-of-care testing (POCT) (Kamel et al., 2024).

In summary, the detection performance of ICTS is influenced by multiple factors, and individual optimization strategies often yield only marginal improvements in sensitivity, particularly when addressing with complex sample matrices. Building on the advancements discussed, this study aimed to construct a combined-optimization ICTS by integrating the strengths of multiple strategies. In this research, a multifunctional signal probe integrating “enrichment-recognition-amplification” capabilities was developed by leveraging the affinity between streptavidin-modified magnetic beads and biotinylated nanobodies. Within this system, the magnetic beads enable target enrichment, while the nanobodies provide specific antigen recognition, and the biotin-streptavidin system facilitates signal amplification. The multifunctional signal probe-based ICTS achieved accurate and stable detection of AFB1 in complex matrix samples including dark-colored and high-fat food samples, without the need for extensive pretreatment. This approach provides a novel and practical solution for rapid screening of trace toxins in grassroots application scenarios.

Materials and methods

Materials and apparatus

The streptavidin-modified magnetic beads (MB-SA) particles (300 nm) were purchased from Suzhou Beaver Biomedical Engineering Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). Nitrocellulose membranes (Poll 90, Poll 120, CN140‐5), absorbent pads, sample pads, and PVC backing plates were purchased from Shanghai Jenin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The aflatoxin B1 (AFB1) standard solution was acquired from Beijing Meizheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The anti-AFB1 nanobody G8 is stored in our laboratory, and G8-Bio was prepared there as well. All chemical reagents used in the experiments were of analytical grade. Ultrapure water (Milli-Q, Millipore, 18.25 MΩ·cm) was used throughout the experiments.

The test strips were fabricated using a strip cutter (CM3030) and an integrated machine for membrane dispensing and reagent spraying (XYZ3020; Shanghai Jenin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). These strips were then dried in a vacuum drying oven (BZF-50; Shanghai Boxun Medical & Bio-Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The MB-SA-G8-Bio signal probe was magnetically separated using a Plexiglas magnetic rack (Haimen Borys Experimental Equipment Operation Department, Jiangsu, China). The preparation and identification of the nanobody G8 were carried out using a constant-temperature incubator shaker (ZWY-240; Shanghai Zhicheng Analytical Instrument Manufacturing Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), an electrophoresis instrument (DYY-6D; Beijing Liuyi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), and a microplate reader (EPOCH; BioTek Instruments, Inc., Vermont, USA).

Preparation of the G8 and G8-Bio

The preparation of G8 and G8-Bio was performed as described in previous work (Li et al., 2024). Briefly, a single colony of transformed E. coli BL21(DE3) strain harboring the pET25b-G8 plasmid was inoculated into Luria-Bertani (LB) liquid medium containing ampicillin. After incubation at 37 °C and 200 rpm for 12 h, 2 % (v/v) of the above culture medium was transferred to another LB liquid medium containing ampicillin, and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) value reaches 0.5–0.8. Protein expression was subsequently induced by addition of isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). After induction, the bacterial culture was harvested by centrifugation at 4 °C and 10,062×g for 10 min. The pellet was then weighed (wet weight) and resuspended for ultrasonic disruption. The collected supernatant was then purified using a nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) affinity column. The column was washed stepwise with imidazole buffers at concentrations of 20, 50, 100, 200, and 500 mmol·L−1. The purified nanobody G8 was then dialyzed in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) at a concentration of 0.1 mol·L−1 at 4 °C for a duration of 24 h before being stored at −20 °C.

For the preparation of G8-Bio, the prepared nanobody G8 was reacted with biotin N-hydroxysuccinimide ester (NHS-Biotin) dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The mixture was then incubated at room temperature with gentle agitation for 30 min. After incubation, the resulting solution was then dialyzed in 0.1 mol·L−1 PBS buffer at 4 °C for 24 h to remove unreacted NHS-Biotin and obtain the biotinylated nanobody G8-Bio.

Preparation of the MB-SA-G8-Bio probe

The signal probes MB-SA-G8-Bio were prepared via bioconjugation through the high affinity between biotin and streptavidin. Briefly, an appropriate amount of MB-SA was vortexed to disperse and transferred to a centrifuge tube for magnetic separation. After separation, the supernatant was discarded. Next, 100 μL of 0.01 mol·L−1 phosphate-buffered saline with Tween-20 (PBST, pH 7.4) was added to resuspend the MB-SA and separated again. This washing procedure was repeated three times. After resuspension with the MB-SA in 100 μL of ultrapure water, an appropriate amount of G8-Bio was added and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. Subsequently, the mixture was subjected to magnetic separation and the supernatant was discarded. Finally, 50 μL of PBST (0.01 mol·L−1, pH 7.4) was added, and the mixture was gently vortexed to mix. The prepared probes were stored at 4 °C.

Assembly of the test strip

Owing to the pre-mixing of the signal probes with the sample solution, a conjugate pad was deemed unnecessary. Briefly, the test strip consists of a support pad, a NC membrane, a sample pad, and an absorbent pad. First, the sample pad was cut into 2.5 cm-wide strips and soaked in blocking buffer for 30 min to block non-specific binding sites. The soaked pads were then dried at 37 °C in a vacuum oven. The absorbent pad was also cut into 2.5 cm-wide strips and stored in a sealed bag. Next, the competitive antigens AFB1-BSA and Bio-BSA were sprayed onto the NC membrane at a rate of 0.5 μL/cm to form the test line (T-line) and control line (C-line), respectively. The sprayed membrane was then dried in a vacuum oven at 37 °C overnight. The NC membrane, sample pad, and absorbent pad were sequentially attached to a polyvinyl chloride (PVC) support pad, with a 2–3 mm overlap between each pad. The assembled strip was then cut into 3 mm-wide strips and sealed in desiccant-containing bags for storage. Notably, the NC membrane is highly hygroscopic, which can affects the sensitivity of the test strips. Therefore, the relative humidity should be kept below 30 % during both preparation and storage of the test strips.

Detection performance evaluation

Construction of standard curve

Under optimal conditions, AFB1 standard solutions with different final concentrations (0, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 5, 10, 50, 100, and 200 ng/mL) were prepared by serial dilution with a buffer solution. Next, 50 μL of each standard solution was incubated with the MB-SA-G8-Bio probes for 1 min. After incubation, the mixture was placed on a magnetic stand for magnetic separation. The precipitate was then resuspended in 50 μL of buffer solution and applied dropwise to the sample pad of the test strip, and the optical density (ODT) values of the T-line were measured using a chromatography reader. Each sample was tested in triplicate to ensure result reliability. AFB1 was quantified by constructing a standard curve based on the relationship between AFB1 concentrations and corresponding ODT values. The IC50 and LOD of the test strip were calculated from the standard curve.

Specificity analysis

To verify the specificity of the constructed test strips for the detection of AFB1, five common mycotoxins in food (DON, ZEN, T-2, OTA, AFM1) were selected for specificity analysis, while a blank sample was used as a reference. The concentration of AFB1 solution was 50 ng/mL, while the other mycotoxins were tested at a higher concentration of 100 ng/mL to rigorously assess cross-reactivity, and the blank sample was PBST solution. Each analyte was processed identically: after incubation with the MB-SA-G8-Bio probes and magnetic separation, the resuspended mixture was applied to the test strip for analysis. The colors of the T and C lines were observed, and the ODT values were recorded using a chromatography reader.

Actual sample detection

To further evaluate the detection performance of the test strip, corn and dark soy sauce were selected as representative samples. The corn was ground into powder, and all of the powder was passed through a 20-mesh sieve. A 5 g portion of the sieved corn was weighed into a centrifuge tube, and 1 mL of AFB1 standard solution was spiked into each sample. After drying at room temperature, 10 mL of extraction reagent (methanol/water, 7:3, v/v) was added. The mixtures were placed on an oscillator and shaken for 30 min, then centrifuged at 5031×g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm filter and diluted 10-fold with buffer solution to achieve final AFB1 concentrations of 0, 0.1, 1 and 10 ng/mL. For dark soy sauce, sample pretreatment was conducted according to the Chinese national standard “GB 5009.22-2016”. Specifically, 5 g of dark soy sauce was weighed into a centrifuge tube and mixed with 20 mL of 70 % methanol solution. The mixture was shaken on a shaker for 20 min, then centrifuged at 6037×g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. AFB1 standard solutions of different concentrations were added to the supernatant, and the mixture was diluted with buffer solution to achieve final concentrations of 0, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 5, 10, 50, 100, and 200 ng/mL. All prepared samples were then analyzed using the constructed test strip to evaluate its sensitivity and accuracy.

For the detection, 50 μL of the sample solution was mixed with 50 μL of the MB-SA-G8-Bio probe solution and incubated for 1 min at room temperature. The mixture was then isolated via magnetic separation and resuspended in PBST solution for subsequent detection. The ODT values of the test strips were recorded using a chromatography reader. Spiked recovery experiments were performed using the corn samples, while a standard curve was established based on the detection results of dark soy sauce samples. These results were compared with the results of AFB1 standard solution to evaluate the practical application of the constructed test strips.

Results and discussion

Detection principle of the multifunctional signal probe-based ICTS

In this study, a multifunctional signal probe-based ICTS was developed for the detection of AFB1 in corn and dark soy sauce. Its detection principle can be decomposed into three processes (Fig. 1). First, the signal probe MB-SA-G8-Bio was constructed through the high-affinity interaction between biotin and streptavidin, enabling the functional assembly of magnetic carriers and recognition elements (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, the magnetic probes were mixed with the sample solution and incubated. Driven by the specific antigen-antibody binding between AFB1 and the nanobody G8 on the probes, AFB1 in the sample was captured by the probes and thus enriched onto the probe surface. An external magnetic field is applied to the incubated mixture, leveraging the magnetic properties of the probe to separate the AFB1-enriched signal probes from the sample matrix. After discarding the supernatant, the collected complexes were resuspended in PBST solution, yielding a purified sample solution substantially free of matrix interference (Fig. 1B). Finally, the resuspended mixture was added to sample pad and migrated along the NC membrane via capillary action. For the ICTS, the C-line displays a yellow band, confirming proper strip functionality and avoid false negatives. The T-line indicates the presence of AFB1. In negative samples, unoccupied G8 sites on the probe bind to the immobilized AFB1-BSA at the T-line, resulting in a yellow band. In positive samples, AFB1 competes with AFB1-BSA for binding to G8, leading to decreased color intensity or the disappearance of the T-line as the AFB1 concentration increases (Fig. 1C). This ICTS platform effectively reduces matrix interference in samples through magnetic enrichment and competitive immunoassay detection, thereby ensuring the accuracy of AFB1 detection and laying a foundation for subsequent practical applications.

Characterizations of MB-SA and MB-SA-G8-bio probe

Probe preparation is critical to experimental success of this study. Therefore, the probes were characterized to confirm their successful synthesis before detection. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Fig. 3A–C) revealed that the pristine MB-SA exhibited uniform spherical microspheres of approximately 300 nm in diameter with smooth surfaces and no aggregation, providing a sufficient surface area for subsequent G8-Bio conjugation (Haukanes & Kvam, 1993). The successful conjugation was directly confirmed by ultraviolet-visible (UV–vis) spectroscopy (Fig. 3D). Free G8-Bio exhibited a characteristic absorption peak at 280 nm, consistent with the typical spectral feature of nanobodies, while MB-SA displayed a peak at 230 nm. The conjugated MB-SA-G8-Bio probe showed a double peak at 230 nm and 280 nm, indicating the coexistence of MB-SA and G8-Bio. Surface charge analysis (Fig. 3E) further supported conjugation: The zeta potential of pristine MB-SA was −11.66 mV; after conjugation with negatively charged G8-Bio, the surface charge decreased to −23.01 mV, confirming the integration of G8-Bio into the probe structure. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Fig. 3F) further validated the conjugation efficacy. The hydrodynamic diameter (Dh) of MB-SA was approximately 300 nm, while that of MB-SA-G8-Bio increased to about 350 nm. In summary, SEM, UV–vis, zeta potential, and DLS results collectively confirm the successful synthesis of MB-SA-G8-Bio probe. This probe exhibits excellent dispersibility and stable surface charge, meeting the technical requirements for subsequent AFB1 capture, magnetic separation, and signal output.

Optimization of detection parameters

To improve the detection sensitivity and colorimetric intensity of the ICTS, several detection conditions were optimized. These key parameters include the amount of MB-SA, the amount of G8-Bio, the amount of MB-SA-G8-Bio, the spotting volume, the concentration of AFB1-BSA on the T line, and the pore size of the NC membrane. The optimal conditions were selected by evaluating the color intensity of both T and C-lines—through visual inspection and quantitative measurement of the optical density using a strip reader—while also considering reagent consumption to achieve an optimal balance between high signal intensity and cost-effectiveness.

Optimization of signal probes

The signal probe is a key component for reporting reaction occurrence, and its preparation directly impact the stability and binding capacity of probes. Excessive MB-SA can increase background interference and even clog the NC membrane. Insufficient MB-SA reduces the effective antibody loading on magnetic beads, thereby decreasing detection sensitivity (Anfossi et al., 2013). As shown in Fig. 4A, the T-line colorimetric intensity increases with rising MB-SA volume but began to decline after reaching a peak. The intensity reached a maximum at 15 μL, thus identifying 15 μL as the optimal MB-SA volume. Similarly, the G8-Bio volume requires optimization. As shown in Fig. 4B, the T-line colorimetric intensity increased with rising G8-Bio volume but plateaued after 10 μL. Therefore, 10 μL was selected as the optimized G8-Bio volume.

Optimal detection conditions. Addition amount of (A) MB-SA, (B) G8-Bio, (C) MB-SA-G8-Bio. (D) Striping volume. (E) Concentration of AFB1-BSA. (F) Pore size of NC membrane.

Additionally, based on the detection principle of this experiment, the amount of signal probe MB-SA-G8-Bio must be optimized to balance detection performance and cost-effectiveness (Meng et al., 2017). As shown in Fig. 4C, when the added volume of the MB-SA-G8-Bio probe was 2 μL or greater, the T-line colorimetric intensity stabilized, while the C-line intensity increased. However, excessive probe addition resulted in reduced test strip sensitivity, which could potentially result in false-negative outcomes and compromising the accuracy of AFB1 detection. Therefore, 2.0 μL was determined to be the optimal quantity for the MB-SA-G8-Bio probe.

Optimization of striping volume

In competitive immunoassays, the dispensing volume of capture reagents on the T line and C line is a critical parameter that determines assay performance, result reliability, and reproducibility, as it directly influences the intensity of visible bands on the NC membrane (Meng et al., 2017). For the T line, excessive volume may cause overly intense bands, reducing assay sensitivity and increasing reagent costs; insufficient volume may result in blurred bands, impairing the differentiation between positive and negative samples. For the C line, insufficient dispensing volume causes the control band to be absent or weakened, rendering the test result invalid regardless of the T-line status. Conversely, excessive dispensing volume for the C line may induce signal interference with the adjacent T line due to diffusion effects. As shown in Fig. 4D, increasing the dispensing volume of reagents for both the T and C-lines expanded the color development region, while the T-line colorimetric intensity remained largely unchanged. To ensure strong C-line visibility without compromising T-line interpretation and to improve detection efficiency, a lower dispensing volume is preferred. The optimal dispensing volume was ultimately determined to be 0.5 μL/cm. This volume achieves an optimal balance between colorimetric intensity and detection accuracy while also reducing reagent costs to a certain extent.

Optimization of AFB1-BSA volume

In immunoassays, antigen-antibody interaction is a critical factor affecting detection efficacy. In competitive assays, regulating the competitive equilibrium between AFB1 in the sample and the competitive antigen AFB1-BSA immobilized on the T-line ensures the test strip meets standards for detection sensitivity, specificity, linear range, and stability. This balance prevents false-positive or false-negative outcomes, thereby guaranteeing the effectiveness of the detection method. Therefore, it is necessary to optimize the concentration of AFB1-BSA on the T-line to prevent competitive imbalance caused by excessive or insufficient antigen coating. As shown in Fig. 4E, the colorimetric intensity of the T-line increased with increasing concentrations of AFB1-BSA but plateaued at 50 μg/mL. Considering both experimental cost and effectiveness, 50 μg/mL was selected as the optimal AFB1-BSA concentration.

Optimization of NC membrane

In magnetic bead-based immunochromatographic assays for AFB1 detection, the NC membrane pore size affects the chromatographic flow rate and the efficiency of probe binding at the T-line and C-line, thereby critically determining overall detection performance. Excessively large pores cause overly rapid migration, insufficient binding, and weak signals. Conversely, excessively small pores slow migration and lead to probe aggregation (Borse & Srivastava, 2019). As illustrated in Fig. 4F, the Polly 90 membrane generated clear T and C lines of moderate intensity, featuring a clean background and minimal membrane blocking. In contrast, the Polly 120 membrane showed weak color development on the T and C lines, with noticeable blocking above the C-line leading to an unclear background.The CN140–5 membrane showed almost no color development on the T and C lines, with severe blocking below the T line and slow chromatographic flow. Therefore, Polly 90 was selected as the optimal NC membrane.

Detection performance evaluation

Standard curve

To quantify the relationship between AFB1 concentration and output signal for assessing the accuracy of ICTS detection, a competitive inhibition standard curve was constructed. The curve plots the logarithmic concentration of AFB1 (log [AFB1], x-axis) against the binding rate (B/B0 × 100 %, y-axis), where B is the T-line absorbance (OD450) of test samples and B0 is the OD450 of the AFB1-free negative control. As shown in Fig. 5A and Fig. 5B, the colorimetric intensity of T-line progressively weakened and eventually disappeared with increasing AFB1 concentration. This phenomenon indicates that higher concentrations of AFB1 exhibit stronger competition for the G8 binding site on MB-SA-G8-Bio, consistent with the detection principle described in section 3.1. The color intensity of the T line exhibited an inverse correlation with AFB1 concentration, consistent with a typical competitive inhibition mechanism. Specificity analysis

To validate the accuracy of the constructed ICTS, five common mycotoxins in grains—DON, ZEN, T-2, OTA, and AFM1—were selected as potential interferents to test specificity. As shown in Fig. 5C, the T line colorimetric intensities for samples containing the interfering mycotoxins were comparable to that of the blank control (PBST solution), indicating no significant cross-reactivity. In contrast, the T line intensity for AFB1 showed a noticeable color change compared to the blank, with a strong and distinct signal observed at the higher concentration of 50 ng/mL. These results demonstrate that the multifunctional signal probe-based ICTS has no cross-reactivity with the selected mycotoxins, confirming its excellent specificity for AFB1 detection.

Actual sample detection

To evaluate the accuracy and practical applicability of ICTS for detecting AFB1 in complex matrices, two sets of experiments were conducted. Corn, the most representative sample for AFB1 detection was selected for spiked recovery tests. As shown in Table 1, the constructed ICTS achieved spiked recovery rates of 98 %–102 % in corn samples, which is comparable to the recovery rates of HPLC (Wang et al., 2022). Concurrently, the coefficient of variation (CV) ranged from 1.4 % to 6.86 %, indicating minimal inter-experiment variation and reliable reproducibility. This stability is attributed to the magnetic enrichment property of the MB-SA-G8-Bio probe (Section 3.1), which minimizes matrix interference during target capture.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a multifunctional signal probe-based ICTS was successfully developed for the detection of AFB1 in corn and dark soy sauce. In this study, the MB-SA-G8-Bio signal probes were synthesized through the high-affinity biotin-streptavidin interaction. The prepared signal probes simultaneously perform three functions: target enrichment, specific recognition and signal amplification, effectively identified target substances in complex matrices. The method demonstrated excellent analytical performance, with recovery rates of 98 %–102 % and coefficients of variation of 1.4 %–6.86 % in spiked corn samples. When applied to dark soy sauce, the calculated IC10 value for AFB1 was 0.069 ng/mL, which was basically the same as that of the standard in PBST buffer. These results demonstrate that the constructed test strip effectively mitigates matrix interference, providing a rapid, cost-efficient approach for screening AFB1 in complex food matrices. In addition, this method can be readily extended to other mycotoxins or contaminants in a wide range of food matrices, providing significant potential for practical applications in food safety control.
