Right ventriculography to guide left bundle branch pacing in pacing-induced cardiomyopathy: a novel case report
Authors: Mansimran Singh Dulay, Raheel Ahmed, Nick Child, Ahran Arnold, Mark Tanner, Fabian Barbieri, Linh Ngo, Binaya Basyal, Ralph Mark Louis Neijenhuis
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is emerging evidence for the potential utility of left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP), as an alternative to conventional cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT). The utility of right ventriculography by way of power injector to facilitate lead placement has not yet been reported in the literature.Case summaryA 79-year-old female, with a background of poorly rate-controlled atrial fibrillation, presented with worsening dyspnoea. She had recently undergone single-chamber pacemaker insertion prior to an atrioventricular nodal (AVN) ablation, owing to failure in achieving successful CRT coronary sinus lead placement. She had clinical evidence of volume overload, and her electrocardiogram demonstrated right ventricular pacing. Echocardiography demonstrated left ventricular (LV) impairment, with an ejection fraction (EF) of 35%, and severe functional mitral regurgitation (MR). Her diagnosis was overall consistent with pacing-induced cardiomyopathy (PIC). In this patient, the use of right ventriculography, using power-injector-delivered contrast, successfully facilitated placement of an LBBAP lead, with confirmation of good threshold and sensing parameters. Following an upgrade to conduction system pacing, the patient recovered well. On recent follow-up, repeat echocardiography (24 months post initial presentation) demonstrated improved LV function (EF 45% from 35%) and only mild-to-moderate MR.DiscussionIn conclusion, we demonstrate the utility of right ventriculography to facilitate placement of an LBBAP lead, successfully treating a patient who developed PIC from chronic right ventricular pacing following AVN ablation.Keywords: Atrioventricular node ablation, Conduction system pacing, His-bundle pacing, Left bundle branch area pacing, Ventriculography, Case report

Learning points

Introduction

Conduction system pacing (CSP) is an emerging area of interest. It broadly refers to His-bundle pacing (HBP), or left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP).1 At present, the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines acknowledge the growing role for CSP, as evidence grows to support its implementation in scenarios in which conventional dual-chamber and biventricular pacing would traditionally be utilized.1 Physiologically, it is likely that CSP confers advantage by helping to ameliorate the deleterious impact of mechanical dyssynchrony induced by right ventricular (RV) pacing.1,2 In the context of new-onset heart failure following such an eventuality, the term ‘pacing-induced cardiomyopathy’ (PIC) is applied.2

We present a novel case in which a patient, who developed PIC following atrioventricular nodal (AVN) ablation preceded by single-chamber pacing lead insertion, was subsequently treated using CSP facilitated by power-injector-contrast-delivered right ventriculography.

Case presentation

A 79-year-old female presented with progressive dyspnoea and reduction in exercise tolerance. The patient had a background of recurrent persistent atrial fibrillation (AF) for over 4 years, with symptoms and high ventricular rates refractory to rate- and rhythm-controlling pharmacotherapies. She had additionally undergone two failed electrical cardioversions, neither of which had yielded prolonged time in sinus rhythm (<1 week). Her other medical history included hypothyroidism and hypertension.

Due to the amount of time she had spent in persistent AF, the presence of severe left atrial dilatation on previous echocardiography, coupled with her age and lack of prolonged time maintaining sinus rhythm post electrical cardioversions, a decision was made against AF catheter ablation. In keeping with ESC guidelines, a ‘pace and ablate’ strategy was proposed.3 Prior to the ablation procedure, an attempt at cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) pacemaker was made (ESC indication IIa1,4), but there were limited coronary sinus targets, and left ventricular (LV) thresholds were unacceptably high. Accordingly, a single RV chamber pacing system was implanted.

She underwent successful AVN ablation 6 months after initial permanent pacemaker implantation. In the ensuing weeks post ablation, however, she developed worsening shortness of breath with features of fluid overload. Her initial LV systolic function was mild-to-moderately reduced [LV ejection fraction (LVEF) 40–45%] with mild mitral regurgitation (MR). Her electrocardiogram (ECG) showed AF with rapid ventricular response (rate ∼120 b.p.m.) and a narrow QRS complex (92 ms). The patient was subsequently initiated on Bisoprolol 10 mg once daily (o.d.), Olmesartan 20 mg o.d., and Furosemide 40 mg o.d. for offloading. Repeat ECG was consistent with AF with RV pacing. Pacemaker interrogation confirmed normal device function. Her blood tests demonstrated deteriorating renal function, with an estimated glomerular filtration rate of 30 down from a rate of 49 pre ablation, and chest X-ray revealed pulmonary oedema with bilateral pleural effusions. Repeat echocardiography demonstrated severe LV systolic impairment (LVEF of 35%) in the context of severe functional MR (Supplementary material online, Video S1).

The features of new-onset heart failure in the context of recent RV pacing, deteriorating LV systolic function, and severe functional MR were consistent with a diagnosis of PIC.2,5 In view of the prior difficulties in achieving CRT through a conventional coronary sinus approach, a plan was devised to mitigate the harmful effects of RV pacing–mediated dyssynchrony by upgrading to CSP. Left bundle branch area pacing is a CSP modality and has a Heart Rhythm Society Class IIa recommendation in those patients who develop heart failure symptoms in the context of high-burden RV pacing.6

The procedure was performed a total of 10 months from baseline presentation, using a left axillary approach. Through a 9 Fr haemostatic sheath, a 5 Fr pigtail catheter (Cordis, FL, USA) was subsequently placed into the RV. Ventriculography was achieved with 25 mL of contrast delivered through a power injector at a rate of 15 mL/s in both left anterior oblique (Figure 1 and Supplementary material online, Video S2) and right anterior oblique positions (Figure 2A and Supplementary material online, Video S3). Conduction system pacing was performed using the SelectSecure 3830 pacing lead (4.1 Fr, 69 cm; Medtronic, MN, USA) delivered via a fixed curve C315-HIS sheath (Medtronic). Left bundle branch area pacing was attempted by advancing the sheath and lead to a position perpendicular to the RV basal septum 1.5–2 cm beyond the tricuspid valve annulus towards the RV apex, guided by the ventriculograms. The initial position on the RV septum is demonstrated in Figures 1 and 2B. Unipolar pacing confirmed a good starting position for lead fixation: notched QS complex (‘W’) in Lead V1, positive Lead II, and augmented vector right and left (aVR/aVL) lead discordance.7 The lead was rotated and progressed towards the LV side of the septum (Figure 3) with confirmation of LBBAP (Figure 4), with good threshold and sensing.

The patient made an uncomplicated recovery and reported improvements in both exercise tolerance and dyspnoea. Her kidney function returned to baseline 6 weeks after the upgrade procedure. On follow-up, a 4-month post-upgrade (24 months post initial presentation overall) echocardiogram confirmed objective improvements with an LVEF of 45% compared with 35% pre upgrade. The severe functional MR had improved significantly (mild-to-moderate; Supplementary material online, Video S1).

Discussion

Pacing-induced cardiomyopathy occurs in ∼20% of patients with a high level of RV pacing.2 To reduce the risk of PIC post AVN ablation, current ESC guidelines allow either CRT or HBP to be considered.1 Cardiac resynchronization therapy has an established role in treating LV impairment secondary to high-burden RV pacing, as occurs in PIC—this has been demonstrated in randomized controlled trials (RCTs), such as the multicentre, blinded BUDAPEST-CRT trial, which highlighted the prognostic value of CRT devices in those patients with heart failure with reduced EF and high-burden RV pacing.8 In this reported case, however, CRT was not achievable.

Left bundle branch area pacing is a promising alternative to HBP for the delivery of CSP and is becoming increasingly established as a treatment for pacing-induced cardiomyopathy.2,4 While RCTs are awaited, observational study data are promising regarding the role of LBBAP in the treatment of PIC, with additional meta-analysis data even supporting the notion that it could supersede biventricular pacing in those patients with a traditional CRT indication.9,10 The success rate of LBBAP varies; however, a recent literature review estimates that it could be as low as 81%.5 Factors contributing to lower success rates include septal fibrosis, difficulty obtaining septal orientation, a lack of coaxial orientation of the pacing lead with regard to the sheath, and difficulties in ensuring that the sheath itself approaches the septum at a perpendicular angle.5,11

This patient case demonstrates a novel use for right ventriculography in guiding lead placement in LBBAP. Gu et al.7 reported the use of manual contrast injections to delineate the peri-tricuspid area in HBP, but power-injector-delivered ventriculography has not been reported in CSP. Recent literature has highlighted the use of right ventriculography as a tool to assist accurate lead positioning in other pacing procedures. In one study, Shimeno et al.12 used right ventriculography to successfully anchor RV septal leads in 98% of cases, with confirmation of successful lead placement using computed tomography imaging post procedure. This carries pertinence, given the often-false reassurance provided by fluoroscopic images and ECG data regarding true RV septal lead positioning.13 Moreover, a recent study involving 108 patients undergoing leadless pacing demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in successful RV mid-septal lead deployment with right ventriculography.14

In this case, we further demonstrate the benefit of total RV opacification by way of power-injector-delivered contrast injection; this is something that is not readily achievable with a manual contrast injection. In heart failure, RV dimensions and orientations are commonly distorted. Power-injector-delivered ventriculography might assist in orienting the operator and in helping to identify the starting point in the basal septum by delineating the tricuspid valve annulus summit and hence proximal His-bundle area. Subsequent deployment of the lead in an orientation truly perpendicular to the septum is then readily achievable with such direct contrast visualization. For example, in this patient, the RV ‘apical’ lead is some distance from the true apex (Figure 2A and Supplementary material online, Video S3), which could mislead the operator in choosing a basal septal position.

Power-injection-mediated right ventriculography is technically straightforward, readily achievable in most modern cardiac catheter laboratories, and carries relatively minimal procedural risks in the right-sided venous system. If biplane projection is available, the amount of contrast required would be only 25 mL to perform ventriculography—i.e. similar to (if not less than) the volumes of contrast administered in CRT cases. Nonetheless, the utility of the contrast itself could be deemed a procedural disadvantage when using right ventriculography, and appropriate post-procedural renal function monitoring should be considered.

Conclusion

In summary, we have demonstrated how right ventriculography can assist with successful LBBAP, in the context of treating PIC post AVN ablation.

Right ventriculography is simple, quick, and readily achievable in all cardiac catheter laboratories. It may contribute to the achievement of successful LBBAP and potentially reduce total procedure and fluoroscopic times.
