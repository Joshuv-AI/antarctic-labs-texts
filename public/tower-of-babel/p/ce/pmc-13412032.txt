Awareness and knowledge of vitamin D deficiency among Palestinian adults recruited online: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hamsa Saad, Beesan Sarahna, Haneen A Melhem, Nardeen Hamad, Hala H Abdo, Dua R Brash, Hadil Salahiddin, Elaf Abubaker, Sara Jaber, Fatima Attar, Salahaldeen Deeb, Alhareth M Amro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVitamin D deficiency remains a major public health concern in the Middle East and North Africa despite abundant sunlight. The public’s understanding of vitamin D sources, risk factors, and preventive practices may influence recognition and prevention of deficiency, yet data from Palestine remain limited. This study assessed awareness and knowledge of vitamin D deficiency among Palestinian adults recruited online and examined whether knowledge differed according to sociodemographic characteristics and vitamin D-related experiences.MethodsAn analytical online cross-sectional study was conducted among Palestinian adults using an adapted Arabic self-administered questionnaire distributed through social media between November and December 2025. The instrument assessed sociodemographic characteristics, vitamin D-related experiences, and knowledge across benefits, sources/determinants, and toxicity domains. Knowledge scores ranged from 0 to 14, and scores greater than 7 were classified as adequate knowledge, consistent with the source instrument. Descriptive statistics, bivariate analyses, and multivariable logistic regression were performed using SPSS version 25.ResultsA total of 756 respondents were included (mean age 27.5 ± 9.9 years; 79.1% female). The mean knowledge score was 10.21 ± 2.25, and 88.8% of participants were classified as having adequate knowledge. Correct responses were highest for skeletal benefits and the role of sun exposure in vitamin D production. Lower correct response rates were observed for items related to vitamin D toxicity, vegetarian-related risk, and the effect of darker skin pigmentation on deficiency risk. Higher mean knowledge scores were observed among males, single participants, participants without children, and those who had undergone vitamin D testing or had taken supplements. However, no independent predictors of adequate knowledge were identified in the adjusted logistic regression model.ConclusionsPalestinian adults in this online non-probability sample demonstrated a generally high level of vitamin D knowledge, particularly regarding bone health and sun exposure. However, important gaps persisted in specific domains, especially toxicity and less commonly recognized risk factors. Because the sample was recruited online and was disproportionately female and highly educated, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive of this sampled group rather than representative of all Palestinian adults.Keywords: Vitamin D deficiency, Awareness, Knowledge, Palestinian adults, Cross-sectional study, Public health, Health education

Introduction

Vitamin D is a fat-soluble secosteroid essential for calcium and phosphorus homeostasis, skeletal mineralization, and musculoskeletal health. It is derived from cutaneous synthesis after ultraviolet B exposure and from dietary intake, then undergoes sequential hepatic and renal hydroxylation to form its biologically active metabolite. Vitamin D deficiency is a well-established cause of rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults and remains a significant global public health concern [1, 2].

Despite abundant sunlight, vitamin D deficiency remains highly prevalent across the Middle East and North Africa (MENA), reflecting a multifactorial interplay of limited sun exposure, clothing practices, indoor lifestyles, inadequate dietary intake, obesity, and skin pigmentation [3]. Recent regional evidence continues to describe a substantial burden of low vitamin D status in MENA populations, highlighting a persistent gap between environmental availability of sunlight and vitamin D sufficiency [4]. Although vitamin D deficiency has been widely documented across the MENA region, Palestine-specific population-level data remain relatively limited. Available local evidence and the high burden reported in neighboring Middle Eastern populations suggest that vitamin D deficiency is a relevant public health concern in Palestine, highlighting the need to understand community awareness and knowledge in this setting [5].

Although regional studies on vitamin D awareness generally show good recognition of sunlight exposure and skeletal benefits, knowledge is often weaker for dietary sources, toxicity, risk groups, and appropriate preventive practices [6]. This distinction is important because awareness alone does not necessarily translate into safe supplementation, appropriate testing, or sustained preventive behavior. Previous research on vitamin D-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices has shown that public knowledge may be linked to preventive behaviors such as supplementation and health-seeking practices, although these relationships are context-dependent and cannot be assumed from knowledge data alone [7, 8]. Therefore, studying vitamin D knowledge in Palestine is important not only for describing awareness, but also for identifying specific misconceptions that future education and counseling strategies can target.

Because prevention of vitamin D deficiency depends not only on clinical detection and supplementation but also on public understanding of vitamin D sources, risk factors, and preventive practices, assessment of community awareness is of clear public health importance [5]. However, data specifically evaluating vitamin D-related knowledge among Palestinian adults remain limited. Therefore, this study aimed to assess awareness and knowledge of vitamin D deficiency among Palestinian adults and to examine whether knowledge levels differed according to sociodemographic characteristics and vitamin D-related experiences in an online cross-sectional sample.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This study employed an analytical online cross-sectional design to assess knowledge regarding vitamin D deficiency and to examine differences in knowledge according to sociodemographic and vitamin D-related experiential factors among Palestinian adults recruited through social media. Data were collected through an online survey administered over a predefined data-collection period between November and December of 2025, as documented in the study datasheet. The survey was implemented using a structured, self-administered questionnaire hosted on Google Forms. Distribution was conducted using a snowball sampling strategy through widely used social media platforms, including WhatsApp, Facebook, and Instagram, to facilitate broad geographic reach across Palestinian communities. While this approach enabled rapid dissemination and high response volume, it inherently limited researcher control over sampling and may have resulted in selection biases, including overrepresentation of younger, more educated, and digitally connected individuals.

Participants

Eligible participants were individuals residing in Palestine, aged between 18 and 65 years, who were able to read and understand Arabic and voluntarily consented to participate. Individuals outside this age range and non-residents were excluded. To minimize professional knowledge bias and to better reflect community-level awareness, healthcare professionals and students enrolled in health-related disciplines (including medicine, nursing, pharmacy, dentistry, laboratory sciences, and allied health fields) were excluded. Participation was entirely voluntary, and only fully completed questionnaires were included in the final analysis. The minimum required sample size was calculated before data collection using Cochran’s single-proportion formula. Because no prior Palestinian population-based estimate of adequate vitamin D knowledge was available, an expected proportion of 50% was used to generate the most conservative sample size estimate. The calculation assumed a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and alpha level of 0.05, yielding a minimum required sample size of 384 participants. After allowing for approximately 10% incomplete or invalid responses, the target minimum sample size was increased to 423 participants. The final analyzed sample of 756 respondents exceeded this requirement.

Questionnaire development and adaptation

The study questionnaire was adapted from a previously published peer-reviewed instrument developed and used to assess awareness of vitamin D deficiency among the general population in Syria, including knowledge of vitamin D benefits, sources, risk factors, and toxicity [6]. The original knowledge section contained 14 items, all of which were retained in the present study to preserve comparability with the source instrument. Minor contextual and linguistic modifications were made to improve clarity for Palestinian respondents without changing the intended meaning or scoring of the items. No knowledge items were added or removed.

The final questionnaire comprised two main components. The first collected sociodemographic and health-related information, including age, sex, marital status, educational attainment, employment status, income level, place of residence, chronic disease history, parental status, prior symptoms suggestive of vitamin D deficiency, previous vitamin D testing, self-reported test result, and previous supplement use. The second component assessed vitamin D knowledge across three domains: perceived benefits of vitamin D, sources and determinants of vitamin D status, and consequences of vitamin D toxicity. All items were closed-ended and designed for completion in an online format.

Translation and content validation

The questionnaire was administered in Arabic. A standardized forward–backward translation procedure was used to support linguistic accuracy and conceptual equivalence. The original English version was translated into Arabic by bilingual researchers familiar with medical and public health terminology. An independent bilingual translator then back-translated the Arabic version into English. Discrepancies between the original and back-translated versions were reviewed and resolved by consensus.

Face and content validity were assessed by an expert panel consisting of three researchers with experience in public health, epidemiology, nutrition, and questionnaire-based research. The panel reviewed the Arabic version for clarity, cultural appropriateness, conceptual equivalence, and suitability for the Palestinian context. The questionnaire was then pilot tested among two Arabic-speaking adults who met the eligibility criteria but were not included in the final analysis. Feedback from the pilot phase was used to refine wording and improve readability; no substantive changes were made to the scoring structure.

Scoring system and reliability assessment

Each knowledge item was scored dichotomously, with one point awarded for a correct response and zero points for an incorrect response. The total vitamin D knowledge score ranged from 0 to 14, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge. Consistent with the methodology of the original validated study [6], a cutoff of > 50% correct responses (score > 7) was used to classify participants as having “adequate” knowledge, while scores ≤ 7 were classified as “inadequate” knowledge. This threshold was retained to allow direct comparison with previously published regional studies, although it has not been formally validated specifically for the Palestinian population.

Internal consistency reliability of the 14-item knowledge scale was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha based on responses from the final analyzed sample collected during the November–December 2025 study period. The adapted Arabic scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.82. Item-level correct response rates were also examined to identify domains with lower knowledge and to assess variability across the knowledge items.

Data collection procedures

The survey link was disseminated electronically through social media channels and messaging applications. The introductory page of the questionnaire provided a concise explanation of the study objectives, eligibility criteria, estimated completion time, and assurances regarding anonymity and data confidentiality. Participants were required to provide electronic informed consent before accessing the questionnaire. No personally identifiable information was collected. Responses were automatically recorded and stored in a secure Google Forms database, accessible only to the research team.

To reduce duplicate participation, the Google Forms settings were configured to limit each Google account to one response. After data export, responses were screened for incomplete questionnaires, duplicate entries, identical response patterns, implausible timestamps, and out-of-range values. A total of 21 incomplete questionnaires were excluded before analysis. No duplicate responses were identified because the survey settings permitted only one submission per Google account. Because the survey was anonymous and no personally identifying information was collected by the research team, duplicate screening was limited to non-identifiable response characteristics and the form’s one-response restriction.

Data management and statistical analysis

Bivariate analyses were conducted to compare vitamin D knowledge outcomes across participant characteristics and vitamin D-related experiences. Independent-samples t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to compare mean knowledge scores across groups, while chi-square tests were used to compare the proportion of participants classified as having adequate knowledge. Because the dichotomized adequate-knowledge outcome was highly imbalanced, the continuous knowledge score was retained as an important complementary outcome in the bivariate analyses.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to examine factors independently associated with adequate vitamin D knowledge, defined as a score > 7. Candidate variables were identified based on bivariate screening at p < 0.20 and theoretical relevance. Because vitamin D testing and supplement use may occur as consequences rather than determinants of knowledge, the final adjusted model focused on sociodemographic predictors. All final candidate variables were entered simultaneously. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors/tolerance values, and model fit was evaluated using McFadden’s pseudo-R². Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Because of the high prevalence of adequate knowledge, a supplementary multivariable linear regression analysis using the continuous knowledge score was also conducted to assess whether predictors differed when knowledge was analyzed without dichotomization.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol, including the questionnaire, recruitment strategy, and informed consent process, was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Al-Quds University. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary, anonymity was strictly maintained, and participants retained the right to withdraw at any stage prior to submission of the questionnaire. No incentives were offered for participation, and data were used exclusively for research purposes.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 756 respondents were analyzed, with their sociodemographic and vitamin D-related characteristics presented in Table 1. The mean age was 27.5 ± 9.9 years. The largest age group was 20–29 years (435/756; 57.5%), whereas the smallest was ≥ 60 years (8/756; 1.1%). Females comprised 598/756 (79.1%). Slightly more participants lived in towns/villages (412/756; 54.5%) than in cities/urban areas (344/756; 45.5%). Most participants were single (488/756; 64.6%), followed by married (255/756; 33.7%); divorced (9/756; 1.2%) and widowed (4/756; 0.5%) participants were uncommon. Educational attainment was high: 618/756 (81.7%) reported a university degree (Bachelor’s or higher), while only 2/756 (0.3%) reported being illiterate. Students were the most common occupational group (332/756; 43.9%), and retired participants were the least common (5/756; 0.7%). Most participants reported no chronic illness (672/756; 88.9%). Approximately one-third reported having children (222/756; 29.4%). Vitamin D-related experiences were common: 431/756 (57.0%) reported vitamin D deficiency related symptoms, 452/756 (59.8%) reported having had a vitamin D blood test, and 474/756 (62.7%) reported ever taking vitamin D supplements. Among those tested, 365/452 (80.8%) reported vitamin D deficiency (Table 1).

Overall vitamin D knowledge distribution

Total knowledge scores ranged from 2 to 14 out of 14, with a mean score of 10.21 ± 2.25. The median score was 10, and 75% of respondents scored ≥ 9. Using the prespecified cutoff (score > 7/14), 672/756 (88.8%) met criteria for adequate knowledge and 84/756 (11.2%) for inadequate knowledge. Domain means (as defined by the questionnaire structure) were highest for perceived benefits (mean 4.39 ± 0.98 out of 5), followed by sources (mean 5.06 ± 1.59 out of 8) and toxicity (mean 0.76 ± 0.43 out of 1).

At the item level, correct response rates were highest for core statements on skeletal health and calcium metabolism, including “Vitamin D is used to treat bone diseases and rickets” (719/756; 95.1%), “Vitamin D is important in maintaining the balance of calcium and phosphorus in the body” (678/756; 89.7%), and “Vitamin D is important for maintaining healthy bones and teeth” (693/756; 91.7%). Correct response rates were also high for immune and muscle-related items: “Vitamin D helps strengthen the immune system” (643/756; 85.0%) and “Vitamin D helps strengthen muscles” (641/756; 84.8%). Knowledge of common sources and modifiers was similarly high: “Sun exposure stimulates the production of vitamin D in the skin” (683/756; 90.3%), “Vitamin D is found in animal meats, not in vegetables and fruits” (612/756; 81.0%), and “People living in cloudy areas are more prone to vitamin D deficiency” (571/756; 75.5%). Items with comparatively lower correct response rates included “Repeated exposure to sunlight does not lead to vitamin D toxicity” (418/756; 55.3%), “Vegetarians are more prone to vitamin D deficiency than non-vegetarians” (423/756; 56.0%), and “People with dark and brown skin are more prone to vitamin D deficiency than those with light skin” (476/756; 63.0%). Recognition of toxicity-related consequences was 574/756 (76.0%) for “One of the effects of severe vitamin D excess is high blood calcium levels (hypercalcemia)” (Table 2).

Bivariate associations between participant characteristics and vitamin D knowledge

Vitamin D knowledge scores were generally similar across sociodemographic subgroups, with mean scores clustering around 10 out of a possible 14 points and a high prevalence of adequate knowledge across categories. Statistically significant differences in mean knowledge scores were observed for sex, marital status, and parental status. Male participants had higher mean knowledge scores than females (10.59 ± 2.25 vs. 10.12 ± 2.24; p = 0.018), although the prevalence of adequate knowledge did not differ significantly (91.8% vs. 87.9%; p = 0.227). Mean knowledge scores showed a modest, non-significant decline with increasing age, and knowledge adequacy did not differ across age groups (p = 0.260).

Marital status was associated with differences in mean knowledge scores (p = 0.002), with higher scores among single participants compared with married individuals; however, knowledge adequacy did not differ significantly (p = 0.247). No significant differences in knowledge scores or adequacy were observed across education level, place of residence, income, or occupation (all p > 0.05). Participants without children demonstrated higher mean knowledge scores than those with children (10.40 ± 2.18 vs. 9.77 ± 2.36; p = 0.0004), although the difference in knowledge adequacy was not statistically significant (p = 0.057). Knowledge scores and adequacy were not associated with chronic illness status (both p > 0.05) (Table 3).

Vitamin D-related experiences and knowledge

Participants who reported vitamin D testing or supplementation had significantly higher mean knowledge scores, although these differences were not reflected in knowledge adequacy. Participants who had undergone vitamin D testing had higher mean scores than those who had not (10.40 ± 2.14 vs. 9.95 ± 2.36; p = 0.008), while adequate knowledge prevalence was similar between groups (p = 0.436). Among tested participants, knowledge scores did not differ according to self-reported test results (p = 0.615). Similarly, participants who reported vitamin D supplementation had higher mean knowledge scores than non-users (10.40 ± 2.18 vs. 9.91 ± 2.30; p = 0.005), although the difference in adequate knowledge was not statistically significant (p = 0.064). Reporting deficiency-related symptoms was not significantly associated with knowledge scores or adequacy (both p > 0.05) (Table 4).

Multivariable logistic regression examined predictors of adequate vitamin D knowledge (score > 7). No variables reached statistical significance at p < 0.05 in the adjusted model. Adjusted odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were as follows: age (per year older) OR 0.969 (0.939–1.002), p = 0.071; sex (Female vs. Male) OR 0.704 (0.383–1.296), p = 0.258; residence (Rural vs. City) OR 1.229 (0.712–2.121), p = 0.461; married vs. single OR 0.789 (0.470–1.325), p = 0.374; divorced/widowed vs. single OR 0.447 (0.084–2.390), p = 0.344; education (Univ vs. ≤High School) OR 1.348 (0.664–2.735), p = 0.410; education (Postgrad vs. ≤High School) OR 1.208 (0.403–3.619), p = 0.735; income (per level increase) OR 0.902 (0.677–1.202), p = 0.482; occupation (Employed vs. Student) OR 1.089 (0.619–1.918), p = 0.765; occupation (Unemployed vs. Student) OR 1.319 (0.697–2.499), p = 0.391; occupation (Other/Retired vs. Student) OR 1.964 (0.663–5.820), p = 0.224; and having children (Yes vs. No) OR 0.658 (0.381–1.137), p = 0.130 (Table 5).

The adjusted model showed modest explanatory capacity, with McFadden’s pseudo-R² of 0.11. Multicollinearity was not detected among predictors, with a maximum variance inflation factor of 4.55. These findings indicate that the included predictors were not affected by severe multicollinearity; however, the regression findings should still be interpreted cautiously because most respondents were classified as having adequate knowledge.

Discussion

This study assessed awareness and knowledge of vitamin D deficiency among Palestinian adults recruited through an online snowball sampling strategy. Overall, respondents demonstrated a high mean knowledge score, and most were classified as having adequate knowledge using the prespecified cutoff. However, this finding should be interpreted in light of the online non-probability sampling frame and the demographic composition of the sample, which was predominantly female, young, and highly educated. The results therefore provide useful descriptive evidence for this sampled group, but they should not be interpreted as representative of all Palestinian adults.

The relatively high level of vitamin D knowledge observed in this study likely reflects a combination of increased public health awareness and the characteristics of the sampled population. The widespread availability of health information through digital platforms and social media may have contributed to greater exposure to vitamin D-related topics, particularly in recent years. In addition, the high proportion of participants with university-level education in this sample may have further facilitated access to and understanding of health-related information, thereby contributing to the overall elevated knowledge scores.

The presence of specific knowledge gaps, particularly in areas related to toxicity, skin pigmentation, and dietary risk factors, suggests that current awareness may be uneven and focused primarily on more commonly emphasized aspects such as bone health and sun exposure. These findings likely reflect the content and emphasis of public health messaging, which often prioritizes general benefits while providing less attention to more complex or context-dependent determinants of vitamin D status.

Some questionnaire items should also be interpreted cautiously because they reflect simplified public-health wording rather than a comprehensive nutritional classification. For example, the item stating that vitamin D is found in animal meats and not in vegetables and fruits was retained from the source questionnaire to preserve methodological comparability, but it does not fully capture the complexity of vitamin D sources, including fortified foods, dietary patterns, and context-specific food practices [6]. Therefore, item-level findings should be interpreted as responses to the adapted knowledge questionnaire rather than as a complete assessment of nuanced nutritional knowledge.

The absence of significant independent predictors of adequate knowledge in the multivariable analysis may be explained by the relatively high and homogeneous distribution of knowledge across the sample, resulting in limited variability to detect meaningful associations. This pattern may indicate a ceiling effect, whereby a large proportion of participants achieve similarly high scores, thereby reducing the ability of sociodemographic variables to differentiate knowledge levels.

The high proportion of participants classified as having adequate knowledge also raises an important measurement consideration. Although the > 50% cutoff was retained to maintain comparability with the source questionnaire and related vitamin D awareness studies, this threshold has not been specifically validated for the Palestinian population [6, 8]. In the present sample, 88.8% of respondents exceeded the cutoff, which likely reduced the discriminatory capacity of the binary adequate/inadequate outcome. Therefore, the absence of statistically significant predictors in the logistic regression model should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that vitamin D knowledge is unrelated to participant characteristics. Rather, it may partly reflect a ceiling effect and the loss of information that occurs when a continuous knowledge score is dichotomized.

The findings of the present study are highly consistent with the Syrian study from which the questionnaire was adapted, particularly at the item level [6]. In the current Palestinian sample, 95.1% of participants correctly identified that vitamin D is used to treat bone diseases and rickets, compared with 91.4% in the Syrian study. Likewise, 89.7% versus 84.6% recognized its role in maintaining calcium and phosphate balance, and 90.3% versus 95.6% identified sun exposure as a major source of vitamin D. Notably, the pattern of weaker knowledge was also remarkably similar between the two studies. In the Palestinian sample, only 55.3% correctly answered the item related to repeated sun exposure and vitamin D toxicity, compared with 54.1% in the Syrian study, while awareness of vegetarian-related risk was 56.0% versus 54.9%, respectively. However, the Palestinian sample appeared to perform better in some less commonly recognized areas, including awareness of the association between darker skin pigmentation and higher deficiency risk (63.0% vs. 29.4%) and recognition of hypercalcemia as a consequence of vitamin D toxicity (76.0% vs. 62.3%). These close parallels support the reproducibility of the questionnaire across related regional populations and suggest that vitamin D knowledge in both settings is characterized by strong awareness of skeletal benefits and sun exposure, but weaker understanding of more nuanced determinants and toxicity-related concepts.

Comparison with the Saudi study also reveals a similar overall pattern, although direct comparison should be interpreted cautiously because that study was restricted to female university students and used a different threshold for good knowledge [7]. In the Saudi study, 49.5% of participants were classified as having good knowledge, 94.1% recognized sunlight as an important source of vitamin D, 43.1% identified reduced intake of vitamin D-rich foods as a cause of deficiency, and only 26.5% correctly identified the recommended daily intake. In addition, only 39.2% had ever examined their vitamin D status and 32.4% reported using vitamin D supplements. By contrast, in the present study, 88.8% of participants met the predefined threshold for adequate knowledge, while 59.8% reported prior vitamin D testing and 62.7% reported supplement use. Taken together, these findings suggest that although awareness of sunlight as a source is consistently high across regional studies, more detailed and practice-related aspects of vitamin D knowledge remains variable and appear to differ according to study population, educational profile, and measurement approach.

From a public health perspective, the present findings suggest that general messages on vitamin D have reached a substantial proportion of this sampled population. This interpretation is supported by the high overall knowledge score, the large proportion of participants classified as having adequate knowledge, and the relatively frequent history of prior vitamin D testing and supplement use. At the same time, the persistence of lower awareness in specific domains, particularly vitamin D toxicity, dietary vulnerability, and the influence of skin pigmentation, indicates that current communication strategies may be effective in conveying basic concepts while remaining less successful in addressing more detailed and behaviorally relevant aspects of prevention. These findings support the need to move beyond broad awareness campaigns toward more focused educational approaches that directly address persistent misconceptions and promote practical preventive behaviors [8].

The findings also have implications for clinical and community-based health promotion. Participants who reported previous vitamin D testing or supplement use had significantly higher mean knowledge scores, suggesting that contact with healthcare services may reinforce awareness and understanding. In this context, routine clinical encounters may provide an important opportunity to deliver more comprehensive counseling on vitamin D, including dietary sources, risk modifiers, appropriate supplementation, and the limitations of relying solely on sun exposure. This may be particularly relevant in settings where deficiency remains a commonly perceived health problem despite relatively good awareness, as reflected by the high proportion of self-reported deficiency among those who had previously undergone testing [9].

From a scientific perspective, this study adds region-specific evidence from Palestine to a still limited body of literature on vitamin D awareness in the Eastern Mediterranean region. The observed pattern is broadly consistent with findings from Syria and Saudi Arabia, where awareness was also strongest for skeletal benefits and sun exposure, while knowledge of dietary sources, toxicity, and other nuanced determinants remained less robust [10]. The consistency of this pattern across related populations suggests that these gaps are unlikely to be isolated findings and may instead reflect broader regional limitations in the way vitamin D is communicated and understood. Accordingly, the present study contributes not only descriptive data on awareness in a Palestinian sample, but also identifies priority areas for future educational, behavioral, and interventional research [11].

Beyond direct comparisons with vitamin D awareness studies, the present findings should be interpreted within the broader literature on nutrition knowledge, literacy, and health-related behavior. Recent nutrition research suggests that higher nutrition knowledge may be associated with healthier dietary behaviors and better adherence to recommended dietary patterns, while awareness-related constructs may also be linked to diet quality and selected health indicators [12, 13]. However, the present study assessed knowledge only and did not measure actual dietary intake, sun-exposure behavior, supplement appropriateness, preventive practices, or biochemical vitamin D status. Accordingly, the public health implications of these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than evidence that knowledge directly translated into behavioral or clinical outcomes in this sample.

These strengths should be interpreted alongside several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationship between knowledge and sociodemographic or vitamin D-related experiential factors. Second, the use of online snowball sampling introduces selection bias and limits representativeness. The sample was disproportionately female, young, highly educated, and digitally connected, which may have contributed to the high observed knowledge scores and restricts generalizability to the wider Palestinian adult population. Third, several subgroup categories were small, including older adults, retired participants, divorced or widowed respondents, and participants with low educational attainment, which may have reduced statistical stability in subgroup comparisons and regression estimates [6, 8].

Additional methodological limitations should also be acknowledged. The study relied on self-reported data, including previous vitamin D testing, supplement use, symptoms, and deficiency status, and is therefore subject to recall and reporting bias. The study also did not include biochemical assessment of serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D, which remains the standard clinical indicator for evaluating vitamin D status; therefore, the findings cannot be used to estimate the prevalence of vitamin D deficiency or determine whether knowledge corresponded to actual vitamin D status [5, 9]. Finally, the dichotomization of knowledge using a cutoff of more than 50% correct responses, although retained for comparability with the source instrument, may have reduced the discriminatory capacity of the outcome because most respondents were classified as having adequate knowledge. Some questionnaire items also used simplified wording and may not fully capture the complexity of vitamin D sources, dietary fortification, or clinical prevention practices.

Future research should employ more representative sampling strategies and, where feasible, incorporate biochemical assessment of vitamin D status to better determine how knowledge relates to actual deficiency risk in the wider Palestinian population. Longitudinal and interventional studies would also be valuable to evaluate whether improving awareness leads to meaningful changes in preventive behaviors and to test targeted educational approaches addressing the specific misconceptions identified in this study, particularly those related to toxicity, dietary risk factors, and other less well-recognized determinants of vitamin D status.

Conclusion

This online cross-sectional study found that Palestinian adults recruited through social media demonstrated a generally high level of vitamin D knowledge, particularly regarding skeletal benefits and the role of sun exposure. However, important gaps persisted in specific domains, including toxicity, dietary risk factors, skin pigmentation, and other less commonly recognized determinants of deficiency. Because the sample was non-probability based and disproportionately female, young, and highly educated, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive of this online sample rather than representative of all Palestinian adults. Future studies using more representative sampling and biochemical assessment of serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D are needed to clarify how vitamin D knowledge relates to preventive behavior and actual deficiency risk.
