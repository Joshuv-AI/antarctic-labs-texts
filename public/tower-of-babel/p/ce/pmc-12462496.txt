Elucidation of the structure and molecular mechanisms of the aspartate antiporter
Authors: Kei Nanatani, Lan Guan, Ryo Kanno, Takeshi Kawabata, Satoshi Watanabe, Satoshi Katsube, Parameswaran Hariharan, Masafumi Hidaka, Takashi Yamanaka, Keita Toda, Takashi Fujiki, Kota Kunii, Akari Miyamoto, Fumika Chiba, Satoshi Ogasawara, Takeshi Murata, Kenji Inaba, Kaoru Mitsuoka, Keietsu Abe, Masayuki Yamamoto, Seizo Koshiba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe transport of compounds across the cell membrane is essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis. Secondary exchange transporters mediate the movement of a wide range of substrates against their concentration gradients by harnessing the energy stored in electrochemical gradients. However, the molecular mechanism of substrate exchange by secondary transporters remains unclear. Here, we determined the structures of the aspartate exchanger AspT from Tetragenococcus halophilus using cryo-EM single-particle analysis and X-ray crystallography. We captured AspT in two distinct conformations: the apo outward-facing state and the substrate (l-Aspartate)-bound partially-open inward-facing intermediate state. AspT functions as a homodimer and comprises three domains: a dimerization domain, a substrate transport domain, and a soluble domain. Within each monomer, two hairpin loops in the transport domain form a single substrate-binding pocket. Upon L-aspartate binding, the transport domain carrying the substrate translocates toward the cytoplasmic side of the membrane, forming an outer barrier that blocks the periplasmic access to the binding pocket. These structural insights reveal that AspT mediates substrate translocation via an elevator-type alternating-access mechanism involving a stable partially-open inward-facing intermediate. By elucidating the mechanism of substrate exchange in secondary transporters, this study advances our understanding of membrane transport leading to translational applications in biotechnology.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, Membrane proteins

Introduction

To maintain homeostasis and generate energy, cells transport molecules and ions across the cell membrane against the diffusion gradient by means of active transport1. This transport is achieved via a variety of membrane proteins and mechanisms. Typically, this process involves one or more transmembrane proteins that change their configuration, causing the transporter’s substrate-binding site to expose from inside to outside or vice versa—known as the alternating access model1. Transporters that consume ATP to transport substrate molecules are called primary active transporters, whereas those that do not consume ATP but are driven instead by an electrochemical gradient of coupled ions are called secondary active transporters2. Both primary and secondary active transporters play essential roles in maintaining cellular function3,4. Of note, secondary transporters play important roles for cellular homeostasis, and their dysfunction can lead to the onset of various diseases, including diabetes, hyperuricemia, and cystinuria5.

The lactic acid bacterium Tetragenococcus halophilus plays several important roles in the brewing of soy sauce. It imparts acidity by producing lactic acid, improves the conditions for yeast growth by lowering the pH, brightens the color of the soy sauce by maintaining a slightly acidic reducing state, and produces a pleasant aroma. We found that T. halophilus strain D10 converts aspartate to alanine by an electrochemical gradient6. The intracellularly produced alanine is exported from the cell by electrogenic exchange with extracellular aspartate. This exchange is mediated by the aspartate:alanine exchange transporter (AspT)7. AspT belongs to the Aspartate:Alanine Exchanger (AAEx) Family (TC#2.A.81 in the Transporter Classification Database8,9. Transporters belonging to the AAEx family are widely conserved among microorganisms and transport a wide range of substrates. For instance, AspT transports aspartate and alanine7,10,11, while others transport succinate12 and glutamic acid13. In T. halophilus, a proton-motive force (PMF) is generated through a coupled reaction involving l-aspartate decarboxylation catalyzed by l-aspartate-4-decarboxylase (EC 4.1.1.12) and an electrogenic aspartate1:alanine0 exchange reaction mediated by the aspartate:alanine antiporter (AspT). The resulting PMF is sufficiently strong to drive ATP synthesis via the bacterial F0F1-ATPase. This interplay between PMF generation and ATP synthesis has been proposed as a proton-motive metabolic cycle, with its prototype observed in Oxalobacter formigenes14.

We have been conducting various characterizations of T. halophilus AspT. Enzymatic analysis using proteoliposomes has revealed that AspT has a low Michaelis constant for l-aspartate but a high Michaelis constant for l-alanine11. AspT has 10 transmembrane (TM) regions and a central hydrophilic loop domain15,16, and the protein undergoes conformational changes upon substrate-binding17,18, indicating that AspT adopts different conformations when binding l-aspartate or l-alanine. However, details of the substrate-transport mechanism of AspT, including the substrate-binding sites and substrate-transport pathways, remain to be elucidated.

AspT acts as a secondary active transporter6. Substrate transport by secondary active transporters is accomplished by one of three mechanisms that alter the conformation of the transporter19. These are the rocker switch mechanism20, the rocking-bundle mechanism21, and the elevator mechanism22,23. In the rocker switch and rocking-bundle mechanisms, selected domains of the transporters act as moving barriers, which block substrate transport alternatively at the intra- or extracellular side. In contrast, in the elevator mechanism, single or multiple domains remain stationary and the transport domains carrying the substrate-binding pocket move along the stationary domain(s). Consequently, the substrate-transport domains shift dramatically from the extracellular side to the intracellular side or vice versa19.

Here, we performed single-particle analysis (SPA) using cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) to elucidate details of the mechanism of substrate transport by T. halophilus AspT. We first determined outward-open apo form of AspT using a stable L60C variant to a resolution at 3.56 Å. In addition, the apo structure of AspT-WT was determined at 3.45 Å resolution using X-ray crystallography. We have finally determined the structure of the wild-type AspT (AspT-WT) in the l-aspartate-bound form using cryo-EM, achieving a resolution of 3.40 Å for the transmembrane region and 4.03 Å for the soluble domain. Our AspT structures provide compelling evidence that the T. halophilus AspT transports substrates via an elevator-type mechanism.

Results

Cryogenic EM density map of apo AspT-L60C homodimer

To determine the structures of AspT of T. halophilus, we purified a stable L60C variant, which forms spontaneous S–S bond between two homomers of AspT24 with reduced substrate transport activity (Supplemental Fig. 1a), as described24. The L60C protein sample, which exhibited a single peak on the gel-filtration chromatography (Supplemental Fig. 1b) and primary band by SDS-PAGE (Supplemental Fig. 1c), was analyzed by cryo-EM SPA (Supplemental Fig. 1d, e).

The 3D structure of AspT-L60C was determined at a resolution of 3.56 Å using the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (GSFSC) (Supplemental Fig. 2a–c), and the local resolution of the core area reaching up to 3.09 Å (Table 1). The quality of the map was sufficient for constructing the complete structure of the apo form of the AspT-L60C homodimer (Supplemental Fig. 3). The AspT-WT also forms a dimer as shown later. AspT forms a dimer and each monomer compose of a dimerization domain (D-domain), a substrate-transport domain (T-domain), and a soluble domain (S-domains) (Supplemental Fig. 4).

AspT has two types of repeat motives. The Repeat Unit 1 harbors the N-terminal D-1 and T-1 domains, and the Repeat Unit 2 contains the C-terminal D-2 and T-2 domains. These Repeat Units exhibit significant sequence identity (20%). Moreover, the structures of Repeat Units 1 and 2 can be superimposed by rotation at −179.4° with 0.96 Å translation, mimicking a pseudo C2 symmetry (Supplemental Fig. 5).

Each T-domain (T-1 and T-2) consists of a relatively long α-helix (from TM4 to TM5 and from TM9 to TM10, respectively) and two helical hairpins (HP1 between TM4 and 5, and HP2 between TM9 and 10, respectively). Each D-domain (D-1 and D-2) consists of three relatively short α-helices (TM1–3, TM6–8). D-1 and D-2 contain 10 and 5 phenylalanine residues, respectively. Among them, seven phenylalanine residues are present at the dimeric interface, including Phe17 (TM1), Phe65, Phe68, and Phe71 (TM3), Phe368 (TM6), and Phe425 and Phe426 (TM8) (Supplemental Fig. 4a). Their aromatic interactions stabilize the dimeric state.

Three other phenylalanine residues in the D-domains, including Phe66 and Phe69 (TM3) and Phe434 (TM8) are located at the interface with the T-domains (Supplemental Fig. 4a). These residues may contribute to the structural changes of the D- and T-domains upon ligand binding. In L60C variant, the substituted Cys residue on TM3 is located at the dimer interface (Supplemental Fig. 4b) and the two Cys residues at position 60 are in close proximity to form a S–S bond (2.0 Å), which supports our previous reports17,24. The S–S-bonded L60C variant favors the apo structure at an outward-open state.

Like the D-domains, the S-domains have dimer interfaces (Supplemental Fig. 4c). The two S-domains (S-1 and S-2) have similar structures, each consisting of two α helices and three β strands. S-domain 1 (S-1) (chain B, residues 212–278) and S-domain 1’ (S-1’) (chain A, residues 210–273) at the N-terminal end were found to produce a hydrophilic dimer interface (Supplemental Fig. 4c). In contrast, S-domain 2 (chain B, residues 289–354) and S-domain 2’ (chain A, residues 289–354) at the C-terminal end are not involved in the dimer interaction, and the gap between S-1 and S-2 is located outside the molecule. The relatively poor resolution of the S-domain compared with the TM region (T- and D-domains) suggests that the S-domain undergoes relatively larger domain movements (Supplemental Fig. 2b).

Crystal structure of the AspT-WT in an outward-facing conformation

The binding of l-aspartate to DDM-solubilized AspT-WT was analyzed by isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) to determine the dissociation constant (Kd). The interaction was exothermic, yielding an exothermic thermogram (Fig. 2a). The resulting binding curve displayed a nearly ideal sigmoidal shape and was well fitted with a stoichiometry (N) close to 1. The dissociation constant (Kd) for l-aspartate was determined to be 0.21 ± 0.02 mM.

Isotopically diffracting crystals were identified from a different crystal form at a space group of I 42 2 2, which were obtained when using lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG), triethylammonium phosphate as a crystallization additive. The structure was determined by molecular replacement using 9VEB_MolA (AspT-L60C in an outward-facing conformation) as the search template. A single copy of AspT is present in the asymmetric unit and refined to a resolution of 3.45 Å (Fig. 2b). When overlaying with AspT-L60C, the rmsd value is 0.674, indicating virtually identical conformation. The S–S bond at position 60 between the two molecules in the L60C variant slightly offsets their interactions. When generating symmetry mates, a dimeric AspT is seen (Supplemental Fig. 6). As shown in the cryo-EM structure, there are two separate contact areas, a larger one in the TM region and a smaller one in the S-domain (Fig. 2c, d). PISA measurement showed that the total contact area between two molecules is 2380.76 Å2 (Supplemental Table 1), including 1693.80 Å2 and 686.96 Å2 in the TM region and the S-domain, respectively. The existence of two large contact areas strongly indicates that AspT is a physiological dimer, as shown by cryo-EM structure, and the crystallographic axes are perfectly aligned with the physiological dimer axes. Interestingly, the L60C mutation decreases the interactions between the two monomers at their TM region while adding a covalent S–S bond.

Structure of L-aspartate-bound conformation of the AspT-WT

Next, we challenged structure analysis of the AspT-WT in the presence of l-aspartate. To this end, the AspT-WT was purified in the presence of l-aspartate (Supplemental Fig. 7a, b) and subjected to prepare vitrification of grids for cryo-EM analysis (Supplemental Figs. 7c, d).

Local refinement was performed separately for the TM region and the S-domain. The resulting volume for the TM region had a GSFSC resolution of 3.40 Å, while the volume for the S-domain reached a GSFSC resolution of 4.03 Å. The local resolution in the core region reached up to 1.79 Å for the TM region and 3.60 Å for the S-domain. (Table 1 and Supplemental Fig. 8a–c). The domain organization (Fig. 3a–c) did not differ markedly from that of apo L60C variant. However, comparing the two maps, the resolution of the S-domain of AspT-WT was noticeably lower (Figs. 1a and 3a). This suggests that the S-domain in AspT-WT has even greater flexibility. A clear density in the cavity allows to model one molecule of l-aspartate in both subunits, so it is a l-aspartate-bound state. Based on the density map, the ligand l-aspartate was modeled into the refined structure (Supplemental Fig. 9). Figure 3d, e show close-up views of the l-aspartate-binding region of AspT-WT (Fig. 3d, apo form) and AspT-WT (Fig. 3e, l-aspartate-bound form). In the bound state, l-aspartate is situated in the pocket between HP1 and HP2 and interacts with six side chains on the position of Thr130 (HP1), Ser132 (HP1), Tyr167 (TM5), Leu431(TM8), Thr496 (HP2) and Ser498 (HP2) (Supplemental Fig. 9c). A conservation analysis among nine AAEx members, which were presumed to perform aspartate/alanine exchange as AspT (Supplemental Fig. 10), showed four of the six substrate-binding residues—Thr130, Ser132, Tyr167, and Thr496—are highly conserved, indicating the important role in transport by AAEx family transporters.

Structural change in substrate-binding site caused by l-aspartate binding

The helical hairpins (HP1 and 2), which host the substrate-binding site, have similar conformations; in the two structures, the loop regions of the hairpin on the periplasmic side (HP1) and on the cytoplasmic side (HP2) are close together (Fig. 3d, e). In the D-domain, three Phe residues (positions 66, 69, and 434) are arranged in a row (Fig. 3d, e). In the apo form (Fig. 3d), the Phe66 and Phe434 are located near the cytoplasmic cavity. As a result, the periplasmic cavity and substrate-binding sites are connected at the periplasmic side of the protein in the apo-, outward-facing structure. In contrast, in the l-aspartate-bound form (Fig. 3e), Phe66 and Phe434 move between the periplasmic cavity and the substrate-binding site, blocking the connection between the periplasmic cavity and the substrate-binding site, functioning as gating residues (Supplemental Movie S1). Additionally, Phe69 is close to the substrate and, along with Tyr524, prevents the access of l-aspartate to the cytoplasmic cavity. The three Phe residues of the D-domain act like a sliding lid for opening and closing of the periplasmic and cytoplasmic cavities of the substrate-binding site.

Substrate entry path in AspT

To identify the pathway by which l-aspartate reaches the substrate-binding site from outside, we compared the changes around the substrate-binding site between the apo (Fig. 4a) and l-aspartate-bound forms (Fig. 4b). In the apo, outward-facing conformation, the empty substrate-binding pocket (shown in yellow and indicated by arrows in Fig. 4a, b) is solvent-accessible from the periplasmic surface (Supplemental Fig. 11a). However, the cytoplasmic side of the substrate-binding pocket is closed. In the l-aspartate-bound form, both the periplasmic and cytoplasmic sides of the substrate-binding pocket are closed (Fig. 4b and Supplemental Fig. 11b), and l-aspartate exists in a partially-open inward-facing intermediate state (i.e., l-aspartate-bound partially-open inward-facing intermediate conformation). Among the six residues (Thr130, Ser132, Tyr167, Leu431, Thr496, and Ser498) involved in the substrate-binding, Tyr167 (T-domain, TM5) is located at the entrance to the substrate-binding pocket from the periplasmic cavity (Fig. 4c). In the l-aspartate-binding conformation, it forms a hydrogen bond with l-aspartate (Supplemental Fig. 9). Gly439 (TM8) is located in the D-domain and is close to Tyr167. The distance between Gly439 and Tyr167 in the apo conformation is 10.2 Å (Fig. 4c). We reasoned that enlarging the side chain of G439 would block the access of l-aspartate to its binding site. To investigate how l-aspartate passes from the periplasmic cavity to the substrate-binding site in the outward-open apo conformation, Gly439 was replaced with Trp. The variant was purified and reconstituted into proteoliposomes for a l-aspartate transport assay. The results showed that transport activity of G439W AspT variant is abolished, supporting that the enlarging G439 blocks the pathway (Fig. 4d) and the bulky Trp side chain might prevent the formation of the barrier on the periplasmic side.

Conformational transition between the apo AspT and ligand-bound AspT

To analyze the changes in the spatial arrangement of domains during substrate transport in AspT, a quasi-rigid body rotation analysis was performed, and the differences between the outward-open apo (AspT-WT) and l-aspartate-bound partially-open inward-facing intermediate (AspT-WT) conformations were examined (Fig. 5a, b, and Supplemental movie S2). We found that the T-domain rotates by 13.3° and the S-domain rotates by 9.5° around the vertical axis of the membrane. The translational magnitudes of these domain motions are relatively small, 3.2 Å for the T-domain and 0.1 Å for the S-domain.

In the outward-open apo conformation, the substrate-binding sites located at HP1 (magenta) and HP2 (purple) in the T-domain are open to the periplasmic cavity but closed to the cytoplasmic cavity due to blocking by the D-domain. Thus, extracellular l-aspartate can pass through the periplasmic cavity and bind to the substrate-binding site. In the l-aspartate-bound partially-open inward-facing intermediate conformation, however, binding of l-aspartate to the substrate-binding site causes the T-domain to rotate toward the cytoplasm, allowing the D-domain to close the connection between the periplasmic cavity and the substrate-binding site. A new substrate permeation pathway is formed between the substrate-binding site and the cytoplasmic cavity.

These structural and biochemical analyses lead us to propose a model of l-aspartate transport mechanism as shown in Fig. 5c, d. In the outward-open apo conformation (Fig. 5c), the l-aspartate-binding site is connected to the periplasmic cavity. However, the D-domain and HP2 form the inner barrier on the cytoplasmic side, blocking the substrate-binding site from connecting to the cytoplasmic cavity. Binding of l-aspartate to the substrate-binding site induces the T-domain to rotate toward the cytoplasm and the D-domains to shift to the periplasmic side (Fig. 5d). Conversely, in the l-aspartate-bound conformation, D-domains (TM3 and TM8) and HP1 form an outer barrier on the periplasmic side (Figs. 3e and 4b), blocking the connection between the periplasmic cavity and the substrate-binding site.

Comparison of conformational changes of AspT and CitS upon substrate-binding

While we could not find homologous transporters with a similar sequence for AspT in the Protein Data Bank, we did find several transporters with 3D structure similar to that of the TM region of AspT Supplemental Fig. 12a). The transporter showing the most similar structure is a CitS of Salmonella enterica (Supplemental Fig. 12b, PDB ID:5a1s25). Although their sequence identity is quite low (12.2%), AspT and CitS have an overall similar 3D structure, with an average template modeling (TM) score26 of 0.679 and a root mean square deviation (RMSD) of 4.53 Å (Cα atoms) over 361 residues (Supplemental Fig. 12a, b). The secondary structure topologies of AspT (Supplemental Fig. 12c) and CitS (Supplemental Fig. 12d) are very similar. The citrate-Na+ binding structure of CitS shows that, while CitS also forms dimers, the two protomers of CitS adopt different outward and inward conformations. It should be noted that while the AspT apo structure and the CitS outward-open structure (PDB ID: 5a1s_A25) present a similar conformation, the AspT l-aspartate-binding structure and the CitS inward-open ligand-bound structure (PDB ID: 5a1s_B) exhibit substantially different conformations. The T-domain in CitS has been reported25 to rotate by 31.5° upon substrate transport. In contrast, in the case of AspT, the T-domain rotation is only 13.3°.

In the l-aspartate-bound conformation of AspT, the pathway for l-aspartate to enter the cytoplasmic side is blocked by F69 and Y524 (Fig. 3e), suggesting that it is a partially-open inward-facing intermediate conformation before inward-opening. Importantly, if the T-domain of AspT rotates by an additional 10° toward the cytoplasm, the substrate-binding site would be connected to the cytoplasmic cavity, and l-aspartate would be released into the cytoplasmic cavity. Although the structure of the inward-opening conformation of AspT remains to be clarified, we surmise that this is the most likely scenario for the l-aspartate transport mechanism.

Discussion

To clarify the mechanism of substrate exchange transport by secondary exchange transporters, in this study, we determined structures of the aspartate:alanine exchanger AspT of the lactic acid bacterium T. halophilus using cryo-EM SPA. Structures of AspT at two discrete conformations, namely, the apo outward-facing state and the substrate l-aspartate-bound partially-open inward-facing intermediate state, were obtained from a stable L60C variant and the AspT-WT, respectively. In addition, the apo outward-facing state of AspT-WT was elucidated by X-ray crystallography. Our analyses revealed that the transport domain, T-domain, carrying one l-aspartate molecule, shifts to the cytoplasmic side of the membrane, and forms an outer barrier that blocks the periplasmic access to the substrate-binding pocket. These results support our contention that AspT transports l-aspartate via the elevator mechanism in which the up–down movements of the T-domains are critical steps, and the D-domain is stationary, supporting the substrate-carrying T-domain sliding and engaging in the formation of mobile barriers. Thus, this study adds to our fundamental understanding of the structural basis of the antiporter function.

When the HP structures were superimposed, all the binding residues in the T-domain aligned well. In contrast, the static D-domain was displaced by approximately 7–8 Å. When the static D-domains were superimposed, the binding residues in the T-domain also shifted by about 7–8 Å. This observation supports an elevator-type conformational change. Surface representations of both structures reveal the presence of a barrier (a region inaccessible to solvent) in each case (Supplemental Fig. 13). The inner barrier appears more pronounced, suggesting that the outward-facing conformation may be more stable.

When we explored similar 3D structures in the Protein Data Bank using the structure of AspT S-domains (208–357), we found that the AspT is the only transporter that harbors the S-domain, while the similar S-domain structure resides in channels27 and other proteins binding to cyclic di-AMP28. These results suggest that, while CitS has a similar substrate-transport mechanism to that of AspT, there are also differences in the regulation of substrate-transport mechanisms. We surmise that the presence of the S-domain may be the basis of the difference, which must be further clarified through the structural comparisons of AspT and CitS.

Structural comparisons of substrate-binding sites of the AspT and CitS show that locations of HP2 of the AspT and CitS are similar. In contrast, locations of HP1 differs in inclination and position. An additional difference observed between AspT and CitS is that the Na+-derived density observed between HP1 and HP2 in CitS is not observed in AspT, consistent with the fact that AspT does not transport Na+. Of note, the HP1 and HP2 are also involved in transport of substrates in INDY29 and NaCT30, which belong to the divalent anion sodium symporter family and have been implicated in the pathogenesis of human diseases31,32. Like AspT and CitS, these transporters form dimers and transport substrates by the elevator mechanism33. Focusing on elevator-type transporters23 that are structurally similar to AspT, it has been reported that in CitS, the subunits move independently within the dimer.

In AspT, the two S domains appear to undergo lateral rotation within the membrane in response to conformational changes, in a manner that is coordinated with structural rearrangements observed in the TM region. Notably, unlike CitS, AspT harbors S domains that engage in inter-monomeric interactions, suggesting that the two protomers function in a concerted and cooperative fashion.

Our study has revealed the overall structure of AspT and the transport mechanism of l-aspartate. On the other hand, the l-alanine-bound structure was not obtained, and the binding site for l-alanine remains unidentified. Previous biochemical studies have shown that the binding of l-alanine induces a conformation distinct from that of the l-aspartate-bound state in AspT18. Future elucidation of the l-alanine binding structure of AspT will enhance our understanding of the substrate exchange transport mechanism of AspT. This study also provides important insights into the substrate exchange transport mechanisms of secondary exchange transporters, including those critical for maintaining homeostasis in living organisms, especially secondary exchange transport by the elevator mechanism.

Materials and methods

Construction of expression plasmids for AspT-WT and its variants

The plasmid pTrc99a-AspD/T, in which the asp operon is inserted into pTrc99A34, was used as the expression plasmid for both AspT-WT and its variants; a His-tag was inserted into the loop region (L331–K332) of the AspT gene for purification17. For expression of the single-cysteine variant AspT-L60C, in which a cysteine residue is introduced into TM3, plasmid pTrc99a-AspD/T L60C was used17. Plasmids used in this study are listed in Supplemental Table 2. The expression plasmid for the G439W variant, in which Gly439 in TM8 was substituted with tryptophan, was created using a QuikChange II Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent Technologies, Inc.) and pTrc99a-AspD/T as the template. Primers used in this study are listed in Supplemental Table 2.

Expression of AspT-WT and its variants

The expression host was Escherichia coli C43 (DE3) (genotype: F- ompT hsdSB (rB- mB-) gal dcm (DE3))35 (LGC Biosearch Technologies). Cultures were grown as previously reported with some modifications24. In short, E. coli transformants were inoculated into 40 mL of Luria-Bertani medium (supplemented with 30 mM d-glucose and 100 µg/mL ampicillin) and incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 125 rpm for 25 h. The preculture medium was then added to 1 L of fresh Luria-Bertani medium (30 mM d-glucose and 100 µg/mL ampicillin) and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 160 rpm until an OD 0.5 was achieved, after which the culture medium was added to an additional 1 L of Luria-Bertani medium (30 mM d-glucose, 100 µg/mL ampicillin, 1 mM pyridoxal-5’-phosphate, and 50 mM l-aspartate [K salt, pH 7.0]). The cells were then incubated with 0.2 mM isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (final concentration) at 37 °C for 12 h. Finally, the cells were centrifuged (5280 × g, 15 min; Avanti J-26 XP, Beckman Coulter), flash frozen using liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C.

Purification of AspT

The AspT structure was examined by cryo-EM analysis using wild-type and stable AspT-L60C variant of AspT. In a previous study using size exclusion chromatography with multi-angle light scattering36 and blue native-PAGE37, AspT-WT was shown to form a homodimer24. The single-cysteine variant AspT-L60C has been shown to form an S–S bond between the monomers at a high rate within cell membranes and Apo form of AspT-L60C can stably maintain its structure, while Apo form AspT-WT cannot maintain stability17.

AspT-WT and its variant proteins were purified as previously reported38. In brief, frozen cells were thawed, suspended in crushing buffer, crushed at 68.9 MPa using a high-pressure crushing device, and centrifuged (27,200 × g, 15 min) to remove uncrushed cells. The supernatant was subjected to ultracentrifugation (138,000 × g, 3 h) to obtain the membrane fraction, which was then suspended in crushing buffer, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C.

The dissolved membrane fraction was solubilized in solubilizing buffer. The solubilized solution was ultracentrifuged at 193,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was mixed with 4 mL of Ni Sepharose resin along with PMSF. Imidazole (pH 6.0) was added to a final concentration of 2 mM. After incubation at 4 °C for 1 h, the resin was replaced with LMNG by washing with 20 mL of LMNG-substituted buffer. Three additional washes were performed with 20 mL of wash buffer. After the washes, the sample was eluted into 20 mL of elution buffer and concentrated using an ultrafiltration membrane (Amicon Ultra-4 Centrifugal Filter Ultracel-100 K). Samples were then subjected to gel-filtration chromatography (mobile phase: 20 mM MES-Na [pH 6.0], 100 mM NaCl, 100 mM l-aspartate [Na salt, pH 6.0], 0.003% LMNG; flow rate: 0.4 mL/min) using an AKTA Purifier 10 chromatograph equipped with a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (both Cytiva).

To improve purity, the sample was further concentrated using an ultrafiltration membrane (Amicon Ultra-4 Centrifugal Filter unit equipped with an Ultracel-100 K filter) and subjected to gel-filtration chromatography (Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column). In the purification of AspT-L60C, a mobile phase of 20 mM MES-Na [pH 6.0], 100 mM NaCl, 0.003% LMNG was used for the second gel-filtration chromatography and the main peak fraction was used for cryo-EM. In the purification of AspT-WT, a mobile phase of 20 mM MES-Na (pH 6.0), 100 mM NaCl, 0.003% LMNG, 100 mM l-aspartate (Na salt, pH 6.0) was used for the second gel-filtration chromatography and the main peak fraction was used for cryo-EM.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data acquisition

For AspT-L60C, the Vitrobot system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to prepare sample grids. Three microliters of protein solution containing 6.14 mg/mL of AspT-L60C was applied to a Quantifoil-Cu-R1.2/1.3–300 grid (Quantifoil Micro Tools) that had been hydrophilized by glow discharge using a JEC-3000FC sputter-coater (JEOL) twice for 30 s. The grid was then blotted for 2 s at 4 °C and 100% humidity, and plunged into liquid ethane at −182 °C. The resultant grids were kept in liquid nitrogen until imaging. A total of 8799 movies were taken under a CRYO ARM 300 II microscope (JEOL) at an accelerating voltage of 300 kV and a nominal magnification of 60,000 × g, which corresponds to a pixel size of 0.788 Å. The movies were recorded using SerialEM software (v3.7)39 and a K3 BioQuantum detector (Gatan) set in counting mode and a total dose of 60 e−/Å2 over 60 frames.

For AspT-WT with l-aspartate, an EM-GP2 plunge freezer (Leica) was used to prepare sample grids. Three microliters of protein solution containing 2.83 mg/mL of AspT-WT was applied to a Quantifoil-Cu-R1.2/1.3–400 grid (Quantifoil Micro Tools) that had been treated in a Solarus plasma cleaner (Gatan) for 30 s. The grid was then blotted for 6 s at 4 °C and 80% humidity, and plunged into liquid ethane at −182 °C. The resultant grids were kept in liquid nitrogen until imaging. A total of 12,357 movies were taken under a Titan Krios electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at an acceleration voltage of 300 kV and a nominal magnification of 96,000×, which corresponds to a pixel size of 0.845 Å. The movies were recorded using EPU software and a Falcon 3 Direct Electron Detector (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in counting mode and a total dose of 40 e−/Å2 over 40 frames.

Image processing and model building and refinement

For AspT-L60C, after motion correction and contrast transfer function estimation of the 8799 movies using RELION software (v3.1.1–4.0.0)40, 1,634,276 particles were automatically picked using crYOLO software (v1.8.3)41, and two-dimensional classification was performed three times. Three-dimensional classification was performed three times using a total of 1,545,867 particles after two-dimensional classification. Particles classified into the good classes (527,782 particles) were re-extracted at a pixel size of 1.182 Å, imported into the CryoSPARC software42,43, and subjected to three rounds of heterogeneous refinement. Ab-initio reconstruction was further performed on 273,097 particles in the good quality class of heterogeneous refinement, and homogeneous refinement was performed using 250,605 particles in the good quality class of the ab-initio reconstruction.

All the particles were exported using the csparc2star.py script from Daniel Asarnow (10.5281/zenodo.3576630), and Bayesian polishing was performed in RELION. The particles were then re-imported into CryoSPARC for non-uniform refinement. The final map had a GSFSC resolution of 3.56 Å, and with the local resolution of the core area reaching up to 3.09 Å.

For AspT-WT with l-aspartate, after motion correction and contrast transfer function estimation using RELION for the 12,357 movies, crYOLO was used to automatically pick 1,636,712 particles and perform two-dimensional classification three times. After the three 2D classifications, 3D classification was performed three times using a total of 653,268 particles. After three rounds, 178,778 particles in the selected two classes were imported into CryoSPARC for heterogeneous refinement. Non-uniform refinement was performed for the good class (125,465 particles). The particles were exported using the csparc2star.py script and subjected to Bayesian polishing in RELION. The particles were then re-imported into CryoSPARC for non-uniform refinement. To improve the resolution, separate masks were created for the TM region and the S-domain, followed by local refinement. As a result, the final map of the TM region achieved a GSFSC resolution of 3.40 Å, with the local resolution of the core area reaching up to 1.79 Å. The final map of the S-domain reached a GSFSC resolution of 4.03 Å, with the core region showing a local resolution as high as 3.60 Å.

For the AspT-L60C and AspT-WT structures, initial models were constructed using the map_to_model tool in the Phenix package44. The models were then manually fitted to the density map using Coot45. The TM region was successfully built for both structures. For the S-domain, the electron density map was complete for the AspT-L60C map, but only a partial map was obtained for AspT-WT. By superimposing the S-domain in AspT-L60C on the partially constructed AspT-WT structure, we were able to infer the structure of the S-domain in AspT-WT. The model was refined using Phenix.real_space_refine.

L-aspartate binding by isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC)

The l-aspartate binding assay was performed at 25 °C using a Nano-ITC instrument (TA Instruments). In a typical experiment, 2 μL aliquots of l-aspartate solution were sequentially injected into the sample cell containing the purified AspT sample or buffer at 300-s intervals, under constant stirring at 250 rpm. All solutions were degassed for 15 min using a TA Instruments degassing station (model 6326) prior to titration. Data were processed using NanoAnalyze software (version 3.6.0). Heats of dilution were estimated from the final injections where no further binding occurred and subtracted from the normalized heat change. The corrected heats were plotted against the molar ratio of titrant to titrate, and binding constants (Ka) were determined by fitting the data to a one-site independent binding model. In most cases, the stoichiometry (N) was fixed at 1 to improve fitting accuracy based on known parameters. The dissociation constant (Kd) = 1/Ka.

Crystallization

Crystallization trials were performed at 23 °C using the hanging-drop vapor-diffusion method. A mixture of 1 μL of AspT-WT solution (10 mg/mL with 0.01% DDM) and 1 μL of reservoir solution (0.015% LMNG, 50 mM TEAP, 200 mM CaCl2, 100 mM cacodylic acid (pH 6.9), and 35% PEG 600) was equilibrated against reservoir solution. AspT-WT crystals were obtained after two weeks, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subjected to X-ray diffraction at beamline BL41XU at SPring-8 at a temperature of 100 K and a wavelength of 1 Å. The data reduction using unmerged data from the automated data processing system at SPring-8 were carried out using AIMLESS in CCP4i2. Data collection statistics are summarized in Table 2.

Crystal structure determination

The structure determination was performed through molecular replacement using the PDB of either of the cryo-EM models: 9VEB (AspT-L60C variant in an outward-facing conformation) or 8Y1X (AspT-WT in a l-aspartate-bound partially-open inward-facing intermediate conformation) as the search model in Phenix Phaser-MR46. The 9VEB-MolA provided a promising solution with one AspT polypeptide per asymmetric unit. The model was refined through rounds of manual building in Coot and Phenix Refine, achieving a resolution of 3.45 Å with R/Rfree values of 0.236/0.293. The structure was modeled from position 1 to the final position 543 without a gap. The inserted 6xHis-tag at position Leu331 was modeled partially. No side chain was modeled for three residues at positions Lys30, Leu245, and Lys411. The statistics of refinement for the final models are summarized in Table 2. Ramachandran was favored at 94.33%, with outliers at 1.10%, clash scores at 2.05, and overall scores of 1.36, as evaluated by MolProbity in Phenix.

3D Structure comparison

Comparisons of the 3D structures of AspT with structures in the Protein Data Bank database were performed using the MATRAS program47. Although a standard BLAST sequence search48 did not identify any homologues in the Protein Data Bank, a 3D structure similarity search identified several similar structures. The structural similarities were examined for two parts: the TM region (T-domain and D-domain) and the soluble region (S-domain). For the TM region (residues 1–201 and 360–543), the most similar structure was CitS from S. enterica (PDB ID:5a1s)25, but several other similar transporter membrane proteins were also found (e.g., PDB_IDs: 6iva, 7b4l, 5bz3, 4czb, 4cz8, 7egl, 7dsv, 8qp9, and 7y3e).

Analysis of 3D conformational changes

To characterize the 3D conformational changes of the outward-open apo conformation and the l-aspartate-bound partially-open inward-facing intermediate conformation, a quasi-rigid-body motion analysis was performed. First, the two structures were superimposed using only their D-domains (residues 12–76 and 365–411). Then, an optimal rotation matrix and a translational vector were calculated for a focused region (T-domain or S-domain) to minimize the RMSD between the focused regions of the two structures. Using this matrix and vector, a screw axis and an origin position, which represent both rotational and translational directions, were calculated using Chasles’ theorem49. Quasi-rigid body analysis using the screw axis was conducted in the MATRAS program47. The regions comprising residues 90–201 and 456–543 were used for the motion of the T-domains, and the region comprising residues 210–357 was used for the motion of the S-domains. The positions of the membrane were calculated with the PPM 3.0 program50.

l-aspartate transport assay

The L-aspartate transport assay was carried out according to the method of Suzuki et al.18. In brief, purified AspT-WT or one of its variants (L60C or G439W) was reconstituted into 100 mM l-aspartate-loaded proteoliposomes and pre-incubated in assay buffer for 3 min at 25 °C. A tritium-labeled transport substrate, 10 mM l-[2,3-3H]aspartate (American Radiolabeled Chemicals), and 1 mM aspartate as the potassium salt (pH 7.0) were added to the proteoliposomes. After 1, 3, 5, and 7 min, 50-µL aliquots of the proteoliposomes were placed on a glass filter (GF-75, 25 mm; Advantec Toyo) and washed with 6 mL of 0.05 M potassium phosphate (pH 7.0) containing 0.1 M K2SO4 to stop the transport reaction. The amount of tritium transported into the liposomes was measured with a liquid scintillation counter. At all sampling points, three rounds of sampling were conducted, and the average values along with the errors were presented. Quantification of proteins reconstituted in proteoliposomes was performed by SDS-PAGE. After electrophoresis, the samples were stained with Lumitein Protein Gel Stain (Biotium). Gels were detected using an ImageQuant LAS 4000 mini camera system (GE Healthcare) and quantified using Image J software (US NIH).
