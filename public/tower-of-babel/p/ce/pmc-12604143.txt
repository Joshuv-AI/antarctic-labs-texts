Severe disseminated tuberculosis complicated by polyneuropathy: a case report and literature review
Authors: Xiaoqing Huang, Lei Pan, Minjie Mao, Xiaohong Pan, Junke Qiu, Xingwu Zou, Dandan Bi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis is a great public health concern. Disseminated tuberculosis carries a high mortality. Polyneuropathy is a rare but serious complication in patients with tuberculosis.Case presentationWe report a 26-year-old female who presented with shortness of breath, anorexia, nausea, black stools, hoarseness, generalized weakness, and progressively worsening skin tingling sensation in the bilateral lower extremities. The patient rapidly developed respiratory failure, and required endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation. Severe disseminated tuberculosis was diagnosed, with Mycobacterium tuberculosis identified in the lung, gastrointestinal tract, and bone marrow (positive sputum smear for acid-fast bacilli, positive stool cartridge-based nucleic acid amplification test, and positive next-generation sequencing test in bone marrow biopsy). After two months of enhanced anti-tuberculosis treatment and intensive specialty nursing care, the patient continued to present with generalized weakness and tingling sensation in the bilateral lower extremities. The electromyography examination revealed multiple peripheral nerve damage, which is consistent with polyneuropathy. Finally, the patient recovered with persistent bilateral lower extremity weakness after the 13-month anti-tuberculosis treatment in addition to nutritional support, nursing care, and rehabilitation.ConclusionsPolyneuropathy is rare, but can occur to patients with tuberculosis. Its clinical presentations can be easily masked when patients have multiple symptoms from serious illness, such as severe disseminated tuberculosis. Careful physical examination, necessary laboratory and imaging studies, appropriate treatments, and close follow-up re-evaluations should be applied to avoid long-term sequela of serious nerve damage and loss of function in these patients.Keywords: Tuberculosis, Disseminated infection, Respiratory failure, Polyneuropathy, Case report

Background

Tuberculosis is an infectious disease caused by the bacterium, Mycobacterium tuberculosis [1]. In 2023, tuberculosis caused 1.30 million deaths globally [2]. It is one of the leading causes of death worldwide, and remains among the most prevalent infectious diseases in humans [3]. In addition to its primary target, which is the lungs, tuberculosis can affect multiple organ systems, resulting in disseminated tuberculosis. Polyneuropathy is a rare but serious extrapulmonary complication in patients with tuberculosis. Its incidence was estimated to be approximately 10% in tuberculosis patients, with its incidence slightly higher in males than in females [4]. Tuberculous polyneuropathy can be caused by direct infection or damage from Mycobacterium tuberculosis, immune-mediated responses, mechanical compression from granuloma, vascular inflammation, anti-tuberculosis medications (such as vitamin B6 deficiency from isoniazid intake), or comorbidities, such as diabetes or human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection [5]. Its diagnosis is based on the clinical presentation, physical examination, electromyography evaluation, and nerve biopsy. However, other etiologies, such as Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS), have to be ruled out [6]. In clinical practice, physicians would focus more attention on major organ damage in patients with disseminated tuberculosis, and miss or delay the diagnosis and management of polyneuropathy, which can lead to serious long-term sequelae [2, 7]. The present report presents a 26-year-old female with severe disseminated tuberculosis, who also developed polyneuropathy. Relevant literature was further reviewed to provide evidence-based recommendations on the management of this rare clinical situation, aiming to support its early diagnosis and prompt treatment for satisfactory outcomes.

Case presentation

On July 21, 2021, a 26-year-old female was admitted to the tuberculosis intensive care unit (ICU) in Zhejiang Provincial Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine in Hongzhou, China. Since one and a half months ago, the patient gradually had shortness of breath, anorexia, nausea, black stools, hoarseness, generalized weakness, and progressively worsening skin-tingling sensation in the lower extremities. Ten days prior to presentation, the patient was not able to walk. Two days prior to presentation, the patient suddenly lost consciousness, and regained consciousness after endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation at a local hospital. The emergency head computed tomography (CT) scan revealed no abnormalities, and the lung CT scan reported multiple bronchiectasis with consolidations in both lungs and the cavity in the right lung (Fig. 2A). The sputum examination revealed acid-fast bacilli 4 + . The patient was diagnosed with severe pulmonary tuberculosis, and received rifampicin, ethambutol, linezolid and amikacin. Then, the patient was transferred to our hospital. The patient’s family denied any past medical history for the patient. At ICU admission, the patient’s Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II score was 18. The patient’s vital signs were, as follows: temperature, 35.90 °C; pulse, 123 beats/min; respiratory, 16 breaths/min; blood pressure, 99/58 mmHg. The body mass index (BMI) was 16.50 kg/m2. The patient had pallor. The patient was placed on mechanical ventilation. The physical examination revealed bilateral lung crackles, mild abdominal distension and tenderness with no rebound tenderness, and muscle power of 1/5 in both lower extremities. Radial periosteum reflex, biceps tendon reflex, triceps tendon reflex, and knee tendon reflex were absent. Furthermore, Achilles tendon reflex was present, but abdominal wall reflex was absent. Moreover, the Babinski sign was absent bilaterally, with hypotonia to four extremities.

The laboratory test results were, as follows: blood gas analysis: pH 7.53 (normal range 7.35–7.45), carbon dioxide partial pressure: 22 mmHg (normal range 35–45 mmHg), oxygen partial pressure: 67 mmHg (normal range 80–100 mmHg), and residual base: −4.50 mmol/L (normal range −3–3 mmol/L); serum chemistry: creatinine: 28.00 μmol/L (normal range 41–111 μmol/L), total protein: 37.20 g/L (normal range 65–85 g/L), albumin: 17.30 g/L (normal range 40–55 g/L), potassium: 2.97 mmol/L (normal range 3.40–4.50 mmol/L), and calcium: 1.71 mmol/L (normal range 2.15–2.57 mmol/L); blood procalcitonin: 2.10 ng/ml (normal range 0–0.50 ng/mL); complete cell count: white blood cells: 5.40 × 109/L (normal range 3.50–10.00 × 109/L), neutrophils: 84% (normal range 46.50–76.50%), lymphocyte count: 0.65 × 109/L (normal range 1–3.3 × 109/L), hemoglobin: 52.00 g/L (normal range 120–170 g/L), mean corpuscular volume: 79.50 fL (normal range 82–98 fL), hematocrit: 0.15 (normal range 0.35–0.50), platelets: 46.00 × 109/L (normal range 100–300 × 109/L), and C-reaction protein: 155.89 mg/L (normal range 0–3.30 mg/L); coagulation panel: activated partial thromboplastin time: 36.60 seconds (normal range 18–34 seconds), D-dimer: 4,170.00 µg/L (normal range 0–750 µg/L), and vitamin B12: 1,014.00 pmol/L (normal range 133–675 pmol/L). Furthermore, hepatitis B surface antigen was negative, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) antibody was negative, syphilis antibody was negative, and thyroid function was within normal range. The lumbar puncture opening pressure was 110.00 mmH2O, with normal cell count and biochemical analysis of the cerebrospinal fluid. The cryptococcal capsular antigen in the cerebrospinal fluid was negative. The cartridge-based nucleic acid amplification test (Xpert MTB/RIF) in the cerebrospinal fluid indicated a negative Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex. However, the sputum Mycobacterium tuberculosis DNA and 16S rRNA tests were positive (pathogenic microorganism nucleic acid extraction and purification kit, MD005; Universal sequencing reaction kit, MD004, Hangzhou Geju Medical Technology Co., Ltd.). Furthermore, the sputum and stool Xpert MTB/RIF tests were positive. The sputum was also collected for Mycobacterium tuberculosis culture and drug-susceptibility test. The next-generation sequencing (NGS) test on the bone marrow biopsy specimen revealed 38 sequence numbers of Mycobacterium tuberculosis. The bone marrow biopsy pathology examination revealed granulocyte hyperplasia, a normal granulocyte-erythroid ratio, scattered visible megakaryocytes, poor platelet production function, and elevated mononuclear system, but with negative acid-fast staining. The bedside ultrasound revealed multiple enlarged left cervical lymph nodes. The laryngoscopy revealed no abnormalities in the pharynx or larynx. The abdominal CT scan reported intestinal dilatation and trace pelvic effusion. There were no obvious abnormalities in the brain, or in the cervical, thoracic, or lumbar spine CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans. The patient was diagnosed with severe disseminated tuberculosis.

In the ICU, considering the patient’s severe disseminated tuberculosis status, high bacterial load (sputum smear acid-fast bacilli 4+), weakened immune function (decreased lymphocyte count), and rapid progression of disease despite being under anti-tuberculosis treatment, the investigators decided to administer an enhanced anti-tuberculosis regimen with the following medications: isoniazid, rifampin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol (HRZE) regimen (isoniazid: 0.30 g daily, rifampicin: 0.45 g daily, pyrazinamide: 0.50 g three times a day, and ethambutol: 0.75 g daily), and moxifloxacin (0.40 g daily), linezolid (0.60 g every 12 hours), and amikacin (0.40 g daily) injections [8, 9]. In addition, vasopressor, hemostasis, methylprednisolone, nutritional support, blood transfusion, vitamin B1, B6 and B12 supplementation, immunoglobulin infusion, intensive specialty nursing care, and rehabilitation were initiated for the patient. The patient’s gastrointestinal bleeding gradually stopped. Furthermore, the vital signs were stable, and the repeat blood gas analysis was normal. However, the patient was unable to be extubated due to weak respiratory efforts and low blood oxygen saturation. On August 23 (day 34 of hospitalization), the patient underwent tracheostomy, and continued to present with persistent symptoms of significantly decreased muscle power and tingling sensation in the bilateral lower extremities. On August 26 (day 37 of hospitalization), the electromyography revealed the absence of motor nerve amplitude of the bilateral femoral nerves and right common peroneal nerve, damaged bilateral ulnar nerves and left common peroneal nerve, damaged bilateral superficial peroneal nerves and bilateral sural nerves, abnormal bilateral tibial nerve H reflex (indicating nerve root damage), damaged bilateral tibial and right rectus femoris nerves, and abnormal lower extremity somatosensory evoked potentials. On September 1 (day 43 of hospitalization), the initial sputum culture reported positive for Mycobacterium tuberculosis with drug sensitive results. After two months of anti-tuberculosis treatment, the patient continued to have persistent fever with low platelet count and hemoglobin level, and was dependent on mechanical ventilation. There was no improvement in extremity muscle power. The sputum acid-fast smear examination remained positive for Mycobacterium tuberculosis. The further sputum bacterial culture and sensitivity test (BACTEC MGIT 960, Becton Dickinson, USA) revealed sensitivity to the HRZE regimen. The enhanced anti-tuberculosis treatment was extended, and passive exercise rehabilitation training was initiated. The patient’s hemoglobin level and platelet count gradually returned to normal. The upper extremity muscle power returned to level 5/5, and the lower extremity muscle power gradually returned to 3/5. On November 1 (day 104 of hospitalization), the tracheostomy tube was removed, and the sputum Mycobacterium tuberculosis culture was repeated, with no bacterial growth for 42 days. On November 9 (day 112 of hospitalization), the patient was discharged from the hospital, and the HRZE treatment was continued. During the follow-up clinic visit on June 22, 2022, the chest CT scan revealed significant improvement (Fig. 2B). The electromyography revealed that the damage to the bilateral ulnar nerves, median nerves, bilateral tibial nerves, and bilateral superficial peroneal nerves recovered, as evidenced by the increase in sensory nerve conduction velocity and motor nerve conduction velocity, the significant increase in amplitude, and the shortened latency period. The ulnar nerve and median nerve recovered to normal levels, but the recovery of lower extremity neuronal conduction damage was relatively slow. On August 26, 2022, the patient’s BMI improved to 23.90 kg/m2. Furthermore, the patient had full strength in the bilateral upper extremities, had no pain but slight weakness in the bilateral lower extremities, and could walk slowly with minimal assistance. Moreover, the patient’s voice hoarseness, fever, cough, sputum, chest tightness, and shortness of breath were all resolved. Hence, the anti-tuberculosis treatment was discontinued (a total of 13 months). The patient continued to participate in the rehabilitation exercises. During the last follow-up visit, the patient was able to walk slowly, and had normal speech.

Discussion

The present study described a patient with severe tuberculosis. The imaging studies and laboratory tests suggested tuberculosis involvement in the lungs, intestine, bone marrow, and lymph nodes, suggesting disseminated tuberculosis. At ICU admission, the patient’s APACHE II score was 18, with septic shock and respiratory failure, and the patient was dependent on mechanical ventilation and vasopressor. The investigators made the diagnosis of severe disseminated tuberculosis. After the anti-tuberculosis treatment, the patient’s circulatory and respiratory functions improved, but the patient continued to have persistent generalized weakness and abnormal extremity sensation. The electromyography examination indicated multiple peripheral nerve damage, which is consistent with polyneuropathy.

Tuberculous polyneuropathy occurs when multiple nerves throughout the body become damaged during tuberculous infection [5]. In addition to skin tingling sensation and muscle weakness in the extremities, the present patient presented with voice hoarseness and difficulty weaning from the ventilator. The hoarseness could be due to the glossopharyngeal nerve involvement. The difficulty in weaning from the ventilator was likely due to respiratory muscle weakness with phrenic nerve involvement. After the enhanced anti-tuberculosis treatment, the patient was successfully weaned off the ventilator. The patient’s muscle power improved, and the hoarseness recovered. After excluding other etiologies based on the clinical presentation, electromyography findings, and treatment response, the investigators considered the diagnosis of tuberculous polyneuropathy.

The pathogenesis of tuberculous polyneuropathy may be correlated to the direct infection or damage from Mycobacterium tuberculosis, immune-mediated responses, mechanical compression from granuloma, vascular inflammation, or medications used to treat tuberculosis (such as vitamin B6 deficiency from isoniazid intake) [5]. Before considering tuberculosis as the cause for polyneuropathy, other causes for neuropathy should be ruled out. The non-infectious causes include diabetes, chronic alcohol use, hypothyroidism, uremia, and chemotherapy (vinca alkaloids, platinum, taxanes, and thalidomide) [10–14]. Infectious diseases, such as leprosy, HIV, hepatitis C, Lyme disease, syphilis, and GBS from Campylobacter jujuni infection, can also present as polyneuropathy [15, 16]. Careful medical history interview, physical examinations, and laboratory tests can help to determine the etiology of polyneuropathy. GBS patients can present with mild upper respiratory tract symptoms for 1–3 weeks, and symmetrical flaccid paralysis of the extremities and cerebrospinal fluid protein-cell separation for 2–4 weeks after disease onset [17]. Furthermore, the electromyography can present with slow nerve conduction velocity and prolonged distal latency. The present patient mainly had symmetrical paresthesias, and decreased extremity muscle power. The electromyography revealed asymmetrical nerve conduction and sensory nerve damage, with no cerebrospinal fluid protein-cell separation, which is not consistent with GBS. The symptoms of chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy are similar to those of GBS, but often lasts for more than six months [18]. The present patient had significant improvement after the anti-tuberculosis treatment. Polyneuropathy is a common complication in HIV patients [19]. However, the present patient had a negative HIV test result. In addition, no abnormalities were found in the cerebrospinal fluid examination and head CT scan, which does not support the diagnosis of tuberculous meningitis or encephalitis, although tuberculous meningoencephalitis can have various presentations, such as stroke, hearing change, visual loss, and seizure [20–22]. Entrapped peripheral neuritis was not likely, since the CT and MRI of the cervical and thoracic vertebrae revealed no abnormalities. Furthermore, the patient had no history of alcohol abuse. The patient’s neuropathy was not likely due to vitamin B1 deficiency, since the patient’s treatment included vitamin B1 supplementation. The patient’s polyneuropathy might have been initially caused by the immune mediated response to tuberculosis, which was later exacerbated by anti-tuberculosis medications, such as linezolid and isoniazid. Both medications have been reported to cause polyneuropathy in previous literature [23, 24]. Pyridoxine (vitamin B6) is suggested to be used together with isoniazid administration.

The investigators further reviewed the previous literature, and identified nine case reports on tuberculous polyneuropathy (Tables 1 and 2) [5, 25–32]. Most of the patients were young, without previous medical history. The majority of the patients had pulmonary tuberculosis, and most of the patients had extrapulmonary involvement. These patients presented with multiple atypical symptoms and signs, although enlarged lymph nodes and masses were frequently reported. The neurological examinations revealed motor and sensory deficits, with abnormal electromyographic results. If a nerve biopsy was performed, the results would reveal nerve injury or granulomas, without evidence of direct bacterial invasion. After effective anti-tuberculosis treatment, nearly half of the patients continued to suffer from residue neurological symptoms, suggesting that the nerve damage might be irreversible. Therefore, early diagnosis of tuberculous polyneuropathy is essential for prompt management, in order to avoid long-term sequela. The present study proposes a subjective peripheral neuropathy rating scale to quickly diagnose polyneuropathy, and assess its severity. In addition, since early, combined, regular, appropriate, and full-course anti-tuberculosis treatment is important for these patients, the investigators proposed a flowchart to evaluate and treat polyneuropathy in patients with tuberculosis (Fig. 3). Careful physical examination, and necessary laboratory and imaging studies should be performed. A differential diagnosis should be considered to rule out other etiologies. Treatments (such as vitamin B6 supplementation in isoniazid administration) and close follow-ups should be conducted.

Conclusions

Polyneuropathy is a rare but serious complication in patients with tuberculosis. Affected patients can have atypical presentations. Clinicians should apply careful physical examination, necessary laboratory and imaging studies, appropriate treatments, and close follow-up re-evaluations to avoid long-term sequelae. Its underling mechanism and best treatment strategies still require further investigation.
