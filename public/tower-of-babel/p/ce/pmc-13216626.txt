A physic-guided YOLO framework for pavement deformation distress detection
Authors: Dorna Sheikholeslami, Amir Golroo, Ali Khodaii, Arya Daneshvar, Fereidoon Moghadas Nejad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This research develops an automated methodology for detecting and assessing deformation pavement distress such as rutting and corrugation, using deep learning algorithms. Utilizing a dataset of road images, various YOLO algorithms were initially explored and YOLOv5 achieved the best results. The initial YOLOv5 model demonstrated strong performance in rutting detection, achieving a 96.3% mAP for high severity, and 81.9% mAP for low severity. Due to limited corrugation instances, a dedicated YOLOv5 model was developed using an auto-annotation method, which achieved a 66.5% mAP. To enhance prediction reliability, a Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) approach was integrated into the YOLO framework by introducing a physics-informed penalty into its loss function. The hybrid PINN-YOLO model statistically improved bounding box accuracy and reduced false positives for both rutting and corrugation, yielding superior mAP results compared to the baseline YOLOv5 models. These results highlight the substantial potential of hybrid PINN-YOLO, offering a robust and accurate solution for pavement distress evaluation.

Introduction

Road pavement is essential to transportation infrastructure, requiring effective management to ensure safety and economic efficiency. Pavements deteriorate due to traffic, environment, and aging1. Pavement Management Systems (PMS) rely on accurate condition assessment for maintenance decisions. While data collection has evolved from time-consuming and subjective traditional methods to automated techniques2, data analysis has advanced through machine learning and, more recently, deep learning for distress detection and classification3–5.

A promising development is the integration of Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), which combine data-driven models with physical principles of pavement behavior to improve the accuracy and interpretability of distress evaluation. This research specifically targets the automated detection of impactful deformation distresses (rutting and corrugation) using deep learning on 2D pavement images. The proposed methodology evaluates models with metrics like precision, recall, F1-score, and mAP, aiming to integrate physics-based constraints via PINNs to develop a robust and accurate detection system.

In summary, existing deep learning–based pavement distress detectors largely focus on cracks and potholes, treat deformation distresses as a single broad category, and seldom consider severity assessment or detecting deformation distresses. Literature review

Deep learning-based object detection for pavement evaluation has evolved through two primary architectures. Two-stage models, such as Faster R-CNN, employ a region proposal network for high accuracy but at greater computational cost, while one-stage models like the YOLO series prioritize speed by performing detection in a single pass6. This technical foundation has enabled widespread application in pavement distress detection, with research demonstrating the effectiveness of models like SSD7, Faster R-CNN8, and various YOLO iterations for identifying cracks, potholes, and other surface defects9–12.

The field shows a clear trajectory toward optimizing one-stage detectors for better accuracy and efficiency, as seen in evaluations of YOLOv4, YOLOv56,13, and enhanced versions like YOLOv814,15. Recent work also explores richer datasets, including multi-sensor benchmarks combining 2D and 3D data for improved detection and severity quantification16. For specific deformation issues like corrugation, specialized classifiers have been developed17, and early attempts to model rutting progression have used neural networks, albeit with noted limitations in training validity18.

A pivotal and transformative shift is the move from purely data-driven models toward physics-informed learning. Emerging research integrates the physical principles of pavement mechanics directly into neural networks. Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) and related frameworks are now being applied to forecast critical performance indicators like rutting19, fatigue life14,15, and roughness (IRI)20,21, and even to model macroscopic deterioration behavior22. These models enforce physical consistency through constrained loss functions, significantly improving prediction stability, accuracy, and interpretability compared to conventional data-driven models.

A critical analysis of the literature reveals a distinct separation between two robust streams of research: one focused on high-resolution visual detection of distresses using object detection models, and another on physics-informed numerical prediction of pavement performance. While object detection studies excel at locating and classifying distresses like rutting in images12,13, they typically lack embedded physical understanding of the deformation mechanisms. Conversely, pioneering PINN studies19,20 excel at producing physically consistent predictions but are not designed for pixel-level identification and localization from image data.

Therefore, a significant gap exists in the development of a unified framework that integrates the precise, image-based detection capabilities of advanced one-stage object detectors with the principled, mechanism-aware reasoning of physics-informed learning for pavement deformation distresses. This study directly addresses this gap. It proposes an automated methodology for detecting rutting and corrugation that not only leverages state-of-the-art deep learning for visual identification but also explores the novel integration of physics-based constraints to ground the model’s predictions in the known mechanical behavior of pavement deformation, thereby aiming to bridge the current divide between detection and physical interpretation.

Research objective and scope

This research aimed to develop a robust and efficient deep learning-based approach for the automated detection and severity assessment of rutting and corrugation, two critical pavement distresses. The study focused on analyzing images collected from Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) lanes in Tehran, targeting two levels of distress severity: Low and High. To achieve this goal, various one-stage (YOLO) and two-stage (Faster R-CNN) deep learning algorithms were explored and optimized. This research also investigates the potential integration of Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) modeling framework in the evaluation of these two pavement distresses. By proposing this framework, we aim to enhance the predictive capabilities of our models. Model performance was assessed using precision, recall, F1-score, and mean Average Precision (mAP) to identify the most effective model for accurate and timely detection of pavement distress.

Research methodology

This study introduces a physics-guided YOLO framework that embeds domain-specific geometric constraints derived from the physical morphology of rutting and corrugation into the loss function. Unlike prior works that rely solely on data-driven learning, the proposed method enforces physically plausible bounding-box shapes and sizes, reducing false positives and improving localization accuracy. Furthermore, this work presents one of the first deep-learning-based detection frameworks for corrugation, a distress type that has received minimal attention due to its rarity and annotation difficulty. Accurate pavement distress detection and classification required the collection and processing of surface data using efficient models. Required images were collected from pavement surfaces using a camera mounted on a vehicle. After data collection, pre-processing techniques were applied, including adjusting brightness and removing unwanted areas (masking). Then, the desired distresses were manually annotated in the 20,000 images using annotation software tools. The outputs from the annotation process, including images and data about the annotated boxes, served as input for the training model. To accelerate the main training process, various one-stage and two-stage algorithms were trained and evaluated to select the most effective algorithm. After that, the initial trained model with appropriate accuracy utilized for auto-annotation of unlabeled images and increase the dataset size. The model’s performance was evaluated using standard classification metrics, and the best-performing model was selected. Finally, a novel modeling framework was utilized to improve the robustness and accuracy of the final model and the performance of this hybrid model was evaluated. Data collection

Data collection was conducted using the Road Surface Profiler. This system utilized a camera with a 1600 × 1200-pixel (2 megapixel) resolution and a 30 frames per second (fps) frame rate. The camera was mounted at a height of approximately 1.7 m above the pavement surface, positioned on the roof of the survey vehicle. To ensure clear and stable images, the camera was equipped with a vibration controller, particularly important when capturing data at high speeds on uneven road surfaces. This camera was protected under the Ingress Protection 67 (IP67) protocol, insulating it against temperature and humidity. This ensured that it can capture high-quality images even in harsh field conditions. Mounted on the vehicle’s roof, the camera captured images of 365 km of Tehran’s Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) lanes pavement at 5-m intervals, resulting in a collection of approximately 70,000 images. Tehran’s BRT was chosen for data collection due to the high occurrence of rutting and corrugation under the heavy weight and frequent passage of bus wheels, making it an ideal case study for this research.

To ensure consistent and reliable labeling, we implemented a standardized image acquisition and preprocessing protocol. Specifically, all distress annotations were confined to a fixed 5-m-wide mask at the bottom of each image, corresponding to the closest portion of the pavement to the camera (approximately 0–5 m ahead of the vehicle). This approach eliminates significant perspective distortion, as all labeled distresses are captured at a nearly uniform distance and scale, preventing a severe rut from appearing minor due to distance or a small rut from being exaggerated. The camera mounting height and angle were fixed (approximately 1.7 m above ground, angled downward at 30°) to consistently cover more than one full lane width (~ 5 m), ensuring comprehensive spatial representation without scale variation across the frame. Regarding vehicle speed, the camera operated at 30 frames per second (fps), enabling one image per meter even at 100 kph—far exceeding our sampling requirement of one image every 5 m.

Data preparation

Image pre-processing

Image pre-processing prepared the data for the annotation process and improved the model’s performance to detect pavement distresses. Image pre-processing in this research involved two stages. First, a Python code was employed to adjust the brightness of low-light images. Limited lighting conditions due to the camera’s position and structures such as tunnels can obscure distress in some images. The Python code selectively enhanced the brightness of low-light images, preserving well-lit images while improving the visibility of distress in darker areas. The second step focused on removing irrelevant background elements, such as buildings, pedestrians, and sky. To achieve this, a black mask was applied to the image beyond a 5-m distance from the camera. This was necessary because the resolution of images decreased significantly beyond this distance, resulting in reduced clarity and lower annotation accuracy. In addition, since the images were collected in a perspective way, only distress within the front 5 m of the captured view was identifiable with high accuracy. Consequently, this process helped the model focus on the central 3 m wide by 5 m long area in front of the photo. Image annotation

Following image pre-processing, manual annotation was performed by defining specific distress characteristics within the images, which are then used as input data for deep learning models. In this research, 18,329 images from the collected dataset were manually annotated using Label Studio software. Experts used annotation software to identify and assess the severity of pavement distresses within the images. For each type of pavement distress (rutting and corrugation), two severity classes (high and low) were defined and annotated on the images using rectangular bounding boxes. This resulted in four annotation classes: corrugation-high, corrugation-low, rutting-high, and rutting-low. The severity classification for rutting was performed by certified pavement engineers based on the local road management agency’s standard assessment criteria, which correlates severity to measured rut depth (RD). Model training

Algorithm selection

A pre-trained algorithm was implemented to expedite the modeling process. To identify the most suitable algorithm, eight single-stage algorithms, including YOLOv5, YOLOv6, YOLOv7, YOLOv8, YOLOv9, YOLOv10, YOLOv11, and YOLOv12 and one two-stage algorithm, Faster R-CNN were assessed. The algorithm demonstrating the highest effectiveness in detecting rutting and corrugation was selected for subsequent stages of the research. All YOLO models were initialized with weights pretrained on the COCO (Common Objects in Context) dataset, a large-scale object detection benchmark with 80 classes and over 330,000 images, which provides robust feature extraction capabilities for transfer learning. Ultimately, YOLOv5 achieved the best results and was used for the remainder of the study. Building upon the superior performance of the YOLOv5 architecture in detecting rutting and corrugation, this study introduces a Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) approach by integrating a physics-informed penalty term into the standard YOLOv5 loss function.

Initial dataset

Following data annotation of 18,329 images, 14,000 rutting instances and 164 corrugation instances (all severity types) were identified. This number of rutting instances was sufficient to train a high-accuracy model. However, with only 164 corrugation instances (less than 1% of the images), it was needed to collect more data to improve the model’s performance for corrugation detection.

The initial dataset was used for training the initial model, containing both rutting and corrugation distresses, each with low and high severity levels. Due to the limited number of corrugation instances, there was a class imbalance that negatively impacted the model’s performance. To address this issue, an auto-annotation method (using the initial model) was implemented to increase the number of corrugation instances and achieve better class balance.

Auto-annotation process

Auto-annotation process involves using a trained model to predict labels for new data points, reducing the need for manual annotation. Faced with the limitations of time-intensive manual annotation and the availability of new data, an auto-annotation method was employed to enhance the initial model’s performance.

To begin, a YOLOv5 model using 182 manually labeled photos was created. As recommended by YOLOv5 developers, allocating approximately 10% of the entire dataset to background images has been shown to improve the performance of the model23. According to this point, this dataset was comprised of 164 images (containing 164 corrugation instances) and 18 background images. Using the trained model based on these 182 images, 40,000 new images of Tehran’s BRT lanes were automatically annotated. Out of 1961 images labeled with corrugation using this method, an expert re-review identified 188 cases as correctly labeled. The expert re-review was conducted by certified pavement engineers with experience in PMS assessments, ensuring domain expertise in identifying distresses like corrugation. The two engineers independently reviewed the auto-annotated proposals. The final set of 188 correctly labeled images was retained only if there was 100% agreement between the two raters on the distress classification and bounding box placement. Any instance where the two raters disagreed was discarded as unreliable. This strict protocol ensured the highest possible quality for the small and crucial corrugation dataset.

Secondary dataset

After auto-annotation, 188 expert-reviewed images were added to the 164 primary images, creating a dataset of 352 images containing corrugation instances. Following the recommendation of the Yolo developer, a secondary dataset of 400 images was created for model training. This dataset included 352 images with corrugation labels and 48 background images. To prevent the new model from replicating previous errors in detecting corrugation, this dataset included background images that were initially mislabeled as containing corrugation during auto-annotation. These images were identified through manual review and confirmed to be without any distress.

Computational setup

The models were trained using the PyTorch framework on a system equipped with an NVIDIA V100s GPU, Intel® Xeon® Gold 6248R CPU, 64 GB DDR4 RAM, and 500 GB disk size. The average training time per epoch for the baseline YOLOv5 model was approximately 80 s. The PINN loss added an overhead of about 20% to the total training time per epoch compared to the baseline YOLOv5. PINN penalty thresholds (above values) were determined empirically from the training dataset (e.g., observed rut geometries in annotated images) and aligned with established engineering guidelines, domain knowledge from pavement engineering literature, which outlines typical dimensions of rutting and corrugation features. These constraints were derived from the physical morphology of rutting and corrugation as described in ASTM D6433 and supported by empirical field measurements. Rutting typically manifests as a longitudinal depression with a high length‑to‑width ratio and shallow deformation, while corrugation appears as a series of periodic, laterally confined undulations. These deformation patterns arise from load‑induced shear flow and material instability in asphalt layers. Although full mechanistic variables (e.g., stress–strain fields) cannot be extracted from 2D imagery, their geometric consequences are consistently observable. Therefore, bounding‑box aspect ratio and area serve as physically meaningful proxies that constrain the detector to shapes consistent with real pavement deformation, preventing implausible predictions and improving localization realism.

The original YOLOv5 loss is:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{YOLO} = L_{box} + L_{cls} + L_{obj}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{box}$$\end{document} is bounding box regression loss function, which quantifies the difference between the predicted bounding boxes and the ground-truth bounding boxes. YOLOv5 typically uses Complete Intersection over Union (CIoU) loss for this purpose.

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{cls}$$\end{document} is classification loss function, which assesses how accurately the model predicts the object class within each bounding box. For multi-class object detection, Cross-Entropy (CE) loss is used to measure the error in classifying the detected objects into their respective categories.

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{obj}$$\end{document} is object confidence loss function, which measures the model’s confidence in the presence of an object within each predicted bounding box. It uses Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss to determine whether a grid cell contains an object or not.

The novel \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{Physics}$$\end{document} term is defined to enforce the geometric constraints specific to deformation distresses (rutting/corrugation) and is mathematically expressed as:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{Physics } = L_{Aspect Ratio} + L_{Area}$$\end{document}

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{Aspect Ratio}$$\end{document} is the loss function related to aspect ratio constraint. For each matched prediction with pixel width (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w}_{i}$$\end{document}), height (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${h}_{i}$$\end{document}), class (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c}_{i}$$\end{document}), and aspect ratio (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}_{i}=\frac{{w}_{i}}{{h}_{i}}$$\end{document}), given class-specific limits \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[{r}_{min}^{(c)},{r}_{max}^{(c)}]$$\end{document}, the loss function term is defined as:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{Aspect Ratio} = \frac{1}{N}\mathop \sum \limits_{i = 1}^{N} \left( {\max \left( {0,r_{min}^{{\left( {c_{i} } \right)}} - r_{i} } \right) + {\mathrm{max}}\left( {0,r_{i} - r_{max}^{{\left( {c_{i} } \right)}} } \right)} \right)$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}_{min}^{\left({c}_{i}\right)}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}_{max}^{\left({c}_{i}\right)}$$\end{document} are lower and upper aspect ratio limits for class \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c$$\end{document}.

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{Area}$$\end{document} is the loss function related to area constraint (in pixels). For the pixel area \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${A}_{i}={w}_{i}{h}_{i}$$\end{document}, given class-specific pixel bounds \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[{A}_{min}^{\left(c\right)},{A}_{max}^{\left(c\right)}]$$\end{document}, the loss function term is defined as:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{Area} = \frac{1}{N}\mathop \sum \limits_{i = 1}^{N} \left( {\max \left( {0,A_{min}^{{\left( {c_{i} } \right)}} - A_{i} } \right) + {\mathrm{max}}\left( {0,A_{i} - A_{max}^{{\left( {c_{i} } \right)}} } \right)} \right)$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${A}_{min}^{\left({c}_{i}\right)}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${A}_{max}^{\left({c}_{i}\right)}$$\end{document} are lower and upper pixel area limits for class \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c$$\end{document}.

The hyperparameter λ controls the weight of the physics-informed penalty, allowing for fine-tuning the influence of the physical constraints on the overall training process. Validation of the final models on the test dataset

Once the final YOLOv5 models were selected, they were tested on the unseen test dataset. This provided a reliable estimate of how well the model would perform on real-world data. Evaluating on new data ensures the model doesn’t just memorize the training data but can learn and apply what it learned to new situations. The test dataset hasn’t influenced the model, so the evaluation is more objective.

Generally, the precision-confidence curve shows an upward trend. This means as the model’s confidence threshold for a prediction increases, the precision also increases. Conversely, the recall-confidence curve typically trends downward. This indicates that stricter confidence thresholds decrease the number of true positives identified (recall).

Initially, with a very low confidence threshold (close to zero), the model might predict bounding boxes for almost everything in the image. This might lead to 100% recall, meaning it identifies all possible instances (including some false positives). However, since the model predicts distress even in images without it, the precision at this point would be zero. As the confidence threshold increases, the model becomes more selective. It only predicts bounding boxes for which it has high confidence (closer to 100%). This can lead to 100% precision for images without distress. However, the trade-off is that the model might miss some actual instances of distress, resulting in lower recall. Figure 11 demonstrated this expected behavior for both the overall model and both rutting severity levels (low and high). However, due to the limited number of corrugation instances in the test dataset, the corrugation results deviated from this trend.

Limitations

A key limitation of this study is the reliance on visually interpreted labels assigned by pavement engineering experts. Although the annotators were certified pavement engineers with extensive field experience, the classification of distress severity from 2D images inevitably involves subjective judgment. This subjectivity is especially relevant for borderline cases between low and high severity, where visual cues such as shadow intensity or deformation extent may be ambiguous. As a result, human bias may influence the training labels and affect the model’s accuracy and generalizability.

Additionally, the absence of a comprehensive ablation study poses another limitation due to computational constraints. While qualitative geometric improvements and physics-aligned sensitivity analyses provide strong evidence of the reliable performance of the physics-guided YOLOv5 model, conducting a full ablation study to isolate the physics-informed term from other regularization effects is strongly recommended for future work. This would allow for a definitive quantification of its independent contribution.

Furthermore, data scarcity for certain distress types, such as corrugation, presents another challenge, particularly given the dataset’s specificity to urban asphalt BRT lanes in Tehran. This dataset reflects particular pavement materials (asphalt) and traffic conditions (heavy bus loads), which may limit its generalizability to other road types, such as concrete highways or rural roads with varied surfaces, as well as to different environmental conditions (e.g., rain or low light). Future research should explore synthetic data generation to augment rare classes like Corrugation-Low, employ domain adaptation techniques to enhance robustness across diverse pavements and conditions, and consider integrating 3D sensors for direct geometry inference.

Moreover, the geographic and environmental scope of the dataset imposes an additional limitation. Since all images were collected from asphalt BRT corridors in Tehran under relatively consistent lighting and weather conditions, the model has not yet been evaluated on other pavement materials (e.g., concrete, composite, gravel) or under more challenging scenarios such as wet surfaces, nighttime imagery, or strong shadows. This restricts the generalizability of the findings to broader roadway networks. Expanding the dataset to include diverse pavement types, climatic conditions, and illumination environments is therefore essential for validating the robustness and transferability of the proposed framework.

Future work

To overcome the subjectivity inherent in visual severity labeling, future research should prioritize:Integration of quantitative ground-truth measurements as training targets or validation references, such as laser profilometer-derived rut depth (mm) for rutting and profilometer-measured wavelength/amplitude for corrugation severity.Adoption of rigorous, standardized labeling protocols including multiple independent annotators, calculation of inter-rater reliability metrics, and adjudication procedures for discrepant cases.Application of explainable AI (XAI) techniques—such as Grad-CAM, SHAP, or attention visualization—to interpret model decisions on ambiguous samples, thereby increasing trust and enabling diagnosis of failure modes related to visual cues (shadows, texture, perspective).Expansion of the dataset to include diverse pavement materials (e.g., concrete, composite, gravel), roadway environments, and environmental conditions such as wet surfaces, nighttime imagery, and strong shadowing. Broader cross‑network validation will be essential for assessing the generalizability and robustness of the proposed framework. Future studies may also explore domain adaptation or transfer learning techniques to improve performance when deploying the model across different regions and pavement types.

Conclusion

Maintaining road pavement integrity is critical for transportation safety and efficiency, yet manual distress evaluation remains time-consuming and subjective. While automated methods using deep learning have shown promise, challenges persist in accurately detecting complex deformational distresses like corrugation from 2D images and ensuring physically plausible predictions. This study addresses these gaps by (1) evaluating YOLO architectures for rutting and corrugation detection, (2) developing a dedicated corrugation detection model to overcome data limitations, and (3) integrating physics-based constraints via a novel PINN-YOLO hybrid to enhance detection reliability. The following findings have been achieved in this study:YOLOv5 outperformed other models (e.g., Faster R-CNN), achieving 96.3% mAP for high-severity rutting.Corrugation detection improved by 20% recall when excluding severity classification due to 2D data limitations.Physics-guided-YOLO hybrid increased accuracy by embedding physical constraints (e.g., aspect ratios), reducing false positives.Demonstrated the feasibility of physics-guided deep learning for pavement assessment.
