Evaluating the effect of SNPs located on some biofilm genes on their protein function and structure in Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Klebsiella pneumonia isolates
Authors: Khamaael Mohammed Fayyadh, Mohammad Abdulrahman Al-Maeni, Shaymaa Fouad Rasheed Al-Khazraji, Omar Abdulrahman Mohammed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To evaluate the impact of biofilm-associated SNPs (previously identified) on protein structure and function, we employed a suite of bioinformatics tools. SIFT, Mupro, INPS-3D, and NCBI Conserved Domain Search were utilized to analyze changes in function, stability, secondary structure, phi/psi angles, and Relative Solvent Accessibility (RSA). Furthermore, SwissDock and molecular dynamics simulations were conducted to compare free energy, RMSF, polarity, flexibility, and molecular contacts between wild-type and mutant proteins. In silico analysis indicated that mutations in quorum-sensing (lasI, rhlI) and biofilm-associated genes significantly impact bacterial biofilm formation. Specifically, SIFT and Mupro predicted that lasI/rhlI mutations D39N (0.01/0.2) and D44N (0.5) impair signaling molecule synthesis. Furthermore, the S32N substitution in ndvB was predicted by INPS-3D to alter secondary structure (β-sheet to coil) and torsion angles (ϕ: −99 to 118; ψ: −94 to 5), likely disrupting protein function. Additionally, the R292A mutation in tssc1 was predicted to impact protein stability (SIFT: 0.01; Mupro: −0.9) and increase burial (RSA 37% to 23%, helix to coil). A frame-shift mutation in tssc1 was also identified, suggesting further modulation of biofilm production. Regarding with docking results, D44N in the rhII gene showed a slight change ΔΔG values from −7 to −7.2 and a change in polarity from 16 to 14, however, molecular dynamic simulation showed significant increase in RSMF value from 1.5 to 3.5 and increase the flexibility in mutant compared with wild type which reflects the importance of this SNP in changing the function of the protein mediating biofilm formation.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa, as well as Klebsiella pneumonia recorded as pathogens with opportunistic infection, associated with many diseases such as cystic fibrosis, sepsis, and urinary tract infection.1, 2, 3 Mutation and horizontal genetic transfer are tools that are used by Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Klebsiella pneumonia to diversify their genome and acquire resistance to antimicrobial agents and causing sever infection.1, 2 In addition to their ability to diversify their genomes,3 Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Klebsiella pneumonia are able to form biofilm as a strategy, which helps to escape the immune system of the host and other killing environmental factors. Biofilm, as a sheath of exopolysaccharide with 90% while other substances are protein and DNA, increases the ability of bacteria to cause infection in hospitals (nosocomial infection) as well as inside the host.4, 5 The colonies of bacteria inside the biofilm possess a high rate of horizontal genetic transfer; therefore, they become multidrug resistant, as seen in Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Klebsiella pneumonia.6 Genes mediated biofilm construction are correlated with quorum Sensing, fimbriae, polysaccharides, and efflux pump.7 Quorum Sensing is a chemical signal that is produced by the community of bacterial cells to regulate different biological activities, mainly the biofilm formation. These signals are called autoinducers. In Klebsiella pneumonia, one of the quorum-sensing genes is the luxS gene, which plays a vital role in biofilm construction.8, 9, 10 In Pseudomonas aeruginosa, quorum-sensing signaling pathways are controlled by many genes, mainly lasI and rhII genes. These genes produce autoinducers that contribute in biofilm initiation as well as biofilm maturation. Autoinducer production is correlated with the density of bacterial colonies, by that their concentration is low when bacterial colonies are low. High amount of autoinducers production when colonies of bacteria are high, therefore these autoinducers can control gene expression for different genes as seen in biofilm formation stages.11, 12 lasI and rhII genes produce two types of autoinducers (Acyl homoserine lactones) that intact with their receptors to participate in biofilm formation stages.13, 14 Another gene that contributes to biofilm formation is the ndvB gene, this gene encodes to glucosyltransferase that participates in the formation of glycans that serve as a Key element in making a sheath around bacterial colonies, hence biofilm construction.15, 16, 17 It has been reported that the strain luck ndvB gene can still form biofilm, but it becomes sensitive to different antibiotics such as gentamicin and tobramycin as glucan sequesters different antibiotics and prevents them from penetrating the bacterial cells.18 TssC1 gene contributes to antibiotic resistance in the presence of biofilm. SNPs mediating biofilm formation are more crucial than other SNPs mediating other virulence genes in bacterial infection due to many reasons, mainly due to the function of biofilm as a barrier protecting bacterial cells from antimicrobial attack and immune response. In addition, SNPs mediating biofilm formation may affect traits in comparison with other SNPs, enabling the bacterium to persist for a long time in its host.19 All mentioned genes contribute directly or indirectly in biofilm formation; therefore, we selected them in our investigation. Many researchers focused on enhancing drug delivery using nanoparticles to treat different diseases related to microorganisms forming biofilm20, 21, 22, 23 while others used microbial extraction to fight against carcinoma.24 Many bioinformatics tools are used by researchers to predict the effect of SNPs in different genes on the function and structure of their protein, especially in humans. However, to date, no record has been announced for evaluating the SNPs' effect on protein structure and function in prokaryotes. Therefore, we intended to visualize the effect of SNPs located on genes mediating biofilm formation on the function and structure of their protein.

Material and methods

Isolates collection and DNA sequencing

Isolation of P. aeruginosa and K. pneumonia from different samples, with 20 isolates using different morphological and biochemical methods for each, was achieved. In addition, the sequencing was carried out on 6 out of 20 isolates (2 weak biofilm formers, 2 moderate biofilm formers, and 2 strong biofilm formers) as mentioned previously in our published papers.25, 26 SNPs were identified in isolates relying on Sanger sequencing technologies based on PCR product sequencing. SNPs that are located within biofilm genes were analyzed separately in both P. aeruginosa and K. pneumonia.

SIFT program

The idea of the SIFT program is to detect the SNP effect on protein function and structure, relying on the evolutionary aspect. The program started to make alignments for amino acids of protein from a large database hosted on different bioinformatics websites, then looked for the changed amino acid in a particular position in the alignment. Finally, the program calculates the score relying on the physical properties of amino acid change in the alignment (for example, a hydrophilic amino acid change to a hydrophobic amino acid in the position of alignment for the large database is scored low, which means this SNP affects protein function and structure). The program works on SNPs in humans using human databases, but there is the possibility of running the program on prokaryotic databases by submitting alignment from prokaryotic databases along with substitution. We got the database from https://pubmlst.org/organisms/pseudomonas-aeruginosa for a large number of isolates. The database was a DNA sequence with multifasta format that was converted to amino acid sequences through EMBOSS transeq then we submitted an aligned multifasta amino acid database to the SIFT program.27 For the SIFT program default parameters are as follows: scores less than 0.05 are predicted as affecting the function, while scores higher than 0.05 are predicted as not affecting the function of the protein.28

Mupro program

The program calculates the change in the stability of the protein by measuring ΔΔG value by subtracting Gibbs free energy from the mutant protein and the wild-type protein. The result with a negative value means a decrease in stability, while positive values mean an increase in stability. The amino acid sequence for the protein was submitted with substitution amino acids, and the program is available online at (http://mupro.proteomics.ics.uci.edu).29 For the Mupro program, default parameters are as follows: ΔΔG with a negative value indicates the protein is destabilized, while a positive value indicates the protein is stabilized with value should be more than 0.5.28

NetSurfP3 program

It calculates the difference in phi/psi angles along with protein secondary structure, disorder, and surface accessibility for substituted amino acids. The amino acid sequence for the protein was submitted for the mutant and wild-type proteins through (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/NetSurfP-3.0) 30. For NetSurfP3 program default parameters are as follows: change in values of phi/psi angles along with protein secondary structure, disorder, and surface accessibility for substituted amino acids between mutant and wild-type protein indicates the SNPs is affecting protein function. For surface accessibility, a value that is less than 25%, amino acids are considered buried. However, a value that is more than 25%, consider as exposed. For the disorder, a difference in percentage of less than 10% is a minor change, while a difference above this threshold is a major change. The changes in the phi/psi angles and shift in their values on a Ramachandran plot are generally considered a statistically significant indicator.

NCBI conserved domain databases (CDD program)

CDD program hosted in NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi) was used to predict the domain for amino acids sequence of a particular protein and E value was considered to look for the significant result with cutoff 1e −2. The boundaries of domain were predicted and the mutations were manually searched for their location within the domain boundaries.

Molecular docking

The Swiss docking website (https://www.swissdock.ch/) (AutoDock Vina version1.2.3) was used to carry out the simulation of molecular docking for mutant and wild-type proteins. First, the pdb structure of wild-type was taken from pdb database with PDBID (2WYD) (https://www.rcsb.org/) and then submitted to the Swiss docking website along with a specific ligand (which is meloxicam with compound CID 54677470), after that, the parameters were adjusted to cover the binding site region. Secondly, the mutant amino acid was submitted to the Swiss model (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/) to achieve homology modeling and predict the PDB structure of the mutant, after refinement of pdb structure relying on different criteria, which was submitted along with the ligand to the Swiss docking website. Before submitting receptor to the Swiss docking website, the water molecules were removed and hydrogen bonds were added. Furthermore, missing atom were repaired. Subsequently the grid box size was established to cover almost of protein focusing on biding site (30*30*30). The grid centers' XYZ coordinates was set with default (X = 15.5, Y = 20.1, Z = 5.2) and the docking process was achieved using the Genetic Algorithm (GA) (Exhaustiveness = 8, Number of Modes = 9, Energy Range = 3 kcal/mol). The receptor is rigid while ligand is fully flexible. The result was retrieved focusing on the change in free energy and root mean square deviation (RMSD) between mutant and wild-type proteins. RMSD value refers to the atoms of the mutant protein deviating from their wild-type protein structure (initial structure). Finally, Chimera 1.18 was used to visualize and annotate binding between ligand and protein, highlighting the bonds created between ligand and the binding site of the protein. Formation of different bonds that can be visualized and measured by Chimera between ligand and receptor indicates this ligand is specific for this receptor. In addition, the difference in other parameters such as ΔΔG, RMSD, and polarity values between wild and mutant protein through comparison of docking results reflect change in stability of protein. The default parameters for the ΔΔG should be equal or less than −5 and A change of more than 1–2 kcal/mol is considered relevant to altering binding affinity. The RMSD should be less than 2 and altering the polarity is reflecting the change in hydrogen bonding through comparison wild and muatnt models.31 Ligand (meloxicam with compound CID 54677470) was selected based on binding energy (strong binding energy from literature.32 Ligand structures were downloaded in SDF format from PubChem, and 3D conformers were generated using Open Babel. PDBQT files were prepared for AutoDock Vina, with Gasteiger partial charges and defined rotatable bonds.

Molecular dynamics simulation

The structural dynamics of [PDBID (2WYD) along with ligand] were investigated using the CABS-flex 2.0 web server (http://biocomp.chem.uw.edu.pl/CABSflex2) to simulate conformational fluctuation. The 2WYD structure was uploaded to Pymol to remove all hydrogen atoms and model missing residues. Secondary structure was automatically assigned by the server using the DSSP method, with coil regions allowing for greater flexibility. Simulations were performed using default distance restraints. These restraints are derived to maintain the stability of regular secondary structure elements (α-helices and β-sheets) while allowing flexibility in loop regions. To ensure adequate sampling of the conformational space, the simulations were run for [1000] cycles. Simulations were performed at a reduced temperature of 1.4. The number of cycles and the cycle interval between trajectories were set to 50. The CABS-flex pipeline generated a trajectory of 1000 models, which were subsequently clustered to produce 10 finals, representative cluster medoids. The results were analyzed in terms of Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) to identify flexible and rigid regions and through residue-residue contact maps to examine conserved intramolecular interactions.33

2 - lx gene in K. pneumonia

Investigation of the importance of variation in different genes mediating biofilm formation revealed that variation in the luxS gene in K. pneumonia was synonymous and detected in two positions at 262, thereby (T) changed to (G), and at position 358, thereby (C) changed (T), and their amino acids did not change. Therefore, these variations may have no role in biofilm construction, and we exclude them from our analysis.34Moreover, this may explain why the quantification of biofilm in P. aeruginosa isolates is overestimated in K. pneumonia isolates, as mentioned previously.

2 - lasI gene in P. aeruginosa

On the other hand, the variation in the lasI gene in P. aeruginosa isolate was determined with nonsynonymous changes resulting in R31K and D39N mutations, while variation at position 130 of the DNA sequence was synonymous and therefore was excluded from our analysis.25 SIFT program was used to show the importance of these nonsynonymous changes in changing the function and structure of protein coded by lasI gene, the result revealed that substitution at position 31 from K to R is predicted to not effect function of protein with a score of 0.34 while substitution at position 39 from D to N is predicted to effect function of protein with a score of 0.01. Further analysis was achieved to show the effect of these SNPs on protein stability, by which the Mupro program prediction for R31K showed decreased stability with a value of −1.6 which is considered a significant value, while D39N was correlated with increased stability with a value of 0.2 and is generally considered not a significant change. Although the D39N mutation was predicted using the Mupro program with no significant change in stability of the protein, a portion of the stability of the protein is increased; hence, the functionality of the protein increases as well. Therefore, this is the second piece of evidence indicating the D39N mutation is more effective on protein function compared with the R31K mutation. The INPS-3D program was used to predict the solubility, disorder, phi and psi, and secondary structure for each SNP. Results revealed that there was a slight increase in RSA from 34% to 36% which is generally not considered significant and a slight decrease in phi angle from −97 to −98, while other parameters were stable for R31K. Moreover, there was also a slight increase in RSA from 68% to 69% and other parameters were stable; however, phi and psi angles were increased from 53, 34 to 58, 40, respectively, for D39N. In spite of these changes in phi and psi angles is not considered highly significant but it may represent a moderate local structural adjustment in allowed regions of Ramachandran plot. The SNPs also could be predicted as affecting the protein structure and function in case it is located inside the domain (the part responsible for the protein structure and function). Both changes in amino acid sequences at R31K and D39N are located within the lasI superfamily domain.

3 - rhII gene in P. aeruginosa

The variation in the rhII gene in P. aeruginosa isolate was determined with a nonsynonymous change in the protein sequence as D44N. SIFT prediction declared that substitution at position 44 from D to N is predicted to be tolerated with a score of 0.82. Moreover, INPS-3D analysis showed that in most parameters with RSA and phi, psi angles were slightly changed, by which RSA increased from 67% to 81% and both those values with more than 25% consider as exposed. Therefore, there is no change from exposed to buried. Regarding with phi, psi angles, phi increased from 57 to 63, and psi also increased from 18 to 22 and is generally not considered a significant conformational change, while other parameters showed stability with no change in their values at all. However, the Mupro program predicted that the SNP affects the stability of the protein by increasing in stability with a value of 0.5 which is considered as significant change in stability of protein. SNP mediating D44N change was located within the domain.

4 - ndvB gene in P. aeruginosa

The variation in the ndvB gene in the P. aeruginosa isolate was detected with a nonsynonymous change in the protein sequence as S32N.35 SIFT prediction highlights that substitution at position 32 from S to N is predicted to be tolerated with a score of 0.46. Moreover, the Mupro program predicted that the SNP affects the stability of the protein by increasing in stability with a value of 0.2 and is generally not considered significant. Furthermore, INPS-3D analysis showed that most parameters were changed, by which RSA highly increased from 30% to 41% and both those values with more than 25% are considered as exposed. Therefore, there is no change from exposed to buried. However, phi and psi dramatically decreased from −99, 118 to −94, 5, this change led to a change predicted secondary structure of this particular SNP from B-sheet to coil, while disorder was stable. The variation was located within GH17 family domain.

5 - tssC1 gene in P. aeruginosa

Finally, the variation in the tssC1 gene in the P. aeruginosa isolate was detected with a nonsynonymous change in the protein sequence as R292A. SIFT prediction annotated that the substitution at position 292 from R to A is predicted to affect protein function with a score of 0.01. Moreover, the Mupro program predicted that the SNP affects the stability of the protein by decreasing in stability with a value of −0.9 and is generally considered significant. Furthermore, INPS-3D analysis revealed that most parameters were changed, by which RSA highly decreased from 37% to 23% and considered as significant as changing from exposed to buried. Moreover, phi angle decreased from −64 to −98, while psi dramatically increased from −41 to 22. This change led to a change predicted secondary structure of this particular SNP from helix to coil, while disorder was stable with the value of zero. The variation was located within the C0G3517 superfamily domain.

Discussion

Microbial community complexity and host interactions play a role in causing disease in patients with elevated harmful bacteria and decreased beneficial bacteria.38 However, SNPs detection within virulence genes may contribute mainly to the persistence of these pathogenic bacteria in their host and SNP-driven structural changes can influence pathogenic behavior.39 SNP detection can be an important issue if it leads to a change 3D structure of the protein and in turn leads to a change function of the protein. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate all SNPs detected in genes mediating biofilm formation in our previous work, as they change the structure and function of a protein that can permit changes in the capacity of bacteria to construct biofilm. Molecular docking and molecular dynamic simulation were performed using Meloxicam as ligand due to the effect of Meloxicam in enhancing downregulation for quorum-sensing genes in P. aeruginosa and can binds with active sites of P. aeruginosa QS transcriptional regulator proteins as proven by molecular docking studies.32, 40 Meloxicam exhibits synergistic effects with different antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin and inhibits the production of different virulence factors like elastase.40 Starting with the luxS gene hosted in K. pneumonia, all the variations were synonymous, which means the protein encoded by the luxS gene does not change at all; therefore, this variation has no role in changing the capacity of bacteria to construct biofilm. However, Tang et al. 2024 showed that the deletion of the luxS gene from some isolates decreases the virulence and the ability of these strains to construct biofilm.41 Variation in the amino acid sequence was detected in two positions within the protein encoded by the lasI gene in P. aeruginosa isolate at R31K and D39N. These SNPs were located within isolates, forming moderate biofilm compared with non-biofilm producer as reference isolate [25]. Another study showed that mutations in lasI and rhII affect bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation.34 SIFT and Mupro programs predicted that the D39N change was important because it changes the function, as SIFT scored less than 0.05 and Mupro showed an increase in the stability of the protein with a value of 0.2, which serves as a quorum-sensing signal to stimulate the P. aeruginosa to form biofilm. In addition, The INPS-3D program predicted that the D39N mutation affects the phi and psi angles (phi and psi angles were elevated from 53, 34 to 58, 40, respectively). Although the effect was not significant, it may represent a moderate local structural adjustment in allowed regions of the Ramachandran plot. Furthermore, the variation was located within the lasI protein domain. On the other hand, the R31K change was less important in serving as a quorum-sensing signal to stimulate P. aeruginosa to form biofilm as predicted by SIFT with score 0.34, Mupro, and INPS-3D program. Mupro program predicted that the R31K change would significantly decrease the stability of the protein with −1.6 and may affect its functionality. In addition, INPS-3D program revealed no significant change in RSA from 34% to 36% and phi angle from −97 to −98, while other parameters were stable. Regarding with rhII gene, Variation in the amino acid sequence was detected in one position in P. aeruginosa isolate at D44N. This SNP was located within isolate, forming moderate biofilm compared with non-biofilm producer as reference isolate. This gene code for protein also serves as a quorum sensing signal to stimulate the P. aeruginosa to form a biofilm. For the D44N mutation, although SIFT scored higher than 0.05, which is tolerant, and INPS-3D programs showed there was no significant change in RSA (67% to 81%), phi (57 to 63), and psi (18 to 22) angles, and other parameters. In addition, the simulation of molecular docking showed slightly change in binding energy from −7 to −7.2 kcal/mol. Polarity is also decreased from 16 to 14. Bridging the gap between computational models and clinical results is a major hurdle in computational biology. Our structural and molecular dynamics analyses demonstrate that specific mutations (D44N) in (rhlI), (S32N) in (ndvB), and (R292A) in (tssc1)—drastically disrupt protein stability, structure, and active-site polarity, consistent with known clinical data.49 Furthermore, quorum-sensing networks like (lasI) and (rhlI) drive biofilm development. Mutations altering these enzymes disrupt autoinducer production, resulting in the defective biofilms observed in laboratory experiments.46 Regarding with clinical diagnosis, identifying these specific SNPs in clinical isolates could serve as rapid molecular markers to predict the virulence and biofilm-forming capacity of bacterial strains, allowing for tailored therapeutic interventions. Moreover, the mutant proteins (e.g., the highly flexible structure of the D44N rhlI mutant) can serve as targets for the rational design of quorum-sensing inhibitors (QSIs) or novel anti-biofilm agents. Our RMSF and (ΔΔG values) predictions provide clear targets for future in vitro site-directed mutagenesis and experimental functional assays (such as HPLC/LC-MS to measure autoinducer concentrations) to validate these computational models. However, this study serves as a hypothesis-generating workflow, prioritizing high-value nsSNPs for future experimental validation via site-directed mutagenesis and functional assays. In addition, the limitation of this study is the lack of evaluation of the effect of SNPs experimentally through measuring the change in biofilm quantification mediating genome variation. We entirely focused on prediction that some SNPs located within biofilm genes strongly affect biofilm formation. Another limitation in the current study is that predictions from different algorithms can sometimes be contradictory; therefore, analysis needs to be carried out using different programs. However, our analysis was carried out on the most famous programs, such as the Mupro program for detecting the stability of proteins.
