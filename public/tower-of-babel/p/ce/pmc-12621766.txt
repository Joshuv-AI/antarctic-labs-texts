Predictive value of aortoiliac calcification of deceased donors for chronicity score of allograft baseline biopsies and graft outcomes in kidney transplantation: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Young Ju Oh, Jongmin Sim, Heungman Jun, Myung-Gyu Kim, Cheol Woong Jung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe quality of kidneys from deceased donors is critical for successful kidney transplantation. Due to donor shortages, expanded criteria donors are increasingly used, raising concerns about posttransplant outcomes. This study evaluated the predictive value of donor aortoiliac calcification on graft outcomes to improve donor assessment and transplant planning.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed pretransplant non-contrast CT scans to classify donor aortoiliac calcification as non-to-mild or moderate-to-severe (MTS). Donor and recipient characteristics, time-zero biopsy findings, and graft outcomes were compared using chi-square tests and logistic regression.ResultsMTS donors were significantly older (58.2 ± 5.2 years vs. 49.7 ± 12.8 years, P = 0.002), had more diabetes mellitus (50.0% vs. 10.0%, P = 0.004), and higher Kidney Donor Profile Index (KDPI) scores (79.0 ± 15.2 vs. 59.4 ± 25.1, P = 0.001). Tubular atrophy (TA) was more frequent in the MTS group (81.8% vs. 46.7%, P = 0.022). Logistic regression showed donor diabetes mellitus (β = 0.496, P = 0.001) and Banff TA (β = 0.431, P = 0.003) were significant predictors of calcification.ConclusionAortoiliac calcification in deceased donors is associated with older donor age, higher KDPI scores, and increased incidences of diabetes mellitus and TA. Although it correlated with donor-related risk factors known to influence graft outcomes, it did not independently predict graft function or survival. Therefore, its role in donor selection appears limited and warrants further validation through larger prospective studies.

INTRODUCTION

Kidney transplantation (KT) is a vital intervention for patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), significantly improving their survival rate and quality of life [12]. Despite advancements in medical science, the demand for kidney transplants continues to exceed the supply of suitable donor kidneys. Over 40% of recovered kidneys are deemed unsuitable and subsequently discarded [3], necessitating greater reliance on expanded criteria donors (ECDs), including older donors and those with comorbidities. This situation introduces new hurdles for posttransplant outcomes and underscores the urgent need to better understand and predict factors that influence these outcomes.

To address these challenges, the Kidney Donor Profile Index (KDPI) has been developed as a comprehensive tool to predict transplant outcomes [3]. KDPI considers factors such as age, diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension (HTN), and cause of death, providing a score that reflects the donor profile’s favorability [34]. Higher KDPI scores indicating less favorable donor profiles are associated with poorer graft outcomes. Additionally, time-zero biopsy (TZB) offers crucial insights into a kidney’s condition before transplantation, helping identify potential issues that could affect graft performance and longevity [35].

While KDPI provides valuable insights into the quality of a kidney graft, it does not capture all factors influencing graft survival. In addition, it only has a moderate predictive power [6]. Moreover, it is limited in representing the chronicity of kidney grafts. Although TZB can partially represent chronic changes in the kidney graft, its results are not available at the kidney allocation stage, thereby providing no assistance in determining the usability of kidney grafts.

Vascular calcification is well known to be closely associated with CKD [78]. It can be readily assessed during donor work-up. Previous studies have extensively examined vascular calcification in recipients, identifying it as a significant predictor of adverse outcomes, including myocardial infarction and mortality [789]. However, research on vascular calcification in donors, particularly its impact on kidney function and graft outcomes, remains limited. This gap in research is critical, given that donor aortoiliac calcification could influence the success of a transplant.

This study aimed to assess the predictive value of vascular calcification in deceased donors using a CT scan-based scoring system and its correlations with kidney biopsy scores and graft outcomes.

METHODS

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Korea University Anam Hospital (No. 2023-AN-0001). Informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective study involved a comprehensive review of patients’ databases at Korea University Anam Hospital. We identified 147 in-house brain-death donors who underwent organ procurements between 2010 and 2020. Among these, 52 donors were selected based on the availability of pretransplant CT scans and TZB. Correspondingly, 52 recipients who received kidney grafts from these donors were included in this study.

Imaging and calcification scoring

Pretransplant non-contrast CT scans were utilized to assess the degree of aortoiliac calcification. The aortoiliac calcification score was assigned according to the method described by Lee et al. [10] (Fig. 1). This method involves evaluating the infrarenal aorta and common iliac arteries separately, with scores assigned based on the presence and extent of calcification. Based on the presence and extent of calcification, aortoiliac calcification scores were categorized into 2 groups: non-to-mild (NTM) and moderate-to-severe (MTS). A single vascular surgeon conducted all evaluations to ensure consistency. For the infrarenal aorta, a score of 0 indicated no calcification, a score of 1 indicated short calcification of 3 cm or less, and a score of 2 indicated diffuse calcification greater than 3 cm. Similarly, for the common iliac artery, a score of 0 indicated no calcification, a score of 1 indicated unilateral calcification, and a score of 2 indicated bilateral calcification (Fig. 2A). The total calcification score was the sum of individual scores from the infrarenal aorta and common iliac arteries. A total score of 3 or more was classified as MTS, while a score of less than 3 was classified as NTM (Table 1).

Pathological assessment

Chronicity scores of kidney grafts were evaluated using the Banff scoring system by a single pathologist. Specifically, the Banff scoring system provides a standardized approach to assessing kidney transplant biopsies, ensuring consistency and accuracy in evaluating graft health. For this study, only 2 key components of the Banff score were utilized: tubular atrophy (TA) and interstitial fibrosis (IF) (Table 2).

TA measures the extent of TA in the kidney graft. It is characterized by shrinkage and loss of tubular epithelial cells and a decrease in the size of the tubular lumen. TA is a critical indicator of chronic damage to the kidney graft. It is graded on a scale from 0 to 3, with a higher score indicating a more severe atrophy.

IF refers to the accumulation of fibrous tissue in the interstitial space of the kidney graft. This fibrosis impairs the kidney’s ability to function properly. It is a hallmark of chronic graft injury. Similar to TA, IF is graded on a scale from 0 to 3, with a higher score reflecting a more extensive fibrosis.

Scores for TA and IF were determined by examining kidney biopsy samples under a microscope following guidelines set by the Banff classification. These scores were then recorded and used as indicators of chronic graft injury.

Data collection and analysis

Data collection and analysis Baseline characteristics, including demographic information and clinical parameters of donors and recipients, were meticulously recorded. Key variables for donors included age at KT, sex, body mass index (BMI), presence of DM and HTN, cause of death, KDPI, kidney weight and size, and TZB findings. For recipients, key variables included age at KT, sex, KT waiting period, panel reactive antibody (PRA) levels, human leukocyte antigen (HLA) mismatches, and presence of donor-specific antibodies (DSA).

Statistical methods

Data are displayed as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and as number of patients and percentage for categorical variables. The chi-square statistic was used to assess significant differences between categorical variables. For the multivariate analysis, variables that were significant (P < 0.1) in the univariate logistic regression model were included in the multiple logistic regression model. This adjustment to a P-value threshold of 0.1 was made due to a relatively small sample size in this study, ensuring that potentially important variables were not excluded. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 24.0 (IBM Corp.).

RESULTS

Donor characteristics

Donors in the MTS group were significantly older than those in the NTM group (58.2 ± 5.2 years vs. 49.7 ± 12.8 years, P = 0.002). The incidence of DM was also significantly higher in the MTS group than in the NTM group (50.0% vs. 10.0%, P = 0.004). There was a trend towards higher BMI in the MTS group than in the NTM group (25.3 kg/m2 ± 4.5 vs. 23.0 ± 3.4 kg/m2, P = 0.069), although the difference did not reach statistical significance. Although HTN was more prevalent in the MTS group (50.0% vs. 23.3%, P = 0.089), the difference was not statistically significant. The KDPI was significantly higher in the MTS group (79.0 ± 15.2 vs. 59.4 ± 25.1, P = 0.001). There was no significant difference in kidney weight or size between the 2 groups (Table 3).

Time-zero biopsy

In TZB results, TA was significantly more common in the MTS group than in the NTM group (81.8% vs. 46.7%, P = 0.022), whereas IF was not significantly different between the 2 groups (3.3% vs. 9.1%, P = 0.567) (Table 3).

Recipient characteristics

Recipient characteristics were similar between the NTM and MTS groups. There was no significant difference in recipient age (52.5 ± 10.0 years vs. 54.2 ± 9.3 years, P = 0.539), sex distribution (63.6% vs. 66.7% male, P > 0.999), KT waiting period (1,423.1 ± 857.8 days vs. 17,71.4 ± 1,077.8 days, P = 0.101), prevalence of PRA (54.5% vs. 60.0%, P = 0.967), HLA mismatch (P = 0.159), or presence of DSA (P = 0.253) (Table 3).

Graft outcomes

Graft outcomes showed no significant differences between the NTM and MTS groups. Delayed graft function was observed in 13.6% of patients in both groups (P > 0.999). Rates of biopsy-proven acute rejection were similar (33.3% vs. 31.8%, P > 0.999). There was no significant difference in serum creatinine (sCr) level at 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 years post-KT (P = 0.680, 0.518, 0.519, 0.826, and 0.492, respectively). The estimated glomerular filtration rates (eGFRs) were also similar between the 2 groups at 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 years post-KT (P = 0.900, 0.932, 0.684, 0.591, and 0.656, respectively) (Table 3).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis

To explore factors associated with aortoiliac calcification in kidney transplant recipients, 2 multivariable logistic regression models were employed. Model A excluded KDPI but included donor DM and HTN. In this model, donor DM was significantly associated with increased calcification (β = 0.496, P = 0.001). Donor HTN was also significantly associated with increased calcification (β = 0.197, P = 0.003). However, Banff TA did not show a significant association with calcification (β = 0.431, P = 0.199) (Table 4). Model B included KDPI but excluded variables used in KDPI calculation (DM and HTN). In this model, KDPI was significantly associated with increased calcification (β = 0.009, P = 0.003). Donor BMI was also significantly associated with increased calcification (β = 0.017, P = 0.009). However, Banff TA did not show a significant association with calcification (β = 0.099, P = 0.490) (Table 5). These findings indicated that different factors were highlighted as significant predictors of calcification depending on whether KDPI was included in the model. While donor DM and HTN were significant in Model A, KDPI and donor BMI were significant in Model B, suggesting the importance of these factors in assessing aortoiliac calcification in deceased donors.

DISCUSSION

Previous studies have demonstrated that vascular calcification, particularly in the aortoiliac region, can have significant impacts on kidney transplant outcomes [910]. Vascular calcification is more prevalent in older individuals and those with metabolic disorders such as DM and HTN. It has been associated with increased risks of CKD and cardiovascular diseases [11112]. Given these findings, our study aimed to evaluate the specific impact of donor aortoiliac calcification on kidney transplant outcomes. This has been relatively unexplored compared to recipient calcification.

To address the challenges of selecting suitable donor kidneys, the KDPI, which considers various donor factors to predict transplant outcomes, has been developed as a comprehensive tool [3]. KDPI includes factors such as donor age, presence of DM or HTN, cause of death, and other comorbid conditions, providing a score that reflects the overall quality and suitability of the donor kidney [34]. Higher KDPI scores are associated with less favorable donor profiles and poorer graft outcomes. However, despite being an improved prediction tool from the ECD indicator, KDPI only has a moderate predictive power. In addition, it lacks the ability to differentiate kidneys with similar KDPI values at a high confidence [13]. Additionally, KDPI is solely based on pre-retrieval factors without accounting for chronic changes in kidney grafts, which can be partially represented by TZB findings. However, TZB results are not available at the kidney allocation stage, providing no assistance in determining the usability of kidney grafts.

While our initial objective was to evaluate whether donor aortoiliac calcification predicts graft outcomes, our findings did not demonstrate a direct correlation with graft function, rejection, or survival. Instead, the calcification score appeared to be more reflective of donor risk factors such as age, DM, and KDPI. In our study, we found a significant association between KDPI and the degree of aortoiliac calcification, suggesting that aortic calcification could be a useful surrogate marker for predicting graft outcomes. This finding addresses some limitations of KDPI by providing additional information that can help predict the chronicity of kidney grafts and their long-term function [13].

Kidney biopsy plays a crucial role in evaluating graft health by identifying chronic injury markers such as TA and IF. TA is characterized by the shrinkage and loss of tubular epithelial cells with a decrease in the size of the tubular lumen, indicating chronic damage to the kidney graft. IF, on the other hand, refers to the accumulation of fibrous tissue in the interstitial space, impairing the kidney’s ability to function properly and marking chronic graft injury. These markers are critical in understanding the extent of graft damage and predicting long-term outcomes. Although TA was more common in the MTS group, it did not retain statistical significance in multivariable logistic regression models. Therefore, its association with calcification should be interpreted with caution and not as independent evidence of predictive value.

Importantly, our primary outcome analyses did not reveal significant differences in graft function, rejection rates, or graft survival between the MTS and NTM groups. This indicates that donor aortoiliac calcification within the context of this study may not serve as a direct predictor of graft outcomes in the current cohort. Instead, the primary associations observed—such as with donor age, DM, and KDPI—highlight calcification as a possible surrogate for donor quality, rather than an independent determinant of graft performance.

Previous studies have suggested that systemic vascular calcification, including abdominal aortic calcification, may reflect underlying chronic vascular and parenchymal damage in the kidney, potentially contributing to TA or IF [11]. CKD has also been identified as a significant risk factor for vascular pathologies such as abdominal aortic aneurysm, further supporting the systemic link between renal dysfunction and vascular remodeling [14]. Although direct evidence in deceased donors is limited, these findings support the observed association between donor aortoiliac calcification and TA in our study.

The lack of significant differences in long-term graft functions, including eGFR and sCr levels, may be due to limited statistical power from the relatively small sample size. Therefore, the possibility of type II error should be considered when interpreting these negative findings. Despite initial concerns regarding vascular calcification, results suggested that age, KDPI, and the presence of DM and HTN were all closely related factors that could influence outcomes. Previous studies have identified a recipient’s vascular calcification as a predictor of lower survival rates and increased cardiovascular events in recipients [7891115]. However, our study specifically focused on donor aortoiliac calcification, which may reflect donor quality rather than directly predict graft function. By assessing aortoiliac calcification using donor’s non-contrast CT scans, we can potentially identify high-risk donors and tailor posttransplant care strategies accordingly. However, the present results suggest that such use should be limited to supplementary assessment rather than independent risk prediction. Given the absence of outcome differences, the clinical applicability of calcification scoring in donor selection remains uncertain. While it may provide supplementary information on donor vascular health, its predictive value for graft prognosis was not established in this study. Its impact on transplant outcomes should be further validated through additional large-scale prospective studies.

Future research should focus on prospective, multicenter studies to validate our findings and establish stronger evidence for the clinical utility of donor aortoiliac calcification assessment in KT. Incorporating artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning models to analyze pretransplant imaging and clinical data could further enhance the predictive accuracy of graft outcomes. Developing and validating these models could revolutionize the pretransplant evaluation process. Long-term follow-up studies are necessary to assess the impact of donor aortoiliac calcification on graft survival and recipient cardiovascular outcomes beyond the initial years posttransplant. Interventional studies aiming to reduce vascular calcification in potential donors or to manage high-risk recipients more effectively could provide actionable strategies to improve transplant outcomes. Additionally, one limitation of this study was the potential subjectivity in assessing calcification scores. The scoring process might vary depending on the evaluator’s experience and interpretation of CT images, which could introduce variability in results. Standardizing the calcification scoring methodology and utilizing automated systems or AI could help mitigate this limitation in future studies, further enhancing the reliability and clinical utility of aortoiliac calcification assessment in KT.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that aortoiliac calcification in deceased donors is associated with donor-related factors such as advanced age, DM, and higher KDPI, all of which are established determinants of graft outcomes. However, it was not independently associated with graft function or survival. Therefore, while calcification scoring may provide supplementary information during donor assessment, it should not yet be used as a stand-alone criterion for donor selection. Further large-scale, prospective studies are warranted to elucidate its potential role in clinical decision-making for deceased donor KT.
