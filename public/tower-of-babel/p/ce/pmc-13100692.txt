Distinct Effects of Maternal Stress and Exercise on Offspring Metabolic Health
Authors: Felix T. Yang, Joao V. Esteves, Lisa A. Baer, Kelsey M. Pinckard, Branden G. Verosky, Olivia A. Togliatti, Caden Burkhart, Sara A. Alzuhaili, Nickolai P. Seculov, Tamar L. Gur, Kristin I. Stanford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMaternal physical activity enhances offspring metabolic resilience, whereas prenatal psychosocial stress predisposes offspring to lifelong metabolic dysfunction. Whether maternal exercise can attenuate the deleterious programming effects of stress has remained unclear. Here, we investigated the effect of maternal exercise and/or prenatal psychosocial stress on the metabolic health of male and female offspring. C57BL/6 dams were assigned to four conditions: sedentary/non‐stressed (SED‐NS), sedentary/stressed (SED‐S), exercised/non‐stressed (EX‐NS), or exercised/stressed (EX‐S). Male EX‐NS offspring had improved glucose tolerance compared to SED‐NS and SED‐S; however these effects were blunted in male EX‐S offspring, indicating that prenatal stress abolished this exercise‐induced benefit. In contrast, there was no effect of maternal stress or exercise on glucose tolerance in female offspring. Maternal exercise chronically elevated basal corticosterone in male offspring, regardless of maternal stress. Maternal stress increased mineralocorticoid receptor (Nr3c2) mRNA and glucocorticoid receptor (NR3C1) protein expression in brown adipose tissue in male mice, while exercise restored NR3C1 expression. These findings indicate that prenatal stress blunts the metabolic benefits of maternal exercise in male offspring and is associated with altered corticosteroid signaling in brown adipose tissue. Together, the data identify a stress–exercise interaction that may influence offspring metabolic programming through tissue‐specific regulation of glucocorticoid pathways.

Introduction

Pregnancy is a critical window of fetal development that affects longitudinal health outcomes in offspring. Maternal psychosocial stress affects ~75% of women [1], and these stressors can include obesity, environmental pollutants, and psychosocial stress. Each stressor can lead to negative outcomes in offspring physiology [2, 3, 4]. Increased prenatal psychosocial stress increases the susceptibility of adult offspring to obesity and other metabolic disorders [5, 6], thus mitigating the effects of prenatal stress through therapeutic or lifestyle interventions could have important consequences toward long‐term maternal and offspring health.

Exercise is a well‐established lifestyle intervention known to mitigate the effects of psychosocial stress in adults and result in many positive health outcomes in clinical studies [7, 8]. Maternal exercise has beneficial effects on adult offspring, including improved metabolic and cardiovascular health [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. Additionally, a substantial body of work has established that maternal psychosocial stress during gestation programs long‐term metabolic dysfunction in offspring. Stress‐induced alterations in the fetal environment, including elevated glucocorticoid exposure, modify metabolic tissues and epigenetic pathways and can result in metabolic disturbances later in life. These findings have been consistently demonstrated in rodent models of in utero stress, where maternal stress alters pathways regulating glucose homeostasis, adipose tissue development, and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis function [15].

Despite extensive evidence that maternal exercise and prenatal stress independently influence offspring metabolic outcomes, whether these exposures interact during pregnancy remains poorly understood. Here, we tested the hypothesis that maternal exercise improves offspring metabolic health through corticosteroid‐dependent pathways and that concurrent prenatal stress disrupts these effects by altering glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid receptor signaling in metabolic tissues.

Methods

Animal Husbandry and Maternal Exercise and Maternal Stress Paradigms

All procedures were followed as approved by the IACUC at The Ohio State University. Six‐week‐old virgin C57BL/6 female mice (Charles River Laboratories) fed a standard chow diet (17% kcal/fat, Inotiv; 7912) and were divided into four groups: sedentary‐non‐stressed (SED‐NS), sedentary‐stressed (SED‐S), exercised‐non‐stressed (EX‐NS), and exercised‐stressed (EX‐S). Sedentary mice (placed in a static cage) or exercise‐trained mice (housed with running wheels for 2 weeks prior to conception and during gestation) [10, 11, 12, 13]. Female mice were paired individually with males for a period of three days in static cages and checked for vaginal plugs and weighed each morning. Upon confirmation of a vaginal plug, females were separated from males, maintained in static cages or placed back in wheel cages, and further subdivided into either the stress or control group. The morning of initial vaginal plug confirmation was designated as E0. Impregnated female mice were weighed daily. Successful pregnancy was confirmed by an increase in body weight by 2 g from E0 to E10. At E10, we commenced prenatal stress exposure. Pregnant mice were restrained in 50‐mL conical tubes, ventilated with holes throughout, for 2 h from 0900 to 1100. Upon completion of the daily stressor period, mice were released from the tubes and gently cleaned. Tubes were also cleaned and dried. The stressor continued for 7 consecutive days, up to E16 [16]. For the exercise training experiments, mice had access to a running wheel at all times and performed voluntary running (24 cm diameter; Nalgene). The total number of wheel cage revolutions was monitored daily, and the average running distance was calculated at the end of gestation using meters (Sigma). There were no differences in running distances and body weights among stressed and non‐stressed dams (Figure 1A,B). Litters were culled to six and weaned at 21 days, and offspring were maintained sedentary and on a chow diet.

Body Composition and Metabolic Testing

Body weight was recorded weekly (OHAUS NV212). EchoMRI LLC measured body composition (fat and lean mass). For glucose tolerance tests (GTT), mice were fasted overnight (12 h) with water available. Baseline glucose was obtained via tail‐vein prick (OneTouch Ultra2), followed by intraperitoneal glucose (2 g/kg). Glucose levels were measured at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min. Insulin tolerance tests (ITT) were conducted after a 2 h afternoon fast. Baseline glucose was measured as above, then insulin (1 U/kg) was given intraperitoneally, and glucose levels were measured at 10, 15, 30, 45, and 60 min, as described previously [10].

Western Blot and ELISAs

Tissues were homogenized in EBT buffer (with protease inhibitor) on a Qiagen TissueLyser, incubated 1 h at 4°C, and centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 15 min. Supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were measured by Bradford assay. Samples were diluted in Laemmli buffer, and 25 μg protein per lane was resolved on an 8% SDS‐PAGE gel. Proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, blocked in 5% milk (TBS/T) for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4°C with antibodies specific for Glucocorticoid Receptor/NR3C1 (1:1000, Cell Signaling, cat #12041), Mineralocorticoid Receptor/NR3C2 (1:100, rMR1‐18) 9C2 gifted from Professor Celso E. Gomez‐Sanchez, University of Mississippi Medical Center [17], β‐Tubulin (1:1000, Thermo Fisher, cat #MA5‐16308). After washing, membranes were incubated with secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature and visualized using ECL (Bio‐Rad ChemiDoc). Images were analyzed using ImageJ (NIH; https://imagej.net/ij/ij/). Serum insulin was measured using a commercial ELISA (Millipore, Cat. #EZRMI‐13 K), and corticosterone was assayed using the Cayman Chemical ELISA kit (No. 501320) (1:10 serum dilution), both following manufacturer protocols [18].

Real‐Time qPCR

Tissues were homogenized with Qiazol Lysis Reagen on the Qiagen TissueLyser for RNA extraction and cDNA was made with the Quantabio qScript cDNA Synthesis Kit following the manufacturer's instructions. Real‐time quantitative PCR reactions were performed using SYBR green detection (QuantaBio) or TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix protocol (Thermo Fisher). Gene expression data were normalized to the housekeeping gene Rpl7, b‐actin, or Rpl19 using the 2−ΔΔCt method. All primer sequences and TaqMan assays are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

Statistical Analysis

Scientific software GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for data visualization and statistical analysis. All datasets were first evaluated for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. When data met assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, parametric statistical tests were applied. Non‐normally distributed data were analyzed using appropriate nonparametric alternatives. For single‐factor comparisons among the four maternal treatment groups (SED‐NS, SED‐S, EX‐NS, EX‐S), one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test was used.

For outcomes involving two experimental factors (maternal exercise × maternal stress), two‐way ANOVA models were applied to assess main effects and interaction terms. Significant interactions were followed by Tukey post hoc testing. Litter, rather than individual offspring, was treated as the biological unit in all analyses.

Animal numbers (n) for each experiment are reported in the figure legends and reflect distinct litters. Sample size calculations were performed a priori based on effect sizes reported in prior maternal‐exercise programming studies, which indicated that detecting 20%–25% differences in glucose tolerance with α = 0.05 and power ≥ 0.8 required 5–8 liters per group. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results and Discussion

Beneficial Effects of Maternal Exercise on Metabolic Health Are Blunted by Maternal Stress in Male Offspring

To determine the effects of maternal exercise and maternal stress on offspring metabolic health, body weight, body composition, and glucose and insulin tolerance, tests were performed on male and female offspring at 8 weeks of age. This timepoint was selected because it corresponds to the established onset of physiological adulthood in mice. Male offspring from exercise‐trained dams tended to have reduced body weight at 8 weeks of age compared to offspring from sedentary dams (Figure S1A; p = 0.052) with no effect on fat mass or lean mass (Figure S1B,C). There was no effect of maternal stress on body weight in female offspring (Figure S1D). Maternal exercise or stress did not affect fat mass in female offspring at 8 weeks of age (Figure S1E) but maternal stress, in the absence of exercise, resulted in an increase in % lean mass in female offspring (Figure S1F).

Maternal exercise improved glucose tolerance in male offspring compared to SED‐NS offspring (Figure 1C). Interestingly, there was no effect of maternal stress to impair offspring glucose tolerance, but maternal stress blunted the beneficial effects of maternal exercise on glucose tolerance in male offspring (Figure 1C). There was no effect of maternal exercise or stress on insulin tolerance or fasting insulin in male offspring (Figure 1D,E). There was no effect of maternal exercise or stress on glucose tolerance, insulin tolerance, or fasting insulin on female offspring at 8 weeks of age (Figure 1F–H), and no effect of maternal stress or exercise on fasting glucose in male or female offspring (Figure S1G,H).

Exercise During Pregnancy Increases Offspring Corticosterone Levels

Because glucocorticoid signaling can reflect both adaptive and maladaptive physiological processes, we examined whether maternal exercise or prenatal stress altered basal corticosterone levels and corticosteroid receptor expression in offspring metabolic tissues. There was an overall effect of maternal exercise to increase basal corticosterone in male offspring, independent of maternal stress (Figure 2A). As glucocorticoids such as corticosterone regulate hepatic glucose metabolism [19], and considering that the corticosterone levels were altered in male offspring only, we investigated whether maternal exercise or stress altered hepatic expression of key glucose and mitochondrial metabolism transcripts in male offspring. With the exception of the Pdk4 gene, which was upregulated in EX‐S, there was no effect of maternal exercise or maternal stress in the regulation of genes involved in glucose or mitochondrial metabolism in the liver (Figure S2A,B).

Given that there were no changes in expression of hepatic glucose metabolism or mitochondrial genes, and that previous studies indicate that excessive corticosteroid signaling can impair BAT function [20], we measured the expression of glucocorticoid (Nr3c1) and mineralocorticoid (Nr3c2) receptor genes in BAT of male and female offspring. Maternal stress, regardless of maternal exercise, upregulated Nr3c2 mRNA in male offspring (Figure 2C). There is no effect of maternal stress or exercise on Nr3c1/2 mRNA in female offspring (Figure 2D). Additionally, maternal stress upregulated NR3C1 protein expression in male offspring (Figure 2E), whereas maternal exercise blunted this effect and did not alter NR3C1 protein expression by itself (Figure 2E). Additionally, neither maternal stress nor exercise affected NR3C2 (Figure 2F). With the exception of Pgc1a mRNA, which was upregulated in male offspring of the exercise group, there were no changes in glucose, fatty acid, and mitochondrial metabolism genes in the brown adipose tissue (Figure S2C,D). These results indicate that maternal stress and exercise distinctly affect corticosterone concentration and signaling.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that prenatal psychosocial stress negates the improvement in glucose tolerance conferred by maternal exercise in male offspring. While maternal exercise and prenatal stress have each been shown to influence offspring metabolic health, these findings indicate that stress exposure during gestation can modify the metabolic programming effects of exercise, revealing an interaction between these maternal factors.

Several previous studies, including work from our lab, have identified a role for maternal exercise to improve offspring cardiometabolic health through epigenetic and tissue‐specific mechanisms [10, 11, 14, 21, 22], with male offspring having a stronger metabolic responsiveness to maternal exercise [12, 13, 23, 24]. Similarly, studies of prenatal stress show that maternal stress elevates fetal glucocorticoids and disrupts HPA‐axis development [25, 26], increasing long‐term metabolic risk through endocrine and epigenetic pathways. Few studies have evaluated the combined impact of maternal exercise and stress. Here, we show that stress can negate a well‐established metabolic benefit of maternal exercise, providing insight into how psychosocial environments may limit the effectiveness of otherwise beneficial maternal exercise.

Glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids are compelling targets because both regulate energy balance and adipose tissue biology. Excess glucocorticoid signaling impairs BAT thermogenesis and disrupts systemic glucose metabolism [25, 26, 27]. Mineralocorticoid pathways regulate adipocyte remodeling, and antagonism of MR signaling can restore metabolic function in mice. These pathways provide a plausible explanation for how maternal stress and exercise may exert opposing effects on BAT and systemic metabolism.

Maternal stress upregulates NR3C1 and Nr3c2 expression in BAT, while exercise normalizes NR3C1, which is consistent with a model where maternal exercise preserves BAT corticosteroid receptor homeostasis in male offspring, maintaining metabolic resiliency that would otherwise be compromised by maternal prenatal stress. The absence of changes in genes involved in hepatic glucose or mitochondrial function in metabolism in males underscores the specificity of the BAT and supports future functional interrogation of BAT as a primary effector tissue [28].

The metabolic phenotype and associated changes in corticosteroid signaling were observed in male but not female offspring at 8 weeks of age, consistent with prior studies demonstrating sex‐specific responsiveness to maternal exercise and prenatal stress [12, 13, 23, 24]. Similarly, sexual dimorphism in corticosteroid signaling and adipose biology is well documented, including sex‐specific GR functions in adipose tissue that influence metabolic homeostasis [29]. These likely contribute to the sex‐specific phenotypes observed here, but further investigation will be needed to fully understand the mechanisms regulating these sex‐specific effects.

Several limitations should be noted. Offspring were assessed at a single early adult time point, corticosterone was measured under basal conditions, and direct functional interrogation of brown adipose tissue was not performed. In addition, while the study was powered for metabolic outcomes, some molecular analyses may benefit from larger sample sizes. These considerations define important directions for future studies aimed at establishing temporal dynamics and tissue‐specific mechanisms.

In summary, these findings indicate that prenatal stress can disrupt the beneficial effects of maternal exercise on offspring metabolic health and implicate corticosteroid receptor regulation in brown adipose tissue as a potential mediator of this interaction. This work provides a framework for understanding how psychosocial factors may modify exercise‐based interventions during pregnancy and highlights the importance of considering maternal stress context in studies of developmental metabolic programming.
