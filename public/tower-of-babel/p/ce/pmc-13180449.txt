Rurality and long-term outcomes after critical illness
Authors: Matthew F Mart, Megan T Jones, Rameela Raman, Kyle R Stinehart, Kirby P Mayer, James C Jackson, Pratik P Pandharipande, Han Su, Leanne M Boehm, E Wesley Ely, Nathan E Brummel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationaleHow rurality may influence recovery after critical illness is unknown.ObjectivesTo examine the association between rurality and disability, cognitive function, and health-related quality of life in intensive care unit survivors and whether it is modified by area-level socioeconomic deprivation.MethodsWe measured rurality using Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes (range: 1 [most urban] to 10 [most rural]). At 3 and 12 months postdischarge, we assessed cognition, basic and instrumental activities of daily living, and quality of life. We measured area-level socioeconomic deprivation using the Area Deprivation Index (ADI). We used multivariable regression with inverse probability of attrition weighting, adjusting for prespecified covariates, including ADI. We conducted prespecified analyses assessing the interaction between rurality and ADI.Measurements and Main ResultsWe enrolled 1040 critically ill patients, with 781 surviving to hospital discharge. Survivors had a median (IQR) age of 62 (52-71) years and RUCA score of 4 (1–7). At a 3-month follow-up, greater rurality was associated with greater odds of disability in basic activities of daily living (adjusted odds ratio, 2.16; 95% CI, 1.27-3.67; P = .02). This association remained significant at the 12-month follow-up (P = .03). We found no association between rurality and other outcomes. There was no interaction between RUCA and ADI with basic activities of daily living, suggesting that socioeconomic deprivation did not alter these associations.ConclusionsAmong survivors of critical illness, greater rurality was associated with greater disability in basic activities of daily living but not cognitive function or quality of life.Keywords: critical illness survivors, rural, activities of daily living, post–intensive care syndrome, cognitive impairment

Introduction

Despite declining mortality from critical illness,1 patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) who survive their hospitalization frequently experience long-term disability, cognitive impairment, and reduced quality of life.2-5 Survivors experience different trajectories of recovery after the ICU,6 yet the factors influencing these differences in outcomes after critical illness remain unclear.

Patients residing in rural areas face systemic health care disparities that may impact recovery. Among those without critical illness, the age-adjusted mortality rate in rural areas is 20% higher than in urban areas due to higher rates of chronic conditions such as heart disease, cancer, and chronic pulmonary conditions.7,8 Likewise, mortality rates due to acute conditions such as pneumonia, sepsis, and traumatic injury are greater.7,9,10 Socioeconomic factors may further exacerbate these differences.7,8

In addition, acute care provided in rural areas differs. Rural residents are less likely to receive physical or occupational ­therapy services while in the ICU.9,11 After hospitalization, rural sepsis survivors have significantly greater odds of seeking care in an emergency department, perhaps due to lower rates of primary care visits and home health care.12 Lastly, rural residents are also impacted by challenges such as health care provider shortages and difficulties with transportation that may also impair recovery.13 Whether rural residence is associated with worse outcomes after critical illness, however, remains understudied.

To address this gap in knowledge, we analyzed the association of rurality with long-term cognitive function, disability, and health-related quality of life among patients who survived a critical illness. We hypothesized that survivors living in areas of greater rurality would be associated with worse individual cognitive impairment, disability, and quality of life and that these associations would be modified by area-level socioeconomic deprivation.

Methods

Study design and setting

We tested our hypotheses in 2 prospective identical cohort studies: the BRAIN-ICU (Bringing to Light the Risk Factors and Incidence of Neuropsychological Dysfunction in ICU Survivors; NCT00392795) and MIND-ICU (Delirium and Dementia in Veterans Surviving ICU Care; NCT00400062) multicenter, prospective cohort studies, which were performed under identical study protocols, differing only in enrolling sites. The studies were conducted at Vanderbilt University Medical Center (Nashville), Saint Thomas Hospital (Nashville), Tennessee Valley Healthcare System Nashville VA Medical Center (Nashville), George E. Wahlen Department of VA Medical Center in VA Salt Lake City Health Care System (Salt Lake City), and Seattle Division of the VA Puget Sound Healthcare System (Seattle). Each center’s institutional review board approved the study protocol (Vanderbilt IRB approval number: 060593). Some of these data have been previously reported in the form of a conference abstract.14

Patient population

Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria have been previously published.2 In brief, we enrolled adults aged 18 years or older who were being treated for shock and/or acute respiratory failure in a medical or surgical ICU at enrolling sites. Participants with organ failure longer than 72 hours, a recent ICU study longer than 5 days, inability to communicate in English, residence >200 miles from an enrolling site, or active substance use disorder were excluded from the original cohorts. Additionally, participants who had severe cognitive, mental health, or neurodegenerative disorders that precluded independent living at baseline were excluded, including traumatic brain injury, anoxic brain injury, cerebrovascular accidents with moderate to severe cognitive impairment or disability, Parkinson disease, Huntington disease, Alzheimer disease or related dementias, severe mental illness requiring institutionalization, known brain lesions, or acquired or congenital severe intellectual disabilities.

Exposures

The primary exposures were Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes generated from participants’ or surrogate-reported primary residence addresses. The RUCA codes are a measure of rurality calculated on the census tract level, developed by the US Department of Agriculture Economic Research Service.15 The RUCA codes use measures of population density, urbanization, and daily commuting flows to classify the degree of rurality for US census tracts. Because the majority of enrollment occurred prior to 2010 and important methodological changes occurred in the 2010 census, which included differences in measurement of commuting flows, we used RUCA codes from the data released in 2000 as patient residences were geocoded based on the 2000 Census. Primary RUCA codes were ordered 1 to 10, with 1 indicating metropolitan areas (most urban) and 10 indicating rural areas (most rural).16 To allow for modeling of potential nonlinear participant-level associations and avoid the substantial loss of statistical power that comes with categorization of variables,17-19 we modeled RUCA codes as ­continuous ordered variables in our primary analysis, a validated approached used in studies of other non-ICU populations.20,21

Socioeconomic status is a known confounding variable in rural health research, and it is recommended to account for socioeconomic status when evaluating the effect of rurality on outcomes.8 As such, we determined participants’ degree of area-level socioeconomic deprivation using the Area Deprivation Index (ADI).22-24 At the census block group level, ADI incorporates data regarding constructs such as education, income, housing, employment, and poverty. The ADI generates a composite measure with percentiles ranging 1 to 100, with a higher score indicating greater neighborhood disadvantage. We used the 2009 version of the ADI because it was the most recent version available that used 2000 Census data for geocoding. The ADI was defined a priori as a covariate in our primary statistical models and was also evaluated as an interaction term with RUCA scores in our prespecified secondary analysis. Additional information on both RUCA codes and the ADI are included in the online supplement.

Outcomes

At 3 and 12 months after hospital discharge, trained study personnel assessed the following outcomes: global cognition using the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS)25; executive function using the Trails Making Test Part B26; self- or surrogate-reported disability in basic activities of daily living (ADLs) such as bathing, toileting, eating, and transferring using the Katz Activities of Daily Living index27; self- or surrogate-reported disability in instrumental ADLs such as managing finances and transportation or preparing meals using the Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ)28; and health-related quality of life using the 36-item Short Form Survey Physical Component and Mental Component scores.29

Covariates

Considering prior research, we chose covariates a priori that may confound associations between our exposure and outcomes of interest or may increase the precision of our estimates. In all models, we included the following covariates: age, education in years, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index,30 ADI, modified mean daily Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score,31 days of coma or delirium within the study period, days of mechanical ventilation during the study period, and hospital type (Veterans Affairs vs civilian). For the models investigating disability outcomes, the baseline Katz ADL index27 and baseline FAQ28 were included. For cognitive models, the baseline Short Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly32 was included as a covariate. In the model assessing the interaction between rurality and area deprivation, we included an interaction term between RUCA code and ADI (RUCA*ADI).

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive statistics to characterize the cohort. For continuous variables, we reported median and interquartile range; for categorical variables, we used number and percentage per category. To allow for modeling of potentially nonlinear patient-level associations, we used multivariable regression, adjusted for prespecified covariates, to determine associations between rurality and outcomes. We modeled continuous and normally distributed outcomes (RBANS, Trails B, and 36-item Short Form Survey Physical Component and Mental Component) using multiple linear regression. We modeled ordinal outcomes (Katz ADL and FAQ) using proportional odds logistic regression. We restricted our analyses to participants who had at least 1 cognitive or disability outcome assessed at either the 3- or 12-month follow-up. All continuous variables were allowed to have a nonlinear relationship with the outcome using restricted cubic splines. Robust standard errors were used in all models to perform valid inference under potential model misspecification.

Because differential follow-up due to mortality and loss to follow-up may introduce bias, we used inverse probability of attrition weighting to reduce attrition-related selection bias.33,34 Using a logistic regression model for all participants who were discharged alive from the hospital, we estimated the probability that each participant would be alive, not withdrawn, not lost to follow-up, and not missing all cognitive (for cognitive models) or disability (for disability models) outcomes at both the 3- and 12-month time points. We included the inverse of these predicted probabilities as participant weights in their respective models.

Among patients alive and who participated in follow-up with missing covariate or partially missing outcome data, we used multiple imputation using predictive mean matching, allowing nonlinear variable transformations with 15 imputations.35 We performed multiple imputation to fill in missing baseline variables and partially missing outcomes for each of the 3- and 12-month disability, cognitive, and health-related quality of life models. Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1 (The R Project for Statistical Computing).

Results

From January 2007 through December 2010, we enrolled 1047 patients with critical illness into the BRAIN-ICU and MIND-ICU prospective cohort studies from civilian and Veterans Affairs hospitals in the southeastern, Mountain West, and Pacific West regions of the United States from 11 states. Seven participants withdrew from the study and requested that all study data be removed from the database, leaving 1040 participants enrolled. Of these, 781 participants survived to hospital discharge and were eligible for inclusion in the study. At the 3-month follow-up, after accounting for death and study attrition, 624 participants remained alive and in the study, with 551 completing at least partial follow-up assessments (88%). At the 12-month follow-up, 527 participants remained alive and in the study, with 470 having at least partial follow-up assessments (89%) (Figure 1).

Patient enrollment and follow-up.

Those who survived until hospital discharge were a median [IQR] age of 62 years [52-71], and 318 (41%) were female. The median Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II score at ICU admission was 23 [17-29], the median Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score was 9 [7-11], and the median duration of mechanical ventilation was 2 days [1-6]. The median [IQR] RUCA score was 4 [1-7], corresponding to residence in a micropolitan core with <50 000 residents and indicating that half the participants in this cohort resided in an area defined as rural.16 The median [IQR] ADI percentile was 69 [52-85], indicating moderate area-level socioeconomic disadvantage (Table 1).

After adjusting for prespecified covariates, including baseline Katz ADL, at the 3-month follow-up, greater rurality was associated with greater odds of a higher Katz ADL score (adjusted odds ratio, 2.16; 95% CI, 1.27-3.67; P = .02; Table 2 and Figure 2). Thus, a typical patient in our cohort with a RUCA score of 7 (75th percentile, indicating residence in a town of <10 000 people) was 2 times more likely to have worse disability in ADLs at the 3-month follow-up than a similar patient with a RUCA score of 1 (25th percentile, indicating residence in a metropolitan area), keeping all the other covariates the same. This association persisted at the 12-month follow-up (global P = .03), although the association was nonlinear and is best described graphically, as shown in Figure 2.

Rurality was not associated with global cognition, as measured by the RBANS, or executive function, as measured by the Trails B score (Table 2). Similarly, rurality was also not associated with physical health–related quality of life at 3 or 12 months. Nor was greater rurality associated with mental health–related quality of life at 3 months. Greater rurality, however, was associated with a significantly (P = .03) better mental health–related quality of life score at 12 months (Table 2 and Figure S1). Nevertheless, the confidence interval was wide, and the median difference approached but did not meet recommended minimal clinically important difference.36 Finally, we found no evidence of a significant interaction or modification effect between rurality and area-level socioeconomic deprivation (as measured by the ADI) with disability, cognition, or health-related quality of life at either 3 or 12 months.

Discussion

In this multicenter cohort study analyzing long-term outcomes from 781 survivors of critical illness, greater rurality was associated with greater disability in basic activities of daily living at the 3- and 12-month follow-up, although the association at 12 months between rurality and disability was nonlinear and complex. Rurality was not associated with significant differences in cognitive function and inconsistently associated with mental health–related quality of life. Notably, these findings were not affected by socioeconomic deprivation. Our findings suggest that rural-urban health disparities may uniquely increase the risk of disability in basic ADLs, particularly during the early recovery period after hospitalization.

Our study builds upon prior research on the impact of rurality on disability after the ICU. Rural ICUs are caring for increasingly complex patients, despite often having fewer resources,37 and patients residing in rural areas may not receive equivalent rehabilitative care while hospitalized for critical illness. Jain and colleagues11 found that critically ill older adults living in rural areas were significantly less likely to receive any physical or occupational therapy in the hospital, potentially increasing the risk of hospital-acquired disability among older adults. Our study, among the largest to study the association between rural residence and post-ICU outcomes such as disability and cognitive function, expands these findings beyond the walls of the ICU to highlight how rural residence affects outcomes months after hospitalization.

All patients in our study were enrolled in ICUs at tertiary care or Veterans Affairs hospitals located in urban areas. Nevertheless, we found that survivors whose primary residence was in a more rural community had a greater risk of disability in basic ADLs (eg, bathing, toileting, transferring). These findings may be due to disparities in access to follow-up care and rehabilitation services available in rural communities in the early postdischarge period. For example, rural sepsis survivors were equally likely to be referred for home health and outpatient physical therapy as their urban counterparts, but they had fewer actual home health and physical therapy visits.12 Those findings are also consistent with prior work by Falvey et al38 showing that rural Medicare beneficiaries discharged home after critical illness received fewer home health rehabilitation services. Access to health care resources, including rehabilitation services, following discharge may be a key driver of increased disability in ADLs among rural ICU survivors.

We found that the association between rurality and disability in ADLs was not modified by socioeconomic deprivation. This finding stands in contrast to prior studies where socioeconomic deprivation was associated with greater disability burden and worse mental health–related quality of life among ICU survivors.39,40 Nevertheless, neither of these studies included rurality in their analyses. Thus, our data suggest that challenges faced by rural residents, such as travel distance and limited local health care workforces, affect rural ICU survivors across the spectrum of socioeconomic status.

We also found no association between greater rurality and global cognition or executive function. Unlike physical or occupational therapy, cognitive rehabilitation is not commonly provided after hospitalization, except for certain disease processes such as traumatic brain injury or stroke. Therefore, rural health care disparities may not affect cognition to the same degree as physical function and disability, given the limited utilization of cognitive rehabilitation in general ICU survivors. The lack of association between rurality and cognitive function may also explain the lack of association between rurality and disability in instrumental ADLs, which require higher-order cognitive processing and are intricately linked to cognitive decline.41 Our findings also stand in contrast to findings in non–critically ill adults. After adjusting for socioeconomic status, education, and demographic differences, patients living in rural areas have an increased relative risk of developing cognitive impairment and dementia.42 Cognitive deficits after critical illness are likely an accelerated form of brain aging following an acute insult, such that long-standing exposures, such as rural residence, may have less impact than other well-validated ICU-associated predictors such as delirium.

Our study also found no consistent associations between rurality and health-related quality of life. Although there was a statistically significant association at the 12-month follow-up, this estimate was nonlinear (Figure S1), imprecise, and did not reach the minimally clinically important difference of 0.5 standard deviation.36 These findings differ from those seen in cancer survivors, in whom rurality is associated with worse health-related quality of life.43 Health-related quality of life is a complex domain that can be affected by not only disability but also health status perceptions, individual characteristics and values, and environmental factors,44 such that it may not correlate directly with objective measures of disability or cognition.45

Our findings have important implications for both clinical care and health policy. Care coordination interventions for critical illness survivors to date have not consistently demonstrated improvement in patient-centered outcomes such as mortality or quality of life.46,47 Identifying patients from rural areas at high risk of functional impairment for intensive postdischarge care coordination and rehabilitation referrals may improve outcomes by focusing on a population with greater needs after discharge. In the hospital, critically ill patients from rural areas are less likely to receive in-hospital rehabilitation and, after discharge, are less likely to receive home health services such as home health rehabilitation.11,12 Policy initiatives, such as increased payments to rural health care and rehabilitation providers, investment in rural hospitals and postacute care, and novel care delivery approaches such as telerehabilitation, may also decrease the risk of disability among rural ICU survivors. Addressing both individual and structural health care barriers will likely be needed to adequately address the difficulties faced by rural survivors of critical illness.

Our study has multiple strengths. We performed our analyses in a large cohort of critically ill adults with a variety of admission diagnoses from academic, community, and Veterans Affairs hospitals, increasing generalizability of our findings. We also used a measure of rurality assessed at the census tract level that incorporates novel domains important to rural outcomes such as commuting flows. In-person detailed assessments of disability, cognition, and quality of life were performed with high rates of follow-up. Additionally, we used inverse probability of attrition weighting to account for bias due to competing risks, which is common in studies of populations with a high risk of mortality or attrition.34,48 Finally, because pre-ICU cognitive and functional status affect outcomes after the ICU,49,50 we included baseline measures of cognitive function and disability in our models, increasing the strength of the observed associations.

Our study also has several important limitations. The RUCA codes incorporate census data on population density, urbanization, and daily commuting patterns and therefore do not necessarily capture all potential factors that contribute to disparities among rural residents. Nevertheless, RUCA codes are widely used and validated, and their inclusion of commuting flows is helpful in health-related studies, as commuting destinations may also be where patients receive health care services.19 Moreover, we adjusted other contributing factors, such as socioeconomic deprivation, in our models. Future studies should measure community-based factors, such as health care density and access, that could impact rural survivors. Second, we also did not collect measures of individual participants’ socioeconomic status, such as income, in the original cohort study. We did, however, include education, a commonly used proxy for individual socioeconomic status.51 Additionally, we included a well-validated census tract–level measure of area-level socioeconomic deprivation in our models based on participant-level geographic IDs, which is a common approach, given that individual-level socioeconomic data often have greater nonrandom missingness,51-53 although correlations between neighborhood-level measures of socioeconomic status are stronger in urban areas.54 Third, we were unable to collect detailed data on in-hospital rehabilitation, which could influence long-term outcomes. We did, however, include important in-ICU measures, such as days of delirium and coma and the duration of mechanical ventilation, which could directly influence the receipt of in-hospital rehabilitation. Additionally, while we enrolled patients from 3 separate geographic regions of the United States, the original cohort study limited enrollment to patients living less than 200 miles from an enrolling site to ensure follow-up, so our findings may not be generalizable to participants in all communities across the United States. We also cannot rule out the possibility of fundamental differences in outcomes among patients referred to the regional hospitals that enrolled in this study and those cared for in smaller, rural ICUs only. Future studies should test these hypotheses in a large nationally representative sample of ICU survivors across a range of hospital systems. Finally, as is true with all observational study designs, the risk of residual confounding exists. However, we used statistical approaches to reduce bias due to mortality or attrition, and we defined several clinically relevant covariates a priori and adjusted for these in our models to reduce the risk of confounding.

Conclusions

Among survivors of critical illness, greater rurality was associated with worse disability in basic ADLs but was not associated with cognitive function or health-related quality of life. Future studies are needed to identify the specific modifiable factors faced by survivors of critical illness living in rural areas so that interventions to improve recovery can be developed and tested.
