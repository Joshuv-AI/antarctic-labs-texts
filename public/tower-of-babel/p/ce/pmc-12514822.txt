Fe-MOF loaded with capmatinib to reverse immunosuppressive microenvironment and inhibit tumor metastasis after radiofrequency ablation
Authors: Jinchao Zhao, Lei Lei, Cheng Zeng, Xianfei Zhou, Junkang Ding, Wenbin Dai, Angfeng Jiang, Shaomin Zou, Qiao Jin, Zhe Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIncomplete radiofrequency ablation (iRFA) of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) has the risk to exacerbate the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment and promote lung metastasis. There is an urgent need to reverse the immunosuppressive microenvironment and reduce lung metastasis after iRFA. Herein, an iron-based metal-organic framework loaded with capmatinib (Fe-MOF/Cap) is employed to reverse the immunosuppressive microenvironment dominated by M2-like tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) after iRFA and inhibit lung metastasis of HCC. On one hand, Fe-MOF/Cap repolarizes M2-like TAMs to M1-like TAMs and inhibits the expression of PD-L1 after iRFA, promoting CD8+ T cell infiltration. The infiltration of CD8+ T cells and the decrease of PD-L1 are conducive to enhance the immunotherapy after iRFA. On the other hand, Fe-MOF/Cap can inhibit lung metastasis of HCC via inhibiting the expression of c-MET. In vitro and in vivo experiments confirm that Fe-MOF/Cap is able to significantly inhibit the lung metastasis of HCC after iRFA by inhibiting the c-MET/STAT3/VEGF pathway. Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the third most common cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide and the second most common cause of cancer-related deaths in China [1]. Thermal ablation, especially radiofrequency ablation (RFA), is one of the most commonly used techniques for HCC treatment [2–5]. RFA has several advantages over other modalities, including safety, minimal invasiveness, shorter hospitalization time, and lower costs [5–7]. Nevertheless, the therapeutic efficacy of RFA is frequently affected by various factors, such as tumor size and the heat sink effect, which can lead to incomplete radiofrequency ablation (iRFA) [8–10]. The residual tumor cells that survived from RFA demonstrate an increased capacity for invasion and migration, which ultimately results in tumor progression and metastasis [9]. Recent studies highlighted the role of an immunosuppressive microenvironment in promoting tumor progression following iRFA. Furthermore, the immunosuppressive microenvironment following iRFA is predominantly characterized by the infiltration of M2-like tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which further promotes tumor growth [6, 11–14]. Macrophages play a crucial role in the innate immune response and could be classified into two main types: M1-like macrophages and M2-like macrophages. The M1 polarization of macrophages has been demonstrated to possess anti-tumor properties by enhancing antigen presentation and secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, TNF-α and iNOS, which may stimulate T-cell immune responses. Conversely, M2 polarization of macrophages has been shown to hinder adaptive immune responses and contributed to tumor growth, due to the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-10, TGF-β and Arg-1 [6, 15, 16]. Consequently, the reprogramming of M2-like TAMs to M1-like TAMs within the immunosuppressive microenvironment following iRFA is a crucial step in reversing the immunosuppressive microenvironment and potentially enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy after iRFA. Additionally, iRFA has also been demonstrated to promote lung metastasis of HCC, a significant factor contributing to poor prognosis in patients [9, 17]. Therefore, in addition to enhancing immunotherapy after iRFA, suppressing lung metastasis of HCC is an urgent clinical priority.

c-Mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor (c-MET) represents a transmembrane receptor with inherent autophosphorylating activity, encoded by the MET gene, and classified as a member of the tyrosine kinase receptor superfamily [18]. c-MET plays a significant role in the epithelial-mesenchymal transition of tumor cells, with its expression being strongly correlated with HCC metastasis [18–21]. Following iRFA, the promotion of c-MET expression and the subsequent activation of signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) have been implicated in the development of lung metastases in HCC [22–24]. Therefore, the inhibition of c-MET is an effective strategy to decrease the incidence of lung metastasis of HCC after iRFA. For example, capmatinib, a selective c-MET inhibitor approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), has demonstrated exceptional therapeutic efficacy in NSCLC cases with high c-MET expression [18, 25].

Metal ions, including iron (Fe), calcium (Ca), copper (Cu) and manganese (Mn) were essential for the maintenance of normal physiological processes [26, 27]. The disruption of ionic homeostasis within the human body has been demonstrated to give rise to the development of numerous pathological processes. Furthermore, emerging evidence also suggested that metal ions play a significant role in tumor therapy and the activation of anti-tumor immunity [26, 27]. As one of the most widely studied metal ions, Fe is widely used for the treatment of tumors. For instance, a considerable number of studies have verified that Fe could induce ferroptosis for cancer therapy [28–31]. In addition, Fe was shown to contribute to the activation of interferon-regulatory factor 5 (IRF5) gene, which is a gene closely related to M1 polarization of TAMs [32, 33]. Additionally, capmatinib could inhibit the phosphorylation of STAT3, which has been reported to be closely related to the expression of PD-L1 [34, 35]. Therefore, capmatinib has the potential to reduce PD-L1 expression by inhibiting STAT3, which may further amplify the immune response. The potential for combining Fe with capmatinib to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of iRFA would be very interesting. In order to achieve the goal of simultaneous delivery of Fe and capmatinib, it is imperative to construct a suitable carrier of Fe. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are a kind of crystalline compounds composed of metal ions or metal clusters linked by organic ligands [36–38]. Because of their excellent biocompatibility, MOFs are widely used as a drug carrier for tumor therapy [39]. In particular, Fe-MOF has received considerable attention for its catalytic properties and drug delivery capability [40]. Therefore, the Fe-MOF was used as a drug carrier for enhancing the efficacy of iRFA in this study.

Herein, a Fe-MOF loaded with capmatinib (Fe-MOF/Cap) was designed to reverse tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment and inhibit lung metastasis of HCC after iRFA (Scheme 1). On one hand, Fe-MOF/Cap was expected to repolarize M2-like TAMs to M1-like TAMs and inhibit the expression of PD-L1, thereby reversing the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment. On the other hand, Fe-MOF/Cap was expected to inhibit lung metastasis of HCC after iRFA by inhibiting c-MET. The efficacy of Fe-MOF/Cap combined with iRFA was rigorously evaluated in vitro and in vivo, offering a promising strategy for reversing immunosuppressive microenvironment and reducing lung metastasis of HCC after iRFA.

Materials and methods

Materials

FeCl3·6H2O, 1,4-benzenedicarboxylic acid and N, N-Dimethylformamide (DMF) were provided by Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co.,Ltd (Shanghai, China). Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) and trypsin-EDTA (0.25%) were purchased from Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Dalian, China). Anti p-MET, anti c-MET, anti STAT3, anti p-STAT3 and anti CD206 were purchased from CST. VEGF, PD-L1, IL-1β, IL-10, TGF-β and TNF-α antibodies were purchased from abcam. Anti CD86 and anti Arg-1 was provided by Proteintech Group, Inc. Anti iNOS was purchased from Thermo Fisher. Fetal bovine serum (FBS) and mice Hepa 1–6 HCC cell lines were offered by Baidi Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Zhejiang, China). Human LM3 HCC cell line, murine L929 normal cell line and Raw264.7 macrophage were purchased from Meisen Biotech Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China).

Preparation of Fe-MOF/Cap

FeCl3·6H2O (20.8 mg), capmatinib (10 mg) and 1,4-benzenedicarboxylic acid (19.2 mg) were completely dissolved in 70 mL DMF. The solution was then heated at 100 °C for 12 h. After the reaction, the resulting precipitate was washed with ultrapure water by centrifugation and subsequently lyophilized to obtain Fe-MOF/Cap.

Characterizations

The morphology of Fe-MOF/Cap was observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscope (SEM). XRD patterns were obtained using an x-ray diffractometer (D8 Discover, Bruker, Germany) across the scanning range of 5° to 70°. The Fe content of the Fe-MOF/Cap was detected by ICP-MS (NexION 300x, PerkinElmer, USA). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and elemental mappings of samples were collected by Perkin Elmer PHI 5400 and Titan Themis Cubed G2 300 TEM, respectively. The zeta potential of the Fe-MOF/Cap was measured using a Zetasizer Nano-ZS (Malvern, UK). Demonstration of capmatinib loading in Fe-MOF/Cap using fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Thermo Scientific Nicolet Summit X) and ultraviolet visible (UV-vis) absorption spectrometry (UV-2600, Shimadzu). What’s more, the standard curve of capmatinib was determined using UV-vis (Figure S1).

Drug release in vitro

Fe-MOF/Cap (1 mL) was added into a dialysis bag (molecular weight: 8,000–14,000). Then, the dialysis bag was put into a solution of PBS with 1% (w/v) Tween-80 (pH = 5.5, pH = 6.5 or pH = 7.4) and incubated at 37 °C. At 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h, collected the solution outside the dialysis bag and measured the content of capmatinib by enzyme marker (Synergy H1, Bio-Tek) at 255 nm.

In vitro simulation of iRFA

Based on reports in the literature and our previous studies, iRFA was simulated using a water bath at 43 °C for 10 min [2, 41–43].

Cell culture and co-culture

Hepa 1–6 cells, LM3 cells, L929 cells and Raw264.7 cells were cultured with DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. For co-culture, transwell chambers were used. Raw264.7 cells were placed in the lower chamber and Hepa 1–6 cells which exposed to 43 °C for 10 min were co-cultured in the upper chamber.

Western blot analysis

The levels of p-MET, c-MET, STAT3, p-STAT3, VEGF, PD-L1, IL-1β, TNF-α, iNOS, CD86, IL-10, Arg-1, CD206 and TGF-β after different treatments were detected by western blot. Hepa 1–6 cells or Raw264.7 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well in DMEM. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C under 5% CO₂, the cells were administered with different treatments for an additional 4 h. Following treatments, the cells were washed three times with PBS and harvested by trypsin. Total protein was quantified using a BCA Protein Quantification Kit. Proteins from each sample were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked with T-TBS containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h, then the Hepa 1–6 cells incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody, anti-p-MET (CST, 3077), anti-c-MET (CST, 3127), anti-STAT3 (CST, 9139), anti-p-STAT3 (CST, 9145), anti-VEGF (abcam, ab214424), and anti-PD-L1 (abcam, ab213480), respectively. Raw264.7 cells incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody, IL-1β (abcam, ab254360), TNF-α (abcam, ab6671), iNOS (Thermo, PA1-036), CD86 (Proteintech, 13395-1-AP), IL-10 (abcam, ab310329), TGF-β (abcam, ab179695), Arg-1 (Proteintech, 16001-1-AP) and CD206 (CST, 24595), respectively. After washing with T-TBS for three times, the membrane was incubated at room temperature for 1 h with the secondary antibody. Protein bands were visualized using X-ray films and detected by chemiluminescence with the SuperSignal® West Dura Extended Duration Substrate.

Immunofluorescence staining

Approximately 4 × 104 Hepa 1–6 cells or LM3 cells were seeded into each well and incubated for 24 h. The cells were then treated with PBS, Fe-MOF/Cap (20 µg/mL), 43 °C, and 43 °C + Fe-MOF/Cap (20 µg/mL) and incubated for an additional 8 h. After treatment, the cells were washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. Following fixation, the cells were washed three times with PBS. Then, the cells were incubated with 0.3% Triton-100 at room temperature for 20 min. After washing with PBS, 5% BSA solution was added and the cells were incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Primary antibodies (anti p-MET, anti p-STAT3, or anti PD-L1) were then added, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h. Then, the secondary antibody was added and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 1 h after washing with PBS for three times. Finally, the cells were incubated with 1 mL of DAPI (10 µg/mL) for 10 min. The stained cells were then observed under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Ti2).

Transwell migration assay

The transwell migration assay was performed using transwell chambers (3422, Corning, Kennebunk, ME, USA), which feature a permeable membrane with 8 μm pores. A total of 8,000 cells, suspended in serum-free medium, were placed in the upper compartment of the transwell chambers, while the lower compartment contained complete growth medium. After 24 h incubation, the cells were fixed with formaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. Cells remaining on the upper surface of the membrane were carefully removed, and the migrated cells on the lower surface of the membrane were visualized using a Nikon Ti2 microscope.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR

Raw264.7 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well. The medium was then replaced with fresh DMEM after 24 h, and the cells were treated with different treatments for another 24 h. Following incubation, total RNA was extracted using Trizol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA), and cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Japan). Finally, the expression levels of IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-10, and TGF-β were analyzed by qRT-PCR. The sequence of target gene primer was included in Table S1.

ELISA assays in vitro

The Raw264.7 cells were seeded into 48-plate with 5 × 104 cells each well. After 24 h, the Raw264.7 cells were incubated with IL-4 (20 ng/mL), LPS (20 ng/mL) and Fe-MOF/Cap (20 µg/mL) for another 24 h. Subsequently, the cell culture medium was collected and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 20 min, and the supernatant was collected. Then, the content of IL-1β, TNF-α, iNOS, IL-10, TGF-β, and Arg-1 were measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For co-culture experiments, the cell culture medium was collected at 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h. The subsequent measurement steps were the same as described above.

Cell viability assays

Approximately 8 × 103 Hepa 1–6 cells, LM3 cells or L929 cells were seeded into each well and incubated for 24 h. The cells were then treated with different concentration of Fe-MOF/Cap and incubated for an additional 24 h. Then, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 10% CCK-8 solution and incubated for another 2 h. Finally, the cell viability was measured by enzyme marker (Synergy H1, Bio-Tek) at 450 nm. The following formula (1) was used to calculate cell viability.

Cell viability assays of co-culture

To induce the polarization of Raw264.7 into M1 macrophages, Raw264.7 cells were seeded into 24-plate and incubated with LPS (20 ng/mL) or Fe-MOF/Cap (20 µg/mL) for 24 h. Then, M1-polarized macrophages were cultured with serum-free medium for another 24 h. Next, the medium was collected as conditioned medium [44]. Finally, the Hepa 1–6 cells were cultured with conditioned medium for 24 h and the cell viability was measured as described above.

The establishment of iRFA model in vivo

Following RFA, the occurrence of a central zone of coagulative necrosis was observed at temperatures exceeding 50 °C, accompanied by a zone exhibiting temperatures below this threshold. The survival of residual tumor cells in the zone of <50 °C resulted in iRFA [2]. To simulate this process, the RFA needle was inserted in the non-central site of tumor tissues. Thus, some tumor tissues would residue and the iRFA model was established in vivo.

Tumor growth Inhibition on a subcutaneous HCC model

All animal experiments were conducted following the guidelines for Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, Zhejiang Center of Laboratory Animals (IACUC, ZJCLA) and the “Principles of Laboratory Animal Care” (NIH publication no.86 − 23, revised 1985). The assigned approval number was ZJCLA-IACUC-20,010,750. The anti-tumor efficacy of the Fe-MOF/Cap was evaluated in subcutaneous Hepa 1–6 tumor models. Healthy male C57 mice (20 ± 2 g, 4–5 weeks old) were purchased from the Zhejiang Academy of Medical Sciences. Hepa 1–6 cells (1 × 106) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of the mice. When the tumors reached approximately 200 mm3 in size, the mice were randomly divided into four groups (5 mice each group): Saline, iRFA, Fe-MOF/Cap, and iRFA + Fe-MOF/Cap. The Fe-MOF/Cap (6 mg/kg) was administrated at Day 0, Day 1 and Day 2 via tail vein injection. For the iRFA treatment, mice were anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane and placed on the electrode plate. The RFA instrument (MedSphere, Shanghai, China) was connected, and the needle of RFA was inserted into the non-central site of the tumor to simulate the clinical iRFA. Ablation was performed using parameters of 5 W for 10 s. Tumor volume and body weight of each group were measured and recorded regularly to assess the anti-tumor efficacy of the drugs. After 14 days, the mice were sacrificed and the tumor was collected, photographed and weighted. Mice blood was also collected for blood biochemistry and blood routine analysis. The following formula (2) was used to calculate tumor volume.

Anti-metastatic potential evaluation in vivo

The lung metastasis model was established by injecting 2 × 106 Hepa 1–6 cells suspended in PBS via the tail vein. Then, mice were subcutaneously injected 1 × 106 Hepa 1–6 cells 7 days after the injection of the Hepa 1–6 cells into the tail vein. 7 days after the subcutaneous cell injection, the mice were randomly divided into four groups (5 mice each group): Saline, iRFA, Fe-MOF/Cap, and iRFA + Fe-MOF/Cap. The Fe-MOF/Cap (6 mg/kg) was administrated at Day 0, Day 1 and Day 2 via tail vein injection. Then, the iRFA and iRFA + Fe-MOF/Cap group received the ablation of 5 W for 10 s at Day 2. After the treatments, the mice were sacrificed and the lung of each group was collected and photographed. To further evaluate the metastatic degree, the metastatic lesions in the lungs were evaluated by H&E staining.

Flow cytometry

Mice were sacrificed 7 days after different treatments, and the tumors were collected, cut into pieces, digested, and resuspended. Tumors and spleens were also harvested and resuspended. Both tumor tissue and spleen tissue suspensions were filtered through 70 μm nylon mesh filters. The Zombie Aqua Fixable Viability Kit was used for cell membrane staining. For intracellular staining, cells were first stimulated with the Cell Activation Cocktail, then stained for cell surface markers. Next, the cells were fixed, permeabilized, and incubated with antibodies against all molecules according to the experimental scheme (anti-CD45, anti-CD11b, anti-CD3, anti-F4/80, anti-CD86, anti-CD206, anti-CD4 and anti-CD8). Finally, the samples were analyzed using the BD LSR Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, NJ, USA). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star, Ashland, OR).

Hemolysis test

Mouse blood was diluted 50 times with PBS and centrifuged for 5 min (3000 rpm) to get 2% (v./v.) red blood cell suspensions. In order to evaluate the degree of hemolysis, negative and positive groups were set up. Then, the hemolysis rate was detected by microplate Bio-Rad at 576 nm. The hemolysis rate was calculated by following Eq. (3), where Abs was the absorbance at 576 nm.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). The statistical differences between two groups were analyzed by student’s t-test and three or more groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post-hoc test by Graphpad Prism 9.4.0. The NS indicated no significant difference. *P < 0.05 represented significant difference and ***P < 0.001 represented obvious significant difference.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of Fe-MOF/Cap

To reverse the immunosuppressive microenvironment and reduce lung metastasis after iRFA, a Fe metal-organic framework (Fe-MOF) loaded with capmatinib (Fe-MOF/Cap) was designed and synthesized. The morphology of Fe-MOF/Cap was investigated by TEM and SEM. It could be observed that the Fe-MOF/Cap exhibited a rod-shaped morphology. The average width was approximately 50 nm and the average length was approximately 650 nm (Fig. 1a and b). Subsequently, the zeta potential of the Fe-MOF/Cap was measured to be −5.8 mV. The Fe content in Fe-MOF/Cap was 17% detected by ICP-MS. The crystal structures of Fe-MOF/Cap were analyzed by XRD. The results of XRD indicated the successful formation of Fe-MOF/Cap crystals (Fig. 1c). Then, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to analyzed the valence state of Fe and the elemental composition of Fe-MOF/Cap. The results indicated that the Fe-MOF/Cap contained the elements of C, O, Fe and N (Fig. 1d - g). The element of N belonged to the capmatinib and the element of Fe belonged to the Fe-MOF. The peaks located at 711.76 eV and 725.37 eV in the high-resolution spectrum of Fe 2p could be attributed to the Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2 cleavage peaks of Fe (III) in the Fe-MOF/Cap, and the peaks at 717.49 eV and 731.5 eV were the satellite peaks (Fig. 1d - g). The above results confirmed the successful preparation of Fe-MOF/Cap containing Fe (III). The loading of capmatinib on Fe-MOF/Cap was verified by UV-vis spectroscopy because the characteristic peak of capmatinib at 255 nm was observed in the Fe-MOF/Cap (Fig. 1h). Based on the standard curve of capmatinib, Fe-MOF/Cap exhibited the drug loading content of 14% and the encapsulation rate of 70%. Subsequently, the effective loading of capmatinib was further confirmed by FTIR (Fig. 1i). C-N = C was the characteristic structure of the imidazole cycle in capmatinib. N-H was the characteristic structure of the amide group in capmatinib. The characteristic peaks at 1258 cm−1 C-N = C and 3310 cm−1 N-H suggested that the capmatinib was successfully loaded into Fe-MOF (Fig. 1i). Furthermore, the mapping was used to further analyzed the elements of Fe-MOF/Cap. The N and F was the characteristic element of capmatinib. The results suggested that Fe-MOF/Cap contained the C, O, Fe, N and F (Fig. 1j), which indicated that Fe-MOF/Cap was successfully prepared. It was important to note that drug release was vital for capmatinib to exert its function. Therefore, further morphological changes of Fe-MOF/Cap and the release of capmatinib within 48 h were measured at acidic environment. As shown in Fig. 1k and l, Fe-MOF/Cap lost its intact rod-shaped morphology, indicating the decomposition of the Fe-MOF/Cap at pH = 5.5. At the same time, approximately 69% of the capmatinib could be released within 48 h at pH = 6.5. The release of capmatinib was further increased to 75% within 48 h when the solution pH was further reduced to 5.5.

Inhibition of c-MET-STAT3-VEGF pathway and PD-L1 in vitro

The c-MET-STAT3-VEGF pathway is a major regulatory pathway for tumor metastasis after iRFA [21–24]. The function of c-MET-STAT3-VEGF pathway was mainly dependent on c-MET phosphorylation (p-MET). The overactivation of p-MET signaling would upregulate the expression of both p-STAT3 and VEGF. The expression of PD-L1 on the surface of tumor cells was also increased after the activation of p-STAT3. Therefore, this pathway not only promoted tumor cell migration but also enabled tumor cells to avoid recognition and clearance by the host immune system. Inhibition the expression of c-MET could effectively inhibit the c-MET-STAT3-VEGF pathway thereby effectively inhibiting the metastatic of tumors and potentially activating anti-tumor immunity. The western blot results of Hepa 1–6 cells demonstrated that Fe-MOF/Cap could effectively suppress the expression of p-MET, consequently downregulating the expression of p-STAT3 and VEGF (Fig. 2a and Figure S2). What’s more, the expression of PD-L1 was also reduced when this pathway was inhibited (Fig. 2a and Figure S2). In contrast, when the tumor cells were exposed to 43 °C for 10 min, the expression of p-MET, p-STAT3, VEGF and PD-L1 was enhanced. However, when the tumor cells treated with 43 °C + Fe-MOF/Cap, the expression of p-MET, p-STAT3, VEGF and PD-L1 was reduced compared to 43 °C alone (Fig. 2a and Figure S2). Furthermore, the immunofluorescence of p-MET, p-STAT3 and PD-L1 in both Hepa 1–6 cells and LM3 cells demonstrated that the green fluorescence was strongest in the group of 43 °C but it was reduced in the group of 43 °C + Fe-MOF/Cap (Fig. 2b - d and Figure S3-S4). Meanwhile, the green fluorescence was weaker in the group of Fe-MOF/Cap than PBS group (Fig. 2b - d and Figure S3-S4). These findings suggested that iRFA had the potential to promote tumor metastasis via the activation of c-MET pathway but Fe-MOF/Cap could reverse this phenomenon. Meanwhile, the increased expression of p-STAT3 after the activation of p-MET resulted in the increase of PD-L1 expression. PD-L1 had the potential to impede immunotherapy after iRFA but this situation was also reversed by Fe-MOF/Cap. To further investigate whether Fe-MOF inhibited PD-L1, the expression of PD-L1 was measured by western blot after Hepa 1–6 cells were incubated with Fe-MOF, free capmatinib (Free Cap), and Fe-MOF/Cap. As shown in Figure S5, Fe-MOF could not inhibit the expression of PD-L1. However, Free Cap and Fe-MOF/Cap could inhibit the expression of PD-L1 at the same extent, which indicated that capmatinib in Fe-MOF/Cap could inhibit PD-L1, rather than Fe-MOF. Finally, the transwell migration assay demonstrated that Fe-MOF/Cap could effectively inhibit the migration capacity of Hepa 1–6 and LM3 cells after iRFA (Fig. 2e and f and Figure S6). All above findings indicated that Fe-MOF/Cap could inhibit metastasis of HCC after iRFA and might enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy following iRFA via PD-L1 inhibition.

Fe-MOF/Cap induced macrophage M1 polarization in vitro

It is well known that M1-like macrophages secreted pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, TNF-α and iNOS to inhibit tumor growth, whereas M2-like macrophages secreted anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-10, TGF-β and Arg-1 to promote tumor growth. In addition, CD86 and CD206 were the most widely used surface markers for M1 and M2, respectively. Therefore, IL-1β, TNF-α, iNOS, and CD86 were used as markers for M1-like macrophages. IL-10, TGF-β, Arg-1, and CD206 were used as markers for M2-like macrophages. IL-4 as a positive control for M2-like macrophages and LPS as a positive control for M1-like macrophages. Firstly, the gene expression of IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-10, and TGF-β was analyzed. As shown in Fig. 3a - d, the gene expression of IL-1β and TNF-α was increased when LPS or Fe-MOF/Cap was added, while the gene expression of IL-10 and TGF-β showed no significant difference compared to the PBS group. However, IL-10 and TGF-β was significantly lower in LPS or Fe-MOF/Cap group than that of the IL-4 group. Then, the western blot assay was used to analyze the expression of IL-1β, TNF-α, iNOS, CD86, IL-10, TGF-β, Arg-1 and CD206 after different treatments. Similar to the LPS group, the Fe-MOF/Cap group significantly promoted the expression of IL-1β, TNF-α, iNOS and CD86. However, the expression of TGF-β, IL-10, Arg-1 and CD206 did not undergo a notable change in comparison to that of the PBS group but was found to be significantly lower than that observed in the IL-4 group (Fig. 3e and Figure S7-S8). Finally, the content of IL-1β, TNF-α, iNOS, IL-10, TGF-β and Arg-1 were measured by ELISA. As shown in Fig. 3f - k, the content of IL-1β, TNF-α and iNOS was significantly higher than control but the content of IL-10, TGF-β and Arg-1 didn’t show significant change. All above results indicated that Fe-MOF/Cap could induce the M1 polarization of macrophage in vitro, which implied that Fe-MOF/Cap has the potential to promote M1 polarization of TAMs to reverse the immunosuppressive microenvironment.

Fe-MOF/Cap-induced repolarization of M2-like macrophages to M1-like macrophages after iRFA in vitro

This study aimed to observe the effects of Fe-MOF/Cap on macrophage polarization after iRFA in vitro. A water bath maintained at 43 °C for a period of 10 min was employed to simulate iRFA, in accordance with the methodology previously reported in literatures [2, 41–43]. The transwell chambers were used as a device for tumor cell and macrophage co-culture. As shown in Fig. 4a, Raw264.7 cells were placed in the lower chamber, and Hepa 1–6 cells were co-cultured in the upper chamber. Firstly, the ELISA kits were used to measure the contents of IL-1β, TNF-α, iNOS, IL-10, TGF-β, and Arg-1 after co-culture for different time. It found that there were no statistical differences between three groups after 12 h of co-culture (Figure S9-S10). However, the contents of IL-10, TGF-β, and Arg-1 were increased and the contents of IL-1β, TNF-α and iNOS were decreased in 43 °C group after 24 h of co-culture (Figure S9-S10). Meanwhile, compared to the 43 °C group, the M1 markers were increased and the M2 markers were reduced in 43 °C + Fe-MOF/Cap group after 24 h of co-culture (Figure S9-S10). Then, the further investigation revealed that there was no significant difference in various markers of M1 or M2 macrophage between co-culture for 24 h and 48 h (Figure S9-S10). These findings indicated that incubation of Raw264.7 cells at 43 °C for 10 min could induce M2 polarization of macrophages, but this situation could be reversed by Fe-MOF/Cap. In addition, the above results also showed that co-culture for 24 h could significantly affect polarization of macrophages. Therefore, co-culture for 24 h was selected in subsequent experiments. To further demonstrate the effect of Fe-MOF/Cap on macrophages after iRFA, qRT-PCR and western blot were performed. After 24 h of co-culture, Raw264.7 cells from different treatment groups were collected and subjected to qRT-PCR analysis for IL-1β, TNF-α, TGF-β and IL-10. The Raw264.7 cells were further subjected to western blot analysis for the expression of IL-1β, TNF-α, iNOS, CD86, IL-10, TGF-β, Arg-1, and CD206. As shown in Fig. 4b - e, the polarization of macrophages was influenced by tumor cells which underwent 43 °C incubation for 10 min. The gene expression of IL-10 and TGF-β, markers of M2 polarization, was notably upregulated, while the gene expression of IL-1β and TNF-α, markers of M1 polarization, was downregulated. However, when Fe-MOF/Cap was given, there was a notable increase in the gene expression of IL-1β and TNF-α, accompanied by a significant decrease in the gene expression of IL-10 and TGF-β. This phenomenon suggested that the sublethal heat treatment of Hepa 1–6 cells resulted in M2 polarization of macrophages. However, M2-like macrophages were repolarized when Fe-MOF/Cap was added. Next, the western blot results further suggested that 43 °C could induce M2 polarization of TAMs. What’s more, the M1 markers in 43 °C + Fe-MOF/Cap group were significantly higher than 43 °C group and the M2 markers in 43 °C + Fe-MOF/Cap group were obviously lower than that in 43 °C group (Fig. 4f and Figure S11-S12). These results indicated that iRFA has the potential to induce M2 polarization of TAMs, which may result in the worsen of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Conversely, Fe-MOF/Cap has the potential to repolarized M2-like TAMs to M1-like TAMs and enhancing the immunotherapeutic effect after iRFA.

Tumor cell killing effect of M1 macrophages in vitro

It is well known that M1 polarization of macrophages could inhibit tumor growth both in vivo and in vitro [45–48]. The above results proved that Fe-MOF/Cap could promote M1 polarization of macrophages. After incubated with Fe-MOF/Cap, whether macrophages could kill tumor cells was an important issue that needed to be addressed. To investigate whether M1-polarized macrophages could kill tumor cells, co-culture experiments were performed using Hepa 1–6 or LM3 cells with Raw264.7 cells. According to the literature, the Hepa 1–6 cells or LM3 cells were incubated with conditioned medium of M1 macrophages [44]. The viability of Hepa 1–6 cells was 67% in LPS-induced M1 macrophage group and 78% in Fe-MOF/Cap-induced M1 macrophage group (Figure S13). The viability of LM3 cells in LPS or Fe-MOF/Cap-induced M1 macrophage group was 63% and 76%, respectively (Figure S13). The results indicated that M1 macrophages could kill tumor cells, which was consistent with previous reports [45, 46]. Therefore, Fe-MOF/Cap could indirectly kill tumor cells by inducing M1 polarization of macrophage.

Cytotoxicity assessment of Fe-MOF/Cap in vitro

The above experiments demonstrated that Fe-MOF/Cap had the potential to activate anti-tumor immunity and the potential to indirectly kill tumor cells through inducing M1 polarization of macrophage. Herein, the direct cell-killing effect of Fe-MOF/Cap was further investigated. L929, LM3 and Hepa 1–6 cells were used to evaluated the cytotoxicity of Fe-MOF/Cap. As shown in Figure S14, the viability of L929 cells was consistently above 80% as the concentration increased, which implied that Fe-MOF/Cap didn’t show obvious cytotoxicity to L929 cells. In addition, the cytotoxicity of Fe-MOF/Cap to Hepa 1–6 and LM3 cells was increased when the concentration increased. The viability of the Hepa 1–6 and LM3 cells was 69% and 61% at a concentration of 20 µg/mL, respectively (Figure S14). The differences in cell viability among different cell lines may be due to different sensitivity of the cells to the same drug. These results also indicated that Fe-MOF/Cap not only induced M1 polarization of macrophage to indirectly kill tumors, but might also directly kill tumor cells. The direct killing effect of Fe-MOF/Cap on Hepa 1–6 and LM3 cells may be due to Fe-induced ferroptosis in tumor cells [40, 49].

Fe-MOF/Cap remodels tumor-associated macrophages in vivo

Based on the potential of Fe-MOF/Cap to induce M1 polarization of TAMs in vitro, in vivo experiments were used to ascertain the effect of Fe-MOF/Cap to induce M1 polarization of TAMs. In order to explore the effect of Fe-MOF/Cap in vivo, Hepa 1–6 cells were utilized to establish the subcutaneous HCC model. Then, qRT-PCR, western blot, and immunohistochemistry were used to assess the polarization level of TAMs in tumor tissues 7 days after iRFA. As shown in Fig. 5a - e and Figure S15, the M1 markers (IL-1β, TNF-α) were obviously upregulated and the M2 markers (IL-10, TGF-β) were dramatically downregulated in the Fe-MOF/Cap group compared to the Saline group, which suggested that Fe-MOF/Cap could induce M1 polarization of TAMs in vivo. Subsequently, further experiments demonstrated that mice treated with iRFA exhibited a significant increase in markers of M2 polarization (IL-10, TGF-β) and a significant decrease in markers of M1 polarization (IL-1β, TNF-α). It showed iRFA induced M2 polarization of TAMs, which may contribute to the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment after iRFA. When Fe-MOF/Cap was given after iRFA, the immunosuppressive microenvironment following iRFA was reversed, accompanied by the increase of IL-1β and TNF-α and the decrease of IL-10 and TGF-β (Fig. 5a - e and Figure S15). Then, the expression of CD86 and CD206 detected by western blot was used to further evaluate the level of immunosuppressive microenvironment after iRFA. CD86 represented M1 polarization of TAMs and CD206 represented M2 polarization of TAMs. As shown in Fig. 5e and Figure S15, Fe-MOF/Cap could increase the CD86 expression and inhibit CD206 expression, implied the repolarization of M2-like TAMs to M1-like TAMs by Fe-MOF/Cap after iRFA. Finally, immunohistochemistry was used to further evaluate the extent of TAMs polarization. It could be observed that iRFA induced the TAMs M2 polarization, but Fe-MOF/Cap could repolarization of M2-like TAMs to M1-like TAMs (Fig. 5f and Figure S16-S21). These findings collectively suggested that iRFA may induce immunosuppression by promoting M2 polarization of TAMs. However, Fe-MOF/Cap was potentially capable of reversing this immunosuppressive microenvironment, thereby facilitating immunotherapy after iRFA.

The potential of Fe-MOF/Cap to activate anti-tumor immune

Following the observation that Fe-MOF/Cap could induce the repolarization of M2-like TAMs to M1-like TAMs within an immunosuppressive microenvironment post-iRFA, a flow cytometry analysis of the M1 and M2 polarization proportion within the tumor tissues was performed 7 days after iRFA. It was well known that F4/80+CD86+ represented M1 macrophages and F4/80+CD206+ represented M2 macrophages. The proportion of M1 in tumor tissues was 38.7%, 27.2% and 49.2% in Saline, iRFA and iRFA + Fe-MOF/Cap groups, respectively (Fig. 6a and b). The proportion of M2 in tumor tissues was 11.2%, 20.2% and 5.32% in Saline, iRFA and iRFA + Fe-MOF/Cap groups, respectively (Fig. 6c and d). This indicated that Fe-MOF/Cap could repolarize M2-like TAMs to M1-like TAMs after iRFA. It may contribute to the improved efficacy of immunotherapy after iRFA. M1 polarization of TAMs promoted the antigen presentation ability and might contribute to the activation of T cell immunity. Additionally, PD-L1 expression on tumor tissues was enhanced following iRFA. Conversely, Fe-MOF/Cap was found to inhibit the expression of PD-L1 (Figure S22) in HCC tissues. This might be due to the inhibition of p-MET. After the inhibition of p-MET, the expression of p-STAT3 was reduced and thus inhibiting the expression of PD-L1 in HCC tissues. Although PD-L1 was expressed in various tissues, it was mainly expressed in tumor tissues. The PD-L1 on tumor tissues was strongly associated with immunosuppressive microenvironment [50–52]. PD-L1 in tumor tissues could prevent T cells from exerting tumor-killing effects by binding to PD-1 on the surface of T cells. Consequently, the inhibition of PD-L1 by Fe-MOF/Cap might contribute to the activation of anti-tumor immune. CD8+ T cells were the main immune cells that exerted anti-tumor effects in vivo. Therefore, the proportion of CD8+ T cells was analyzed in both tumor and spleen tissues. The proportion of CD8+ T in tumor tissues was 44.7%, 33.7%, and 53.9% in Saline, iRFA and iRFA + Fe-MOF/Cap groups, respectively (Fig. 6e and f). The proportion of CD8+ T cells in the spleen was also increased in the group of iRFA + Fe-MOF/Cap than iRFA group (Fig. 6g and h). These results indicated that the infiltration of CD8+ T cells was increased following the remodeling of tumor immune microenvironment by Fe-MOF/Cap after iRFA. The CD8+ T cells in tumor tissues might be helpful in boosting anti-tumor immune response. Subsequently, in order to further evaluate whether the function of CD8+ T cells was enhanced, immunofluorescent staining of Granzyme B and IFN-γ were performed. In iRFA group, the secretion of Granzyme B and IFN-γ was lower than that in the Saline group and iRFA + Fe-MOF/Cap group (Figure S23). However, the highest secretion of these two markers was observed in the group of iRFA + Fe-MOF/Cap (Figure S23). Therefore, Fe-MOF/Cap not only promoted CD8+ T cells infiltration, but also enhanced the function of CD8+ T cells after iRFA. All above results suggested the potential of Fe-MOF/Cap for significant enhancement of immunotherapy following iRFA.

Anti-HCC effect of iRFA combined with Fe-MOF/Cap in vivo

The above in vitro and in vivo experiments have confirmed that Fe-MOF/Cap has the potential to activate anti-tumor immunity following iRFA. Subsequently, in vivo anti-tumor experiments were conducted to assess the efficacy of Fe-MOF/Cap. When the tumor volume arrived at 200 mm3, the mice were divided into four groups (Saline, iRFA, Fe-MOF/Cap, iRFA + Fe-MOF/Cap). Fe-MOF/Cap was injected via tail vein on Day 0, Day 1 and Day 2. What’s more, the mice in group of iRFA and iRFA + Fe-MOF/Cap were treated with iRFA (5 W, 10 s) at Day 2 (Fig. 7a). Then, the mice were sacrificed at Day 14 and tumor tissues were collected for photographing and weighing. As shown in Fig. 7b and c, the largest tumor was observed in the group of Saline and the smallest tumor was observed in the group of iRFA + Fe-MOF/Cap. The application of iRFA alone did not result in an effective therapeutic outcome. However, the combination of iRFA with Fe-MOF/Cap demonstrated a notable inhibitory effect on tumor growth. Tumor volume in the iRFA group was 2.4-fold larger than that in the iRFA + Fe-MOF/Cap group (Fig. 7b and c). Then, the isolated tumor was weighted, and the tumor inhibition rate was calculated. The average tumor weights were 0.77 g, 0.62 g, 0.47 g, and 0.29 g in Saline, iRFA, Fe-MOF/Cap and iRFA + Fe-MOF/Cap groups, respectively (Fig. 7d). The tumor inhibition rates were 19.1%, 38.6% and 61.6% in group of iRFA, Fe-MOF/Cap and iRFA + Fe-MOF/Cap, respectively (Fig. 7e). Combined with the results of in vivo immune activation experiments, Fe-MOF/Cap might be able to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of iRFA through anti-tumor immune activation. Then, the in vitro cytotoxicity experiments showed that Fe-MOF/Cap also exhibited cytotoxicity on tumor cells. It could be speculated that Fe-induced ferroptosis might also play an important role in tumor growth inhibition [40, 49]. Over the past 14 days, the body weights of mice didn’t show obvious changes in all groups (Fig. 7f).

Finally, the H&E staining and immunohistochemistry of Ki-67 were used to further evaluate the anti-tumor effect in vivo. In the iRFA + Fe-MOF/Cap group, the highest degree of cellular apoptosis and the lowest level of cellular proliferation were observed (Fig. 7g and Figure S24). All above results suggested that Fe-MOF/Cap could dramatically improve treatment effects of iRFA in HCC treatment.

In vivo anti-metastatic evaluation of Fe-MOF/Cap

Lung metastasis was the most common site of extrahepatic metastasis in HCC, and iRFA has been shown to promote this process. Therefore, the ability of Fe-MOF/Cap to inhibit lung metastasis of HCC after iRFA was evaluated. According to the literature, the injection of tumor cells into the tail vein was a method to simulate lung metastasis [53–56]. As shown in Fig. 8a, the lung metastasis model was established by injection of Hepa 1–6 cells by tail vein two weeks before treatment, and then the subcutaneous HCC model was established one week before treatment using the Hepa 1–6 cells. Subsequently, Fe-MOF/Cap was intravenously injected via tail vein at Day 0, Day 1 and Day 2. And the subcutaneous tumors were treated with iRFA (5 W, 10 s) in the iRFA and iRFA + Fe-MOF/Cap groups at Day 2 (Fig. 8a). At Day 14, the mice were sacrificed and the lung was collected and fixed in Bouin’s solution for 72 h. It could be seen from the photograph that a great number of pulmonary metastatic nodules were observed in Saline treated group and the most lung metastatic nodules were observed in iRFA treated group (Fig. 8b and c). In the Fe-MOF/Cap and iRFA + Fe-MOF/Cap treated groups, the number of lung metastatic nodules was significantly reduced compared to the Saline and iRFA groups (Fig. 8b and c). Then, the H&E of lung tissue revealed that iRFA has the largest area of lung metastasis and Fe-MOF/Cap could obviously reduce the area of lung metastasis after iRFA (Fig. 8d and Figure S25). All above results suggested that iRFA promoted the lung metastasis of HCC, while Fe-MOF/Cap could effectively inhibit the lung metastasis of HCC after iRFA. Finally, the anti-metastatic mechanism was studied. iRFA was found to activate the p-MET/p-STAT3/VEGF signaling axis, which was closely related to tumor metastasis (Fig. 8e and Figure S26). Therefore, iRFA might promote lung metastasis by activating the p-MET/p-STAT3/VEGF signaling pathway. However, Fe-MOF/Cap could effectively inhibit the phosphorylation of c-MET and thus reduces lung metastasis of HCC after iRFA. It could be concluded that iRFA promoted lung metastasis of HCC by activating c-MET/STAT3/VEGF pathway, and subsequently Fe-MOF/Cap had the potential to inhibit this pathway and thus reduce lung metastasis after iRFA.

In vivo biosafety evaluation

Biosafety was a critical parameter for the clinical application of biomaterials. Hemolysis assay, blood biochemistry, blood routine and H&E staining of heart, liver, spleen, lungs and kidneys were used to evaluate the in vivo biosafety of Fe-MOF/Cap. When the concentration of Fe-MOF/Cap reached 4 mg/mL, its hemolysis rate was still less than 1% (Figure S28). Then, the blood biochemistry and blood routine of each group was detected and the results revealed that there was no obvious change 14 days after different treatments (Figure S29 and Figure S30). In order to assess long-term toxicity in vivo, the blood routine 28 days after different treatments was performed. The results shown that the dose of Fe-MOF/Cap used in the experiment did not cause long-term toxicity (Figure S31). Finally, the main organs of each group were analyzed by H&E staining and no obvious damage was observed (Fig. 9). All of the above results indicated that Fe-MOF/Cap has a favorable biosafety and was promising for clinical use.

Biosafety. The H&E staining of heart, liver, spleen, lungs and kidneys in group of Saline, Fe-MOF/Cap, iRFA and iRFA + Fe-MOF/Cap. Scale bar: 100 μm

Although Fe-MOF/Cap was very effective to reverse immunosuppressive microenvironment and inhibit tumor metastasis of HCC after iRFA, this study still has some limitations. Firstly, this study was unable to adequately exclude the influence of subcutaneous tumors on lung metastasis. Secondly, using tumor tissues to detect PD-L1 did not clearly identify which specific cells in the tumor tissues expressed PD-L1 and it only provided a brief validation of the function for CD8+ T cells. Thirdly, the tumor immune microenvironment is very complicated. There are many types of immune cells. Besides macrophages, the changes of other immune cells may also influence the therapeutic efficacy of immunotherapy. This study did not specifically demonstrate the effect of M1-polarized macrophages on tumor growth in vivo.

Conclusions

In conclusion, a rod-shaped Fe-MOF/Cap was prepared for enhancing immunotherapy and inhibiting lung metastasis of HCC after iRFA. The successful preparation of Fe-MOF/Cap was confirmed by XPS, UV-vis, FTIR and mapping. The TEM showed that the Fe-MOF/Cap has a length of about 650 nm and a width of about 50 nm. Fe-MOF/Cap exhibited excellent biosafety and improved the therapeutic outcome of iRFA by reversing the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment as well as inhibiting lung metastasis of HCC. Firstly, Fe-MOF/Cap promoted T-cell infiltration by remodeling TAMs and inhibiting PD-L1 expression on tumor cells. This process remodeled the immune microenvironment and might promote immunotherapy after iRFA. In vivo tumor inhibition experiments further confirmed its significant anti-tumor effects in combination with iRFA. Secondly, Fe-MOF/Cap reduced lung metastasis of HCC after iRFA by inhibiting the c-MET/STAT3/VEGF pathway. The lung metastasis model showed the excellent anti-metastatic effect of Fe-MOF/Cap after iRFA. In conclusion, this study presented a novel nanoparticle, Fe-MOF/Cap, which not only has the potential to enhance immunotherapy but also reduces lung metastasis of HCC after iRFA. These findings provided a new clinical strategy that might be clinically applied to improve iRFA outcomes in HCC treatment.
