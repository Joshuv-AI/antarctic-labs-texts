Molecular mechanisms of astragaloside-IV in hepatocellular carcinoma therapy: a systematic review
Authors: Xin Gao, Wen Hao, Yike Wang, Xiaqiu Wu, Feiye Zhu, Yongsheng Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAstragaloside IV (AS-IV), a key monomeric compound extracted from the traditional Chinese medicine Astragalus membranaceus, has demonstrated significant therapeutic potential in cancer treatment. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), as a malignant tumor posing severe threats to global health, is characterized by high incidence and mortality rates, imposing substantial burdens on patients, families, and society. Numerous studies have demonstrated that AS-IV exhibits significant inhibitory effects on HCC. However, the precise underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Therefore, this systematic review provides a comprehensive summary of current research findings.MethodsThis systematic review comprehensively evaluates the molecular mechanisms of AS-IV in HCC through extensive literature retrieval from five authoritative databases, encompassing studies published from their inception to March 2025.ResultsA total of 172 potentially relevant articles were retrieved through database search. After screening, a total of 16 articles finally met the qualification criteria.ConclusionCurrent evidence indicates that AS-IV exerts crucial therapeutic effects in HCC through multiple pathways: inhibiting tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion; inducing apoptosis; modulating immune responses; reducing drug resistance while enhancing chemosensitivity; and suppressing angiogenesis. However, large-scale, well-designed multicenter randomized controlled trials are warranted to establish more robust clinical evidence, thereby solidifying the foundation for AS-IV's broader application in HCC therapeutics.Keywords: Astragaloside-IV, Hepatocellular carcinoma, Malignant tumors, Molecular mechanisms

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the sixth most common malignancy worldwide, ranks among the top three causes of cancer-related mortality in terms of disease burden. According to estimates from the WHO International Agency for Research on Cancer, HCC accounted for over 870,000 new cases and 760,000 deaths globally in 2022 [1, 2]. Despite significant advancements in HCC diagnosis and treatment in recent years—including innovations in surgical techniques, optimization of radiotherapeutic strategies, iterative development of chemotherapeutic agents, precision-targeted therapies, breakthroughs in immunotherapy, and the adoption of multidisciplinary collaboration models—the overall survival rates for advanced HCC patients remain suboptimal, with limited prognostic improvements [3, 4]. Furthermore, the high recurrence rate and risk of distant metastasis exacerbate clinical management challenges, creating a"treatment-relapse-retreatment"vicious cycle that imposes immense burdens on patients'families and healthcare systems [5]. Consequently, the development of innovative therapeutic strategies and novel anticancer agents has become an urgent priority in HCC research. Natural plant resources, serving as a"green treasure trove"for drug discovery, are increasingly recognized as a promising source of bioactive small-molecule compounds for anticancer drug development [6]. These compounds exhibit multi-target and multi-pathway mechanisms of action, offering diverse exploration pathways for HCC treatment—from traditional chemotherapy to molecularly targeted approaches, and from monotherapy to combination regimens [7]. With continuous advancements in modern separation and purification technologies, structural elucidation methods, and pharmacological evaluation systems, the translational potential of plant-derived small-molecule compounds in HCC drug development is becoming increasingly evident, holding promise for delivering breakthrough solutions to this clinical challenge [8].

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) has garnered increasing international attention in comprehensive cancer therapy due to its multi-target regulatory properties, low toxicity, and favorable clinical tolerability [9]. Natural products, especially plant-derived components, have demonstrated unique advantages in cancer therapy. For example, paclitaxel extracted from the bark of Taxus brevifolia has been used as one of the core drugs in combination chemotherapy regimens for treating breast cancer, ovarian cancer, non-small cell lung cancer, etc [10]. Additionally, a randomized controlled trial has shown that berberine, an alkaloid component from the traditional Chinese medicine Coptis chinensis, can prevent the recurrence of colonic adenomas [11]. Therefore, plant-based therapies hold broad application prospects in cancer treatment. Astragalus membranaceus (Huangqi), a classic medicine of TCM with a millennia-long history, was first documented as a superior Qi-tonifying herb in the Shennong Bencao Jing (Divine Farmer’s Classic of Materia Medica). Its immune-modulating effects, epitomized by the principle of"strengthening vital Qi and stabilizing constitutional integrity,"have been extensively validated by modern pharmacology [12]. Rich in bioactive constituents such as saponins, flavonoids, and polysaccharides, Astragalus is particularly notable for Astragaloside-IV (AS-IV)—a characteristic lanostane-type tetracyclic triterpenoid saponin first isolated and structurally characterized from Astragalus roots by Kitagawa et al. in 1983. AS-IV is now recognized as the official quality marker for Astragalus in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia [13]. Chemically designated as 3-O-β-D-xylopyranosyl-6-O-β-D-glucopyranosyl-cycloastragenol (C41H68O14), Fig. 1 shows AS-IV’s chemical structure. AS-IV serves not only as the principal bioactive compound underlying Astragalus’s pharmacological effects but also demonstrates remarkable therapeutic efficacy in cardiovascular protection, hepatorenal preservation, mitigation of diabetic complications, and antitumor activity [14]. In oncology, accumulating evidence highlights AS-IV’s potent inhibitory effects across multiple cancer types [15]. Recent studies further reveal its strong anti-HCC activity mediated through diverse molecular mechanisms, though its precise therapeutic pathways in HCC remain incompletely elucidated [16].

Chemical structure of astragaloside IV

This review systematically synthesizes and critically analyzes existing research to comprehensively delineate the potential mechanisms of AS-IV in HCC treatment. Our review focus on its interventional effects on critical pathophysiological processes driving HCC progression. Our team conducted rigorous bias risk assessments of included studies, systematically evaluated dose–response gradients and administration routes, and dissected AS-IV’s molecular targets and signal transduction pathways underlying its anti-HCC effects. Our review systematically reviews the potential therapeutic targets of AS-IV in HCC, aiming to refine AS-IV-based treatment strategies and accelerate innovative advancements in HCC therapy.

Material and methods

Data sources and search strategy

This review referenced the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines [17] and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [18]. Two researchers (XG and WH) conducted searches using PubMed, Cochrane, EMBASE, the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) database, and Wanfang Data to retrieve eligible studies from inception up to March 2025. The search keywords used were"astragaloside IV"and"Hepatocellular carcinoma,"along with additional terms such as"astraloside,""ASIV,""astragalus,""astragaloside,""AS,""Huangqi,""Hepatoma,""Hepatic Neoplasm,"and"liver cancer."Furthermore, a manual review of the reference lists in relevant literature was performed to ensure that no eligible articles were overlooked.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion Criteria: (1) The experimental drug must be AS-IV. The experimental group should consist of animals treated solely with AS-IV, while the control group should not receive any other pharmacological interventions; (2) The experimental subjects must be either hepatocellular carcinoma cells or animals diagnosed with hepatocellular carcinoma through appropriate modeling techniques.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Studies such as reviews, abstracts, reports, letters, etc.; (2) Studies with incomplete or duplicate data; (3) Studies where the experimental group received a combination of AS-IV with other drugs.

Study selection and data collection

Two researchers (XG and WH) independently extracted relevant data. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, ultimately leading to a consensus. The specific data extracted from the included studies were as follows: authors, cell types, animal species, drug dosage, drug exposure time, and the molecular mechanisms of AS-IV in the treatment of HCC. Risk of bias assessment

Two researchers (XG and WH) independently assessed the risk of bias in the included studies. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion to reach a consensus. For the in vitro experimental component, the OHAT Risk of Bias Tool was employed to systematically evaluate the quality of cell model studies [35]. For the in vivo experimental component, a comprehensive methodological review of animal studies was conducted using the SYRCLE Risk of Bias Tool [36]. Low Risk is defined as studies that employed appropriate randomization methods to generate the allocation sequence, ensuring the unpredictability of assignment. High Risk is defined as studies that either did not use any randomization methods or utilized inadequate randomization procedures, resulting in an allocation sequence generation method that may introduce selection bias, and unclear Risk is defined as studies that provided insufficient information to judge the adequacy of the random sequence generation method, rendering it impossible to assess its appropriateness. Each component was assigned a rating of"high risk,""low risk,"or"unclear risk."

Results

A total of 172 potentially relevant articles were retrieved through database searches, and an additional 9 relevant reports were identified through manual searching. After removing 36 duplicate papers, 145 papers underwent further scrutiny based on their titles and abstracts. Among these, 110 were deemed irrelevant, leaving 35 papers available for full-text reading. Ultimately, 19 records were excluded, leaving 16 that met the eligibility criteria and underwent final analysis. Each step of the study selection process was independently conducted and cross-checked by two investigators. Effects of astragaloside IV on hepatocellular carcinoma

This review systematically collates and thoroughly analyzes the existing research findings on the use of AS-IV in the treatment of HCC, and delves into the underlying pharmacological mechanisms of its therapeutic effects. The current research results strongly highlight the potential application value and positive effects of AS-IV in the field of HCC treatment. Additionally, this study has developed original schematic diagrams (Figs. 5– 6) illustrating the potential molecular mechanisms underlying AS-IV-mediated treatment of liver cancer. Figure 5 depicted the regulatory effects of AS-IV on apoptosis-related pathways, including cell proliferation, metastasis, and invasion, while Fig. 6 depicted the regulatory effects of AS-IV on the apoptosis-related pathways of apoptosis, immune regulation, inhibits angiogenesis, reverses drug resistance and enhances chemosensitivity.

Astragaloside IV inhibits proliferation

The rapid proliferation of malignant tumor cells is a hallmark of tumor progression and deterioration. Blocking the proliferation of cancer cells can inhibit tumor progression and metastasis, leading to better prognostic outcomes for patients [37]. Almost all studies have demonstrated that AS-IV exerts a significant inhibitory effect on the proliferation of HCC cells. Many of these studies indicate that even low concentrations of AS-IV can inhibit HCC cells, with a notable dose- and time-dependent relationship [20, 21, 29, 31]. However, possibly due to the low concentration of AS-IV, the study by Pengfei Ma et al. indicated that treatment with 5-100 μg/mL AS-IV for 24, 48, and 72 h had no significant effect on the viability of MeCC9 7-e cells [19]. Three studies have shown that AS-IV can inhibit HCC cell proliferation through the Nrf2-mediated TGF-β/Smad3 pathway [21, 31, 32], while also suppressing intracellular reactive oxygen species levels and regulating different phosphorylation forms of Smad3 to inhibit HCC progression. Succinylation, a prevalent and dynamic post-translational modification predominantly occurring within mitochondria, plays a critical regulatory role in tumor development [38]. A recent study demonstrated that AS-IV inhibits HCC cell proliferation and glycolysis by regulating kat2a-mediated succinylation of PGAM1 [33]. Xiaocui An et al.'s research indicated that AS-IV can inhibit HCC cell proliferation by modulating the NF-κB signaling pathway [20]. Additionally, AS-IV can reduce the expression of anti-apoptotic proteins in HCC cells, thereby inhibiting their proliferation [24]. The effects of AS-IV on HCC cell proliferation are summarized in Fig. 5.

Astragaloside IV inhibits metastasis and invasion

The metastasis and invasion of tumor cells are critical factors contributing to tumor dissemination and poor prognosis. Aberrant reactivation of the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is closely associated with cancer progression and metastasis [39]. Yaling Li et al.'s study found that AS-IV significantly down-regulated the expression of lncRNA-ATB in HCC cells in a dose- and time-dependent manner, inactivating the IL-11/STAT3 signaling pathway, inducing apoptosis of HCC cells, reducing HCC cell viability, and thus inhibiting the metastasis and invasion of HCC cells [22]. Meanwhile, Chengdong Qin et al.'s research demonstrated that AS-IV treatment can reduce the expression of EMT-related molecules by targeting the Akt/GSK-3β/β-catenin pathway, thereby weakening the invasive and migratory capabilities of HCC cells [26], with a clear dose-dependent relationship. Furthermore, AS-IV at various concentrations can inhibit EMT and subsequently suppress the metastasis and invasion of HCC cells by reducing the expression levels of N-cadherin and vimentin, also exhibiting a significant dose-dependent effect [25]. The effects of AS-IV on HCC cell metastasis and invasion are summarized in Fig. 5.

Astragaloside IV induces apoptosis

Apoptosis represents an active, intrinsic form of programmed cell death that not only contributes to the pathogenesis of cancer but also plays a pivotal role in the efficacy of cancer therapy [40]. Currently, numerous studies support the notion that AS-IV can inhibit HCC progression by promoting apoptosis in HCC cells. Xiaocui An et al.'s research demonstrated that AS-IV promotes apoptosis in HCC cells by modulating oxidative stress and the NF-κB signaling pathway, with a concentration-dependent effect [20]. Chunmin Su et al. discovered that AS-IV exhibits cytotoxic and exogenous/endogenous apoptotic effects on HCC cells, triggering G(1) arrest and reducing the expression of anti-apoptotic proteins, thereby facilitating apoptosis in HCC cells [24]. Meanwhile, AS-IV can enhance the expression of Caspase-3 and Caspase-9, promoting apoptosis in HCC cells and inhibiting HCC progression [25]. Additionally, AS-IV induces the upregulation of miR-150-5p both in vitro and in vivo, suppressing β-catenin and increasing the apoptosis rate of HCC cells [28]. The effects of AS-IV on apoptosis in HCC cells are summarized in Fig. 6.

Astragaloside IV promotes immunomodulation

Currently, immunotherapy has emerged as a focal point of interest in recent years. Programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) on cancer cells, as a crucial immune checkpoint in immunotherapy, interacts with programmed death-1 (PD-1) on immune cells, promoting cancer immune evasion [41]. Yang Ma et al.'s study found that AS-IV reduces PD-L1 levels on the cell surface through the miR-135b-5p/CNDP1 pathway, alleviating PD-L1-related immunosuppression and thereby inhibiting HCC progression [30]. Furthermore, AS-IV can suppress HCC by inhibiting M2 polarization of macrophages via the TLR4/NF-κB/STAT3 signaling pathway, significantly reducing tumor volume and body weight in mice [34]. The effects of AS-IV on immunomodulation in HCC are summarized in Fig. 6.

Astragaloside IV reverses drug resistance and enhances chemosensitivity

Tumor patients often develop drug resistance and reduced sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents after prolonged treatment, which can lead to chemotherapy failure and cancer recurrence [42]. Peipei Wang et al.'s research indicated that AS-IV reverses drug resistance in Bel-7402/FU human HCC cells by downregulating MDR1 expression through the inhibition of the JNK/c-Jun/AP-1 signaling pathway [23]. Additionally, AS-IV can enhance the sensitivity of HCC to cisplatin chemotherapy by inhibiting MRP2 expression in HCC cells and HCC-bearing mice [29]. The effects of AS-IV on reversing drug resistance and enhancing chemosensitivity are summarized in Fig. 6.

Astragaloside IV inhibits angiogenesis

Tumorigenesis requires a substantial supply of nutrients and blood, and angiogenesis depends on the secretion of vascular endothelial growth factors (VEGFs) by tumor cells and the proliferation of endothelial cells [43]. Shuo Zhang et al.'s study revealed that AS-IV promotes the expression of miR-122 and downregulates the expression of miR-221, along with reducing the expression of VEGF, hepatocyte growth factor, and thrombosis-related factors such as tissue factor. This leads to a decrease in tumor microvessel count, thereby inhibiting tumor growth and angiogenesis [27]. In addition, AS-IV can reduce VEGF in HepG2 cells in vitro experiments and inhibit the progression of liver cancer at the same time [25]. Discussion

To our knowledge, no existing reviews have systematically focused on the effects of AS-IV on HCC. This synthesis demonstrates that AS-IV inhibits HCC progression through multifaceted mechanisms, including suppression of tumor growth and metastasis, induction of apoptosis, modulation of immune responses, enhancement of chemosensitivity, and inhibition of angiogenesis. Within the HCC therapeutic framework, while surgical resection remains the primary intervention for early-stage patients, postoperative pharmacological management and systemic therapies for advanced cases constitute critical components of comprehensive care [44]. Notably, AS-IV—a bioactive compound derived from traditional Chinese medicine—has recently emerged as a promising candidate in HCC-related research. Its multi-target efficacy and favorable safety profile position it as a potential therapeutic agent, potentially paving the way for novel treatment strategies in HCC management.

The mechanisms underlying the unlimited growth and metastasis of tumors are closely associated with the infinite proliferative capacity of cancer cells [45]. Cellular proliferation is precisely regulated by DNA replication processes and cell cycle-related proteins, with key regulatory proteins including proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), nuclear antigen Ki-67, and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor P21 playing crucial roles in this regulatory system [46, 47]. Multiple high-quality studies demonstrate that AS-IV persistently suppresses hepatocellular carcinoma proliferation through the Nrf2/TGF-β signaling axis [21, 31]. AS-IV not only inhibits hepatocellular carcinoma proliferation to improve patient prognosis but also demonstrates significant inhibitory effects in other cancers. In colon cancer, AS-IV treatment significantly and dose-dependently reduces proliferation in colon cancer cell lines while showing no significant effect on normal colon cells [48]. Moreover, AS-IV has been observed to significantly inhibit proliferation in various cancer types including breast cancer [49], cervical cancer [50], prostate cancer [51], and vulvar squamous cell carcinoma [52].

As malignant tumors typically exhibit high invasiveness and strong metastatic potential—critical factors contributing to poor patient prognosis—inhibiting cancer cell invasion and migration represents a vital therapeutic target [53]. EMT plays a pivotal role in early tumor invasion and metastasis by enabling tumor cells to acquire mesenchymal-like characteristics, reduce intercellular adhesion, and gain invasive capabilities [54]. Current research indicates that AS-IV can inhibit progression in various malignancies including hepatocellular carcinoma [26], gastric cancer [55], cervical cancer [56], and oral cell carcinoma [57] through EMT suppression. AKT, a well-established oncogene, is closely associated with EMT occurrence. Studies by Jihong Zhu et al. demonstrate that AS-IV inhibits EMT in gastric cancer cells by suppressing the PI3K/Akt/NF-κB pathway [58], while research by Yanyan Zhao et al. reveals that AS-IV inhibits cell invasion and metastasis in vulvar squamous cell carcinoma through the TGF-β1/FAK/AKT signaling pathway [59].

Apoptosis, also known as programmed cell death, serves as a crucial defense mechanism for eliminating abnormal cells [60]. This process comprises three stages: initiation signal activation, effector protein cascade activation, and apoptotic body degradation, constituting a core regulatory mechanism for maintaining developmental homeostasis [61]. However, resistance to apoptosis commonly observed in malignancies such as liver cancer, lung cancer, and breast cancer facilitates rapid tumor growth and drug resistance [62]. Recent studies show that AS-IV induces cancer cell apoptosis in various malignancies including hepatocellular carcinoma [20], colorectal cancer [63], lung cancer [64, 65], and vulvar squamous cell carcinoma [52] by targeting the Bcl-2 family protein regulatory network to increase the Bax/Bcl-2 expression ratio, thereby activating the mitochondria-dependent intrinsic apoptotic pathway. In the extrinsic apoptotic pathway, receptors such as Fas ligand and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) activate caspase-8-dependent pathways leading to apoptosis through caspase cascades [66]. AS-IV enhances cleavage and activation of both extrinsic apoptosis-dependent caspase-8 and intrinsic apoptosis-dependent caspase-9 to induce apoptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma cells [24]. In osteosarcoma cells, combination therapy with AS-IV and cisplatin significantly promotes cleavage of caspase-8 and −3 through the Fas/FasL signaling pathway to enhance apoptosis [67].

Breakthroughs in modern medicine have led to the emergence of increasingly effective immunotherapies, which have revolutionized the clinical management of numerous cancer types [68]. Immunotherapy activates the patient’s own immune system to precisely identify and eliminate cancer cells while minimizing damage to normal tissues [69]. Additionally, the immune system’s"memory"function enables sustained monitoring of cancer cells post-treatment, significantly reducing recurrence risks compared to conventional therapies [70]. Beyond its application in hepatocellular carcinoma via immunotherapy [30], AS-IV demonstrates notable efficacy against other cancers. For instance, AS-IV inhibits lung cancer progression by interfering with IDO-mediated immune functions of Tregs and CTLs [71], and remodels the tumor microenvironment to enhance anti-PD-1 therapy [64]. Moreover, AS-IV exerts antitumor effects in colorectal cancer by significantly reducing M2 macrophages and increasing M1 macrophages in mouse models [72].

Chemoresistance remains a critical factor contributing to treatment failure in current malignant tumor management. As chemotherapy applications increase, drug resistance has become a major clinical challenge [73]. The included literature indicates that AS-IV enhances cisplatin chemosensitivity and reverses 5-fluorouracil resistance in HCC patients, thereby inhibiting HCC progression [23, 29]. Consequently, there is rationale to believe that AS-IV may potentiate chemosensitivity and reverse resistance mechanisms, holding significant potential in combination therapeutic regimens. AS-IV enhances paclitaxel sensitivity in breast cancer by targeting oxidant-induced caveolin-1 damage [74]. Furthermore, research by Yanmei Lou et al. reveals that AS-IV activates the Nrf2-mediated signaling pathway to induce P-glycoprotein and breast cancer resistance protein expression, thereby inhibiting breast cancer progression [75]. In non-small cell lung cancer, AS-IV sensitizes cancer cells to gefitinib by regulating SIRT6, effectively suppressing tumor progression [76].

Sustained neovascularization not only accelerates tumor growth and invasion but also promotes malignant progression through dynamic regulatory mechanisms, making abnormal angiogenesis a hallmark of malignancies [77]. Angiogenesis involves multiple key factors, with VEGF being the most clinically significant regulatory hub [78]. Numerous studies demonstrate that VEGF activates endothelial cell-specific signaling pathways to induce neovascular sprouting and lumen formation, providing tumors with nutritional support and metastatic pathways [79, 80]. Beyond its effects in hepatocellular carcinoma, AS-IV significantly reduces VEGF levels in glioma cells and suppresses tumor progression via the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway [81]. Additionally, AS-IV inhibits colorectal cancer progression by reducing VEGF-A production and promoting M2-to-M1 macrophage polarization [72].

Despite demonstrating certain potential in relevant research fields, the application of AS-IV in the treatment of HCC still faces numerous limitations. Firstly, current research on AS-IV for HCC treatment primarily focuses on animal experiments and cell culture studies, lacking sufficient clinical validation data for support. First, during the early stages of drug/therapy development, rigorous preclinical evaluation utilizing cellular and animal models is an internationally recognized standard process. This aims to screen promising candidates, optimize protocols, assess preliminary safety, and understand mechanisms of action, thereby minimizing risks in subsequent human trials and improving success rates. In the absence of clinical trials, we encourage researchers to leverage advanced experimental technologies—such as organoids and patient-derived xenografts—to establish scientifically valid pathways for bridging preclinical findings to clinical applications [82]. Simultaneously, we hope that the exploratory work in this study will attract broader scholarly attention to research on AS-IV for liver cancer treatment, facilitating the translation from preclinical investigations to clinical studies. Second, most included animal studies did not report specific therapeutic outcome metrics such as post-treatment tumor volume. Consequently, rigorous meta-analysis of concrete efficacy indicators could not be performed. Meanwhile, due to objective reasons such as conditions, we cannot guarantee the consistency of the environment and feed in in vivo experiments. Therefore, confounding factors such as the environment and diet in animal experiments may affect the reliability of the results. In the included studies, the dosage of AS-IV varied significantly across different research protocols. In vitro studies demonstrated inhibitory effects on HCC at concentrations ranging from 4–200 μM, while in vivo experiments showed similar anticancer activity at doses from 20–150 mg/kg. This dosage variability may stem from differences in AS-IV purity across commercial sources, variations in HCC cell lines or animal models employed, divergent mechanistic pathways under investigation, and disparities in experimental conditions. Although current in vivo experiments indicate that different doses of AS-IV cause no significant impairment to the liver and kidneys of mice, rigorous preclinical validation and clinical trials are still necessary to determine the clinically effective and safe dosage range of AS-IV for hepatocellular carcinoma treatment. Due to the lack of clinical data, preclinical evidence suggesting dose-dependent hepatorenal toxicity in animal models warrants vigilance.

Moreover, due to existing experimental and evidence limitations, our exploration of immune modulation and angiogenesis mechanisms may lack sufficient depth. The conclusions regarding VEGF regulation rely heavily on support from a single literature source, with a lack of corroborating evidence from other robust studies. This may introduce certain limitations to the findings. Additionally, many included studies were rated as having an unclear risk of bias in multiple domains. This may introduce systematic errors in study design, implementation, or analysis, potentially compromising the validity of the findings. Finally, owing to language barriers, this review has only consulted English and Chinese databases, failing to collect relevant research materials reported in other languages. This may introduce a certain degree of selection bias in the research conclusions.

Conclusion

In summary, this review systematically summarizes the molecular mechanisms by which AS-IV inhibits HCC in both in vivo and in vitro settings. It was found that AS-IV can suppress HCC progression both in vivo and in vitro by inhibiting tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion; inducing apoptosis; modulating immune responses; reversing drug resistance; enhancing chemosensitivity; and suppressing angiogenesis. Therefore, AS-IV shows promise as an anti-HCC therapeutic agent. However, its primary mechanisms of action, safe dosage, and effective dosage still require further clarification through large-scale clinical trials in the future.
