Portable mixed reality navigation system for neurosurgery: a clinical feasibility study
Authors: Zhongjie Shi, Yilong Peng, Xin Gao, Xiaojun Li, Gang Chen, Shujie Sun, Zhanxiang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to develop a portable mixed reality navigation (PMRN) system for neurosurgery and assess its feasibility, providing design insights for the optimization and wider adoption of mixed reality navigation. The PMRN system linked a head-mounted display (HMD) and a portable laptop via a router, forming a local network. Active infrared tracking enabled real-time localization of a custom surgical probe. Accuracy was first tested in a laboratory using simulation models, then evaluated in a prospective clinical study of 42 patients with intracranial lesions. Clinically, the probe of a traditional optical navigation (TON) system was placed at PMRN-localized targets, and the Euclidean distance between the two systems was measured to quantify localization error. Doctors also subjectively assessed the alignment of mixed reality holograms with patient anatomy to evaluate reliability. In laboratory testing, two doctors each performed 10 simulated head model localizations, with an average time of under 5 min. The times decreased with practice and stabilized at approximately 3.5 min. The maximum fiducial registration error (FRE) and target registration error (TRE) were 2.5 mm and 2.3 mm, respectively, with no significant difference between doctors. In the clinical study (n = 42 patients), the mean localization time was 4.3 ± 1.0 min, the FRE was 2.2 ± 0.7 mm, and the TRE was 1.7 ± 0.5 mm. Doctors rated the hologram–patient alignment as satisfactory in 40 cases (95%); the remaining two cases showed a 3 mm deviation, which was attributed to slight patient movement or reduced device performance in low-battery mode. The PMRN system is portable, easy to use, and achieves millimeter-level accuracy in neurosurgical procedures with high surgeon satisfaction. It meets the needs of most routine operations but requires further refinement for submillimeter-precision procedures. Introduction

Medical navigation, as a widely applied computer-assisted technology in modern precision surgery, primarily relies on multimodal medical imaging, spatial localization systems, and real-time tracking techniques to provide surgeons with accurate 3D anatomical localization and surgical guidance. This enhances surgical precision, reduces tissue damage, shortens operation time, and lowers intraoperative risks1–3. In neurosurgery, navigation systems offer critical technical support for high-precision cranial procedures4. However, existing optical navigation systems still suffer from complex hardware, bulky size, cumbersome workflows, high costs, and maintenance demands, limiting their broader clinical adoption.

To address these issues, researchers have explored lighter, more economical and simplified navigation solutions, leading to the rise of low-cost portable navigation systems5. Such systems significantly reduce procurement and maintenance costs and improve accessibility and adoption in small- to medium-sized hospitals and resource-limited settings.

The rapid development of mixed reality (MR) technology offers a novel direction for portable navigation6,7. MR-based systems utilize head-mounted displays (HMDs) to accurately overlay virtual 3D anatomical models onto the patient’s surface, enabling surgeons to operate with direct visualization and eliminating the need to frequently look away at external monitors. This greatly improves surgical efficiency and immersion, effectively addressing the “hand–eye dissociation” problem in traditional navigation8.

Nevertheless, current MR navigation technologies are still in early stages and face several challenges. For example, when doctors view virtual models from a single perspective in the MR scene, limited viewing angles and insufficient depth cues often cause parallax errors leading to localization inaccuracies. This phenomenon, termed “Depth Perception Bias (DPB)”, mainly results from the optical projection characteristics of single-viewpoint HMDs, which lack sufficient stereoscopic and depth information, causing errors in judging front-back spatial positions9.

Building upon existing MR navigation techniques, this study designed and developed a portable mixed reality navigation system (PMRN) that integrates 3D visualization with a 2D guidance panel, enabling synchronized 2D/3D display to effectively mitigate DPB. Through a structured experimental design, this study aims to validate the clinical feasibility of PMRN and provide innovative insights and technical references for the optimization and clinical translation of MR navigation systems.

Materials and methods

System architecture

The PMRN system consists of hardware and software components. The hardware includes a Microsoft HoloLens 2, a standard consumer-grade router, and a mid-range laptop. The laptop is primarily responsible for data processing, while the router establishes a local area network to enable communication between the laptop and the HoloLens 2. Figure 1 illustrates the overall system architecture.

PMRN hardware components and architecture. The laptop reads medical images and performs 3D reconstruction. It connects to the HoloLens 2 via a local area network to transmit 3D model data, thereby integrating MR into the surgical environment.

The spatial stability of virtual models in the MR environment primarily depends on the simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) algorithm within the HoloLens 2, which uses spatial feature recognition for localization10. To enable tracking of dynamic surgical tools, we independently designed a surgical probe composed of a rigid metal frame and four high-reflective spheres identical to those used in clinical optical navigation systems. Each sphere has a diameter of 12 mm and is fixed in position by the metal frame to ensure stable spatial arrangement.

The software consists of the PMRN main program running on HoloLens 2 and a control program installed on the laptop. The main program handles the import, rendering, and display of 3D medical models, as well as real-time tracking of the surgical probe. The control program communicates with the main program via the local area network, allowing remote adjustment of model properties such as transparency, color, and visibility, enabling flexible human-computer interaction.

Although MR navigation typically supports gesture-based interaction, frequent or large hand movements during surgery risk contaminating the sterile field. To address this, we introduced voice command keywords, enabling surgeons to perform model registration and other operations verbally. This reduces unnecessary hand movements and enhances operational efficiency and system usability.

To mitigate DPB, two auxiliary panels were added to the software to display the current position of the probe tip from different angles in real time (Fig. 2).

Laboratory evaluation

The PMRN system employs a point-based registration method to align the virtual model with the patient11. During model reconstruction, four characteristic points are predefined. These points can be anatomical landmarks such as the nasal root, nasal tip, tragus, medial canthus, or lateral canthus, or artificially added physical markers.

During registration, the surgical probe is used to select the corresponding points on the patient’s surface. The system then automatically computes the optimal transformation matrix between the two point sets to achieve automatic registration. To evaluate the accuracy of PMRN, we designed 10 simulated head models with varying shapes. Each model’s surface was randomly marked with 10 spherical markers for registration purposes. Additionally, 5 spherical markers were randomly placed at different internal locations within each model to serve as target points. Each spherical marker had a diameter of approximately 3 mm. Thin-slice CT scans (slice thickness ≤ 1 mm) were performed on each head model to obtain DICOM data. Three-dimensional reconstruction of the virtual models was completed using 3D Slicer (https://www.slicer.org, version 5.7) software13.

Two neurosurgeons (Doctor A and Doctor B), both without prior experience in MR software, received 30 min of training before independently using PMRN. For each head model, four non-coplanar surface spherical markers were randomly selected as registration reference points. Each model was registered three times, and the average was taken as the FRE for that model. Subsequently, the surgical probe was used to localize each target point. The localization error, measured as the distance between the actual and true positions, was recorded three times per target, and the average was taken as the TRE. The time from the start of model registration to completion of all target localizations was recorded for each case to preliminarily assess the learning curve of the two neurosurgeons. All data were independently recorded by personnel proficient in MR localization techniques to ensure objectivity and accuracy.

Imaging acquisition and data processing

All patients underwent preoperative CT and MRI scanning with continuous thin-slice acquisition (slice thickness ≤ 1 mm). Imaging data were exported in DICOM format, anonymized, and imported into 3D Slicer software. A biomedical engineering PhD experienced in the software, together with a senior neurosurgeon with over 20 years of experience, performed 3D reconstructions based on lesion location. Reconstruction included skin, lesion, and bony landmarks for registration. According to the surgical approach, the lesion’s scalp projection was reconstructed on the skin model. The projection boundary and center were used as references, and the corresponding CT regions were filled with 1000 HU values. After processing, images were resampled and re-exported in DICOM format for use with the traditional optical navigation (TON) system. The 3D models were exported for use with the PMRN system (Fig. 3A–F).

Surgical workflow

To minimize interference between the two navigation systems and reduce errors from differing workflows, PMRN and TON were not used to localize targets independently. Instead, after PMRN localization, a physical mark was made on the scalp at the target point. The TON probe tip was then placed directly on this mark, and the TON system was used to measure the Euclidean distance to the planned target. This distance represented the localization error between PMRN and TON. This approach ensured that both systems measured the same physical location, thereby minimizing errors caused by workflow differences or intraoperative environmental changes (Fig. 3G–I).

Intraoperatively, after navigation registration, the doctor evaluated the PMRN system from two perspectives: anatomical congruence, referring to the degree of overlap between the skin model and the patient’s scalp; and visual clarity of the path, referring to the stability and intuitiveness of the MR display. It should be noted that, in this study, the final surgical approach and operation were determined based on the surgeon’s clinical judgment, with the localization results provided by PMRN serving only as an intraoperative auxiliary reference.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Python version 3.12.6. All quantitative variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). In the laboratory phase, paired t-tests were used to compare the differences between the two surgeons in terms of localization time, FRE, and TRE. A learning curve was plotted based on localization time to evaluate changes in time consumption with increasing trial numbers. In the clinical phase, intraoperative time, FRE, and TRE were calculated for each case and analyzed descriptively. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Laboratory

Both doctors completed localization on 10 simulated head models. The mean localization time was 4.1 ± 0.9 min (range: 3.3–6.2 min) for Doctor A and 4.3 ± 1.0 min (range: 3.2–6.6 min) for Doctor B. Paired t-test analysis showed no significant difference between the two doctors (t = 1.16, p = 0.28). The learning curve plotted from localization time (Fig. 4A) suggested a gradual reduction in time with increasing trials, eventually stabilizing at approximately 3.5 min. Qualitative evaluation

The doctor subjectively assessed the congruence between the MR images and the patient’s anatomical structures from two perspectives. Among the 42 cases, 2 showed visible deviations of approximately 3 mm. One case was likely related to slight patient bed movement after registration, while the other occurred after the HoloLens 2 entered a low-power, reduced-performance mode and subsequently resumed operation, presumably due to decreased SLAM tracking accuracy affecting model overlap precision. Both cases achieved satisfactory alignment following manual adjustment, and the remaining 40 cases were rated as satisfactory. Discussion

As a novel medical navigation approach, PMRN has fundamentally transformed the traditional navigation paradigm in neurosurgery by providing intuitive 3D visualization and mitigating the “hand–eye dissociation” inherent in TON surgery. Its compact, portable design makes it particularly suitable for operating rooms with limited space and for deployment in resource-constrained settings. Nevertheless, due to its current localization accuracy, PMRN may have certain limitations in procedures requiring submillimeter precision, such as deep brain stimulation (DBS) and brain biopsy14,15. For most routine neurosurgical procedures, including intracerebral hematoma evacuation, external ventricular drainage, and the majority of brain tumor resections (e.g., meningiomas), the achieved millimeter-level accuracy is sufficient16.

Comparison with previous MR/AR systems

Several MR/AR-based surgical navigation systems have been reported in recent years, with variations in hardware platforms, tracking modalities, registration techniques, and clinical validation stages17,18. For example, a previously reported MR navigation system combined the Vuforia platform with HoloLens, achieving approximately 1.7 mm accuracy through image-based tracking via the color camera—comparable to the accuracy typically achieved with near-infrared optical tracking, as in our system—and simplifying certain development processes19. However, its reliance on patient-specific 3D-printed markers resulted in a relatively long preoperative preparation time (about 30 min), potentially limiting its use in time-critical or emergency cases. In contrast, the PMRN system utilizes the depth camera’s near-infrared tracking mode, effectively mitigating the impact of visible-light interference in the operating room and thereby achieving slightly higher localization accuracy. In addition, the PMRN system introduces voice keyword recognition to simplify the surgeon’s intraoperative interactions, reducing the need for frequent hand gestures and thereby minimizing the risk of contaminating the sterile field. It also leverages the depth camera’s near-infrared tracking mode to reduce the impact of visible light interference in the operating room, achieving slightly higher localization accuracy. Furthermore, the control interface is relocated to a laptop for remote operation within the local network, allowing the surgeon to concentrate fully on probe tracking while an assistant adjusts display settings as needed.

Evaluation of PMRN performance and clinical applicability

Preliminary laboratory studies demonstrated that the PMRN system exhibits good robustness across different operators. Testing by two neurosurgeons revealed a steep learning curve, with the average localization time stabilizing at approximately 3.5 min after around five localization attempts. Although this time does not include the processing of imaging data, it is negligible within the context of neurosurgical procedures lasting several hours. In the laboratory setting, the system’s navigation registration error did not exceed 2.5 mm.

In the clinical study, we designed an indirect error measurement method to avoid manual measurements directly on the patient’s scalp. Specifically, the position determined by PMRN was precisely localized using the TON system, and the TRE of PMRN was calculated by measuring the real-time Euclidean distance between the probe tip and the target point displayed by TON. Results showed that the clinical navigation registration error of PMRN was 2.2 ± 0.7 mm, and the TRE was 1.7 ± 0.5 mm, slightly higher than those observed in the laboratory tests. This difference may be related to the mobility of the patient’s scalp in the clinical environment—slight displacement of the scalp may occur between preoperative imaging acquisition and intraoperative localization, resulting in the anatomical point not being absolutely fixed.

Furthermore, after craniotomy, cerebrospinal fluid release and changes in intracranial pressure can cause brain shift20. Under such circumstances, localization using PMRN based solely on preoperative imaging may produce larger errors—a challenge faced by many navigation systems. In these scenarios, the use of intraoperative ultrasound may be more advantageous, as it is more portable and cost-effective than intraoperative CT or MRI while still enabling real-time anatomical updates21.

Nevertheless, PMRN provides reliable localization performance for most neurosurgical procedures requiring millimeter-level accuracy, such as intracerebral hematoma evacuation, external ventricular drainage, and brain tumor resection22. For deep targets requiring submillimeter precision, such as DBS, the current system’s performance still has room for improvement. This highlights the need for future advancements in soft tissue shift compensation and high-precision registration algorithm optimization.

System hardware performance evaluation

Similar to most lithium battery-powered electronic devices, the performance of the HoloLens 2 gradually decreases as the battery level drops, entering a low-power, reduced-performance mode23. In our study, we observed that when the battery level fell below 50%—corresponding to more than one hour of continuous operation—system performance degraded, manifesting as delayed voice recognition and jittering of virtual models, likely due to fluctuations in the system’s refresh rate. Therefore, it is advisable to ensure a fully charged device prior to performing mixed reality–assisted surgeries. Although neurosurgical procedures may last several hours, navigation is primarily used for preoperative incision planning and surgical approach localization, particularly in emergency cases such as intracerebral hemorrhage. Moreover, in this study, the average PMRN localization time in the operating room did not exceed 10 min across different surgical types, indicating that the current HoloLens 2 can maintain high performance for a sufficient duration.

In addition, the SLAM functionality relies on spatial features in the environment for localization and mapping. When the surrounding background is overly uniform (e.g., a monochromatic sterile drape), the number of identifiable features is significantly reduced, which may lead to virtual model drift. Conversely, complex backgrounds with richer textures and structures can provide more stable features, thereby enhancing the spatial stability of the virtual model.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, which limits the generalizability of the evaluation results for PMRN localization accuracy. Second, only two neurosurgeons participated in the laboratory testing, which lacks representativeness for a broader user population; therefore, the observed learning curve may not fully reflect adaptation patterns across varying experience levels. Third, although PMRN can achieve millimeter-level accuracy in preoperative localization, it currently lacks the ability to compensate for intraoperative brain shift in real time, which poses limitations for procedures requiring continuous target tracking. Finally, the physical design of the AR headset limits its simultaneous use with an operating microscope, which remains a standard tool in many neurosurgical procedures.

Future directions

Beyond ongoing technical optimization—such as improving registration algorithms, enhancing robustness to soft tissue shift, and refining tracking accuracy—future work will focus on integrating PMRN with existing neurosurgical tools. Given that the AR headset currently limits simultaneous use with an operating microscope, future development will explore seamless integration into microscope-guided workflows. A potential solution is real-time streaming of the microscope’s view into the AR headset, allowing surgeons to access both magnified visualization and navigation guidance without compromising ergonomics or efficiency. Other priorities include promoting adoption in diverse surgical settings, establishing standardized training protocols, and engaging early with regulatory bodies to facilitate approval. Large-scale, multicenter clinical studies will also be essential to validate performance across varied patient populations and surgical environments, supporting the transition of PMRN from prototype to a widely adopted clinical tool.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the PMRN system can achieve millimeter-level target localization accuracy in neurosurgical procedures, while offering advantages in portability, ease of use, and high surgeon satisfaction. It is capable of meeting the navigation requirements for most routine neurosurgical operations and is particularly suitable for use in space-constrained or resource-limited settings. The present work validates its feasibility in a clinical environment and lays the groundwork for the broader adoption of MR navigation in neurosurgery.
