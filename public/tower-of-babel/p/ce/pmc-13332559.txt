Disease Site‐Specific Outcomes in p16‐Positive Non‐Oropharyngeal Mucosal Head and Neck Cancer
Authors: Revadhi C Chelvarajah, Lessandra Y S Chee, Jie Su, Brian O'Sullivan, Christopher M K L Yao, Enrique Sanz‐Garcia, Stephen M Smith, Ilan Weinreb, Scott Bratman, B C John Cho, Ezra Hahn, Andrew Hope, Ali Hosni, John Kim, Andrew McPartlin, Nauman Malik, Chiaojung Jillian Tsai, John de Almeida, Anna Spreafico, Li Tong, Shao Hui Huang, John N Waldron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo report disease‐free survival (DFS) in p16‐positive non‐oropharyngeal head‐and‐neck squamous‐cell carcinoma (HNSCC).MethodsCuratively‐treated non‐oropharyngeal HNSCC (2009–2021) were reviewed. DFS was compared among p16‐positive, p16‐negative, and p16‐untested cases for the overall cohort and by disease site. Multivariable analysis estimated adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) for p16‐positivity.ResultsAmong 3061 non‐oropharyngeal HNSCC, 91 p16+, 375 p16−, 2595 untested; median follow‐up 4.7 years. P16‐positive patients had superior 5‐year DFS versus p16−/untested (74% vs. 48%/59%; aHR 0.40 [0.26–0.61], p < 0.001). This advantage was evident in the larynx (86% vs. 52%/63%; aHR 0.26 [0.09–0.71], p = 0.005), hypopharynx (70% vs. 25%/37%; aHR 0.55 [0.10–0.50], p < 0.001), and nasal cavity (82% vs. 38%/66%; aHR 0.31 [0.11–0.88], p = 0.028). No significant DFS differences were seen in paranasal sinus (40% vs. 64%/41%; p = 0.39) or oral cavity (50% vs. 57%/58%; p = 0.94).Conclusionp16‐positivity confers a DFS benefit in larynx/hypopharynx/nasal cavity, but not in paranasal sinus or oral SCC.Keywords: head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, HPV, non‐oropharyngeal cancer, outcomes, p16 status

Introduction

It is well‐established that p16‐positive status, a surrogate for human papillomavirus (HPV)‐driven tumors in the oropharynx, is associated with favorable oncologic and survival outcomes in oropharyngeal carcinoma [1, 2]. However, the prevalence and prognostic value of p16‐positive status in other mucosal head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) outside of the oropharynx remain uncertain, largely due to selected testing and a paucity of robust outcome data [3, 4].

Evidence regarding the survival benefit of p16/HPV positivity in non‐oropharyngeal HNSCC remains inconsistent. Some studies have reported improved outcomes [5, 6, 7], particularly in laryngeal and hypopharyngeal cancers [8, 9, 10, 11] while others noted no difference [12, 13] or even worse survival outcomes with p16‐positivity, especially in p16‐positive oral cavity tumors [14, 15]. Interpretation of these divergent findings is limited by several factors, including the large proportion of untested patients that results in small analyzable cohorts and substantial heterogeneity in HPV detection methodologies across studies [3, 16, 17].

Assays used to ascertain tumor HPV association vary and include detection of high‐risk HPV DNA via in situ hybridization (ISH) or polymerase chain reaction (PCR), HPV E6/E7 RNA expression by ISH or quantitative reverse transcriptase‐PCR (qRT‐PCR), and/or p16 protein expression by immunohistochemistry (IHC), a surrogate marker of oncogenic HPV in oropharynx [3, 5, 18]. In addition, some testing probes detect only specific high‐risk HPV subtypes, most commonly HPV16, and may miss other oncologic variants.

Our center is a tertiary cancer center treating more than 650 HNSCC patients annually. We maintain a large in‐house prospective database [19] that records p16 and/or HPV testing results and outcomes at the point of care for all head and neck cancer patients treated by radiation and surgical oncologists in a multidisciplinary setting. This comprehensive resource provides an opportunity to assess the rate of p16‐positive non‐oropharyngeal HNSCC and its prognostic relevance in a contemporary cohort.

Methods and Materials

Study Population

This study obtained the institutional Research Ethics Board approval (17‐5871) with individual consent waived due to its retrospective design. We identified all newly diagnosed, non‐metastatic mucosal HNSCC of the larynx, hypopharynx, nasal cavity, paranasal sinus, and oral cavity, treated curatively with either primary surgery (± radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy) or primary radiotherapy (± chemotherapy) between January 2009 and December 2021. Nasopharyngeal carcinoma was excluded, as it was previously reported separately [20]. Large tumors involving both the oropharynx and the hypopharynx or supraglottic larynx were classified according to the tumor epicenter. When the epicenter was ambiguous between the oropharynx and laryngo‐hypopharynx, p16‐positive tumors were preferentially classified as oropharyngeal. Radiology consultation and multidisciplinary peer review established the most likely primary site, with discrepancies resolved by consensus. Consequently, all p16‐positive hypopharyngeal and supraglottic laryngeal tumors demonstrated no or only minimal oropharyngeal extension. Clinical characteristics and outcomes were collected prospectively. Survival outcomes were also linked with the provincial population cancer registry.

Assessment of p16 Status

Since May 2009, we have implemented prospective p16 immunohistochemistry testing for all oropharyngeal carcinomas (OPC) and selected non‐oropharyngeal HNSCC cases. p16 immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining was routinely performed in laryngeal and hypopharyngeal cancer during 2014 and 2017 for EORTC1219‐DAHANCA‐29 trial [21] eligibility (NCT01880359), and otherwise performed at clinicians' discretion, generally for cases without typical risk factors for HNSCC (e.g., non‐smokers/non‐drinkers) or for tumors with non‐keratinizing or basaloid morphology. All p16 IHC was performed and interpreted at our institution by experienced head and neck pathologists, and in accordance with College of American Pathology (CAP) guidelines [18, 22] and ASCO guidelines [23]. p16‐positivity was defined by diffuse cytoplasmic and nuclear staining in > 70% tumor cells. For p16‐positive cases, high‐risk HPV DNA or RNA detection by in situ hybridization (ISH) or polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed in recent years, but not for earlier cases.

Statistical Analysis

Clinical characteristics and disease outcomes were compared between p16‐positive, p16‐negative, and p16‐untested cohorts and reported using descriptive methods in overall and in disease site‐specific cohorts. Kruskal Wallis and Wilcoxon Rank‐Sum tests versus Chi‐Squared tests, Fisher's exact tests, and MC simulation were applied for comparisons of continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Actuarial rates of locoregional control (LRC) and distant control (DC) were estimated using the competing risks method (deaths without LRC or DC were considered as “competing risk”) whilst overall survival (OS) and disease‐free survival (DFS) were estimated with the Kaplan–Meier method from the date of treatment initiation. Outcomes were compared between p16‐positive versus p16‐negative versus p16‐untested. Multivariable analysis (MVA) was performed to evaluate the prognostic value of p16‐positivity versus p16‐negativity and p16‐untested status on DFS and OS in the overall cohort, adjusted for primary tumor site, age, smoking history, TNM8 T‐categories, N‐categories, and use of systemic therapy. Additional MVAs were performed separately for the larynx, hypopharynx, nasal cavity, paranasal sinus, and oral cavity. All tests were two‐sided, with a p value < 0.05 considered statistical significance.

Results

Prevalence and Clinical Characteristics of Study Patients

A total of 3061 consecutive non‐oropharyngeal HNSCC were identified, including 1110 larynx, 225 hypopharynx, 117 nasal cavity, 57 paranasal sinus and 1552 oral cavity SCC. P16 was tested in 466 (15%) patients, revealing 91 p16‐positive (26 larynx, 20 hypopharynx, 30 nasal cavity, 5 paranasal sinus, and 10 oral cavity) and 375 p16‐negative (162 larynx, 67 hypopharynx, 17 nasal cavity, 14 paranasal sinus, and 115 oral cavity). Table 1 depicts the rates of p16‐positive non‐oropharyngeal HNSCC in the overall and p16‐tested cohorts and by disease sites. Table E1 compared baseline characteristics of p16‐tested versus p16‐untested cases. Most characteristics between p16‐tested versus untested cohort were comparable, including sex (p = 0.45), smoking pack‐years (p = 0.20), smoking status (p = 0.30), TNM8 T‐category (p = 0.15), and overall stage (p = 0.13). Modest differences were observed in age (median 62.5 vs. 65.3 years, p < 0.001), ECOG performance status (ECOG 2–4: 8% vs. 12%, p = 0.12), and more obviously in N‐category distribution (N2–3: 40% vs. 27%, p < 0.001). DFS and OS were also similar (Figure E1).

Outcomes and Prognostic Value of p16 Status in Overall Cohort

Median follow‐up was 4.7 years (range 0.1–15.7). Patients with p16‐positive non‐oropharyngeal HNSCC had higher 5‐year DFS (74% [65–84] vs. 48% [43–54] vs. 59% [57–61], p < 0.001), OS (80% [72–90] vs. 59% [54–65] vs. 65% [63–67], p < 0.004), LRC (90% [80–94] vs. 74% [69–79] vs. 79% [78–81], p = 0.005) and DC (92% [83–96] vs. 80% [75–84] vs. 89% [87–90], p < 0.001) vs. p16‐negative vs. untested cohort (Table 3 and Figure 1). The survival plots for p16‐untested cases closely resembled that of the p16‐negative cohort. Kaplan–Meier plots of LRC by p16 status across the various disease sites are presented in Figure E2.

For the nasal cavity and paranasal sinus subset, the MVA was adjusted only for age and N categories due to the small sample size and limited number of events. Bolded p values indicate statistical significance.

Outcomes by p16 Status by Disease Sites

Larynx and Hypopharynx

Patients with p16‐positive laryngeal SCC (vs. p16‐negative vs. p16‐untested) had a higher 5‐year DFS [86% (73–100) vs. 52% (44–61) vs. 63% (60–66), p = 0.004], OS [90% (78–100) vs. 64% (56–73) vs. 70% (66–73), p = 0.036], LRC (100% vs. 78% [70–84] vs. 83% [80–86], p = 0.030), and DC [92% (68–98) vs. 81% (73–86) vs. 91% (89–93), p < 0.001], respectively. MVA confirmed the prognostic value of p16‐positivity (vs. p16‐negativity) for DFS [aHR 0.26 (0.09–0.71), p = 0.008] and OS [aHR 0.27 (0.08–0.85), p = 0.025] (Table 3 and Figure 2A).

Kaplan–Meier plots of disease‐free survival by p16 status in the larynx (A), hypopharynx (B), nasal Cavity (C), paranasal Sinus (D), and oral cavity (E). [Color figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary.com]

Patients with p16‐positive hypopharyngeal SCC (vs. p16‐negative vs. p16‐untested) also had a higher 5‐year DFS [70% (53–93) vs. 25% (15–40) vs. 37% (29–46), p < 0.001], OS [79% (62–100) vs. 37% (25–54) vs. 42% (34–52), p = 0.004], but no significant difference in LRC [85% (56–95) vs. 68% (54–77) vs. 69% (60–76), p = 0.219], or DC [80% (51–92) vs. 67% (53–76) vs. 69% (60–76), p = 0.319]. MVA confirmed the significant prognostic value of p16‐positivity in DFS [aHR 0.22 (0.10–0.50), p < 0.001] (Table 3 and Figure 2B).

Nasal Cavity and Paranasal Sinus

Patients with p16‐positive nasal cavity SCC (vs. p16‐negative vs. p16‐untested) had a non‐significant but numerically higher DFS [82% (69–98) vs. 38% (19–76) vs. 66% (55–79), p = 0.053], OS [86% (74–100) vs. 44% (22–87) vs. 80% (70–91), p = 0.072], and LRC [89% (66–96) vs. 61% (24–80) vs. 77% (64–85), p = 0.103]. MVA showed that p16‐positivity was associated with higher DFS [aHR 0.31 (0.11–0.88), p = 0.028] and trended toward higher OS [aHR 0.34 (0.11–1.07), p = 0.066].

Only five cases of p16‐positive paranasal sinus SCC were identified. There was no significant difference in DFS (p = 0.456), OS (p = 0.537), LRC (p = 0.751), and DC (p = 0.463) by p16 status in paranasal sinus SCC (Table 3 and Figure 2C,D).

An exploratory analysis comparing outcomes of p16‐positive nasal cavity (n = 30) vs. paranasal sinus (n = 5) SCC showed that the former had a higher DFS [82% (69–98) vs. 40% (14–100), p = 0.038] although OS was not significantly different [86% (74–100) vs. 60% (29–100), p = 0.153].

Oral Cavity

OSCC cohort contained 10 p16‐positive, 115 p16‐negative, and 1427 p16‐untested cases. No difference in OS (57% vs. 67% vs. 64%, p = 0.42) and DFS (50% vs. 57% vs. 58%, p = 0.869) was observed in p16‐positive versus p16‐negative versus p16‐untested cases (Table 3 and Figure 2E). The lack of prognostic value of p16‐positivity was confirmed by MVA for both OS [aHR 1.07 (0.38–3.05), p = 0.89] and DFS [aHR 1.00 (0.39–2.51), p = 0.99] (Table 4).

Discussion

Our study has confirmed the observations of others that p16‐positive/HPV‐driven SCC can arise in various head and neck disease sites. Using tested cases as the denominator, we observed that the p16‐positive rate varied from 8% to 64% across different disease sites, with the overall prevalence of 20% in the tested cohort. This likely overestimates the prevalence in the overall population due to selection bias in which patients actually undergo testing. The tested population may be enriched for HPV+ HNSCC, including younger patients, non‐/light‐smokers, and those with non‐keratinizing or basaloid morphology. The survival curves of the p16‐untested cohort in the overall cohort closely resemble those of the p16‐negative cohort (Figure 1), supporting this notion. Using both tested and untested cases as the denominator, we observed an overall rate of 3% for the entire population, with a range of 0.6 to 26% across disease‐specific sites. Since survival curves of p16‐untested laryngeal and hypopharyngeal SCC closely resemble those of p16‐negative, we may infer that the rate of 2% in the larynx and 9% in the hypopharynx more accurately reflect the true prevalence of p16/HPV‐positive tumors, and the superior outcomes associated with p16‐positivity appear to be genuine. The observation that DFS in the untested population trends toward superiority over the tested p16‐negative cohort in nasal cavity would suggest a proportion of those untested are p16‐positive. Despite a large untested cohort with potential p16‐positive cases embedded within the p16‐untested group, we observed a prognostic benefit of p16 status in larynx, hypopharynx and nasal cavity subsites, but no apparent survival benefit in paranasal sinus and oral cavity SCC.

Although only 43% (39/91) of p16‐positive tumors underwent HPV‐specific testing, we confirmed the presence of high‐risk HPV DNA or RNA in all, supporting the etiological role of HPV in non‐oropharyngeal HNSCC. Our study contributes to the growing understanding that HPV‐mediated carcinogenesis can affect the entire head and neck mucosal lining, with a higher neoplastic affinity in mucosal tissues rich in lymphocytes. This may be due to the reliance on cytokines during HPV carcinogenesis process. Within the tonsillar lymphoid rich tissues, the deep invaginations of the cryptic epithelium harbor a rich environment of lymphocytes, epithelial cells and inflammatory cells promoting an ideal location for viral deposition. Furthermore, the compromise in epithelial integrity due to the discontinuous epithelium within crypts in this location allows for direct communication of antigen presenting cells and viral particles promoting immune evasion and unregulated growth ultimately permitting carcinogenic pathways [24, 25, 26, 27]. Waldeyer's ring is especially vulnerable since it primarily harbors four tonsillar tissues: “palatine tonsil” and “lingual tonsil” in oropharynx, “tubal tonsil” in nasopharynx, and “pharyngeal tonsil” (a.k.a. “adenoids”) in the skull base and nasopharynx. However, the presence of adjacent pharyngeal tonsillar tissue in the skull base and tubal tonsil in nasopharynx may explain the emerging identification of a relatively high prevalence of HPV associated SCC in the skull base and nasopharynx [20, 28, 29].

Beyond Waldeyer's ring, ectopic tonsillar tissue (a tonsillar tissue that develops in areas outside of the four major tonsil groups) has been reported in other mucosal head and neck subsites, including the larynx [30, 31, 32], hypopharynx [33], nasal cavity [34], and oral cavity [35, 36, 37, 38]. This suggests that HPV‐mediated HNSCC can also arise in anatomic sites outside of the oropharynx and nasopharynx, and may be more prevalent depending on anatomic variations of tonsillar tissue and duration of viral exposure in these sites.

In our cohort, the highest proportion of p16‐positive non‐oropharyngeal HNSCC is found in the nasal cavity, moderate in the hypopharynx and paranasal sinus, and relatively rare in larynx and oral cavity. The prevalence of p16 positive HNSCC outside of oropharynx varies significantly in the literature, likely due to selection bias, testing variation, and difference in sexual behavior [3]. Studies show that the incidence of p16 non‐OPSCC cases is rising, as evident in the significant rise in sinonasal SCC in the US population from 1995 to 2018 particularly after 1999 and seen predominantly in younger patients aged 30–54 years and male gender [39]. Interestingly, a large cohort study reviewing all non‐OPSCC subsites from 1995 to 2012 commented that although OPSCC HPV mediated disease had a rising prevalence, p16 positivity was stable for non‐OPSCCs except for white patients who exhibited a rise from 6% to 19% [40]. Both these studies did not include commentary about the impact of testing rates and whether this may contribute as a confounding factor to prevalence rates.

Our study showed improved DFS in p16‐positive laryngeal, hypopharyngeal, and nasal cavity SCC, but not in paranasal and oral SCC. The prognostic value of HPV status or p16 status in non‐OPSCC head and neck mucosal SCCs has been investigated in several small retrospective series, many with conflicting results. To our knowledge, this is the largest cohort of patients tested over these separate disease subsites. Furthermore, our study includes an analysis of the p16‐untested non‐oropharyngeal HNSCC cohort and shows that their oncological outcomes more closely resembled those of p16‐negative disease, except paranasal sinus, where the untested population showed trends for slightly better outcomes. It is speculated that this group may well contain a small proportion of p6‐positive cases.

In addition to DFS and OS, we also evaluated LRC outcomes in the overall cohort and by each disease site. We found significantly higher LRC in p16‐positive non‐oropharyngeal HNSCCs overall, which contrasts with the findings of Lassen et al. [41], who reported an LRF benefit with p16‐positivity only in oropharyngeal, but not in non‐oropharyngeal, HNSCCs. However, the proportion of non‐oropharyngeal HNSCCs that underwent p16 testing was not reported in their study. There is a clear LRC benefit in laryngeal cases, a possible benefit in the nasal cavity and hypopharynx, reflecting its inherent radiosensitivity. However, no significant difference in outcomes was observed in p16‐positive SCC of the paranasal sinus and oral cavity, possibly due to small sample sizes (5 and 10, respectively), selective testing, and a higher proportion of surgically managed cases, where the anticipated radiosensitivity associated with p16‐positive tumors may not have been leveraged.

We observed p16‐positive nasal cavity SCC is associated with better DFS, but this benefit was not observed in p16‐positive paranasal sinus SCC. In addition to the smaller sample size (only 5 p16‐positive paranasal sinus SCC cases) and inherent selected testing bias, the challenges associated with the anatomic location of sinonasal tumors may also contribute to the lower DFS. This aligns with current literature, where, due to the rarity of sinonasal SCCs and inconsistent HPV testing, some studies show a prognostic benefit, while others demonstrate a trend without statistical significance [42, 43, 44, 45].

Interestingly, our data show that oral cavity SCC has the lowest prevalence of p16‐positivity, with no benefit to DFS or OS observed compared to the p16‐negative counterparts. In fact, p16‐positive oral SCCs may have slightly worse DFS and OS outcomes. This lack of survival benefit, or even worse survival outcomes, in p16‐positive oral SCC has been reported previously by other authors, including in a systematic review and meta‐analysis that identified HPV as an adverse prognostic factor [14, 21].

Limitation of this study includes its retrospective nature, the high proportion of p16 untested cases, and the relatively small population of p16‐positive cases further contributing to selection bias since only some cases underwent HPV‐PCR testing. Non‐random testing of p16 could potentially bias observed survival differences, as the untested group likely contains a mixture of true p16‐positive and p16‐negative cases, and their clinical behavior remains unknown, although tested and untested cohorts had similar smoking pack‐years and TNM stage distributions. Furthermore, while not all cases underwent HPV‐PCR testing, of those that did, the 100% concordance with the p16 testing result affirms that our institutional practice of routine p16‐surrogate marker testing is reliable. It aligns with existing evidence that, in populations with a high attributable fraction of HPV‐related OPSCC, p16 is a valid and practical surrogate for HPV status [46, 47].

Conclusions

Our study shows that the rates of p16‐positive non‐oropharyngeal HNSCC vary by disease site and by whether untested cases are included. We observe a prognostic benefit of p16‐positivity in DFS for laryngeal, hypopharyngeal, and nasal cavity SCC, while no apparent survival benefit is seen in paranasal sinus and oral cavity SCC. Though the sample size is small, the significant improvements in outcomes for p16‐positive SCCs of the larynx, hypopharynx and nasal cavity may offer opportunities for treatment refinement, including potential organ‐sparing approaches in advanced p16‐positive disease arising in these sites. It remains unclear whether the lack of prognostic value of p16 status in oral cavity and paranasal sinus SCC is due to small sample size, biological differences, treatment approaches (more surgery), or selection bias. Additionally, since not all p16‐positive tumors in our study are tested for high‐risk HPV DNA or RNA, not all p16‐positive tumors can be definitively classified as HPV‐associated. Prospective testing of tumor HPV status in non‐oropharyngeal HNSCCs using standardized algorithms would help clarify the true prevalence, prognostic significance, and potential treatment implications of HPV‐associated mucosal HNSCCs outside the oropharynx.
