Progression From Patterns A to B in Wellens Syndrome
Authors: Edwin A. Rodríguez, Anali Salva, José M. Aladio, Diego Costa, Sandra P. Swieszkowski
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
We describe a clinical case series of 3 patients whose electrocardiogram evolved from type A Wellens syndrome to a type B. We emphasize that the diagnosis and treatment for both patterns is the same and that these findings suggest the evolution of the same disease.

Wellens syndrome is characterized by a critical coronary stenosis of the left anterior descending artery (LAD). We demonstrate in 3 patients the electrocardiogram (ECG) progression from Wellens patterns A to B in an average of 12 hours. We emphasize that clinical history in addition to this ECG finding strongly suggests an acute coronary syndrome (ACS) because of the compromise of the LAD. All patients were studied by coronary angiogram, and they all had coronary artery involvement that correlates with Dr Wellens’s description. All patients had favorable outcomes and were successfully discharged.Learning Objectives•To identify Wellens syndrome as a high-risk pattern for NSTE ACS and its correlation with subocclusion of the LAD.•To distinguish both patterns as a possible evolution of the same disease.

Case 1

A 59-year-old man with a 42-pack-year history of smoking was admitted for burning chest pain. On admission, he was hemodynamically stable, and physical examination and basic laboratory test results were unremarkable. His troponin I level was 989 μg/L (reference value: 19 μg/L), and the ECG had a Wellens type A pattern (Figure 1). A repeat ECG 6 hours later showed changes in the T-wave consistent with Wellens type B pattern (Figure 2). Coronary angiography was performed, revealing a subocclusive lesion in the mid LAD (Figure 3). A cobalt chromium stent was implanted with subsequent TIMI flow grade of 3. Case 2

A 73-year-old man with a 35-pack-year history of smoking was admitted for angina. On arrival, he was hemodynamically stable, and physical examination and basic laboratory tests showed no particularities. His troponin level peaked at 905 μg/L (reference value: 19 μg/L). A first ECG showed a biphasic component of the T-wave in leads V2 and V3 (Figure 4), consistent with a Wellens type A pattern, which evolved in 12 hours to a deep and symmetric T-wave inversion from V2 to V5, compatible with a Wellens type B pattern (Figure 5). Coronary angiography was performed and revealed a severe lesion in the mid LAD (Figure 6), for which angioplasty was performed with successful implantation of a drug-eluting stent. Case 3

A 58-year-old man with hypertension, a 35-pack-year history of smoking, obesity, dyslipidemia, coronary artery disease, and heart failure (left ventricular ejection fraction of 38%) presented with angina. On admission, his blood pressure was 168/114 mm Hg, with no relevant findings on physical examination. Initial ECG showed positive and negative components of the T-wave in the V2 to V3 leads (Wellens type A pattern) (Figure 7) that evolved in 6 hours to a negative T-wave from V2 to V6 (Wellens type B pattern) (Figure 8). Admission laboratory test results showed up-trending troponin I with a peak of 744 μg/L. (reference value: 19 μg/L). Coronary angiography was performed, showing a subocclusive lesion in the proximal LAD, as well as chronic, nonacute disease of the left circumflex and right coronary arteries. Discussion

The T-wave in the ECG is the manifestation of ventricular repolarization, and it is usually asymmetric, with a rapid ascent and a progressive descent. It is typically positive in leads I, II, V5, and V6; negative in aVR; and variable in leads III, aVL, aVF, V1, and V2. Abnormalities in the orientation of the T-wave can be primary or secondary.1

Wellens syndrome was described in 1982 by Wellens et al, who characterized a subgroup (18%) of patients with unstable angina2; discrete enzymatic changes; and 2 patterns that involved the T-wave in the precordial leads, with intermittent chest pain at the time of onset and with the high risk of developing an extensive anterior wall myocardial infarction because of the compromise of the LAD.1, 2, 3

The first Wellens pattern (A) has a biphasic, or positive and negative, component of the T-wave, typically in leads V2 and V3, and appears in approximately 25% of patients. The second pattern (B) has a deep and symmetric inversion of the T-wave in V2 and V3, which can be extended up to V6, and is the most common, representing 75% of all cases. In both patterns, there is adequate R-wave progression in the precordial leads, absence of ST-segment elevation or ST-segment depression, and no pathologic Q waves, and typically the evolution from one pattern to another is not described.1,2,4

It is worth noting that most of the descriptions Wellens made were of unstable angina, likely because at the time of the original study they did not have biomarkers such as high-sensitivity troponin; in fact, they used enzymes such as creatine phosphokinase, lactic dehydrogenase, and aspartate transaminase.2,4 Therefore, it is possible that many of the past descriptions could actually represent myocardial infarctions based on today’s definitions.

In a cohort of 2,127 patients with non–ST-segment elevation (NSTE) ACS, Li et al3 found that 9.4% had Wellens syndrome.3 In this study, the Wellens pattern had a similar prevalence for both sexes, and the cardiovascular risk factors, such as hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, were also coinciding. Regarding treatment, more stents were implanted in the Wellens group than in the rest of the NSTE patients.3 The progression from pattern A to B is not typically described in the global literature.

The typical evolution of Wellens syndrome is anterior wall acute myocardial infarction, and the persistence of the ECG alteration can last for hours to weeks, depending on the time of intervention.5

Our patients are characterized as being all men, with an average age of 58 to 73 years; they were all smokers, and they all had ECG changes that denote the shift from pattern A to a pattern B. They also were studied and treated successfully before discharge. These findings are the result of high suspicion, early recognition of the syndrome, and serial ECGs performed on the patients and could be explained as the ECG evolution of the same disease. After searching the medical literature for similar findings, only Arisha et al6 reported their experience with a NSTE that evolved from Wellens syndrome pattern A to B.

The ECG changes observed in Wellens syndrome are not fully explained; some theories are the possible myocardial stunning caused by spontaneous reperfusion of the LAD acutely obstructed by atherosclerotic plaque, coronary spasms, or microvascular disease.1

Another explanation for the T-wave changes could be the “post-tsunami” or postischemia T-wave changes described by Bayes de Luna,7 argued because, at the time of admission, the patient does not have chest pain. As it is well known, the diagnosis and treatment for both patterns do not vary.3 However, these high-risk ECG patterns are not thoroughly considered in ACS guidelines, even as we know their correlation with the compromise of the LAD.8

Conclusions

Although the shift from pattern A to B in the ECGs did not modify the diagnostic and treatment approach for the 3 cases, it is worth highlighting that this progression is the second report found in the global literature.
