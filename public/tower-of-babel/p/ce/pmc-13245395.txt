A cohort study of the duration of immunity to yellow fever after 4 years of primary vaccination in children and adults
Authors: Camylla Veloso Valença Saucha, Raquel de Vasconcellos Carvalhaes de Oliveira, Maria de Lourdes de Sousa Maia, Eduardo Sérgio Soares Sousa, Patrícia Mouta Nunes de Oliveira, Janaína Reis Xavier, Thalita da Matta de Castro, Robson Leite de Souza Cruz, Waleska Dias Schwarcz, Renata Carvalho Pereira, Adriana de Souza Azevedo, Ana Maria Bispo de Filippis, Clara Lucy de Vasconcellos Ferroco, Maria de Fatima de Sousa Andrade, Marisol Simões, José Cerbino Neto, Olindo Assis Martins-Filho, Ana Carolina Campi-Azevedo, Sheila Maria Barbosa de Lima, Luiz Antonio Bastos Camacho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLifelong protective immunity from a single dose of yellow fever (YF) vaccine remains controversial. We evaluated variations in YF neutralizing antibody titres over 4 years after primary vaccination.MethodsA cohort study was conducted in three municipalities in northeastern Brazil. The 17DD vaccine was administered between 2016 and 2018. Blood samples were collected to measure YF neutralizing antibodies before, 30–45 d, 1 y and 4 y after vaccination. Generalized linear mixed models with a Tweedie distribution were constructed for children and adults separately to estimate variation in antibody titres over the 4-y period.ResultsOf the 4761 individuals, 2753 were children between 9 months and 4 y of age and 2008 adults ages 18–50 y. Four years after vaccination, seropositivity rates were 67.9% and 72.1% in children 9–23 months and 2–4 y, respectively, and 91.2% and 94.4% in adults 18–35 and 36–50 y, respectively. The ratio of means for neutralizing antibody titres 4 y after vaccination decreased to about one-third of that observed at 30–45 d after vaccination.ConclusionsThe evidence of waning immunity after a single dose of YF vaccine appears to support revaccination if primary vaccination targets infants and toddlers in endemic countries.

Introduction

Yellow fever (YF) is an acute, vaccine-preventable febrile infectious disease caused by the arbovirus Orthoflavivirus flavi. Its public health significance arises from its zoonotic transmission from non-human primates (the primary hosts) to humans through vectors, specifically mosquitoes of the genera Haemagogus, Sabethes and Aedes in the Americas and Africa.1 Although most cases are asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic, the disease can worsen with jaundice, haemorrhagic manifestations and renal failure, leading to death in 20–50% of cases.2

The disease has no specific treatment, highlighting vaccination and avoiding mosquito bites being the primary preventive measures. Currently, attenuated virus vaccines from the 17D strain are produced in six countries. A vaccine from the substrain 17DD is produced in Brazil and exported to several countries in Latin America and Africa. The effectiveness of YF vaccination has been established in routine and campaign immunization achieving disease prevention in residents and travellers to endemic areas. Immunogenicity assessed by neutralizing antibody titres is generally accepted as a proxy of vaccine protection based on the serological correlate in non-human primates.3

The post-vaccination seropositivity defined by neutralizing antibody titres can reach 98% in adults, whereas in children 9–23 months of age, it is approximately 85%, 30–45 d after vaccination.4,5 In adults, seropositivity can decrease to 76% 10 y after vaccination, and to approximately 50% 5 y after vaccination in children.6,7 In infants and toddlers (ages 9–23 months), seropositivity decreased to 59% 31–72 months after vaccination.8

Since 2013, the World Health Organization (WHO) has recommended a single dose of the YF vaccine.9 It was based on a systematic review of eight studies about the duration of YF vaccine immunity, most of which were observational and retrospective, with sample sizes ranging from 17 to 298 participants residing in non-endemic areas for YF.10 However, the Brazilian National Immunization Program has established that vaccination should begin at 9 months of age, with a booster dose at 4 y of age, based on immunogenicity studies in children.5,8 Individuals ages 5–59 y should receive a single dose of the vaccine.2

Although the urban cycle of YF transmission has not been recorded in Brazil since 1942,2 the mosquito involved in this cycle (Aedes aegypti) is also the vector of other widespread arboviruses with a major public health impact, including dengue, chikungunya and Zika.11 Dengue and Zika also belong to the Orthoflavivirus genus, which raises the possibility of cross-reaction in serological tests as well as the possibility of interference of flavivirus infections in the immune response to YF vaccination.3 Previous dengue infections can induce an immune response capable of reducing severity in individuals diagnosed with YF, but it will not prevent infection.12 Brazilian adults and children who had dengue neutralizing antibodies showed lower levels of YF neutralizing antibodies 30–45 d after YF vaccine administration.13

Given the epidemiological scenario of arboviruses in Brazil and the uncertainties regarding the duration of immunity conferred by the YF vaccine, this study aims to evaluate the variation in YF neutralizing antibody titres over 4 years after the primary vaccination in children and adults living in a non-YF-endemic area in Brazil. This study follows the initiatives of Bio-Manguinhos and partner institutions to generate scientific evidence for the Ministry of Health’s decisions.4–6,8 The study underscores the relevance and originality of conducting serological monitoring in a cohort of vaccinated children and adults living outside endemic and epizootic areas, but with the circulation of other orthoflaviviruses. Previous infections with those viruses can influence the vaccine response and therefore should be considered in the analysis of the duration of immunity to YF.

Methods

Design, area, population and period of study

This was a postmarketing uncontrolled cohort study, strategically planned and structured by the Department of Medical Affairs, Clinical Studies and Post-Market Surveillance (DEAME, Bio-Manguinhos, Fiocruz, Brazil). The study was conducted in three municipalities in the state of Paraíba, in the Northeast region of Brazil, where there was no circulation of the YF virus in the region, nor was routine vaccination recommended at the time the study began. Individuals 9 months–4 y and 18–50 y of age were invited to participate in the study, provided they had not been previously vaccinated against YF, had not resided in or travelled to an endemic area and had no contraindications established by the Brazilian National Immunization Program for YF vaccination.2 After exclusions, the cohort totalled 4761 individuals, comprising 2753 children and 2008 adults (Figure 1).

Flow chart of study participants and blood sample collection pre-vaccination and at 30–45 d, 1 y and 4 y post-vaccination. Individuals who missed a follow-up contact were still eligible for subsequent blood collections.

All participants received a single 0.5-ml dose of the vaccine (17DD substrain produced by Bio-Manguinhos), administered subcutaneously. Vaccination was conducted from July 2016 to July 2018 and used three batches of vaccine: 159VFA016Z (4.99 log10 pfu/dose), 172VFA002Z (4.91 log10 pfu/dose) and 174VFA035Z (4.63 log10 pfu/dose). The batch 159VFA002Z had a manufacturing date of 2015 and an expiration date of September 2017, whereas the others had an expiration date of 2020. The results presented here are based on neutralizing antibody titres against YF at four time points: before vaccination and 30–45 d, 1 y and 4 y after vaccination. Further methodological details related to sampling, inclusion and exclusion criteria and vaccination procedures have been published previously.13

Laboratory analysis

Neutralizing antibody titres for YF were measured using the micro-focus reduction neutralization test (μFRNT), which had been validated against the reference 50% plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT50) in six-well plates, according to Simões et al.14 Antibody titres were determined based on the cut-off point of the last serum dilution that reduced the number of lysis plaques by 50% compared with the viral control. This test was performed at the Laboratory of Virological Technology and the Immunomolecular Analysis Laboratory (LATEV and LANIM, respectively; Bio-Manguinhos).

Based on the reciprocal dilution of neutralizing antibody titres, individuals were categorized as seronegative (titres ≤70), indeterminate (titres ≥71–<100) and seropositive (titres ≥100). The cut-off points were based on comparison of the distribution of neutralizing antibody titres generated with the same readout methods in pre- and post-vaccination sera. Samples presenting antibody titres against the YF virus ranging from 1:71 to 1:99 were retested. Samples classified as negative (≤1:70) or positive (≥1:100) were not subjected to repeat testing. Individuals who did not seroconvert, i.e. remained seronegative after the primary vaccination, and individuals who became seronegative or indeterminate (seroreverted) at any point during follow-up were offered a second dose of the YF vaccine. For the study, seronegativity after vaccination and the time of detection of seroreversion were the outcomes of interest. For participants, it indicated the need for a booster dose due to the potential for primary or secondary vaccine failure (illness in vaccinated individuals).

Serological analyses for dengue were performed using Panbio and Euroimmun IgG tests, run by the Immunological Technology Laboratory (LATIM; Bio-Manguinhos).15,16 Serology for Zika was performed using immunochromatographic tests (IgM and IgG) manufactured by Bio-Manguinhos, in which the test support has two windows that identify the reaction between the antigen and the IgM and IgG antibodies separately.17 These serological analyses were performed at the Arbovirus and Hemorrhagic Virus Laboratory of the Oswaldo Cruz Institute (LARBOH; Fiocruz-Rio de Janeiro, Brazil).

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis of the study population at baseline considered the following variables: municipality of vaccination, age, sex, pre-vaccination YF serological status and neutralizing antibody titres, batch of vaccine administered and dengue (IgG) and Zika (IgG) pre-vaccination serological status. In addition, a variable derived from the combination of pre-vaccination dengue and Zika serological status was constructed with three categories: seronegative for both dengue and Zika, negative or indeterminate for one of them and positive for both. This analysis presented absolute and relative frequencies and their respective 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The distribution of neutralizing YF antibody titres (reciprocal of dilution) over 4 y were shown in violin plots with scattered individual points for adults and children separately. We also plotted the antibody mean titres (arithmetic mean of the reciprocal dilution of neutralizing antibody titres and the respective 95% CI) in each time point, stratified by age subgroups.

To characterize individuals who remained seropositive throughout the 4-y follow-up after the primary vaccination, we excluded pre-vaccination seropositive individuals, as it was not possible to rule out unreported prior vaccination or infection; individuals who remained seronegative or indeterminate after the primary vaccination; and individuals without serological results 1 or 4 y after vaccination (lost to follow-up). The proportions of seropositive participants 4 y after one dose of YF vaccine and their respective CIs were presented in subgroups of sex, age, vaccine batch and pre-vaccination serological status for dengue (IgG) and Zika (IgG).

Due to the asymmetric distribution of YF neutralizing antibody titres and the correlation among repeated measurements within individuals, Tweedie–Poisson mixed linear models were used to estimate the variation in antibody titres over the 4-y period. It is a compound Poisson-gamma model, which is a particular type of Tweedie model (p parameter values considered were in the range 1<p<2).18 Separate models were constructed for children and adults, excluding pre-vaccination seropositive individuals and those who remained seronegative or indeterminate 30–45 d after vaccination. Individuals who did not seroconvert and those who seroreverted during follow-up were considered for the modelling until the booster dose was administered, to assess the duration of immunity after the primary vaccination. The outcome variable was YF neutralizing antibody titre and the time variable was included as a categorical variable (pre-vaccination, 30–45 d, 1 y and 4 y). The following adjustment variables were included: age group (9–23 months, 2–4 y, 18–35 y and 36–50 y), sex (male and female), batch of vaccine (159VFA016Z, 172VFA002Z and 174VFA035Z), combination of dengue (IgG) and Zika (IgG) pre-vaccination serological status (positive and negative) and the random effect on the individual. Individuals who did not have pre-vaccination IgG results for dengue and Zika or presented indeterminate results were excluded from this analysis due to the possibility of classification error. Although batch 174 was linked to the age group, we did not observe this in the regression models, as the variance inflation factor values did not indicate multicollinearity in a similar fixed effects model. The goodness-of-fit was verified using the Akaike information criterion (AIC). The model coefficients were exponentiated to be interpreted as a ratio of means, which reflects the increase in antibody titres compared with pre-vaccination levels. These results were presented in a table with their respective 95% CIs. The cpglmm() function from the cplm package of R software version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used for this analysis.18

Ethical aspects

The study followed the requirements of the Declaration of Helsinki and Resolution no. 466 of the Brazilian National Health Council.19,20 Formal written consent was obtained from all participants and from parents/legal guardians for all participants <18 y of age and all vulnerable patients. The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Federal University of Paraíba (CAAE: 51160215.7.3001.5183; document of approval no. 6911270).

Results

Of the 4761 individuals, children ages 2–4 y constituted the largest age group. Children had a balanced sex distribution, whereas among adults >76% were female. Of all the individuals enrolled, 98 children and 413 adults were pre-vaccination YF seropositive. The proportion of pre-vaccination YF seropositive individuals was 19.4% and 22.6% in the 18–35 y and 36–50 y age groups, respectively. Batch 174VFA035Z was used exclusively in children, while most adults were vaccinated with batch 159VFA016Z. The baseline serological status for dengue and Zika showed that most adults were seropositive, especially in the 36–50 y age group, whereas children were mostly seronegative (Table 1).

The distribution of YF neutralizing antibody titres showed more clusters of lower titres at the four follow-up time points in children than in adults (Figure 2). Before vaccination, few children had titres (reciprocal of dilution) >100, the cut-off considered for seropositivity (Figure 2A), whereas a substantial number of adults had high titres (Figure 2B). After vaccination, a marked shift towards higher antibody titres was observed, especially among adults, who showed a cluster around the upper limit titre of 1458. However, 4 y after vaccination, the antibody titres clustered around lower and borderline titres, especially in children, who seroreverted in droves.

The mean YF neutralizing antibody titres showed a similar pattern in all age ranges of a marked increase 30–45 d after vaccination, with a subsequent gradual decline over the 4-y follow-up period. Individuals ages 36–50 y had higher YF antibody titres in all blood collections, whereas children had the lowest levels. There was no significant difference between the age groups 9–23 months and 2–4 years, except in the collection 30–45 days after vaccination (Figure 3). Consistent with the decrease in the mean antibody levels, the proportion of seropositive individuals decreased in all age groups over the 4-y follow-up period, particularly among children, who had seropositive rates of 67.9% and 72.1% (9–23 months and 2–4 y, respectively; before exclusions), whereas adults maintained seropositive rates >90%.

After excluding individuals who were seropositive before vaccination and those who remained seronegative or indeterminate after vaccination, the total number of individuals studied was 4048. Of these, 34 did not have serology results at 1 and 4 years after vaccination. Therefore, of 4014 individuals, 3139 (78.2%) remained seropositive 4 y after vaccination. This group differed from the individuals who seroreverted over the 4 y, mainly in relation to age group. Among children ages 9–23 months, 67.3% remained seropositive, compared with 94.8% of adults ages 36–50 y. Seropositivity differed substantially between batches of vaccine (Table 2), consistent with the distribution of batches by age group (Table 1).

Discussion

Our study aimed to evaluate the variation in YF neutralizing antibody titres over 4 years after administration of a single dose of the 17DD vaccine in children and adults living in a non-YF-endemic area of Brazil. Our results demonstrated an increase in YF neutralizing antibody titres 30–45 d after vaccination in all age groups, although at lower levels among children. Over the 4-y follow-up period, neutralizing antibody titres decreased substantially in all age groups, but more markedly among children. Statistical modelling allowed us to estimate the magnitude of reduction in antibody titres adjusted by age range, sex, batch of vaccine, IgG dengue and IgG Zika pre-vaccination. It is noteworthy that adults, despite showing a greater initial mean increase compared with children, showed a similar sustained tendency of reduction over the 4 y.

Our results are consistent with a cross-sectional study conducted with approximately 600 Brazilian adults, which showed a reduction in the geometric mean antibody titres from 8762.8 at 30–45 days after vaccination to 3007.5 in the period from 1 to 4 y after vaccination and a sustained reduction in the period from 5 to 9 y.6 It is also noteworthy that adults who received two doses of the YF vaccine throughout their lives also presented reductions in antibody titres to seronegativity levels 1–5 y after the last dose.21 In contrast to our study, those findings were generated by several cohorts whose proportion of seroconverters was not known. Nevertheless, they showed that the reduction in YF antibody titres over the years turned a substantial proportion of individuals seronegative.22 The decrease in humoral immunity correlated with short-lived YF-specific cellular immunity, mediated by memory T and B cells, observed after 4 y with a single dose.23 Another study showed that revaccination restored PRNT seropositivity as well as the levels of effector memory CD4+, CD8+ and interferon-γ+CD8+ T cells.24

Another cross-sectional study of residents of non-endemic areas suggested the need for a booster dose 10 y after the primary vaccination in cases of travel to areas endemic for YF, due to the loss of immunity mainly between the ages of 3 and 12 years.25 In contrast to our findings, a prospective cohort of 36 individuals living in non-endemic areas but with a history of travel to endemic areas, ages 18–28 y and 60–81 y, found a 100% seropositivity rate for YF 10 years after vaccination.26 Similarly, a study in children from Mali and The Gambia found that 22.2% of the cohort had undetectable antibody concentrations 4–5 y after one dose of YF vaccine.27

A meta-analysis of the long-term protection (≥10 y) of a single dose of YF vaccine found that studies in sub-Saharan Africa were scarce and vaccination status could not be ascertained. The pooled seroprotection rate was 47% (35–60%) in children (ages 9–23 months at the time of vaccination), 94% (86–99%) for healthy adults in a non-endemic setting and 76% (65–85%) in an endemic setting (all Brazilian studies).28

The weaker primary antibody response in infants also observed for other live attenuated vaccines (e.g. measles, mumps and rubella and Japanese encephalitis) has been attributed to immaturity of the immune system and interference from maternal antibodies and from other live vaccines.29,30 These two latter factors are not plausible in our study population, as no other vaccine was given within 30 d of administration of the YF vaccine and data from participants seropositive to YF before vaccination were disregarded. It should also be highlighted that the uniform 0.5-ml dose of vaccine is dimensioned to exceed the minimum required dose (at least 3.0 log10 IU) at the end of its shelf life.9

A review of the immunogenicity and protective ability of different vaccines against Japanese encephalitis (caused by Orthoflavivirus japonicum) showed that neutralizing antibody levels from live attenuated vaccines (both the chimeric and Sa14-14-2 virus strain) decreased substantially after a single dose in children, thus requiring a booster dose.31 The review also indicated that the duration of protective responses may be longer in countries where there is natural exposure to flaviviruses and in individuals previously immunized with related flavivirus vaccines and relatively shorter in naïve individuals.

In this study, pre-vaccination serological status for dengue and Zika was included as an adjustment variable in the statistical modelling to more reliably estimate the time variation in YF antibody titres. This decision is based on previous findings, including from a study conducted with this same cohort followed for 1 y, which indicated that previous dengue and Zika infections reduced the immune response to the YF vaccine.13

A recent systematic review found no differences between the results of studies on the duration of YF immunity in endemic and non-endemic areas, albeit highlighting important differences in the comparability of results. Such points to be questioned are the means of proving vaccination; different cut-off points for the YF serological test; age at the time of vaccination, especially for children; and not ruling out the possible unrecorded booster doses or natural infection of residents and travellers to endemic areas.32 In the present study we were able to ensure that a single dose of vaccine from known lots had been given at specified dates, ascertain participants’ immune response, select previously naïve individuals for data analysis with safeguards against booster doses and natural YF infection, but accounting for exposure to infections by other flaviviruses.

The significant downward trend in neutralizing antibody titres in a relatively short period of time appears to support the need for booster doses for residents in endemic areas and for travellers to areas with a high risk of transmission. YF breakthrough infections documented in the literature are considered rare.33 To the extent that seroreversion entails a reduction in protection, it might justify some of the YF cases in vaccinated individuals reported in outbreak investigations. Consistent with the lower immune response to YF vaccination in infants, Diniz et al.34 found that among probable YF cases, the proportion of breakthrough infections was considerably higher (35% versus 3%) among those vaccinated at a young age (median age 1 y) than those vaccinated as adults (median age 31 y).

Our cohort of >4000 participants, including children (ages 9 months–4 y) and adults (ages 18–50 y), provided robust data for measuring the duration of immunity to YF. At the time of writing, the authors were unaware of any other ongoing YF cohort studies analysing the duration of primary vaccination in non-endemic areas. We took advantage of the opportunity to study a naïve population. Similar studies evaluating primary vaccination will be unfeasible in Brazil as the YF vaccine is now recommended nationwide. Considering that routine serological testing in health services to disclose seroreversion is unfeasible, the vaccine is affordable and severe adverse events are even rarer than in primary vaccination, booster doses are warranted for children and might be considered for adults in outbreaks.

Currently, the WHO recommends a single dose with no booster. In any area with reported cases the WHO also recommends vaccination to everyone ≥9 months of age, which seems to imply that booster doses are allowed.9 Agencies from several countries, such as the Centers for Disease Control Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices, have recommended booster doses of YF vaccine in certain populations at increased risk of exposure to YF virus or suboptimal immune response to YF vaccination.35

One limitation to consider was the presence of individuals seropositive for YF before vaccination, despite the inclusion criteria aimed at selecting those who were unvaccinated and had not resided in or travelled to endemic areas or areas where YF outbreaks had occurred. Therefore, we took care to exclude these individuals from analyses of immunity duration after the primary vaccination. Random variation of results from imperfect laboratory tests may have led to underestimation of the decreasing trend in antibody levels. The exclusion of individuals 5–18 y of age could be considered another limitation, but we chose to focus resources on the two age groups expected to show the greatest contrast in immunity duration, based on data from cross-sectional studies. Similarly, limiting vaccination to age 50 y defines the group of adults in whom the immune response is generally considered most intense and who will best inform the optimal duration of immunity before they reach age 60 y and are affected by immunosenescence.

We conclude that the results presented from this cohort of >4000 individuals vaccinated against YF provided more robust evidence of waning immunity than previous studies, particularly given the availability of data on the exact timing of vaccination and serological testing for other orthoflaviviruses. Furthermore, the statistical modelling provided more precise estimates of the variation in YF neutralizing antibody titres over time, separately for children and adults. The sharp decrease in YF neutralizing antibody titres observed in children reinforces the scientific basis for the Brazilian National Immunization Program’s recommendation to administer a booster dose to children at 4 y of age. Other countries with YF virus circulation and recommended vaccination for infants may consider the results of this study in their vaccination strategy.
