Automated fractal analysis for mandibular bone evaluation in type 1 diabetes mellitus using a novel single click approach
Authors: Melek Tassoker, Yunus Balel, Mediha Erturk, Kaan Sagtas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to assess mandibular bone alterations in individuals with Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM) using a novel single-click fractal analysis module and to compare their fractal dimension (FD) values with those of healthy controls. A total of 184 panoramic radiographs from patients with T1DM—each of whom was receiving insulin therapy—and 184 age- and gender-matched healthy individuals were retrospectively evaluated. A newly developed Python-based module was used to calculate FD with a single click in six predefined mandibular regions of interest (ROIs), including gonial, interdental, and condylar areas. The significance of the test results was determined according to the p < 0.05 level. There were no statistically significant differences in FD values between groups in the condylar region (ROI 1 and 6). However, FD values in ROI 2, 3, 4, and 5—corresponding to the gonial and interdental areas—were significantly higher in the T1DM group compared to controls, suggesting microstructural differences in trabecular bone. The results indicate that T1DM is associated with region-specific alterations in mandibular trabecular bone architecture. The single-click fractal analysis module demonstrated efficient and standardized assessment of bone structure, offering a promising tool for clinical and research applications. Introduction

Fractal analysis, a mathematical technique designed to quantify intricate geometric patterns and structural irregularities, has become increasingly prominent in dental research over the last thirty years. By measuring fractal dimension (FD), researchers can evaluate variations in trabecular bone architecture, bone density, and structural complexity, especially utilizing radiographic imaging methods. Employing fractal analysis in dentistry has notably enhanced diagnostic capabilities for systemic conditions like osteoporosis1, periodontal disease2, and peri-implant alveolar bone health, and has also been instrumental in assessing bone regeneration and the stability of dental implants3,4.

Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM) arises from the autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β-cells, which disrupts bone metabolism and contributes to impaired bone growth and the development of osteoporosis5. In patients with poorly controlled T1DM, reductions in cortical area and trabecular volumetric bone mineral density have been observed6. Reduced bone turnover in T1DM patients is due to low insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) levels, impaired PTH-vitamin D axis, inflammatory cytokines, and disease-induced microvascular diseases and insulin deficiency7. The results obtained in various studies investigating the effect of T1DM on the jaw bones using panoramic images are contradictory. Karasu et al.5 reported that FD values ​​were lower in T1DM patients, while others6,8 reported no difference. These conflicting results prevent a clearer understanding of the effects of this disease, which is reported to have a worldwide prevalence of 9.5%9 and is frequently encountered in dental clinics, on the jaw bones.

Given the widespread prevalence of T1DM and its potential impact on bone metabolism, early and efficient detection of mandibular bone alterations is crucial for dental professionals. Such microstructural changes are particularly important when planning surgical procedures such as tooth extraction, dental implant placement, or other interventions requiring sufficient bone quality. Identifying these changes using a fast and standardized method can enhance clinical decision-making and long-term treatment outcomes. Several methods exist for determining the FD, with the box-counting technique, introduced by White et al.10, being among the most commonly applied. This method enables detailed examination of trabecular bone structure and bone marrow spaces. However, this method involves many complex steps that are prone to error by the observer and are time-consuming. Therefore, the aims of this study are twofold: (1) to introduce and apply a newly developed single-click fractal analysis module that enables rapid assessment of mandibular bone architecture, and (2) to compare the FD values between T1DM patients and healthy individuals to determine region-specific alterations with clinical significance.

Methods

Sample

The study was conducted in alignment with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, incorporating all required protocols and amendments. Ethical compliance of the research was reviewed and approved by the Scientific Research Evaluation Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Necmettin Erbakan University (Approval No: 2025/569).

This retrospective study used panoramic images of individuals who applied to Necmettin Erbakan University Faculty of Dentistry for dental examination between 2020 and 2024. The control group consisted of 184 T1DM patients aged between 18 and 40 years (29.2 ± 7.1), all of whom used insulin and had T1DM information in the anamnesis database, and 184 healthy individuals matched to these patients in terms of age and gender. A total of 368 patients were included, consisting of 160 males and 208 females. Each of the case and control groups comprised 80 males and 104 females. Central jaw pathologies, trauma patients, maxillofacial anomalies and deformities, systemic diseases other than T1DM, individuals using drugs that may affect bone metabolism, and images with low diagnostic value due to artifacts or angular errors were excluded from the study.

Single-Click fractal analysis module

This module was developed by researchers Yunus Balel and Kaan Sağtaş in August 2024. In the model’s working system, a radiograph is loaded into the system as input, then the ROI value is defined and the radiograph is automatically opened. When the section on which fractal analysis is desired is clicked from the opened radiograph, the model automatically performs fractal analysis of the region (Fig. 1). In this study, 6 different ROIs of 50 × 50 pixels selected from left and right gonial, mandibular 1 st molar and 2nd premolar interdental and condylar regions were manually selected and analyzed. To ensure reproducibility across all patients, each ROI was manually placed by the same experienced dentomaxillofacial radiologist according to standardized anatomical landmarks visible in all panoramic radiographs. Additionally, a subset of 50 randomly selected images was reanalyzed 15 days after the initial assessment, and intraobserver agreement was calculated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The ICC values demonstrated excellent agreement (ICC = 0.87), confirming the reliability of the ROI placement and measurement process.

The entire analysis process—from image loading to output generation—is completed in less than one millisecond. Most of the perceived delay is related to image loading and manual ROI selection. To evaluate accuracy and consistency, the module’s results were compared to the classical ImageJ-based box-counting method described by White and Rudolph, which uses a Gaussian filter with sigma = 35 and a kernel size of 33 × 33 pixels. While ImageJ restricts manual control of kernel size, the Python-based module allows for precise customization, enhancing methodological reproducibility. By consolidating all preprocessing and analysis steps into a single automated workflow, this module minimizes user variability, reduces processing time, and offers a reliable, reproducible, and efficient tool for the fractal evaluation of trabecular bone—making it suitable for both clinical and research applications.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A post hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). Based on the observed effect size in ROI_3 (Cohen’s d = 0.27), the minimum required sample size was calculated as 99 participants per group to achieve 80% power at α = 0.05. With 184 participants in each group, the sample size was sufficient to detect a statistically meaningful difference. Descriptive statistics (mean and standard deviation) were calculated for each ROI. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the data. Since the data were not normally distributed, group comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To account for multiple comparisons across six ROIs, the Bonferroni correction was applied to control for the increased risk of type I error. Accordingly, the adjusted significance threshold was set at 0.0083 (0.05/6). Only p-values below this threshold were considered statistically significant after correction.

Results

Among the six evaluated ROIs, no statistically significant difference in FD values was found between the T1DM and control groups in the condylar regions (ROI 1 and ROI 6) (p = 0.087 and p = 0.122, respectively). In contrast, significant differences were observed in ROI 2 (p = 0.036), ROI 3 (p = 0.004), ROI 4 (p = 0.003), and ROI 5 (p = 0.030). These regions correspond to the gonial and interdental areas of the mandible (Table 1). Specifically, the T1DM group exhibited consistently higher FD values in these regions compared to the control group.

Discussion

This study investigated mandibular trabecular bone architecture in individuals with T1DM using a newly developed single-click fractal analysis module on panoramic radiographs. The findings revealed significantly higher FD values in the gonial and interdental regions (ROI 2–5) of T1DM patients compared to healthy controls, suggesting diabetes-related microstructural alterations in trabecular bone. These localized changes may reflect region-specific remodeling responses to systemic metabolic disturbances, and could have implications for surgical planning in diabetic patients, such as tooth extraction or dental implant placement, where sufficient trabecular integrity is essential for osseointegration and wound healing. Interestingly, no significant FD difference was observed in the condylar regions (ROI 1 and 6), despite selecting trabecular areas. This may be due to the condyle’s continuous functional remodeling under masticatory forces, which could help maintain trabecular organization even in the presence of systemic disease. Moreover, mechanical loading has been shown to stimulate local bone formation and prevent resorption, possibly overriding the negative metabolic effects of T1DM in these regions. Such biomechanical resilience may explain the preservation of trabecular architecture in the condyle. Furthermore, the sensitivity of bone to insulin and inflammatory cytokines may vary by anatomical site, with the condylar bone potentially being less responsive to the catabolic effects associated with hyperglycemia. On the other hand, poor glycemic control in T1DM is associated with reduced radiomorphometric indices and decreased bone mineral density, particularly in cortical structures. Several studies have reported an inverse relationship between bone density and HbA1c levels, suggesting that chronic hyperglycemia and advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) may impair bone quality11,12. However, our study could not evaluate this relationship due to lack of biochemical data. Future prospective studies incorporating glycemic control metrics, such as HbA1c levels and disease duration, are warranted to further clarify the relationship between metabolic status and trabecular bone alterations, particularly in younger adults with insulin-treated T1DM.

All individuals included in the study were receiving insulin therapy. Given that insulin exerts anabolic effects on bone metabolism by stimulating osteoblast activity, enhancing IGF-1 production, and inhibiting osteoclast-mediated bone resorption13, the higher FD values observed in the T1DM group may reflect compensatory remodeling or stabilization of trabecular bone structure induced by insulin. These findings align with the hypothesis that insulin-treated diabetic individuals may undergo partial restoration of bone microarchitecture, particularly in metabolically active regions of the mandible. Previous studies examining the impact of T1DM on mandibular trabecular bone structure using FD analysis have yielded inconsistent findings. These discrepancies may result from methodological heterogeneity, including variations in imaging techniques, FD calculation protocols, sample demographics, and anatomical site selection. For instance, Karasu et al.5 evaluated pediatric patients using relatively small sample sizes (n = 36 per group) and focused on the angulus and corpus regions, reporting decreased FD values in T1DM patients. Conversely, Kurşun-Çakmak et al.8 analyzed younger adults aged 19–32 and found no significant differences, possibly due to narrower age range and less metabolic variability. Similarly, Tercanlı Alkış et al.6 used a larger pediatric cohort (n = 114) but did not stratify participants based on disease duration or glycemic control status, which are known modulators of bone metabolism. Additionally, FD values are highly sensitive to ROI placement, image resolution, and preprocessing steps such as filtering and binarization. The lack of standardized protocols across studies likely contributes to inconsistent outcomes. Our study attempts to address these limitations by using a larger sample size (n = 368), standardized ROI selection based on consistent anatomical landmarks, and a fully automated single-click analysis module that minimizes observer-dependent variability. These methodological improvements enhance both the reproducibility and potential clinical relevance of our findings. Nevertheless, despite controlling for age and gender, biochemical markers such as HbA1c levels and disease duration were not available due to the retrospective design. These variables are critical for distinguishing between metabolic compensation and progressive bone deterioration in T1DM. Therefore, future prospective studies incorporating detailed metabolic profiles are warranted to better understand the bone-specific effects of T1DM and the modulating role of insulin therapy.

In this study, a newly developed Python-based analysis tool was employed, which enables FD measurement through a single-click operation. Unlike conventional fractal analysis methods that require multiple manual preprocessing steps—such as image cropping, Gaussian filtering, binarization, and skeletonization10,14–19— the proposed tool integrates all these processes into a unified and automated workflow. Users simply define the ROI by clicking on the target area, and the tool performs the complete analysis within milliseconds. This simplified workflow reduces procedural complexity and minimizes observer-dependent variability, thereby enhancing both the reliability and reproducibility of the measurements. As shown in Fig. 1, the analysis time for a single ROI is approximately 0.04 s, making it substantially faster than traditional box-counting methods using software like ImageJ, which may require several minutes per case due to manual input at each step. Moreover, conventional tools often lack flexibility in parameter control (e.g., kernel size or sigma values), limiting their adaptability across diverse imaging datasets. In contrast, our Python-based module allows full customization of preprocessing parameters, which is critical for ensuring consistency in large-scale studies. Beyond speed and standardization, the open-source nature of the tool (available at https://github.com/semruktech/fractal-box-counter) allows for transparency, reproducibility, and future integration into artificial intelligence (AI) pipelines. This accessibility also enables independent validation by other researchers, strengthening the scientific credibility of the approach. Given these advantages, the proposed tool holds considerable promise for clinical applications where rapid and reliable assessment of trabecular bone structure is needed. These may include osteoporosis screening, monitoring metabolic bone diseases such as diabetes or thyroid disorders, or evaluating bone quality prior to implant placement. Its ease of use and high throughput make it particularly suitable for large-scale retrospective studies and busy clinical settings.

Limitations

This retrospective study has several limitations. Data on glycemic control, disease duration, and biochemical markers were unavailable, which may have influenced FD values. Although all T1DM patients were on insulin therapy, treatment duration and dosage were not standardized. The use of two-dimensional panoramic radiographs inherently restricts the evaluation of complex three-dimensional trabecular bone structures. Although panoramic imaging is widely available, cost-effective, and commonly used in routine dental diagnostics, it lacks the spatial resolution of advanced three-dimensional modalities such as cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) or micro-computed tomography (micro-CT). Lastly, while the single-click analysis module improved standardization, it requires further validation in broader clinical settings.

Conclusions

This study showed that T1DM patients exhibit region-specific increases in mandibular trabecular bone FD, potentially influenced by insulin therapy. The single-click analysis module enabled fast and standardized assessment, suggesting its potential for broader clinical use. In the future, further development of the module could incorporate artificial intelligence-based automated ROI selection, eliminating the need for manual input and enhancing standardization. Moreover, future clinical studies should include key metabolic parameters—such as HbA1c levels, duration of diabetes, and overall metabolic control status—to better understand their relationship with mandibular bone architecture and further improve diagnostic accuracy.
