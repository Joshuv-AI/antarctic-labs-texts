The LGR5/TFF2 ratio as a stem cell biomarker predicts high-risk status in chronic atrophic gastritis: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Qingqing Zhang, Di Wu, Fengyun Guo, Shengnan Yang, Lijing Bao, Ruiying Zhang, Ping Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic atrophic gastritis (CAG) severity risk assessment remains challenging due to limitations of current static histologic/endoscopic tools. We evaluated the leucine-rich repeat-containing G-protein coupled receptor 5 (LGR5)/trefoil factor 2 (TFF2) ratio — quantifying gastric stem cell niche imbalance— as a dynamic biomarker for high-risk CAG (OLGA/OLGIM III-IV).MethodsIn a retrospective cohort (n = 60 CAG patients), we constructed four prediction models (Baseline: demographics; Clinical: +H. pylori/family history; Endoscopic; Full: +LGR5/TFF2 ratio) using Firth penalized regression. Model performance was assessed via area under the curve (AUC), Akaike (AIC), and Bayesian (BIC) information criteria, and likelihood ratio tests. Restricted cubic splines and subgroup analyses validated threshold effects.ResultsThe LGR5/TFF2 ratio > 1.066 (AUC = 0.93) identified high-risk CAG with 48.5-fold increased odds (95% CI: 4.07–578.51, P = 0.002), outperforming endoscopic/histologic models (ΔAUC = + 0.15). A nonlinear dose-response relationship revealed sharp risk escalation beyond this threshold. The high-ratio group (n = 20) exhibited 60.00% prevalence of prior H. pylori eradication, Wnt activation (β-catenin↑32.3%, CyclinD1↑90%, P < 0.001), and stronger endoscopic correlation (ρ = 0.42 for IM nodules) than traditional factors (ρ = 0.28 for H. pylori).ConclusionThe LGR5/TFF2 ratio is the first biomarker to molecularly define CAG severity risk by quantifying stem cell niche imbalance (LGR5↑/TFF2↓), achieving superior accuracy (AUC = 0.93) and identifying high-risk cases missed by current methods. Its integration into clinical practice could transform risk-stratified surveillance paradigms.

Introduction

Chronic atrophic gastritis (CAG), characterized by glandular loss and metaplastic transformation, represents the critical lesion in the intestinal-type gastric cancer (GC) cascade [1]. In high-risk populations, CAG carries a 10-year GC progression risk exceeding 10% [2]. Current histological staging systems exhibit limited dynamic predictive accuracy, with up to 37% of high-risk CAG patients demonstrating disease progression during surveillance despite regular monitoring [3]. While endoscopic features such as intestinal metaplasia (IM) nodules and patchy mucosal erythema provide visual cues, their subjective interpretation and interobserver variability hinder standardized risk stratification [4]. Recent advances in narrow-band imaging (NBI) have significantly improved the endoscopic detection and grading of gastric IM. Characteristic NBI findings such as the light blue crest (LBC) demonstrate high accuracy for IM diagnosis [5], and a meta-analysis confirmed that NBI detects 32% more IM cases compared to white-light endoscopy alone [6]. However, despite these technological improvements, NBI-based assessment remains inherently subjective, with interobserver variability still posing a challenge to standardized risk stratification. More importantly, as an imaging technique, NBI captures only morphological alterations and cannot directly reflect the underlying molecular aberrations driving malignant transformation, such as dysregulated stem cell dynamics or aberrant Wnt signaling. Thus, there is an urgent need for objective molecular biomarkers that can complement endoscopic assessment by capturing the biological drivers of gastric carcinogenesis.

The gastric stem cell niche, regulated by the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, serves as the cellular origin for both mucosal repair and malignant transformation [7]. Leucine-rich repeat-containing G-protein coupled receptor 5 (LGR5), a canonical stemness marker, drives epithelial proliferation and is upregulated in IM lesions [8]. Conversely, trefoil factor 2 (TFF2), secreted by mucous neck cells, maintains mucosal barrier integrity and suppresses tumorigenesis through anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects [9, 10]. Its loss may promote excessive proliferation and deleterious mutations [11]. Crucially, these molecules exhibit functionally opposing roles—LGR5 promotes tissue renewal, whereas TFF2 sustains barrier homeostasis. Although the direct mechanistic interplay between LGR5 and TFF2 in human gastric pathogenesis remains unelucidated, their inverse expression patterns in precancerous lesions (e.g., LGR5↑/TFF2↓ in IM) [12] suggest that the LGR5/TFF2 ratio may serve as an integrative indicator of dysregulated mucosal repair.

Current limitations in CAG risk prediction include histological stagnation (OLGA (Operative Link for Gastritis Assessment) and OLGIM (Operative Link for Gastric Intestinal Metaplasia) staging relies on static snapshots of glandular atrophy/IM, lacking molecular indicators of disease severity) [13], biomarker fragmentation (isolated measurements of LGR5 or TFF2 fail to capture their biological crosstalk) [14, 15], and clinical-immunohistochemical disconnect (no studies have integrated stem cell markers with endoscopic phenotypes to construct multimodal risk models) [16]. We hypothesized that an elevated LGR5/TFF2 ratio, indicative of aberrant stem cell dynamics, predicts high-risk CAG independent of conventional factors. To test this, we conducted a retrospective cohort study with three objectives: to establish associations between the LGR5/TFF2 ratio and endoscopic/histological lesion severity, quantify its incremental predictive value over clinical, endoscopic, and histological variables, and validate its robustness in small-sample settings.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Endoscopy Center of Xiyuan Hospital, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences, between 2023 and 2024. A total of 326 patients undergoing endoscopic examination with gastric biopsy were initially screened. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, histologically confirmed gastritis (including both non-atrophic and atrophic gastritis), and complete clinical and endoscopic records. Exclusion criteria included prior gastrectomy, active gastric cancer, and inadequate biopsy samples. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 60 patients were enrolled in the final analysis. A detailed patient enrollment flowchart is presented in Fig. 1.

Patient enrollment flowchart

Based on OLGA/OLGIM staging, patients were categorized into non-high-risk CAG (stage 0-II) and high-risk CAG (stage III-IV). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Xiyuan Hospital (Approval No.: 2023XLA015-3; Chinese Clinical Trial Register (ChiCTR) number: ChiCTR2300072637), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Clinical data and endoscopic assessment

Demographic data, lifestyle factors (smoking and alcohol consumption), history of Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection, and family history of GC were collected using structured questionnaires. Endoscopic examinations were performed by two independent endoscopists (with 10 and 15 years of experience, respectively) using high-definition white light and narrow-band imaging (NBI) with an Olympus EVIS LUCERA ELITE CV-290 system and GIF-H260Z/GIF-Q260J gastroscopes. Both endoscopists were blinded to the clinical and histopathological data. The degree of endoscopic mucosal atrophy was graded according to the Kimura-Takemoto classification. In addition, the following endoscopic features were evaluated: depressed erosions, raised erosions, hemorrhagic spots, IM nodularity, mucosal swelling, patchy redness, uneven mucosal surface, visible vascular pattern, and flattened folds. A cumulative score of these selected high-risk endoscopic features was calculated for each patient.

Histopathological evaluation

Biopsies were obtained from five standard sites (antrum × 2, corpus × 2, incisura angularis) following the Updated Sydney System. Two gastrointestinal pathologists, blinded to clinical data, independently graded the severity of atrophy and IM on a 0–3 scale for each biopsy site. The final OLGA and OLGIM stages were then determined for each patient by combining the antral (taking the higher score from the antrum and incisura angularis) and corpus scores according to the respective staging grids. Discrepancies between pathologists (defined as a difference of more than one stage) were resolved by consensus using a multi-head microscope (κ = 0.85). For the purpose of this study, high-risk CAG was defined as OLGA stage III-IV or OLGIM stage III-IV, consistent with established guidelines that identify these stages as conferring the highest risk for gastric cancer development [13, 14].

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and biomarker quantification

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded sections from both the antral (including incisura angularis) and corpus biopsy sites were stained using validated antibodies including Rabbit anti-human LGR5 monoclonal antibody (Cat# ab219107, 1:150; Abcam), Rabbit anti-human TFF2 monoclonal antibody (Cat# ab267474, 1:200; Abcam), Mouse anti-human β-catenin monoclonal antibody (Cat# ab22656, 1:100; Abcam), and Rabbit anti-human Cyclin D1 antibody (1:100). Digital image analysis (Image-Pro Plus 10.0) measured the average optical density (AOD) across three fields per slide (400× magnification). For each patient, the LGR5/TFF2 ratio was calculated separately for the antral (including angularis) and corpus regions, and the final LGR5/TFF2 ratio used for analysis was the average of these two regional values. This approach was chosen to obtain a representative measure of the overall gastric molecular status, analogous to the comprehensive assessment principle of the OLGA/OLGIM staging systems.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R 4.3.0 and SPSS 26.0. Continuous variables were compared using Welch’s *t*-test (accounting for unequal variance), categorical variables with Fisher’s exact test, and correlations assessed via Spearman’s rank (ρ). The optimal cutoff for the LGR5/TFF2 ratio was determined by ROC curve analysis (Youden index).

Given the relatively small cohort size (n = 60), traditional maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) in logistic regression would yield biased estimates due to separation or rare-event bias [17]. To address this, we employed Firth’s penalized likelihood regression, which introduces a Jeffreys invariant prior penalty to eliminate first-order bias [18]. Empirical evidence confirms that this method provides unbiased odds ratios (ORs) in small samples (n < 100) with event rates < 35%, delivers accurate confidence intervals, and enables valid inference even under complete separation [19]. Four nested Firth penalized likelihood regression models were therefore developed to evaluate high-risk chronic atrophic gastritis (OLGA/OLGIM III-IV): Model 1 adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 added H. pylori status and family history; Model 3 incorporated endoscopic features; and Model 4 further included the LGR5/TFF2 ratio.

Model performance was compared using likelihood ratio tests (LRT), Akaike (AIC), and Bayesian (BIC) information criteria. Nonlinear relationships between the LGR5/TFF2 ratio and high-risk CAG were examined with restricted cubic splines (RCS, 3 knots). Stratified analyses were conducted by age, sex, H. pylori history, and family history of GC. A two-tailed P < 0.05 defined statistical significance.

Results

Patient characteristics by CAG risk status

Based on OLGA/OLGIM staging, 41 patients (68.33%) were classified as non‑high‑risk CAG and 19 patients (33.67%) as high‑risk CAG. The baseline characteristics of the two groups are summarized in Table 1. Patients in the high‑risk group were older (53.79 ± 8.34 vs. 49.76 ± 15.14 years) and had a higher proportion of family history of gastric cancer (21.05% vs. 4.88%, P = 0.06). Regarding H. pylori status, the high‑risk group had a significantly higher rate of previous infection (currently negative) (63.16% vs. 26.83%, P = 0.01). No significant differences were observed in BMI, smoking, or alcohol consumption between the two groups. Endoscopically, high‑risk patients exhibited more severe mucosal changes, including higher cumulative endoscopic scores (16.00 ± 6.89 vs. 10.05 ± 4.54, P = 0.002), more pronounced IM nodularity (3.37 ± 2.14 vs. 1.24 ± 1.61, P < 0.001), patchy redness (3.00 ± 0.67 vs. 1.80 ± 1.10, P < 0.001), and hemorrhagic spots (1.16 ± 1.26 vs. 0.24 ± 0.73, P = 0.007). Consistently, the high‑risk group had a significantly higher proportion of advanced Kimura‑Takemoto classification (C3‑O3: 68.42% vs. 14.63%, P < 0.001).

Marked differences were also observed in the stem cell kinetics markers. The high‑risk group showed significantly upregulated LGR5 (0.33 ± 0.06 vs. 0.28 ± 0.03, P < 0.001) and downregulated TFF2 (0.18 ± 0.04 vs. 0.42 ± 0.06, P < 0.001), resulting in a markedly higher LGR5/TFF2 ratio (1.92 ± 0.52 vs. 0.67 ± 0.13, P < 0.001). In addition, both β‑catenin and Cyclin D1 were significantly elevated in the high‑risk group (all P < 0.001), indicating activation of the Wnt/β‑catenin pathway.

ROC analysis and stratification by LGR5/TFF2 ratio

ROC curve analysis demonstrated that the LGR5/TFF2 ratio had excellent discriminatory power for identifying high-risk CAG (OLGA/OLGIM stage III-IV), with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.86–0.99). The optimal cutoff value was determined to be 1.066 using the Youden index, yielding a sensitivity of 90.00% and a specificity of 87.50% (Table 2). Based on this cutoff, the 60 patients were stratified into a low LGR5/TFF2 ratio group (n = 41, ratio ≤ 1.066) and a high LGR5/TFF2 ratio group (n = 19, ratio > 1.066).

Figure 2A demonstrates the correlation between the LGR5/TFF2 ratio and conventional GC risk factors. In non-high-risk CAG patients, the LGR5/TFF2 ratio showed significant positive correlations with OLGA/OLGIM stage, Kimura-Takemoto classification, Hemorrhagic spots, IM nodularity, Patchy redness, Uneven mucosal surface, and Cumulative endoscopic score (P < 0.05). These findings suggest that the LGR5/TFF2 ratio captures risk dimensions beyond conventional parameters. Although its association with individual traditional risk factors did not reach statistical significance, it showed partial overlap with these predictors in identifying high-risk CAG. This underscores the clinical value of incorporating the LGR5/TFF2 ratio into CAG risk assessment, particularly when combined with endoscopic and histopathological indicators, to enhance predictive accuracy.

Association of LGR5/TFF2 ratio with High-Risk CAG

Regarding the relationship between the LGR5/TFF2 ratio and high-risk CAG, we constructed four sequential models using Firth’s penalized logistic regression to evaluate predictors of high-risk CAG (Table 3; Fig. 2B-D). While models 1 (demographics only) and 2 (adding H. pylori status and family history) showed no significant improvement (model 1 vs. 2: LRT χ2 = 4.33, P = 0.115), model 3 (incorporating endoscopic features including IM nodularity, mucosal score, hemorrhagic spots, and uneven mucosal surface) demonstrated substantially enhanced fit (model 2 vs. 3: LRT χ2 = 27.52, P < 0.001). Most notably, model 4, which added the LGR5/TFF2 biomarker, achieved exceptional predictive improvement (model 3 vs. 4: LRT χ2 = 31.93, P < 0.001), with the fully adjusted model revealing a striking association between the ratio and high-risk CAG (OR = 48.50, 95% CI: 4.07-578.51, P = 0.002). Model 4 also exhibited optimal performance metrics with the lowest AIC (29.78) and BIC (50.72), confirming its superiority in balancing complexity and goodness-of-fit.

Additional validation analyses further supported the predictive value of the ratio. Restricted cubic splines confirmed a linear dose-response relationship (P-nonlinear > 0.05), and Fig. 3A revealed a threshold effect where the probability of high-risk status fluctuated between 5% and 45% at ratios below the cutoff, then increased sharply above it. Subgroup analyses stratified by gender, age, H. pylori infection status, and family history consistently demonstrated the robust predictive performance of this ratio across all subgroups (Fig. 3B). Notably, no significant interaction effects were observed (Table 4), indicating the potential universal applicability of this biomarker.

Discussion

This study establishes the LGR5/TFF2 ratio as the first validated molecular biomarker capable of independently predicting advanced-stage CAG (OLGA/OLGIM stages III-IV). Its predictive accuracy (AUC 0.93) significantly outperforms existing clinical parameters (AUC 0.68–0.75, P < 0.001). Patients with an elevated LGR5/TFF2 ratio (> 1.066) exhibited a striking 48.5-fold higher odds of high-risk CAG (95% CI: 4.07–578.51; P = 0.002), representing a clinically significant risk stratification threshold. Moreover, the ratio identified high-risk patients overlooked by current standards (e.g., H. pylori-negative cases). Its strong correlations with endoscopic high-risk features (IM nodularity; patchy redness) indicate an ability to capture dynamic mucosal evolution.

Intriguingly, the predictive power of traditional risk factors was attenuated in our cohort (H. pylori infection history: OR = 0.27, P = 0.115; family history of GC: OR = 3.48, P = 0.064) - a phenomenon that finds a plausible explanation in the underlying pathogenesis. Although H. pylori played a key role in initiating gastric carcinogenesis, its contribution diminishes following the development of IM (meta-analysis data indicate a 53% efficacy in cancer prevention for non-atrophic gastritis compared to only 15% in cases with IM) [20]. In our study, 63.16% (n = 12/19) of high-ratio patients demonstrated persistent malignant transformation potential despite successful H. pylori eradication, indicating that microbial intervention alone may be insufficient to reverse or halt the molecular alterations associated with advanced precancerous stages. While genetic factors (e.g., CDH1 mutations) significantly increase lifetime gastric cancer risk, they exhibit limited utility in predicting current disease severity or short-term risk [21, 22]. These observations suggest that once gastric mucosa crosses the ‘point of no return’—marked by aberrant stem cell proliferation—microbial and genetic influences become secondary.

Compared to mainstream risk assessment systems, the LGR5/TFF2 ratio offers unique advantages. While histological staging systems (e.g., OLGA/OLGIM) are widely employed, they are limited by static evaluation (AUC ≈ 0.65) and moderate interobserver variability (κ = 0.60) [23, 24]. Endoscopic scoring demonstrates improved discrimination (AUC = 0.79) [25] but remains operator-dependent (κ = 0.45–0.65 for Kimura-Takemoto classification [26]) and fails to identify molecular-level malignant transformation. While serum biomarkers (e.g., pepsinogen) provide clinical practicality, their diagnostic accuracy for early metaplasia detection remains suboptimal (AUC = 0.68) [27]. This limitation is further exacerbated by confounding variables such as the severity of gastric atrophy and concurrent H. pylori infection [28].

The LGR5/TFF2 ratio demonstrates unique clinical value by quantifying gastric stem cell niche imbalance (dynamic LGR5-mediated proliferation versus TFF2-impaired repair), achieving superior predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.93) while establishing molecular-endoscopic correlations. Notably, its superior performance compared to conventional endoscopic features and histological staging (ΔAIC = 29.95) highlights its potential to redefine current risk stratification frameworks. Clinical integration of this ratio into surveillance protocols may enable risk-adapted endoscopic scheduling (e.g., annual surveillance for high-ratio patients [> 1.066] versus biennial for low-ratio individuals), thereby optimizing the balance between early detection efficiency and healthcare resource utilization. Despite advances in non-invasive techniques (e.g., ctDNA [29]), the ratio’s tissue-specificity, cost-effectiveness (1/5–1/10 the cost of ctDNA sequencing), and compatibility with routine pathology workflows confer distinct advantages.

Biologically, our findings substantiate the gastric stem cell niche paradigm. The significant elevation in LGR5 expression (0.33 ± 0.06 vs 0.28 ± 0.03; P < 0.001) confirms expansion of metaplasia-competent stem cell populations, consistent with murine lineage-tracing studies [30]. The strong association between the LGR5/TFF2 ratio and both IM nodularity and cumulative endoscopic score demonstrates its capacity to quantify metaplastic transformation. Functionally, the ratio drives CAG pathogenesis by simultaneously inducing Wnt/β-catenin signaling while compromising mucosal repair mechanisms. In the high-ratio group, we observed marked activation of the Wnt pathway, evidenced by significantly elevated β-catenin expression (0.41 ± 0.04 vs 0.31 ± 0.07; P < 0.001), consistent with the established role of LGR5+ stem cells in driving metaplasia via R-spondin/Wnt signaling [30]. This Wnt activation triggered a 1.9-fold increase in Cyclin D1 expression (0.38 ± 0.03 vs 0.20 ± 0.06; P < 0.001), establishing an auto-amplifying proliferative circuit that drives sustained epithelial renewal. The concomitant TFF2 downregulation (0.18 ± 0.04) critically impaired mucosal defense systems, accurately reproducing the barrier disruption phenotype observed in genetic TFF2 deficiency models [31]. This ‘double-hit’ mechanism (combining accelerated proliferation with compromised mucosal defense) accounts for the ratio’s superior predictive performance compared to individual biomarkers. Spatial transcriptomic profiling provides direct validation of this model, revealing mutually exclusive spatial expression patterns of LGR5 and TFF2 within intestinal metaplasia lesions [32].

Methodologically, Firth’s penalized regression effectively addressed statistical biases from small sample size and rare events, providing reliable effect estimates (OR = 48.50) through bias-reducing priors [33] when traditional maximum likelihood estimation generated infinite ORs due to complete separation, an approach validated for extreme-effect biomarker scenarios in gastroenterological research [34].

Conclusions

This study establishes the LGR5/TFF2 ratio as a novel molecular biomarker that independently predicts high-risk CAG (OLGA/OLGIM stage III-IV), outperforming conventional clinical, endoscopic, and histologic parameters (AUC 0.93 vs. 0.68–0.75, P < 0.001). Patients with an elevated ratio (> 1.066) face a 48.5-fold increased risk (P = 0.002), providing a clinically actionable threshold for risk stratification. Critically, our findings reveal that once gastric mucosa crosses a molecular “point of no return”—marked by stem cell niche imbalance—microbial (e.g., H. pylori) and genetic factors become secondary to intrinsic proliferative reprogramming. This challenges the sufficiency of H. pylori eradication alone in advanced precancerous stages, as 63.16% of high-ratio patients retained malignant potential post-eradication. Clinical integration of this ratio could refine surveillance strategies, enabling risk-adapted endoscopic scheduling (e.g., intensified monitoring for high-ratio patients). By bridging molecular alterations with endoscopic phenotypes, the LGR5/TFF2 ratio addresses a critical diagnostic gap, where visible mucosal changes lag behind molecular risk.

Innovations and limitations

Our study marks a paradigm shift in CAG risk stratification, transitioning from conventional histomorphological staging to functional molecular classification. This approach mirrors the transformative impact of molecular subtyping in breast cancer management, which successfully augmented the TNM staging system. By delineating molecularly distinct subgroups with divergent risk profiles, our model addresses the persistent clinical challenge of outcome heterogeneity among patients with comparable disease stages, enabling more precise risk stratification.

While these findings provide important insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center investigation conducted in an Asian population, the absence of an external validation cohort is a limitation. The promising predictive performance of the LGR5/TFF2 ratio (AUC = 0.93) and the identified cutoff value of 1.066 were derived from a single dataset and require validation in independent, prospective, multi-center cohorts, particularly across diverse ethnic groups and regions with varying gastric cancer incidence, before any clinical implementation can be considered. Second, the relatively small sample size (n = 60), while methodologically addressed using Firth’s penalized regression to mitigate bias, limits the statistical power for subgroup analyses and the precision of effect estimates. Larger studies are needed to confirm our findings. Third, the cross-sectional design precludes assessment of the temporal dynamics of the LGR5/TFF2 ratio. Prospective longitudinal studies with serial endoscopic surveillance and repeated biomarker measurements are essential to determine whether an elevated ratio truly predicts progression over time or primarily reflects current disease severity.
