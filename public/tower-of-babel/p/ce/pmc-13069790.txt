Complement protein C1q induces endothelial apoptosis through C1qBP regulation of mitochondrial function in lung endothelial cells in systemic sclerosis
Authors: Zixi Yi, Kexin Liu, Mohammed Shabin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSystemic sclerosis (SSc) is an autoimmune disease characterized by vasculopathy and fibrosis, with pulmonary complications as the leading cause of mortality. Although abnormal activation of the complement system is implicated in SSc pathogenesis, the role of complement protein C1q in pulmonary endothelial injury remains unclear.MethodsIn this study, we employed a bleomycin (BLM)-induced SSc mouse model and cultured lung microvascular endothelial cells to investigate the effect of C1q on mitochondrial function and endothelial apoptosis. We further investigated the involvement of complement component 1q subcomponent-binding protein (C1qBP) in this process. We performed immunohistochemistry, Western blotting, immunoprecipitation, mitochondrial function assays, and flow cytometry to assess protein expression, mitochondrial integrity, and apoptotic responses.ResultsExpression of C1q and C1qBP was significantly elevated in the SSc model group, accompanied by translocation of prohibitin 2 (PHB2) from the mitochondria to the cytoplasm, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, reduced ATP synthesis, and increased apoptosis. Knockdown of C1qBP inhibited PHB2 translocation, improved mitochondrial function, reduced endothelial apoptosis, and attenuated lung fibrosis.ConclusionC1q regulates the mitochondrial localization of PHB2 via C1qBP, inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and endothelial apoptosis, thereby contributing to SSc-associated lung injury. Targeting the C1q-C1qBP-PHB2 axis may provide a new therapeutic strategy for SSc.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10020-026-01445-0.

Introduction

SSc is a systemic autoimmune disease characterized by vascular pathology, immune dysregulation, and widespread fibrosis (Gogulska et al. 2024). Pulmonary complications are the leading cause of mortality, with interstitial lung disease (ILD) and pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) accounting for over 60% of SSc-related deaths (Cottin and Brown 2019). Approximately 80% of patients with SSc develop ILD, which presents as pulmonary interstitial fibrosis and alveolar structural destruction, leading to restrictive ventilatory dysfunction and impaired gas exchange (Roofeh et al. 2019). Injury to pulmonary arterial endothelial cells is a key pathological feature of PAH, the progression of which can lead to right heart failure (Tonutti et al. 2024). Although immunomodulatory and antifibrotic therapies can slow disease progression, the molecular mechanisms underlying endothelial dysfunction remain incompletely understood, limiting the development of targeted therapies.

Dysregulated activation of the complement system is implicated in various autoimmune diseases. As the initiating component of the classical complement pathway, C1q plays a critical role in the clearance of apoptotic cells and immune complexes (Vandivier et al. 2002). Structurally, C1q is a hexameric protein composed of 18 polypeptide chains that form a bouquet-like structure with collagen-like tails and globular heads (Jonkman et al. 2025). The globular heads recognize a broad range of ligands, including immunoglobulins, pentraxins, and apoptotic cell surfaces, while the collagen-like regions interact with cellular receptors such as cC1qR/CR and C1qBP, thereby triggering intracellular signaling (Thielens et al. 2017b). Studies suggest that C1q deficiency contributes to diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), while abnormal deposition of complement components in patients with SSc may exacerbate vascular injury (McAdoo et al. 2014). Notably, C1q not only contributes to immune regulation but also directly affects cellular function by binding to C1qBP on the surface of endothelial cells (Thielens et al. 2017a). However, the precise role of C1q in SSc-related pulmonary endothelial injury and its association with mitochondrial dysfunction remains unclear, providing an important direction for further investigation.

C1q-binding protein (C1qBP, also known as p32 or gC1q-R) is a multifunctional protein that binds to the globular heads of C1q to inhibit complement activation and localizes to the mitochondrial matrix, where it contributes to oxidative phosphorylation and energy metabolism (Zhang et al. 2013). Acting as a multifunctional adapter, C1qBP engages with C1q on the cell surface to modulate immune and inflammatory responses (Ghebrehiwet et al. 2024). Within the mitochondria, it participates in the assembly and stability of respiratory chain complexes and regulates energy metabolism (Gutiérrez-Aguilar et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2025). Mutations in the C1qBP gene have been associated with mitochondrial complex dysfunction, leading to cardiomyopathy and metabolic disturbances (Wang et al. 2022). In SSc-related pulmonary vasculopathy, endothelial mitochondrial dysfunction, such as reduced membrane potential and ROS accumulation is closely linked to apoptosis (Tang et al. 2014). These observations suggest that C1q may regulate mitochondrial homeostasis through C1qBP, thereby influencing endothelial cell survival; however, this hypothesis has yet to be validated in the context of SSc.

Mitochondria serve as the central hub for energy metabolism and apoptotic regulation in endothelial cells (Morales et al. 2022). Mitochondrial dysfunction is characterized by loss of membrane potential, cytochrome c release and caspase cascade activation, ultimately triggering apoptosis (Liu et al. 2024). In pulmonary endothelial cells of SSc patients, oxidative stress and impaired mitophagy contribute to fibrotic progression. For example, palmitate-induced endothelial injury models exhibit significant activation of mitochondrial apoptotic pathways, evidenced by elevated Bax/Bcl-2 ratios. Furthermore, C1qBP modulates mitochondrial respiratory chain complex activity and calcium homeostasis, potentially serving as a critical nexus linking complement signaling to mitochondrial apoptosis (Li et al. 2025b; Wang et al. 2022). These findings suggest that the C1q-C1qBP axis may promote SSc-associated pulmonary endothelial apoptosis via mitochondrial pathways, although the underlying mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated.

While the clinical significance of pulmonary vascular pathology in SSc is well established, the relationship between the complement system and mitochondrial function has not been systematically investigated. Current literature predominantly focuses on immune complex deposition and fibroblast activation, often overlooking the intrinsic mitochondrial regulatory mechanisms within endothelial cells (Bryon et al. 2024; Nagy et al. 2025). This study is the first to propose the hypothesis that C1q induces pulmonary endothelial apoptosis in SSc via C1qBP-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction, employing both in vitro and in vivo models to validate the underlying molecular pathways. Our findings provide new mechanistic insights into SSc-associated pulmonary injury and establish a theoretical foundation for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies focused on complement-mitochondrial regulation, highlighting significant scientific and clinical implications.

Materials and methods

Animals

Female C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from Beijing specific pathogen-free (SPF) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All animals were housed under SPF conditions, with controlled ambient temperature (21–26 °C), humidity (40–70%), and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Mice had ad libitum access to food and water throughout the study. After a one-week acclimatization period, experimental procedures were initiated. All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the guidelines approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University (2024-Y-0719).

Establishment of the SSc mouse model

As previously described, an SSc mouse model was induced via a single tail vein injection of BLM hydrochloride (200 mg/kg) (Chang-li et al. 2015). Experimental treatments were initiated 21 days post-injection. At the endpoint, all mice were euthanized, and lung fibrotic tissues were harvested for analysis.

Female C57BL/6J mice were randomly assigned to four experimental groups (n = 6): (1) Control group: Received an equal volume of saline via tail vein injection. (2) Model group: Administered a single tail vein injection of BLM hydrochloride (200 mg/kg). (3) Model + LV-sh-NC group: Injected with BLM hydrochloride (200 mg/kg) followed by 50 µL of C1qBP scrambled control lentivirus (LV-sh-NC, titer: 1 × 109 TU/mL) on day 2. (4) Model + LV-sh-C1qBP group: Treated with BLM hydrochloride (200 mg/kg) and 50 µL of C1qBP-targeting lentivirus (LV-sh-C1qBP, titer: 1 × 109 TU/mL) on day 2.

Cell culture and treatment

Primary mouse lung microvascular endothelial cells (PMECs) were obtained from CyberKnife Biologicals Ltd. and cultured in endothelial cell medium (ECM) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1% primary endothelial cell growth supplement (1001, ScienCell). According to the supplier’s certification, immunofluorescence staining confirmed that the purity of endothelial cells exceeded 90%. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. All in vitro cellular experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates.

Cells were stimulated with 3 mU/mL BLM for 24 h (Merck, B1141000) and then cultured until the cells reached 70%-80% confluence. For transfection experiments, cells were transfected with Lipofectamine 8000 (4 µL) encoding si-C1qBP (2.5 µL) and si-NC (2.5 µL), si-C1qBP+PHB2-S293A WT (2.5 µL, lentiviral titer: 5 × 108 TU/mL) and si-C1qBP+PHB2-S293A MUT (2.5 µL, lentiviral titer: 5 × 108 TU/mL) of empty vectors were used to transfect cells. After 24–48 h of transfection, cells were harvested for subsequent analysis.

Histological staining

Lung fibrotic tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Histopathological evaluation was performed using the following staining methods:

H&E staining: tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. After staining with hematoxylin (G1004, Xavier) for 5 min and eosin (G1001, Xavier) for 2 min, the slides were dehydrated, cleared and mounted on slides. Microscopic observation was used to assess the structure and inflammatory response of mouse lung fibrotic tissue. Subsequently, fibrosis severity was semi-quantitatively assessed using the Ashcroft scoring system. Tissue sections were comprehensively examined at low magnification, with the field of view divided into multiple regions. Each region was scored at high magnification according to the following criteria. The final Ashcroft score for each sample was calculated as the average of all region scores across the entire section.

Masson staining: using Masson trichrome staining kit (G1340, Solarbio), sections were dewaxed and hydrated, stained with Weigert iron hematoxylin for 5–10 min, acidic differentiation solution for 10 s and then stained with Lichun’s magenta for 8 min. It was then cleared with phosphomolybdic acid solution and finally stained with aniline blue staining solution for 1–2 min. Collagen deposition in the treated lung tissue was observed microscopically.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded lung tissue Sect. (4 μm) underwent deparaffinization and rehydration, followed by antigen retrieval. Following addition of endogenous peroxidase blocker, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-C1qBP (1:100, MA1-40312, Thermo fisher), anti-α-SMA (1:100, BM3902, Boster), anti-COL1A2 (1:100, 14629-1-AP, Biodragon), anti-PHB2 (1:100, 66424-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti-BAX (1:100, A00183, Boster), anti-Bcl-2 (1:100, Boster, BA0412), anti-Beclin1 (1:100, Ab210498, Abcam), anti-Parkin (1:100, Boster, BM4909), and anti-Pink1 (1:100, BD-PB3889, Biodragon). After washing, sections were incubated with universal secondary antibody (PV-6000, Zsbio) at 37 °C for 20 min. The antigen-antibody complex was visualized using the DAB kit (G1212, Servicebio), which produced a brown precipitate. Finally, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin (G1004, Servicebio) to label nuclei, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Images were acquired using a light microscope (Leica).

Immunofluorescence staining

Lung fibrosis tissue: Paraffin-embedded lung tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated using PBS. Sections were then permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 to enhance antibody penetration and then blocked with 3% BSA for 30 min. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a C1qBP primary antibody (1:200, 68084-1-Ig, Proteintech). After incubation, sections were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with FITC-labelled goat anti-mouse IgG (1:100, A0568, Beyotime) secondary antibody. After washing, the cells were stained with DAPI and analyzed under the fluorescence microscope.

Lung endothelial cells: Primary lung microvascular endothelial cells were cultured to approximately 80% confluence, and then washed with PBS and treated with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min. After permeabilization, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: C1qBP primary antibody (1:100, 68084-1-Ig, Proteintech), PHB2 primary antibody (1:100, 12295-1-AP, Proteintech), BAX primary antibody (1:100, CY5059, Abways), Bcl-2 primary antibody (1:100, A00040-2, PhD), Beclin1 primary antibody (1:100, 66665-1-Ig, Proteintech), Parkin primary antibody (1:100, BM4909, PhD) and Pink primary antibody (1:100, A00201-2, PhD). After incubation, the cells were incubated with secondary antibodies of FITC-labelled goat anti-mouse IgG (1:100, A0568, Beyotime) and Cy3-labelled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:100, A0516, Beyotime) for 1 h at 37 °C. After DAPI nuclear counterstaining, images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope to assess protein expression and subcellular localization.

Immunofluorescence co-staining

For immunofluorescence co-staining studies, lung tissue Sect. (4 μm) and cultured lung microvascular endothelial cells were prepared as described above. After permeabilization and blocking, samples were incubated simultaneously with pairs of primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, including combinations of: C1qBP (1:100, 68084-1-Ig, Proteintech) with PHB2 (1:100, 12295-1-AP, Proteintech); C1qBP (1:200, 80490-1rr, Proteintech) with CD31 (1:200, Ab222783, Abcam); CD31 (1:200, Ab222783, Abcam) with Hypoxyprobe HypoxyprobeTM-1 Plus Kit (HP2-100kit, mangkangbio) for hypoxia detection; CD31 (1:200, Ab222783, Abcam) with HIF-1α (1:200, Ab179483, Abcam); and TUNEL detection kits (G1501, Servicebio) with CD31 (1:200, Ab222783, Abcam) for apoptosis-endothelial co-localization. After washing, samples were incubated with Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:100, A0516, Beyotime) for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. For TUNEL co-staining, TUNEL reaction was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol, followed by CD31 immunostaining. All samples were counterstained with DAPI (G1012, Servicebio) and imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Keyence, BZ-X800).

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl Transferase dUTP Nick End Labeling (TUNEL) staining

Cells were processed according to the TUNEL Apoptosis Detection Kit (C1086, Biotronik), followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS was added and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. 100 µL of TUNEL assay solution was added to the samples and incubated at 37 °C, protected from light, for 60 min. Subsequently, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence microscopy was used to acquire and analyze images.

FITC-FD4 permeability assay

Cells were inoculated in the Transwell chamber at a density of 1 × 105/mL. After fusion, 10 µL of 1 mg/mL FITC-labeled dextran (4 kDa, Beyotime, ST2930-50 mg) was added and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 4 h. A 100 µL aliquot of the culture medium was collected from the lower chamber. The fluorescence intensity was then measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher, Multiskan FC) with excitation/emission wavelengths of 490 nm and 520 nm, respectively. The amount of FD4 permeation was reflected by the relative fluorescence value.

Red CMXRos staining to detect mitochondrial activity

Mitochondrial activity was assessed using the Mito-Tracker Red CMXRos (Beyotime, C1049B) fluorescence probe method. After the cells were inoculated in 48-well plates and cultured for 24 h, the culture medium was discarded and washed 3 times with PBS. 200 µM Mito-Tracker Red CMXRos working solution was added and the cells were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 15–30 min. After removing the probe, the preheated medium was replaced. The cell nuclei (Hoechst 33258, Beyotime, C1017) were treated in the dark for 10 min and washed 4 times with PBS. Fluorescence images were acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Keyence, BZ-X800) and five random fields per group were selected for quantitative analysis.

Fluo-4 AM fluorescent probe detects intracellular Ca2+ levels

Intracellular calcium levels were detected using the Fluo-4 AM fluorescent probe (Seville, G1724). After the cells were cultured in 48-well plates for 24 h, they were washed 2–3 times with PBS. Then, 100 µL of Fluo-4 AM working solution was added, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Following the removal of the probe, the cells were washed 2–3 times with PBS and subsequently stained with Hoechst 33,258 (Beyotime, C1017) for 10 min to label the nuclei. Fluorescence images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Keenz, BZ-X800). The fluorescence intensity was analyzed quantitatively using ImageJ software. Five random fields per group were analyzed, and each experiment was independently repeated three times.

Mitochondrial Membrane Potential (MMP) and ATP determination

Mitochondrial MMP determination: The JC-1 fluorescence probe method (C2006, Beyotime) was adopted. After the cells were washed with PBS, they were incubated at 37 °C in the dark with 1 mL of JC-1 working solution for 20 min. Then, they were washed twice with pre-cooled 1×JC-1 buffer. Changes in MMP were evaluated by measuring the red-to-green fluorescence ratio via flow cytometry (RMNNC-3000, Agilent).

ATP content detection: Use the ATP detection kit (BC0300, Solabol). After cell lysis, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation, and absorbance at 340 nm was measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. ATP concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve.

Evaluation of mitochondrial ETC activity

Mitochondrial respiratory chain complex activity was measured using CheKine™ series of kits provided by Abbkine (Complex I KTB1850, Complex II KTB1860, Complex III KTB1870, Complex IV KTB1880). After homogenization of the cells, mitochondria were separated by centrifugation at 600 g (5 min) and 11,000 g (10 min) in sequence. The activities of each complex were detected respectively after resuspension: For complex Ⅰ (340 nm, ΔA = A₁-A₂), complex Ⅱ (605 nm, ΔA = A₁-A₂), complex Ⅲ (550 nm, ΔA = A₂-A₁) and complex Ⅳ (550 nm, ΔA = A₁-A₂), the enzyme activities were calculated by the change in absorbance per unit time.

Cell apoptosis was detected by flow cytometry

Cell apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining method (Seville, G1511). After the cells were cultured to an 80%-90% fusion degree, they were digested and collected by trypsin, washed twice with PBS and resuspended with 1×Binding Buffer to adjust the cell concentration to 1–5 × 106/mL. Take 100 µL of the cell suspension and add 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL of PI. Incubate at room temperature and in the dark for 8–10 min. Then add 400 µL of Binding Buffer and mix well. Apoptosis was immediately analyzed using flow cytometry (Agilent, RMNC-3000).

Flow cytometry analysis of cell viability

Live cell viability was assessed using PI (Servicebio, GA1174) staining method. After the cells were fused to 80–90%, they were collected by trypsin digestion and washed twice with PBS (1000 g, 4 °C, 5 min). The cell suspension was taken and incubated with 5 µL of PI for 8–10 min at room temperature in the dark. Stained cells were immediately analyzed using flow cytometry (Agilent, RMNNC-3000).

Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Treated lung endothelial cells from each group were collected and treated with RIPA lysate (Thermo Fisher). Subsequently, the immunoprecipitation kit was manipulated according to the instructions (Beyotime). Primary antibodies (anti-C1qBP or anti-PHB2) were incubated with Protein A + G magnetic beads for 2 h. Cell lysates were incubated with primary antibody-head complexes overnight at 4 °C on a shaker. After centrifugation, protein head complexes were suspended in protein lysates. The immunoprecipitants were finally analyzed by protein immunoblotting.

Quantitative Real-Time fluorescence PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from lung endothelial cells using TRIzol reagent (G3013, Servicebio). The extracted RNA was used as a template for PCR amplification according to the instructions for TaqMan One-Step PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (RR037Q; Takara). Statistical analysis

All experimental data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 16.0 software (IBM, USA), and intergroup comparisons were conducted by one-way ANOVA. Graphical representations were generated with GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Complement proteins C1q and C1qBP were upregulated in SSc endothelial cells

Following BLM induction, the SSc mice model exhibited significant pulmonary fibrosis, as demonstrated by H&E and Masson staining. H&E staining revealed that compared to the Control group, lung tissue in the Model group showed marked structural disruption, including alveolar collapse and parenchymal consolidation, as corroborated by a significant increase in the semi-quantitative Ashcroft score (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, Masson’s trichrome staining confirmed enhanced collagen deposition in the fibrotic lungs (Fig. 1B). Immunohistochemistry and hydroxyproline assays revealed elevated levels of α-SMA, COL1A2 and hydroxyproline (HYP) (Fig. 1C-D), further confirming fibrotic progression. TUNEL and CD31 co-staining confirmed a significant increase in endothelial cell apoptosis in the Model group, consistent with elevated cleaved caspase-3 expression in pulmonary fibrosis, as shown by Western blot analysis (Fig. 1E-F). Additionally, qRT-PCR, Western blot and immunofluorescence analyses showed that complement components C1q, C1qA, C1qB and C1qBP were significantly upregulated, while the p-PHB2/PHB2 ratio was decreased (Fig. 1G-I). To investigate mitochondrial dysregulation, endothelial cells were isolated from lung tissues. Western blot analysis revealed a notable reduction in mitochondrial PHB2 expression and a corresponding increase in cytoplasmic PHB2 levels, suggesting enhanced mitochondrial-to-cytoplasmic translocation of PHB2 in the Model group (Fig. 1J). Collectively, these results indicate that in the SSc model, lung tissues exhibit upregulated complement protein expression (C1q and C1qBP), mitochondrial dysfunction, and increased apoptosis, all of which may contribute to fibrotic pathogenesis. Importantly, isolated endothelial cells exhibited PHB2 translocation, suggesting mitochondrial dysfunction may contribute to endothelial injury during fibrosis.

C1qBP knockdown inhibited BLM-induced apoptosis and protected endothelial cells

To investigate the role of C1qBP in endothelial injury, we first confirmed the knockdown efficiency of si-C1qBP in an in vitro endothelial cell injury model (Supplementary Fig. 1A-B). Western blot assays revealed that BLM induction significantly upregulated C1qBP and cleaved caspase-3 expression, whereas si-C1qBP treatment reversed this effect (Fig. 2A-B). TUNEL detection results showed that cell apoptosis exhibited the same trend (Fig. 2C). In the early stages of SSc, endothelial cell apoptosis directly impairs barrier function. To simulate this critical pathological pathway, we employed a hydrogen peroxide-induced endothelial cell hyperpermeability model to evaluate the role of C1qBP in maintaining endothelial barrier integrity. As shown by a permeability assay, H₂O₂ treatment markedly increased endothelial hyperpermeability compared to the Control group, but this effect was significantly ameliorated by si-C1qBP (Fig. 2D). qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses demonstrated that H₂O₂ exposure upregulated ICAM1 while downregulating tight junction components (ZO1, ZO2, OCLN, JAM3). In contrast, si-C1qBP treatment restored the expression of these barrier-associated proteins (Fig. 2E-F). Flow cytometry further confirmed the protective role of C1qBP knockdown, showing a significant increase in viable endothelial cells in the si-C1qBP group compared to the si-NC group (Fig. 2G). Next, we explored the mechanistic link between C1q and endothelial survival. Flow cytometry revealed that BLM significantly reduced endothelial cell viability compared to the Con group. Notably, purified C1q (12.5 and 25 µg) potentiated the BLM-induced injury in a dose-dependent manner, resulting in a further significant reduction in cell survival (Fig. 2H). Conversely, blocking C1qBP with a neutralizing antibody significantly enhanced endothelial cell survival under BLM-induced stress (Fig. 2I). These findings indicate that C1qBP exacerbates endothelial injury by activating cleaved caspase-3-dependent apoptosis and disrupting barrier function.

C1qBP inhibited PHB2 phosphorylation and promoted its translocation from mitochondria to cytosol, leading to endothelial mitochondrial dysfunction

BLM-induced endothelial cells were treated with si-C1qBP. In BLM-induced endothelial cells, C1qBP expression was significantly increased while the p-PHB2/PHB2 was reduced. However, after si-C1qBP treatment, the opposite trend was shown (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, after BLM treatment, the PHB2 level was decreased, but the PHB2 level in Cyto was increased. si-C1qBP treatment reversed this phenomenon, indicating that si-C1qBP inhibited the BLM-induced transport of PHB2 from mitochondria to cytoplasm (Fig. 3B). Co-immunoprecipitation and immunofluorescence assays confirmed enhanced C1qBP-PHB2 interaction and colocalization in BLM-stimulated endothelial cells, which was attenuated by C1qBP knockdown (Fig. 3C-D, Supplementary Fig. 2A-B). BLM treatment resulted in endothelial cell apoptosis, impaired mitophagy and suppressed mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt), as indicated by increased expression of pro-apoptotic BAX, decreased anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 expression, reduced levels of mitophagy-related proteins (Beclin1, Parkin, and Pink1), and downregulated UPRmt markers (mtDnaJ, ClpP, LonP1, Hsp10), whereas C1qBP knockdown alleviated these BLM-induced abnormalities in apoptosis, mitophagy, and UPRmt (Fig. 3E-F, Supplementary Fig. 2C-E). Mitochondrial functional assays demonstrated that C1qBP knockdown partially reversed BLM-induced impairments, including decreased mitochondrial activity (Fig. 3G, Supplementary Fig. 2F), elevated intracellular Ca2+ levels (Fig. 3H), loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) (Fig. 3I-J), reduced ATP production (Fig. 3K) and diminished electron transport chain (Complexes I-IV) activities (Fig. 3L). These findings confirmed that C1qBP mediated phosphorylation and cytosolic translocation of PHB2, contributing to mitochondrial dysfunction in BLM-induced endothelial injury.

C1qBP promoted endothelial apoptosis via PHB2-mediated mitochondrial dysregulation

To elucidate the pivotal role of PHB2 phosphorylation in C1qBP-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction, experimental systems featuring PHB2-S293A wild-type/mutant and C1qBP knockdown/non-knockdown were established in a BLM-induced endothelial cell model. Western blot analysis revealed that, under identical PHB2 conditions (WT or MUT), si-C1qBP treatment significantly reduced C1qBP levels and increased the p-PHB2/PHB2 ratio compared to respective si-NC groups. Conversely, under identical siRNA treatment conditions (si-NC or si-C1qBP), the PHB2-S293A mutation markedly reduced the p-PHB2/PHB2 ratio compared to respective WT groups, confirming mutant efficacy (Fig. 4A). Regarding apoptosis, mitophagy, and unfolded protein response: Under identical PHB2 genotypes, C1qBP knockdown markedly downregulated pro-apoptotic proteins Bax, upregulated anti-apoptotic proteins Bcl-2, key mitophagy-related proteins (Beclin1, Parkin, and Pink1), and UPRmt markers (mtDnaJ, ClpP, LonP1, Hsp10). Conversely, under the same siRNA treatment conditions, the PHB2-S293A mutation significantly reversed these molecular changes (Fig. 4B-E). Functional analysis further confirmed that the PHB2-S293A mutation significantly exacerbated mitochondrial dysfunction under C1qBP knockdown conditions, manifested as reduced mitochondrial activity, elevated intracellular Ca²⁺ levels, diminished electron transport chain activity (Complexes I-IV), loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, decreased ATP production, and reduced endothelial cell survival (Fig. 4F-K). These results indicate that phosphorylation of the PHB2 Ser293 site is a key molecular mechanism of C1qBP-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction and blocking the phosphorylation of this site significantly improves mitochondrial function and inhibits endothelial cell apoptosis.

C1qBP knockdown attenuated lung fibrosis in the SSc fibrosis model

A lentiviral vector carrying sh-C1qBP was constructed and administered via thoracic injection into the SSc mouse model. Knockdown efficiency was verified (Supplementary Fig. 1C-D). Western blot showed that C1qBP protein expression was significantly increased in the Model group, while the p-PHB2/PHB2 ratio was reduced and the sh-C1qBP treatment showed the opposite trend (Fig. 5A). Immunohistochemistry further confirmed the down-regulation of C1qBP expression in sh-C1qBP-treated lung tissues (Fig. 5B). Immunofluorescence co-localization studies demonstrated enhanced association between C1qBP and the endothelial marker CD31 in the Model group, which was markedly attenuated upon C1qBP knockdown (Fig. 5C). H&E staining analysis demonstrated severe alveolar destruction and parenchymal consolidation in the Model group, which were significantly ameliorated by C1qBP knockdown, resulting in markedly reduced Ashcroft scores (Fig. 5D). Correspondingly, Masson’s trichrome staining revealed extensive collagen deposition in model mice, which was substantially attenuated upon C1qBP knockdown (Fig. 5E). Immunohistochemical assays further confirmed that the expression of fibrosis markers α-SMA and COL1A2 was down-regulated after sh-C1qBP treatment compared to the sh-NC group (Fig. 5F).

Subsequently, the mechanism of C1qBP in pulmonary fibrosis was further investigated. Discussion

Recent studies have demonstrated a strong association between aberrant complement activation and vascular injury in SSc, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear (Xie et al. 2025). While previous research has primarily focused on complement-mediated inflammatory responses and immune complex deposition, our study is the first to reveal a novel mechanism by which C1q regulates mitochondrial function through its interaction with C1qBP and PHB2. In contrast to the autoimmune abnormalities caused by C1q deficiency in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), SSc pulmonary endothelial cells exhibit C1q/C1qBP overactivation — a striking divergence suggesting that complement components may exert opposing regulatory effects in different diseases (Shah et al. 2015). Although mitochondrial dysfunction has been implicated in PAH progression, direct evidence of its role in SSc-associated pulmonary fibrosis has been lacking.

In this study, we first confirmed the upregulation of complement proteins C1q and C1qBP in lung endothelial cells in the SSc mice model, which was accompanied by abnormal mitochondrial function and increased apoptosis. Following bleomycin (BLM) induction, we observed significant fibrotic features in lung tissue, including disruption of alveolar structure, increased collagen deposition and significantly elevated α-SMA and COL1A2 expression. These results are consistent with previous studies, supporting the successful establishment of our model (Sadeghi Shaker et al. 2024; Shah et al. 2015). Wenxin et al. reported that C1q expression was significantly elevated in a BLM-induced lung fibrosis model (verified by QPCR and immunohistochemistry) and was positively correlated with alveolar structural disruption and increased hydroxyproline content (Kou et al. 2022). Moreover, SSc patients have been shown to exhibit increased pulmonary capillary permeability (99mTc-DTPA clearance elevated) and plasma ET-1 levels, which correlate positively with fibrosis severity. ET-1 and PDGF released by endothelial cells activate peripheral fibroblasts, forming a ‘vascular-mesenchymal dialogue’ (Grönberg et al. 2024). Further analysis revealed that the expression of C1q and C1qBP was significantly upregulated in endothelial cells in the Model group, which was accompanied by abnormalities in mitochondrial function and increased apoptosis of endothelial cells. Meanwhile, in the BLM model, C1q expression was significantly elevated in lung tissue. It exacerbates ECM deposition by activating fibroblasts and type II alveolar epithelial cells and promoting the expression of pro-fibrotic genes (Collagen I, Fibronectin) (Kou et al. 2022; Li et al. 2025a ). It has been demonstrated that endothelial cell expression of Bax and cleaved caspase-3 is elevated, along with reduced levels of Bcl-2 and Beclin-1 in the BLM model, suggesting increased mitochondrial membrane permeability and apoptotic vesicle formation (Chien et al. 2023). This suggests that the C1q-C1qBP signaling pathway plays an important role in SSc lung injury.

Second, by constructing an endothelial cell injury model and silencing C1qBP expression, we confirmed that C1qBP is involved in endothelial cell injury by promoting the cleaved caspase-3-mediated apoptotic pathway. Cell permeability experiments showed that H₂O₂ treatment significantly increased endothelial permeability, which was significantly improved by si-C1qBP treatment. It has been shown that H₂O₂ induces the reorganization of endothelial cytoskeletal proteins (F-actin) to form dense stress fibers, leading to cell contraction and gap widening (Simoneau et al. 2012). This change is accompanied by the degradation of intercellular adhesion junctional proteins (VE-Cadherin), weakening endothelial intercellular junctions. For example, immunofluorescence staining revealed disrupted continuity of VE-Cadherin with burr-like breaks after 60 min of H₂O₂ exposure, an effect that was mitigated by pretreatment with sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P) (Hecquet et al. 2008a, b). It has also been reported that C1qBP directly interacts with the target protein of cyclosporin A (CsA), the cyclophilin D (CypD) and inhibits MPT pore opening. This inhibition prevents mitochondrial swelling, cytochrome C release and subsequent apoptosis. Experiments showed that C1qBP overexpression significantly reduced H₂O₂-induced cell death, whereas si-C1qBP enhanced the stability of the MPT pore (McGee and Baines 2010). In addition, we found that C1qBP causes mitochondrial dysfunction by affecting the cytoplasmic translocation of PHB2, which in turn promotes apoptosis in endothelial cells. Flow cytometry confirmed that cell viability was significantly reduced in the si-C1qBP group compared to the si-NC group. It was shown that after loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, cytochrome C is released and binds to Apaf-1, which activates the Caspase-9/3 cascade reaction and induces endothelial cell apoptosis (Tang et al. 2014). It was shown that C1qBP defects lead to an imbalance in the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, which further exacerbates this process (Wang et al. 2022). These findings provide new insights into the role of C1qBP in mitochondrial dysfunction and endothelial injury in SSc lung fibrosis, addressing a previously unexplored aspect of disease pathogenesis.

In our animal experiments, we observed that C1qBP knockdown could attenuate the process of lung fibrosis in SSc mice by modulating the PHB2 signaling pathway and improving mitochondrial function. Immunohistochemical analysis showed that the expression of C1qBP was significantly down-regulated in lung tissues after sh-C1qBP treatment, while the expression of fibrosis markers α-SMA and COL1A2 was also significantly reduced. This result further confirms the important role of C1qBP in the process of SSc lung fibrosis. It has been shown that in patients with SSc combined with pulmonary fibrosis, the mRNA and protein expression levels of C1qBP are significantly elevated and negatively correlated with lung function (decreased DLco), suggesting that it can be used as a predictive marker for disease progression (Saha et al. 2019). In addition, we observed that the expression of the pro-apoptotic protein BAX was decreased after treatment with sh-C1qBP, whereas the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 was increased and the expression of the mitophagy markers Beclin1, Parkin and Pink1 was also reduced. It has been shown that C1qBP regulates ROS generation through XDH, and the reduction of ROS after sh-C1qBP led to a decrease in apoptosis and a decrease in the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio (Li et al. 2022). A similar protective mechanism has been validated in other studies, such as Hydroxytyrosol protects cardiomyocytes by reducing ROS to inhibit Bax and up-regulate Bcl-2 (Miao et al. 2018). In our study, we experimentally verified the critical role of C1q-C1qBP in SSc lung endothelial injury and elucidated the specific mechanism by which it leads to mitochondrial dysfunction by affecting PHB2 cytoplasmic translocation.

This study demonstrates that C1qBP knockdown can improve mitochondrial function by regulating the PHB2 signaling pathway, thereby attenuating the progression of lung fibrosis in SSc mice and providing new insights into the pathological mechanisms of SSc. However, this study still has certain limitations. This newly discovered mechanism was only tested in vivo and in vitro using the bleomycin-induced C57BL/6J female mouse model. Future research should include validation of the effects in additional animal models to enhance generalizability. In addition, a review highlighted multiple signaling molecules and pathways involved in SSc, such as PRMT5 and Notch1 (Liang et al. 2024). While our study mainly focused on the induction of endothelial apoptosis by C1q through the modulation of mitochondrial function by C1qBP, a comprehensive understanding of the important role of C1q-C1qBP in endothelial injury of the lungs in SSc will require further investigation of its downstream effector molecules or other related pathways (TGF-β/Smad) (Xing et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2014). Future studies exploring these mechanisms may lead to more effective therapeutic strategies for SSc.

Conclusion

In this study, we elucidated for the first time the mechanism by which C1q mediates mitochondrial dysfunction in SSc-associated lung endothelial injury, using both in vivo and in vitro experimental models. We demonstrated that activation of the C1q-C1qBP signaling pathway induces the translocation of PHB2 from mitochondria to cytoplasm, which in turn triggers mitochondrial dysfunction and endothelial cell apoptosis. In animal models, knockdown of C1qBP significantly attenuated lung fibrosis and restored endothelial barrier integrity. These findings provide a novel theoretical basis for understanding the pathological process of SSc pulmonary fibrosis and establish a theoretical foundation for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies focused on complement-mitochondrial regulation .
