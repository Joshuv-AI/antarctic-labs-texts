Activation of nuclear receptor pregnane-X-receptor protects against abdominal aortic aneurysm by inhibiting oxidative stress
Authors: Zhi Shen, Jinxi Wang, Yifei Chen, Peiliang Fang, Ancai Yuan, Alex F. Chen, Xiaoxiang Yan, Yuyan Lyu, Jun Pu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is a life-threatening condition, but effective medications to prevent its progression and rupture are currently lacking. The nuclear receptor pregnane-X-receptor (PXR) plays a crucial role in vascular homeostasis. However, the role of PXR in AAA development remains unknown. We first detected the PXR expression in human and murine AAA tissues by RT-qPCR and Western blot. To investigate the potential role of PXR in the development of AAA, we used adeno-associated virus-mediated overexpression of PXR and pharmacological activation of PXR by ginkgolide A (GA) in mouse AAA models induced by both angiotensin II (AngII) and calcium phosphate [Ca3(PO4)2]. The underlying mechanism was further explored using RNA-sequencing and molecular biological analyses. We found a significant decrease in both mRNA and protein levels of PXR in both human and murine aortic smooth muscle cells from AAA tissues, accompanied with phenotypic switching of vascular smooth muscle cell and increased oxidative stress. PXR overexpression in abdominal aortas and GA treatment successfully suppressed AAA formation in both mouse AAA models. RNA-sequencing data revealed that PXR activation inhibited gamma-aminobutyric acid type A receptor subunit alpha3 (GABRA3) expression. Additional mechanistic studies identified that PXR suppressed AAA through mitigating GABRA3-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and subsequent phosphorylation of c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK). Interestingly, p-JNK was found to induce ubiquitin-proteasome degradation of PXR. In summary, our data unveiled, for the first time, the protective role of PXR against AAA pathogenesis by inhibiting oxidative stress. These findings suggested PXR as a promising therapeutic target for AAA.

Introduction

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA), characterized by the localized expansion of the abdominal aorta [1], is a fatal event associated with a mortality rate as high as 50 % [2]. The majority of AAAs remain asymptomatic until they rupture, emphasizing the significance of early intervention. Currently, surgical procedures (vascular replacement and endovascular repair) are the only interventions advocated for large or symptomatic aneurysms. Thus, there is a pressing need for cost-effective therapeutic approaches, particularly during the initial stages of AAA [3]. A deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying AAA's initiation and progression is imperative to identify potential therapeutic targets.

Nuclear receptors (NRs) are ligand-activated transcriptional modulators and play pivotal roles in vascular physiology and pathophysiology, offering promising avenues for drug development, especially in cardiovascular diseases [4]. The nuclear pregnane-X-receptor (PXR, nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group I member 2), has been implicated in diverse pathophysiological processes, for instance, apoptosis, inflammation, and oxidative stress [5]. Numerous studies have reported that PXR exerts its effects by modulating downstream gene expression through direct or indirect interactions with transcriptional factors [6], thereby influencing various aspects of vascular function, such as pregnancy-related vascular adaptations and neointimal development [[7], [8], [9]]. However, there is no data available on its therapeutic potential in AAA management. This research endeavors to bridge this gap by examining the impact of PXR and its agonist Ginkgolide A (GA) on the onset and progression of AAA, aiming to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and contribute to the development of novel therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Human studies

Samples of human AAA and adjacent nonaneurysmal aortic segments (non-AAA) tissue were obtained from patients (n = 6) with surgical repair from December 1, 2018, to March 1, 2020. All recruited patients met the criteria for elective repair, with a dilated diameter of more than 55 mm [10]. The human study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013), and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Renji Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. Informed consents were collected from all recruited patients.

Animal studies

All animal experiments complied with the National Institutes of Health Guidelines on the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Institutes of Health Publication, 8th edition, 2011), and approval was obtained from the Animal Ethics Committee of Renji Hospital, Affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. Angiotensin II (AngII)- or calcium phosphate [Ca3(PO4)2]-induced AAA models were established as previously reported [4]. The mice were housed in an environment with a 12h-light/12h-dark cycle, a temperature of 24 ± 2 °C and the humidity maintained at 40 ± 5 %, with free access to diet and water. To realize the genetic intervention to the smooth muscle cells (SMCs) in murine abdominal aortas, adeno-associated virus AAV-2/9 vectors were recombined with enzyme cutting method to carry SMC-specific smooth muscle protein 22-alpha (SM22α) promoter [11]. Both an AAV2/9 encoding mouse PXR (AAV-Pxr) and its control virus (AAV-Con) were constructed. A single exposure of 5 × 108 plaque forming units (pfu) of AAV was mixed with hydrogel pluronic F-127 and perivascularly applied to the abdominal aortas of 8-12-week-old mice [12].

To examine the effects of pharmacological activation of PXR on AAA formation, PXR agonist GA was used. GA (100 mg/kg body weight, BN-52020; MedChemExpress LLC, USA) or dissolved vehicle (Vehicle) was administrated intragastrically to mice once a day [13], along with the establishment of AAA models.

Establishment of an AngII-induced mouse models for AAA

Male 8 to 12-week-old ApoE−/− mice were subcutaneously implanted with a mini-osmotic pump (Alzet model 2004, 28-day delivery; Durect Corporation, USA) loaded with AngII (1000 ng/kg/min, A9525; Sigma, MO, USA) or saline (0.9 % NaCl) for 28 days with a high-fat diet. At 28 days after mini-pump implantation, mice were sacrificed to validate AAA formation, and the maximum diameters of external abdominal aortas were measured using a Digital Vernier Caliper. The abdominal aorta was considered as aneurysm if the diameter increased ≥50 % compared with the saline group, as previously reported [4]. If any mouse died prior to the study endpoint, it would be subjected to autopsy. The presence of egress with blood clots outside the adventitia of the dilated aortic wall was determined as aneurysm rupture.

Establishment of a Ca3(PO4)2-induced AAA model

Under general anesthesia, infrarenal abdominal aortas of male 8 to 12-week-old C57BL/6J mice were isolated and incubated with 0.5 mol/L calcium chloride (10 min), followed by phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (5 min) to form Ca3(PO4)2. Before suturing, the abdominal cavities were washed with saline (0.9 % NaCl). At 28 days after surgery, the infrarenal abdominal aortas were collected for the quantification of the maximal diameters by a Digital Vernier Caliper.

Isolation, culture, and treatment of cells

As previously described [4], mouse aortic smooth muscle cells (MASMCs) were isolated from the mouse abdominal aortas. Briefly, mouse abdominal aortic tissues were isolated and rinsed in cold PBS. After the removal of the endothelium and adventitia, the tissues were cut into 1–2 mm pieces, and treated with 0.25 % collagenase type II (B204; Worthington, USA) and 0.5 % elastase type II (LS002279; Worthington, USA) at 37 °C for 1 h. MASMCs were cultured in Dulbecco’ s modified Eagle's medium (DMEM)/F12 supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin and identified by immunofluorescence staining of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA).

Human aortic smooth muscle cells (HASMC) (#6110, ScienCell, USA) were cultured in smooth muscle cell medium supplemented with 2 % fetal bovine serum, 1 % smooth muscle cell growth supplement and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (#1101, ScienCell, USA). Human aortic endothelial cells (HAEC) (#6100, ScienCell, USA) were cultured in endothelial cell medium supplemented with 5 % fetal bovine serum, 1 % endothelial cell growth supplement and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (#1001, ScienCell, USA). THP-1 (M-C1190; Mcellbank, China) were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin. The cells were treated with 100 nM AngII [4] or 50 μM GA [14] for 24 h in corresponding groups.

In vivo micro-ultrasound imaging

To examine the morphological features of abdominal aortas in vivo, the Micro-Ultrasound Imaging (MUI) was performed using a Vevo2100 High-Resolution In Vivo Micro-ultrasound Imaging System (VisualSonics Inc., Ontario, Canada) for mice. In accordance with the manufacturer's instructions, MUI images were acquired using the B mode and Color Doppler Mode. Three measurements of the maximal diameters of internal abdominal aortas on both short axis and long axis were performed by a blinded investigator.

Histological analysis

Abdominal aortas isolated from mice were perfused with saline and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde. Then, the aortas were embedded in paraffin, followed by serial sectioning. Paraffin-embedded sections (3–4 μm thick) were deparaffinized and prepared for hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining to observe general morphology, Elastin van Gieson (EVG) staining to assess elastin degradation, and Masson's Trichrome staining to determine collagen content. In EVG staining, the degree of elastin degradation was graded with a semiquantitative method according to the 4-grade scale proposed by Satoh et al. [15]: Grade 1: No Degradation; Grade 2: Mild Degradation; Grade 3: Severe Degradation; Grade 4: Aortic Rupture. Fold changes of collagen content were calculated using Masson's Trichrome staining by ImageJ.

Molecular docking

The three-dimensional crystal structure of human and murine PXR was downloaded from RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB, https://www.rcsb.org) for docking research. The chemical structure of GA was extracted from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). Finally, GA was docked into PXR with a web tool, CB-Dock2 [16] and the PyMOL Molecular Graphics System version 2.5.8.

Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry staining

As previously described [4], paraffin-embedded sections (3–4 μm thick) were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Then, antigen retrieval was performed with 10 mM sodium citrate buffer. For the immunofluorescence staining, the sections were incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies against α-SMA (1:200, ab7817; Abcam, USA), PXR (1:100, 67912-1-Ig; Proteintech, USA), gamma-aminobutyric acid type A receptor subunit alpha3 (GABRA3) (1:200, 12708-1-AP; Proteintech, USA), and phosphorylated c-Jun N-terminal kinase (p-JNK) (1:200, 12708-1-AP; Proteintech, USA). Subsequently, the sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-/555-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:200, Invitrogen, USA) at 37 °C in the dark for 1 h, and then mounted with ProLong Gold anti-fade reagent with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindol (DAPI) (P36931; Invitrogen, USA) for confocal microscopy (Olympus, FV 3000; Japan). For the immunohistochemistry staining, sections (3–4 μm thick) were incubated with primary antibodies against matrix metalloprotein-2 (MMP-2) (1:200, ab86607; Abcam, USA) at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with the corresponding biotinconjugated secondary antibodies. An Avidin-Biotin Complex peroxidase system was used to detect the staining signals. The visualization of positive antibody binding was realized using a 3,3′- diaminobenzidine (DAB) peroxidase substrate kit (SK4100; Vectorlabs, USA). Nuclei were stained with hematoxylin. The bright-field images were captured by a slide scanner (NanoZoomer S360 Digital slide scanner C13220-01; Hamamatsu, Japan).

Dihydroethidium (DHE) staining

To determine the superoxide generation, we used in situ dihydroethidium (DHE) staining (S0063; Beyotime, China). For in vivo detection, the abdominal aortas of ApoE−/− mice after infused with AngII or Saline for 28 days were extracted and snap-frozen into optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (4583; SAKURA Tissue-TEK, USA). Cryosections (5 μm thick) were incubated with DHE staining (red). A fluorescence microscope was used to capture images. For in vitro detection, HASMCs were incubated with DHE (10 μM) at 37 °C for 30 min. After being washed twice with PBS (5 min each time), the chemiluminescence intensity was detected by a MultiSkan FC reader (ThermoFisher, USA).

Lucigenin-enhanced chemiluminescence

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were measured by lucigenin-enhanced chemiluminescence. HASMCs were incubated with lucigenin (10 μM, HY-D0720, MedChemExpress LLC, USA) at room temperature for 15 min. After being washed twice with PBS (5 min each time), the chemiluminescence intensity was detected by a MultiSkan FC reader (ThermoFisher, USA).

Assay of MMP activity

Cryosections of fresh abdominal aortas (8–10 μm thick) were used for MMP activity assays by in situ zymography. In brief, cryosections were incubated with a fluorogenic gelatin substrate (DQ gelatin) (E−12055; Molecular Probes, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocols. MMP activity was shown as green fluorescence. SMCs were co-stained with an anti-α-SMA antibody and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI.

MMP activity was also assessed by gelatin zymography, as previously described [4]. Proteins in vascular smooth muscle cells or aorta tissue homogenates were electrophoresed in SDS-PAGE gels containing 0.8 mg/ml gelatin. After being washed in 2.5 % Triton X-100, the gels were incubated in zymography buffer [50 mmol/L Tris (pH 8.0), 10 mmol/L CaCl2, and 0.05 % Brij 35] for 48 h (37 °C), followed by Coomassie brilliant blue staining.

Assessment of cell apoptosis

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay was performed on paraffin-embedded sections (3–4 μm thick) to estimate cell apoptosis in abdominal aortas by an in situ Cell Death Detection Kit (11684817910; Roche, Germany). SMCs were stained by an anti-α-SMA antibody and nuclei were stained by DAPI. After visualized by a confocal microscope, the ratio of TUNEL-positive nuclei to total nuclei was calculated.

Co-immunoprecipitation

After PBS wash, cells were lysed with NP40 Cell Lysis Buffer and incubated on ice for 30 min. Then, the cell lysate was centrifuged at 12,000×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies against PXR (1:100, 67912-1-Ig; Proteintech, USA), p-JNK (1:100, 12708-1-AP; Proteintech, USA) and Normal Rabbit IgG (1:100, 2729; CST, USA). Next, protein G magnetic beads (88847; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were added to the mixture and incubated at 4 °C for another 2 h. Finally, the magnetic beads were collected to obtain precipitated proteins.

For the co-immunoprecipitation of ubiquitinated PXR, HASMCs were treated with proteasome inhibitor MG-132 (5 μM) (HY-13259; MedChemExpress LLC, USA) for 24 h. Next, the nucleus and cytoplasm proteins were extracted using Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (P0027; Beyotime, China) and incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibody against p-JNK and Normal Rabbit IgG. Then, protein G magnetic beads were added to the mixture and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h. Finally, the magnetic beads were collected to obtain precipitated proteins.

Protein extraction and Western blot

Proteins were extracted from cells and abdominal aortas. The separation of proteins was performed on 8%–12.5 % SDS-PAGE gels and the proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The membranes were blocked with protein-free rapid blocking buffer (PS108P; Epizyme, China) and incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies against PXR (1:1000, 67912-1-Ig; Proteintech, USA), GADPH (1:2000, 2118; Cell Signaling Technology, USA), β-Actin (1:1000, 4970; Cell Signaling Technology, USA), MYH11 (1:1000, 21404-1-AP; Proteintech, USA), α-SMA (1:1000, ab7817; Abcam, USA), Caspase-3 (1:1000, AF1261; Beyotime, China), Cleaved Caspase-3 (1:1000, AC033; Beyotime, China), Bcl2 (1:2000, 68103-1-Ig; Proteintech, USA), Bax (1:2000, 50599-2-Ig; Proteintech, USA), GABRA3 (1:2000, 12708-1-AP; Proteintech, USA), GABBR2 (1:2000, 27567-1-AP; Proteintech, USA), GABRG2 (1:2000, 14104-1-AP; Proteintech, USA), p-JNK (1:2000, 12708-1-AP; Proteintech, USA), JNK (1:1000, AF1048; Beyotime, China), ubiquitin (80992-1-RR; Proteintech, USA), NOX1 (1:2000, 17772-1-AP; Proteintech, USA), NOX2 (1:2000, 19013-1-AP; Proteintech, USA), NOX4 (1:2000, 14347-1-AP; Proteintech, USA), SOD1 (1:2000, 10269-1-AP; Proteintech, USA), SOD2 (1:2000, 24127-1-AP; Proteintech, USA). After the incubation with corresponding secondary antibodies (1:10000, Jackson Immuno Research Laboratories, USA), enhanced chemiluminescence (Share-bio, China) was used to show the protein bands with the ImageQuant LAS 4000 mini detection system (General Electric Co., USA).

RNA isolation, real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) and RNA-sequencing

TRIzol was used to isolate total RNA which is then used for cDNA synthesis with a PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Japan). The mRNA expression was detected by Real-time quantitative PCR with SYBR Premix Ex Taq™ (Takara, Japan) using a LightCycler® 480 Realtime PCR System (Roche Applied Science). Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as a housekeeping gene. Primer information is shown in Table S1.

For RNA-sequencing, after the establishment of AngII-induced AAA model for 28 days, the suprarenal abdominal aortas of mice were extracted rapidly in the condition of ice bath and washed with DEPC-treated water. Next, the tissues were quickly frozen with liquid nitrogen for isolation of total RNA and generation of a cDNA library. Then, RNA-sequencing was conducted by a sequencing service contractor (HaploX, Shenzhen, China). Differentially expressed genes were defined as adjusted (Benjamini and Hochberg) p < 0.05 and |log2 (fold change)|> 1.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs)

Abdominal aortas from AAA models (20 mg) were homogenized with 180 μm PBS and centrifuged (4 °C, 15,000×g, 15 min). The supernatants were used to determine the levels of IL-6 and TNF-α with ELISA Kit (88-7064-88 and 88-7324-88; Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. At 450 nm, the absorbance was read by a MultiSkan FC reader (ThermoFisher, USA).

RNA interference, plasmid construction and adeno-associated virus packaging

The siRNAs targeting human GABRA3 and PXR were designed and synthesized by Genomeditech Co. Ltd (China) (Table S1). Transfection of siRNAs was performed with lipofectamine 3000 reagent (L3000015; Thermo-Fisher, USA). The highest interfering efficiency was validated by RT-qPCR and Western blot (Figs. S1 and S2).

In addition to AAV-Pxr, to validate the effects of GABRA3 and JNK on AAA formation, AAV2/9s carrying an SMC-specific SM22α promoter separately encoding shRNA targeting Gabra3 (AAV-shGabra3, Table S1) and dominant-negative Jnk (AAV-dominant-negative Jnk) were applied to the murine abdominal aortas. The shRNAs targeting murine Gabra3 were designed and synthesized by Genechem Co. Ltd (China) (Table S1). The dominant-negative Jnk was prepared by substitution of the base sequence of two phosphorylation sites of JNK (Thr183 and lyr185) with Ala and Phe, as previously reported [17].

NADPH oxidase activity measurement

The NADPH oxidase activity of HASMCs was measured with a kit (ml092872; Enzyme-linked Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance was read at 340 nm by a MultiSkan FC reader (ThermoFisher, USA).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are shown as mean ± SD, and categorical variables are presented as number (percentage). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and the equality of variances was determined using the Levene test. For two-group comparisons, the Student's t-test (equal variances) or unequal variance t-test (unequal variances) was applied for normally distributed variables, otherwise the Mann-Whitney U test was performed. In comparisons involving more than two groups, a 2-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc analysis was employed. The Chi-square test or the Fisher's exact test was used for the bivariate comparisons of categorical variables. Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS software version 25.0 and GraphPad Prism 8. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

PXR is down-regulated in human and murine AAA tissues

To investigate whether PXR is involved in the pathogenesis of AAA, we first detected the expression of PXR in human AAA tissues. The mRNA and protein levels of PXR in AAA tissues were both down-regulated compared with non-AAA tissues (Fig. 1A–D). Moreover, both Pxr mRNA and PXR protein expression of the abdominal aortas were significantly reduced in AngII-infused mice (Fig. 1E–H) and Ca3(PO4)2-treated mice (Fig. 1I–L) compared with the control mice. Through immunofluorescence staining, the decrease of PXR protein predominantly happened in the vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs). Consistent with previous studies [18,19], decreased protein levels of contractile VSMC markers (α-SMA and MYH-11) and increased protein levels of oxidative stress markers (NOX1 and NOX2) were observed in human and murine AAA tissues (Fig. 1B and C, 1F & 1G, 1J&1K). In in vitro study, the reduction of Pxr mRNA expression and PXR protein expression was determined in mouse aortic smooth muscle cells (MASMCs) after AngII treatment (Fig. 1M–P). The protein expression of contractile VSMC markers and oxidative stress markers showed similar changes to those observed in vivo (Fig. 1N and O). PXR overexpression represses AAA formation

To investigate the role of PXR in AAA formation, hydrogel containing AAV-Pxr was applied to the abdominal aortas of 8 to 12-week-old mice [12] (Fig. S3). And the efficiency and specificity of AAVs were validated by RT-qPCR and Western blot of abdominal aortas at the endpoints of all experiments (Fig. 2A). For the AngII-infused AAA model, lower AAA incidence and rupture were observed in the PXR overexpression group compared with the control group (Fig. 2B–C). AAV-Pxr treatment significantly decreased the maximal external and internal diameters of the suprarenal abdominal aortas (Fig. 2D–G). Additionally, histological analysis showed that PXR overexpression mitigated arterial wall thickening, elastic fiber degradation and collagen deposition in AngII-induced murine abdominal aortas (Fig. 2H–J). For the Ca3(PO4)2-induced AAA model, compared with the controls, the mice with AAV-Pxr administration had significantly lower incidence of AAA and smaller aortic diameters (Fig. 2K–L), as well as slighter arterial wall thickening, elastic fiber degradation and collagen deposition (Fig. 2M–O). Pharmacological activation of PXR suppresses AAA formation

Of clinical relevance, we further investigated the effects of pharmacological activation of PXR on AAA formation in both AngII-induced and Ca3(PO4)2-induced AAA mouse models. Along with the establishment of AAA models, 100 μL GA solution (100 mg/kg body weight) [13] or dissolved vehicle was administrated intragastrically to each mouse once a day for 28 days (Fig. S4). GA was identified as the PXR agonist in previous studies [13]. Based on the stable docking between GA and PXR, GA was shown to be able to activate PXR both in vivo and in vitro [13,20]. Consistently, we found GA docked with both human-derived and mouse-derived PXR (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, GA administration up-regulated the mRNA and protein expression of PXR in the abdominal aortas of mice, as shown by RT-qPCR, Western blot and immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 3B–C). In the AngII-induced AAA model, GA treatment significantly attenuated AAA rupture and incidence in comparison with Vehicle treatment (Fig. 3D–E). Moreover, decreased maximal external and internal diameters of suprarenal abdominal aortas were observed after GA treatment compared with Vehicle treatment (Fig. 3F–I). Histological analysis also showed that GA suppressed elastic fiber degradation and collagen deposition in AngII-infused mouse aortas (Fig. 3J–L). Consistent with the results of AngII-induced AAA model, GA administration significantly reduced Ca3(PO4)2-induced maximal diameter of infrarenal abdominal aortas (Fig. 3M–N). Additionally, GA inhibited elastic fiber degradation and collagen deposition induced by Ca3(PO4)2 treatment (Fig. 3O–Q). GABRA3 is the downstream target of PXR activation

To investigate the underlying mechanisms linking the activation of PXR to the suppression of AAA, RNA-sequencing of suprarenal abdominal aortas from AngII-infused mice with/without the treatment of PXR agonist-GA was conducted. After the establishment of AngII-induced AAA model for 28 days, the suprarenal abdominal aortas of mice were extracted rapidly in the condition of ice bath and washed with DEPC-treated water. Next, the tissues were quickly frozen with liquid nitrogen for RNA-sequencing. With GA treatment, the differentially expressed genes were predominantly enriched in gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-ergic synapse pathway (Fig. 4A–D). Next, we analyzed the reads counts of all subunits comprising GABA receptors in suprarenal abdominal aortas, and identified three differentially expressed subunits (Gabbr2, Gabrg2, and Gabra3), which were selected for further investigation (Fig. 4E and Fig. S6A). Subsequently, human-derived smooth muscle cells (HASMCs), endothelial cells (HAECs) and macrophages (THP-1) were used to investigate the impact of GA on the three GABA receptor subunits in vitro. Consistent with the RNA-sequencing data, we observed a reduction of the protein expression of GABRA3 in HASMCs and GABBR2/GABRG2 in HAECs (Fig. 4F). Based on the reads count values and protein expression, GABRA3 in HASMCs was ultimately chosen for further analysis. The RT-qPCR showed that GA administration reversed the decrease of Gabra3 mRNA level induced by AngII in HASMCs (Fig. S5B). GA treatment inhibited AngII-induced ROS production in HASMCs, but the protective effects were nullified by PXR knockdown using siRNA (Fig. S6A). Metabolism enzymes of ROS and mitochondria are important regulators of the level of ROS [23,24]. NADPH oxidases catalyze the generation of ROS, while superoxide dismutases catalyze its degradation [25]. Additionally, the dysfunction of mitochondria is associated with the accumulation of ROS [24]. To clarify the mechanism by which GABRA3 regulated the level of ROS in HASMCs, we measured the levels of metabolism enzymes related to ROS generation or decomposition as well as mitochondria function. First, we found that the treatment of siGABRA3 reversed the increase of NOX activity in HASMCs induced by AngII (Fig. 5E). Moreover, after knockdown of GABRA3, there was a decrease in the level of NADPH oxidases (NOX1, NOX2 and NOX4) and an increase in the level of superoxide dismutases (SOD1 and SOD2) in AngII-treated HASMCs (Fig. 5F). In addition, siGABRA3 treatment reversed the reduced oxygen consumption rate of HASMCs caused by AngII (Fig. 5G). These findings suggested that GABRA3 participated in the metabolism of ROS through multiple pathways.

Oxidative stress can affect the phosphorylation of JNK [26], and then promote AAA formation [27]. In the present study, the overexpression of PXR and knockdown of GABRA3 decreased the level of p-JNK (Fig. 5B and H). It was reported that the activation of JNK pathways is associated with VSMC survival, MMP production and inflammatory cell infiltration during the formation of AAA [27]. Similarly, we found that AAV-Pxr treatment was accompanied with decreased apoptotic protein expression and cell apoptosis in AngII-infused suprarenal abdominal aortas via Western blot (Fig. S7A) and TUNEL assay (Fig. S7B). Additionally, MMP activity decreased in the aortas from AAV2-Pxr-treated AAA mouse model (Fig. S7C), as well as MMP-2 expression and activity (Figs. S7D and S7E). The overexpression of PXR also decreased TNF-α and IL-6 levels in the aortas of AngII-induced AAA mouse model (Figs. S7F and S7G).

To validate the protective role of PXR mediated by GABRA3/p-JNK pathway during AAA formation, the AAV-shGabra3 (Fig. S8) or AAV-dominant-negative Jnk were constructed and applied to murine abdominal aortas. Consistently, in vivo experiments showed lower AAA incidence, lower rupture incidence, decreased maximal external and internal diameters of the suprarenal abdominal aortas in AAV-shGabra3 and AAV-dominant-negative Jnk groups compared with AAV-shCon/AAV-Con groups (Figs. S9–S10). In summary, PXR overexpression inhibited apoptosis, MMP activation, and inflammation in the formation of AAA through inhibiting the GABRA3/ROS/p-JNK pathway.

P-JNK induces ubiquitination and degradation of PXR

Apart from increasing PXR expression, we further explored whether GA could increase the level of PXR via suppression of protein degradation. Previous studies reported that PXR could interact with p-JNK [28]. As expected, co-immunoprecipitation results demonstrated the interaction of p-JNK and PXR in AngII-treated HASMCs (Fig. 6A). Then, in vitro and in vivo studies verified that GA treatment not only inhibited the interaction of PXR and p-JNK, but also reversed the decrease of PXR and increase of p-JNK after AngII treatment (Fig. 6B–C). Next, we inferred that the interaction between PXR and p-JNK promoted PXR degradation. It has been reported that protein degradation through the ubiquitin-proteasome system occurred in both the nucleus and cytoplasm [29]. In the present study, to exam whether the degeneration of PXR was through p-JNK-induced ubiquitination, AngII-treated HASMCs were treated with proteasome inhibitor MG-132 and the subsequent immunoprecipitation showed that poly-ubiquitinated PXR was precipitated from both the nucleus and the cytoplasm of AngII-treated HASMCs with the antibody against p-JNK (Fig. 6D). The administration of proteasome inhibitor MG-132 rescued the level of PXR protein in HASMCs (Fig. 6E). Besides, the suppression of JNK phosphorylation by an inhibitor (SP600125) has a consistent effect on PXR expression in HASMCs (Fig. 6F). Discussion

In this study, we identified a novel role of nuclear receptor PXR in the pathogenesis of AAA. We detected a functional expression of PXR in vascular smooth muscle cells and it was down-regulated in both human and murine AAA tissues. Overexpression of PXR in VSMCs of abdominal aortas repressed AAA formation and development in both AngII- and Ca3(PO4)2-induced AAA mouse models. Further, pharmacological activation of PXR by the specific agonist-GA, consistently showed an anti-AAA effect in mouse models. Lastly, our mechanistic investigations found that PXR suppresses AAA through inhibiting GABRA3/ROS/p-JNK pathway and p-JNK can in turn induce the ubiquitin-proteasome degradation of PXR (Fig. 8). Nuclear receptors, as transcription factors, are known to regulate gene expression in various physiological and pathological processes including cardiovascular diseases [4], but among the nuclear receptors discovered in humans, the role of majority of them in AAA remains unknown. NRs represent one of the most important drug targets in terms of potential therapeutic application [4]. The agonists or antagonists of nuclear receptors account for approximately 13 % of all FDA-approved drugs [30]. Thus, the characterization of NR pathways in the pathological processes of AAA would supply promising targets for pharmacological intervention of AAA. In our recent studies, three NRs, NR1D1, NR4A1, and NR1H3 have been identified as regulators contributing to AAA pathogenesis [4,31,32]. The current study further provides evidence that PXR, a significantly down-regulated NR, acts as a negative regulator in the pathogenesis of AAA.

PXR, a pivotal member of the NR subfamily, stands as a vital regulator of drug metabolism by orchestrating the expression of a broad array of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters [33]. Moreover, it has long been implicated in the development of cancer, inflammatory disorders, and metabolic diseases, underscoring its multifaceted influence on health [34,35]. Importantly, PXR has been indicated to be expressed in vascular cells, playing important roles in vascular physiology/pathology [[7], [8], [9]]. In the present study, we uncovered a strong inhibitory effect of PXR on the formation and progression of AAA, presenting the potential therapeutic value of PXR in AAA.

GA, as a member of ginkgolide family, is a selective natural agonist of PXR and a promising lead compound toward the development of new PXR-regulating modulators, eliciting numerous favorable biological effects (i.e., anti-inflammation, anti-oxidant, and anti-apoptosis [36,37]). Furthermore, GA has been shown to inhibit MMPs activation, which is involved in extracellular matrix degradation [36]. A recent study found that GA alleviated cardiac remodeling in mice with myocardial infarction, through suppressing inflammation in cardiac fibroblasts and cardiomyocytes [36]. Moreover, GA protected heart against septic cardiomyopathy by attenuating inflammation, oxidative stress and apoptosis in cardiomyopathy [38]. In addition, GA has been found to attenuate homocysteine-induced endothelial dysfunction in porcine coronary arteries in vitro [39]. Despite these promising findings, the exact mechanisms underpinning GA's effects on vascular function remain largely unexplored. Of clinical relevance, we investigated the effects of pharmacological activation of PXR by GA on AAA formation. We found that activation of PXR by GA protected against AAA through inhibition of ROS. Previous studies have indicated that AAA was closely related to oxidative stress [[40], [41], [42]], and that antioxidant was a potential AAA therapeutic strategy. For instance, the intake of antioxidant vitamin or fruits rich in antioxidants was beneficial in the management of AAA [43,44]. In addition, drugs, like NOX inhibitors, were identified to provide protective effects on AAA development [45]. In line with these findings [[40], [41], [42], [43], [44], [45]], our study revealed that PXR influences AAA by regulating ROS through the repression of GABRA3, offering novel insights into antioxidant-based strategies for combating AAA.

To explore the mechanism in the up-regulation of PXR induced by GA, we treated HASMCs with a proteasome inhibitor and found that the interaction of p-JNK and PXR promoted the ubiquitination of PXR, which would be degraded by proteasome. Although the regulation of PXR levels by ubiquitination-proteasome degradation pathway has been reported in cancer cells [46], we identified a novel regulation of p-JNK on the ubiquitination of PXR, and revealed for the first time the ubiquitination-proteasome degradation of PXR in VSMCs. Similar to our results, Win et al. found the direct interaction with active JNK would promote ubiquitination and degradation of glutamat-cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (GCLC) in liver cells [47]. Conversely, Lee et al. found that inhibition of JNK phosphorylation enhanced the nucleoplasm translocation and ubiquitination of GLTSCR2 in glioblastoma [48]. These pleiotropic effects of p-JNK on ubiquitination highlight the complexity of its role, which may be contingent upon cell types or target protein specificity. Thus, our study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of the intricate interplay between p-JNK and ubiquitination processes.

Limitations of the study

The study is constrained by several limitations that underscore the complexities of unraveling the AAA phenomenon. First, our reliance on two AAA mouse models [subcutaneous AngII infusion and adventitial Ca3(PO4)2 incubation], while insightful, inherently falls short of comprehensively mirroring the intricate, multifaceted mechanisms underpinning AAA formation in human subjects. This limitation stresses the imperative for a more holistic approach that integrates diverse models to bridge the gap between preclinical findings and clinical practices. Second, we used in vivo SMC-specific overexpression of PXR by recombinant adeno-associated virus vector with SM22α promoter to regulate the expression of PXR in murine abdominal aortas. However, PXR transgenic mouse models could provide a better verification of our findings. Lastly, our findings shed light on GA supplementation as a promising therapeutic avenue for AAA. More clinical research is warranted to confirm its benefit on AAA observed in this study.

Clinical implications of this study

The discovery of PXR as a novel functional receptor involved in regulating ROS in vascular tissues has the potential to enhance our understanding of PXR's diverse biological roles. Our observations, coupled with recent discoveries regarding PXR's pivotal role in cardiovascular tissues, collectively underscore its status as a crucial regulator of cardiovascular biology. Notably, our study highlights the therapeutic potential of GA supplementation in preventing AAA. GA, a natural compound derived from the ginkgo biloba plant and readily available as a dietary supplement, serves as the basis for this promising therapeutic approach. Consequently, these findings point to targeting PXR as a potential therapeutic strategy aimed at mitigating oxidative stress and halting the pathological progression of AAA.

Conclusions

Our study provides novel evidence for the pivotal role of PXR in the pathogenesis of AAA. Activation of PXR was observed to mitigate AAA progression by inhibiting GABRA3-mediated ROS production and subsequent JNK phosphorylation. Furthermore, upregulation of PXR expression by GA treatment was mediated by suppression of p-JNK-induced ubiquitin-proteasome degradation. These discoveries suggest that PXR serves as a promising therapeutic target for AAA by inhibiting oxidative stress.
