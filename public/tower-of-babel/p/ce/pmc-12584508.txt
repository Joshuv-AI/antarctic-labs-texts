Endogenous treatments for migraine pain
Authors: Adriana Della Pietra, Andrew F. Russo
Published: 2025

This thematic issue, “Endogenous Treatments for Migraine Pain”, describes molecular mechanisms underlying endogenous pain regulation and how leverage of these natural pain-relief systems may counteract headache symptoms. These endogenous mechanisms include neuropeptides and neurotransmitters that naturally suppress pain signals through inhibitory neuronal networks. Enhancing these pathways could offer alternative or complementary therapeutic strategies for existing pharmacological treatments for migraine.

How do we distinguish between endogenous and exogenous treatments? A treatment designed to amplify the intrinsic elevation of endocannabinoid levels that naturally rise during a migraine attack would be considered an “endogenous treatment”. In contrast, “exogenous treatments” work by introducing an external agent that acts independently of the body’s intrinsic response. For example, drugs that inhibit calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) activity are a process that does not naturally occur as a headache-counteracting mechanism. As such, the papers in this issue align with the research priorities outlined by the American Headache Society, addressing novel diagnostic, testing both human and animal models, laying the basis for future alternative therapeutic approaches, and deepening our understanding of the pathophysiology of migraine [1].

An attractive strategy to harness endogenous pathways is to directly enhance the anti-nociceptive effects of the endocannabinoid system. Such endocannabinoid-related strategies are covered in papers from Mangutov et al., Krivoshein et al., and Brugia et al. [2–4]. Alternatively, other authors focus on exploiting the cross-talk between the endocannabinoid system and the adrenergic receptors [5], TRPV1 receptors [6], and dopaminergic system [7].

Moving beyond endocannabinoids, several papers explore alternative mechanisms and targets for migraine treatment. Jang et al. focus on modulating intrinsic neuronal excitability via endogenous pregnenolone sulfate [8], while Alpay et al., discuss inhibition of excitability by enhancing the GABAergic system to reduce allodynia and photophobia [9]. Finally, looking towards the future, Lou et al., describe four migraine-associated loci, examining the link between gene expression, protein levels, and migraine pathophysiology [10].

While CGRP/CGRP receptor monoclonal antibodies and gepants target CGRP or CGRP receptors directly, endogenous descending pathways can also reduce migraine pain, possibly through modulation of CGRP signaling as well [11–13]. Therefore, studying the sites of CGRP actions and roles is crucial, as Rees et al. show that amylin and CGRP interact with receptors in the upper cervical dorsal root ganglia [14]. Dux et al. investigated the interconnection between CGRP and substance P (SP) release in the cranial dura mater of rodents after nitroglycerin treatment. They found that nitroglycerin induces high CGRP release but low SP levels, questioning the role of SP and the relevance of plasma extravasation in migraine [15].

Finally, migraine is a complex condition influenced by many endogenous molecular mechanisms and comorbidities. Salinas-Abarca et al. propose that targeting endogenous hypothalamic mechanisms, which regulate the release of multiple neurotransmitters influencing nociception and modulate sex hormone–dependent migraine signaling, could lead to the development of personalized migraine treatments [16]. Neuroimaging studies are likely to reveal insights to how existing therapies such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs may allow better prediction of individual responses as described by Wei et al. [17]. Another line of research described by Han et al. is the connection between circadian rhythms endogenously regulating migraine related pathways and serotonin receptor activation [18].

In summary, this thematic issue on “Endogenous Treatments for Migraine Pain” highlights intrinsic pathways and targets for developing alternative treatments for migraine.
