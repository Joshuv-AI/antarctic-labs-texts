ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveWe aimed to develop a program director–derived model of essential components of child neurology residency training.MethodsAll 79 child neurology residency programs in the United States were invited to submit a block diagram with 48 months of required rotations, the minimum clinical requirement across all approved pathways. These block diagrams were then analyzed for consensus. Program directors were anonymously surveyed regarding whether a child neurology resident could be adequately trained using the consensus curriculum if implemented in either a 4‐ or a 5‐year training program, and whether 4 years of residency could provide adequate training.ResultsFifty of 79 residency programs (63%) submitted a block diagram (54% in pediatrics departments, 46% in neurology departments). Greater than 75% of program directors recommended the following rotations with the average number of months recommended across all program directors in parentheses: pediatric inpatient/hospital medicine (3), pediatric non‐consult intensive care (3), healthy newborn (0.5), pediatric acute/emergency care (1), genetics (1), child development (1), child neurology inpatient/consults (8), child neurology general outpatient (4), child psychiatry (1), adult neurology inpatient/consults (3), neurology specialties outpatient (4), electroencephalography (2), neuroradiology (1), and electives (7). Of the program directors (53 of 79, 67%) who completed the post‐survey, 87% agreed that these requirements would be adequate, and 89% agreed that child neurologists could be adequately trained for independent practice within 4 years.InterpretationThe program director consensus supports modification of existing child neurology training requirements, with general agreement that 4 years of clinical training would be adequate.

Introduction

The continued advancement of the field of child neurology—and the care of children with neurological disorders—depends on our ability to train child neurologists for the future. Although the field of child neurology began its infancy in the 1950s–1960s [1], it was only in the 2016–2017 academic year when it was first recognized as an independent specialty as opposed to a subspecialty of neurology by the Accreditation Council of Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) [2]. In that respect, the field may still be emerging from its adolescent state, not completely independent from the parent fields of pediatrics and neurology. With another recent growth spurt, most notably the rapid developments in neurogenetics and precision medicine, along with certification of new child neurology–specific fellowships such as neonatal neurocritical care [3], the practice of child neurology continues to diverge from pediatrics and adult neurology [1]. However, the standard child neurology training model is still largely based on the traditional pathway, which adopted heavily from the parent fields. The last major revision of ACGME requirements in 2014 did not change the overall framework of training [4, 5].

Modifications of training pathways should incorporate both the available evidence and the experience of those in the field. Recent papers on child neurology training, many published since 2014, include opinion pieces and surveys of child neurology educators and recent graduates [1, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. These publications demonstrate mixed views on training models, including total training duration and the timing and duration of the three broad components of clinical training: general pediatrics, child neurology, and adult neurology. Currently, child neurology–specific training comprises only 40% of the total residency training [11]. Many favor reducing general pediatrics and adult neurology time in favor of more pediatric specialty training, such as in genetics and child development [7, 10], but support for this is not universal [9]. Debate also persists over maintaining a 5‐year total training model versus universal reduction to 4 years [1, 7, 12]. The ACGME and American Board of Psychiatry and Neurology (ABPN) have already acknowledged that 4 years of clinical training is adequate by approving a neuroscience research pathway that requires only 4 total years of clinical training, with only 1 year of general pediatrics [11, 13]. Notably, prior studies generally asked questions rooted in the current system [7, 8, 10, 12], limiting the range of responses and ability to conceptualize an entirely different model.

Before the upcoming ACGME Shaping GME initiative [14], during which child neurology program requirements may undergo substantial revisions, it is critical to obtain updated input from child neurology program directors (PDs), who are experts in child neurology training. We aimed to engage child neurology PDs using a more open‐ended and analytical method to develop a PD‐derived model of essential training components, with the ability to innovate beyond the current system.

Methods

Survey Content and Distribution

All 79 ACGME‐approved child neurology residency programs in the United States were invited to participate via e‐mail to PDs and associate PDs (APDs) in November 2023. These program leaders were asked to complete an Excel template of a block diagram of their recommendations for child neurology training (Figure S1). Only one block diagram per program was accepted. If the PD and APD both submitted a response from the same program, only the PD's response was included. The only constraint was a limit of 48 months (4 years) of total rotations, as one currently accredited pathway allows for only 4 years of clinical training. Departmental affiliation (pediatrics or neurology) for all 79 programs was determined by the program website. Program size (residents per year) was ascertained by the 2023 recruitment data from the Child Neurology Society website [15] and verified with National Resident Matching Program Match data [16]. For ease of analysis, the Excel template specified the following rotations, which were derived from current ACGME requirements at the time of the survey: pediatric intensive care unit (PICU); neonatal intensive care unit (NICU); other ICU; pediatric inpatient/hospital medicine; healthy newborn; advocacy/diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI); child development; pediatric emergency/acute care; outpatient/community pediatrics; adult neuro inpatient/consults; child neuro inpatient/consults; child neuro general outpatient; neuro specialties outpatient; genetics; neuroradiology; electroencephalography (EEG); child psychiatry; neuropathology; and electives. Respondents could specify the setting of the rotation (inpatient, outpatient, elective, or leave it blank) and which field of study the rotation should satisfy (pediatrics, adult neurology, child neurology, neurology [adult or child], or blank). Importantly, participants were not mandated to add any of the current requirements to their block diagram, and the template also included an option for “other” that allowed respondents to specify alternative requirements. We define “electives” as rotations for which residents have complete freedom of choice; as such, respondents were not asked about the specific electives. Similarly, we did not ask respondents to specify which specialties should be included in “neuro specialties outpatient” because this level of specification is more granular than current ACGME requirements. In addition to the block diagram, respondents were asked to specify the duration and setting in which continuity clinic should be conducted for each year of training.

Data Standardization

Excel was used to compile the data for analysis. Compiled, anonymized data were redistributed to the authors. A subgroup of authors met to discuss how to handle missing data. For example, if a rotation such as pediatric ICU that can only be completed inpatient was specified but the setting was left blank, the setting was designated as inpatient. However, if a rotation such as child psychiatry was specified with blank data in the setting column, the setting was left blank because child psychiatry can be completed in multiple settings, and the intention was to give the program or individual trainee a choice if possible.

Data Analysis and Statistics

After data standardization and anonymization, we determined the percentage of PDs recommending each rotation, the number of months recommended, and the amount of time dedicated to rotations in each setting and in each field. We used the Mann–Whitney U test to compare the recommendations for number of months of adult neurology inpatient rotations among PDs from pediatric and neurology departments. A sample size calculation showed that 40 PDs (20 in each type of department) would be adequate if the difference in recommendations was at least 2 months (P(X > Y) 0.76, alpha two‐tailed 0.05, power 0.8).

Determining Consensus

We used a cutoff of 75% of participating PDs recommending a rotation as a threshold for consensus. All rotations that met this threshold were included in a final curriculum recommendation. The duration of each rotation in the final curriculum recommendation was determined based on the mean number of months recommended by all respondents.

Qualtrics Post‐Survey

All 79 child neurology PDs were invited to complete an anonymous Qualtrics survey [17] in February 2024 about the consensus curriculum recommendations. As this survey was anonymous, APDs were not included in this follow‐up survey to ensure only one response per program. PDs were asked whether the consensus requirements would provide adequate training if implemented in either a 4‐ or 5‐year child neurology training program. They were also asked whether a resident could be adequately trained in 4 years. For both questions, PDs could select agree or disagree, with a range of qualifiers regarding revisions and implementation preferences.

Protocol Approval

This study was deemed exempt by the Institutional Review Board at Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center.

Results

Response Rate and Demographics

PDs representing 50 of 79 child neurology residency programs (63%), from all nine Association of American Medical Colleges–designated geographic regions [18], participated by submitting a block diagram (Figure 1A). Twenty‐seven respondents (54%) were primarily affiliated with a pediatrics department; the remaining 23 respondents (46%) were affiliated with neurology (Figure 1B). Twenty‐nine respondents (58%) came from programs that recruited two or fewer residents per year, including one newly accredited program that had not started recruiting residents yet at the time of the survey (Figure 1C).

The response rate for the anonymous post‐survey was slightly higher with completion by 53 PDs (67%). To preserve anonymity, no demographic data were collected with the post‐survey.

Field Specification (Pediatrics vs. Adult Neurology vs. Child Neurology)

Current requirements for child neurology training specify time that must be spent in pediatrics, adult neurology, and child neurology. We allowed respondents to specify the field in which each rotation should be completed (Figure 3C). For example, a respondent could specify that neuro specialties outpatient be completed in adult neurology, child neurology, or a combination of both. We used this to determine if rotations should be restricted to a specific field or if flexibility should be given. Using the cutoff of 75% of respondents for consensus, six rotations were clearly specified as pediatrics rotations: child development, healthy newborn, NICU, PICU, pediatric emergency/acute care, and pediatric inpatient/hospital medicine. The only rotation that met the cutoff for adult neurology was the adult neurology inpatient rotation. Child neurology inpatient and outpatient rotations were delineated as child neurology rotations. Child psychiatry, EEG, genetics, neuro specialties outpatient, neuroradiology, and electives did not meet the consensus threshold to be specified to a particular field.

Continuity Clinic Requirements

We also asked PDs to specify in which field residents should complete their continuity clinics during each of the 4 years (Figure 3D). There was consensus (78%) for a continuity clinic in the first year to be in pediatrics clinics, with 22% recommending the child neurology continuity clinic in the first year instead. There was a strong consensus that continuity clinic should be in general child neurology clinics throughout each of the remaining years of training.

Final PD Consensus Requirements

Combining the above specifications, we derived the PD consensus requirements (Figure 4A). To maintain program flexibility, we rounded the requirements down to the nearest month, except in the case of healthy newborn, which we rounded down to 0.5 months because the initial average was less than 1. We also combined the pediatric (non‐consult) ICU rotations into a single requirement to acknowledge the various opportunities that might exist at some programs for primary pediatric neurocritical care or cardiac ICU exposure. After combining the pediatric ICU rotations, the total rounded down to 3 months. If not combined, NICU and PICU would both have rounded down to 1 month each, so a 1‐month minimum requirement each for NICU and PICU was maintained within the total of 3 months of pediatric ICU rotations. There were 39.5 total months of consensus requirements, leaving 8.5 months that could be used at the program's discretion within a 48‐month training model. Pediatric vs. Neurology Departments

We next analyzed whether PDs in pediatrics versus neurology departments recommended different numbers of months of adult neurology rotations. The total number of adult neurology months recommended was 5.9 for PDs in neurology departments and 5.1 for PDs in pediatric departments. This difference was almost completely accounted for by the difference in recommendations for adult neurology inpatient rotations: 3.9 months by neurology department–affiliated PDs and 3.0 months by those with pediatric affiliation (Figure 5). The difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.11).

Box and whisker plot comparing the number of adult neurology inpatient months recommended by PDs in neurology and pediatrics departments. The X on each plot shows the mean recommendation, which was 3.9 months from neurology departments and 3.0 from pediatric departments (p value = 0.11, n.s. = not significant).

Post‐Survey

Following analysis of the block diagrams, we sent the results back to PDs along with a short survey to assess agreement that these minimum requirements would provide adequate training regardless of whether they were included as part of a 4‐ or 5‐year program (Figure 6A). Eighty‐seven percent of PDs agreed that these requirements would be adequate. Thirteen percent of PDs agreed but had concerns with implementing these requirements. We then asked whether PDs agreed that child neurologists could be adequately trained for independent practice within 4 years (Figure 6B). Eighty‐nine percent of PDs agreed that 4 years would be adequate. Fifteen percent of PDs agreed but noted concerns about implementation of a 4‐year training pathway. Nine percent of PDs felt that 5 years of training will always be necessary.

Discussion

Our training pathways must evolve to best train child neurologists for the future. We must be informed by the past but not entrenched in traditional training models that may be suboptimal for educating the residents of today, in the rapidly changing world of child neurology practice. This study is unique in the process of obtaining input: child neurology residency PDs built their own models of residency training, as opposed to completing a survey regarding agreement or disagreement with specific components of the current training structure. The creative consensus generation process likely allowed for less constrained and more comprehensive input than conventional survey methods.

The consensus requirements differ from the current training requirements [11, 13], both in duration of certain required rotations and also in providing more specificity regarding individual rotations. The greatest difference is in the preliminary pediatrics rotations. The consensus requirements include 9.5 months of specified pediatrics rotations plus a year‐long continuity clinic in pediatrics. The 9.5 months of specified pediatrics rotations are comparable to the 8 months of internal medicine training required for adult neurology residents [19]. However, the total duration is much shorter than the 2 years currently required for the most common training pathway [11, 13]. Notably, whether implementing the consensus requirements as part of a 4‐ or 5‐year pathway, programs could opt to require additional pediatrics months as part of the “program discretion” time. Residents could also choose to complete additional rotations in pediatrics in their 7 months of elective time.

Within child neurology itself, the PD consensus requirements specify 8 months of inpatient and 4 months of outpatient, which is similar to the current ACGME requirements of at least 12 months of clinical child neurology, including at least 4 months of outpatient experience [11]. Other consistencies include 3 years of child neurology continuity clinic and the requirement for child psychiatry. There is additional specificity for requirements in genetics, EEG, and neuroradiology; these do not exist as mandatory rotations in the current training requirements [11].

The consensus requirements would mandate only 3 months of inpatient adult neurology, which is a reduction from current requirements of 6 months. This change is in line with prior surveys of PDs and broader groups of child neurologists that demonstrate support for reducing required adult neurology time [7, 8, 10, 12]. Notably, there was no significant difference in the number of recommended months of adult neurology between PDs in pediatrics versus neurology departments. A 3‐month adult neurology minimum requirement is comparable to the 3 months of clinical child neurology required of adult neurology residents [19]. Programs would have flexibility to allocate additional time in adult neurology during the 4 months of neurology outpatient specialty rotations, EEG, and neuroradiology. Additionally, “program discretion” months could be used to ensure adequate training in all areas of neurology, depending on available experiences in child and/or adult neurology at their institution. As the field of child neurology has expanded, many programs now have broader child neurology–specific opportunities in subspecialty areas that were historically not available or inadequate for comprehensive training. If these opportunities are available and robust, child neurology residents should be able to take advantage of them in child neurology, their ultimate field of practice, as opposed to being limited only to adult neurology. There may also be opportunities to develop neurology rotations across the lifespan, which could more easily satisfy these requirements while also fulfilling the need for education in transitions of care.

The 7 months of required elective time, which could be completed in pediatrics, child neurology, and/or adult neurology, would allow for individualization of the child neurology residency curriculum at the resident level. The emphasis on individualization has precedent in the upcoming 2025 changes to ACGME requirements for pediatrics residencies [20]. The current ACGME child neurology residency requirements specify 3 months of elective time in child neurology and 3 months of elective time in adult neurology (as a component of the total 12 months of adult neurology) [11]. In the PD consensus pathway, the 7 months of electives would be separate from the 4 months of neurology outpatient specialty rotations and from consensus requirements in genetics, EEG, and neuroradiology that are often counted as electives under the current requirements. Residents could choose to complete the 7 elective months as additional clinical training; neuroscience rotations such as neuroanatomy, neuropathology, or broader neurophysiology; or an entirely different opportunity such as research, medical education, quality improvement, and/or advocacy, as available in their program.

Importantly, the results show that despite differences in institutional structure, geographical location, and size of child neurology residency programs, child neurology PDs—who are content experts in child neurology residency education—can reach consensus regarding the essential elements of residency training. Most responding PDs believe that child neurology residency training could be completed in 4 years, though opinions vary regarding whether this should be optional or required. Whether or not the minimum residency duration is reduced to 4 years from the current 5, a strong majority of responding PDs believe that the consensus training requirements would adequately train residents for independent practice of child neurology.

There are several important limitations to the study. Not all child neurology programs were represented in the block diagram and post‐survey input due to lack of response. PDs from neurology departments (46% in the sample as opposed to 51% for all 79 programs) and those from programs with 2 or fewer residents per year (58% in the sample as opposed to 66% for all 79 programs) may have been slightly underrepresented in block diagram input. Neurodevelopmental disabilities programs were also not represented, as the specific focus was on child neurology training. The block diagram input was not anonymous, which may have influenced responses, though data were anonymized for analysis and the subsequent post‐survey was anonymous. While we attempted to minimize constraints in the block diagram data collection to allow for greater innovation, we did constrain to 4 years and included the language of current ACGME rotation requirements as potential options to allow for some uniformity and ease of analysis.

The power of this study to yield statistically significant results of the different opinions of program leaders in adult versus pediatrics departments was limited. Inherently, the maximum possible sample size is 79 PDs, since there are only 79 US child neurology residency programs. There were 27 participating PDs from pediatric departments and 23 from neurology departments. A sample size of 20 PDs in each group would have been adequate to detect a difference of 2 months between groups. However, in our data, even the PDs in neurology departments recommended fewer months of adult neurology inpatient than the current requirements. Given the difference of only 0.9 in the mean number of months recommended by PDs in pediatrics versus neurology departments and our standard deviations, we would need a minimum of 82 PDs to have a sample size with adequate power.

In conclusion, the PD consensus shows strong support for modification of existing child neurology training requirements, with general agreement that 4 years of clinical training would be adequate. There is support for reduction in the required pediatrics and adult neurology rotations and additional delineation of specific subspecialty rotations while also allowing flexibility for neurology rotations to occur in adult or pediatric settings as available. As the field of child neurology matures into adulthood, it is time to acknowledge the important and continued contributions of our parent fields of pediatrics and neurology, and maintain our key relationships with both, while ensuring independence in our own training pathway.
