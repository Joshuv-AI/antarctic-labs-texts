Ancestry and morphoagronomical traits of cacao clones from the Cuban genebank reveal the diversity and potential for breeding of national genetic resources
Authors: Angel Rafael Ramirez-Ramirez, Osbel Miranda-Barbier, Aylin Cantillo-Gainza, Yaneski Columbié-Durán, Thalía Rodriguez-Lambert, Ismaray Hinojosa-Marzo, Miguel Menéndez-Grenot, Georgina Espinosa-López, Igor Bidot-Martínez, Pierre Bertin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCacao breeding programs relying on a wide genetic base could contribute to increase the sustainability of cacao production. In Cuba, a short list of cacao clones was endorsed for breeding and farming practices; all of them were imported from other countries and show low genetic diversity. Cacao National Genebank (CNGB) conserves plants derived from breeding experiments and from in-farm prospections, which could be considered as national genetic resources (Cuban clones). The limited characterization of these resources has hampered their use for breeding purposes. This study aimed (1) to characterize the diversity of 89 clones from CNGB on the basis of both cacao ancestry and 22 morphoagronomical descriptors and (2) to identify national genetic resources with the highest potential for breeding considering their genetic and morphoagronomical traits.ResultsCacao ancestry analysis using SNP markers, mainly identified seven lineages in the studied clones: Amelonado, Contamana, Criollo, Iquitos, Marañón, Nacional and Nanay. Most clones were hybrids of Amelonado with another ancestry. Cuban clones were more diverse in terms of cacao ancestry than clones currently used in breeding. A wide morphoagronomical variability was observed among the 89 clones and for most traits, Cuban clones were not significantly different from the imported ones. Clones with high values in agronomical-relevant traits (e.g. seed and fruit weight-related) tended to be hybrids (Q < 0.7), while low values of these descriptors were frequently observed in plants with high membership (Q > 0.7) to certain cacao ancestries. Seven Cuban clones showed values of seed weight-related traits similar to the clones currently exploited in breeding and farming practices, but they were more diverse considering their cacao ancestries. Six clones had light or pale colored cotyledons, which has been associated to a better sensorial quality of cocoa, and most of them showed high membership to Amelonado ancestry.ConclusionCuban genetic resources of Theobroma cacao L. from CNGB show a wide diversity which could contribute to broaden the genetic base of the breeding program. Several Cuban clones showed ancestry and seed-related traits that support their use to breed new cacao varieties to increase the sustainability of cacao production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08694-5.Keywords: Cuban cacao, Genetic resources, Genetic diversity, Morphological variability, Cacao genebank

Background

Cacao (Theobroma cacao L.) is a tropical perennial tree and its seeds are the source of cocoa powder and cacao butter, very important products for chocolatery and cosmetic industries. In the period from 2019–2023, the global production of cacao beans ranged from 5.5 − 5.7 million tons and the value of the annual cocoa market was estimated in USD 9–11 billion. Millions of people worldwide are involved in cacao cultivation as well as in the harvest and postharvest processing, which reinforces the relevance of cacao bean as an international commodity [1, 2].

The cacao tree is native to South America in the Amazon rainforest and the Upper Amazon region is the putative center of origin of the species. From there, cacao migrated to Central America and other South American regions during pre-Columbian times, and later to other tropical areas of the planet [3]. The first attempts at cacao classification were based on plant morphology and recognized three groups: Forastero, Criollo and Trinitario.

Forastero trees are robust, disease-resistant plants that were originally found in South America. Fruits of Forastero trees show a high variability considering shape, surface rugosity, apex form and basal contriction. However, plants of this group conserved at the International Cocoa Genebank, Trinidad (universal collection) had relatively small and flat seeds with dark purple cotyledons in most of the cases. Cocoa from Forastero plant has an astringent intense flavor and is considered of low sensorial quality. It represents the 95% of the world’s production and is known as “bulk cocoa” [4–7].

Criollo cacaos were mostly detected in Central America. They are less vigorous plants with high disease susceptibility. Fruits contain a small number of large size seeds with a ‘‘rounded’’ or ‘‘plump’’ shape, and their cotyledons are white or pale in color (pink or light purple). This last trait is frequently related to the high quality of cocoa of Criollo plants because of the flavor notes it produces, such as fruity, nutty and caramel, which supports their classification as fine and flavor cacao. However, other cacaos have also received this classification, such as Nacional from Ecuador [8–11]. Fine and flavor cacaos reach the best prices and represent only the 5% of the market, but in the period of 2011–2020, they showed a growth rate of 7–11%, which was higher than that of the other groups [12].

Trinitarios were primarily described in Trinidad and Tobago and derived from crosses between Forastero and Criollo plants. This is a very heterogenous group of plants with morphological traits that vary between those of the parental groups and occasionally exceed them [8, 13]. With respect to agronomical-relevant traits, such as cotyledon length, width and weight, Trinitario plants usually perform better than Forasteros, supporting the differentiation of these two groups. Criollo and Trinitario clones are similar in terms of seed traits, but Trinitario fruits tend to be larger. Some Trinitarios have light-colored cotyledons and produce fine and flavor cocoa [5, 11, 14, 15]. Self-incompatibility and disease susceptibility are major drawbacks of Trinitario cacaos as they could lead to yield reduction [15, 16].

Motamayor et al. [17] established a new genetic classification system based on Simple Sequence Repeats (SSR) markers and ten cacao ancestry genetic groups were identified. This system confirmed the Criollo group and its location in Central America, but divided Forastero into nine ancestries or lineages: Amelonado, Contamana, Curaray, Guiana, Iquitos, Marañón, Nacional, Nanay and Purús; all of them were located in South America. Most Trinitarios are hybrids of Amelonado and Criollo, although they may contain proportions from other ancestries [18].

The wide genetic and morphological diversity of cacao is underrepresented in cacao productive areas worldwide. Frequently cacao cultivation is based on varieties bred more than 50 year ago, using a narrow genetic base, which led to an increased vulnerability of cacao plantations to biotic and abiotic stresses [3, 19, 20]. Therefore, there is a need to make a better use of the available genetic diversity of the species to breed improved and more diverse cacao varieties [19, 21]. Furtunately, several initiatives started to move on in that direction and national improvement programs using a broader genetic base have been inplaced for a while [12, 22–25].

Currently, global cacao breeding efforts are focused on the development of high-yielding, disease-resistant, abiotic stress tolerant genotypes with good flavor potential, though new attributes might be added in the future. Such genotypes could provide the foundation for developing resilient and sustainable cacao production systems able to properly respond to the environmental challenges posed by the climate change [19–21, 26]. The characterization of genotypes associated to breeding programs with both molecular markers and morphological descriptors provides a comprehensive assessment of their potential and ensures the proper selection of the genotypes, especially when it is performed in early stages [19, 21, 27, 28].

T. cacao is an introduced species in Cuba and 1,428 tons were produced in 2022. There is a debate about the first introduction of cacao in Cuba, pointed either to the central region near Cabaiguán in 1540 from México or by French colonists who settled down in Ti Arriba, eastern Cuba in late XVIIIth century. Several introductions occurred during the following centuries and the information about the origin of the plants is scarce. However, it is recognized the harvesting of cacao beans of the types “Criollo” or “Cubano”, “Guayaquil” (Ecuador) and “Caracas” (Venezuela). Currently, most cacao plantations (80%) are located in the Baracoa region in the easternmost part of the country [29, 30].

The Cacao National Genebank (CNGB) is also located in Baracoa and supports a breeding program that started in the 1990 s [30]. CNGB is the major repository of cacao germplasm in the country and three categories of plants or clones could be found at the CNGB, considering their origin: 1) imported clones from other countries, 2) prospected plants from field expeditions, and 3) clones derived from the breeding program. Plants belonging to the last two categories could be considered as national genetic resources or Cuban clones.

The imported clones include well known cacao accessions of different series (e.g. GS, ISC, Pound, SCA, UF, among others) which were introduced during the second half of the last century [30]. Cuban prospected plants were collected in field expeditions especially in the Baracoa region, and include Cuban traditional cacao, which are very old plants surviving in the farms and supposedly, are the closest ones to the cacao introduced in Cuba [31]. Clones derived from the breeding program came from hybridization experiments of this program [32, 33].

As part of the breeding program, several clones from the genebank were characterized with morphoagronomical descriptors, including resistance to Phytophthora palmivora B., which allowed the identification of superior clones and the development of procedures for hybrid seed production [32–35]. However, the morphoagronomical characterization of recently prospected accessions, such as Cuban traditional cacao, and new hybrid clones from breeding experiments, is still pending, which might be limiting their use.

Another result of the breeding program was the establishment of the Cuban Official List of Cacao Commercial Varieties (COLCCV) [36]. The list includes the clones endorsed for farming purposes either to produce grafted seedling or to obtain certified hybrid seeds by specific crosses. COLCCV include the following clones: CCN- 51, ICS-6, ICS- 8, ICS-95, IMC-67, GS-29, GS-36, UF-221, UF- 296, UF-613, UF-650, UF654, UF-667, UF-668, UF-676, UF-677, Pound-7 and Pound-12 [36]. Notably, several of these clones have also been used as parents in the breeding program. COLCCV has 18 different cacao clones and twelve of them are classified as Trinitarios cacao supporting a limited used of cacao diversity in Cuba. Furthermore, all 18 clones from COLCCV were imported from other countries; therefore, Cuban clones have been poorly utilized thus far.

Recently, we used and validated a new ddRADseq protocol (double digestion restriction site associated DNA sequencing) for cacao SNP genotyping; which allowed the assessment of population structure and genetic diversity at CNGB [37, 38]. This genetic characterization combined with morphoagronomical description data of cacao clones of the CNGB could contribute to a better characterization the cacao diversity conserved in the CNGB and to improve the use of cacao national genetic resources for breeding and farming purposes.

The present study aims included: 1) to characterize the diversity of the CNGB on the basis of both cacao ancestry and 22 morphoagronomical descriptors and (2) to identify national genetic resources with the highest potential for breeding considering their genetic and morphoagronomical traits. The results derived from this study could provide a comprehensive assessment of the genetic diversity conserved at the CNGB in order to make a better use of cacao diversity available in Cuba to breed improved varieties. These varieties could be used to develop sustainable cacao production systems with an increased resilience to present and future environmental changes.

Methods

Plant material

The CNGB is held by the Unidad de Ciencia y Técnica de Base—Baracoa/Instituto de Investigaciones Agroforestales (UCTB—Baracoa/INAF), formerly known as Estación de Investigaciones de Cacao de Baracoa (EICB). Population structure of CNBG was previously established using SNP markers and cacao clones were grouped into seven genetic clusters. Each cluster carried a distintive cacao ancestry proportion, though some accessions were difficult to allocate and were classified as “Admixed” (Adm) [37]. On the basis of clone assignment to the identified genetic clusters, 89 accessions were selected for the present study which represented 37.4% of the total genotyped clones (Table 1). The selection was made in such way, that the number of clones from each genetic cluster, as well as for “Admixed” plants, represented about 30—40% of the total of their own groups, except for the clusters with the lowest number of clones (Table 1). This approach ensured a proper representation of each cluster among the studied clones. Whenever possible, clones with high membership coefficients (> 0.7) to the identified clusters were preferably selected.

Regarding the clone category at the CNGB based on their origin, the 89 selected clones split into: 23 imported from other countries, 45 prospected plants in the farms in Cuba – mostly in Baracoa region – and 21 clones derived from the breeding program [30]. Eleven clones from the Cuban Official List of Cacao Commercial Varieties (COLCCV) were included in this study (GS- 29, GS-36, ICS-8, UF-221, UF-613, UF- 654, UF-667, UF- 668, UF-676, UF-677 and CCN-51) and used as references because of their good agronomical performance.

For ancestry estimation of CNGB clones, data from 65 plants were used as references of the cacao ancestry genetic groups [17] (see Additional file 1). DNA sequences of these 65 plants were downloaded and processed according to Cornejo et al. [39], who established the cacao ancestry of these plants with whole genome sequencing experiments.

Cacao ancestry of the studied clones

DNA sequences of the 89 cacao clones were used for SNP calling as previously described [37]. The generated SNPs dataset was employed for the estimation of the membership coefficients to the ten cacao ancestry genetic groups described by Motamayor et al. [17], using the supervised mode of ADMIXTURE software v1.3 [40]. DNA sequences of the 65 reference plants of cacao ancestries were used to build a reference SNP dataset, which was employed for training ADMIXTURE software for cacao ancestry estimation.

To confirm ADMIXTURE results, a clustering analysis was performed by UPGMA (Unweighted pair group method with arithmetic averages) with a Hamming distance matrix calculated from the SNPs dataset. The analysis included the 89 clones from the genebank and the 65 reference plants of the cacao ancestry genetic groups. The tree was built with a bootstrapping procedure (1,000 replicas) with aboot function of poppr package v2.9.4 [41] from R statistical language v4.2 [42] and visualized with ggtree package v3.10.0 [43].

Morphoagronomical characterization of the studied clones based on fruit and seed descriptors

Morphoagronomical descriptors were chosen from the list of 65 cacao descriptors recognized by International Plant Genetic Resources Institute (IPGRI, now Biodiversity International) [44] and from those used by UCTB—Baracoa/INAF (Cuba) for cacao plant characterization [34, 35]. In total, 22 fruit and seed descriptors were used in this study, comprising 10 qualitative and 12 quantitative traits (Table 2).

For the 89 clones – each containing from 6 to 8 plants –, 30 healthy undamaged fruits (5 or 6 per plant) were collected. From the middle section of each fruit, between 8 and 12 seeds were harvested and one hundred of them were used for seed characterization. Therefore, fruit descriptors had 30 replicas while the seed descriptors had 100, except for the fresh weight of one hundred seeds, which were estimated in triplicate.

The proper estimation of cotyledon color requires to perform clone self-pollination to discard the pollen effect [10, 15]. As our measurements were performed under open pollination conditions, the procedure for estimating this trait was modified based on Engels et al. [45]. In this respect, we defined the descriptor “Predominant cotyledon color” with two phenotypic classes: light and dark. For assignment to classes, the cotyledon color of one hundred seeds per clone was classified and counted according to their color when cut in: white, light purple, medium purple and dark purple. When the sum of white and light purple was higher than the sum of medium and dark purple, the clone was assigned to “light predominant cotyledon color”, otherwise it was assigned to “dark predominant cotyledon color”.

Clustering of studied cacao clones based on morphoagronomical descriptors

Mean and mode values of quantitative and qualitative descriptors, respectively, were used for a principal component analysis (PCA) with PCAmix function of PCAmixdata package v3.1 [48]. Traits with eigenvectors higher than 75% of the highest eigenvector of the first two principal component axes (PCs) were selected as the more contributing to the total variation. The selected traits included both qualitative (2) and quantitative (8) traits which were used to calculate the Gower distance matrix of the 89 clones with the function daisy from cluster package v2.1.6 [49]. This matrix was employed for clustering analysis by Ward’s method [50] with hclust function from stats package of R [42].

Pattern of variation among the studied clones considering both genetic and morphoagronomic traits

To describe the pattern of genetic and morphoagronomical variation among the studied clones and Ward’s identified groups, we assessed the correlations between individual heterozygosity, based on SNPs, and the eight quantitative morphoagronomical traits included in the more contributing descriptors. Individual heterozygosity was estimated with the SNPs dataset and the function gl.report.heterozygosity from dartR package v2.9.7 [51, 52]. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated with cor.test function from stats v4.1.2 package of R [42]. The means per Ward’s group of individual heterozygosity, cacao ancestry membership coefficients and the selected morphoagronomical traits were also determined.

Clones with the highest potential for breeding on the basis of both cacao ancestry and agronomical-relevant descriptors

To identify the cacao clones with the best profiles for breeding, we compared the members of the group with the highest values of the selected quantitative descriptors from Ward’s clustering. The comparison was based on membership coefficients to the cacao ancestry genetic groups, and the descriptors: seed number per fruit, seeds fresh weight per fruit and the fresh weight of one hundred seeds. Indeed, these traits have been related to cacao productivity [6, 15, 34] and coincidently, the two related with seed weight were among the traits selected for clustering analysis. Predominant cotyledon color was also included in the comparison because of its association with cocoa quality [10, 15, 53].

Analyses of variance for the quantitative traits were performed with aov function from stats package, to detect significant differences among clones. Clone means were submitted to paired comparisons according to Tukey’s test with glht function from multcomp package v1.4- 25 [54]. Letters were assigned with the function cld from the same package to highlight significant differences (p < 0.05).

Results

Cacao ancestries of the studied clones

The membership coefficients of the 89 studied clones to the ten cacao ancestry genetic groups were estimated with ADMIXTURE software run under supervised mode. For this purpose, we employed a large dataset with 11,295 SNPs − built from the DNA sequences of the 89 clones − and the genotypes of the 65 references of cacao ancestry genetic groups. These coefficients were used to analyze the genetic profiles based on cacao ancestry of individual clones as well as of each category level (imported, prospected and from breeding). Seven cacao ancestries or lineages were mainly identified among the 89 clones, carrying high frequency differences: Amelonado (QMEAN = 0.549), Criollo (0.171), Iquitos (0.093), Contamana (0.071), Nanay (0.053), Nacional (0.048) and Marañón (0.011) (Fig. 1).

In the analysis at the individual level, the value of Q > 0.7 was utilized to differentiate plants with high membership to any cacao ancestry genetic group from the hybrids [17]. A total of 35 clones had a high membership coefficient (Q > 0.7) for a given cacao ancestry, while the other 54 were hybrids (Q < 0.7). The plants with high membership belonged mostly to the lineage Amelonado (29), with some other lineages poorly represented: Contamana (3), Iquitos (2) and Marañón (1).

The hybrids were mainly a combination of Amelonado with the other detected ancestries, with the exception of Marañón. Hybrids of two different cacao lineages were more common (25) and included the combinations Amelonado/Criollo (Trinitario cacao, light green bar) (18) and Amelonado/Nacional (light red bar) (6) (Fig. 1). However, some hybrids had contributions from four or five different cacao ancestry genetic groups, such as the small group of accessions highlighted by the blue bar, which shared ancestry proportions from Amelonado, Iquitos, Contamana and Criollo (Fig. 1).

Clustering analysis by UPGMA of the 89 clones and the 65 references of cacao ancestries confirmed ADMIXTURE results. The dendrogram revealed that clones with similar cacao ancestry proportions clustered together (highlighted by the colored sidebars), and next to the expected cacao ancestry reference plants (Fig. 1). Finally, several recognized cacao accessions, such as the UF’s, SCA’s and CCN-51, were correctly assigned to their known ancestry, confirming the results here presented (see Additional file 3).

Cacao ancestry proportions by clone category (imported, prospected and derived from breeding) revealed that most imported clones (19 out 23) were hybrids. Among them, the Trinitario type (57.9%) was the most abundant (light-green sidebar) and comprised eight out of the 11 clones from the Cuban official list of cacao commercial varieties (COLCCV) included in this study: ICS-8, GS-29, GS-36, UF-221, UF-667, UF-668, UF-676 and UF- 677 (Fig. 1). The remaining three had contributions from Nacional (UF-613) and Iquitos/Criollo (CCN-51 and UF-654) lineages. These results supported a low diversity among the clones from COLCCV based on cacao ancestry.

In contrast, Cuban cacao clones either prospected (45) or derived from breeding (21), were much more diverse in terms of ancestry than COLCCV clones and even imported accessions (Cuban clones are labeled with EICB, which stands for “Estación de Investigaciones de Cacao de Baracoa”). They spanned six of the ancestries found in the present study and were allocated to more branches of the dendrogram than COLCCV and the imported clones (Fig. 1).

Cuban clones presented ancestry combinations not detected among imported clones, either as high membership plants or hybrids. Several plants with high membership coefficient to Amelonado ancestry were detected in both prospected (24) and bred (5) clones, a genetic profile missing among imported accessions (Fig. 1). Similarly, hybrid clones either prospected (21) or derived from breeding (14), had ancestry combinations not found among all imported accessions and hence among COLCCV clones. That was the case of the clones carrying Nanay lineages (dark green bar, Fig. 1) or the hybrids formed by the ancestries: Amelonado/Iquitos/Contamana/Criollo (blue bar, Fig. 1).

It was noteworthy that Trinitario cacaos identified among Cuban clones (7) were prospected accessions as none of the clones derived from breeding were allocated in the Trinitario clade (light green sidebar, Fig. 1B). This is probably a consequence of the extensive use of certain Trinitario cacaos as progenitors of the breeding program in Cuba [30].

Morphoagronomical characterization of the studied clones based on fruit and seed descriptors

Normalized Shannon Weaver diversity index (H’) of the 10 fruit and seed qualitative descriptors supported a wide morphological variability among the 89 studied clones. H’ values were high (H’ > 0.7) for 6 descriptors (60%), intermediate (0.4–0.7) for two (20%), and low (H’ < 0.4) for another two of them (20%) (see Additional file 4) [55]. Pulp flavor (0.945) had the highest H’ values, followed by fruit apex form (0.849) and fruit surface rugosity (0.838), while the descriptors with the lowest H’ values were predominant cotyledon color (0.355) and fruit pulp color (0.184). The H’ average of fruit traits (0.696) was higher than among the seed ones (0.542).

Interestingly, six cacao clones had light color (white and light purple) as the predominant cotyledon color (EICB-89, EICB-190, EICB-194, EICB-195, EICB- 359 and OC-61) and five of them were Cuban prospected clones (EICB). This trait has been associated with a better cocoa sensorial quality, especially in Criollo cacaos and is very valuable for market purposes [1, 10, 53].

For the quantitative traits, a wide variability was also observed among the studied clones in the 12 fruit and seed quantitative descriptors as the coefficients of variation (CV) ranged from 10.9 to 46.4% (see Additional file 5). The descriptor with the highest CV was fruit furrow depth (46.4%) followed by husk weight (37.0%) and fruit weight (34.1%). The traits: seed width (13.4%), fruit width (11.5%) and seed length (10.9%) had the three lowest CV values.

Cuban clones were compared against the imported ones considering each qualitative and quantitative descriptors. For most traits no signicant differences were detected between both groups of clones, except for two qualitative traits (fruit shape and fruit surface rugosity) and two quantitative traits (fruit length and seed thickness) (see Additional files 6 & 7). These results indicated some Cuban clones could show values in agronomical-relevant descriptors similar to the currently used for breeding and farming purposes.

Principal component analysis confirmed the wide variability observed in the 22 fruit and seed traits. The first two principal component axes (PCs) explained only 27.76% of the total variation among the 89 cacao clones (Table 3). To perform a clustering of the clones based on the phenotypes, the descriptors with eigenvectors higher than 75% of the largest eigenvector of each of these two PCs were selected as the ones with more contribution to the total variation. Eight quantitative and two qualitative traits shared this property which comprised seven and three descriptors from the fruit and seed, respectively (Table 3). Seven of them contributed to PC1 and three were relevant for PC2.

Mean values by group of the selected descriptors properly supported the differentiation of the four groups (Fig. 3). G3WARD and G4WARD showed the lowest values for seven quantitative traits: four of the fruit (width, weight, husk weight and seeds fresh weight) and three of the seed (length, width and fresh weight of one hundred seeds). For most of these seven traits, these groups were indistinguishable. However, the remaning three fruit descriptors fully differentiated these two groups: shape (G3WARD: elliptic and G4WARD: oblong) surface rugosity (G3WARD: low and G4WARD: high) and furrow depth (G3WARD: low and G4WARD: high) (Fig. 3).

G2WARD had intermediate values to the ones of the other groups for the seven quantitative traits aforementioned, which allowed the differentiation from these groups (Fig. 3). Interestingly, G2WARD and G3WARD shared some similararities considering the fruit descriptors: shape, surface rugosity and furrow depth. The observed values of these descriptors in G3WARD (elliptic fruit shape, low fruit surface rugosity and low furrow depth), in combination with the low fruit weight (Fig. 3), have been described for cacao plants of the Amelonado type [8]. These findings are consistent with the results of cacao ancestry analysis that detected Amelonado as the most common lineage among the 89 studied clones, including plants with high membership (Fig. 1).

Pattern of variation among the studied cacao clones considering both genetic and morphoagronomical traits

The correspondence between genetic and morphological data was difficult to establish since the clones of each morphoagronomical group were dispersed into different cacao ancestry genetic groups. To detect a pattern of variation by combining both genetic and morphoagronomical data, we assessed the correlations between clone individual heterozygosity, based on SNP markers, and individual values for each of the eight selected quantitative descriptor (Table 4). Pearson correlations were all significant (p < 0.05) and positive values of correlation coefficients were obtained for the eight descriptors (Table 4). Seeds fresh weight per fruit and fresh weight of one hundred seeds showed the largest positive correlation coefficients. These two descriptors are the most related to cacao productivity and were used to facilitate subsequent analysis.

In agreement with the correlation results, the G1WARD group had the largest mean of individual heterozygosity as well as for the descriptors: seeds fresh weight per fruit and fresh weight of one hundred seeds (Figs. 2B and 3). Genetic and cacao ancestry analyses of the 25 clones of this group revealed the prevalence of hybrids (92.0%, 23) (Fig. 2C), which was consistent with the high individual heterozygosity values observed. Eight of these hybrids were of the Trinitario type (Amelonado/Criollo), including six from the COLCCV.

G3WARD and G4WARD groups showed the lowest means of individual heterozygosity and for the two mentioned descriptors (Figs. 2B and 3). These low heterozygosity values were congruent with the presence of several clones with high membership (Q > 0.7) to the identified cacao lineages (Fig. 2C and D). In this respect, 12 out of 17 members of G3WARD (70.6%), had Q > 0.7 for Amelonado (11) and Iquitos (1); similarly, seven clones − out of 15 − from G4WARD had high membership coefficients to different Forastero cacao ancestries: Contamana (3), Amelonado (2), Iquitos (1) and Marañón (1). It was remarkable that the five Cuban clones with light-colored cotyledons (white and light purple) were allocated in G3WARD, although this descriptor was not selected for clustering. These five clones showed an average membership coefficient to Amelonado ancestry of 0.775.

The mean of individual heterozygosity of the G2WARD group was between the values of the other groups; similarly occurred with the group means of the two descriptors aforementioned (Figs. 2B and 3). G2WARD combined hybrid clones (18, 53.6% of group total) and 14 clones with Q > 0.7 for Amelonado lineage (Fig. 2C and D). The mean of the heterozygosity was higher among the 18 hybrids (0.329) than among the 14 plants with high Amelonado membership (0.187) according to Tukey’s test (p < 0.05). The means of the two descriptors for the hybrids were also slightly higher than those for Amelonado plants. Such behavior could support the presence of a variation pattern based on genetic and morphological traits among the 89 studied clones and the Ward’s identified groups.

According to this pattern of variation, cacao clones with high values of seeds fresh weight per fruit and fresh weight of one hundred seeds, like G1WARD members, showed higher individual heterozygosity values and tended to be hybrids, among them excelled Trinitario cacaos. In contrast, clones with low values of these descriptors − G3WARD and G4WARD group members −, also had low individual heterozygosity and high membership (Q > 0.7) to different cacao ancestries, especially those of the Forastero type.

Clones with the highest potential for breeding on the basis of both cacao ancestry and agronomical-relevant descriptors

To identify clones with the highest potential for breeding, we compared the group members of G1WARD as they showed the highest mean values of agronomical-relevant descriptors. Members of this group accounted for 25 accessions and included eight clones from the COLCCV (CCN-51, GS-29, GS-36, UF-654, UF-667, UF- 668, UF-676 and UF-677) (Fig. 2A). These last clones were used as references during the comparisons because they have been employed in breeding and farming purposes in Cuba [30, 36]. Clones were compared based on their membership coefficients to cacao ancestries and four traits related to productivity and quality in cacao (Table 5). Three clones (EICB-124, EICB-203 and EICB-291) were not included in Table 5, as their values for the two seed-weight related descriptors in comparison were lower than those of the clones from COLCCV with worst performance in these descriptors; therefore only the remaining 22 clones are shown.

The eight clones from COLCCV were imported and had cacao ancestry contributions mainly from three lineages: Amelonado (0.531), Criollo (0.365) and Iquitos (0.092). At the individual level, six clones shared very similar membership coefficients to Amelonado and Criollo ancestries confirming their Trinitario origin (Table 5). Only CCN-51 markedly differed among COLCCV clones because of its Iquitos background (Q = 0.556).

The 14 clones of G1WARD group not included in COLCCV consisted in nine accessions derived from breeding, two prospected and three imported (Table 5). Cacao ancestries among these 14 clones were mainly split into Amelonado (0.376), Criollo (0.231), Iquitos (0.118), Nanay (0.128) and Contamana (0.098), though some clones showed ancestry proportions higher than 10% from Nacional (EICB-164 and EICB-174) and Curaray (EICB-173) (Table 5, Fig. 1). Most of these 14 clones were hybrids and some of them showed a high mixture of cacao ancestries with contributions from four (EICB-174, EICB-210 and EICB-324) and five (EICB-164, EICB-173 and EICB176) cacao lineages. However, the clone EICB-353 showed high membership coefficients (Q > 0.7) to Amelonado (Table 5). These results revealed the cacao clones not included in the COLCCV as more genetically diverse than the included ones.

Analyses of variance of the morphoagronomical traits: seeds fresh weight per fruit, fresh weight of one hundred seeds and seed number, revealed significant differences among the 22 cacao clones (p < 0.05) (Table 5). The eight clones from the COLCCV had high values of seeds fresh weight per fruit and fresh weight of one hundred seeds, and some of them differed significantly from each other (Tukey’s test, p < 0.05). The mean of seeds fresh weight of several clones not included in the COLCCV showed no significant differences from the included ones (Tukey, p < 0.05). Eleven clones (EICB-164, EICB-172, EICB-174, EICB-176, EICB-210, EICB-278, EICB-324, EICB-342, EICB-346, EICB-353 and RIM-2) had a mean for this descriptor higher than the clone from COLCCV with worst mean (GS- 29, 153.4 g) (Table 5).

Similarly, in several cases, there were no significant differences in the fresh weight of one hundred seeds among clones from COLCCV and the others (Tukey, p < 0,05). Eleven of these last ones (EICB-164, EICB-172, EICB-173, EICB-174, EICB-176, EICB-278, EICB-346, EICB-353, OC-61, RIM-2 and SGU-54) showed a mean of this descriptor higher than the one from COLCCV with the lowest mean (GS-36, 381.4 g) (Table 5). When both traits were combined for analysis, eight clones (EICB-164, EICB-172, EICB- 174, EICB-176, EICB-278, EICB-346, EICB-353 and RIM-2) had seeds fresh weight per fruit (> 153,4 g) and fresh weight of one hundred seeds (> 381,4 g) in the range of COLCCV clones, and were classified as “Similar to COLCCV” (Table 5). For the remaining six accessions, only one of these two descriptors was in the same range of COLCCV clones and were named as “Partly similar to COLCCV” (Table 5). Seven and four Cuban clones classified as “Similar to COLCCV” and “Partly similar to COLCCV”, respectively.

The seed number per fruit behaved differently as the means of COLCCV clones varied from 29.5 to 43.3 and most of the other clones had values in that range showing no significant differences among them (Tukey, p < 0,05) (Table 5). Only four clones had means out of the aforementioned range: EICB-324, EICB-342, OC-61 and SGU- 54. EICB- 324 and EICB-342 had the largest mean of seed number, which was combined with low value of fresh weight of one hundred seeds.

On the contrary, OC-61 and SGU-54 had the lowest seed number and seeds fresh weight per fruit but with relatively large fresh weight of one hundred seeds (Table 5). In fact, OC-61 had the second largest value for this last trait, with no significant differences from the best clone based on this descriptor (UF-676) (Tukey, p < 0,05). These results supported the presence of small fruits with a low number of large seeds in OC-61. This clone also differed by the prevalence of cotyledons with light colors (white and light purple) and for carrying the highest membership coefficient to Criollo ancestry (0.656) among all 89 studied clones. All these traits distinguished OC-61 among the characterized accessions of the Cuban cacao genebank.

Discussion

Ancestry and morphoagronomical variability among the studied clones from the Cacao National Genebank

Cacao plant genetic and morphoagronomical characterization is the basis for genotype classification. Proper classification is a prerequisite for the sustainable management of cacao genetic resources and helps in the selection of genotypes for breeding and farming purposes. Several studies employed SNPs markers and morphological data to assess cacao variability allowing a better understanding of the available genetic diversity [8, 21, 56–58].

In this work, 89 cacao clones from the CNGB were studied with SNP markers and 22 fruit and seed morphoagronomical descriptors, firstly to characterize the diversity of the CNGB and later to identify national genetic resources with the highest potential for breeding on the basis of these traits. The characterization of cacao clones from CNGB in this study differed from previous efforts on the matter, which assessed specific groups of accessions (e.g. imported or prospected clones) with morphological data [34, 59, 60]. Likewise, the characterization of Cuban traditional cacao exploited SSR markers and qualitative descriptor excluding quantitative traits from the analysis [31, 61], hence the productive potential of this germplasm remained unknown.

Our research included all types of cacao clones from CNGB according to both category based on their origin (imported, prospected and from breeding) and their genetic classification [37], and exploited both qualitative and quantitative traits. Compared with previous studies, this approach could provide a more comprehensive assessment of the genotypic and phenotypic variation at the CNBG and constitute a significant stepforward for cacao agroindustry in region as it could lead to the sustainable management and use of the available cacao genetic resources in Cuba. Additional the combination of genetic and morphological data could open new research topics for cacao in Cuba such genetic association and gene functional analysis.

The significant variation observed in both cacao ancestries and morphoagronomical traits among the 89 studied clones has been also described in similar studies [56–58]. As expected, these clones had contributions from the seven cacao ancestry genetic groups previously identified in the CNGB: Amelonado, Criollo, Contamana, Iquitos, Nacional, Nanay and Marañón [37]. Most of the clones were hybrids of Amelonado with other detected lineages, regardless clone category (imported, prospected or derived from breeding). The presence of hybrids among imported clones, and especially the prevalence of the Trinitario type (Amelonado/Criollo) (Fig. 1), is probably related to the spread of Trinitario cacao germplasm worldwide because of their good agronomical performance [19, 21, 27]. Some clones of this type such as: GS-29, GS-36, ICS-8, UF-667 and UF- 668, were introduced in Cuba during the second half of the last century and showed good productive profile supporting their use for breeding and farming purposes [30, 36].

Hybrids detected among Cuban clones of the CNGB either prospected or derived from breeding were mostly collected in cacao farms from Baracoa region and added to genebank thereafter. These hybrids could be the result of the habit of certain farmers of producing their own seedlings from seeds obtained under open pollination conditions in the farms [30]. Another possibility is the use of seeds derived from hand pollination between specific clones for seedling production in Cuba, associated to the breeding program or not. Several crosses have been assayed, such as: UF-650 (Trinitario) x IMC- 67 (Iquitos), UF-654 (Trinitario) x Pound-7 (Nanay), UF-29 (Amelonado/Nacional) x UF- 677 (Trinitario) and UF-667 (Trinitario) x SCA-6 (Contamana) [30, 32, 33, 36]. A highly mixed progeny, in terms of cacao ancestries, had been obtained from such crosses. Notably, the conservation of these hybrids as CNGB clones could broaden the use of clonal propagation to new genotypes for seedling production, which is frequently associated to a better agronomical performance than progenies from hybrid crosses [62].

The conformation of four morphological groups among the 89 clones using ten fruit and seed descriptors confirmed the relevance of traits of these organs for cacao morphological classification as has been previously described [5, 16, 63–66]. Interestingly, clones with high ancestry proportion to certain Upper Amazon Forastero lineages (G4WARD members, Fig. 2) were differentiated from clones with high contribution of Amelonado ancestry, also a Forastero ancestry (G3WARD members, Fig. 2). This result suggests that the list of ten descriptors used for clustering in this study would be good enough to properly differentiate these ancestries. (Figs. 2 and 3). However, more data are required to confirm these findings and to apply them to a broader scenario.

Pattern of variation among the studied cacao clones considering both genetic and morphoagronomical traits

The identification of positive correlations between individual heterozygosity and the eight quantitative selected descriptors (Table 4) allowed the detection of a pattern of variation among the studied clones based on both genetic and morphological traits. According to this pattern, clones with high values in agronomical-relevant descriptors (e.g. seed and fruit weigth-related) (Fig. 3), showed high values of individual heterozygosity and tended to be hybrids, especially of the Trinitario type (G1WARD members); while clones with low values of these descriptors, showed low individual heterozygosities and had high membership coefficients (Q > 0.7) to cacao ancestries associated to Forastero morphological group: Amelonado, Contamana, Iquitos and Marañón (G3WARD and G4WARD members).

Dias et al. [67] described positive correlations between genetic distances of the parents and the seeds fresh weight per fruit and per plant of the progeny, during the identification of progenitors for genetic improvement in cacao. These correlations were associated to the expression of heterosis (hybrid vigor) in cacao. The exploitation of heterosis to achieve hybrid plants with better performance has been described in cacao genetic improvement programs worldwide [20, 21, 68].

The prevalence of hybrid plants in the G1WARD group, especially of the Trinitario type, is consistent with the origin of these clones in cacao breeding programs both in Cuba and other countries. As these programs were probably under the premise of achieving heterosis, therefore, they would have led to obtain plants with high heterozygosity and high productive performance [21, 32, 33, 69].

The presence in G3WARD and G4WARD groups of well-known Forastero cacao clones such SCA’s, EEG-27 and EET-48 is consistent with the genetic and morphoagronomical traits described for these groups [5, 70]. Plants from Forastero morphological group are characterized by small flat seeds and low pod filling [5, 6, 64]. This could explain the low values of seed traits found in both groups (Fig. 3). Likewise, these results were consistent with the low heterozygosity found in plants with high membership (Q > 0,7) to any cacao ancestry group, in comparison with the hybrids [17].

Five Cuban clones with light or pale colored cotyledons (white and light purple) were located in G3WARD which revealed an association among them as this descriptor was not selected for clustering. This trait is related to a better sensorial quality of cocoa and is common in Criollo cacaos, though it has also been observed in Amelonado and Trinitario [10, 15, 53, 71]. These five clones showed an average membership to Amelonado of 0.775, therefore the light-colored cotyledons in Cuban clones were more related to this ancestry. This was particularly interesting because Amelonado is considered as a traditional cacao cultivar along with Criollo and Nacional ancestries [72] and it could reveal new insights about the cacao cultivars firstly introduced in Baracoa region.

Cuban clones with high membership to Amelonado lineage identified in the CNGB were prospected in farms from Baracoa and some of these clones were classified as traditional Cuban cacao. These are very old plants found in the farms and supposedly are the closest ones to the primary used for cacao farming in the region, and probably in Cuba. The survival of these plants in cacao plantations is frequently associated to their good sensorial quality as farmers use them for selfconsumption [29, 31, 61].

These results revealed Amelonado as the more common ancestry among the traditional cacao varieties from Baracoa conserved at CNGB and suggested this lineage was among the ones primary introduced in the region, and probably in Cuba, for cacao farming purposes. These findings agreed with the presence of plants with high membership to Amelonado ancestry in farms from Baracoa [37]. Furthermore, it was suggested that plants with high Amelonado proportion from Martinique may produce cocoa with attractive sensorial properties [73], which could support the aforementioned five clones, along with Cuban traditional cacao, as potential sources of high-quality cocoa.

The identification of a pattern of variation among the 89 studied clones, and probably in the CNGB, on the basis of cacao ancestries and morphoagronomical data allowed a better description of the diversity of cacao clones at CNGB. Additionally, it helped in the identification of groups of accessions carrying attractive traits for designing new breeding strategies in the ongoing cacao improvement program to provide new varieties to the cacao agroindustry in Baracoa region.

Clones with the highest potential for breeding on the basis of both cacao ancestry and agronomical-relevant descriptors

The selection of cacao clones as parents for breeding requires several rounds of evaluation considering different aspects. However, a detailed initial characterization of the genotypes to be used for improvement purposes constitutes an asset for the proper development of the breeding program [19, 21]. In our case, members of G1WARD group showed attractive traits such as high values of seeds fresh weight per fruit and fresh weight of one hundred seeds, and included eight clones from COLCCV which are recognized by their good agronomical performance in Cuba [30, 36]. Clones of this group were compared on the basis of cacao ancestry and agronomical-relevant descriptors to identify clones with the highest potential for breeding and the clones from COLCCV were used as references in the comparison.

The eight cacao clones from the COLCCV had low genetic variability (six were of the Trinitario type) and high values of seeds fresh weight and fresh weight of one hundred seeds (Table 5). The low genetic diversity of COLCCV clones is consistent with the poor use of cacao genetic diversity for production purposes that threatens the sustainability of cacao agroindustry worldwide [3, 19]. The persistence in the use of COLCCV clones in Cuban cacao farming practices could lead to plantations with low diversity and increased vulnerability to biotic and abiotic stresses, including disease outbreaks, which are becoming more frequent because of climate change [1].

The seven Cuban clones classified as “Similar to COLCCV” were more diverse in terms of cacao ancestry than COLCCV clones and showed similar values in agronomical-relevant traits. The higher genetic diversity of these Cuban clones, supported their use either for the ongoing breeding program and probably for the development of a cacao sustainable production system with more diversity and putatively better adapted to environmental changes. In addition, these clones attest the feasibility of using genetic resources available in Cuba for cacao breeding by crossing clones with attractive genetic and morphological profiles.

Interestingly, all clones in comparison had a good performance considering the fresh weight of one hundred seeds as their values varied between 327.0–513.8 g (Table 5). This range represented values of fresh weight of a single seed higher than 2.86 g, which is equivalent to an average weight of one fermented dried bean of 1 g, the lower limit commonly accepted for cacao bean commercial quality [6]. However, the use of clones not included in COLCCV for breeding, requires a thorough characterization based on agronomical traits such as vigor, yield and disease resistance/susceptibility among others. These evaluations should be performed at different locations to assess phenotype stability [8, 21]. Positive results in the evaluations could finally support the use of some of these clones for the aforementioned purpose.

The sensorial quality of cocoa is another important aspect of cacao improvement as it could lead to increased incomes from cacao production. The demand for high quality cacao, such as fine and flavor, has increased at a higher rate than that for low quality cacao [12]. For low producing countries such as Cuba, which is not recognized as an exporter of fine and flavor cacao [11], an increase in the sensorial quality of cocoa could positively impact both the profitability and sustainability of cacao productions.

In Cuba, cacao improvement focused on sensorial quality has been hampered by the lack of progenitors carrying this trait, especially of the Criollo type. Cacao clone OC-61 was among the accessions classified as “Partly similar to COLCCV”. Clones of “OC” serie (Ocumare) have been considered Criollo, but often without any genetic evidence [10]; actually OC-61 individuals at CNGB had not been properly described. Morphoagronomical characterization of OC-61 conserved at CNGB revealed the presence of small fruits with a low number of large “plump” seeds carrying cotyledons with light color (white and light purple) (Table 5). This trait combination is usually related to Criollo cacao and two types are recognized: “true or ancient” and “modern” [10, 74]. The former refers to plants with high membership to the Criollo ancestry genetic group as described by Motamayor et al. [17] and few true Criollo clones are conserved in genebanks. The second goup includes plants displaying some morphological traits of ancient Criollos, but they are indeed Trinitarios, relatively close to Criollos. The differences between the commercial products from both types of Criollos remain unknown [10].

OC-61 had the highest membership coefficient to Criollo ancestry (0.656), not only of the clones in comparison but also for all the CNGB sequenced plants [37]. Therefore, this clone was the closest one to Criollos, on the basis of both genetic and morphoagronomical traits, and is probably a modern Criollo. The higher sensorial quality of Criollos [8] supports the use of this clone in the breeding program focused on cocoa quality in Cuba.

Another ancestry with recognized high cocoa quality is the Nacional lineage from Ecuador either in high membership plants or hybrids [9, 75]. Eight clones used in this study showed some ancestry proportion from Nacional lineage, ranging from 0.297 to 0.636, (Fig. 1) and one of them (EICB-174) had morphological traits similar to those of the COLCCV clones (Table 5). Sensorial characterization of these accessions could reveal their actual potential for breeding focused on cocoa quality. Similarly, the five EICB clones with light cotyledon colors and high Amelonado membership could be used in cacao improvement based on quality, once they prove value for this goal. In addition, the use of these clones in cacao improvement efforts could also contribute to broaden the genetic base of the breeding program which has largely relied on Trinitario cacaos and two or three Upper Amazon Forastero clones [30, 32, 33].

Conclusions

The wide morphoagronomical variability of most fruit and seed descriptors among the 89 studied cacao clones from CNGB was in agreement with their genetic diversity based on membership coefficients to cacao ancestry genetic groups and constitutes a favorable scenario for new developments within the cacao breeding program in Cuba. The seeds fresh weight per fruit and fresh weight of one hundred seeds of seven Cuban clones were similar to the COLCCV clones but they were more diverse in terms of membership to cacao ancestry genetic groups. These seven clones might contribute to the design of new strategies in the breeding program to develop varieties better adapted to local conditions and more resilient to future environmental changes. OC-61, as well as clones with Nacional ancestry and those combining the Amelonado lineage and light-colored cotyledons, are national cacao genetic resources that could putatively produce high quality cocoa. Cacao plants with this property are very much needed for improvement focus on cocoa sensorial quality and could contribute to increase the profitability and sustainability of cacao production in Cuba in the middle and long terms.
