Relationship between intraoperative hypertension and postoperative ischemic stroke in neurosurgery—a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yu-Mei Feng, Qian-Yun Pang, Ya-Jun Yang, Hong-Liang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative ischemic stroke is a severe complication in neurosurgery, while intraoperative hypertension is a frequent event. However, their clinical relationship remains poorly understood.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed data from patients with brain tumors undergoing craniotomies between January 2017 and December 2023 at Chongqing University Cancer Hospital. Multivariate logistic regression identified risk factors for postoperative stroke. Intraoperative hypertension was defined as a mean arterial pressure (MAP) > 105 mmHg, with indices including peak MAP, duration, and area under the curve (AUC). Restricted cubic spline analysis with three knots (10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles) was performed. The outcome was postoperative in-hospital acute ischemic stroke. The risk of stroke was assessed by quartile categorization of hypertension indices.ResultsA total of 1,786 patients were included in the final analysis. Intraoperative hypertension was identified as a risk factor for postoperative ischemic stroke, alongside age and intraoperative crystalloid infusion rate. Quatile analysis revealed the following risk thresholds of intraoperative hypertension: 1) peak value > 123 mmHg (aOR: 2.118, 95% CI: 1.360–3.297); 2) duration > 5 min (aOR: 1.604, 95% CI: 1.033–2.491); 3) AUC > 530 mmHg*min (aOR: 1.811, 95% CI: 1.165–2.816). Intraoperative hypertension was not significantly associated with postoperative stroke risk.ConclusionIntraoperative hypertension is a significant predictor of postoperative ischemic stroke in neurosurgical patients, with peak value, the duration, and AUC all demonstrating independent predictive values. These findings underscore the critical need for vigilant intraoperative blood pressure monitoring and management to mitigate stroke risk in neurosurgical patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13741-025-00600-1.

Introduction

Perioperative stroke represents a severe complication, with a notably higher incidence in neurosurgery compared to non-cardiac and non-neurological surgeries (Mashour et al. 2014; Vascular Events in Noncardiac Surgery Patients Cohort Evaluation(VISION) Study Investigators et al. 2019; Batman et al. 2009; Mashour et al. 2011). The majority of these events are ischemic strokes, posing significant challenges for both prevention and treatment (Ng et al. 2011).

Chronic hypertension can lead to impaired cerebral blood flow auto-regulation, resulting in subsquent cerebral hypoperfusion (Hoffman et al. 1981; Muller et al. 2012). Although intraoperative hypertension is typically transient, it has been linked to postoperative acute kidney injury and increased mortality in noncardiac surgeries (Reich et al. 2002; Tang et al. 2023). In neurosurgical contexts, the association between intraoperative hypertension and postoperative stroke remains poorly defined. Intraoperative hypotension has been associated with perioperative stroke and mortality in non-cardiac surgery (Monk et al. 2015; Bijker et al. 2012; Mazzeffi et al. 2021; Bijker and Gelb 2013), though conflicting evidence exists (Hsieh et al. 2016; Wongtangman et al. 2021). A critical unresolved question in neurosurgery is identifying the duration and severity thresholds of intraoperative hypertension or hypotension that significantly increase the risk of postoperative stroke.

This retrospective cohort study analyzed the data from patients undergoing elective craniotomy for brain tumors, to evaluate the relationship between intraoperative hypertension/hypotension and postoperative stroke, and further determine the thresholds that significantly increase the risk of postoperative stroke.

Methods

Research design

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Chongqing University Cancer Hospital (Approval No. CZLS2024020-A) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Patient informed consent was waived due to the anonymized nature of the data.

Study participants

The study included adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) with brain tumors who underwent elective craniotomies at Chongqing University Cancer Hospital between January 2017 and December 2023. The inclusion criteria were: (1) elective surgery; (2) ASA physical status classification of 2–4; and (3) procedures performed under general anesthesia. The exclusion criteria included: (1) emergency surgeries; (2) uncontrolled preoperative hypertension (defined as systolic blood pressure > 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure > 90 mmHg) after admission; (3) preoperative coma; (4) clinically manifest preoperative intracranial hypertension (ICD-10 code: G93.2); (5) prior stroke (ICD-10 codes: I64.0, I63.9, I63.8, I63.4); (6) missing intraoperative blood pressure records; and (7) incomplete data for stroke diagnosis. For patients with multiple craniotomies during the study period, only data from the initial procedure were analyzed.

Data collection

Patient data were extracted from the electronic medical record system for individuals diagnosed with brain tumors who underwent craniotomy at Chongqing University Cancer Hospital between January 2017 and September 2023 and met the inclusion criteria. The collected data included: (1) demographic characteristics [age, sex, body mass index (BMI), ASA physical status classification]; (2) comorbidities [coronary artery disease (CAD), hypertension, arrhythmia, diabetes mellitus (DM), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), thyroid disease, renal disease, and hepatic disease]; (3) smoking status; (4) preoperative long-term medication (including antihypertensive agents, antiplatelet drugs, thyroid medications [thyroxine or methimazole], and hypoglycemic agents); (5) preoperative laboratory parameters [Hemoglobin (Hb), blood glucose, and albumin (ALB)]; and (6) intraoperative variables (surgical duration, surgical site, estimated blood loss, infusion rate, and transfusion). Preexisting comorbidities were identified using ICD-10 codes and were included as potential confounding variables in the analysis.

Blood pressure monitoring

Arterial catheterization was performed in all patients for continuous blood pressure (BP) monitoring, with data recorded every 5 min throughout the surgical procedure. Intraoperative hypertension was defined as a mean arterial pressure (MAP) > 105 mmHg at any time during surgery, based on its association with postoperative acute kidney injury (Tang et al. 2023). Intraoperative hypotension was defined as MAP < 65 mmHg at any time during surgery (Weinberg et al. 2022), while MAP values between 65 and105 mmHg were considered normal. In the electronic data, MAP values < 30 mmHg or > 180 mmHg were considered artifacts and excluded from analysis (Weinberg et al. 2022). The following indices were used to characterize intraoperative hypertension: (1) peak MAP value (mmHg); (2) cumulative duration of hypertension exposure (minutes); (3) cumulative area under the curve (AUC) of hypertension (mmHg*min), the calculation formula was (MAP—105) multiplied by the time above the threshold, which reflects both the magnitude and duration of blood pressure elevation. The following indices were used to characterize intraoperative hypotension: (1) the nadir value of MAP value (mmHg); (2) cumulative duration of hypotension exposure (minutes); (3) cumulative area under the curve (AUC) of hypotension (mmHg*min) calculated using the formula: (65—MAP) multiplied by the time spent below the threshold. This metric reflects both the magnitude and duration of the blood pressure decrease.

Outcome

The outcome was postoperative ischemic stroke (ICD-10 codes: I64.04, I63.902, I63.903, I63.801, I63.904, I63.401). The stroke refers to a new diagnosis of acute ischemic stroke following surgery during hospitalization. The diagnosis was confirmed and validated through medical record review and brain MRI results.

Statistic analysis

Continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range). Those with a normal distribution were compared using the t-test, and those with a non-normal distribution were compared using the Mann–Whitney test. Categorized variables were presented as n (%) and compared using Fisher’s exact test.

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent risk factors for postoperative ischemic stroke. The odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were used to present the risk associated with each variable. Three knots from restricted cubic spline analysis were set and located at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles, and the indices of intraoperative hypertension/hypotension were categorized into quartiles. The OR and 95% CI for the risk of stroke in quartiles of the indices of intraoperative hypertension/hypotension were estimated and adjusted for the covariates listed in Table 1. Patients with missing data on intraoperative blood pressure or postoperative stroke were excluded. For continuous variables exhibiting less than 5% missing data, we employed multiple imputation (10 iterations) to handle the missing values. Many patients experienced both intraoperative hypertension and hypotension. To explore the impact of intraoperative hypotension on the association between intraoperative hypertension and postoperative stroke, an interaction analysis was conducted, and the joint effects of intraoperative hypotension and the indices of intraoperative hypertension were analyzed. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis and re-evaluated the results after excluding patients with concomitant chronic hypertension.

Data analyses were performed using Stata 16 (Stata Corp LP, USA) and R software 4.12 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). All tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 1912 patients met the inclusion criteria. After excluding 11 patients undergoing emergency procedures, 36 with uncontrolled chronic hypertension, 21 with preexisting stroke, and 58 patients with missing intraoperative BP data, 1786 patients remained for univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis (Fig. 1). Of these, 1626 patients underwent supratentorial surgery, while 160 underwent infratentorial surgery. The baseline characteristics of these patients are presented in Table 1. All patients underwent central venous catheterization, with norepinephrine administration when necessary to maintain normal blood pressure. Intraoperative end-tidal carbon dioxide (etCO2) levels were kept between 30 and 40 mmHg. The lowest MAP recorded was 30 mmHg, while the highest was 159 mmHg among all patients during surgery. Logistic regression analysis

The logistic regression analysis identified three independent risk factors for postoperative ischemic stroke (intraoperative hypertension, advanced age, and intraoperative crystalloid infusion rate). Notably, intraoperative hypotension (MAP < 65 mmHg) was not associated with increased stroke risk. Surprisingly, preexisting chronic hypertension emerged as a protective factor against postoperative ischemic stroke (Supplementary Table S1). Among the study cohort, 210 patients (11.8%) experienced postoperative ischemic stroke.

Restrictive cubic spline analysis

Restrictive cubic spline analysis demonstrated a non-linear association between intraoperative hypertension and postoperative stroke (Fig. 2). The indices of intraoperative hypertension were categorized into quartiles based on the knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles. The thresholds of intraoperative hypertension were identified: peak value was 123 mmHg (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 2.118, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.360–3.297); the duration was 5 min (aOR: 1.604, 95% CI: 1.033–2.491); the AUC was 530 mmHg*min (aOR: 1.811, 95% CI: 1.165–2.816). Sensitivity analysis

After excluding 760 patients with combined chronic hypertension, 1026 patients without chronic hypertension remained for analysis. The incidence of postoperative stroke was 14.6%. The baseline characteristics of these patients are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. The thresholds of intraoperative hypertension associated with increased stroke risk were identified: the duration was 55 min (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 1.760, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.062 to 2.917); the AUC was 6075 mmHg*min (aOR: 1.862, 95% CI: 1.005 to 2.623). However, the peak value of intraoperative hypertension was not significantly associated with postoperative stroke (Table 4). Discussion

Our study demonstrates that intraoperative hypertension is a significant risk factor for postoperative ischemic stroke in neurosurgical patients. The peak value (> 123 mmHg), the duration of exposure (> 5 min) and the area under the curve (AUC) (> 530 mmHg*min) independently predict this outcome, while intraoperative hypotension showed no significant association with stroke risk in our cohort.

Perioperative stroke rates are consistently higher in neurosurgery than in non-cardiac, non-neurologic surgeries (Mashour et al. 2014; Vascular Events in Noncardiac Surgery Patients Cohort Evaluation(VISION) Study Investigators et al. 2019; Batman et al. 2009; Mashour et al. 2011). While intraoperative hypotension has been linked to adverse outcomes in general surgery (Monk et al. 2015; Bijker et al. 2012; Mazzeffi et al. 2021; Bijker and Gelb 2013), its clinical impact remains debated (Hsieh et al. 2016; Wongtangman et al. 2021). Our findings specifically highlight intraoperative hypertension, but not hypotension, as the predominant hemodynamic risk factor for postoperative stroke, aligning with evidence linking intraoperative hypertension to acute kidney injury and mortality in non-cardiac surgery (Reich et al. 2002; Tang et al. 2023), while acknowledging contradictory reports (Monk et al. 2015; Shimada et al. 2022). Our findings indicate that intraoperative hypertension can predict the risk of postoperative stroke when the duration exceeds 5 min or the AUC exceeds 530 mmHg*min or the peak value of hypertension exceeds 123 mmHg, suggesting that intraoperative hypertension should be avoided in neurosurgical procedures.

Intraoperative MAP < 65 mmHg is widely accepted as the threshold for intraoperative hypotension (Wongtangman et al. 2021). In this study, we defined intraoperative hypertension as MAP > 105 mmHg at any time during neurosurgery, consistent with prior research that associated this threshold with postoperative acute kidney injury in laparoscopic surgery (Tang et al. 2023). Our study demonstrated that intraoperative hypotension did not modulate the relationship between intraoperative hypertension and postoperative stroke. The effect of intraoperative hypertension on postoperative stroke risk was independent of hypotension—patients with concurrent hypotension exhibited the same pattern of risk based on hypertension duration or severity.

Animal and clinical studies suggest that chronic hypertension shifts the range of cerebral blood flow autoregulation (Hoffman et al. 1981; Muller et al. 2012; Ikeda et al. 2006; Cipolla et al. 2018), potentially leading to arteriole constriction and hypoperfusion (Cipolla et al. 2018). Paradoxically, our study identified chronic hypertension as a protective factor against postoperative stroke in neurosurgery, though its mechanism remains unclear and requires validation in other surgical contexts. In neurosurgery, transient intraoperative hypertension may impair cerebral autoregulation, trigger vasoconstrictor release, and compromise cerebral blood flow stability, thereby increasing ischemic stroke risk. Despite evidence linking hypotension to cerebral hypoperfusion (Bijker et al. 2012; Mazzeffi et al. 2021; Bijker and Gelb 2013), our study failed to replicate this association, possibly due to arterial monitoring artifacts (eg., zero-point misplacement or measurement bias). Direct arterial pressure reading during hypotension may underestimate true severity (Wax et al. 2011), highlighting the need for accurate monitoring. Therefore, in neurosurgical surgery, both hypertension and hypotension should be avoided, and blood pressure stability should be maintained to minimize fluctuations.

Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. As a single-center cohort study, caution should be exercised when generalizing the results to other institutions. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these findings. The outcome measured in this study was postoperative in-hospital ischemic stroke, which represents a relatively short-term outcome. Additionally, we defined intraoperative hypertension as MAP > 105 mmHg, but definitions vary across studies, leading to conflicting results regarding the relationship between intraoperative hypertension and postoperative outcomes (Reich et al. 2002; Tang et al. 2023; Monk et al. 2015; Shimada et al. 2022). Thirdly, the variables such as tumor vascularity, pathology, size, proximity or invasion of major cerebral arteries, the extent of surrounding edema, temporary vessel occlusion, vasoactive drug use, and anesthetic depth, which might potentially influence the outcome, were not systematically recorded, introducing potential bias.

Despite these limitations, our findings provide compelling evidence that intraoperative hypertension should be actively managed in neurosurgical patients to mitigate postoperative stroke risk. Currently, this critical factor remains underrecognized in clinical practice, highlighting the urgent need for standardized definitions and targeted intervention protocols.

Conclusion

Intraoperative hypertension is a significant risk factor for postoperative stroke in neurosurgical patients, with its peak value, duration, and AUC all serving as predictive indicators. Therefore, heightened clinical attention and proactive management of intraoperative hypertension are warranted to mitigate stroke risk during neurosurgical procedures.
