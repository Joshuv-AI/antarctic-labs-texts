Unveiling the crucial genomic players in pulmonary tuberculosis-associated ferroptosis through bioinformatics scrutiny
Authors: Xing Wei, Lan Zheng, Yifeng Yu, Ge Xu, Huijie Bao, Xia Wu, Cuo Qing, Dacai Gong
Published: 2025

Background

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB), is a persistent infectious disease primarily impacting the Lungs. The 2022 Global Tuberculosis Report from the World Health Organization (WHO) revealed 10.6 million new TB cases globally in 2021, of which a large proportion from Asian countries bore a significant burden [1].

Early diagnosis and effective treatments are pivotal for curbing TB transmission and mortality rates [2]. Despite the availability of various clinical diagnostic methods for MTB, several challenges persist. Conventional diagnostic methods, such as mycobacterial culture and acid-fast staining, suffer from prolonged detection times and low sensitivity, leading to diagnostic challenges and suboptimal patient outcomes. While mycobacterial culture is considered the gold standard approach, its lengthy turnaround time of several weeks to detect bacterial growth imposes a significant limitation. Acid-fast staining, though widely used, suffers from low sensitivity, and results from both methods may not always be consistent. Furthermore, reliance on clinicians’ subjective judgment, coupled with imaging findings, often falls short of ensuring accurate diagnoses, leading to approximately 30% of patients not achieving full recovery from the disease [3, 4].

In recent years, ferroptosis has emerged as a notable phenomenon in medical research, delineating a distinctive mode of cell demise. Unlike conventional cell death mechanisms, ferroptosis hinges on the perilous accumulation of iron-dependent lipid peroxides and subsequent lipid peroxidation [5, 6]. Extensive studies have underscored the nexus between ferroptosis and an array of maladies spanning liver diseases, cancers, kidney disorders, and beyond, with ferroptosis-associated genes evolving as promising prognostic indicators [7–10].

Despite this, research into the implications of ferroptosis-related genes in TB remains scarce. Consequently, these genes hold the potential to serve as molecular signposts for the early diagnosis and prognosis of pulmonary TB (PTB) in the future. Leveraging transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatics tools, our investigation aims to pinpoint differentially expressed genes in TB patients. Additionally, we harnessed the FerrDb database to sift through TB-related ferroptosis genes, identifying pivotal candidates entwined with the ferroptosis cascade. Subsequent validation of these key genes using whole blood samples from TB patients lays a robust groundwork for advancing early diagnostic modalities and refining clinical interventions for TB management.

Methods

Source of specimen

Initially, we procured whole blood specimens from three individuals who were clinically diagnosed with TB and three healthy volunteers at the People’s Hospital of Pidu District in Chengdu, China. These samples were then dispatched to Shanghai Sangong Bioengineering Technology & Services Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for comprehensive gene transcription sequencing analysis (RNA sequencing). Detailed clinical information pertaining to these six samples can be found in Table 1. Subsequently, another set of whole blood samples was gathered from an additional 10 TB patients and 10 healthy volunteers for the purpose of validating key genes, serving as controls through RNA extraction. Diagnosis of TB relied solely on thorough examinations, including positive sputum TB DNA tests and identification of lung lesions via CT scans. To ensure the experiment’s integrity, individuals with confounding comorbidities were excluded from participation. Healthy control samples were obtained from individuals undergoing routine physical examinations, with those displaying pulmonary infection or other illnesses being excluded from the study. Adherence to the ethical standards set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki was strictly observed, and the research protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of Chengdu Pidu District People’s Hospital. Prior to enrollment, written informed consent was obtained from all participants, including patients and healthy volunteers.

Reagent and instrument

A Spin Column Blood Total RNA Purification Kit (B518653) was procured from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and a PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit and TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNaseH Plus) were acquired from Takara Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing). Instruments utilized in this study comprised the QuantStudio™ 3 RT-PCR system and Arktik™ TCA0096 thermal cycler (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc, USA), Prism™ C2500 low-temperature centrifuge (Labnet, USA), XH-B vortex mixer (HINOTEK, China), UPH-Ⅱ−10T ultrapure water manufacturing system (Haicoer, China), SYQ-DSX-280B portable stainless steel pressure steam sterilizer (Hanchen, China), and a biological safety cabinet (Haier, China).

RNA sequencing

For RNA sequencing, we employed the Illumina X-Ten platform (Illumina, USA) to generate raw image data from the six specimens. Subsequently, these images underwent conversion into raw reads via CASAVA base recognition analysis and were stored in FASTQ format. Trimmomatic, a flexible read trimming tool for Illumina sequencing data, was applied to eliminate sequences containing N bases, adapters, and regions of low quality, thereby yielding clean data. Following a meticulous assessment of sequencing data quality, we proceeded to analyze the gene expression levels. RNA-seq data were normalized using the voom transformation implemented in the limma package. Since covariates such as age and sex were balanced between groups (as shown in Table 1), they were not included as adjustment variables in the differential expression model.

Screening and analysis of key ferroptosis genes

Differential expression analysis of ferroptosis-related genes was performed using the Limma package from Bioconductor to compare mRNA expression levels between the experimental and control groups based on genome sequencing data. The analysis was repeated with age and sex as covariates; however, effect sizes for the top 50 genes changed by less than 3%, so the original results are retained. Genes were considered differentially expressed if they met the criteria of |log₂FC| >2 and P value < 0.05. Supplementary Table S1 provides the complete List of sequenced genes and the 2,241 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), along with their log₂ fold changes and adjusted P values. R package facilitated the generation of volcano plots to visualize the outcomes. In total, 259 ferroptosis-related genes were amassed from the FerrDb database (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb/). Gene overlaps were visualized using Venny 2.1, an online tool for generating Venn diagrams (Oliveros, 2007). Leveraging the Venny online drawing tool Venny 2.1(https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/), overlapping genes within the amalgamated iron death dataset were delineated, followed by mapping utilizing Wayne’s methodology.

For functional annotation and pathway elucidation, differentially expressed genes underwent Gene Ontology (GO) analysis as well as Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway exploration utilizing R language and the “clusterProfiler” package. Significance was determined with a false discovery rate threshold of < 0.05. The investigation of gene functions and interactions was conducted through STRING 10.0 (http://string-db.org), applying a minimum interaction score of > 0.4 to construct protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks. Subsequently, Cytoscape 6 and MCODE algorithms were employed to pinpoint densely connected subnetwork modules in PPIs, with the highest-scoring module gene being identified as the key module gene.

Validation using the external dataset GSE83456

The GSE83456 dataset, comprising peripheral blood transcriptome profiles from 61 healthy controls and 45 patients with pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB), was obtained from the GEO database. Key ferroptosis-related genes identified in the discovery cohort were validated in this independent dataset. Gene expression distributions were visualized using violin plots generated with the ggplot2 package in R, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed using the pROC package to evaluate diagnostic performance.

Experimental verification

Total RNA extraction of the experimental specimens.

For the RNA extraction process, 200 µl of anticoagulated whole blood samples was procured from each of the 20 participants. Total RNA was extracted using standard protocols, utilizing a Spin Column Total RNA Purification Kit (Sangong Biotech) and strictly adhering to the manufacturer guidelines. The acquired total RNA was preserved at −80 °C to ensure its integrity for subsequent experiments. To access the complete gene sequences, qRT-PCR was employed, referencing the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database of the United States. Primer Premier, the most comprehensive software for designing and analyzing PCR primers, was instrumental in developing and evaluating gene-specific primers. These primers were synthesized and purified through ULTRAPAGE (Sangong Biotech). Refer to Table 2 for detailed primer specifications.

Discussion

The ongoing global threat of TB necessitates continual advancements in diagnostic and treatment strategies to mitigate its impact. Accurate identification and prompt management of latent TB infection are pivotal in reducing the incidence of active PTB cases [11]. While conventional detection methods such as acid-fast staining and culture of MTB sputum specimens remain fundamental, emerging technologies such as fluorescence staining and nucleic acid detection offer improved sensitivity and efficiency. However, despite these advancements, limitations persist, highlighting the ongoing need for innovative diagnostic approaches [12].

In recent years, the exploration of non-sputum markers for TB diagnosis has gained traction as a rapid diagnostic tool [13, 14]. Studies have identified specific blood biomarkers with diagnostic and prognostic potential for PTB, offering promising avenues for early detection and intervention. Notably, the integration of transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses has shown promise in identifying “integrated” biomarkers, presenting novel diagnostic paradigms for TB.

Our research focuses on the study of ferroptosis-related genes as biomarkers in the diagnosis of tuberculosis. Ferroptosis is driven by disrupted redox homeostasis, marked by excessive oxidant production that surpasses cellular antioxidant defenses [15–17]. This imbalance arises from the dysregulation of enzymes involved in free radical metabolism and lipid peroxidation. The ferroptotic process is tightly regulated by multiple mechanisms, including epigenetic modifications, transcriptional control, and post-translational regulation [18]. Moreover, the emergence of databases such as FerrDb, which house comprehensive collections of ferroptosis-related biomarkers and regulatory factors, has opened new avenues for investigating molecular markers for TB diagnosis. The establishment of the FerrDb database marks a significant milestone in the field of ferroptosis research, providing a comprehensive repository of ferroptosis-related biomarkers, regulatory factors, and their associations with various diseases. Since its inception in 2020, FerrDb has rapidly evolved, housing an extensive collection of data encompassing 259 related genes, 111 markers, and 95 ferroptosis-disease associations [19]. This database serves as a valuable resource for researchers and clinicians alike, offering insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying ferroptosis and its implications for disease pathogenesis. By compiling and curating a vast array of ferroptosis-related information, FerrDb facilitates knowledge dissemination and accelerates research progress in this burgeoning field.

In our study, differential expression and bioinformatics analyses were conducted to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) pathogenesis and to identify potential biomarkers for TB diagnosis. Firstly, we screened 2,241 differentially expressed genes associated with PTB using a self-established transcription database derived from the whole blood samples of PTB patients. Concurrently, a bioinformatics analysis of 259 ferroptosis genes from the FerrDb database was conducted, resulting in the identification of 20 intersection genes.

Subsequently, we utilized PPI network analysis to prioritize candidate genes, selecting a module with the highest score from the PPI network of the 20 identified genes. Within this module, 16 genes were further screened based on their centrality and connectivity. GO enrichment analysis and KEGG pathway analysis of these genes revealed their involvement in crucial cellular processes, including the regulation of oxygen species and various enzyme activities.

Of particular interest, six densely connected genes were identified from the module with the highest score, encoding proteins implicated in essential cellular pathways such as apoptosis, DNA damage and repair, cell carcinogenesis, and hypoxia signaling. These findings suggest a potential role for these genes in TB pathogenesis and underscore their significance as potential biomarkers for TB diagnosis.

To validate the expression levels of these six genes, we collected blood samples from 10 patients with TB and 10 healthy volunteers. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was performed using designed primers, which revealed significantly higher expression levels of ATF3 and MAPK8 genes in TB patients compared to healthy volunteers (P < 0.05). This experimental validation further corroborates the potential diagnostic utility of ATF3 and MAPK8 as molecular markers for TB.

Overall, our study provides novel insights into the molecular landscape of PTB and highlights ATF3 and MAPK8 as promising candidates for TB diagnosis. Further validation in larger patient cohorts and mechanistic studies elucidating the functional roles of these genes in TB pathogenesis are warranted to translate these findings into clinical practice effectively.

The identification of ATF3 as a ferroptosis-associated gene in tuberculosis (TB) offers important insights into the disease’s underlying pathophysiology. ATF3 is an adaptive stress-response gene involved in regulating inflammation and apoptosis. It is known to be upregulated under oxidative stress, an environment commonly seen in TB infection [20]. In our study, elevated ATF3 expression in TB patients suggests a potential role in modulating immune responses and cell survival during Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection. This highlights ATF3 as not only a candidate diagnostic biomarker but also a possible therapeutic target [21, 22].

Similarly, MAPK8 (also known as JNK), a key component of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway, plays a critical role in cellular responses to stress and inflammation [23]. Its significant upregulation in TB patients implicates it in disease pathogenesis, particularly in relation to ferroptosis, a regulated form of cell death increasingly recognized in host-pathogen interactions. By modulating the balance between survival and death of immune cells, MAPK8 may act as a central regulator in the host response to M. tuberculosis, representing another promising target for therapeutic exploration [24–26].

Furthermore, the association between ferroptosis and various lung diseases, including PTB, underscores the intricate interplay between iron-mediated cell death and inflammation. The potential involvement of ferroptosis in lung lesions during MTB infection suggests its relevance as a therapeutic target in TB management. However, further studies are warranted to elucidate the mechanistic underpinnings of ferroptosis in TB pathogenesis and its implications for therapeutic interventions.

While our study provides valuable insights into potential molecular markers for TB diagnosis, it is not without limitations. The sample size remains modest, warranting validation through larger clinical cohorts to ascertain the diagnostic utility of ATF3 and MAPK8. Additionally, ongoing research efforts should explore the broader landscape of ferroptosis-related genes and their interactions in TB pathogenesis to inform targeted therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings underscore the potential of ATF3 and MAPK8 as diagnostic markers for TB and highlight the intricate interplay between ferroptosis and TB pathogenesis. Continued research endeavors are essential to unravel the complexities of TB pathophysiology and translate these insights into improved diagnostic and therapeutic modalities for TB management.
