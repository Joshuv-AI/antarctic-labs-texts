Coverage-Dependent Free-Energy Bottlenecks in CO Adsorption and Desorption on Ru(0001)
Authors: Jin-En He, Mingjun Yang, Zhe-Ning Chen, Tong Zhu, Jun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The adsorption and desorption of gas-phase molecules on solid surfaces are elementary steps in heterogeneous catalysis. However, capturing these dynamics under finite-temperature and coverage-dependent conditions remains challenging because static models cannot fully describe the coupled motion of adsorbates and surface atoms. In this study, we investigate the adsorption/desorption dynamics of CO on Ru(0001) by combining machine learning potential energy surfaces (ML-PESs) with umbrella sampling molecular dynamics (US-MD). A 2 × 2 × 5 Ru(0001) model containing one CO molecule (0.250 monolayer, ML) is used to analyze temperature-dependent potential of mean force (PMF) profiles, local free-energy landscapes, and molecular orientation distributions. To clarify coverage and finite-size effects, enlarged 4 × 4 × 5 Ru(0001) models containing 1, 4, and 8 CO molecules are used to cover 0.063, 0.250, and 0.500 ML, respectively, while 20 × 20 × 5 Ru(0001) models containing 100 and 200 CO molecules are further examined at 0.250 and 0.500 ML using a fine-tuned DPA-2 potential. The PMF profiles show that CO adsorption is nearly barrierless at low coverage, whereas an adsorption-side free-energy bottleneck becomes most clearly resolved under crowded high-coverage conditions. This bottleneck arises from the cooperative effect of configurational/rotational entropy loss and lateral CO–CO repulsion, with finite-size periodicity and surface coverage modulating the strength of entropic confinement in the PMF profile. Comparison between rigid and relaxed 2 × 2 × 5 surface models further shows that neglecting lattice motion qualitatively preserves the overall temperature-dependent PMF trend but suppresses the small adsorption-side bottleneck relative to the relaxed-surface model. These results highlight the coupled roles of coverage, entropy, lateral adsorbate interactions, and surface lattice degrees of freedom in CO/Ru(0001) gas–surface dynamics.

Introduction

Over the years, experimental techniques have advanced to enable real-time measurement of a catalyst under reaction conditions, known as in situ/operando characterization. , However, in theoretical calculations, the limitations of static models, incomplete phase-space sampling, and the high computational cost of ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) make it challenging to simulate gas–surface dynamics with both high accuracy and sufficient statistical sampling under realistic finite-temperature and coverage-dependent conditions.

As a fundamental step in heterogeneous catalysis, the adsorption and desorption of CO on Ru(0001) have been extensively studied using in situ characterization. Michalk et al. utilized low-energy electron diffraction (LEED) to identify the top site as the preferred adsorption configuration. Regarding dynamics, Pfnür et al. revealed that while CO adsorbs directly at low coverage (θ < 0.2), it must overcome barriers separating chemisorbed and “precursor” states at medium coveragea phenomenon subsequently corroborated by Tang et al. using molecular beam technology. − These experimental observations indicate that the adsorption barrier is not an invariant property of an isolated CO molecule but depends sensitively on surface coverage and adsorbate–adsorbate interactions. At low coverage, the incoming CO molecule can access the metal surface with minimal steric or electrostatic interference. At higher coverage, lateral CO–CO repulsion and restricted orientational freedom may create a crowded approach region before chemisorption, leading to a coverage-dependent free-energy bottleneck. Theoretical efforts have sought to elucidate the origin of these barriers. Doren and Tully demonstrated for the CO/Ni(111) system that entropic effects could induce free-energy barriers even on barrier-free potential energy surfaces (PESs). Similarly, on Ru(0001), Stampfl and Scheffler found that although repulsive interactions create barriers at metastable sites, the most stable top-site pathway has no potential-energy barrier. However, incorporating finite-temperature effects changes this picture. Dell’Angela et al. combined X-ray laser spectroscopy with density functional theory (DFT) to show that distinct free-energy barriers arise from entropic effects. This was further supported by Xin et al., who confirmed that the reduced entropy of the adsorbate leads to temperature-induced desorption barriers. To bridge the gap between microscopic simulations and macroscopic experimental observations, representative experimental findings for the CO/Ru(0001) system, such as LEED characterization of ordered CO adlayers, adsorption/desorption kinetics, temperature-programmed desorption (TPD), molecular-beam adsorption dynamics, and in situ Fourier-transform infrared reflection absorption spectroscopy (FT-IRAS) observations, are summarized in Table S1. Rather than attempting a direct numerical comparison of nonequivalent observables, Table S1 highlights the qualitative and mechanistic correspondence between experimentally established trends and the key computational quantities examined in this work, including coverage-dependent adsorption bottlenecks, lateral adsorbate interactions, finite-temperature adsorption/desorption free energies, and local surface-response effects.

The existence of adsorption barriers for CO on Ru(0001) remains a subject of debate. Réocreux et al., accounting for thermal surface corrugation, reported a small barrier consistent with experimental observations, suggesting that adsorbate interactions and diffusion significantly influence thermodynamic measurements. Conversely, Saalfrank and co-workers argued that such barriers might be artifacts arising from neglecting mode coupling, as their high-dimensional dynamics simulations showed no distinct barrier for CO adsorption or desorption. , More recently, simulations incorporating surface degrees of freedom indicated that adsorbate–adsorbate interactions could induce precursor states, further complicating the understanding of these barriers at varying coverages.

Beyond static models, the cooperative interplay between adsorbates and catalyst surfaces is crucial for understanding heterogeneous catalysis. Spencer proposed that reaction conditions keep catalysts far from equilibrium, giving rise to energetic and metastable local surface environments. This concept has been substantiated by recent advances in machine learning and enhanced sampling. For instance, Jiang and co-workers demonstrated that neglecting lattice motion leads to underestimated desorption entropies and inaccurate rate coefficients. Furthermore, dynamic simulations across various systems have shown that adsorbate–surface coupling can generate fluctuating local coordination environments and surface responses during reactions. − These findings underscore the need to include surface dynamics when describing finite-temperature catalytic surfaces.

Previously, we investigated the diffusion dynamics of CO on Ru(0001) using AIMD combined with integrated tempering sampling (AIMD-ITS) and machine learning potential energy surfaces (ML-PESs). − Inspired by the ML-PES and umbrella sampling (US) strategy , employed by Hu et al. for CO adsorption/desorption on Pt(111), this work combines ML-PES with US-MD to investigate CO adsorption/desorption on Ru(0001) under finite-temperature and coverage-dependent conditions while explicitly incorporating the degrees of freedom of surface atoms. Recent grand-canonical machine-learning-potential approaches, including grand-canonical global optimization methods and replica-exchange grand-canonical simulations, have provided powerful routes for exploring open-system surface thermodynamics, adsorbate-induced restructuring, and chemical-potential-dependent surface phases. − In contrast, the present work focuses on a complementary question: for selected CO/Ru(0001) surface states and coverages, how does the adsorption/desorption process proceed along a continuous free-energy coordinate, and how do temperature, coverage, orientational entropy, lateral CO–CO interactions, and surface lattice motion contribute to the resulting activation free-energy profile?

To address this, the CO coverage is explicitly defined as the ratio of CO molecules to top-layer Ru atoms. Accordingly, a 2 × 2 × 5 Ru(0001) surface containing one CO molecule corresponds to 0.250 ML and serves as a well-sampled periodic reference model. Temperature-dependent thermodynamic properties (300–800 K) were derived from the converged potential of mean force (PMF) profiles utilizing the weighted histogram analysis method (WHAM). , By analyzing local PMF landscapes and CO orientation distributions, we elucidated the microscopic origins of the coverage-dependent entropic–repulsive bottlenecks observed at high temperatures. A comparative analysis between rigid- and relaxed-surface models highlights the critical role of surface lattice motion and local structural fluctuations in adsorption thermodynamics. Furthermore, to distinguish coverage effects from finite-size periodicity, enlarged 4 × 4 × 5 Ru(0001) surfaces containing 1, 4, and 8 CO molecules were constructed, corresponding to 0.063, 0.250, and 0.500 ML, respectively. Large-cell simulations on 20 × 20 × 5 Ru(0001) surfaces containing 100 and 200 CO molecules were further performed using a fine-tuned DPA-2 potential − to assess the robustness of the coverage-dependent trends at 0.250 and 0.500 ML. Crucially, the nominal CO coverage does not uniquely determine the microscopic interaction and entropy environment within a finite periodic cell. Specifically, the 2 × 2 × 5 cell not only imposes a highly ordered CO–CO interaction pattern but also artificially restricts the lateral configurational and orientational fluctuations available to the adsorbate along the adsorption/desorption coordinate. In contrast, an enlarged 4 × 4 × 5 cell at the same nominal 0.250 ML coverage allows multiple CO molecules to sample a broader distribution of local arrangements, thereby partially relaxing both lateral repulsion and finite-size-induced entropic confinement. By linking finite-temperature entropy loss with coverage-dependent lateral adsorbate interactions and finite-size periodicity, this work provides a microscopic framework for understanding adsorption/desorption free-energy bottlenecks in dynamic gas–surface processes, highlighting the indispensable role of treating coverage, entropy, and surface response in a unified manner.

Computational Methods

The overall simulation strategy is outlined in Figure . First, Ru(0001) surface models with diverse lateral sizes and explicit CO coverages were constructed. These include a periodically repeated 2 × 2 × 5 model at 0.250 ML, enlarged 4 × 4 × 5 models at 0.063, 0.250, and 0.500 ML, and large 20 × 20 × 5 models at 0.250 and 0.500 ML. Second, machine-learning potentials were employed to enable finite-temperature MD simulations: the embedded atom neural network (EANN) potential − combined with the many-body expansion strategy ,,− was used for the 2 × 2 × 5 and 4 × 4 × 5 models, while a fine-tuned DPA-2 potential − was utilized for the 20 × 20 × 5 large-cell simulations, both driven by an in-house MD code (the corresponding code and the trained ML-PES parameter models are openly available at GitHub , ). Third, US-MD , was performed along the surface-normal CO centroid-height coordinate, z. The resulting biased trajectories were analyzed using the WHAM , to reconstruct PMF profiles. Finally, the PMF profiles were utilized to extract thermodynamic quantities, rate constants, coverage-dependent PMF bottlenecks, molecular orientation distributions, and local surface response metrics.

Three classes of Ru(0001) surface models were employed for complementary purposes. The original 2 × 2 × 5 Ru(0001) model contains one CO molecule and serves as a well-sampled, periodically repeated 0.250 ML reference system. Enlarged 4 × 4 × 5 Ru(0001) models containing 1, 4, and 8 CO molecules were explicitly constructed to represent coverages of 0.063, 0.250, and 0.500 ML, respectively, thereby enabling a systematic examination of coverage and finite-size effects. Furthermore, large-cell 20 × 20 × 5 Ru(0001) models containing 100 and 200 CO molecules were utilized to rigorously verify whether these coverage-dependent PMF trends persist in substantially larger surface cells. To ensure clarity, the CO coverage is explicitly defined as θCO = N CO/N Ru,top, where N CO is the number of CO molecules and N Ru,top is the number of top-layer Ru atoms. Initial configurations were generated by placing CO molecules at top Ru sites matching the target coverage, with the C–O bonds initially oriented approximately along the surface normal (C atom facing the surface). These configurations were thermally equilibrated at the respective target temperatures prior to US. In the subsequent production runs, the selected CO molecule was biased exclusively along the surface-normal z axis.

To rigorously evaluate the thermodynamics and kinetics of the adsorption/desorption process, key free-energy quantities were explicitly defined according to the specific shape of the PMF profile. Let G ads denote the chemisorbed-state free-energy minimum, G gas denote the gas-phase plateau, and G ‡ denote the PMF maximum when a finite bottleneck is present. When a PMF maximum exists, the adsorption free-energy barrier is defined as ΔG ads ‡ = G ‡-G gas, whereas the desorption free-energy barrier is defined as ΔG des ‡ = G ‡-G ads. Crucially, when no PMF maximum is observed, the adsorption pathway is intrinsically barrierless (ΔG ads ‡ = 0), and the corresponding desorption free-energy cost is quantified strictly by the minimum-to-plateau well depth, ΔG des = G gas – G ads. Based on the computed PMF profiles, the desorption rate constant, k des, was evaluated using standard transition-state theory (TST): 1 kdes=kBThexp(−ΔGdes‡RT) where k B is the Boltzmann constant, h is the Planck constant, and R is the universal gas constant. For monotonic PMF profiles (i.e., barrierless adsorption), the desorption free-energy cost (ΔG des) was utilized in place of the activation barrier in the TST expression.

Comprehensive details are documented in the Supporting Information (Tables S2 and S3). These include the rigorous validation of the ML-PES models against DFT single-point calculations at elevated temperatures, specific US and WHAM parameters for all selected surface models, explicit free-energy zero definitions, and detailed trajectory analysis protocols for apparent thermodynamic decompositions (ΔH ‡, ΔS ‡), Jacobian-corrected orientation distributions, local surface structural responses, and Monte Carlo bootstrap uncertainty estimation.

Results and Discussion

Finite-Temperature Adsorption/Desorption Free Energies at 0.250 ML

The 2 × 2 × 5 Ru(0001) model containing one CO molecule corresponds to an effective coverage of 0.250 ML and represents a well-sampled, periodically ordered reference model for analyzing finite-temperature adsorption/desorption free energies, entropic confinement, and surface-flexibility effects. The thermodynamic profiles derived from the two-layer-relaxed 2 × 2 × 5 surface model are presented in Figure , with convergence tests provided in Figure S2. As illustrated in Figure a, the free-energy curves exhibit two distinct local minima at CO centroid heights of 2.71 and 2.16 Å, respectively. At 300 K, the calculated desorption free energy is 34.26 kcal/mol, which is approximately 3.74 kcal/mol lower than the value reported in previous thermal desorption mass spectrometry studies. , As the temperature increases, CO adsorption becomes thermodynamically less favorable, as reflected by the reduced desorption free-energy cost. At elevated temperatures, a finite PMF bottleneck also emerges along the adsorption/desorption coordinate, reflecting the kinetic free-energy constraint associated with desorption. Experimental TPD studies have reported a CO desorption peak from Ru(0001) at approximately 490 K, placing appreciable CO desorption in the several-hundred-kelvin regime. In the present PMF analysis, the finite-temperature free-energy bottleneck and the corresponding TST-derived desorption rates also become more pronounced over this temperature range. The 490 K peak is therefore utilized here strictly as a qualitative experimental temperature-scale reference for the PMF-derived desorption kinetics.

To further analyze the microscopic origin of the free-energy bottleneck, we examined the relative enthalpic and entropic contributions to the temperature-dependent desorption free-energy quantity. At 300 K, the PMF profile of the 2 × 2 × 5 model does not show a distinct adsorption bottleneck; adsorption from the gas-like region to the chemisorbed state is therefore barrierless. However, desorption is not barrierless at this temperature. Because no additional PMF maximum is present, the desorption free-energy cost is represented by the well depth between the gas-like reference and the chemisorbed minimum. This minimum-to-plateau PMF well depth was utilized to define the 300 K desorption free-energy cost, which serves exclusively as the baseline for the relative thermodynamic comparison in Figure c. At 400 K and above, a finite PMF bottleneck appears, and the desorption activation free energy is evaluated from the PMF maximum relative to the chemisorbed-state minimum. As shown in Figure c and Table S5, the temperature dependence of the free-energy bottleneck contains a significant entropic component. In the periodically repeated 2 × 2 × 5 model, one CO molecule represents an ordered 0.250 ML adlayer under short lateral periodicity. Such a finite periodic cell not only imposes a regular lateral CO environment but can also concentrate the loss of lateral configurational and orientational freedom experienced by the adsorbate along the adsorption/desorption coordinate. Consequently, the entropy loss associated with CO approaching or leaving the surface can be amplified in the 2 × 2 × 5 model, rendering it visible as a finite-temperature free-energy bottleneck. Desorption rate constants from 300 to 800 K were calculated using transition-state theory (TST) and exhibit Arrhenius behavior (Figure d). As part of this continuous temperature dependence, k des increases from 3.82 × 10–6 s–1 at 400 K to 6.16 × 10–2 s–1 at 500 K, indicating that desorption becomes more kinetically accessible on this temperature scale.

Coverage and Finite-Size Effects on the Adsorption Bottleneck

To clarify whether the free-energy bottleneck observed in the 0.250 ML 2 × 2 × 5 model is an intrinsic finite-temperature effect or is significantly influenced by coverage and finite-size periodicity, enlarged 4 × 4 × 5 Ru(0001) surfaces containing 1, 4, and 8 CO molecules were simulated, corresponding to coverages of 0.063, 0.250, and 0.500 ML, respectively. These enlarged-cell simulations enable a systematic examination of the entropic confinement identified in the 2 × 2 × 5 model across diverse lateral adsorbate environments. In this context, the entropy loss observed in Figure should not be interpreted simply as the isolated rotational entropy penalty of a single CO molecule. Instead, its manifestation in the PMF profile depends sensitively on how the finite periodic cell constrains the lateral positions and orientations accessible to the adsorbate and how this confinement is cooperatively coupled with lateral CO–CO interactions.

As illustrated in Figure , the PMF profiles exhibit a pronounced coverage dependence. At 0.063 ML, CO adsorption is nearly barrierless at 300, 500, and 800 K, indicating that adsorption on a nearly bare Ru(0001) surface proceeds through a downhill free-energy pathway. In this low-coverage regime, an incoming CO molecule can adjust its lateral position and molecular orientation with minimal steric or electrostatic hindrance. Although configurational and orientational entropy loss intrinsically occurs as CO approaches the surface, this entropy loss alone is not sufficiently concentrated to generate a clearly resolved free-energy bottleneck. At 0.250 ML, the enlarged 4 × 4 × 5 model retains the same nominal coverage as the 2 × 2 × 5 model but mitigates the artificial short-range periodicity of the adsorbate environment. The corresponding PMF profiles are noticeably smoother and do not exhibit as sharp a bottleneck as that observed in the 2 × 2 × 5 model. This difference does not contradict the entropy-based interpretation of Figure ; rather, it reveals that the entropy loss observed in the small cell is artificially enhanced by the highly ordered periodic environment. In the larger 4 × 4 × 5 cell, the adsorbates possess greater configurational freedom to redistribute locally, thereby making the orientational/configurational restriction experienced by a selected CO molecule less spatially concentrated. Consequently, 0.250 ML can be regarded as a crossover regime where the emergence of a small free-energy bottleneck is acutely sensitive to both finite-size-induced entropic confinement and the local CO–CO arrangement.

At 0.500 ML, a distinct free-energy bottleneck appears prior to chemisorption and becomes systematically more pronounced at elevated temperatures. In this high-coverage regime, lateral CO–CO repulsion creates a crowded approach region, severely restricting the accessible positions and orientations for an incoming CO molecule. The entropic penalty is therefore amplified by the repulsive lateral environment, rendering it visible as a robust adsorption-side PMF bottleneck. By defining the gas-like reference region near the largest sampled z value as G = 0, the excess bottleneck heights at 0.500 ML are calculated to be approximately 0.13, 0.53, and 1.48 kcal mol–1 at 300, 500, and 800 K, respectively.

Crucially, this small adsorption-side bottleneck carries a modest, coverage-dependent kinetic implication. It does not act as a massive kinetic gate that completely suppresses adsorption; instead, it represents a modest free-energy resistance along the selected surface-normal approach coordinate. A free-energy barrier on the order of 0.5–1.5 kcal mol–1 is comparable to the thermal energy (RT) over the 500–800 K range. Consequently, it can moderately reduce the thermally equilibrated flux traversing the bottleneck, particularly under crowded high-coverage conditions. However, the present equilibrium PMF calculations do not yield a direct sticking probability, which would strictly require nonequilibrium gas–surface scattering simulations that incorporate explicit incident translational energies and energy dissipation pathways. Thus, the observed bottleneck is best interpreted as a finite-temperature free-energy signature of restricted approach configurations, rather than as a direct quantitative prediction of macroscopic experimental sticking coefficients.

These findings substantially refine the mechanistic interpretation initially derived from the 2 × 2 × 5 reference model. While configurational and orientational entropy loss remains a fundamental contribution to the adsorption-side bottleneck, whether this loss manifests as a resolvable free-energy barrier depends crucially on the lateral adsorbate environment. In the small 2 × 2 × 5 model, short-range periodicity artificially concentrates this entropic confinement. Conversely, under genuine high-coverage conditions, lateral CO–CO repulsion produces a similar confinement by severely restricting the accessible spatial and orientational configurations during the molecular approach. The adsorption-side bottleneck is therefore best described as a finite-size- and coverage-sensitive entropic–repulsive free-energy bottleneck, arising from the cooperative interplay between configurational/orientational entropy loss and lateral CO–CO interactions. Consequently, its magnitude must be regarded as system-specific and coverage-dependent, rather than as a universal constant. Nevertheless, this underlying physical mechanism may be broadly relevant to other gas–surface systems where lateral adsorbate interactions or orientational confinement restrict the accessible phase space during the molecular approach to the surface. Finally, large-cell simulations on 20 × 20 × 5 Ru(0001) surfaces containing 100 and 200 CO molecules (corresponding to 0.250 and 0.500 ML, respectively) using the fine-tuned DPA-2 potential reproduce this qualitative coverage-dependent trend, further substantiating the robustness of this interpretation (Figure S5).

To further examine the entropic contribution to CO adsorption/desorption on Ru(0001), we analyzed the orientation distributions of CO as a function of the CO centroid height, z, for the different surface models. Complementary site-resolved local PMF maps and broader temperature-dependent orientation distributions for the two-layer-relaxed 2 × 2 × 5 model are provided in Figures S6 and S7. The local PMF maps reveal that CO is strongly localized near the top site in the chemisorbed state, whereas configurations associated with hollow-site regions are more delocalized but thermodynamically less stable.

This distinct site dependence explicitly separates the thermodynamic stability of the adsorption site from the entropic cost of localization. Crucially, the lower free energy of the top configuration primarily reflects stronger CO–Ru binding and electronic stabilization, whereas the stronger translational and orientational confinement at the top site actually imposes an entropic penalty. Conversely, the more delocalized hollow-site regions gain configurational freedom but remain less favorable due to weaker adsorption energetics. Therefore, these local PMF features are utilized here strictly to analyze the configurational component of entropy loss along the adsorption/desorption coordinate, rather than to explain the origin of the top-site preference. For the multi-CO systems (i.e., the enlarged 4 × 4 × 5 and 20 × 20 × 5 models), the orientation distribution was calculated specifically for the selected CO molecule biased along the CO centroid-height coordinate, while the remaining CO molecules naturally provided the corresponding coverage-dependent surface environment.

Figure compares representative polar-angle distributions near the PMF bottleneck region at 500 K. Panel (a) shows the 2 × 2 × 5 reference model at 0.250 ML, whereas panels (b) and (c) display the high-coverage 4 × 4 × 5 and 20 × 20 × 5 models at 0.500 ML, respectively. In each panel, the bars represent the binned probability distribution of α, whereas the solid line shows the Jacobian-corrected probability/sin­(α) distribution. The latter explicitly removes the geometric sin­(α) weighting associated with the spherical-coordinate phase space, thereby helping to rigorously distinguish genuine polar orientational preferences from trivial solid-angle contributions. Although these models differ in lateral surface size, explicit CO coverage, and potential-energy representation, the selected bottleneck-region distributions exhibit a consistent preference for smaller α values. This indicates a preferential C-terminal approach and a significantly reduced number of accessible polar orientations near the surface. The broader z-dependent orientation distributions in Figures S7–S9 further reveal that while CO samples a wider range of α values in the gas-like region, it becomes progressively more orientationally biased near the chemisorption region.

These orientation distributions suggest that the loss of accessible polar orientations contributes to the entropy penalty along the adsorption/desorption coordinate. However, the α-based distribution, including the Jacobian-corrected probability/sin­(α) representation, should be viewed strictly as a descriptor of polar orientational confinement rather than a complete measure of full three-dimensional rotational entropy. Moreover, the coverage-dependent PMF profiles demonstrate that orientational confinement alone does not necessarily produce a visible adsorption bottleneck. Therefore, the observed adsorption/desorption free-energy bottleneck is better interpreted as arising from the cooperative interplay of configurational/orientational entropy loss, finite-size or coverage-induced spatial confinement, and lateral CO–CO interactions.

The 2 × 2 × 5 model serves as a well-sampled 0.250 ML reference for detailed analyses of orientational confinement, local free-energy landscapes, and surface-flexibility effects. The high-coverage 4 × 4 × 5 and 20 × 20 × 5 cases further demonstrate that similar polar orientational confinement persists in larger surface models, where lateral CO–CO interactions are more explicitly represented. Consequently, the orientation analysis indicates that the loss of accessible configurations is fundamentally involved in CO adsorption/desorption on Ru(0001), while the actual visibility and magnitude of the adsorption free-energy bottleneck are decisively modulated by surface coverage, finite-size periodicity, and lateral adsorbate interactions.

Cooperative Effect between the Adsorbate and Surface

To investigate the cooperative effect between the adsorbate and the substrate, we performed 50 ns unbiased MD simulations for both bare and CO-adsorbed Ru(0001) surface models. The adsorbate-induced surface response was quantified using the root-mean-square displacement (RMSD) of Ru atoms relative to the equilibrated bare-surface reference, as defined in the Computational Methods section. In the equilibrated adsorption structures, the Ru–C distances are approximately 1.91 Å for top CO adsorption and 2.15 Å for hollow-site CO adsorption. To encompass the Ru atoms within the local CO–Ru bonding environment during finite-temperature MD, a Ru–C cutoff distance of 2.20 Å was systematically applied to identify the Ru atoms directly interacting with CO.

The temperature-dependent RMSD values are summarized in Figure and Table S6, and the corresponding RMSD distributions are provided in Figures S10 and S11. Here, “outermost-layer” denotes the Ru layer exposed to the vacuum. For the bare Ru(0001) surface, the RMSD of the outermost-layer Ru atoms increases monotonically with temperature, reflecting intrinsic thermally driven lattice fluctuations. Upon CO adsorption, the outermost-layer Ru atoms exhibit larger RMSD values than those of the bare surface across the entire temperature range. For example, at 300 K, the RMSD increases from 0.129 Å for the bare surface outermost layer to 0.159 Å for the CO-adsorbed surface outermost layer. This comparison clearly indicates that CO adsorption induces additional displacement and fluctuations of the surface Ru atoms beyond the inherent thermal fluctuations of the bare substrate.

The plotted RMSD values compare the outermost Ru layer of the bare Ru(0001) surface, the outermost Ru layer of the CO-adsorbed Ru(0001) surface, and the specific Ru atoms directly interacting with CO. Here, “outermost layer” denotes the Ru layer exposed to the vacuum. The RMSD reference is explicitly defined as the equilibrated bare Ru(0001) surface structure, ensuring that the calculated deviations capture both intrinsic thermal lattice fluctuations and adsorbate-induced local distortions. The CO-coupled Ru atoms were identified using a Ru–C cutoff distance of 2.20 Å.

Crucially, this local structural perturbation is significantly more pronounced for the Ru atoms directly interacting with CO. As shown in Figure , these CO-coupled Ru atoms consistently exhibit the largest RMSD values among the three examined surface environments. At 300 K, their RMSD reaches 0.225 Å, corresponding to a 1.75-fold increase relative to the bare surface outermost layer and a 1.42-fold increase relative to the CO-adsorbed outermost layer. This localized enhancement persists at elevated temperatures, with the RMSD of the CO-coupled Ru atoms increasing to 0.291 Å at 800 K. These results demonstrate that the adsorbate-induced structural response is spatially localized around the CO–Ru bonding environment rather than being uniformly distributed over the extended surface.

The RMSD distributions further substantiate this microscopic picture. Compared with the bare surface distributions (Figure S10), the CO-adsorbed system (Figure S11) exhibits broader distributions shifted toward larger RMSD values, particularly for the Ru atoms directly interacting with CO. At high temperatures, the distribution of the CO-coupled Ru atoms becomes exceptionally broad, reflecting intense fluctuations within the local CO–Ru bonding environment and enhanced adsorbate–substrate dynamic coupling. These results strongly support a dynamic picture in which CO–Ru bonding and adsorbate–surface coupling continuously modulate the transient local adsorption environment of Ru­(0001). Fundamentally, this indicates that CO adsorption perturbs not only the adsorbate degrees of freedom but also the local surface motion. This critical observation directly motivates a quantitative comparison between relaxed- and rigid-surface simulations in the subsequent section, thereby allowing us to rigorously assess the applicability and intrinsic limitations of the rigid-surface approximation in predicting finite-temperature adsorption free-energy profiles.

Surface Flexibility and the Rigid-Surface Approximation

To further evaluate the role of surface flexibility in the adsorption process, we compared the PMF profiles obtained from the relaxed 2 × 2 × 5 Ru(0001) reference model with those derived from a strictly rigid-surface model, wherein all Ru atoms were fixed during the MD sampling. This explicit comparison directly links the local surface response identified in the preceding RMSD analysis to its thermodynamic consequences, providing a rigorous assessment of whether the adsorption free-energy profile can be adequately described by a simplified rigid-lattice approximation.

As illustrated in Figure a and Table S7, both models yield broadly similar temperature-dependent PMF profiles. With increasing temperature, the chemisorbed-state minimum is progressively destabilized relative to the gas-phase plateau in both models. However, the adsorption-side free-energy bottleneck is significantly less pronounced in the rigid-surface model. This critical difference becomes distinctly clear upon comparing the specific adsorption free-energy barriers extracted from the PMFs (Figure b and Table S8). For the relaxed-surface model, the adsorption free-energy barrier increases from 0.032 kcal mol–1 at 400 K to 1.450 kcal mol–1 at 800 K. In contrast, the rigid-surface model yields much smaller adsorption-side barriers, increasing only from 0.002 to 0.077 kcal mol–1 over the same temperature range, indicating that the rigid-surface model qualitatively preserves the overall PMF trend but suppresses the small adsorption-side bottleneck relative to the relaxed-surface model.

To further compare the relaxed- and rigid-surface models on a consistent desorption free-energy scale, the apparent PMF-derived adsorption free energies, ΔG ads, evaluated using a minimum-to-plateau definition, are summarized in Table S9. Here, ΔG ads is operationally defined as the positive free-energy difference between the gas-phase PMF plateau and the chemisorbed-state PMF minimum. This operational definition provides a PMF-based well-depth/free-energy-scale metric for the relaxed-versus-rigid comparison, rather than representing a full basin-integrated adsorption thermodynamic decomposition. The rigid-surface model yields ΔG ads values remarkably close to those obtained from the relaxed-surface model, with APDs ranging only from 0.18% to 4.28% over the 300–800 K temperature range. The corresponding relative apparent enthalpic and entropic contributions, referenced to the 300 K baseline for each respective model, are compared in Figure S12. These data reveal that the close agreement in ΔG ads arises from an apparent compensation between the PMF-derived enthalpic and entropic contributions. According to the standard sign convention, ΔG = ΔH – TΔS, the rigid-surface model yields larger apparent ΔH ads and TΔS ads terms than the relaxed-surface model. Crucially, the larger apparent enthalpic term tends to increase ΔG ads, whereas the larger apparent entropic term acts to decrease it. These two opposing effects effectively offset each other, culminating in similar minimum-to-plateau ΔG ads values for both models.

Consequently, the rigid-surface approximation can reasonably reproduce the overall minimum-to-plateau desorption free-energy scale due to this apparent enthalpy–entropy compensation, even while it substantially underestimates the small adsorption free-energy bottleneck. This critical distinction highlights that kinetic adsorption barriers and minimum-to-plateau desorption free-energy scales respond fundamentally differently to the rigid-surface approximation.

Fundamentally, this underestimated adsorption bottleneck indicates that the rigid-surface approximation fails to fully capture the local lattice response associated with the adsorption event. Consequently, the adsorption-side bottleneck does not arise solely from the constrained translational and orientational motion of CO along the surface-normal coordinate; it also reflects a thermodynamic contribution from the dynamic coupling between the approaching CO molecule and the local structural response of the Ru surface. In other words, allowing the surface atoms to thermally fluctuate and relax explicitly modifies the finite-temperature adsorption free-energy bottleneck. At the same time, the absolute magnitude of this deviation remains relatively modestwithin approximately 1.4 kcal mol–1 over the temperature range examined here. Therefore, for the relatively stiff, close-packed Ru(0001) surface at the present level of description, the rigid-surface model may still serve as a computationally efficient and qualitatively useful approximation for capturing the general PMF landscape and overall temperature-dependent trends. However, it is fundamentally unreliable for quantitatively predicting small, kinetically relevant adsorption free-energy barriers or precise adsorption rate constants.

Crucially, this conclusion regarding the qualitative utility of the rigid-surface approximation should not be overgeneralized to all metal surfaces. Adsorbate-induced surface restructuring is well documented for several catalytic metals, particularly under crowded high-coverage or genuine operando conditions. For instance, CO adsorption is known to lift or severely modify the reconstructed Pt(100), Pt(110), and Au(001) surfaces, , demonstrating that macroscopic adsorbate–surface coupling can become an essential, dominant component of the adsorption mechanism. In such highly responsive systems, a rigid-surface approximation would likely fail completely, as the relevant adsorption and reaction pathways are inextricably coupled to substantial surface deformation or global reconstruction. Thus, while the rigid approximation remains computationally attractive and qualitatively insightful for relatively stiff, close-packed surfaces like Ru(0001), explicit surface flexibility is absolutely mandatory whenever adsorbate-induced local lattice responses, macroscopic reconstructions, or coverage-dependent structural rearrangements are expected to actively participate in the catalytic mechanism.

Conclusions

In summary, this work investigated the adsorption/desorption dynamics of CO on Ru(0001) using ML-PES MD combined with US. A periodically repeated 2 × 2 × 5 Ru(0001) model at 0.250 ML was utilized for detailed finite-temperature analysis, while enlarged 4 × 4 × 5 and 20 × 20 × 5 models were systematically employed to explicitly examine coverage and finite-size effects.

The PMF profiles reveal a pronounced coverage dependence. CO adsorption is nearly barrierless at low coverage, whereas a distinct free-energy bottleneck emerges under crowded high-coverage conditions. The critical comparison between the 0.250 ML 2 × 2 × 5 and 4 × 4 × 5 models demonstrates that nominal coverage alone does not uniquely determine the adsorption/desorption PMF profile. The small 2 × 2 × 5 cell represents a highly ordered periodic reference in which short-range finite-size periodicity artificially enhances entropic confinement, whereas the enlarged 4 × 4 × 5 model allows for greater local configurational relaxation. Together with the orientation-distribution analyses, these findings indicate that the bottleneck is best described as a finite-size- and coverage-sensitive entropic–repulsive free-energy bottleneck, arising from the cooperative interplay between configurational/orientational entropy loss and lateral CO–CO interactions.

Furthermore, the rigorous comparison between relaxed- and rigid-surface models reveals that the rigid-surface approximation substantially underestimates the small adsorption-side free-energy bottleneck, although it can reproduce the overall minimum-to-plateau desorption free-energy scale reasonably well through an apparent enthalpy–entropy compensation. Unbiased MD simulations corroborate that CO adsorption enhances local Ru-atom fluctuations specifically around the CO–Ru bonding environment; in conjunction with the relaxed-versus-rigid comparison, this result explicitly links dynamic adsorbate–surface coupling to meaningful changes in the finite-temperature adsorption free-energy profile.

Overall, these results establish a microscopic framework for understanding how surface coverage, configurational/orientational entropy loss, lateral CO–CO interactions, finite-size periodicity, and local lattice motion cooperatively shape the CO/Ru(0001) adsorption/desorption dynamics under finite-temperature conditions. Consequently, the present ML-PES/US protocol is best viewed as a high-fidelity benchmark approach for identifying finite-temperature and coverage-dependent free-energy bottlenecks, rather than as a routine high-throughput screening method. Once the fundamental importance of such effects is recognized, computationally lower-cost indicatorssuch as coverage-dependent lateral interaction energies, orientational/configurational entropy estimates, and short constrained MD simulationsmay be utilized to rapidly screen whether a full PMF treatment is warranted. More broadly, this work highlights that gas–surface free-energy bottlenecks should not be viewed simply as intrinsic barriers of isolated adsorbates, but rather as collective free-energy features emerging dynamically from the local adsorbate environment and the finite-temperature surface response. By empowering the current ML-PES/US framework with advanced density-functional approximations − and systematic model-adaptation strategies, future research is well-positioned to extend this high-fidelity approach to increasingly complex interfacial problems in energy, materials science, and heterogeneous catalysis.
