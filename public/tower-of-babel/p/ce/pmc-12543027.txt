Medial orbitofrontal cortex activation reduces discrimination for threat in male rats
Authors: Jia-Zhen Shih, Chang-Chih Kuo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecognizing threats is crucial for animals to adopt appropriate behaviors. The orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) is essential for emotional regulation and cognitive flexibility. This study investigated the effect of medial OFC (mOFC) manipulation on discrimination ability in male rats in a fear differential conditioning paradigm. The rats were conditioned to two different types of conditioned stimuli (CS+ and CS−); the CS+ was paired with an electric footshock to increase its threat significance, whereas the CS− was made to be less threatening by not pairing with a footshock. The mOFC was manipulated during either the acquisition or retrieval stage. Inactivation of the mOFC during either stage did not significantly change the differential fear responses to CS+ and CS− or the fear discrimination index. However, mOFC activation during both the acquisition and retrieval stages of the fear differential conditioning paradigm reduced the fear discrimination index by enhancing fear expression to the less threatening signal. These results suggest that mOFC activation impairs the discrimination of environmental information and plays a critical role in modulating fear responses. This research provides insights into the neural mechanisms involved in cognitive flexibility and emotional regulation by clarifying the role of the mOFC in fear discrimination.

Fear, a crucial survival instinct, helps animals avoid threats and ensure survival. Fear regulation allows animals to adapt their behaviors in response to environmental changes, demonstrating cognitive flexibility (Hartley and Phelps 2010; LeDoux 2022). Recognizing dangerous and safe signals is essential for animals to respond appropriately (Genud-Gabai et al. 2013; Sangha et al. 2020). Maladaptive fear regulation is associated with various mental disorders, including anxiety-related disorders such as phobias, panic attacks, generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) (Etkin and Wager 2007; Etkin et al. 2009; Shin and Liberzon 2010; Stein et al. 2019). These disorders often exhibit high comorbidity and share many symptoms, including difficulty with fear extinction and an inability to distinguish between safety and danger signals (Kessler et al. 2005; Lohr et al. 2007; Spiegler et al. 2018; Grasser and Jovanovic 2021).

The orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) is crucial for emotional regulation, decision-making, and adaptive behavior in response to environmental stimuli (Bechara et al. 2000; Rolls 2004; Rempel-Clower 2007; Rolls and Grabenhorst 2008; Rudebeck and Rich 2018). It can be divided into the medial orbitofrontal cortex (mOFC) and the lateral orbitofrontal cortex (lOFC) (Kringelbach and Rolls 2004; Desikan et al. 2006). Structural and functional abnormalities in different brain regions have been reported in several types of mental disorders. Abnormalities in the OFC have been observed across various anxiety spectrum disorders (Jovanovic et al. 2012; Liu et al. 2024). Functional neuroimaging studies have revealed that patients with GAD, PTSD, and OCD exhibit abnormal OFC activity, suggesting that disruptions in neural circuits are involved in fear regulation and cognitive flexibility (Shin and Liberzon 2010; Milad and Quirk 2012; Maren et al. 2013).

The OFC is involved in the fear circuit and is interconnected with the relative regions that play pivotal roles in processing and regulating fear responses, including the amygdala, hippocampus, and prefrontal cortex (Shin and Liberzon 2010; Milad and Quirk 2012; Maren et al. 2013). Evidence points to the OFC as a key region in modulating fear responses. Activation of the lOFC alters the expression of learned fear and negatively impairs extinction outcomes (Chang et al. 2018), while activation of the mOFC abolishes fear extinction acquisition and interferes with fear expression (Hsieh and Chang 2020). Medial or lateral orbitofrontal cortex activation during fear extinction differentially regulates fear renewal. Rats undergoing extinction training with either lateral or medial OFC activation fail to use contextual cues to determine whether they are in a safe or dangerous environment during the renewal test. Interestingly, rats with activated lOFCs during extinction generally show high levels of fear, whereas those with activated mOFC during extinction generally show low levels of fear (Shih and Chang 2021). These findings suggest that mOFC activation during extinction leads to a lack of renewal effects. One possible explanation is that manipulations of the mOFC impair the ability of rats to discriminate information within the surrounding environment.

To further explore the role of the mOFC in fear discrimination, we adopted a differential conditioning paradigm to explore the roles of the mOFC in discriminating between two signals with different threat levels. This paradigm involves the acquisition of two distinct conditioned stimuli (CS): a positive conditioned stimulus paired with a footshock (CS+) and a negative conditioned stimulus not paired with a footshock (CS−). The different significance of the threat is attributed to two types of CSs during the differential conditioning training process. In the retrieval session, animals respond to CS+ and CS− differently by retrieving the conditioned memories associated with the two CSs. The ability to differentiate between CS+ and CS− can be evaluated by measuring freezing levels in response to the two types of CSs (Lissek et al. 2005; Jovanovic and Norrholm 2011; Dunsmoor and LaBar 2013; Bergstrom 2020).

In this study, we investigated the effects of mOFC manipulation on the fear differential conditioning paradigm. We employed pharmacological methods to inactivate or activate the mOFC in rats at the acquisition and retrieval phases of the differential conditioning paradigm, in which rats must discriminate between the two types of CSs. The discrimination index (DI) was used to reflect the discriminating ability to a high-threat stimulus (CS+) and a low-threat stimulus (CS−) in the recall test. It was calculated as the percentage of response difference between the CS+ and CS− over the sum of the CS+ response and the CS− response. We hypothesize that abnormal activity in the mOFC leads to an impaired ability to discriminate between the two types of CSs.

Results

Experiment 1: Effects of mOFC inhibition on the fear discrimination ability of rats during the acquisition session of the fear differential conditioning paradigm

In the acquisition session of this behavioral paradigm, animals were exposed to two types of CSs paired with or without an aversive unconditioned stimulus (US). The differential conditioning processes lead to the distinguishing between the CS+ and CS− in terms of their level of threat. Animals must discriminate the CS+ from the CS− to associate the CS+ with the footshock appropriately. To evaluate whether mOFC inhibition before the conditioning session (CON) of a fear differential conditioning paradigm affects fear discrimination ability in the acquisition session, saline (Sal) or muscimol (Mus) was administered to the mOFC before the fear differential conditioning session on Day 1 in the control (Pre-CON/Sal) or experimental (Pre-CON/Mus) group, respectively. A total of 20 rats were used in this experiment. After excluding rats with incorrect cannula placements and those who died during surgery, 15 rats were included in the final analysis (Pre-CON/Sal [n = 7] and Pre-CON/Mus [n = 8]).

On Day 1, rats received either Sal or Mus injection into the mOFC, and the fear differential conditioning session was conducted after 1 h. Figure 1A shows the average freezing levels of each CS+ and CS− trial in the Sal and Mus groups during the conditioning session. Freezing levels increased during the conditioning process in both groups. The freezing levels in the CS+ trials were greater than those in the CS− trials during the conditioning session. A three-way repeated measure analysis of variance (RM ANOVA) demonstrated a significant main effect of CS type [F(1,13) = 7.49, P < 0.05], trial [F(14,182) = 7.56, P < 0.001], and interactions between CS type and trial [F(14,182) = 1.88, P < 0.05].

mOFC inhibition during the acquisition session of the fear differential conditioning paradigm does not affect the differential freezing levels to the CS+ and CS− and the fear discrimination index. (A) During the conditioning session, male rats in both the Sal and the Mus groups were differentially conditioned with CS+ and CS−. Freezing levels were greater in the CS+ trials than in the CS− trials. (B) In the recall test, both groups exhibited significantly higher freezing in response to CS+ than to CS−. In the right panel, each gray line represents the data pair from a single rat across the two trial types (CS+ and CS−). (C) There was no significant difference between the discrimination indices of the Sal and Mus groups in the recall test. All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). (***) P < 0.001.

On Day 2, rats underwent the recall test. The left panel of Figure 1B shows average freezing levels of each CS+ and CS− trial in both groups during the retrieval session. In both groups, the freezing levels of the CS+ trials decreased in the testing trials. A three-way RM ANOVA demonstrated a significant main effect of CS type [F(1,13) = 161.63, P < 0.001] and trial [F(9,117) = 2.71, P < 0.01]. The right panel of Figure 1B shows the average freezing levels in the CS+ and CS− trials in the two groups in the recall test. The rats in both the Sal and the Mus groups demonstrated significantly higher freezing levels in response to the CS+ than to CS− during the recall test of this paradigm. A two-way RM ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of CS type [F(1,13) = 44.84, P < 0.001].

Figure 1C shows the average discrimination indices of the Sal and Mus groups. An unpaired t-test revealed no significant difference between these two groups [t(13) = 0.35, P = 0.73]. The results of Experiment 1 indicate that Mus-induced inhibition of the mOFC before the conditioning session of the fear differential conditioning paradigm did not significantly affect memory acquisition in this paradigm or the discrimination ability between signals with different threat levels in the recall test.

Experiment 2: Effects of mOFC inhibition before the retrieval of the fear differential conditioning paradigm on fear discrimination ability

After the training process of the differential conditioning paradigm, the proper association between CS+ and CS− with different levels of threat was established. In the retrieval session, animals must appropriately discriminate between CS+ and CS− to respond to the two CSs with different threat levels. To evaluate whether mOFC inhibition in the retrieval (RET) session of the fear differential conditioning paradigm affected fear discrimination ability, Sal or Mus was administered to the mOFC before the recall test on Day 2 in the control (Pre-RET/Sal) and experimental (Pre-RET/Mus) groups, respectively. A total of 20 rats were used in this test. After excluding rats with incorrect cannula placement and those who died during surgery, 19 rats were included in the final analysis (Pre-RET/Sal [n = 9] and Pre-RET/Mus [n = 10]).

On Day 1, rats underwent the fear differential condition session. Figure 2A shows the average freezing levels during each CS+ and the CS− trial in the Sal and the Mus groups during the conditioning session. The freezing levels in both groups increased during conditioning, with higher freezing during CS+ trials compared to CS− trials. A three-way RM ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of CS type [F(1,17) = 6.35, P < 0.05] and trial [F(14,238) = 4.59, P < 0.001].

Both groups showed higher average freezing levels in the CS+ trials than in the CS− trials. (B) In the recall test, freezing levels of both the Sal and Mus groups in the CS+ trials were significantly greater than those in the CS− trials. Each gray line in the right panel represents the data pair for a single rat across the two trial types (CS+ and CS−). (C) There was no significant difference between the discrimination indices of the Sal and Mus groups in the recall test. All data are presented as mean ± SEM. (***) P < 0.001.

On Day 2, rats received Sal or Mus injections into the mOFC, and the recall tests were conducted 1 h later. The left panel of Figure 2B shows the average freezing levels of each CS+ and CS− trial in both groups during the retrieval session. A three-way RM ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of CS type [F(1,17) = 39.45, P < 0.001] and drug treatment [F(1,17) = 5.93, P < 0.05]. However, there was no main effect on the interaction effect of drug treatment and CS type. The right panel of Figure 2B shows the average freezing levels of the CS+ and the CS− trials in the two groups in the recall test. Rats in both the Sal and the Mus groups exhibited significantly higher freezing levels to CS+ than to CS− during the recall test of this paradigm. A two-way RM ANOVA revealed a significant main effect on CS type [F(1,17) = 14.37, P = 0.001].

Figure 2C presents the average discrimination indices of the Sal and Mus groups. An unpaired t-test revealed no significant difference [t(17) = 0.47, P = 0.65]. The results of Experiment 2 indicate that Mus-induced inhibition of the mOFC before the recall test of the fear differential conditioning paradigm did not significantly affect memory retrieval or disturb the discrimination ability between signals with different threat levels.

Experiment 3: Effects of mOFC activation during the acquisition session of the fear differential conditioning paradigm on fear discrimination ability

Although Mus-induced inhibition of the mOFC did not significantly affect memory acquisition or retrieval in this paradigm, hyperactivity of the OFC has been reported to be characteristic of some mental disorders. These findings suggest that mOFC activation may contribute to the malfunction of emotion regulation. To evaluate whether mOFC activation during the acquisition session of the fear differential conditioning paradigm affects fear discrimination ability, Sal or N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) was administered to the mOFC before the fear differential conditioning session in the control (Pre-CON/Sal) and experimental (Pre-CON/NMDA) groups. A total of 16 rats were used in this experiment. After excluding those with incorrect cannula placement and those who did not survive during the surgery, 13 rats were included in the final analysis (Pre-CON/Sal [n = 6] and Pre-CON/NMDA [n = 7]).

On Day 1, rats received either Sal or NMDA into the mOFC, followed by the fear differential conditioning session 1 h later. Figure 3A shows the average freezing levels during each CS+ and CS− trial in the Sal and NMDA groups during the conditioning session. In both groups, freezing levels increased in the CS+ and CS− groups during the conditioning session of the fear differential conditioning paradigm. A three-way RM ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of trial [F(14,154) = 18.77, P < 0.001].

mOFC activation during the acquisition session of the fear differential conditioning paradigm increases the freezing levels after CS− exposure and reduces the fear discrimination index. (A) During the conditioning session, rats in both the Sal and the NMDA groups presented increased average freezing levels during both CS+ and CS− trials. (B) In the recall test, the average freezing level during the CS+ trials was significantly greater than that during the CS− trials in both the Sal and NMDA groups. However, the average freezing level of the CS− trials in the NMDA group was significantly greater than in the Sal group. In the right panel, each gray line represents the data pair from a single rat across the two trial types (CS+ and CS−). (C) The discrimination index in the NMDA group was significantly lower than in the Sal group during the recall test. All data are presented as mean ± SEM. (*) P < 0.05; (***) P < 0.001.

On Day 2, rats underwent the recall test. The left panel of Figure 3B shows the average freezing levels for each CS+ and CS− trial in the Sal and NMDA groups during the retrieval session. A three-way RM ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of the CS type [F(1,11) = 132.89, P < 0.001], drug treatment [F(1,11) = 4.866, P < 0.05] and interaction between the drug treatment and CS type [F(1,11) = 16.64, P < 0.001]. The right panel of Figure 3B shows the average freezing levels to CS+ and CS− in both groups in the recall test. A two-way RM ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of CS type [F(1,11) = 27.17, P < 0.001] and interaction between drug treatment and the CS type [F(1,11) = 27.17, P < 0.001]. Post hoc comparisons revealed significantly higher freezing to CS+ than to CS− in both groups (P < 0.001). Moreover, compared to the Sal group, the freezing level of the CS− trials in the NMDA group was significantly higher (P < 0.05).

Figure 3C shows the discrimination indices of the Sal and the NMDA groups. An unpaired t-test showed that the discrimination indices of the NMDA group were significantly lower than those of the Sal group [t(11) = 4.85, P < 0.001]. The results of Experiment 3 indicate that NMDA-induced activation of the mOFC before fear differential conditioning may interfere with the training effect of CSs during conditioning sessions. The interference may result in a greater freezing response to the CS− in the NMDA group in the recall test and disturb the discrimination ability between the signals with different threat levels in the recall test.

Experiment 4: Effects of mOFC activation during the retrieval session of the fear differential conditioning paradigm on fear discrimination ability

To evaluate whether mOFC activation during the retrieval session of the fear differential conditioning paradigm affects fear discrimination ability, Sal or NMDA was administered to the mOFC before the recall test on Day 2 in the control (Pre-RET/Sal) or the experimental (Pre-RET/NMDA) group, respectively. A total of 16 rats were used in this stage of the experiment. After excluding those with incorrect cannula placement and those who did not survive during the surgery, 15 rats were included in the final analysis (Pre-TEST/Sal [n = 8] and Pre-TEST/NMDA [n = 7]).

On Day 1, rats in both groups were conditioned differentially to CS+ and CS−. Freezing levels increased to CS+ and CS− during the conditioning session. A three-way RM ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of trial [F(14,182) = 3.71, P < 0.001].

On Day 2, rats received either Sal or NMDA into the mOFC, and the recall tests were conducted 1 h later. The left panel of Figure 4B shows the average freezing levels of each CS+ and the CS− trials in the Sal and the NMDA groups during the retrieval session. A three-way RM ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of the CS type [F(1,13) = 44.11, P < 0.001], drug treatment [F(1,13) = 4.08, P < 0.05], trial [F(9,117) = 2.97, P < 0.01], and interaction between the drug treatment and CS type [F(1,13) = 20.98, P < 0.001]. Furthermore, the freezing level of the CS− trials in the NMDA group was significantly greater than in the Sal group (P < 0.05).

Figure 4C compares the discrimination indices of the two groups. An unpaired t-test revealed that the index in the NMDA group was significantly lower than in the Sal group [t(13) = 3.16, P < 0.01]. These findings suggest that NMDA-induced activation of the mOFC during the recall test of the fear differential conditioning paradigm enhanced the freezing responses to the CS− and impaired the ability to discriminate between signals with different threat levels.

Discussion

This study investigated the role of the mOFC in distinguishing signals with different threat levels using a fear differential conditioning paradigm with pharmacological interventions at the acquisition and retrieval phases. Our findings revealed that Mus-induced mOFC inhibition during either phase did not significantly affect fear discrimination ability in male rats. These results suggest that baseline mOFC activity may not be critical for fear discrimination. Conversely, mOFC activation through NMDA administration during both phases impaired discrimination abilities in this paradigm, primarily by increasing freezing responses to the CS−. These contrasting outcomes highlight the complex and potentially state-dependent role of the mOFC in fear discrimination and regulation. While the mOFC may not actively engage in routine fear discrimination, its aberrant activation may significantly disrupt adaptive function.

The activation of the mOFC during fear conditioning interferes with the acquisition and consolidation of contextual and cued fear (Sun and Chang 2022). However, in this study, mOFC activation during fear conditioning did not interfere with acquisition of cued fear to the CS+ because the fear response to CS+ in the NMDA group was not significantly different from the control group in the recall test. The differences in the conditioning effects between this study and Sun and Chang's (2022) study may be due to the differences in the conditioning paradigms used. In the current study, a fear differential paradigm and a strong training procedure were adopted. However, the acquisition of cued fear for CS− was influenced; rats in the NMDA group presented significantly higher freezing levels in response to the CS− compared to controls. It has also been reported that the mOFC plays a causal role in instrumental punishment avoidance but not in Pavlovian fear conditioning (Ma et al. 2020). In addition, increased fear to CS− was also observed when the mOFC was activated in the retrieval stage. Our results suggest that the rats under mOFC activation might not be able to distinguish between CS+ and CS−, leading to an inability to correctly acquire or retrieve the association between the CSs and US. Therefore, rats in the mOFC activation groups displayed long freezing times to both CS+ and CS− during the recall test, resulting in generalized fear.

When the mOFC was inactivated before the fear differential conditioning session or recall test, the fear response to CS+ was significantly greater than the response to CS− in both the Sal and the Mus groups. Our results suggest that rats with mOFC inactivation can still distinguish appropriately between the two types of CSs. These results indicate that the mOFC interacted less with the fear circuit under basal conditions. Cue-dependent fear retrieval may be intact even without the functional mOFC during differential fear conditioning. Similar results have been shown in fear extinction (Shih and Chang 2023) and lesion studies (Ma et al. 2020). Although the present study did not observe an effect of Mus infusion into the mOFC on behavioral performance in the fear discrimination task, several previous studies have demonstrated the functional efficacy of Mus in modulating OFC activity across various behavioral paradigms. For example, anxiety-like behavior and impulsive aggression increased after chronic inactivation of the OFC with muscimol, but depression-like behavior decreased in rats (Kuniishi et al. 2016). Medial OFC inactivation with muscimol impaired probabilistic learning during the first discrimination, increased perseverative response, and reduced sensitivity to positive and negative feedback. In contrast, lateral OFC inactivation with muscimol preferentially impaired performance during the reversal phase (Dalton et al. 2016). Furthermore, temporary inactivation of the ventrolateral OFC with muscimol prior to conditioning did not affect later discrimination, but inactivation after learning and prior to recall impaired discrimination between safety and danger cues (Sarlitto et al. 2018). Collectively, these findings suggest that while muscimol is effective in modulating OFC activity in various behavioral contexts, it did not influence fear discrimination performance in the present study.

Previous research has demonstrated that there are some differences between the lOFC and mOFC in anatomical connections and functional roles (Kringelbach and Rolls 2004; Bonelli and Cummings 2007; Hoover and Vertes 2011; Bedwell et al. 2017). Anatomically, the lOFC primarily accepts sensory input and connects reciprocally with other regions of the prefrontal cortex. In contrast, the mOFC predominantly receives projections from autonomic centers and subcortical structures, including the ventral striatum, hippocampus, and amygdala (Milad and Rauch 2007; Izquierdo 2017). The lateral part of the OFC (LO) interacts with the lateral (LA) and basolateral amygdala (BLA) nuclei and plays a more prominent role in sensory processing (McDonald et al. 1996; Milad and Rauch 2007). The agranular insula (AI), a subregion of the lOFC, has strong projections to the central nucleus of the amygdala (CeA) and directly modulates emotional responses (Shi and Cassell 1998). It has been reported that the mOFC projects to the LA, BLA, and CeA (Hoover and Vertes 2011). These anatomical connections also reflect their functional roles. Activation of either the lOFC or mOFC during extinction impaired the ability of rats to determine whether the environment was safe or dangerous, depending on context. However, rats with lOFC activation exhibit high levels of fear, while rats with mOFC activation exhibit low levels of fear (Shih and Chang 2021). In addition, a similar study reported that ventrolateral OFC inactivation after learning and prior to recall impaired discrimination between safety and danger cues (Sarlitto et al. 2018). This difference may be explained by differences in injection sites and behavioral paradigms. In Sarlitto et al.’s study, the injection site focused on the lOFC, and rats were conditioned for 3 consecutive days with a fear discrimination paradigm in the morning and subjected to recall tests for saline or Mus for within-subjects comparisons.

Activating the mOFC during either the acquisition or retrieval phases of fear discrimination conditioning can disrupt the regulatory flexibility of fear-related circuits, leading to impaired discrimination between safety and danger signals. This impairment manifests as increased freezing responses to the conditioned stimulus negative (CS−, a safety signal) to demonstrate the involvement of the mOFC in modulating fear and anxiety responses, as well as the importance of balanced OFC activity for proper emotional regulation (Quirk and Beer 2006; Hoover and Vertes 2011). In various anxiety disorders, such as PTSD, GAD, and OCD, abnormal OFC activity is a common factor linked to impaired fear discrimination. In PTSD, for example, individuals exhibit hyperactivation of the OFC during tasks requiring emotional regulation and fear discrimination, leading to increased sensitivity to potential threats and heightened fear responses to nonthreatening stimuli (Baxter and Murray 2002; Liberzon and Abelson 2016). This hyperactivation disrupts the regulatory effect of the OFC on amygdala activity, resulting in generalized anxiety and avoidance behaviors. Similarly, GAD patients also demonstrate increased OFC activation during emotional regulation tasks and are associated with an exaggerated response to nonthreatening stimuli (Etkin et al. 2009; Greenberg et al. 2013). Disruptions in the encoding and retrieval of safety information contribute to chronic anxiety and difficulty in differentiating realistic threats from everyday concerns. OCD patients, characterized by intrusive thoughts and repetitive behaviors, exhibit significantly increased OFC activity during threat assessment and decision-making tasks. Hyperactivation of the OFC is correlated with an inability to dismiss intrusive thoughts and a compulsion to perform safety behaviors (Menzies et al. 2008; Harrison et al. 2013). It also impairs the discrimination of threat levels, resulting in persistent anxiety and repetitive actions to mitigate perceived threats. The role of the OFC in the pathophysiology of anxiety disorders and the importance of balanced OFC activity for proper emotional regulation have also been emphasized (Milad and Rauch 2007; Schoenbaum et al. 2009; Milad and Quirk 2012).

In this study, rats in Experiments 1 and 2 exhibited differential freezing times during conditioning, but this difference was not observed in Experiments 3 and 4. However, rats in the control (Sal) groups across the four experiments demonstrated significant discrimination between the CS+ and CS− during the recall test. This finding indicates that associative learning was successfully established through the training procedure, even in the absence of clear discrimination during conditioning. In addition, the lack of differential freezing times during learning occurred in both experiments with mOFC activation before conditioning and retrieval, and behavioral performance was affected in both experiments. These results suggest that mOFC activation interfered with both the acquisition and retrieval stages of the fear differential conditioning paradigm.

Regarding the observed variability in discrimination during the conditioning phase, previous studies using fear differential conditioning paradigms have reported mixed results. Some studies have reported clear differences in freezing behavior between CS+ and CS− during conditioning (Knippenberg et al. 2002; Foilb et al. 2018), while others have not (McHugh et al. 2013; Chen et al. 2016; Foilb and Christianson 2016; Sarlitto et al. 2018). In our study, the control animals in Experiments 1 and 2 exhibited differential freezing behavior during conditioning, but this difference was not observed in Experiments 3 and 4. This discrepancy may be attributed to variability in animal batches or subtle environmental factors that are difficult to control fully.

This study has several limitations, including the exclusive use of male rats, which may overlook potential sex differences in fear processing and OFC function. Sex-specific neural mechanisms involved in fear and anxiety have been previously highlighted (Lebron-Milad and Milad 2012; Glover et al. 2015; Binette et al. 2022). Drugs were administered 1 h before conditioning or recall testing to minimize the effect of injection volume. The long-term effects of NMDA and Mus in modulating brain neuronal activity for at least 2–3 h have been reported (Legault et al. 2000; van Duuren et al. 2007; Ramanathan et al. 2018; Sarlitto et al. 2018). The pharmacological effects could still overlap with the conditioning or retrieval process. The intervention of pharmacological methods is another limitation of this study. Because drug effects taper off gradually, controlling the impact on neuronal activity with consistent modulation is difficult. Optogenetic techniques can change neuronal activity, depending on the onset of light, to offer high temporal resolution in controlling neuronal activity and preventing the volume effect. This technique could be used in future studies to explore the role of the OFC in fear processing by instantly manipulating neuronal activity (Deisseroth 2015; Kim et al. 2016).

In conclusion, our findings suggest that reducing mOFC neuronal activity does not significantly affect fear discrimination ability. However, enhancing mOFC neuronal activity impairs this ability by generalizing fear responses to less threatening signals. These findings imply that OFC hyperactivation could be a factor in developing anxiety-related symptoms in psychiatric disorders and highlight the importance of balanced mOFC activity in emotional regulation.

Materials and Methods

Subjects

A total of 72 male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (BioLASCO Taiwan Co., Ltd.), aged 6–8 weeks, were used in this study. The subjects were housed in the Tzu Chi University Animal Center under controlled conditions: humidity 60%–70%, temperature 24°C, and a 12 h light/dark cycle (lights on at 7:00 a.m.). Rats had ad libitum access to food and water. Before the commencement of behavioral tests, each rat was handled daily for 10 min over at least 4 days to acclimate them to handling during the behavior paradigm. Rats were assigned randomly to one of the four experimental groups. All experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Tzu Chi University (108077) and were conducted following their guidelines.

Apparatus

All experiments were conducted within a sound-attenuating cubicle (66 cm W × 56 cm H × 56 cm D; Med Associates, ENV-017M) equipped with a ventilation fan and a near-infrared/visible lighting system. The conditioning chamber (24 cm W × 30.5 cm L × 29 cm H; Med Associates, ENV-009) featured speakers and lights on both sides, with a ceiling-mounted video monitoring camera. The system was connected to a computer, enabling control and recording of animal behavior via specialized software.

Fear conditioning was performed in context A and was characterized by a stainless-steel grid floor linked to an electric shock stimulator. The recall test was conducted in context B, with a flat black acrylic floor wiped with 1% acetic acid, differentiating it from context A. Ethanol (75%) was used to clean the chamber after each rat completed all test sessions.

Surgical procedure

Rats were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of a Zoletil (40–60 mg/kg) and xylazine (80–100 mg/kg) mixture, then secured in a stereotaxic apparatus. A midline scalp incision was made to expose the skull, and the bregma was identified as the reference point for stereotaxic coordinates. Bilateral microinjections targeted the mOFC with coordinates set at +4.4 mm anterior to bregma, 1.0 mm lateral to the midline, and −2.8 mm ventral to dura. Small holes were drilled in the skull to implant stainless steel guide cannulas (26 G; PWD, San Diego, CA). Additionally, two stainless steel screws were fixed into the parietal bone to serve as anchors for the dental cement, which was used to secure the cannulas. After surgery, rats were allowed to recover for 4–7 days. During recovery, the stainless steel cannulas were regularly cleaned with a dummy cannula to prevent blockage. Each rat was allowed to explore the chamber for 10 min to habituate to context A for 3 days before the behavior paradigm.

Fear differential conditioning session (Day 1)

In the fear differential conditioning session (Fig. 5, left), rats were placed in context A and allowed to explore the chamber freely for 2 min. The conditioning session then commenced with the presentation of a 15 sec, 1 kHz tone. This paradigm used two types of CS: white noise (75 dB) and white light. One type was paired with an electric footshock to serve as the positive conditioned stimulus CS+, while the other served as the CS−, which was not paired with an electric footshock. The conditioning session comprised 30 trials: 15 CS+ trials and 15 CS− trials. In each trial, the CS was presented for 15 sec, with an intertrial interval of 55 sec. The CS+ trials terminated with a 0.5 sec electric footshock at 1.2 mA (unconditioned stimulus, US), while CS− trials ended without a footshock. The CS+ and CS− trials were presented in a quasi-random order, ensuring that no more than two consecutive presentations of the same trial type occurred. The assignment of CS types was counterbalanced throughout the experiments.

In the fear conditioning session (left), rats underwent 15 CS+ trials and 15 CS− trials presented in a quasi-random order with 55 sec intertrial intervals. Each CS+ and CS− lasted 15 sec. Recall test (Day 2)

On Day 2, a recall test was conducted in context B to assess memory of the fear differential conditioning (Fig. 5, right). This session began with a 2 min habituation period, followed by 10 CS+ trials and 10 CS− trials. Each trial lasted for 30 sec, with a 30 sec intertrial interval. During the recall test, no footshock was administered in the CS+ trials. The order of the CS+ and CS− presentations occurred in a quasi-random order.

Drug infusions

All rats received bilateral mOFC infusions before the conditioning session or recall test. In Experiments 1 and 2, rats in the experimental groups received muscimol (0.05 μg/0.5 μL of 0.9% saline) (Legault et al. 2000). In Experiments 3 and 4, rats in the experimental groups received NMDA (0.75 μg/0.5 μL of 0.9% saline). Rats in the control groups received saline (0.5 μL) in all experiments (van Duuren et al. 2007; Ramanathan et al. 2018). An internal injection needle (33 G; PWD, San Diego, CA) was connected to a 10 μL Hamilton syringe via flexible PE tubing. Saline or the drug was injected into the brain with an infusion pump. After injection, the needle was left in place for 1 min to prevent backflow of the drug. All pharmacological manipulations were conducted 1 h before the corresponding behavioral session (Sarlitto et al. 2018).

Histology

To verify drug infusion sites, all rats were deeply anesthetized with a mixture of Zoletil (40–60 mg/kg) and xylazine (80–100 mg/kg) following completion of behavioral testing. Cardiac perfusion was performed using 0.9% saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Brains were extracted and postfixed in 4% PFA for at least 48 h. Before sectioning, brains were transferred to a 30% sucrose solution overnight. Coronal brain sections (50 μm) were prepared using a cryostat (CM3050 S, Leica) and mounted on gelatin-coated slides for air drying. The dried sections were stained with cresyl violet, mounted on slides with DPX mounting medium, and coverslipped. Injection sites were verified under a microscope (SZX-ILLB100, OLYMPUS). A representative example of coronal section is shown in Figure 6A. Data from rats with incorrectly placed guide cannulas were excluded from the analysis. Data analysis and statistics

Freezing behavior, defined as interframe motion below a threshold of 24 frames over 30 consecutive frames (per second), excluding respiration, was monitored using digitized video images and analyzed with the FreezeScan system (Clever Sys., Inc.). The percentage of freezing time during the CS presentation, excluding the intertrial interval (ITI) period, was defined as the freezing level. Subsequent data analysis was performed using Prism GraphPad 9.0, with all data expressed as means ± SEMs. Statistical analysis of the freezing levels in each experimental session was conducted using a repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). Post hoc analysis was performed using Fisher's LSD test. An unpaired t-test was used to compare the discrimination index between the experimental and control groups.
