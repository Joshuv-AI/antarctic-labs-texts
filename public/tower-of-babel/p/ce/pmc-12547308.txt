The construct validity and responsiveness of measures of the predictability of intermittent knee pain in knee osteoarthritis
Authors: Gillian A. Hawker, Hayton Chui, Ian Stanaitis, Lauren K. King, Aileen M. Davis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe Intermittent and Constant Osteoarthritis Pain (ICOAP) questionnaire was developed to assess the osteoarthritis (OA) pain experience, but does not currently incorporate pain predictability, which people with OA considered important. We assessed the construct validity and responsiveness of two supplemental questions assessing the frequency of predictable and unpredictable intermittent knee pain.DesignThis was a secondary analysis of a prospective cohort of individuals aged 30 years or older undergoing total knee arthroplasty, TKA, for knee OA. Standardized questionnaires assessed socio-demographics, health status, ICOAP intermittent and constant pain subscales, the frequency of predictable and unpredictable intermittent knee pain (5-point Likert scale from never to very often), KOOS-QOL, and two ICOAP comparator measures (Pain Catastrophizing, and Perceived Arthritis Coping Efficacy), pre- and 12-months post-TKA. Construct validity was assessed by examining the Spearman correlations between pre-TKA predictability scores, ICOAP, KOOS QOL and comparator measures. Responsiveness was assessed with standardized response means (SRMs) for pre-post TKA change in predictability scores.ResultsOf 1366 participants (mean age 67.2 years; 60.8 ​% female), 1302 had intermittent knee pain. Predictable and unpredictable pain were reported ‘very often’ by 35.3 ​% and 18.1 ​%, respectively. Spearman correlations for frequency of predictable intermittent knee pain with KOOS QOL, PCS and Coping Efficacy were 0.39, 0.32 and −0.15, respectively; for unpredictable pain, correlations were 0.46, 0.46 and −0.27, respectively (p ​< ​0.0001 for all). SRMs were 1.23 for predictable and 1.31 for unpredictable pain.ConclusionOur findings support the construct validity and responsiveness of the two predictability items.

Introduction

In people with osteoarthritis (OA), joint pain causes sleep disturbance, fatigue, disability, depression and participation restrictions, and drives health care use, including joint replacement surgery [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. To enhance understanding of the elements of OA pain that precipitate these downstream effects, we conducted focus groups of people living with hip and knee OA under the auspices of an OARSI/OMERACT initiative [5,6]. We were specifically interested in how people with symptomatic OA thought about their pain – their OA pain experience - distinct from assessment of pain with activities or pain intensity as measured by the other commonly used OA measures, e.g., WOMAC. Participants identified two distinct types of OA pain – pain that was intermittent and generally severe or intense, and a more persistent ‘background’ pain or aching. For each, they identified pain intensity, frequency, impact on sleep, mood and quality of life as ‘distressing’. Based on these findings, the Intermittent and Constant Osteoarthritis Pain (ICOAP) measure was developed [6,7], and subsequently shown to be reliable, valid and responsive to treatment effects [[7], [8], [9]]. ICOAP has been translated into more than a dozen languages [10].

Focus group participants also told us that intermittent knee pain might occur predictably ‘after a trigger’, e.g., physical activity, or unpredictably, ‘without warning’ [6]. While they considered all intense OA pain distressing, intense unpredictable pain was felt to be most distressing [6]. They could plan for and pre-emptively manage predictable knee pain, but pain that occurred without warning left them exhausted and was a barrier to activity, particularly valued social activities [6,7]. In the initial iteration of the ICOAP intermittent scale, we included an item that assessed the degree to which the participants' intermittent knee pain was predictable. However, the item was excluded based on its low correlation with other subscale items [7]. We subsequently developed and refined two standalone ‘predictability’ items to be completed in those with intermittent knee pain on ICOAP. Respondents are asked “How often does your knee pain that ‘comes and goes’ occur after a specific trigger, e.g., specific activities or joint positions?” to determine the frequency of predictable intermittent knee pain, and “How often does your knee pain that ‘comes and goes’ come on without warning?”, to elicit the frequency with which the intermittent knee pain is unpredictable. In a community cohort with symptomatic knee OA, we re-confirmed the relevance of the two items and their comprehensibility [11], and showed that unpredictable intermittent knee pain was more likely than predictable intermittent pain to be associated with unacceptable knee symptoms [11], supporting the potential usefulness of these items in assessment of the OA pain experience.

While these predictability items have been made available to clinicians and researchers for over a decade (https://oarsi.org/research/outcome-measures), their measurement properties have not been formally assessed, limiting their useability in clinical studies. To address this knowledge gap, the current study assessed the construct validity and responsiveness of the predictability items in the context of total knee arthroplasty (TKA) for knee OA. We hypothesized that: 1. predictable intermittent knee pain will be experienced more frequently than unpredictable intermittent knee pain; 2. a greater frequency of both types of intermittent knee pain will be associated with worse ICOAP subscale scores, worse knee-related quality of life, more pain catastrophizing and lower perceived OA coping efficacy and the associations will be stronger for unpredictable than predictable pain; and 3. both predictability items would be responsive to changes in OA-related knee pain following TKA.

Methods

Setting and design

This cross-sectional study was nested within the BEST-Knee prospective cohort study [12]. The study recruited adults aged 30 years and older with primary knee OA referred for consultation regarding elective unilateral TKA at two provincial hip and knee clinics in Alberta, Canada. Participants with a diagnosis of inflammatory arthritis and those undergoing revision or bilateral TKA were excluded. All participants were required to have sufficient comprehension of English to provide written informed consent.

Assessments

Consenting individuals scheduled for TKA completed a standardized questionnaire that assessed socio-demographics (age, sex, and post-secondary education), health status (height and weight to calculate body mass index, number of comorbidities, and other troublesome joints), and knee symptom severity (Western Ontario and McMaster University OA Index, WOMAC, pain subscale [13], ‘ever’ use of anti-inflammatory medications and opioid analgesics for knee pain, the 4-item Knee Osteoarthritis Outcome Survey, KOOS, Quality of Life, QoL, subscale (higher scores worse) [14], and ICOAP [7]). ICOAP is comprised of a 5-item scale assessing severity of constant knee pain and a 6-item scale for the assessment of intermittent knee pain severity, with responses from ‘not at all’ (0) to ‘extremely’ (4) for items asking about intensity or ‘never’ (0) to ‘very often’ (4) for items about frequency. Subscale scores are created by summing item scores and normalizing the score from 0 (no pain) to 100 (extreme pain). Those with intermittent pain were asked to report the frequency with which the pain occurs ‘without warning’ (i.e., unpredictably) and ‘after a trigger’ (i.e., predictably), from ‘never’ (0) to ‘very often’ (4). We also assessed two comparator measures of the OA pain experience: the 4-item Arthritis Coping Efficacy Scale and the Pain Catastrophizing Scale, PCS. The former assesses level of agreement (strongly disagree to strongly agree) with four statements: I am successfully coping with the pain of my knee arthritis; I am successfully coping with the day to day problems that living with my knee arthritis creates; I am successfully coping with the emotional aspects of my knee arthritis; and I am successfully managing my interactions with others (higher scores are better) [15]. The latter assesses how individuals feel and what they think about when in pain, including rumination (excessive thinking about pain), magnification (overestimating pain's severity), and helplessness (feeling unable to cope); higher scores are worse. At 12 months post-TKA, we reassessed KOOS-QOL, ICOAP subscales and ICOAP intermittent knee pain predictability.

Ethics approval for the BEST-Knee Study was obtained from Research Ethics Boards of the University of Alberta (PRO-00051108), University of Calgary (REB 14–1294), and Women's College Hospital (REB 2014-0092) at the University of Toronto. Patients provided written informed consent.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated using means, standard deviation [SD]s, medians, inter-quartile ranges, and proportions as appropriate. Construct validity examines how well a test measures its intended concept [16]. To assess construct validity, we assessed the Spearman correlation coefficients for frequency of predictable and unpredictable intermittent knee pain with ICOAP subscale scores, KOOS QOL and our two comparator measures in participants with non-zero scores for ICOAP intermittent pain. We hypothesized moderate correlations (r ​> ​0.3) between pain predictability and these measures [17], but the correlations would be stronger with unpredictable than predictable knee pain. Responsiveness refers to the ability of a measure to detect changes over time in the construct of interest where change would be expected. To test responsiveness [16], we calculated change scores for ICOAP subscales, KOOS-QOL and predictability items by subtracting the pre-TKA score from the 12-month post TKA score. We calculated the standardized response means (SRM) for each score by dividing the change in mean score by SD of the change. A SRM >0.8 was considered indicative of large responsiveness [8].

Analyses were performed using SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Given 99.4 ​% of participants had complete scores for the ICOAP subscale and predictability items at baseline, all analyses were performed on a complete case basis.

Responsiveness

Of the 1302 participants, 1201 (92.2 ​%) completed ICOAP scores at 12-month follow-up. All ICOAP scores improved significantly following TKA (Table 4). Mean (SD of change) scores were 1.6 (1.3) for frequency of predictable intermittent knee pain and 1.7 (1.3) for frequency of unpredictable intermittent knee pain. The proportion of TKA recipients with predictable intermittent knee pain often or very often declined from 82.8 ​% pre-TKA to 19.5 ​% 12 months post-TKA (p ​= ​0.03), while the proportion of TKA recipients with unpredictable intermittent knee pain often or very often declined from 64.5 ​% to 8.2 ​% (p ​< ​0.0001). Mean changes in ICOAP predictability item scores were significantly and positively correlated with mean change in scores for KOOS-QOL and ICOAP intermittent and constant pain scores (Spearman correlation coefficients 0.41–0.50, p ​< ​0.0001 for all). Discussion

In a large cohort of individuals undergoing TKA for knee OA, we examined the construct validity and responsiveness of single-item measures of intermittent knee pain predictability. While most participants reported both predictable and unpredictable intermittent knee pain pre-TKA, the proportion with predictable intermittent pain ‘very often’ was almost twice that for unpredictable pain. Higher frequencies of predictable and unpredictable knee pain were associated with worse knee-related QOL, more pain catastrophizing, and lower perceived OA coping efficacy. Mean changes in ICOAP predictability item scores following TKA were similar to those for KOOS-QOL and ICOAP subscales, with SRMs demonstrating responsiveness. These findings support the construct validity and responsiveness of these items alongside ICOAP subscales for assessment of the distinct aspects of the OA pain experience.

In prior qualitative work, people living with painful knee OA told us that predictable intermittent pain was characteristic of symptomatic knee OA at the onset of symptoms, while unpredictable intermittent knee pain occurred later and often in the setting of intermittent and constant knee pain [6]. Consistent with these findings, and despite participants having advanced knee OA (they were undergoing primary TKA for knee OA), the prevalence and frequency of unpredictable pain was lower than that for predictable intermittent knee pain, few had unpredictable pain without concomitant predictable intermittent knee pain, and higher ICOAP subscale scores were associated with higher frequencies of both types of intermittent pain. These findings suggest that the development of unpredictable intermittent knee pain, as for constant knee pain, may be a marker of OA ‘illness’ progression. Longitudinal studies are needed in individuals at earlier stages of knee OA to test this hypothesis and to determine if intermittent pain predictability contributes to a worse OA pain experience independent from the severity of intermittent and constant OA pain.

Prior focus group participants told us that unpredictable intense intermittent knee pain was most distressing as it could not be planned for and thus pre-emptively managed. Consistent with this, the correlations with knee-related quality of life, pain catastrophizing and perceived OA coping efficacy were stronger for frequency of unpredictable than predictable intermittent knee pain. Predictability item scores were only moderately correlated, further lending support for the fact that predictable and unpredictable intermittent knee pain represent distinct constructs. These findings underscore the importance of pain predictability in knee OA and suggest that its assessment may enhance understanding of individuals OA pain experience.

The mechanisms underlying predictable and unpredictable intermittent knee pain in people with knee OA are unclear, making it difficult to tailor the therapeutic approach to pain management [18]. The relative contributions of nociceptive mechanisms, related to direct stimulation or damage to tissue, versus nociplastic mechanisms, related to amplification of neural signalling leading to pain hypersensitivity [19], is unclear. To our knowledge, only one prior study has addressed this question. In the MOST cohort, Carlesso et al. [20] showed that participants with unpredictable versus predictable intermittent pain were more likely to have signs of pain sensitization on quantitative sensory testing. Others have shown that higher ICOAP scores increase the likelihood of having diffuse versus isolated knee pain and suspected neuroplastic pain (SLANSS) [21]. How pain mechanisms influence the predictability of ICOAP intermittent pain warrants further study.

Strengths of our study include the large sample with good representation of men and women across a wide range of ages, and the diversity of measures of OA impact with which to compare ICOAP scores. However, there were also limitations. This was a secondary data analysis and data were unavailable to assess the test-retest reliability of the predictability items. Study participants were scheduled for elective TKA. Thus, their self-reported symptoms reflect those of individuals with more advanced knee OA. The prevalence and frequency of intermittent predictable and unpredictable knee pain are likely different at earlier stages of OA, but we feel it is unlikely that this would affect the construct validity or responsiveness of the measures.

In conclusion, in a large cohort of individuals with knee OA undergoing TKA, we demonstrated the construct validity and responsiveness of two auxiliary ICOAP questions that assess the frequencies with which OA-related intermittent knee pain occurs predictably, such as after a trigger, and unpredictably, or without warning. To continue to expand our understanding of OA pain, and potential treatment approaches, we encourage OA researchers to incorporate ICOAP together with the two predictability items in studies of OA illness and disease progression.
