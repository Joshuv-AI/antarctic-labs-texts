Mechanically locking enzymes in covalent organic frameworks via light-responsive nanohands for stable biocatalysis
Authors: Tiantian Wang, Ruobing Xin, Yang Qian, Hongli Zhou, Jinlin Zhang, Qingshuang Yang, Peng Lei, Yongqi Pan, Haojie Geng, Hao Zhang, Yujun Wang, Qiang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnzyme immobilization is pivotal to sustainable biocatalysis, yet the most widespread protocol of physical adsorption on static porous supports suffers from enzyme leakage and short catalytic lifespan. We introduce a dynamic-pore mechano-locking strategy employing tetrafluoroazobenzene-engineered covalent organic frameworks (COFs), where visible-light-mediated isomerization enables precise and reversible pore-size variation for adaptive enzyme confinement. This approach significantly enhances immobilization stability across diverse enzymes, improving the retention of horseradish peroxidase, cytochrome c, and laccase by 6.7-, 9.7-, and 1.8-fold, respectively. Mechanically locked formate dehydrogenase retained 99.2% activity after five cycles, surpassing most prior systems. In continuous-flow microreactors, productivity sustained over 22000 min, extending half-life 47-fold versus static-pore counterparts. Experimental and computational analyses reveal that synergistic pore compression and nanohands embedding during photoresponsive locking induce enzyme conformational restructuring and restricted residue mobility. This platform enables ultra-stable enzyme immobilization by orchestrating adaptive pore engineering with mechanically reinforced biocompatible interactions, unlocking broad potential for green biomanufacturing.Subject terms: Catalyst synthesis, Heterogeneous catalysis, Porous materials, Biocatalysis, Sustainability

Introduction

Enzymes stand among nature’s most sophisticated inventions, masterfully integrating catalytic precision, substrate specificity, and sustainable operation under ambient conditions1. These paragon biocatalysts have thus become pivotal across pharmaceutical manufacturing, diagnostic systems, and green chemical synthesis2,3. Yet, this very biological elegance is intrinsically linked to a profound vulnerability. The delicate three-dimensional structures of enzymes exhibit exquisite sensitivity to environmental perturbations such as temperature variations, pH shifts, and organic solvents, which compromise structural integrity and lead to rapid activity loss with limited operational longevity4–6. In response, enzyme immobilization within porous scaffolds has evolved as a critical endeavor to reconcile biological sophistication with industrial demands7,8. While methodologies such as encapsulation9,10, covalent attachment11,12, and cross-linking13 have seen considerable advancement, they continue to navigate a fundamental compromise, often plagued by substantial enzyme inactivation or formidable mass-transfer limitations. Physical adsorption, in contrast, retains its industrial stronghold due to operational simplicity and economic viability14. However, its implementation with conventional carriers is constrained by inherent drawbacks, including low loading efficiency, loose enzyme immobilization, and leakage resulting from weak non-specific interactions15,16. The enduring challenge, therefore, lies in intensifying the enzyme–carrier interaction while scrupulously preserving the innate catalytic prowess of the enzyme, a delicate balance central to the pursuit of robust immobilized systems.

The emergence of crystalline porous materials, particularly covalent organic frameworks (COFs), signaled a paradigm shift, offering engineered pore architectures, molecular-level precision, and extensive chemical tailorability17–19. These modular platforms grant unprecedented control over pore dimensions20, surface chemistry21, and overall topology22, enabling the deliberate design of favorable interactions between a confined enzyme and its microenvironment. Research has illuminated that molecularly engineered COFs can profoundly enhance enzyme stability by orchestrating a symphony of hydrogen bonding23, hydrophobic interaction24, metal coordination25, and other chemical dialogs26 within their porous confines. Nonetheless, the vast majority of these COF structures are static and rigid, necessitating near-perfect dimensional and chemical complementarity with the target enzyme27,28. The inherent limitations of static pores, rooted in their fixed dimensions and weak host–guest interactions, preclude their broad applicability across enzymes with diverse sizes and surface chemistries. Moreover, the concurrent nature of adsorption and stabilization within these immutable pores creates an intractable conflict: spacious pores welcome enzymes but are difficult to retain them, while constricted pores offer confinement at the expense of accessibility. We posit that a transformative leap requires moving beyond the innovation of static systems and toward a fundamental reimagining of the host–guest relationship. We envisioned a dynamic material capable of evolving from a passive host into an active collaborator, one that adapts to its biological partner. Recent breakthroughs in photoresponsive materials, particularly those harnessing the reversible isomerization of azobenzene units29, have unlocked the potential for such smart and reconfigurable frameworks30,31. While these light-gated COFs have shown promise in small-molecular separations32,33, their potential to bestow unprecedented stability upon biomacromolecules has remained a largely unexplored frontier.

Here, we report a strategy for achieving ultra-stable immobilization of the enzyme through reversible mechanical locking within photo-responsive COFs engineered with rigid epitaxial tetrafluoroazobenzene nanohands. This approach leverages light-regulated isomerization of azobenzene functionalities to enable programmable reconfiguration of the nanopore architecture. Under specific visible-light illumination, the framework undergoes a precise and reversible transition between expanded and contracted states, facilitating a sophisticated dynamic-pore immobilization mechanism (Fig. 1). Enzyme introduction proceeds through the expanded nanochannels, allowing for optimal distribution and high loading efficiency. A subsequent phototriggered structural contraction then generates what we term a mechanical lock, securing individual enzyme molecules via synergistic interfacial contact and gentle spatial constraint. This temporal separation of the adsorption and stabilization phases constitutes a fundamental advance beyond conventional immobilization methods that rely on static pore structures (Fig. 1). We systematically investigated the evolution of the COF pore geometry during photoisomerization and the underlying kinetic processes. The universal efficacy of this dynamic-pore strategy is demonstrated across four representative enzymes with distinct structural characteristics. Most notably, when applied to formate dehydrogenase for the enzymatic conversion of CO2 to formate, the system exhibits exceptional operational resilience, retaining near-complete activity over extended cycling and demonstrating high stability under continuous-flow processing for 22,000 min. By integrating experimental data with theoretical simulations, we gleaned molecular-level insights into the structural adaptations and changes in conformational freedom of the confined enzyme during the locking process. This work establishes a paradigm for enzyme immobilization, wherein adaptive host materials form a dynamic partnership with biological catalysts, thereby unlocking sustainable manufacturing pathways once limited by biocatalyst instability.

Results

Structure and light-responsive properties of the COFs

The fabrication of enzyme carriers featuring smart dynamic pores necessitates the integration of large-aperture crystalline frameworks with adaptable intra-pore architectures. To generate mesoporous structures capable of enzyme accommodation, a template COF (designated as TempCOF) was constructed through Schiff base condensation between extended amine monomers and p-benzaldehyde monomers under solvothermal conditions (Scheme S1). Aldehyde monomers functionalized with azobenzene units can impart predetermined photoresponsive characteristics within the COF pores, although conventional systems typically rely on ultraviolet irradiation to drive isomerization. Given the inherent incompatibility between enzymatic activity and UV exposure, combined with the thermally unstable nature of isomeric states, fluorinated azobenzene units with red-shifted excitation profiles and enhanced stability in the cis isomeric were synthesized (Scheme S2). These specialized units were subsequently incorporated into aldehyde monomers via a two-step coupling protocol (Supplementary Fig. 1), followed by Schiff base condensation with extended amine monomers to yield the target AzoCOF (Scheme S3). The combination of substantial pore dimensions and fluorinated azobenzene moieties significantly exacerbates interlayer slippage, thereby presenting enhanced challenges in achieving crystallinity comparable to TempCOF. Through optimized synthetic conditions, we achieved a moderately crystalline AzoCOF with well-defined periodicity (Supplementary Fig. 2). For nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherm measurements of AzoCOF, the degassing temperature was set at 80 °C to avoid partial framework collapse that would otherwise occur at higher temperatures. The results revealed distinct Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface areas of 572 and 513 m2/g for the trans- and cis-isomeric states, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3), demonstrating the preservation of an ordered porous architecture across both photoisomeric configurations.

The chemical structure of AzoCOF (Fig. 2a) was validated through Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy and solid-state 13C cross-polarization magic angle spinning nuclear magnetic resonance (13C CP-MAS NMR). Critical evidence emerged from the complete disappearance of characteristic stretching vibrations corresponding to the precursor aldehyde C=O groups (∼1694 cm−1) and amine N–H groups (∼3380 cm−1), concomitant with the emergence of a distinct band at 1636 cm−1 attributable to C=N stretching of the newly formed imine linkages (Supplementary Fig. 4)34. 13C CP-MAS NMR analyses further revealed that AzoCOF and TempCOF share analogous resonance profiles, confirming their identical fundamental framework (Fig. 2b). Structural differentiation arose from additional signals in AzoCOF associated with F-substituted C and their adjacent C, originating from the tetrafluoroazobenzene units. This was further corroborated by an upfield shift near 139 ppm, assigned to the terminal C of fluorinated benzene, and a downfield shift near 127 ppm, corresponding to the azo-adjacent C. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of AzoCOF confirmed that the F content was 4.6%, in agreement with theoretical values, and identified a characteristic azo N 1s peak at 403.47 eV, which serves as a key differentiator from TempCOF (Supplementary Fig. 5). The uniform distribution of fluorine within the AzoCOF framework was further demonstrated by HAADF energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 6). Furthermore, the FTIR of AzoCOF exhibited weak signatures for the N=N stretching vibration at 1468 cm−1, indicating the incorporation of the azo side chains (Supplementary Fig. 7). Taken together, these multifaceted spectroscopic analyses provide compelling evidence that the synthesized COFs successfully conform to the expected structural architecture.

To elucidate the crystalline nature of the COFs, a combination of experimental powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) and structural simulations was performed. The AzoCOF demonstrated pronounced crystallinity, featuring a dominant peak at 2.20° alongside secondary reflections at 4.14° and 23.15° (Supplementary Fig. 2), corresponding to the 100, 200, and 001 planes, respectively. Pore size analysis based on PXRD data yielded a diameter of 4.01 nm for trans-AzoCOF, which is in good agreement with BET measurements. In contrast, TempCOF showed high crystallinity with a characteristic 100 reflection at 2.04° (Supplementary Fig. 8). Pawley refinement employing unit cell parameters specifically a = 54.3 Å, b = 53.3 Å, c = 3.5 Å for AzoCOF and a = b = 53.2 Å, c = 3.5 Å for TempCOF (Supplementary Table 1) reproduced the experimental curve with good agreement factors (Rwp = 3.96% and Rp = 3.06% for trans-AzoCOF, and Rwp = 4.61% and Rp = 3.39% for TempCOF), which indicated a preserved unit cell after the incorporation of fluorinated azobenzene nanohands. Structural assignment was further supported by comparing experimental patterns with simulated stacking modes, which identified AA stacking as the predominant configuration in trans-AzoCOF (Supplementary Fig. 9), a geometry particularly suitable for enzyme immobilization. Direct visualization by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) revealed well-ordered porous arrays in both materials (Fig. 2c and d), with measured inter-pore distances of 3.3 nm for trans-AzoCOF and 3.4 nm for TempCOF. Pore sizes measured by TEM are smaller than those predicted by simulated models, a discrepancy likely arising from non-perpendicular electron-beam viewing angles relative to the pore axis35,36.

The strategic introduction of fluorine significantly enhances the excitation profile of the azobenzene unit, thereby facilitating efficient cis–trans isomerization under visible light irradiation. Initial investigation using fluorinated azobenzene monomers as model compounds confirmed their photoswitching behavior. Upon irradiation at 560 nm, the gradual decay and eventual disappearance of the Ha and Hb peaks in the 1H NMR spectra, concurrent with the emergence and growth of the Ha3 and Hb3 signals, demonstrated a complete trans-to-cis conversion within 120 min (Supplementary Fig. 10). Conversely, effective cis-to-trans reversion was driven by 420 nm light. However, owing to the intrinsic limitations of NMR spectroscopy for solid-state analysis, this technique could not be directly applied to monitor the analogous isomerization process within the fluorinated azobenzene-engineered COFs.

To determine the photoisomerization kinetics of the COFs, quantitative analysis was performed using electronic absorption spectroscopy29. As shown in Fig. 2e, irradiation of trans-AzoCOF with 560 nm light triggers pronounced spectral changes, with the dominant π → π* absorption band of the trans isomer at 350 nm attenuating while the peaks at 284 and 424 nm increase slightly. The decrease in intensity of the 350 nm peak directly reflects the consumption of trans-AzoCOF as isomerization proceeds, and the 284 nm peak corresponds to the π → π* transition of cis-AzoCOF, where the strong electron-withdrawing effect of tetrafluoro substituents combines with adjacent steric hindrance to elevate the π → π* transition energy, causing a blue-shift from the trans isomer’s 350 nm band to form this cis-specific absorption feature whose intensity rises with cis-AzoCOF accumulation. The 424 nm peak arises from the n → π* transition, which is symmetry-forbidden in the highly symmetric trans isomer, resulting in negligible absorbance, and isomerization to the cis form twists the N=N bond, increasing the dipole moment and breaking molecular symmetry to lift the symmetry restriction, rendering the n → π* transition partially allowed and enhancing absorbance. Therefore, these spectral changes confirm the trans-to-cis isomerization of tetrafluoroazobenzene moieties within the COF framework to generate cis-AzoCOF, and this process is reversible as exposure of cis-AzoCOF to 420 nm light induces cis-to-trans back-isomerization, evidenced by spectral changes with opposite trends (Fig. 2f). In stark contrast, no appreciable spectral alterations were detected for TempCOF under identical 560 nm illumination, effectively excluding any spurious contribution from the COF scaffold itself (Supplementary Fig. 11). The associated increase in bandgap from 1.85 to 1.89 eV and the corresponding color shift from reddish brown to yellowish brown (Supplementary Fig. 12) are ascribed to the reduced electronic conjugation17 resulting from the bent configuration of the cis-isomer.

Photoisomerization kinetics confirmed that the conversion proceeds via a first-order mechanism. The trans-to-cis and cis-to-trans conversions exhibited rate constants of 0.013 and 0.075 s−1, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 13). Notably, the electronic absorption spectrum of AzoCOF reverted to the trans state following a complete switching cycle, superposed perfectly with that of the pristine material (Supplementary Fig. 14), indicating exceptional reversibility. This promising cycling potential was substantiated through five consecutive trans–cis–trans cycles, which revealed no significant photofatigue, as both isomers essentially retained their original absorbance values (Fig. 2g). The highly reversible nature of this photomechanical switching underpins the proposed strategy for dynamically modulating nanohands to mechanically lock confined enzymes.

In situ PXRD analysis elucidated the dynamic crystalline structural evolution of AzoCOF during photoinduced isomerization. As shown in Fig. 2h, continuous 560 nm illumination for 17 h induced a progressive shift of the first intense diffraction peak from 2θ = 2.4° to 2.2°, confirming pore expansion associated with the trans-to-cis transition. Subsequent irradiation at 420 nm reversed this shift toward higher 2θ values (Fig. 2i), demonstrating nanopore contraction. In contrast, under 560 nm/420 nm illumination, the first intense diffraction peak in the PXRD pattern of TempCOF showed no shift (Supplementary Fig. 15), ruling out any interference from the COF framework under illumination. These results establish that light-controlled isomerization of the engineered nanohands induces dynamically reversible nanopore expansion and contraction. Using 1H NMR spectroscopy, electronic absorption spectroscopy, and in-situ PXRD, we comprehensively demonstrate the successful realization of cis–trans photoisomerization in COF materials, from the monomer level to the framework level, with the cis–trans isomerization process being reversibly controllable via light of specific wavelengths. The achievable extension/retraction amplitude of these nanohands dictates the feasibility of enzyme mechanical locking. Complementary evidence from small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) revealed a reduced scattering aperture of 3.7 nm for trans-AzoCOF relative to the 4.2 nm aperture in parent TempCOF, with trans-to-cis isomerization expanding this value to ~4.0 nm (Fig. 2j). The characteristic aperture size inferred from equatorial scattering halo dimensions decreased sequentially as TempCOF > cis-AzoCOF > trans-AzoCOF (Fig. 2k), indicating a ~0.3 nm dynamic modulation range between conformers. ‌Collectively, these data demonstrate light-regulated cis–trans isomerization achieves a dynamic pore size modulation of ~0.3–0.9 nm in AzoCOF, thereby enabling mechanical locking via nanohands insertion equivalent to 10–30% of the diameter for enzymes around 3 nm.

Enzyme adaptability of the cis-to-trans mechanical locking strategy

The photoisomerization study establishes three defining characteristics of the AzoCOF: (1) the reversibility of pore expansion/contraction during cis–trans isomerization; (2) its precise regulation via visible light at specific exposure duration; (3) the resulting pore modulation range that is commensurate with enzyme dimensions. Capitalizing on these attributes, we propose the dynamic pore strategy for the mechanical locking of enzymes, as conceptually illustrated in Fig. 3a. In this approach, synthesized trans-AzoCOF is first photoexcited with green light to generate cis-AzoCOF with expanded pores, facilitating enzyme adsorption via pore infiltration and forming enzyme@cis-AzoCOF. Subsequent blue light irradiation then triggers the cis-to-trans back-conversion, yielding the final product, enzyme@cis-to-transCOF. This is contrasted with the conventional static pore strategy, wherein enzymes are directly adsorbed into either the trans or cis state, yielding enzyme@trans/cis-AzoCOF. We hypothesize that this dynamic pore strategy enhances enzyme loading and retention in COFs. Specifically, initial adsorption under the expanded cis-pore state is anticipated to promote enzyme diffusion and maximize loading capacity. Following this, induced pore contraction before catalytic application facilitates the insertion of clustered nanohands into the enzyme’s 3D structure from all radial directions, thereby strengthening spatial confinement through mechanical locking and ultimately improving enzyme stability within the porous framework.

To assess the broad applicability and efficacy of the dynamic pore strategy, we selected three representative enzymes with distinct molecular dimensions: Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP; 2.9 × 3.4 × 5.5 nm3), Cytochrome c (Cyt c; 2.8 × 2.8 × 3.3 nm3), and Laccase (LAC; 3.6 × 4.7 × 6.5 nm3). Following the procedure outlined in Fig. 3a, enzyme@cis-to-transCOF, enzyme@trans-AzoCOF, and enzyme@cis-AzoCOF composites were prepared. Enzyme loading in the resulting COF–enzyme composites were quantified via the established Bradford method, which exhibited a linear calibration response (Supplementary Fig. 16). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was employed as a complementary method9,23,26 to validate enzyme loading in FDH@TempCOF, yielding a value closely aligned with that obtained by the Bradford assay, with a relative error of only 5.8% (Supplementary Fig. 17), thereby confirming the accuracy and reliability of the Bradford assay for quantifying enzyme loading. The variation in enzyme loading, ranging from 10 to 40 wt%, is attributed to differences in hydrophilicity, electrostatic interactions, and hydrogen bonding between the various COF structures and enzyme molecules (Supplementary Fig. 18 and Supplementary Table 2). Notably, enzyme loading slightly increased after cis-to-trans isomerization compared to the enzyme@cis-AzoCOF, with the exception of LAC. In this case, a partial release of LAC occurred during isomerization, likely owing to the enzyme’s larger molecular size. For Cyt c or HRP, the higher enzyme loading in enzyme@cis-to-transCOF compared to enzyme@cis-AzoCOF stems from the enhanced retention conferred by the post-adsorption photoisomerization-induced mechanical locking, which reduces enzyme leaching during washing treatment.

The retention capacity of the nanopores was quantitatively evaluated by monitoring the enzyme detachment kinetics of various immobilized preparations under identical solution conditions. The temporal evolution of enzyme concentration in solution, indicative of leakage, was precisely determined. As illustrated in Fig. 3b–d, increasing stirring duration led to differential enzyme leakage profiles. Notably, enzymes immobilized within cis-AzoCOF via the conventional static pore strategy exhibited substantial leakage after 12 h: 88.3% for HRP, 91.7% for Cyt c, and 48.7% for LAC. In stark contrast, the enzyme@cis-to-transCOF composites, prepared by triggering the cis-to-trans isomerization of the parent enzyme@cis-AzoCOF, demonstrated markedly superior retention. The leakage for HRP, Cyt c, and LAC from the dynamic pore immobilization was significantly reduced to 20.7%, 19.1%, and 11.3%, respectively.

For enzymes with molecular dimensions spanning ~2–5 nm, the evaluated immobilization matrices demonstrated a consistent hierarchy in retention ability: enzyme@cis-to-transCOF > enzyme@trans-AzoCOF > enzyme@cis-AzoCOF. Impressively, the retention capacities achieved by cis-to-transCOF were quantified at 79.3%, 80.9%, and 88.7% for HRP, Cyt c, and LAC, respectively (Fig. 3e–g). When benchmarked against their parent enzyme@cis-AzoCOF, these retention values represent a substantial enhancement, corresponding to 6.7-fold, 9.7-fold, and 1.8-fold for HRP, Cyt c, and LAC, respectively. This performance unambiguously establishes the capability of the dynamic pore strategy to drastically improve the immobilization stability across a spectrum of enzyme sizes. Notably, the COF models reveal that during the cis-to-trans transition, the confinement size formed by the pore-side chains decreases from 4.0 to 2.6 nm (Supplementary Fig. 19). This size evolution enables effective mechanical locking of guest enzymes with radial dimensions within this range. In contrast, for protein molecules with radial dimensions outside this interval, either smaller or larger, the dynamic pore strategy may offer only limited enhancement in enzyme retention. The leakage curves of enzyme@cis-to-transCOF, characterized by an initial ascent followed by a pronounced plateau, suggest that the early-stage release is attributable to incomplete mechanical locking of a fraction of enzyme molecules (Fig. 3b–d). After this initial phase, the leaching from the composite becomes negligible, thereby projecting exceptional operational stability for the dynamically immobilized enzymes in subsequent continuous applications.

The application of second-derivative infrared spectroscopy to deconvolute enzyme secondary structures provides profound insight into the enzyme–pore wall interaction within COF scaffolds and the consequential effect of photo-regulated pore dynamics on enzyme conformation. Our analysis reveals that the light-induced cis-to-trans isomerization of the COF exerts a substantial mechanical force on the confined enzyme, triggering significant conformational rearrangements. This effect is most acute for HRP, an enzyme of intermediate dimensions, which exhibits considerable deviation from its native secondary structure in both the cis and cis-to-trans AzoCOF states (Fig. 3h). Quantitative Gaussian deconvolution of the amide I band corroborates this, attributing the over-restriction effect observed after pore contraction primarily to distortions in β-turn and β-sheet elements (Supplementary Fig. 20). An analogous, though less pronounced, conformational perturbation is observed for the larger LAC enzyme upon immobilization and pore transition (Fig. 3j), with fitting analysis indicating a 17% increase in β-sheet content concomitant with an 18% decrease in β-turn content following the cis-to-trans shift (Supplementary Fig. 21). These findings collectively demonstrate that the photoresponsive structural evolution of the COF pore directly modulates enzyme conformation through enhanced enzyme-framework interactions. The magnitude of this effect exhibits a clear dependence on enzyme size relative to the dynamic pore dimensions. The larger LAC molecule, already substantially constrained within the cis-state pore, experiences an attenuated response to further contraction. In stark contrast, the smallest enzyme, Cyt c, maintains its intrinsic conformation with high fidelity across all COF states (Fig. 3i, Supplementary Fig. 22), mirroring its free-state structure despite enhanced confinement. This hierarchy of responses underscores that the efficacy of dynamic pore immobilization strategies is critically governed by a precise match between the enzyme’s hydrodynamic volume and the COF’s tunable aperture range, alongside interfacial physicochemical properties.

To evaluate the impact of this stability-enhancing strategy on enzyme activity, we determined the Michaelis constant (Km) and catalytic rate constant (kcat) of HRP, Cyt c, and LAC immobilized within trans-AzoCOF, cis-AzoCOF, and cis-to-transCOF, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 23). The results underscore the robust practical utility of the dynamic pore strategy for enzyme immobilization based on cis–trans isomerization. Notably, the catalytic efficiency (represented by kcat/Km values) of HRP or LAC immobilized via this strategy is comparable to that of enzymes adsorbed within static pores. Specifically, the kcat/Km values for HRP range from 3.40 to 4.36 M−1 s−1, while those for LAC fall between 6.69 and 11.95 M−1 min−1. Moreover, a marked enhancement is observed in the catalytic efficiency of Cyt c, with its kcat/Km value increasing from 27.22–32.62 to 51.07 mM−1 s−1. This improvement is likely attributable to conformational changes in the enzyme’s active center induced by the cis-to-trans dynamic process37. It is critical to emphasize that the effects of cis–trans isomerization on the activity and stability of adsorbed enzymes are not solely governed by pore size, as changes in pore chemistry, such as polarity and hydrophilicity, also play an indispensable role by modulating interfacial interactions, mass transfer, and the hydration shell surrounding the enzyme23,24. The activity data further reveal distinct preferences of guest enzymes with different surface properties for the pore microenvironment. For HRP and LAC, the kcat/Km values in trans-AzoCOF are higher than those in cis-AzoCOF. In contrast, Cyt c exhibits a higher kcat/Km value in cis-AzoCOF. This differential behavior highlights the intricate interplay between enzyme surface chemistry and the physicochemical characteristics of the COF pore environment. It should be noted that our mechanical locking strategy does not fully arrest enzyme motion, but rather tends to limit long-range diffusion and global conformational drift via sparse interfacial side chains within the COF pores. This restraint appears to suppress large-scale thermal fluctuations, which may contribute to greatly enhanced operational durability while preserving the nanoscale flexibility generally considered essential for catalytic function. As indicated by our results, enzyme@cis-to-transCOF exhibits notably improved retention across tested enzymes, with no measurable decline in catalytic efficiency (Supplementary Fig. 23). While a comprehensive mapping of stability-efficiency trade-offs across diverse pore designs, surface chemistries, and enzyme families remains an ongoing challenge28, our findings point to a promising pathway where precision nanoscale confinement could potentially decouple durability from activity loss in immobilized enzyme systems.

Performance of mechanically locked enzymes in CO2 reduction to formic acid

To assess the performance of mechanically locked enzymes in practical biocatalytic processes, we employed formate dehydrogenase (FDH) as a model enzyme, given that its molecular radial dimension lies within the previously established optimal range of 2–5 nm. FDH possesses inherent high selectivity and efficiency in catalyzing the reduction of CO2 to formate38, rendering it highly attractive for environmental remediation and clean energy production. However, its relatively large molecular dimensions (4.5 × 5 × 9 nm3) and sensitivity to environmental variables, including temperature, pH, and organic solvents, compromise its adsorption stability on conventional porous supports, thereby constraining its utility in long-term continuous catalytic operations39. To address these limitations, we implemented our dynamic pore strategy for the mechanical locking of FDH and systematically compared the catalytic activity and stability of the resulting immobilized preparations with those fabricated via static-pore immobilization strategies in the context of CO2 reduction to formic acid (Fig. 4a).

The immobilized enzyme catalysts FDH@trans-AzoCOF, FDH@cis-AzoCOF, and FDH@cis-to-transCOF were synthesized according to the protocol outlined in Fig. 3a. Three-dimensional confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) imaging of FITC-labeled FDH within the cis-to-transCOF matrix reveals uniform intraporous distribution, with fluorescence intensity persisting across sequential Z-plane sections (Supplementary Fig. 24). In addition, a significant reduction in the specific surface area (from 513 to 15 m2/g) after FDH loading further provides evidence of enzyme penetration into the microporous channels (Supplementary Fig. 25). These results verify that FDH was immobilized inside the COF instead of on its surface, as surface-bound enzymes were washed away. Enzyme loading quantification via the Bradford method revealed FDH contents of 10.4, 27.2, and 16.1 wt% for FDH@trans-AzoCOF, FDH@cis-AzoCOF, and FDH@cis-to-transCOF, respectively (Fig. 4c). The superior loading capacity of cis-AzoCOF relative to its trans counterpart is attributed to its larger pore aperture and enhanced hydrophilicity, as corroborated by water contact angles of 79.8° for cis-AzoCOF and 92.6° for trans-AzoCOF (Supplementary Fig. 26). Notably, TempCOF exhibited the highest FDH uptake, consistent with its maximal pore size and strongest hydrophilic character (water contact angle: 72.9°). Although the enzyme loading in FDH@cis-to-transCOF was lower than that in FDH@cis-AzoCOF—likely due to partial enzyme release during the structural contraction from cis to trans—it remained substantially higher (~1.6-fold) than that in FDH@trans-AzoCOF. This enhancement stems from the initial cis state possessing a more open porous architecture, enabling more efficient enzyme ingress prior to pore tightening. These results underscore the efficacy of the dynamic pore strategy in boosting enzyme loading beyond what is achievable with static porous scaffolds of comparable dimensions.

To ascertain the catalytic feasibility of the FDH@cis-to-transCOF system in the enzymatic reduction of CO2 to formic acid, we conducted in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) under an atmosphere of CO2 and water vapor (Fig. 4b). The DRIFTS profiles revealed markedly intensified carbonate-related bands, underscoring the system’s pronounced capacity for CO2 and H2O adsorption and subsequent chemical interaction. Characteristic absorptions at 1659 cm−1, together with emerging peaks at 1727, 1757, and 1782 cm−1, were assigned to formate species, while the signal at 1640 cm−1 likely corresponds to carboxylate moieties. Over the course of light irradiation, absorptions associated with *COOH intermediates (1226, 1521, and 1563 cm−1) intensified progressively, consistent with the accumulation of CO2•– radicals and continued proton trapping. Notably, illumination also induced a gradual increase in the signal at 1691 cm−1, which is attributed to the *HCOOH intermediate—a key species en route to formic acid formation. These spectroscopic observations confirm that the mechanically locked FDH maintains a responsive catalytic interface with CO2, facilitating its efficient conversion to formic acid. A comparative analysis of FDH@cis-to-transCOF and free FDH supported the practical viability of the immobilized construct (Supplementary Fig. 27). While the immobilized system showed overall comparable activity to the free enzyme, an estimated 35% decrease in apparent activity was tentatively linked to the elevated NADH diffusion resistance introduced by the COF matrix.

The reusability of the constructed FDH-immobilized catalysts for CO2 reduction to HCOOH was quantitatively assessed using ion chromatography (Supplementary Fig. 28). This immobilized catalyst maintained 99.2% of its initial activity over five consecutive reuse cycles (Fig. 4d), representing the highest operational stability reported to date for immobilized FDH systems under elevated formate production conditions (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Table 3). In contrast, FDH@cis-AzoCOF (without cis-to-trans isomerization) retained only 74.6% of its original activity after the same number of cycles, indicating that the photo‑induced mechanical locking substantially enhances enzyme stability within the porous framework. Systems immobilized via static pore strategies, namely FDH@trans‑AzoCOF and FDH@TempCOF, showed markedly lower residual activities after cycling (81.7% and 68.8%, respectively). While static-pore immobilization reveals an inverse correlation between carrier pore size and enzyme stabilization, underscoring the critical importance of dimensional matching between enzymes and pores. To investigate the interaction between immobilized FDH and NADH, we employed global molecular docking to assess their binding energy (Supplementary Fig. 29). The lowest binding energies of NADH in its optimal docking conformations with FDH@cis-AzoCOF and FDH@cis-to-transCOF were determined as −7.257 and −8.671 kcal/mol, respectively, indicating a possible enhancement in NADH affinity for FDH following the cis-to-trans isomerization.

Given the good reusability of FDH@cis-to-transCOF, we assembled a continuous‑flow microreactor40,41 packed with the enzyme–framework composite to evaluate its long‑term operational stability (Fig. 4f). Reaction progress in the CO₂‑to‑formate conversion was quantified by monitoring the characteristic UV–vis absorption of NADH at 340 nm (Supplementary Fig. 30), as NADH consumption directly reflects the yield. Initial batch experiments with interval sampling revealed a progressive decline in NADH concentration accompanied by a commensurate increase in formate, culminating at 23 mg L−1 after 1020 min (Supplementary Fig. 31). Under flow conditions, the outlet stream was continuously analyzed using an in‑line UV detector following calibration (Supplementary Fig. 32)42. Through systematic optimization, a flow rate of 4.8 μL min−1 was identified as optimal, balancing conversion efficiency and space–time yield (Supplementary Fig. 33). The FDH@cis‑to‑transCOF‑packed microreactor achieved an output formate concentration of 25 mg L−1 within a residence time of only 25 min (Fig. 4g), highlighting substantially enhanced catalytic efficiency over conventional batch operation.

To our delight, FDH@cis-to-transCOF packed microreactor maintained sustained catalytic performance with formic acid concentrations consistently exceeding 15 mg L−1 throughout an uninterrupted 22,000-min operational period. Real-time monitoring revealed only a 27.2% activity reduction after 15 continuous days (Fig. 4f), corresponding to an estimated half-life (t₁/₂) of 34270 min. In stark contrast, the FDH@TempCOF system exhibited rapid deactivation under flow conditions; its output formate concentration declined to 10.3 mg L−1 within 800 min, exhibiting a half-life of merely 728 min. These comparative results unequivocally demonstrate that the dynamic pore strategy substantially enhances enzymatic stability compared to the conventional static strategy, achieving a remarkable 47-fold extension in operational longevity that underscores its promising potential for sustainable and scalable biocatalytic applications.

Upon completion of the continuous-flow reaction, the enzyme@COF composite was recovered for subsequent structural analysis. Second-order derivative infrared spectroscopy revealed no significant alteration in the secondary structure of FDH within the cis‑to‑transCOF even after 22,000 min of continuous operation (Supplementary Fig. 34). In contrast, FDH immobilized in TempCOF exhibited pronounced spectral shifts, underscoring its vulnerability in the absence of the structural stability conferred by mechanical locking. Further demonstrating the system’s versatility, the recovered FDH@cis‑to‑transCOF was reconverted to its cis state through photoisomerization. Subsequent stirring in solution facilitated complete FDH release within 300 min via the trans‑to‑cis structural transition (Supplementary Fig. 35). Quantitative analysis showed an enzyme content of 11.2 wt% in the recovered FDH@cis‑to‑transCOF, closely matching the theoretical value of 11.7 wt%—calculated from the initial loading (16.1 wt%) and the residual activity (72.8%) after flow catalysis. This close correspondence indicates that the activity loss during prolonged 22,000-min operation arises predominantly from minor enzyme leakage rather than structural degradation. Together, these findings confirm that the dynamic pore strategy not only significantly enhances the operational stability of immobilized enzymes but also offers fully reversible immobilization with potential for carrier recycling, thereby advancing prospects for sustainable enzyme-based processes.

Insights into enhancing enzyme stability through mechanical locking within COF pores

The mechanically locked configuration confers substantially enhanced cycling stability to FDH relative to systems immobilized through static pore adsorption. We attribute this improvement to the deep insertion of photoresponsive side chains into the enzyme’s core architecture and the compressive forces exerted by pore contraction during the cis-to-trans structural transition. To further explore this mechanism, FDH@cis-to-transCOF was irradiated at 560 nm to regenerate the cis-state side chains, yielding FDH@cis-to-trans-to-cisCOF. In five consecutive reuse cycles, this reconverted system exhibited a marked decline in residual activity to 71.5%, compared with 99.2% retained by FDH@cis-to-transCOF (Fig. 5a). Notably, this value aligns closely with the 74.6% residual activity observed for FDH@cis-AzoCOF, which lacks the cis-to-trans locking effect. Molecular modeling further reveals that during the cis-to-trans transition, the radial extension of the single-arm side chain increases from 0.87 to 1.34 nm, enabling a 0.47 nm penetration depth into the enzyme matrix—equivalent to ~19% of the FDH molecular dimension (Fig. 5b). These results confirm that the reversible conformational switching of side chains establishes an effective mechanical locking of the enzyme molecule, thereby enhancing its operational durability under continuous catalytic conditions.

To elucidate enzyme conformational adaptation during pore confinement transitions, we employed in situ second-derivative FTIR spectroscopy to monitor structural changes in FDH throughout the cis-to-trans isomerization process. As illustrated in Fig. 5c, the spectrum of FDH@cis-AzoCOF remains invariant during the first 30 s of 420 nm irradiation, indicating minimal perturbation to the enzyme’s structure despite COF pore contraction, as the photoresponsive side chains have not yet penetrated the enzyme matrix. Upon extending irradiation to 40–60 s, substantial spectral reorganization emerges, signaling a disruption of the enzyme’s secondary structure (Supplementary Fig. 36). This temporal evolution of spectral features indicates that significant conformational rearrangement occurs as side chains extend and progressively embed into the enzyme architecture. In control experiments using free FDH, no significant changes were observed in the second-derivative FTIR spectrum under identical irradiation conditions, thereby ruling out structural interference induced by this light exposure (Supplementary Fig. 37). Quantitative Gaussian deconvolution of the amide I band further revealed distinct changes in secondary structure components, particularly in β-turn and β-sheet content (Supplementary Fig. 38, Supplementary Table 4), underscoring that the confinement effect elicited by pore shrinkage during the cis-to-trans transition predominantly drives structural reorganization. In contrast, enzymes immobilized via static adsorption, such as FDH@trans-AzoCOF and FDH@cis-AzoCOF, retain spectra closely resembling that of free FDH (Supplementary Fig. 39), affirming that the dynamic strategy substantially enhances enzyme–COF integration. This intensified interaction facilitates effective mechanical entrapment, markedly boosting both conformational rigidity and operational durability. Importantly, the second-derivative spectrum of FDH@cis-to-transCOF after five reuse cycles maintains strong overlap with the initial state, whereas FDH@cis-AzoCOF exhibits discernible spectral deviation (Supplementary Fig. 40), highlighting the critical role of dynamic locking in stabilizing the enzyme’s active form under prolonged catalytic operation.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were employed to investigate enzyme–framework interactions of FDH within the COF pores, with emphasis on the distinct confinement behaviors observed in the cis-AzoCOF and following light-induced cis-to-transCOF (Supplementary Figs. 41 and 42). Prior to production runs, the enzyme was introduced into the cis-AzoCOF channel via steered molecular dynamics, followed by a switch in the force-field parameters to reflect the trans-state side-chain environment, thereby realizing a realistic representation of photo-triggered azobenzene isomerization under spatial constraint. Energy decomposition analyses revealed that van der Waals forces constitute the primary contribution to enzyme–framework binding, overshadowing contributions from hydrogen bonding or electrostatic interactions (Supplementary Fig. 43). This dominance of non-directional interactions implies that the association between FDH and the COFs lacks geometric specificity and cannot be rationalized through discrete binding pockets or fixed orientations. Instead, the enzyme adopts a broad ensemble of transient, structurally related conformations within the pore, dynamically sampling a continuous landscape of low-energy configurations rather than settling into a single, well-defined bound state.

Following a 200 ns simulation, both the cis and cis-to-trans systems exhibit clear plateaus in their root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) profiles (Fig. 5d), indicating stable conformational sampling over the simulation timescale. This convergence is corroborated by complementary metrics, including the radius of gyration (Rg) of FDH (Fig. 5e), enzyme–COF contact area, and total interaction energies (Supplementary Fig. 44), all of which reach steady-state values consistent with equilibrium. Analysis of the equilibrium trajectories reveals reduced RMSD of FDH in the cis-to-transCOF relative to the cis-AzoCOF (Fig. 5d), demonstrating that the light-induced isomerization of azobenzene units enhances the structural confinement of the enzyme within the pore. Concurrently, the FDH in cis-to-transCOF exhibits a higher Rg value and larger solvent-accessible surface area compared to the FDH in cis-AzoCOF, reflecting the extension of the enzyme structure caused by light-induced pore contraction (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 45). Statistical evaluation of interaction energies and contact areas, as presented in Fig. 5f, shows that both parameters are markedly increased in the cis-to-transCOF compared to the cis-AzoCOF. This enhancement arises directly from the photo-induced contraction of COF pores, which intensifies contacts between the enzyme and the framework walls. The resulting tighter spatial constraint not only stabilizes the enzyme’s dynamic ensemble but also underpins the mechanistic basis for improved enzyme retention under the dynamic pore strategy.

Based on comprehensive experimental and simulation evidence, we elucidate the fundamental distinctions between the dynamic and static pore strategies through the evolution of enzyme–framework interactions across operational stages. Initially, utilizing larger nanopores during enzyme adsorption enables faster diffusion and enhanced loading capacities; however, this configuration affords only weak interfacial contacts. This interpretation is reinforced by radial distribution function analyses of azobenzene side-chain atoms relative to the FDH surface (Supplementary Fig. 46). For 2F1 and 2N, the bent conformation in the cis state increases their spatial exposure toward the enzyme, leading to a more pronounced distribution within the enzyme’s proximity layer (Fig. 5h). Following the cis-to-trans transition, the distributions of 2F1 and 2N retract from the FDH surface, whereas 2F2 and 3C move markedly closer to the enzyme upon isomerization43. These observations verify that side-chain penetration during the structural transition underpins the mechanical locking of the enzyme. This direct geometric interlocking substantially enhances enzyme–COF integration, surpassing the limited stabilization achievable through weak intermolecular forces in static pores. Throughout catalysis, the enhanced confinement substantially reduces motional freedom of the locked enzyme, minimizing structural perturbation and enzyme leaching under prolonged operation (Fig. 5g). In contrast, enzymes immobilized within static pores retain greater conformational flexibility under operational conditions, thereby exhibiting larger interaction fluctuations and diminished recyclability. This light-triggered mechanical locking offers a versatile and intelligent immobilization platform that accommodates a broad range of enzyme sizes while delivering exceptional stability and sustained catalytic performance.

High-resolution 2D ssNMR elucidation of intermolecular interactions in mechanical locking

To elucidate the precise intermolecular interactions between FDH and AzoCOFs before and after mechanical locking, we employed high-resolution 2D solid-state NMR spectroscopy to probe atomic proximities within a spatial range of ~5 Å. This analysis encompassed homonuclear decoupled 1H double quantum (DQ)–1H single quantum (SQ) correlation and 1H–13C frequency-switched Lee–Goldburg heteronuclear correlation (FSLG-HETCOR) experiments.

Initial comparative analysis of AzoCOF in its trans and cis states revealed nearly superimposable 1H–1H DQ–SQ and 1H–13C FSLG-HETCOR spectra, demonstrating that the cis-to-trans isomerization of the tetrafluoroazobenzene side chains within the one-dimensional channels does not perturb the intrinsic, highly ordered structure of the COF scaffold (Fig. 6a–c). The prominent 1H signal near 6 ppm and the 13C signals spanning 110–130 ppm were unequivocally assigned to aromatic frameworks. Within the COF architecture, the distal carbon site of the tetrafluoroazobenzene nanohand motif is defined as ω, with the adjacent site designated ω−1 (Fig. 6a). The observed asymmetry in the 1H–1H DQ–SQ spectrum of AzoCOFs relative to the diagonal axis arises from distinct dipolar couplings between the higher-chemical-shift imine Hs and the lower-chemical-shift ωHs (Fig. 6b). Characteristic 1H–13C correlation signals at (127.0, 5.8 ppm) and (111.6, 5.4 ppm) were consistently observed (Fig. 6c), corresponding to the spatial proximity of ωC and ω−1C to at least two or three protons within the rigid framework; notably, this signature remained invariant upon photoisomerization.

For isolated FDH, the 1H signals in the 0.8–1.6 ppm range are characteristic of aliphatic protons from methyl (–CH3), methylene (–CH2–), and methine (–CH=) groups, with no detectable aromatic proton signals (Fig. 6e), consistent with its predominantly aliphatic side-chain composition. Analysis of the proton radial distribution function (RDF) further indicates that CH3 is preferentially localized on the enzyme surface, whereas CH are enriched within the protein interior (Fig. 6d). The 1H–13C correlation spectrum of FDH confirms dipolar couplings associated with its aliphatic chains, evidenced by signals at (24.9, 0.5–1.9 ppm) and (56.2, 3.5 ppm) (Fig. 6f). The successful confinement of FDH within both cis-AzoCOF and cis-to-trans-AzoCOF was unambiguously verified by 2D ssNMR (Fig. 6h and i). Spectra of the composites retained the characteristic FDH signals near (1.6, 3.4 ppm) in ¹H–¹H DQ–SQ and (30.0, 0.6 ppm) in FSLG-HETCOR, while the distinctive NMR signatures of the COF host remained intact. Crucially, both correlation techniques revealed new, pronounced correlation peaks absent in the spectra of the individual components (Fig. 6g–i), providing direct evidence for specific microscopic interactions at the enzyme–framework interface. Both FDH@cis-AzoCOF and FDH@cis-to-trans-AzoCOF composites exhibited two consistent SQ–DQ correlation peaks at (1H SQ, 1H DQ) = (6.1, 5.0/19.2 ppm), originating from spatial proximity between the aromatic protons of the COF and protons of the encapsulated enzyme (Fig. 6h).

A particularly revealing observation was made in the FSLG-HETCOR spectra. The newly emerged 13C–1H correlation peaks for the two composites were distinct and non-overlapping. These peaks arise from the proximity between the ω13C nucleus (127 ppm) of COF’s side chains and aliphatic 1H nuclei (0.6/1.2 ppm) of FDH (Fig. 6i). Strikingly, upon cis-to-trans isomerization, the 1H chemical shift associated with this cross-peak migrated from 0.6 to 1.2 ppm. This shift signifies a change in the local chemical environment of the ω carbon, indicating that its primary interaction partner switches from the surface-abundant methyl protons of FDH to the interior-enriched methine protons (Supplementary Fig. 47). Overall, 2D ssNMR analyses further confirm the immobilization of FDH within the COFs. The emergence of new correlation peaks in the 1H–1H DQ-SQ and 13C–1H FSLG-HETCOR spectrum of the enzyme-loaded COFs provides direct evidence of enzyme–COF interactions based on atomic-level spatial proximity9. Most notably, the cis-to-trans transition induces a distinct shift in the FDH-COF correlation peaks in the 13C–1H spectrum, offering atomically resolved spectroscopic evidence for the insertion and subsequent mechanical locking of the tetrafluoroazobenzene nanohand into the confined enzyme (Fig. 6g), a dynamic process triggered by photoisomerization.

In summary, this study presents a light-controlled dynamic pore strategy using azobenzene-functionalized COFs to address the persistent challenges of enzyme instability and leaching under operational conditions. Unlike conventional static pore methods, which suffer from mismatched enzyme–pore dimensions and weak physical interactions that lead to rapid enzyme detachment, our approach leverages reversible and controllable photoisomerization to separate the adsorption and stabilization steps. Specifically, enzymes first diffuse efficiently into the expanded cis-state nanochannels, after which photo-triggered pore constriction during the cis-to-trans isomerization achieves effective mechanical locking. This strategy demonstrates broad applicability, significantly reducing leakage for three representative enzymes—HRP, Cyt c, and LAC—with retention enhancements of 6.7-fold, 9.7-fold, and 1.8-fold, respectively, relative to the parent cis-state framework. When applied to the typical fragile FDH, the mechanically locked FDH@cis-to-transCOF exhibits exceptional operational durability, retaining 99.2% of its initial activity after five catalytic cycles, thereby surpassing all previous reports for immobilized FDH. In continuous-flow biocatalysis testing, the system remained stable for over 22,000 min, exhibiting a remarkable 47-fold extension in half-life compared to static porous carriers. Integrated experimental and computational analyses reveal that the enhanced confinement originates from the deep insertion of photoresponsive nanohands into the enzyme architecture and the global compressive force exerted by pore shrinkage. Moreover, the dynamic framework enables reversible enzyme immobilization and potential carrier recycling under visible light irradiation, underscoring its sustainability. This work establishes a programmable and intelligent platform for advanced enzyme immobilization, opening pathways to ultra-stable biocatalysis in sustainable manufacturing and offering a generalizable paradigm for adaptive host–guest systems in porous materials.

Methods

Photoisomerization of AzoCOF

When exposed to light of specific wavelengths and intensities in the form of a dispersion or fine powder, AzoCOF can undergo full isomerization between cis and trans forms within a defined timeframe. For the dispersion form, a stock dispersion of 1 mg/mL trans-AzoCOF in 2-ethoxyethanol was prepared by sonication for 10 min. The dispersion was irradiated with 560 nm visible light (power density = 7 mW/cm2) at a distance of 10 cm for 20 min, and the electronic spectral changes were monitored to examine the trans-to-cis isomerization. The reverse cis-to-trans isomerization was similarly realized by irradiating the cis-enriched dispersion with 420 nm visible light (power density = 19 mW/cm²) at a distance of 10 cm for 20 s. Irradiation with 560 nm light for 20 min resulted in a photostationary state highly enriched in the cis-isomer, while subsequent irradiation with 420 nm light for 20 s efficiently restored the material to its original trans state. For the powder form, the solid samples that were evenly spread and continuously flipped over were irradiated with monochrome LED lamps (560 or 420 nm, 45 W) under ambient conditions at 25 °C, and the light source was placed at a distance of 5 cm from the sample.

Preparation of enzyme@cis-, trans-, and cis-to-trans COF

A series of enzyme–COF composites was prepared by immobilizing Cyt c, LAC, HRP, or FDH onto AzoCOF matrices exhibiting distinct photoisomeric states. For enzyme@trans-AzoCOF, the native trans-AzoCOF powder was directly dispersed in a solution of the target enzyme (1 mg/mL in 50 mM PBS, pH 7.4) and incubated under mild stirring for 12 h at 25 °C. The resultant solid was recovered by centrifugation (6700×g, 3 min), washed with deionized water to exclude superficially adsorbed proteins, and lyophilized to afford the final composite. The enzyme@cis-AzoCOF analog was synthesized by photo-irradiating pristine trans-AzoCOF at 560 nm to achieve complete trans-to-cis isomerization. The resulting cis-AzoCOF support was then subjected to an identical enzyme loading and workup procedure as described for the trans counterpart. To access the enzyme@cis-to-transCOF composite, the pre-formed enzyme@cis-AzoCOF material in PBS was irradiated at 420 nm, triggering the structural reversion of the framework from cis to trans. Following irradiation, the sample was washed with deionized water to remove any enzyme molecules potentially released from or loosely associated with the transformed framework, prior to collection via centrifugation and lyophilization. Notably, FDH immobilization employed a higher enzyme concentration (5 mg/mL in PBS). The enzyme loading was quantified by measuring the difference in supernatant protein concentration before and after immobilization using the Bradford assay (Supplementary Fig. 19).

MD simulation

Simulations were performed using GROMACS 2021.444. The AMBER ff14SB45 force field described the protein, while the Generalized AMBER Force Field 2 (GAFF2)46 was assigned to the frameworks. The simulation system was assembled by positioning the protein–COF complex within a triclinic unit cell, which was subsequently solvated with TIP3P water molecules and electrically neutralized by the addition of physiological concentrations of Na⁺ and Cl− ions. System preparation involved sequential energy minimization using the steepest-descent algorithm and a 100 ps equilibration in the canonical ensemble. This was followed by an unrestrained 100 ps production simulation in the isothermal–isobaric ensemble. Equations of motion were integrated with the leap-frog algorithm employing a 2-fs time step. Temperature was maintained at 310 K by the Nose–Hoover thermostat, and pressure was regulated at 1.0 bar isotropically using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat47. Long-range electrostatic interactions were computed with the particle mesh Ewald method, and a standard dispersion correction was applied to account for the truncation of van der Waals interactions beyond the 1.2 nm cutoff. Throughout the simulations, positional restraints were applied to all non-macrocyclic atoms of the COF to maintain its structural framework.

Solid-state 2D NMR analysis

All 2D solid-state NMR measurements were performed on a Bruker AVANCE NEO 800WB spectrometer operating at a static magnetic field strength of 18.79 T, equipped with a 3.2 mm HXY double-resonance magic-angle spinning probe. For each measurement, ~30 mg of powdered sample was packed into a zirconia rotor. Two-dimensional homonuclear 1H–1H DQ–SQ correlation spectra were acquired using the BaBa2 recoupling sequence. The sequence spanned two rotor periods (corresponding to 100 μs under the experimental MAS conditions). The radio frequency (rf) field strength for the DQ excitation and reconversion blocks was experimentally optimized to ~140 kHz to maximize DQ filtering efficiency, and the BaBa pulse duration was set to 1.78 μs. Two-dimensional heteronuclear 1H–13C FSLG–HETCOR correlation spectra were obtained via cross-polarization (CP) with 1H homonuclear decoupling during acquisition. The rf field strengths for the Hartmann–Hahn match were optimized to ~62.5 kHz for ¹H and 104 kHz for 13C. The CP contact time was set to 200 μs, with a linear amplitude ramp applied to the 1H channel to enhance polarization transfer efficiency.
