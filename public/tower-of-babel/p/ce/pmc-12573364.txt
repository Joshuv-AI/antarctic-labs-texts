Making a splash: how to conduct on-water cardiopulmonary resuscitation in sailing sports – guidance from SailGP
Authors: Helene Rousselon, Jon Deakin, Maria Fernandez, Breanna Jager, Wayne Davey, Weston Chu, N Heron
Published: 2025

Introduction

Sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) is one of the most common modes of death in young people1 and the most common in those participating in sport.2 3 In response to this, the Resuscitation Council UK recently published their best practice guideline for resuscitation on the field of play,3 which requires further adaption to the specific demands and settings of individual sports. Indeed, a sport such as sailing presents unique demands for the medical team to efficiently and effectively respond to SCA (see table 1) and this editorial describes how this policy was adapted to an on-water sporting league, which can be repeated for other sports. The policy can also be adapted for other on-water emergencies that occur, such as anaphylaxis.

SailGP

SailGP is a year-round, international, professional sailing league, with typically six sailors per team competing in high-performance foiling catamarans. As the medical team covering this unique sport, the author group presents comprehensive guidance for ‘field of play’, on-water cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) during SailGP events. This brief commentary covers various aspects of an emergency response, including the initial assessment, CPR procedures, automated external defibrillator (AED) usage, oxygenation support, medication administration and addressing reversible (including traumatic) causes. Our recommendations look to expedite transport to the extraction site, while prioritising advanced life support management which aligns with internationally recognised practices.

SailGP field-of-play and on-water medical team

The SailGP ‘field-of-play’ is extensive and currently consists of 12 F50s (total number of sailors/athletes around 72), 12 chase/support vessels, 3 safety/medical vessels, 20 marshall/umpire boats, 3–5 guest chaser boats, 3 camera boats, 3 tech transfer boats plus 2 TV helicopters. These vessels are typically single-role and are mobile, patrolling the field of play. These recommendations refer to any SCA that occurs on-water at a SailGP event. The on-water medical team consists of pairs of medics on safety boats, partnered with a swimmer diver. The medics are experienced in prehospital trauma, airway management and international best medical practice for cardiac arrest. The medics are coordinated by the rescue or safety coordinator who also leads on-water communication back to the on-land Medical Command who then coordinates the on-land medical response (eg, ambulance mobilisation). The ‘field of play’ medical teams at SailGP regularly rehearse responding to on-water emergencies and cardiac arrest calls as part of their emergency action planning. An ambulance is always on-site, based at pre-agreed extraction points, whenever athletes are on the water.

Recognition

The focus on this policy is on early recognition of SCA, early high-quality chest compressions with minimal interruptions and early defibrillation. However, SCA on the ‘field-of-play’ can be difficult to recognise. Some athletes may look as though they are breathing (‘agonal gasps’ or ongoing respiratory movements, occurring in 40%–60% of out-of-hospital cardiac arrests4) or have brief seizure-like activity with their eyes open, particularly in the early stages of cardiac arrest. It is important to recognise that these are features of SCA requiring immediate resuscitation. Any unexpected collapse where someone is unresponsive should be presumed to be an SCA and treated accordingly.

CPR initiation5: if no pulse/breathing, begin CPR on a rigid surface (not on the trampoline part of the F50 boat), aiming for 100–120 compressions per minute, with full chest recoil and appropriate depth (5–6 cm). The ratio for adult patients is 30 chest compressions to two rescue breaths, using a bag-valve mask with supplemental high-flow oxygen.

Rescue breaths: rescue breaths may be provided in the water by a trained rescuer. Rescue breaths are critical if the cause of the arrest is believed to be from submersion drowning and life support in this instance should start with five rescue breaths.6 Chest compressions cannot be attempted while the casualty is in-water and extrication to a firm surface to facilitate chest compressions is paramount.

AED preparation and application: prepare and apply the AED as soon as available—ensuring the chest is dry when applying the pads. Analyse the cardiac rhythm and deliver a shock if advised. Continue CPR without pausing. If the initial rhythm is shockable, the plan would be to deliver at least three rounds of CPR/shocks, before transporting the casualty off the water. In traumatic cardiac arrest, pulseless electrical activity or asystole, immediately transport the casualty off the water while performing CPR and managing the arrest as per international guidelines.7 8 AEDs should never be used while the casualty is submerged in water and the casualty should be dried when removed from water prior to applying the AED pads. From experience, AEDs are likely to be used safely on-water up to Beaufort Scale 6.

Debrief: the medical team and the team of the affected athlete/individual will be encouraged to stay together, supporting each other and undertaking an initial ‘hot debrief’, followed by a ‘cold debrief’ of events approximately 1 month later. Conclusion

This editorial has utilised the Resuscitation Council UK field-of-play best practice guideline for SCA3 and adapted it within the setting of a professional sailing league, SailGP. Other sports should consider how this best practice guideline can be applied to their sport setting and regularly practise their emergency action plan for SCA as applied to their unique landscape for the ‘field of play’.
