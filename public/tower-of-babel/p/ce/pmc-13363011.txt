Nutritional supplements for preventing preeclampsia: A network meta-analysis
Authors: Changxiu Wang, Cuiping You, Yanxin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Research on the effects of different oral nutritional supplements in preventing preeclampsia has yielded controversial results. This network meta-analysis sought to ascertain the effect of diverse oral nutritional supplements in preventing preeclampsia. Randomised controlled trials in preventing preeclampsia were searched in PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science. The primary outcome was preeclampsia. Secondary outcomes included eclampsia and gestational hypertension. 22 studies were included, with 18133 participants in the intervention group and 17971 in the placebo group. Compared with placebo, L-arginine and vitamin B and vitamin C and vitamin E (L-arginine-VB-VC-VE) [Odds ratio (OR)=0.33, 95% confidence interval (CI) (0.20, 0.54)], linoleic-acid-calcium (OR=0.16, 95% CI 0.041, 0.50), vitamin D (VD) (OR=0. 34, 95% CI 0.17, 0.65), and calcium (OR=0.77, 95% CI 0.65, 0.91) were more effective in preventing preeclampsia. The first three nutritional supplements were superior to calcium. There was no difference among these three supplements. None of the four nutritional supplements (calcium, phytonutrient, VC, and VC-VE) were effective in preventing gestational hypertension. Oral calcium, L-arginine-VB-VC-VE, linoleic-acid-calcium, and VD could be effective approaches to prevent preeclampsia.

Preeclampsia complicates 2-4% of pregnancies, resulting in 4.6 million maternal deaths and 5 million foetal and newborn deaths yearly.1,2 There is no definitive cure except delivery.3,4 Therefore, it is crucial to implement preventive measures for global maternal and newborn health.

Oral nutritional supplements serve as an alternative approach to prevention of pre-eclampsia. Specifically, calcium supplementation is beneficial for individuals with inadequate dietary calcium intake.5,6 The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends oral calcium supplementation at 1.5 to 2.0 grams per day for gravidas with inadequate dietary calcium intake to decline the pre-eclampsia risk7. However, the widespread adoption of oral calcium supplementation during pregnancy remains constrained by several factors, including taste, odour, pill size, and convenience.8 The effectiveness of vitamin D in prevention of preeclampia remains a subject of debate. A meta-analysis stated that vitamin D supplementation was not linked with reduced incidence of preeclampsia.9 Another meta-analysis of 33 randomised controlled trials (RCTs) (10,613 participants) demonstrated that vitamin D supplementation substantially reduced the risk by 44.8 % (RR=0.55, 95% CI (0.43, 0.71) and reduced the risk of preterm labour by 30 % (RR=0.70, 95% CI (0.51, 0.960). 10 The results also did not support the preventive effect of multivitamins against preeclampsia.11 L-arginine, an essential substrate for nitric oxide synthesis, has been evaluated as a potential preventive measure for preeclampsia. A meta-analysis revealed that L-arginine supplementation was effective in reducing the incidence of preeclampsia and improving neonatal birth weight, while no statistical differences were unveiled in 1-min Apgar scores or gestational age.12 Another meta-analysis focusing on the antenatal administration of L-arginine for birth outcomes revealed that L-arginine significantly improved Apgar scores at 5 min in high-risk women with preeclampsia, gestational hypertension, or mild chronic hypertension.13

Given that various interventions exhibit distinct mechanisms and differential impacts on the prevention of preeclampsia, we undertook this network meta-analysis to rank the effectiveness of diverse nutritional supplements in preventing preeclampsia and to identify the most effective intervention.

Methods

Protocol registration

This network meta-analysis was reported under the PRISMA guidelines14 and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42025613663).

Inclusion criteria

Eligible studies met these criteria: (i) women aged between 18 and 50, ready for pregnancy or were pregnant at any gestation, and at high risk for preeclampsia, (ii) participants in the intervention received nutritional supplements, and the controls were treated with placebos, (iii) studies: RCTs. The primary outcome was preeclampsia, and secondary outcomes consisted of eclampsia and gestational hypertension.

Exclusion criteria

Retrospective studies, cohort studies, commentaries, case reports, conference abstracts, reviews, animal experiments, articles with unavailable full texts or missing data, and studies in combination with other organic diseases, were excluded.

Search strategy

Two reviewers (CW, CY) independently conducted systematic searches of Embase, Cochrane Library, PubMed, and Web of Science databases up to September 25, 2024, using combinations of Subject Heading ‘Preeclampsia’, ‘Dietary Supplements’, and their free terms. Supplementary Material 1 shows the detailed search strategies.

Supplementary Materials

Study selection and data extraction

Two independent researchers (CW, YZ) screened and cross-checked the retrieved articles. Any disputes were tackled by discussing or consulting a third researcher. All retrieved records were imported into Endnote20. The extracted data encompassed the ﬁrst author, publication year, country, total sample size, nutritional supplements, and outcomes.

Quality assessment

Two independent reviewers (CW, CY) conducted full-text screening and evaluated study quality utilising the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.015 in several domains, including generation of random sequences, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, selective reporting, and incomplete outcome data. These domains were rated as low, high, or unclear. Quality assessment results were cross-checked, and any disputes were tackled via discussion or adjudication by a third party. Review Manager 5.4 was utilised to demonstrate the results of quality assessment.

Statistical analysis

A network meta-analysis was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of different nutritional supplements in preventing preeclampsia with R software 4.3.3 and StataMP-64. For all included outcome measures, Bayesian random-effects models were employed for pooling and analysis. The Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) statistical method was used for calculations, adopting a sampling strategy of 50,000 iterations per chain across four chains, with the initial 20,000 unstable iterations discarded.

Network graphs were plotted to depict the RCTs. In the graph, the intervention was represented by nodes, and the node size symbolised participant cases in RCTs. Direct comparisons between two nutritional supplements or placebo were indicated with lines, and the width of the line symbolised participant cases in RCTs that directly compared the two nutritional supplements.

Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were estimated for preeclampsia and other outcomes, and the heterogeneity index I2 was adopted to determine whether a fixed- (I2<50%) or random-effect (I2>50%) model was selected for data synthesis. The loop method was employed to examine inconsistencies in closed loops generated by network meta-analysis, with inconsistency determined based on the inconsistency factor value. The node splitting method was adopted to test local inconsistencies; P>0.05 implied good consistency. If inconsistencies existed, the inconsistency model was applied for testing, and sensitivity analysis was implemented to observe outcome stability. Results of pairwise comparisons were pooled to compare nutritional supplements with placebo using forest plots and league tables. In addition, the surface under the cumulative ranking (SUCRA) was computed. Sensitivity analysis was employed to judge the robustness of the research findings. This network meta-analysis primarily assessed the impact of high-risk-of-bias studies and small-sample studies (sample size ≤30) on the results. If no considerable change occurred between the two sets of results, the findings were considered relatively reliable. If significant discrepancies or even contradictory conclusions emerged, caution was warranted in interpreting the results. Publication bias and small-sample effect were checked utilising funnel plots.

The GRADE profiler software was utilised to rate the quality of each outcome measure. RCTs were initially assigned a default high quality rating, while observational studies were assigned a default low quality rating. Evidence quality was downgraded if any of the five downgrading factors were present: inconsistency, risk of bias, precision, indirectness, or publication bias. During the assessment, RCTs with one downgrading factor were classified as moderate-quality evidence. Studies downgraded by two levels were considered low-quality, while those downgraded by three or more levels were deemed very low-quality. A summary table was generated based on the final evidence grade.

Results

Study characteristics

Initially, 679 related articles were searched. After 134 duplicates were ruled out, 502 records were eliminated after reading the titles and abstracts. After full texts were reviewed, 22 RCTs were included ( Fig. 1).16-37 These RCTs involved 18133 participants in the intervention group and 17971 in the placebo group. Twelve nutritional supplements were studied, including calcium, calcium and vitamin D (Calcium-VD), fish oil, folic acid, L-arginine, vitamin B, vitamin C, and vitamin E (L-arginine-VB-VC-VE), linoleic acid and calcium (linoleic-acid-calcium), magnesium, phytonutrient, vitamin B-vitamin C-vitamin E (VB-VC-VE), vitamin C (VC), vitamin C and vitamin E (VC-VE), and vitamin D. Sensitivity analysis

Supplementary Material 4 presents the results of sensitivity analysis. Using the leave-one-out method, sensitivity analyses were conducted for the interventions included in each outcome measure. The results demonstrated that removing any single intervention did not alter the direction of outcome measures, indicating stability in the findings.

Publication bias and small-sample effects

Publication bias assessment was conducted using funnel plots and Egger’s test. Corrected-comparison funnel plots were generated, with different colors representing different direct comparisons in the original studies. The scatter plots of each study were distributed on both sides of the zero line, showing roughly uniform symmetry. Three studies on preeclampsia outcomes fell outside the funnel plot, two studies on eclampsia outcomes were located at the bottom of the funnel plot, and two studies on gestational hypertension outcomes fell outside the funnel plot while one study was positioned at the bottom. This suggests potential publication bias or small-sample effects ( Figs. 3 and 4). Quantitative assessment using Egger’s test revealed no pronounced publication bias for PE, eclampsia, or gestational hypertension (P> 0.05), as detailed in Supplementary Material 5.

Discussion

Our results showed that compared with placebo, calcium, L-arginine-VB-VC-VE, linoleic-acid-calcium, and vitamin D were superior in reducing preeclampsia. The nutritional supplements of L-arginine-VB-VC-VE, linoleic-acid-Calcium, and vitamin D were all superior to calcium. There was no difference among these three nutritional supplements. Compared with placebo, only vitamin C reduced eclampsia risk. None of the four nutritional supplements (calcium, phytonutrient, vitamin C, and VC-VE) were effective in preventing gestational hypertension. Our findings were consistent with a previous systematic review.38

Our findings elicited that L-arginine-VB-VC-VE could prevent preeclampsia. This may be the regulation of the L-arginine-nitric oxide (NO) system in biological behaviours and clinical conditions.39 Combined vitamin C and vitamin E supplementation cannot lower the risk of preeclampsiaand is not suitable to gravidas for preventing preeclampsia or hypertensive disorders.40,41 L-arginine, a substrate for NO synthesis, is critical in blood pressure regulation via its vasodilatory effects.42 Previous studies supported a role for asymmetric dimethylarginine and NO in blood pressure of both normal and preeclampsia pregnancies.43-45 Administration of L-arginine injections enhances uteroplacental circulation, thereby effectively reducing maternal blood pressure.46

Our findings demonstrated that vitamin D supplementation was effective in preventing preeclampsia. This may be due to its anti-inflammatory effect and benefits on the endothelium.47 A case-control study found a high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency in both normotensive gravidas and gravidas complicated with preeclampsia, but more amplified in the latter.48 A systematic evaluation of preeclampsia drugs using the accelerating innovation for mothers database identified the potential of L-arginine and vitamin D for preventing preeclampsia.49

For gravidas with low baseline dietary calcium intake (particularly those outside the U.S.), high-dose calcium supplementation is acceptable. It can reduce the risk of hypertensive disorder complicating pregnancy.50 Another meta-analysis found that calcium supplementation reduced preeclampsia risk (5.6% vs. 8.8%; RR 0.49, 95% CI 0.39-0.61).51 Consistent with previous meta-analysis, our study showed that calcium supplementation lowered the risk of pre-eclampsia.

This analysis has several limitations. First, the dose, frequency, and course of treatment of oral nutritional supplements were different across included RCTs, which may be the source of clinical heterogeneity. This heterogeneity could hinder further analysis of different doses within the same regimen across the included RCTs. Consequently, the accuracy of the pooled results requires further validation after integrating findings from multiple studies, which contributes to the relatively general and less precise conclusions drawn in this study. Second, given that this meta-analysis concentrated solely on prevention strategies, the sources of secondary outcome measures were derived from the included studies. Other types of hypertensive disorders complicating pregnancy would have been excluded if the secondary outcome were not grouped. Due to the limited RCTs, the results can easily change as the included RCTs increases. Moreover, the differences in the efficacy of different prevention methods, still need to be verified by clinical trials with larger sample sizes to obtain more definite and strong conclusions. Third, the overall risk of bias shows that most studies have some biases. Since preeclampsia is only a progressive stage of hypertensive disorders complicating pregnancy, future studies should include other types of hypertensive disorders complicating pregnancy. Large sample studies are needed to verify the results and obtain stronger conclusions. Fourth, due to the failure of some included RCTs to adequately or consistently report relevant data, it was impossible to quantitatively assess key factors potentially influencing treatment effects, introducing uncertainty into the transferability hypothesis. Furthermore, the assessment of certain interventions in our analysis relied on evidence from single RCT. While these RCTs were included in the analysis to maintain the integrity of the network structure and provide a baseline for future updates, findings derived from single studies should be regarded as preliminary and exploratory. The robustness of these findings is insufficient, and they may be highly sensitive to changes in statistical methods (such as continuity correction) or individual study data. Therefore, we must exercise extreme caution in interpreting these specific comparative results.

In summary, our study identified that oral different nutritional supplements such as calcium, L-arginine-VB-VC-VE, linoleic-acid-calcium, and vitamin D could be an effective approach to reducing pre-eclampsia risk. Given the limitations, future research should focus on conducting high-quality, large-scale clinical trials with standardised dosages and treatment regimens. These studies should broaden their scope and ensure comprehensive reporting of key data to validate the robustness of current preliminary findings, ultimately providing more reliable and precise evidence for clinical practice.
