Influence of salicylic acid on plant defense, growth and biochemical composition of Salix alba infected with Lymantria obfuscata in Kashmir
Authors: Oyais Ahmad Wagay, Javeed Ahmad Mugloo, Barkat Hussain, Nazir A. Pala, Gowher Nabi Parrey, Jauhar Rafeeq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lymantria obfuscata poses a significant threat to Salix alba plantations in Kashmir, creating an urgent need for sustainable, non-chemical pest management strategies. The present study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of salicylic acid (SA) as a plant defense inducer against this herbivore. In this experiment, Salix alba cuttings were subjected to three concentrations of SA (2, 3, and 4 mM) using spray and dip application methods prior to infestation with L. obfuscata larvae. Our results indicate that salicylic acid significantly reduced leaf defoliation in a dose-dependent manner; specifically, the dip method with 4 mM SA exhibited the lowest defoliation (13.10%) compared to 25.20% in control groups. SA treatment significantly suppressed larval and adult growth, with larvae feeding on 4 mM SA-treated dipped cuttings exhibiting an average weight of 2.82 mg versus 3.92 mg in the control treatment. Furthermore, salicylic acid treatment had a significantly positive impact on vegetative growth, enhancing leaf area (14.25 vs. 10.50 cm2 in control), plant height (37.20 vs. 30.80 cm), and root length (15.25 vs. 12.37 cm). Quantification of defensive metabolites revealed that the treatment significantly increased total phenol, total flavonoid, and total tannin content in SA-treated plants. GC-MS analysis confirmed that SA-treated plants accumulated higher endogenous SA levels, particularly in dipped plants (1.00 mg/g DW), compared to controls (0.10 mg/g DW). In conclusion, the application of 4 mM SA via the dip method effectively bolsters the morphological and biochemical defense mechanisms of Salix alba, offering a potent and sustainable strategy for managing Lymantria obfuscata infestations.

Introduction

Salix alba is native to Europe, Central Asia and North Africa, thrives well in diverse environments, especially in moist, temperate regions1. India is home to approximately 33 species of Salix, most of which are primarily classified as shrubs. However, remarkable exceptions include Salix alba, Salix babylonica, Salix daphnoides, Salix fragilis, Salix elegans and Salix tetrasperma2. Within the Kashmir valley, the Salix genus is represented by 23 species, with 15 of these extending to alpine or subalpine regions3. In India, the plant is widely grown in the Western Himalayas, reaching elevations of up to 2400 m, particularly in the Kashmir and Kulu valleys near river streams, canal banks and lakes. Moreover, it has been cultivated in the dry temperate regions of Lahaul and Ladakh at even higher altitudes, primarily for use as fuel wood and fodder4. Salix alba was introduced to Kashmir in 1927 by Mr. Abbey from Sir Amar singh technical institute, Srinagar (now Amar Singh College, Srinagar) and was first cultivated in compartment 1a of Haran (Ganderbal). Subsequent imports of cuttings were made by Mr. Steane and Sir Peter H. Clutterbuck and these were also planted in the same compartment in Haran. The resulting varieties were named after their importers5. The estimates indicate that approximately 5 million willow trees are present, making up around 16% of the total broadleaf tree plantations in the Kashmir Valley6.

The ecological importance of Salix is well-recognized, as it stabilizes riverbanks, enhances biodiversity7,8. The introduction of Salix alba to Kashmir boosts economy and flourished well in the natural ecosystem9. Kashmir willow widely revolutionized the bat industry of India and subcontinent, as wood of the willow is used to manufacture cricket bats. It is widely used in producing timber, fuelwood and traditional medicines4,10. Beyond its ecological role, Salix alba significantly holds economic and medicinal value. The bark of Salix alba contains salicin, providing relief to pain and anti-inflammatory treatments11.

Despite its immense value, Salix alba faces significant biotic stress12. The Indian gypsy moth, Lymantria obfuscata Walker (Lepidoptera: Lymantriidae), is a destructive pest widely distributed in the North-Western Himalayas13. It is considered a severe threat to Salix species, with Salix alba and Salix fragilis being particularly susceptible to infestation. The pest is univoltine, and its larvae feed voraciously on the foliage, often resulting in the complete defoliation of willow plantations14.This gregarious feeding behavior compromises the tree’s photosynthetic capacity and biomass production, directly threatening the economic viability of the cricket bat industry and the availability of fodder15.

Currently, Salix alba is grown widely in forests of Kashmir and urban settings, as insecticide applications are not advocated by the policy makers as well as by pest managers. Therefore, chemical insecticides are not being used till to this date for its management except non-chemical approaches such as pheromone trapping. This limitation necessitates the exploration of natural defense mechanisms16.

The role of salicylic acid garnered attention for its pivotal role in regulating plant defense mechanisms17. SA orchestrates systemic acquired resistance (SAR) by triggering the production of defense-related proteins and metabolites, bolstering the plant’s resilience against array of insect pests and pathogens18,19. The interactions between plants and insects sustains ecological dynamics, influencing plant health and pest populations20,21. Plants, especially Salix alba, evolved an arsenal of chemical defenses such as phenolics and tannins to mitigate herbivory22,23. These secondary metabolites play a crucial role in repelling or deterring herbivorous insects. However, the role of salicylic acid in modulating these interactions in Salix alba against Lymantria obfuscata remains un-explored.

The present study investigates the influence of salicylic acid on the interactions between Salix alba and Lymantria obfuscata with an aim to develop sustainable pest management strategies that harness the natural defense mechanisms of Salix alba and least effects on non-target organisms24. Due to climate change and shifting pest dynamics, sustainable approaches to pest management are essential to enhance the ecological resilience and economic viability of Salix alba, particularly in Kashmir, where it plays an important role in the socio-ecological landscape and livelihood security of Kashmir. Cricket bat industry of Kashmir known as “The Kashmir Willow” is one of prime industry in India which is providing livelihood security to large chunk of people after apple cultivation. The outcomes of this study will improve the understanding of plant-insect interactions that align with sustainable and ecological goals by pumping the induced defense mechanism on Salix alba.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Salicylic acid, gallic acid, quercetin, tannic acid, catechin, Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and aluminium chloride were purchased from Merck. All analytical grade chemicals were utilized for the present study.

Experimental site

Field experiments were conducted in the Ganderbal district of Kashmir, located at an altitude of 1,600 m above sea level (34°16’41.8” N latitude and 74°46’45.3” E longitude). The study site comprised hardwood stem cuttings from one-year-old shoots of Salix alba obtained from the Faculty of Forestry Germplasm.

Raising of plants

The hardwood cuttings of Salix alba were raised in a controlled polyhouse environment to ensure uniform growth and development. The temperature inside the polyhouse was maintained between 22 and 28 °C, with relative humidity ranging from 70 to 85%. Cuttings were planted in an artificial medium comprising a mixture of sand, soil and farmyard manure (FYM) in a 1:2:1 ratio, which provided adequate aeration and nutrients for root establishment. The cuttings were raised in polybags of size 6 × 9 inches, providing sufficient space for root development. Before planting, the cuttings were soaked in water to enhance hydration. During the initial growth period, plants were irrigated regularly to maintain optimal moisture levels. A balanced fertilizer (NPK: 20:20:20) was applied at a concentration of 2 g/L fortnightly to supplement essential nutrients. Manual weeding was conducted periodically to prevent competition from unwanted vegetation. Environmental parameters such as temperature and humidity were closely monitored to maintain favorable conditions for plant growth.

Treatments and sample collection

Three concentrations of salicylic acid (2.0, 3.0, and 4.0 mM) were prepared by dissolving salicylic acid in 95% ethanol and further diluting it with double-distilled water. The treatments were applied to freshly harvested, unrooted Salix alba cuttings prior to planting. For the spray method, the prepared solutions were sprayed onto Salix alba cuttings using a baby sprayer until runoff. For the dip method, cuttings were immersed in the respective solutions for 2 min, ensuring uniform absorption. Control plants were treated with double-distilled water. Treatments were applied during the active growing season and samples were collected 21 days post-treatment. Leaf samples were randomly collected from each replicate, placed in labelled plastic bags and immediately transferred to the laboratory for biochemical analysis.

Rearing of Lymantria obfuscata culture

A laboratory culture of Lymantria obfuscata was established to study the interaction between salicylic acid-treated plants and the herbivore. Eggs were collected from the field and maintained in a growth chamber at 25 ± 2 °C with a 16:8 light-dark photoperiod. Upon hatching, larvae were reared on fresh leaves of Salix alba, which were replaced daily to ensure a continuous supply of fresh food. To allow sufficient time for the induction of plant defense responses, third instar larvae were introduced to the treated plants 24 h after the final SA application. From the laboratory culture, the third instar larvae of Lymantria obfuscata were introduced to the treated plants at a density of 2–5 larvae per plant.

Measurement of growth and plant metabolites

Leaf area and leaf defoliation

Leaf area was measured using a digital leaf area meter. Visual defoliation was assessed by adopting the leaf defoliation method, where leaf defoliation (%) was calculated as the ratio of infested leaves to the total number of leaves, expressed as a percentage. Root length and plant height were measured manually using a digital Vernier caliper and a measuring scale, respectively. Root length was recorded from the base of the stem to the tip of the longest root, while plant height was measured from the root collar to the highest shoot tip.

Total phenol content

To calculate the total phenol content, the method described by Singleton and Rossi25 was followed. Briefly, 0.5 g of sample was extracted in 10 mL of 80% methanol and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min. To, 0.5 ml of the sample extract, known quantity of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent (FCR) reagent (0.5 ml) was mixed followed by Sodium carbonate (12 ml, 7.5% w/v) after a 5-minute incubation. The reaction mixture was vortexed and incubated for 30 min. Absorbance was read at 760 nm. using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800, Japan). Gallic acid was used as a standard and results were expressed as µg gallic acid equivalents per gram dry weight (µg GAE g− 1 DW).

Total flavonoid content

Samples were prepared by homogenizing 0.5 g of dried powder in 10 mL of 80% methanol and centrifuging at 10,000 rpm for 15 min to obtain the supernatant. The Aluminium chloride assay was used to measure flavonoid content. A sample aliquot of (0.5 ml) was mixed with 0.3 ml of NaNO₂ (5% w/v), incubated for 6 min. Then, AlCl₃ (0.3 ml) of (10% w/v) was mixed, followed by 2 ml of 1 M NaOH, and the final volume was adjusted with distilled water. Absorbance was read at 510 nm. Rutin was used as a standard and results were expressed as µg rutin equivalents per gram dry weight (µg RE g− 1 DW).

Total tannin content

The vanillin-HCl method was used for tannin quantification. To 1 ml of extract, 5 ml of vanillin-HCl reagent was mixed. The mixture was incubated for 20 min and absorbance was read at 500 nm. Catechin served as the standard and results were expressed as µg catechin equivalents per gram dry weight (µg CE g−1 DW).

Laboratory bioassays

Effect on larval weight, Adult weight and mortality of adults

Salix alba treated with salicylic acid were fed to third instar larvae of Lymantria obfuscata, introduced on Salix alba plants. The larval weights of the last larval instar were recorded after their feeding on the treated and control plants. The effects of salicylic acid on adult weights and adult mortality of Lymantria obfuscate was also quantified to observe any effects between treated and control fed plants of Salix alba.

Quantification of salicylic acid through GC-MS

Quantification was performed using Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS) following the method by Deng et al.26

Instrumental setup

The GC-MS analysis was conducted using a Shimadzu GCMS-QP2020 equipped with an Rtx-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm film thickness). Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The injector temperature was maintained at 250 °C and the ion source temperature was set at 200 °C. The oven temperature was programmed to increase from 50 °C to 250 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min, followed by a hold at 250 °C for 5 min. Mass spectra were recorded in electron impact mode at 70 eV over a mass range of 50–500 m/z.

Sample preparation

Freeze dried leaf samples (500 mg) were homogenized in 5 mL of methanol using a chilled mortar and pestle. The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter. The filtrate was evaporated under a stream of nitrogen to near dryness and reconstituted in 1 mL of methanol for GC-MS analysis.

Quantification process

Salicylic acid was identified by comparing the retention time and mass spectra of the sample peaks with those of the standard. The standard curve was prepared using known concentrations of salicylic acid and the amount of salicylic acid in the samples was calculated based on peak areas.

This method provided precise quantification of salicylic acid levels in the treated Salix alba plants, enabling a correlation between exogenous application and biochemical responses.

Statistical analysis

Data collected from various parameters, including leaf defoliation, larval and adult insect weight, leaf area, plant height, root length, total phenols, flavonoids and tannins were subjected to statistical analysis to determine the significance of differences among treatments. A Completely Randomized Design (CRD) was employed for the experiment, with eight treatments and four replicates per treatment. Data were analyzed using a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to evaluate the main effects of salicylic acid concentration and application method (dip vs. spray), as well as their interaction. This was followed by post-hoc tests (Tukey’s HSD) for pairwise comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 software. A p-value threshold of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were presented as means ± standard errors and all analyses were conducted using statistical software to ensure robust and accurate interpretation of the results.

Results

Effect of salicylic acid on insect-plant interactions

Salicylic acid (SA) effectively reduced leaf defoliation in Salix alba caused by third instar of Lymantria obfuscata. The impact dipping of plant in SA showed only 13.10% leaf defoliation at 4 mM SA, whereas sprayed plants exhibited a slightly higher defoliation rate of 15.50%, shown in Fig. 1. These rates were significantly lower than the control plants, which were found significant. Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant effect of SA treatments on leaf defoliation rates (F (3, 120) = 32.67, p < 0.0001). Post hoc analysis using Tukey’s HSD test showed that plants treated with 4 mM SA (dip and spray) had significantly lower defoliation rates compared to the control and other treatments (p < 0.05). The 4 mM SA dip method exhibited the lowest defoliation rate, making it the most effective method for reducing herbivory in Salix alba.

Discussion

The study assessed the impact of salicylic acid (SA) on Salix alba when infested by Lymantria obfuscata larvae, revealing a significant reduction in leaf defoliation that correlated with increased SA concentrations. Furthermore, a Pearson correlation analysis revealed a strong negative correlation (r = -0.90, p < 0.05) between total phenol content and leaf defoliation, suggesting that the accumulation of phenolics directly contributed to insect resistance. As the concentration of SA rose from 2 mM to 4 mM, leaf defoliation decreased from 18.20% to 13.10%, demonstrating SA’s efficacy in bolstering plant defenses. This supports findings by Mishra et al.27 who reported that SA activates defense-related genes in plants. The method of application was critical, with dip resulting in lower defoliation rates compared to spray method, possibly due to improved absorption and distribution of SA. This observation aligns with Sangpueak et al.28 who found that dip enhances systemic resistance more effectively than foliar sprays. Control plants, which did not receive SA, showed higher defoliation rates of 25.20%, further emphasizing SA’s role as a defense inducer29.

SA treatment also had a significant effect on larval weight. Higher SA concentrations, especially when applied by dip, resulted in a more substantial reduction in larval weight, consistent with previous studies on SA’s inhibitory effects on insect growth (Shah et al.30). The dip method led to lower larval weights compared to spray, suggesting enhanced absorption and defense activation, as noted by McLaughlin and Dearden31. The control larvae exhibited higher weights, underscoring the inhibitory impact of SA on larval development, which aligns with the findings of Ngegba et al.32 on plant-derived compounds that inhibit insect growth and potentially reduce pest populations over time (Table 1).

The reduction in adult weight of Lymantria obfuscata was similarly influenced by SA. Adults emerging from larvae that fed on plants treated with 4 mM SA showed significant weight reductions compared to those treated with 2 mM, reflecting the findings of Punia et al.33, who reported that SA induces physiological stress in insects. Dip method again proved more effective than spray method, leading to lower adult weights due to improved uptake of SA (Torres and Bueno34. Control treatments had higher adult weights, further validating the role of SA in mitigating insect growth (Divekar et al.35).

SA treatment also had a positive effect on vegetative growth parameters in Salix alba. Leaf area increased significantly with higher SA concentrations, particularly with dip method, which resulted in the largest leaf areas. This is consistent with Li et al.36, who reported that SA promotes cell expansion under stress conditions. Control plants had smaller leaf areas, highlighting SA’s role in mitigating pest-induced damage (Yu et al.37). Plant height was similarly affected, with significant increases observed at 4 mM SA. These results support the findings of Li et al.35, who suggested that SA enhances growth regulators such as gibberellins and auxins, thus promoting plant growth even under stress. Dip again was more effective, likely due to better SA absorption and distribution (Telles de Moura et al.38). Control plants were shorter, demonstrating SA’s capacity to mitigate growth reductions caused by pest infestation (Khan et al.39).

Root length was also enhanced by SA treatment, particularly at higher concentrations. This suggests that SA supports root development, corroborating Wilson et al.40, who found that SA strengthens root systems under stress. The dip method resulted in greater root length, likely due to superior absorption of SA through the root system, which promotes growth and resilience against pest attacks (Divekar et al.35).

The study further explored the effect of SA on biochemical parameters, such as total phenol content, flavonoid content and tannin content in Salix alba. SA significantly increased phenol content, particularly with dipping at 4 mM, reaching 32.00 mg GAE/g DW compared to 19.10 mg GAE/g DW in controls. This aligns with Gil-Martin et al.41, who reported that higher SA concentrations enhance the synthesis of phenolic compounds. Dip was more effective than spray, likely due to improved absorption (Siddiqui et al.42; War et al.43). The increase in phenol content indicates that SA boosts plant defenses, reinforcing its role in resistance.

Flavonoid content was similarly enhanced, with the highest level of 6.31 mg QUE/g DW observed in dipped plants treated with 4 mM SA. This supports findings by Jeyasri et al.44, who demonstrated that higher SA concentrations improve secondary metabolite synthesis. Control plants exhibited lower flavonoid levels, underscoring SA’s effectiveness in enhancing flavonoid biosynthesis and plant defense45,46.

Tannin content also increased with SA treatment, reaching the highest level of 3.91 mg/g DW in dipped plants treated with 4 mM SA. Mechanistically, this increase is attributed to the role of SA as a signaling molecule that upregulates the activity of Phenylalanine Ammonia-Lyase (PAL). As the key entry-point enzyme of the phenylpropanoid pathway, enhanced PAL activity accelerates the conversion of phenylalanine into phenolic precursors, thereby diverting carbon flux toward the biosynthesis of condensed tannins47. This mechanism supports Shah et al.30, who reported that SA activates defense pathways leading to increased synthesis of secondary metabolites like tannins. Dip was more effective than spray, corroborating findings by Mahmood and Kataoka et al.48. Control plants had lower tannin levels, further highlighting SA’s role in enhancing plant defenses through tannin production49,50.

GC-MS analysis of Salix alba leaves revealed significant increases in free Salicylic Acid (SA) levels in response to treatment, especially with higher concentrations and dip application. While control plants exhibited trace levels of free SA (below the limit of quantification), the concentration surged to 1.00 mg/g DW in plants dipped in 4 mM SA. This sharp induction supports findings by Gaffney et al.51 and Lam et al.52. The substantial difference between the basal levels in controls and the treated plants suggests that SA application effectively primes the plant’s defense system53,54.

Overall, SA treatments, particularly through dip method, not only reduced leaf defoliation and insect growth but also enhanced the biochemical and morphological attributes of Salix alba, suggesting its potential as a plant defense inducer against Lymantria obfuscata. These findings underscore the importance of optimizing SA application methods to improve plant resistance and pest management strategies (Figs. 11 and 12).

Conclusion

The study demonstrates that salicylic acid (SA) effectively enhances plant defenses and growth in Salix alba infested with Lymantria obfuscata. Application of SA, particularly through dip method, significantly mitigates leaf defoliation, suppresses larval and adult insect growth and promotes plant vigour. Specifically, plants treated with 4 mM SA by dip method showed the lowest defoliation rates, reduced larval and adult weights, increased leaf area and improved plant height and root length compared to controls and sprayed treatments.

Furthermore, SA treatment led to a marked increase in total phenol, flavonoid and tannin content, with the highest concentrations achieved at 4 mM through dip method. These findings highlight the efficacy of SA in boosting the accumulation of defense-related secondary metabolites. Crucially, the targeted GC-MS analysis confirmed that these plants also contained the highest internal levels of SA. This accumulation of SA likely served as the chemical trigger that enhanced the overall concentration of biologically active compounds in Salix alba leaves, thereby potentiating their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and stress-resistance properties.

Overall, SA, especially when applied via dip, significantly improves both the defensive and growth attributes of Salix alba, offering a potent strategy for managing pest infestations and enhancing plant resilience.
