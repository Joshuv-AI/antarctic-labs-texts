Occult Candidemia in Durable Mechanical Support
Authors: Niti Dalal, Aabha Divya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: Candida parapsilosis, candidemia, device endocarditis, FDG-PET/CT, intracranial hemorrhage, left ventricular assist device

Few presentations are as sobering as the case described by Itonaga et al1 in this issue of JACC: Case Reports. A man, 9 years after left ventricular assist device (LVAD) implantation, followed closely in the clinic, afebrile, and with a normal C-reactive protein level, presented with vertigo. The first clear evidence of disseminated fungal infection was not fever, leukocytosis, or driveline drainage, but hemorrhage in the occipital lobe. The case is unsettling because several early clinical signs appeared reassuring, even as the underlying infection was already systemic.

Infection remains a persistent vulnerability of durable mechanical circulatory support (MCS). Even with the fully magnetically levitated HeartMate 3, infection-related complications affect a large share of recipients within 2 years of implantation, and fungal bloodstream infection is the less common but more lethal counterpart to the bacterial driveline infections that clinicians manage routinely. In the largest contemporary single-center cohort, Candida bloodstream infection developed in roughly 3% of LVAD recipients, arrived early (median ≈20 days after implantation), was device-specific in most cases, and carried a crude mortality near 67%, strikingly higher than for bacteremia.2 Against that backdrop, a patient who clears his infection and keeps his device, as this one did, is very much the exception rather than the rule.

The most clinically important feature of this case is the near-total absence of a systemic inflammatory signal. The temperature was normal, C-reactive protein was 0.11 mg/dL, and the only leukocytosis was plausibly attributable to recently prescribed corticosteroids. The 2024 International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation infection definitions anchor the diagnosis of device endocarditis on positive cultures, imaging, or embolic evidence of device involvement, and systemic signs such as fever, leukocytosis, or sepsis.3 This patient satisfied the first 2 criteria while lacking the third, a reminder that the definitions describe a syndrome, not a checklist that must be completed before suspicion is permitted. Blunted febrile responses are well described in this population, and Candida parapsilosis in particular can smolder indolently. The authors are admirably candid that, in hindsight, blood cultures were warranted weeks earlier.

If fever was absent, progressive weight loss was the clue that deserved greater diagnostic weight. The patient lost approximately 12 kg over 2 months while clinicians reasonably considered congestion and later attributed his symptoms to steroid-treated hearing loss. In durable-MCS patients, unexplained weight loss should prompt the same diagnostic reflex as a new murmur, rising lactate dehydrogenase, or unexplained neurologic event. Blood cultures are a low-burden test; delayed recognition of occult infection may carry far greater consequences.

Cerebrovascular events are among the most feared LVAD complications; in MOMENTUM 3 (Multicenter Study of MagLev Technology in Patients Undergoing Mechanical Circulatory Support Therapy With HeartMate 3), any stroke and hemorrhagic stroke especially, sharply reduced 2-year survival.4 Infection is an independent contributor to that risk. A ruptured infectious or mycotic intracranial aneurysm, as occurred here, represents one of the most severe manifestations of that spectrum: distal, fragile aneurysms seeded hematogenously, classically signaled by hemorrhage in a patient with occult bacteremia or fungemia. The teaching point is directional. We are trained to ask whether, when an LVAD patient bleeds intracranially, anticoagulation was excessive. We should also ask the reverse question, whether the bleed is the presentation of an infection we have not yet found. Catheter angiography, which demonstrates aneurysm morphology as in this case, reframes the entire problem (Figure 1).

Once suspicion exists, serial blood cultures, β-D-glucan, transesophageal echocardiography, and, increasingly, 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose-positron emission tomography/computed tomography, which has good sensitivity for localizing device-related infection along the driveline, pump pocket, and outflow graft, should be obtained.5 The abnormal outflow-graft and driveline uptake here was the adjunctive evidence that converted candidemia into presumed device endocarditis despite sterile surgical-site cultures. But positron emission tomography/computed tomography carries 2 caveats: that its specificity is variable, and uptake must be read against the clinical picture, and in the sickest candidemic patients, rapid decompensation often means that advanced imaging may not be feasible.2 This patient's clinical stability allowed a complete diagnostic evaluation to proceed, another reason his course is atypical.

Fungal infection of prosthetic material is notoriously difficult to eradicate with medical therapy, and source control is a pillar of Candida management. Yet LVAD exchange in a heavily adherent, reoperative chest carries formidable bleeding risk, and a meta-analysis found no clear mortality or reinfection advantage to exchange over a device-retention strategy.6 The team's choice of micafungin plus liposomal amphotericin B, surgical irrigation, then suppressive voriconazole with a retained device was defensible and, here, successful, with β-D-glucan normalizing and cultures negative at 1 year. It should be read as a thoughtfully individualized decision in a stable patient, not as a license to default to device retention in fungemia.

The enduring lesson of this report is the importance of maintaining suspicion when the usual inflammatory signals are absent. In durable-MCS patients, particularly after device exchange or reoperation, infection may present without fever or elevated inflammatory markers and with neurologic complications before a bloodstream infection is recognized. Itonaga et al1 recognized the diagnosis in time. The next patient may offer even fewer clues.
