The association between basic medical insurance and the management of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease in China: a cross-sectional study based on the national “Happy Breathing” Programme
Authors: Yiwen Yu, Zixuan Feng, Qiushi Chen, Zhuang Hao, Zhong Cao, Ke Huang, Ping He, Xingyao Tang, Cunbo Jia, Yong Li, Fang Fang, Jun Pan, Till Bärnighausen, Simiao Chen, Ting Yang, Chen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChina has established universal basic medical insurance to reduce financial barriers to improve healthcare access. However, the role of medical insurance in addressing broader population health challenges remains understudied. As China faces a significant challenge in managing the substantial burden of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), it is imperatively important to understand how medical insurance is associated with each step of the care for patients with COPD.MethodsWe used individual-level patient data from the national Chinese “Happy Breathing” Programme from 2018 to 2023. We applied region fixed effects models with modified Poisson regression to assess the association between being insured and reaching each care cascade step: (i) had ever undergone a pulmonary function test, (ii) had been diagnosed with COPD in the past, (iii) were currently on treatment for COPD, and (iv) had achieved COPD control. Stratified analyses were performed by local reimbursement policy, age group, sex, occupation, and residential location.ResultsA total of 8841 COPD patients were included in the analysis, with 98.7% covered by basic medical insurance, including 83.1% under the Urban and Rural Resident Basic Medical Insurance (URRBMI) and 15.6% under the Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance (UEBMI). Patients with URRBMI and UEBMI were more likely to receive a diagnosis compared with the uninsured, with modest risk ratios of 2.20 (95% CI: 1.01–4.78, p = 0.047) and 2.32 (1.06–5.05, p = 0.035), respectively. UEBMI was negatively associated with the treatment step (RR:0.73 (95% CI: 0.55–0.98), p = 0.034), but this effect became insignificant in regions where the prescribed medications for COPD were covered by insurance (RR:0.93 (95% CI: 0.80–1.08), p = 0.338). We also observed that residential location could modify the association between insurance status and being tested.ConclusionsWe found that insurance status was not significantly associated with each step of the COPD care cascade, except for a modest association with a higher proportion diagnosed. Our study underscores the need for optimizing the current medical insurance design, integrating public health interventions and clinical care delivery with insurance coverage, and harmonizing regional policies to enhance the effectiveness and equity of COPD care cascade outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04422-2.

Background

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines Universal Health Coverage (UHC) as ensuring that all individuals have access to the full spectrum of quality health services they need, when and where they need them, without suffering financial hardship [1]. Medical insurance is regarded as a feasible health financing mechanism that can promote equitable distribution of health resources through risk pooling, enhance financial protection, and reduce patients’ out-of-pocket expenses. By alleviating the financial burden on the demand side, medical insurance could contribute to the achievement of UHC.

China has made remarkable progress in establishing a comprehensive basic medical insurance system aimed at reducing financial barriers to clinical treatment. Coverage expanded rapidly from less than 30% in 2003 to approximately 98% within a decade [2]. Nowadays, the basic medical insurance system is dominated by two primary schemes: Urban and Rural Resident Basic Medical Insurance (URRBMI) and Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance (UEBMI). While these schemes primarily focus on covering clinical treatment costs, they often fail to address the broader needs of chronic disease management and neglect public health initiatives. International studies on the association between insurance coverage and chronic disease management yield mixed findings. For instance, a study in India reported no significant effect of medical insurance on improving the hypertension care continuum among young and middle-aged adults [3]. A systematic review indicated that while medical insurance may improve treatment adherence, its association with clinical outcomes varies, ranging from no measurable link to substantial improvement [4]. Additionally, other studies suggest that insurance coverage can enhance health outcomes; however, excessive subsidies may lead to overutilization of medical care [5].

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a major global health issue, ranking as the third leading cause of mortality and disability-adjusted life years worldwide [6]. China alone contributed to about one-third of all deaths from COPD worldwide, with around 0.9 million deaths per year [7, 8]. The disease burden is exacerbated by severe air pollution, high smoking rates, and widespread exposure to second-hand smoke, contributing to a prevalence of 45.16 million cases in 2019 [9, 10]. Projections indicate a 1.5-fold increase in new cases and deaths over the next 25 years, placing substantial strain on the healthcare system [11, 12]. COPD thus requires more effective management and serves as a critical case for evaluating the role of basic medical insurance in disease management.

Nevertheless, research on the association between basic medical insurance and COPD management remains limited. Some empirical evidence from the United States has demonstrated the impact of medical insurance on COPD management, though these studies have primarily focused on specific stages of disease management, particularly treatment and health outcomes [13, 14]. To more effectively support health management and reduce the disease burden among COPD patients in China and other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), locally generated research evidence is urgently needed.

We therefore conducted this study to fill the critical evidence gap by examining how the financing mechanisms of basic medical insurance influence COPD management in China and potentially other LMICs. To achieve this, we applied the emerging “care cascade” framework, a model increasingly adopted to evaluate chronic disease management by tracking patient progression through distinct stages of care, from screening to achieving disease control [15]. We used individual-level baseline data from the nationwide “Happy Breathing” Programme, which represents the COPD care cascade at the time of enrollment, i.e., before participants had received any benefits from the programme’s enhanced COPD management activities, and analyzed the association between basic medical insurance coverage and progression along the COPD care cascade. The findings of our study are expected to highlight persistent gaps in COPD management under the current insurance schemes and provide valuable insights to guide policymakers in advancing healthcare reforms.

Methods

Data source

The national “Happy Breathing” Programme is a prospective, nationwide, ongoing cohort study that was initiated on November 3, 2018, in China. The programme, led by the Respiratory Disease Medical Consortium and the National Clinical Research Center for Respiratory Diseases [16], aims to enroll COPD patients aged 40 years and older and enhance the effectiveness of COPD care. To ensure comprehensive coverage, 32 administrative regions were randomly selected following stratification by economic rank, making the sample theoretically nationally representative [17]. However, despite the initial random selection approach, some regions that were approached declined to participate in the “Happy Breathing” Programme. This may have introduced some selection bias and limited the representativeness of the final sample. Nevertheless, this remains the largest COPD cohort in China to date.

Patients were identified and recruited both actively and passively; they accessed the healthcare system during the study period [16]. For active case finding, physicians at participating health care facilities screened their patients using the COPD Screening Questionnaire (COPD-SQ) as part of the “Happy Breathing” Programme. Those with a COPD-SQ score above 16 and who provided consent underwent post-bronchodilator pulmonary function testing (PFT) [18]. Patients with an FEV1/FVC ratio below 0.7 on the PFT were diagnosed with COPD based on Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) guidelines [19]. For passive case finding, patients with a pre-existing, clinically confirmed COPD diagnosis were also enrolled, provided they gave informed consent (Fig. 1). Additionally, patients with a physician diagnosis of asthma or asthma–COPD overlap syndrome were excluded during the enrollment process, ensuring that the cohort reflected the individuals with a clinical diagnosis of COPD only. Overall, the study utilized baseline data from the “Happy Breathing” Programme collected between November 2018 and April 2024, and our sample represents individuals who sought care within the Chinese healthcare system. Findings based on this sample can offer valuable insights into the reform and optimization of insurance policies to improve the COPD care cascade in China.

COPD care cascade outcome measures

The concept of the care cascade has gained prominence in public health studies as a crucial tool for evaluating disease management. It helps identify gaps in healthcare delivery and guides efforts to address them [15]. Additionally, it can serve as a benchmark for setting aspirational goals, enabling health systems to enhance care for the populations most in need [20, 21]. We defined the COPD care cascade in five stages: (1) tested, (2) diagnosed, (3) treated, (4) controlled with mild or no exacerbations in the past year (controlled 1), and (5) controlled with no exacerbations in the past year (controlled 2) [16].

Patients were defined as tested if they answered “yes” to either of the following questions: “Have you received a PFT?” or “Have you ever received a PFT in the past?”. Patients were diagnosed if they were tested and answered “yes” to the question: “Have you ever been diagnosed with COPD?”. Patients were considered treated if they had been diagnosed and reported receiving inhaled medications recommended by COPD treatment guidelines [22]. Patients were classified as controlled 1 if they were diagnosed with COPD and answered “no” to the question: “Have you been hospitalized in the past 12 months due to acute exacerbation?”. Similarly, patients were classified as controlled 2 if they had no COPD exacerbations in the past 12 months and answered “no” to the question: “Have you experienced any acute exacerbations in the past 12 months?” [23].

Basic medical insurance coverage and covariates

In China, basic medical insurance is a government-established system designed to reduce the financial burden of illness for its residents. By the time baseline data for the “Happy Breathing” Programme were collected, most regions had two main insurance schemes in operation: UEBMI and URRBMI [24]. The reimbursement rates for medical expenses under UEBMI and URRBMI are different, with URRBMI enrollees generally facing higher deductibles and copayments than UEBMI enrollees [25, 26]. These rates may vary depending on the region, specific policies, whether the service is outpatient or inpatient, and the level of the healthcare institution [27, 28]. More details on the different types of basic medical insurance and their reimbursement policies for COPD in China are provided in Additional file 1: Text S1 [29–36].

Patients’ insurance coverage status was determined based on their self-reported response to the survey question: “What is your payment method for medical treatment?” The response options included various basic medical insurance types, commercial health insurance, full public funding, uninsured, and other self-specified insurance. We classified each patient's insurance coverage status as a categorical variable with six distinct categories based on their responses: (1) URRBMI, (2) UEBMI, (3) commercial health insurance, (4) fully covered by public funds, (5) uninsured, and (6) other insurance.

Covariates were selected based on Andersen’s Healthcare Utilization Model and derived from baseline survey data and physical measurements [37]. They were adjusted for in the evaluation of the association between health insurance and cascade outcomes. Andersen’s Healthcare Utilization Model provides a theoretical framework for selecting individual-level variables in our analysis. It conceptualizes healthcare utilization as being determined by three core components. The first component is “predisposing factors.” Accordingly, we included age, gender, education, occupation, place of residence (rural/urban), body mass index (BMI), and smoking status as covariates. Age was categorized as < 65 years and ≥ 65 years. Education level was classified as “below high school” and “high school or above.” Occupation was categorized as “unemployed” and “employed.” BMI was grouped into underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), healthy weight (18.5–23.9 kg/m2), and overweight or obese (≥ 24.0 kg/m2). Smoking status was classified as “non-smoker,” “ex-smoker,” and “current smoker” based on participants’ responses to the survey question on smoking behavior. Those who reported “never smoked” were categorized as non-smokers; those who selected “formerly smoked (and have quit for ≥ 6 months)” were classified as ex-smokers; and those who indicated “currently smoking or have quit for less than 6 months” were categorized as current smokers. The second component is “enabling factors,” represented in our analysis by participants’ insurance status. The third component is “need factors,” which refer to individuals’ perceived need for care. As we used baseline data from the “Happy Breathing” Programme, which is collected before any programme-related interventions, we assumed that patients’ perceived need was not yet influenced by the programme.

The missing data for the variables mentioned above accounted for approximately 1.9% of the total sample, with 1.2% due to missing values in insurance status and the remainder primarily due to missing data on occupation and education. These low levels of missingness in key socioeconomic and insurance variables suggest that our study sample is unlikely to be substantially biased due to missing data.

Statistical analysis

We calculated the proportion of individuals reaching each care cascade step (tested, diagnosed, treated, controlled 1, and controlled 2) among the participants stratified by self-reported insurance coverage status. To evaluate the association between medical insurance coverage and the likelihood of reaching each care cascade step, we applied generalized linear regression models with a Poisson distribution, the log link function, and robust standard errors, i.e., modified Poisson regression for binary outcomes [38]. The estimated relative risk (RR) provided a consistent estimate of the average effect and was deemed easier to interpret than an odds ratio estimated from logistic regression [38–42]. As insurance policies are regulated and under supervision at the regional level, we applied region fixed effects to control for variations in insurance reimbursement policies across geographic regions. Standard errors were also adjusted for clustering at the regional level. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

Additionally, we conducted stratified analyses by age group, sex, occupation, and residential location to examine whether the association between basic medical insurance schemes and COPD management varies across major demographic subgroups.

Lastly, we conducted a range of sensitivity analyses to check the robustness and consistency of our findings under different model specifications and assumptions. China’s reimbursement system for COPD is highly complex and varies significantly across regions (Additional file 1: Text S1). At the time of data collection, both diagnosis and testing for COPD were covered under basic medical insurance schemes. However, while some regions had gradually included COPD treatment in their outpatient reimbursement lists (ORL), others provided little or no coverage for outpatient COPD treatment [43]. Given the continuous enrollment in the “Happy Breathing” Programme, patients’ eligibility for outpatient reimbursement depended on whether their prescriptions were issued after COPD had been included in their region’s ORL. To address this variability, we first conducted a sensitivity analysis comparing outcomes for patients in regions where COPD had been added to the ORL (defined by region-year) with those in regions where COPD had not yet been included (see Additional file 1: Table S1 for a complete list of regions and the dates they included COPD in their ORL).

According to the 2024 GOLD guidelines [44], the treatment of COPD includes not only inhaled therapies but also non-inhaled pharmacological treatments and non-pharmacological interventions. Therefore, in our second sensitivity analysis, we broadened the definition of COPD treatment to encompass a wider range of therapeutic modalities. This allowed us to further assess the association between basic medical insurance coverage and this broader definition of treatment, as well as its relationship with subsequent exacerbation control. In this analysis, non-inhaled pharmacological treatments included oral β-agonists (e.g., salbutamol, terbutaline), intravenous or oral theophyllines, expectorants, systemic corticosteroids, and traditional Chinese medicine or proprietary Chinese medicines. Non-pharmacological interventions included influenza vaccination, pneumococcal vaccination, smoking cessation counseling, long-term oxygen therapy, and home non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV). Notably, several of these non-pharmacological interventions, such as smoking cessation counseling, long-term oxygen therapy, home NIPPV, and vaccinations, are not currently covered under the reimbursement scope of basic medical insurance. This lack of coverage may limit both the accessibility and adherence to these treatments among patients.

In the third sensitivity analysis, women in China often have a low prevalence of smoking but higher exposure to household air pollution due to the use of biomass fuels for cooking. To assess the potential confounding effect of biomass fuel exposure on the association between medical insurance and COPD management, we extracted data from the survey question: “Do you primarily use biomass fuel (e.g., corn stalks, corn cobs, coal, animal dung) for cooking or heating?”, with response options of “yes” or “no”. We included a binary indicator of biomass fuel exposure as a covariate in the modified Poisson regression model to examine whether the observed associations remained robust after adjustment.

All data and statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.1.1), and the fixed effects models were implemented using the fixest package version 0.10.1 in R.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval was obtained from the China–Japan Friendship Hospital (approval number: 2019–41-k29). All participants provided written informed consent for participation. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 9040 COPD patients with quality-controlled PFT results and who met our inclusion criteria were included. After excluding individuals with missing sociodemographic information (n = 10), those with insurance types outside our scope, which accounted for less than 2% of the total population (n = 162), and regions with fewer than five patients (n = 27), 8841 COPD patients were included in the final study (Fig. 1). The analysis focused on three primary insurance statuses: Uninsured, UEBMI, and URRBMI. The final cohort included COPD patients from 22 regions. A summary of their baseline characteristics and comparisons across the three insurance subgroups is presented in Table 1. P-values were calculated using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. The cohort had a median age of 73 years, and 24.9% were female. Overall, 98.7% of patients were covered by basic medical insurance, with 83.1% enrolled in URRBMI and 15.6% in UEBMI. COPD care cascade outcomes across varied insurance coverage

Figure 2 demonstrates the proportions of COPD patients reaching each step of the care cascade, categorized by different basic medical insurance statuses. The proportion of COPD patients reaching each cascade step was generally highest among those insured with UEBMI, followed by URRBMI, and lowest in the uninsured group. Specifically, for COPD patients with URRBMI, the proportion reaching each step was higher than for the uninsured, except for the testing step. Among uninsured patients, 22.6% (95% confidence interval (CI): 15.9–31.1%) were tested. In comparison, 18.0% (95% CI: 17.1–18.9%) of patients covered by URRBMI and 30.6% (95% CI: 28.2–33.0%) of patients covered by UEBMI were tested. There is a significant drop in the proportion of patients successfully moving from testing to diagnosis, especially for uninsured groups. At this cascade step, only 5.2% (95% CI: 2.4–10.9%) of uninsured patients were diagnosed with COPD. Among URRBMI patients, 12.2% (95% CI: 11.4–12.9%) were diagnosed, while 22.7% (95% CI: 20.6–25.0%) of UEBMI patients were diagnosed. There were 11.6% samples having uptake of inhaled treatment after being diagnosed with COPD. Among them, 4.3% (95% CI: 1.9–9.8%) of uninsured patients were treated. The proportion was 10.6% (95% CI: 9.9–11.3%) for URRBMI patients and 17.6% (95% CI: 15.7–19.7%) for UEBMI patients. The overall availability of all categories of inhaled medication was presented in Additional file 1: Table S2, suggesting insufficient supply of all COPD-related drugs. The most commonly used was the anticholinergic Tiotropium bromide (LAMA), with a utilization prevalence of 32.7%. The second and third most commonly used medications were Formoterol + Budesonide (ICS + LABA) and Salmeterol + Fluticasone (ICS + LABA), with prevalences of 31.7% and 13.4%, respectively. In terms of disease control, 1.7% (95% CI: 0.5–6.1%) of uninsured patients experienced no severe exacerbations in the past 12 months. This was compared to 5.6% (95% CI: 5.1–6.2%) of URRBMI patients and 9.6% (95% CI: 8.1–11.2%) of UEBMI patients. Association between basic medical insurance and the COPD care cascade

The results of the region-fixed effects models are summarized in Table 2. After adjusting for age group, sex, education level, BMI, smoking status, occupation, residence, and region fixed effects, insurance status did not show a significant association with the likelihood of being tested or achieving successful control of COPD. However, in the diagnosis step, patients with URRBMI and UEBMI were more than twice as likely to receive a clinical diagnosis after receiving PFT compared to the uninsured group, with RRs of 2.20 (95% CI: 1.01–4.78, p = 0.047) and 2.32 (95% CI: 1.06–5.05, p = 0.035), respectively. For receiving treatment after diagnosis, patients with UEBMI had a 26.6% lower probability of receiving standardized care (RR 0.73 (95% CI: 0.55–0.98), p = 0.034) compared to the uninsured group. Similarly, the association between URRBMI and COPD treatment showed a comparable trend, with an RR of 0.76 (95% CI: 0.57–1.00, p = 0.050). Sensitivity analyses

Among the subgroup with outpatient COPD treatment covered by the ORL, insured patients (regardless of insurance type) no longer had significantly lower probabilities of receiving treatment compared to uninsured patients, with RRs of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.80–1.08, p = 0.338) for UEBMI patients and 0.94 (95% CI: 0.83–1.06, p = 0.317) for URRBMI patients (Additional file 1: Table S3a). Similarly, when treatment was defined to include inhaled therapies, non-inhaled medications, and non-pharmacological interventions, insured patients also no longer had significantly lower probabilities of receiving treatment (Additional file 1: Table S3b).

To address the concern regarding potential confounding by biomass fuel exposure, we included biomass fuel exposure as a covariate in our regression model and obtained very similar results (Additional file 1: Table S4), suggesting that our primary findings are robust and not substantially confounded by biomass fuel use.

Discussion

Our study, based on China’s largest national COPD sample from the “Happy Breathing” Programme, is the first comprehensive analysis of the association between basic medical insurance and the full cascade of care for COPD. Among the 8841 COPD patients included in the study, 98.7% were covered by basic medical insurance, and only a small proportion were uninsured (1.3%). This is consistent with the enrollment status of the basic medical insurance system in China, which maintained a coverage rate of around 98% following a series of insurance reforms [2]. Among those covered by basic medical insurance, URRBMI enrollment greatly exceeds UEBMI enrollment, largely because rural residents, who have a higher prevalence of COPD, are predominantly enrolled in URRBMI rather than UEBMI [45, 46].

Notably, there was a high proportion of non-smokers among the uninsured group (Table 1). These uninsured samples represent those who, despite lacking insurance, still sought care at healthcare facilities. Such behavior may reflect a higher level of health awareness, which could also explain their non–smoking status.

Furthermore, our regression results suggested no significant association between current insurance schemes and the rate of PFT. This outcome may be explained by two main reasons. From the perspective of service access, the availability of PFT devices remains limited. According to a 2022 nationwide survey covering 8176 primary healthcare institutions across 31 provinces in China, the overall availability rate of PFT devices in primary care facilities was only 8.9% (667/7458). Specifically, the availability was 18.6% (289/1557) in community health service centers and merely 6.4% (378/5901) in township health centers [47]. In September 2024, COPD was included in the National Essential Public Health Service Programme, which aims to ensure basic healthcare guarantees for COPD patients and provides a platform to accelerate the implementation of PFTs [48]. Our results further highlight the need to address supply-side constraints by expanding the provision and maintenance of PFT equipment. From the perspective of service incentives, the predominant pay-for-service model within the current basic medical insurance system tends to encourage hospitals and physicians to focus on treatment rather than preventive testing. As a result, most PFTs are often performed for symptomatic COPD patients rather than being used as a screening tool for high-risk individuals. Reforms of the medical insurance system, which tilted towards COPD screening, have the potential to improve the well-being of COPD patients [49]. This shift would align financial incentives with public health priorities by promoting preventive care and early diagnosis, ultimately leading to better health outcomes.

We found a modest association between basic medical insurance coverage and a higher diagnosis rate compared with the uninsured. However, our results indicate that patients currently covered by the insurance scheme are still far from achieving the 30% awareness rate set as a target by the Healthy China Initiatives (2019–2030) [50]. Nevertheless, strengthening the role of the insurance scheme could be a key strategy to reach this target, as the cost-sharing mechanism of insurance helps reduce financial barriers that may deter patients from seeking medical evaluation and diagnosis.

Moreover, we observed a negative association between insurance coverage and inhaled medication use at the treatment stage. A possible explanation is that uninsured patients are generally in a more disadvantaged economic position compared to those with insurance. When these patients still choose to seek care at healthcare facilities, it may indicate more severe symptoms of COPD. As a result, they are more likely to take treatment after diagnosis. In contrast, insured patients may have better economic means and more affordable access to medical services, and their condition at the time of diagnosis may be relatively milder, leading to lower reliance on treatment. This hypothesis was supported by our heterogeneity analysis by residential status: we found consistent evidence with the main analysis in rural areas (Table 3). This may be explained by the fact that uninsured rural patients face greater challenges in accessing medical resources and affording care, making them more likely to be diagnosed at a more advanced stage of COPD, thus requiring inhaled medications for symptom control. Although the probability of inhaled medication use is higher in this subgroup, their actual COPD control rate remains low due to disease severity.

Furthermore, as shown in Additional file 1: Table S2, the range of inhaled medications used by COPD patients in the Chinese healthcare system was quite limited, despite their essential role in COPD management. This finding is consistent with multiple studies that have highlighted an unmet therapeutic need for COPD patients in LMICs [51, 52]. For instance, Yang et al. reported that more than 10% of high-risk COPD patients (GOLD group E) had never received any long-acting inhaled medications [53].

Our study provides valuable insights into the association between insurance coverage and the outcomes along the COPD care cascade. First, UEBMI outperformed URRBMI and lack of insurance in promoting progression along the COPD care cascade, likely due to its stronger financial protection. However, the current basic medical insurance system as a whole remains insufficient to comprehensively improve all steps of the COPD care cascade [54]. Second, the current basic medical insurance system must be complemented by supply-side interventions, such as the provision of PFT and inhaled medications, to achieve greater effectiveness. Third, under the traditional fee-for-service model, the financial returns of healthcare providers are closely tied to the severity of patients’ conditions and the volume of medical expenses incurred, which creates little economic incentive for providers to proactively implement public health interventions. This misalignment not only hinders the improvement of population health but also leads to serious waste of medical resources [55]. Our results suggest that the insurance system could further improve population health if it prioritizes health management rather than solely focusing on treatment. To address this, the integration of public health initiatives with medical services should be incorporated into the basic medical insurance system [56]. However, integrating additional services into basic medical insurance would inevitably increase the government's fiscal burden. In this context, a multi-tiered medical insurance system, supported by broader channels of social financing, could help alleviate the limitations faced by basic medical insurance alone. Lastly, insurance schemes are affected by regional policy variations, such as differing outpatient COPD reimbursement policies, leading to disparities in healthcare access and financial protection for COPD patients across regions. To address these inequities, stronger oversight from the central government and a more unified national policy approach may be required.

Our study has several limitations. First, although the initial sample included 32 regions selected to ensure broad national coverage, several regions declined to participate in the “Happy Breathing” Programme, resulting in a final sample of 22 regions. This reduction may limit the external validity of our findings, as the final sample reflects only those regions that were both selected and agreed to participate, and does not constitute a nationally representative sample. Second, due to the survey data being obtained from baseline, we were unable to assess the longitudinal association between basic medical insurance and the long-term care cascade outcomes of COPD patients. Future research is needed to assess whether the relationship between basic medical insurance and COPD care cascade outcomes changes over time. Third, although the multivariable regression analysis showed modest positive associations of the two insurance types with the diagnosed step of the COPD care cascade, considering the unbalanced sizes of the three insurance subgroups for comparison, these results did not demonstrate a substantial role of the insurance in improving the diagnosed proportion, as the possibility of random error or bias cannot be fully excluded. On the other hand, such interpretations are in line with our non-significant findings for other steps of the COPD care cascade, implying an overall limited effect of the current insurance policy on COPD management. Finally, due to the limited number of uninsured participants in our sample, we were unable to perform further stratified analyses to validate and deepen the interpretation of our findings. Future efforts will focus on ongoing recruitment to increase the sample size and enhance the robustness of subsequent analyses.

Conclusions

This study is the first to comprehensively examine the association between basic medical insurance and outcomes in the COPD care cascade for patients aged 40 and above in China, using national data from the “Happy Breathing” Programme. Using region fixed effects models, we found that insurance status was not significantly associated with each step of the COPD care cascade, except for a modest association with a higher proportion diagnosed. Our study underscores the need for optimizing the current medical insurance design, integrating public health interventions and clinical care delivery with insurance coverage, and harmonizing regional policies to enhance both the effectiveness and equity of COPD care cascade outcomes.
