Perceptions, attitudes, practices, and barriers towards research in standardized training of laboratory medicine trainees: a cross-sectional questionnaire-based survey
Authors: Zheng-Xiang Gao, Lingyi Yan, Menglan Zhang, Hua Shi, Ge Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo assess research perceptions, current practices, barriers, and training needs among trainees in a standardized laboratory medicine training program, and to provide evidence to support research capacity building within the training system. A cross-sectional survey was administered to all 56 trainees (32 first-year and 24 s-year) in the Department of Laboratory Medicine at West China Second University Hospital using an anonymous online questionnaire. Trainees generally recognized the value of research, with all participants acknowledging its role in enhancing competitiveness and critical thinking. However, positive perceptions declined from the first to the second training year. The agreement scores regarding research “helpful solve clinical problems” declined significantly from 4.38 ± 0.50 to 3.71 ± 0.75 (P < 0.001). Similarly, scores for “important part of profession” decreased from 4.41 ± 0.50 to 3.88 ± 0.68 (P < 0.01), and scores for “improve thinking” also dropped from 4.47 ± 0.51 to 4.13 ± 0.34 (P < 0.01). Regarding research practice, although more than half of the trainees had participated in basic activities such as data collection and literature retrieval, none had authored or published a scientific paper. Major barriers included insufficient training, limited mentor guidance, and uncertainty about how to begin. Case reports were the most preferred type of research project. Training needs were pronounced, with more than 75% of trainees expressing strong demand for introductory research courses, literature search training, academic writing courses, and one-on-one guidance. The strongest preference for training format was direct participation in mentor-guided research. Although laboratory medicine trainees hold positive perceptions of research, their practical involvement remains limited to rudimentary activities. The main barriers lie in the lack of research training and mentor guidance. Therefore, it is recommended to implement research training and a mentorship system within the training program to enhance trainees’ research capabilities.Keywords: Laboratory medicine, Standardized training, Barrier, Scientific research ability, TraineesSubject terms: Health care, Health occupations, Medical research

Introduction

Laboratory medicine is a multidisciplinary field that integrates advanced scientific technologies with clinical practice and is defined by its distinctive applied functions. Modern laboratory practice has expanded far beyond basic technical operations and the simple issuance of test reports. It now includes the establishment of laboratory systems, optimization of analytical processes, quality control oversight, interpretation of diagnostic findings, and effective communication with clinicians. Consequently, these expanded responsibilities impose more stringent requirements on laboratory professionals, who must possess solid theoretical knowledge, innovative scientific thinking, and strong analytical and problem-solving abilities to support the ongoing development of laboratory medicine1–3. In this context, standardized training for laboratory technologists plays a critical role in cultivating the next generation of laboratory professionals. These programs aim to equip trainees with comprehensive professional knowledge, the ability to identify and resolve challenges throughout the clinical testing process, and essential foundational research skills. This training aims to develop practitioners who not only possess comprehensive diagnostic expertise but are also adept at identifying and solving problems in the clinical testing process, all while maintaining foundational research capabilities.

Although the value of research training is widely recognized, medical residency programs worldwide continue to struggle with implementing this educational objective effectively4. Numerous studies have reported substantial barriers that hinder residents’ participation in research, including limited protected time for both residents and faculty5, insufficient funding, and the lack of structured training curricula6. Moreover, interventions designed to enhance research productivity often require long periods to produce measurable outcomes; many residents may acquire research skills yet still fail to complete research outputs before finishing their training. In China, these challenges are particularly multifaceted. Trainees frequently encounter (1) time constraints due to demanding rotations and heavy workloads; (2) system-level barriers, including the absence of structured research curricula, sustainable mentorship models, and effective incentive mechanisms; and (3) skills-related limitations, as many trainees begin their programs without essential competencies in literature review, study design, data analysis, or academic writing, which hinders their ability to complete research projects7–9.

Despite increasing emphasis on integrating research training into residency education, existing literature has focused primarily on trainees in internal medicine, surgery, and pathology10–12. In contrast, the study focus on the research training of laboratory medicine trainees whose discipline is characterized by strong technical specialization and distinct research pathways remains very limited. Importantly, research in laboratory medicine emphasizes identifying associations between laboratory indicators and clinical phenotypes, indicating that its needs and training models differ substantially from those of traditional clinical departments. However, little is known about laboratory medicine trainees’ research perceptions, the obstacles they encounter, their preferred research activities, or their expectations for training modalities.

Based on these considerations, this study aimed to systematically evaluates research awareness, practical engagement, perceived barriers, and training needs among trainees in the department of laboratory medicine in our hospital using a structured questionnaire. The findings are intended to provide empirical evidence to support the optimization of standardized training programs and inform the development of targeted strategies to strengthen medical research capacity among trainees.

Methods

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design conducted on August 8, 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University, and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Study design and participants

An online questionnaire was simultaneously distributed to all trainees enrolled in standardized training in the Department of Laboratory Medicine, West China Second University Hospital. The primary objective was to systematically assess trainees’ current perceptions of scientific research, their level of engagement in research activities, and the major barriers they encounter. The study population consisted of first- and second-year trainees who participated voluntarily.

Data collection

The questionnaire was developed based on previously published survey instruments13–15, with targeted revisions and additions to reflect the educational characteristics of laboratory medicine and the specific context of research activities. A pilot test involving 15 trainees was conducted to assess clarity and applicability, and their feedback was used to refine item wording and questionnaire structure. The questionnaire was administered during a mandatory monthly training session attended by all trainees. Survey participation was voluntary, and all trainees present (n = 56) opted to complete it, yielding a 100% response rate among the total enrolled population at the time of the survey. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, and validity was evaluated using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure.

The final questionnaire comprised five sections:

The first section collected demographic information, including three statements: gender, training year, and major.

The second section assessed research perception, including five statements (interesting, important part of profession, improve thinking, helpful to solve clinical problems, enhance employment competitiveness).

The third section focused on Research practice and interests, consisting of two subsections: (a) Previous forms of participation in research, including 8 statements (project or experiments design, experimental involvement, data collection, statistical analysis, literature review, paper submission, as a co-author and academic conference attendance). (b) Preferred research types, including 5 statements (literature review, case report, meta-analysis, retrospective study, experimental research).

The fourth section evaluated perceived barriers to research, including 7 statements.

The fifth section evaluated research-training needs, consisting of two subsections: (a) desired topics for research-skills training (4 statements). (b) Preferred training modalities (5 statements).

Research perception toward research was evaluated using a five-point Likert scale from1 to 5 (1 = strongly disagree; 2 = disagree, 3 = uncertain,4 = agree, 5 = strongly agree).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and numbers. For ease of reporting and analyzing data, “agree” and “strongly agree” responses were combined into an “agreement” group, whereas “disagree” and “strongly disagree” responses were combined into a “disagreement” group. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare differences in the frequency of research practice and encountered barriers between training years, whereas t-tests were applied to assess differences in research attitudes. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Reliability and validity of the questionnaire

The reliability of the questionnaire was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. For the research perception domain, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.83, indicating good internal consistency. The KMO value for this domain was 0.82, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ² = 109.29, p < 0.001), indicating that the data were appropriate for factor analysis. Principal component analysis with varimax rotation extracted a single factor that accounted for 61.51% of the total variance.

The response rate for both first- and second-year trainees was 100%. A total of 56 trainees completed the questionnaire, including 32 first-year and 24 s-year trainees. Among the participants, approximately 25% were male and 75% were female.

Perceptions regarding scientific research among trainees

All trainees recognized the importance of scientific research, particularly its role in enhancing competitiveness, strengthening professional competence, and improving thinking. As shown in Fig. 1; Table 1, our research found that all first-year trainees (100%) believed that research contributes to solving clinical problems, whereas only 70.94% of second-year trainees agreed (4.38 ± 0.50 vs. 3.71 ± 0.75, P < 0.001). All trainees unanimously agreed that research enhances competitiveness and improves thinking ability with a significantly greater agreement that research is improve thinking was found in first-year trainees (4.47 ± 0.51 vs. 4.13 ± 0.34, P < 0.01). Regarding interest in research,84.37% of first-year trainees expressed interest, compared with 70.83% of second-year trainees. Similarly, all first-year trainees (100%) agreed that research improves professional capability, whereas this proportion decreased to 70.83% among second-year trainees (4.47 ± 0.51 vs. 4.13 ± 0.34, P < 0.01).

Participation in research activities

As shown in Table 2, more than half of the trainees reported participating in research-related activities. The most common activities among first-year trainees were data collection (87.5%) and literature review (84.38%), followed by statistical analysis (68.75%). Similarly, among second-year trainees, the most frequent activities were data collection (66.67%) and statistical analysis (58.33%), followed by literature review (54.17%). Participation in scientific manuscript authorship was the lowest across both groups, with no trainees having co-authored or published research articles (0%).The proportion of first-year trainees who participated in literature review was significantly higher than that of second-year trainees(P<0.05).

Barriers to conducting scientific research

As shown in Table 3 lack of training was the most frequently reported barrier among trainees in first- (81.25%) and second-year trainees (83.33%). Among first-year trainees, the second reported barriers were lack of mentor guidance (65.63%) and uncertainty about how to begin (59.38%). For second-year trainees, the second reported barriers were lack of mentor guidance (66.67%), limited English and uncertainty about how to begin were equally frequent barriers (both 62.50%). No significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding specific research barriers (P > 0.05).

As shown in Fig. 2, case reports were the most frequently preferred research type among both first- and second-year trainees. Among first-year trainees, retrospective studies were the second most preferred option (59.38%), followed by literature reviews (56.25%) and experimental studies (50.00%). Research training needs and preferred training formats

As shown in Fig. 3, trainees in both groups expressed relatively balanced preferences across all four types of research training content: introductory courses, literature retrieval, one-on-one guidance, and academic writing workshops. At least 75% of trainees in each group selected every training topic. Regarding preferred training formats (Fig. 4), both groups expressed the strongest preference for direct participation in mentors’ ongoing research projects. Discussion

Scientific research competency is a core component of modern medical training. It not only drives disciplinary advancement but also enables physicians to transition from clinical practitioners to academic contributors and future leaders16. Within medical education, standardized residency training represents a pivotal stage in which young physicians develop independent clinical reasoning and professional identity. Against this backdrop, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of laboratory medicine residents’ research perceptions, engagement, and training needs.

In this study, we found that the trainees who completed the questionnaire exhibited an overall positive attitude toward scientific research. All trainees agreed that research contributes to enhancing their competitiveness and critical thinking. This finding aligns with global reports showing that medical students recognize the value of scientific research and commonly express strong interest and favorable attitudes17. The findings of this study further confirmed that all trainees regarded research as important, and over 70% expressed interest in engaging in scientific research, consistent with domestic studies reporting high recognition of research value among clinical medical students13 and slightly higher than the international average of approximately 75% 18.

However, our findings revealed a decline in agreement from the first to the second training year regarding key dimensions such as “helpful to solve clinical problems” “important part of profession”, and “improve thinking”. A plausible explanation is that first-year trainees may hold an idealized perception of research, whereas second-year trainees, after initial exposure to actual research tasks, may develop a more realistic and sometimes discouraged view due to the methodological complexity, challenges, and substantial time investment involved. This interpretation is supported by our finding that “lack of training”, “lack of mentor guidance” and “uncertainty about how to begin” were identified as the most prominent barriers by trainees at both training levels.

More than half of the trainees had participated in literature reviews, data collection, statistical analysis, and manuscript preparation, indicating moderate exposure to research activities. However, their involvement was largely limited to auxiliary or early-stage tasks. Notably, none had published a paper as an author, a rate far lower than those reported for trainees in the United States (75%) or Australia (23.3%) 19,20. Dissemination of research findings through conference presentations and journal publications is a critical step in scientific inquiry and an essential component of research training, helping trainees develop communication skills, appreciate academic contribution, and improve research quality21–23.

The findings of this study indicate that trainees’ engagement in research remains fragmented and superficial, with few experiencing the full continuum of the research cycle from study conception and design to data analysis, manuscript preparation, and eventual publication. Such fragmented participation is insufficient for cultivating independent research capability and may contribute to declining motivation over time. This gap further indicates that current training systems may lack structured mechanisms to transition trainees from passive assistants to active project leaders.

Trainees expressed the strongest interest in case reports, likely because this format is more accessible, clinically intuitive, and feasible for trainees to complete under supervision. The study has shown that cross-sectional studies, case reports, and narrative reviews are the most common research types among medical students, as they require fewer methodological skills and less supervision than more complex designs24. However, these formats also tend to have lower publication acceptance rates.

This study has found that insufficient training as the most widely acknowledged barrier to research engagement among trainees, followed by the lack of mentor guidance. These findings are consistent with previous researches4,24–26. Interestingly, ‘lack of time’ was cited by half of first-year and one-third of second-year trainees, indicating it remains a notable though secondary barrier compared to ‘lack of training’ and ‘lack of mentor guidance,’ which were reported by over 80% and 65% of trainees, respectively. Meanwhile, trainees expressed strong and comparable demand (all ≥ 75%) for four core research competencies: introduction to medical research, literature retrieval, one-on-one guidance, academic paper writing course. This found indicate that deficiencies in structured research curricula and practical methodological guidance represent the primary impediments preventing trainees from effectively conducting research and progressing toward manuscript publication.

Moreover, trainees most strongly favored participating in supervisors’ research projects, indicating a clear preference for practice-oriented learning. Project-based learning (PBL) is widely recognized as an effective pedagogical approach that bridges the gap between theory and practice. Embedding skills such as literature retrieval, research project design, and manuscript writing within authentic research tasks allows PBL to help trainees overcome the common difficulty of “uncertainty about how to begin,” increase authorship opportunities, and improve the likelihood of producing publishable outcomes. Previous studies also have shown that participation in mandatory or elective research programs significantly increases publication rates17,26.Based on the data, the program manager should consider establishing structured mentorship programs in which faculty with strong research backgrounds supervise trainees through accessible research projects such as case reports. Meanwhile, the courses such as literature retrieval, biostatistics, and operation of scientific visualization software should be offered. This approach could integrate essential skills into ongoing projects,

In addition, clearly defining each trainee’s contributions and authorship eligibility may further enhance motivation and sustain their engagement, ultimately fostering the development of research capacity.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. It was conducted at a single institution with a relatively small sample size, which may limit generalizability. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate these findings. Incorporating perspectives from faculty and administrators may help develop more targeted, system-level strategies for strengthening research training.

Conclusion

In this single-center study of laboratory medicine trainees, our findings indicate that trainees recognize the importance of scientific research for enhancing competitiveness, professional development, and critical thinking ability. However, insufficient training in research skills and lack of supervisory guidance remain the principal barriers to research participation. These insights may inform the development of tailored research support strategies within similar institutional training contexts, though broader multi-center studies are needed to generalize these findings.
