Assessing the Utility and Safety of Intravenous Iodinated Contrast Media in CT ‘Pan Scan’ Studies in Geriatric Patients Following Low‐Energy Blunt Trauma
Authors: Christopher Witkowski, Dinesh Varma, Warren Clements
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionGround level falls are common in the geriatric population, often culminating in Emergency Department attendance and a CT ‘pan scan’. These scans are routinely performed with intravenous iodinated contrast media (ICM). The types of injuries identified in this cohort can often be adequately characterised without ICM. Safety issues exist with ICM use, with contrast‐induced acute kidney injury (CI‐AKI) particularly relevant to the geriatric population. This study assessed the diagnostic utility and safety of ICM administration in this cohort.MethodsA retrospective case control study of geriatric patients undergoing CT pan scan over a 6‐month period was performed. Patients were included if they had suffered a ground level fall. The radiology report for each patient was reviewed to assess injury types. The creatinine of each patient who received ICM was assessed to determine the presence of CI‐AKI.ResultsA total of 549 patients were included in the final analysis. Two hundred four patients suffered at least one traumatic injury. The majority of injuries were musculoskeletal and spinal, with solid organ and vascular injuries only occurring in a small minority. Injuries were more frequently reported on non‐contrast studies. 15.4% of patients suffered a CI‐AKI.ConclusionGiven the types of injuries identified in geriatric patients following low‐energy blunt trauma, and when considering CI‐AKI and other costs, routine use of ICM on CT pan scans in this population is not necessary. Reserving ICM studies only for patients deemed clinically high risk or with radiologically concerning findings on an initial non‐contrast CT is a reasonable compromise.

Introduction

Ground level falls are a common occurrence in the geriatric population, often resulting in presentation to the Emergency Department [1]. Despite the low‐energy mechanism, injury in this cohort is common. These patients are increasingly being referred for a computed tomography (CT) trauma study—commonly referred to as a ‘pan scan’—which includes a scan range from skull vertex to proximal femora [1, 2]. The chest, abdomen and pelvis component of this scan is routinely performed with intravenous iodinated contrast media (ICM) to maximise the diagnostic yield of these scans for solid organ and vascular injury [3]. However, given the low‐energy mechanism of trauma, the type of injuries sustained can often be identified and satisfactorily characterised in the absence of ICM, as demonstrated during the global contrast media shortage of 2022 [4].

Numerous safety, cost and efficiency considerations exist regarding the use of ICM. Despite extensive research, there is uncertainty as to the safety of ICM use and contrast‐induced acute kidney injury (CI‐AKI) in those with reduced renal function, particularly with an eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 [5]. The elderly population is the most common age group associated with reduced renal function. Additionally, further safety issues exist regarding the use of ICM, namely adverse/hypersensitivity reactions (including anaphylaxis), thyrotoxicosis, exacerbation of myasthenia gravis and pulmonary effects such as bronchospasm and pulmonary oedema [5, 6, 7]. It has been estimated that administration of contrast media confers a risk of allergy‐related mortality of approximately 0.9 per 100,000 patients [8]. Other considerations regarding ICM use include the requirement for intravenous access (which might otherwise be avoided), the risk of contrast extravasation [5, 6, 7], the cost of the contrast media, sustainability including contrast‐related waste [9] and workflow efficiency as a result of the increased time it takes to perform a contrast‐enhanced study compared with a non‐contrast study.

While numerous research has previously investigated the utility of pan scans in this cohort [10, 11, 12, 13, 14], there is a relative lack of data on the diagnostic benefit of iodinated contrast administration, as well as the incidence of CI‐AKI in this specific population. As such, this study aimed to assess the safety and diagnostic utility of ICM‐enhanced pan scan CT studies in a geriatric population following low‐energy blunt trauma.

Methods

Ethics

Approval for this retrospective case control study was obtained from the Alfred Human Research and Ethics committee, which included a waiver of consent, approval number 807/24. This study is reported according to the STROBE checklist for observational studies.

Study Population, Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

In our cohort, the geriatric population was defined as patients aged ≥ 65 years at the time of scanning. A retrospective search of the Radiology Information System for the period 1 January 2024 to 30 June 2024 was undertaken to identify geriatric patients who underwent ‘CT chest/abdomen/pelvis/thoracolumbar spine’, which constitutes trauma CT of the torso. The studies referred for ground‐level fall from standing or sitting height were included. If the mechanism of injury was unclear or not detailed within the radiology request, the electronic medical record was reviewed to ascertain this information. Patients were excluded if they did not meet these criteria, if it was unclear as to the mechanism of trauma or the reason for being referred, or if they were on dialysis which would preclude assessment for CI‐AKI.

Data Collection—CI‐AKI

Each eligible study was evaluated to determine whether they received ICM. The medical record for those who received ICM was then reviewed, with a baseline pre‐CT serum creatinine and post‐CT serum creatinine recorded if performed (the peak serum creatinine within 48 h of the CT). The presence of a CI‐AKI was then calculated as a serum creatinine rise of ≥ 26.5 μmol/L or ≥ 50% according to our institutional definition [6, 15].

Data Collection—Traumatic Injuries

The radiology report for each study was reviewed, and the injuries identified in each study were recorded. Injuries were categorised into seven injury types—macrovascular, microvascular, abdominal solid organ, abdominal hollow organ, lung/pleural, musculoskeletal (excluding spinal) and spinal. Macrovascular injuries were defined as direct injuries to a named artery or vein. Microvascular injuries were defined as injuries, which demonstrated haematoma with contrast blush, without identifiable injury to a named vessel.

Statistical Analysis

Data were directly entered into a spreadsheet (Microsoft Excel, Microsoft, Redmond, Washington, USA). Data were categorised as continuous or nominal. Continuous data were assessed for normalisation and presented as either mean (standard deviation or SD) or median (range). According to the type of data, groups were compared for differences using Student's t‐test, rank‐sum test or chi‐squared test using Stata (MP 18.5, StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). A two‐tailed p value of < 0.05 was deemed significant.

Results

A total of 550 studies met inclusion criteria during the study period. One of the patients who received ICM was excluded due to the patient being on haemodialysis. This resulted in a final cohort of 549 patients, 461 of which received ICM and 88 did not. The study population included 319 females and 230 males. Summary statistics are shown in Table 1. The mean age of the cohorts was slightly higher in the non‐contrast group (84.5 vs. 82.6 years, p = 0.009).

Traumatic Injuries

Of the 549 patients, 204 (37.2%) had at least one acute traumatic injury identified on the CT. One hundred sixty‐three studies reported an injury to only one injury category, 36 were afflicted by injuries to two categories and five cases had injuries to three categories. No cases suffered injuries to more than three injury categories. Injuries were non‐significantly more frequently reported on non‐contrast studies, with 42.0% of patients suffering at least one injury, compared with 36.2% of patients on contrast enhanced studies (p = 0.301).

The majority of injuries were musculoskeletal in nature, affecting 154 patients (28.1% of total cohort). Rib fractures were the dominant injury within this category, with femoral neck fractures, pelvic fractures and soft tissue haematomas (without contrast blush) comprising the majority of the remainder of the cases.

Spinal fractures were the second most common injury category, affecting 71 patients (12.9%). Lung or pleural injuries were seen in 11 cases (2.0%), microvascular injuries in 10 cases (1.8%), abdominal solid organ injury in three cases (0.5%) and macrovascular injury in only one case (0.2%). No patients were identified as having an abdominal hollow organ injury.

As shown in Table 2, when comparing the groups, the proportion of musculoskeletal injuries were higher in the non‐contrast group (37.5% vs. 26.2%, p = 0.031). The remaining injuries identified were similar between the non‐contrast and ICM groups (p > 0.05 for all other injury categories).

Contrast‐Induced Acute Kidney Injury

Of the 461 patients who received ICM, 260 had a creatinine result prior to and following the CT that allowed assessment for CI‐AKI. Forty (15.4%) of these patients suffered a CI‐AKI.

Discussion

Concordant with worldwide practice, the use of CT pan scans in the geriatric population following low‐energy blunt trauma is common at our institution, with a mean of more than three patients per day across the study period [10]. The presence of at least one traumatic injury was identified in 37.2% of patients, correlating closely with the 39.4% reported by Kania et al. [11] While injuries are common in this population, the types of injuries incurred were predominantly musculoskeletal and spinal in nature. These can be satisfactorily identified and assessed on a non‐contrast study, with 71.2% of the injuries in the non‐contrast group being musculoskeletal and 23.9% being spinal in nature. In fact, identification of musculoskeletal injuries was more common in the non‐contrast cohort than for the ICM cohort (37.5% vs. 26.2%, p = 0.031), although this is potentially confounded by the statistically significant higher mean age of the non‐contrast group (p = 0.009). The identification of injuries in all other groups were similar. Furthermore, the reported lung/pleural injuries could be adequately interpreted in the absence of ICM.

ICM however is particularly important in the assessment of abdominal solid organ injuries, vascular injuries and inferring areas of active bleeding through the presence of contrast blush. These injuries were seen in only a small minority of cases, with three solid organ injuries, one macrovascular injury and 10 microvascular injuries making up only 6.9% of the injuries identified in the ICM group. The macrovascular injury coincided with a high‐grade splenic injury. The presence of an injury would have been identifiable on a non‐contrast study given the perisplenic haematoma and haemoperitoneum; however, it would not have been satisfactorily characterised. The other two solid organ injuries identified in the study cohort were an American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) grade II and AAST grade III splenic injury [16]. Again the presence of a significant injury in the latter case would have been inferred by the presence of perisplenic haematoma and haemoperitoneum on a non‐contrast study; however, it is not completely characterised in the absence of ICM. The grade II injury could plausibly have been missed on a non‐contrast study given the relatively subtle parenchymal change and small subcapsular haematoma; however, the rate of delayed re‐bleed in this cohort is low [17]. If suspicion of injury was identified however, a decision would then be required to determine the need for rescanning with ICM.

Although 10 microvascular injuries were identified, the presence of a haematoma at the site of the contrast blush would be identifiable on a non‐contrast study. Two of these patients required embolisation, including a retroperitoneal haematoma and a pelvic sidewall haematoma. None of the further eight patients underwent endovascular or surgical intervention to specifically address the finding of contrast blush on CT [18]. These were conservatively managed with compression and ice, with one patient also receiving intravenous vitamin K. The foci of bleeding in these patients were predominantly pelvic/gluteal and proximal thigh in location, with the patients haemodynamically stable (shock index < 1).

Overall, the types of injuries most commonly identified in our study correlate well with previous studies investigating outcomes of low‐energy blunt trauma in geriatric patients. Kim et al. [12] observed rib fracture, haematoma and spinal injury as the three most common injuries of the torso, while also noting that perisplenic fluid was only demonstrated in 2% and splenic laceration in 1% of patients. Furthermore, they identified only one of 152 patients (0.7%) requiring ‘major change in management … (surgical or procedural intervention)’, namely the insertion of a thoracostomy tube [12]—the underlying pathology of which would be identifiable on a non‐contrast study. Similarly, Gartin and colleagues reported intra‐abdominal solid organ injury in only 0.9% of patients, while also listing rib fractures as the most common injury [13]. None of the patients with solid organ injuries required surgical intervention [13]. The presence of anticoagulation has been demonstrated to not correlate with higher rates of intra‐abdominal injury or the need for surgical or endovascular intervention [19]. Limited intervention following falls from standing height was also acknowledged by Baig et al., determining that no surgical, endovascular or blood transfusion requirements resulted from patients with ‘body injury’, with no cases of body injury related mortality observed [20].

Of patients who received ICM and had a calculatable creatinine trend, 15.4% suffered a CI‐AKI. Given that only 13 studies (2.4%) included injuries that would not have been accurately characterised on non‐contrast studies—of which eight were microvascular injuries managed conservatively—the improved diagnostic yield provided by ICM is significantly offset by the potential morbidity and increased costs incurred from CI‐AKI in an elderly population. Healthcare costs are becoming increasingly important as healthcare expenditure is rising dramatically in all countries [21].

Based on these results, geriatric patients with a low‐energy injury mechanism may be triaged according to risk. High‐risk patients would be considered those who are shocked at any point during their presentation (shock index > 1), have anaemia on their initial blood testing (serum haemoglobin less than 115 g/dL), are Focussed Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST) positive, or demonstrate peritonism on examination, and should be triaged for ICM CT. All other patients may be triaged for non‐contrast CT initially and reviewed for signs of injury. The presence of any injury would then require further triage to determine if a repeat scan with contrast is required, particularly if there are concerns for solid organ or microvascular/macrovascular injury on the initial CT to ensure satisfactory characterisation. These patients would receive priority access to the scanner to mitigate delays in diagnosis and intervention. This kind of protocol as shown in Figure 1 would improve efficiency, reduce cost, reduce CI‐AKI and still obviate the risk of missed injuries. While workflow efficiency would be reduced on an individual basis in those that need repeat scanning, overall efficiency would be greatly increased by the overwhelming proportion of patients requiring only a non‐contrast study. The use of a ‘hot read’ system for trauma—similar to what is commonly done for stroke CT—may reduce these risks further, while also improving relevancy for radiologists within the trauma multidisciplinary team. The increased radiation dose a patient would receive from a repeat scan would have no deterministic consequences, while stochastic risks are minimal given patient age.

The study acknowledges several limitations. First, the reasons for ICM being withheld for each patient was not known, inherent to the design of a large retrospective study. In the authors' experience, ICM is most frequently withheld in patients with known renal impairment, and in cases of allergy to iodinated contrast and inability to obtain timely intravenous access. Other limitations include the lack of physiological clinical data and information on blood product resuscitation. There is also a risk of missed injury in the non‐contrast CT group, with macrovascular, abdominal solid organ and particularly microvascular injuries potentially underestimated in this cohort. The ability to detect statistically significant differences in macrovascular and abdominal solid organ injuries between the two groups is inherently limited by the rarity of these injuries. The relative lack of follow‐up creatinine data in the ICM group has the potential to overestimate the rate of CI‐AKI. The study also uses injury as an endpoint for determining the value of CT, and there may be other relevant endpoints such as pain, morbidity or mortality.

Conclusion

The use of ICM undoubtedly optimises diagnostic accuracy in the interpretation of CT pan scans, particularly for high‐energy mechanisms. However, this study shows that the use of ICM specifically in a geriatric population following low‐energy blunt trauma had no difference in the total diagnostic yield compared to ICM scans, as the majority of injuries were musculoskeletal or spinal. This is particularly pertinent given the potential morbidity and costs resulting from contrast use including CI‐AKI. Reserving ICM studies for patients at high risk or with radiologically concerning abnormal findings on an initial non‐contrast CT study may be a reasonable compromise.
