Numerical Investigation of Thermally Induced Fracturing via Endothermic Thermal Shock in Ultratight High-Temperature Gas Reservoirs
Authors: Fahad Khan, Zeeshan Tariq, Mohamed Mahmoud, Arshad Raza, Murtadha J. AlTammar, Abdulazeez Abdulraheem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydraulic fracturing operation in ultratight gas reservoirs under high temperature has often proved inadequate due to large fluid requirements, limited fracture propagation, and rapid production decline. This study proposes and evaluates a novel cooling-driven stimulation technique in which an externally heated rock is subjected to rapid thermal shock through the injection of endothermic chemicals. Unlike conventional thermochemical fracturing methods that rely on heat generation and pressure buildup, the proposed approach utilizes rapid heat extraction to induce tensile stresses, fracture initiation, and enhanced reservoir connectivity. Laboratory-scale experiments were conducted on tight sandstone and shale samples, confirming the mechanical degradation and microcrack development due to thermally induced tensile stresses. These findings were integrated into a finite element model using the concrete damage plasticity (CDP) in ABAQUS to simulate fracture development. The resulting fracture morphologies were subsequently incorporated into a field-scale compositional reservoir model in CMG-GEM to evaluate long-term production performance. The simulation study investigates the impact of the thermal shock treatment on production performance across five different scenarios: (i) unstimulated, (ii) single-stage hydraulically fractured, (iii) multistage hydraulic fractures, (iv) thermochemical fractures with limited complexity, and (v) thermochemically fractured with high complexity. The results demonstrate that thermochemical stimulation significantly enhances fracture complexity, stimulated reservoir volume, and gas recovery. The cumulative gas production and the recovery factor were found to be enhanced by approximately 1.6 times with thermochemical stimulation as compared to the multistage hydraulic fracture case. The high-complexity thermochemical case achieved the highest production rate and recovery factor, outperforming conventional methods throughout the simulation period. These findings demonstrate the viability of thermally induced fracturing via endothermic reactions as a promising and efficient stimulation method for enhancing gas recovery in ultratight formations.

Introduction

With a continuous surge in global energy demand and the decline of conventional hydrocarbon reserves, unconventional reservoirs (i.e., shale gas, tight sandstones, and ultralow-permeability formations) are assisting in sustaining energy supply. However, despite their volumetric richness, production from ultratight reservoirs is often economically challenging due to their extremely low matrix permeability, typically less than 0.1 mD. − The extremely low pore connectivity in such reservoir blocks the natural flow of hydrocarbon, leading to poor well deliverability and a rapid decline in production rate. Such reservoirs require unconventional ways, such as acidization or hydraulic fracturing, to produce economically. − Hydraulic fracturing operations have been widely applied to improve reservoir conductivity by injecting a large amount of fracturing fluid into the reservoir at a pressure above formation fracture pressure. , Conventional hydraulic fracturing, while effective in many unconventional reservoirs, often struggles to achieve economically viable production rates in ultratight formations due to high fracturing fluid costs, limited fracture network propagation, high temperature conditions, and rapid production decline. , Thermochemical fracturing has emerged as a promising alternative stimulation technique in high-temperature reservoirs, leveraging endothermic chemical reactions to induce fractures and enhance permeability in these challenging reservoirs. − This approach offers unique advantages, including enhanced fracture complexity and improved reservoir connectivity, making it particularly beneficial for ultratight high-temperature reservoirs such as shale gas, tight oil, and coalbed methane reservoirs. , Endothermic chemicals can react immediately downhole and absorb heat, inducing a sudden thermal shock. This thermal shock develops tensile stress in the rock due to sudden contraction, leading to the generations of microcracks, which facilitate the hydraulic fracturing operations in ultratight reservoirs. , Traditional thermal fracturing through various mechanisms has been widely investigated, including in situ heating via steam injection, electric heaters, or exothermic chemical, generating high temperatures along with additional pressure pulse within the formation. − These methods have shown promising results in reducing formation breakdown pressure, improving fracture propagation, and enhancing the stimulated reservoir volume (SRV). , However, these methods also face operational complexity, safety concerns, and limited control over the reaction kinetics in the subsurface.

In contrast to these heat-generating approaches, the current study proposes a novel technique of thermally inducing fractures based on a thermal shock mechanism in two steps. First, heating the rock externally to simulate subsurface temperature conditions followed by injecting endothermic chemicals that reacts immediately and cool down the formation, resulting in a sharp and sudden temperature drop near the wellbore region. This thermal shock generates localized tensile stresses due to differential contraction between the hot rock matrix and the cooled interface, leading to the generation of fractures in the rock formation. − This approach is fundamentally different from conventional fracturing or thermochemical fracturing. It does not rely on fluid pressure or internal heat generation but utilizes the intrinsic mechanical response of formation rocks to sudden cooling after interacting with endothermic chemicals. This method can be controlled by regulating the heating temperature, endothermic chemicals concentration, and injection timing. While the thermal fracturing has been examined widely in the context of steam injection or exothermic chemical reactions, there is very limited work done on endothermic-based thermal shock as a stimulation mechanism. The novelty of this study lies in demonstrating a cooling-driven fracturing mechanism based on endothermic thermal shock, which differs fundamentally from conventional thermochemical methods relying on heat generation. This study aims to fill the gap by developing a multiscale, simulation-based evaluation framework combining laboratory results, finite element modeling, and compositional reservoir simulation to assess the performance of endothermic chemical-based fracturing in an ultratight gas reservoir. Despite extensive research on hydraulic fracturing, thermochemical stimulation, cryogenic fracturing, and thermal stimulation using steam or exothermic reactions, limited attention has been given to the use of endothermic thermal shock as a primary mechanism for fracture generation in ultratight formations. Most existing thermochemical stimulation methods rely on heat generation, pressure pulses, or cyclic thermal loading to weaken the rock matrix and enhance permeability. In contrast, the present study investigates a fundamentally different cooling-driven stimulation mechanism in which endothermic reactions rapidly extract heat from a preheated formation, generating localized tensile stresses through thermal contraction and inducing fracture initiation and propagation. Furthermore, this work develops an integrated multiscale framework that combines laboratory observations, thermomechanical fracture simulation using the Concrete Damage Plasticity model in ABAQUS, and field-scale compositional reservoir simulation in CMG-GEM to evaluate both fracture development and long-term production performance. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no previous study has systematically linked endothermic thermal shock experiments with thermomechanical fracture modeling and reservoir-scale production forecasting for ultratight gas reservoirs. Therefore, the study provides both a new stimulation concept and a comprehensive methodology for evaluating its effectiveness under realistic reservoir conditions. The overall objective of this study is to demonstrate the viability and advantages of thermochemical fracturing for enhancing gas recovery from ultratight formations and to provide a rigorous simulation-based evaluation of its impact on pressure behavior, gas saturation, and recovery factor over an extended production period.

Materials and Methods

Material Required

The laboratory experiments were conducted on two tight unconventional rocks, namely Kentucky sandstone and Eagle Ford shale, which were procured from Kocurek Industries. The endothermic chemical reactions were performed by mixing ammonium chloride (NH4Cl) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH). Analytical grade NH4Cl was supplied from Honeywell, while the NaOH pellets having a molecular mass of 40 g/mol were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. For heating the core sample, Memmert oven was used which has a maximum heating capacity up to a temperature of 250 °C. The real-time pressure and temperature inside the core sample was monitored using Omega-PX409 series pressure transducer and Omega-RDXL4SD temperature logger, respectively.

Workflow Methodology

This study employs a comprehensive multiscale, multiphysics workflow to simulate the thermomechanical behavior and production performance of an ultratight gas reservoir that is subjected to thermochemical stimulation using an endothermic chemical reaction. The methodology integrates laboratory-scale experiments, finite-element mechanical modeling, and field-scale compositional fluid flow simulation to capture the complex interactions between thermal, mechanical, and flow processes across different scales. The objective of the study is to simulate the fracture initiation, propagation, and long-term production behavior after the induced thermal shock due to the endothermic chemical treatment in an ultratight formation. The workflow comprises the following steps:

Laboratory Experiments for Fracture Generation

Controlled laboratory experiments were conducted using endothermic chemicals to simulate the induced thermal shock and sudden temperature reduction in tight sandstone and shale rock samples. The experiments were conducted under similar conditions on multiple cylindrical core samples of each lithology, having a length of 3 in. and diameter of 1.5 in. The detailed experimental study was conducted in our previous. , The primary objective of the experiments was to investigate the degradation of the mechanical properties of these rock samples and to observe the development of microcracks due to the external heating followed by sudden cooling using endothermic chemicals. The rock samples were initially subjected to controlled heating up to a temperature of 150 °C from the ambient temperature of ∼23 °C, after that 2 h thermal soak period was given in order to ensure the uniform internal temperature in the rock samples.

After the heating process, the rock samples were suddenly cooled down using the endothermic chemical by directly interacting the chemicals with the heated rock surface. The rapid absorption of heat from the rock surface develops a sharp temperature gradient across the rock sample. This sudden cooling generates tensile thermal stress, leading to the development of microcracks in the rock matrix. In previous studies, SEM results showed multiple crack generation on both rock samples due to the thermal shock effect. These experimental observations provided the basis for defining the mechanical damage conditions used in subsequent finite element modeling. It is important to note that the laboratory experiments are intended to provide mechanistic validation of thermal shock–induced damage rather than a direct quantitative reconstruction of field-scale fracture networks. The experiments validate the relationship between rapid cooling, tensile stress generation, and microcrack development, which forms the basis of the numerical modeling framework. Quantitative indicators, including temperature reduction rate, cooling duration, and spatial extent of visible cracking, were used to constrain the damage initiation thresholds applied in the subsequent thermomechanical simulations.

Fracture Morphology Simulation Using Finite Element Method (ABAQUS)

The second stage involved the modeling of the mechanical response of the rock-fluid system under thermochemical stresses using ABAQUS, leveraging the CDP model. The rock matrix was modeled as a homogeneous and isotropic medium to capture the first-order thermomechanical response under thermal shock conditions. This assumption is commonly adopted in thermomechanical fracture modeling to isolate the dominant physics of thermal stress generation and damage evolution. While natural reservoirs exhibit anisotropy due to bedding, fractures, and mineral heterogeneity, incorporating such complexities would significantly increase model uncertainty and computational cost. Therefore, the present study focuses on establishing a fundamental understanding of the thermal shock mechanism at the near-wellbore scale. The rock was subjected to an induced thermal shock through a sudden drop in surface temperature to replicate the cooling effect caused by the endothermic chemicals. The CDP model was employed to simulate rock weakening, crack initiation, and propagation under thermally induced tensile and compressive stresses. The ABAQUS simulations produced detailed 3D fracture morphologies that replicate field-observed patterns including complex, branched networks.

Flow Simulation Using a Finite Difference Compositional Simulator

The fracture geometries obtained from the mechanical simulation were then imported into a compositional reservoir simulator from Computer Modeling Group (CMG-GEM) to analyze fluid flow behavior. A dual-porosity, dual-permeability framework was adopted to simulate gas production from ultratight rocks. The simulation incorporated realistic reservoir dimensions, initial conditions, and production constraints to evaluate the long-term impact of induced fractures on the gas recovery. The high-permeability fracture zones were explicitly modeled to evaluate enhanced fluid flow, while gas–water interactions were accounted for by using multiphase flow equations. The simulations were performed under both stimulation scenarios, including the base case having no induced fractures and the endothermic chemical-treated case having the induced fractures. These scenarios were compared to quantify the enhancement in the recovery factor and stimulated reservoir volume.

Performance Evaluation and Optimization

The final step involved evaluating production performance based on key performance indicators, including pressure distribution, gas saturation profiles, recovery factor, wellbore bottomhole pressure, average reservoir pressure, and field water cut. Simulation results were compared among the three stimulation scenarios, including the base case having no fractures, conventional hydraulic fracturing, and chemically induced fracturing with varying complexity levels.

Governing Equations

The governing equations employed for the numerical simulation are composed of two distinct modeling domains. The first one is the static mechanical simulation used to predict fracture initiation and propagation due to thermal shock, and the second one is the dynamic flow simulation for evaluating the gas transport and reservoir performance after the stimulation. The equations are based on continuum mechanics and multiphase flow theory and are solved by using finite element and finite difference techniques in ABAQUS and CMG-GEM, respectively.

Numerical Simulation Models

Numerical modeling was conducted based on the thermal cooling effect from endothermic chemical reactions. The simulation accounted for temperature-dependent rock failure, stress redistribution, and localized fracturing near the wellbore. The simulation workflow is divided into two main stages: first, the static mechanical modeling using finite element analysis to simulate fracture generation due to endothermic chemicals thermal shock, and second, the dynamic flow simulation using a compositional reservoir simulator to assess the long-term production performance. The fracture morphologies generated in the mechanical simulation were integrated into the reservoir-scale flow model to capture their influence on gas recovery.

Static Numerical Model (ABAQUS)

A three-dimensional model of the tight rock sample was developed using the commercial simulator ABAQUS to simulate thermally induced fracture development. The model geometry comprises a homogeneous rock having a single porous and permeable region and a single vertical borehole at its center. The mesh was refined locally around the borehole by using seed partitioning to accurately capture stress concentrations and reduce numerical dispersion. The CDP model was used to represent the mechanical degradation of the rock under thermal and mechanical stresses. The damage and failure response were evaluated based on inelastic strain and temperature-dependent stress redistribution. Model Validation and Calibration

The thermomechanical model developed in ABAQUS was calibrated and validated using laboratory observations obtained from the endothermic thermal shock experiments performed on Kentucky sandstone and Eagle Ford shale samples. The objective of the validation process was not to reproduce an exact field-scale fracture geometry, but rather to verify that the numerical model captures the dominant physical mechanisms responsible for thermal shock-induced damage. The laboratory experiments demonstrated rapid temperature reduction following the interaction of heated rock samples with endothermic chemicals. Kentucky sandstone exhibited a temperature reduction from 150 to 56.7 °C within approximately 12 s, while Eagle Ford shale cooled from 150 to 57.8 °C within approximately 14 s. These experimentally measured cooling conditions were used to define the thermal loading boundary conditions applied in the ABAQUS simulations.

In addition to thermal calibration, qualitative validation was performed by comparing the observed damage characteristics to the predicted numerical response. Experimental observations reported in previous studies revealed the development of surface cracks, tensile damage zones, and localized mechanical degradation following thermal shock treatment. , ABAQUS simulations reproduced similar behavior, including stress concentration near the cooled boundary, tensile damage localization, crack initiation in high-stress regions, and fracture propagation away from the cooling interface.

The consistency between experimentally observed thermal damage patterns and numerically predicted fracture initiation mechanisms provides confidence that the model captures the dominant thermomechanical processes governing endothermic thermal shock stimulation. Although a direct quantitative comparison of fracture geometry was not possible due to the absence of full-field fracture characterization data, the agreement in cooling behavior, damage localization, and fracture initiation trends supports the validity of the proposed numerical framework.

Field-Scale Numerical Simulation Model (CMG-GEM)

A field-scale compositional simulation was performed by using CMG-GEM to quantify the long-term production performance and the impact of thermochemically induced fractures on gas recovery. This simulation incorporated detailed fracture geometries generated from the static mechanical simulations conducted in ABAQUS. The extracted fracture morphology revealed complex and branched fracture networks characterized by varying apertures, lengths, and orientations, which are representative of fracture geometries typically observed in field-scale thermochemical stimulation.

Fracture Morphology Integration

The fracture networks generated from the ABAQUS simulation were postprocessed and embedded into the reservoir simulation grid. Fracture geometries characterized by varying apertures, lengths, and orientations were mapped onto a dual-porosity/dual-permeability framework. This approach allowed differentiation between matrix flow and fracture flow, allowing accurate modeling of gas transport and accumulation mechanisms in ultratight media. Fractures were modeled as high-permeability conduits to simulate their role in enhancing flow connectivity and reservoir drainage. The methodology for fracture embedding was executed using a pseudocode provided in Appendix A, which generated complex branching fracture patterns representative of thermally induced damage zones.

Reservoir Description

The simulated reservoir model represents an ultratight gas formation with a spatial domain of 20,000 ft × 10,000 ft × 60 ft. The reservoir matrix is characterized by extremely low permeability, which is less than 0.1 mD. The model was discretized into 80,000 simulation cells using a Cartesian grid configuration of 400 × 200 × 1 grid blocks along the X, Y, and Z directions, respectively. A horizontal production well having a length of 8000 ft was placed at the center of the reservoir, as shown in Figure . The initial reservoir pressure was considered to be 3000 psi. The production from the horizontal well was simulated at a constant gas-flow rate of 12 MMSCF/day at a constraint of bottomhole flowing pressure of 500 psi.

Simulation Framework

A multiphase compositional flow model was adopted to account for the simultaneous flow of gas and formation water under reservoir depletion conditions. Several key assumptions were applied. The gas phase was assumed to consist of a dry methane-rich mixture, representing a typical ultratight gas reservoir. Water was assumed to remain immobile beyond the irreducible water saturation (S wi = 0.16) except in cases where capillary-driven flow was initiated. The fractures generated through induced thermal shock during thermochemical stimulation were modeled as high-permeability conduits with a permeability of 5000 mD, significantly exceeding the matrix permeability of 0.1 mD. These fractures were also assigned higher porosity and lower capillary entry pressure as compared to the matrix to reflect their enhanced transmissibility and altered petrophysical characteristics following thermal shock treatment. The governing transport equations for compositional multiphase flow were solved by using the finite-difference method. Capillary pressure and relative permeability were incorporated through correlations based on the Brooks-Corey model to capture the nonlinear saturation-dependent flow behavior in both matrix and fracture domains. Conceptual Design of Multistage Thermochemical Fracturing

The multistage thermochemical fracturing strategy developed in this study involves a combination of horizontal well technology with staged chemical injection to enhance reservoir permeability and improve gas recovery in ultratight formations. The concept is based on inducing fracture complexity and connectivity through thermally driven tensile failure using endothermic chemical fluids. These fluids extract heat from the surrounding rock, thereby generating localized cooling and thermal contraction, leading to the fracture propagation and branching. The rock matrix was modeled as a homogeneous and isotropic medium to capture first-order fracture initiation and propagation driven by thermal shock–induced tensile stresses. This assumption is appropriate at the laboratory and near-wellbore scale considered in this study and has been widely adopted in prior thermomechanical fracture modeling studies. , The stimulation process is divided into the following key stages and is also shown in Figure .

Horizontal Well Drilling

A horizontal well is drilled through the target reservoir interval to maximize the contact with the low-permeability rock matrix. The wellbore traverses the reservoir laterally and is designed to accommodate multiple stimulation stages, enabling distributed access to the reservoir and facilitating the staged stimulation along its length.

Multistage Fracturing

The horizontal well is divided into several stimulation stages using mechanical isolation devices, such as packers or sliding sleeves. In each stage, primary fractures are initiated by using conventional pressure-based techniques to initiate longitudinal fractures extending from the wellbore into the formation. These primary fractures serve as the initial pathways for fluid injection and form the basis for the subsequent fracture growth during thermochemical treatment.

Thermochemical Fracturing at Fracture Tips

Following the initiation of primary fractures in each stimulation stage, endothermic chemical fluids are strategically injected into or near the fracture tips. These fluids induce localized thermal gradients by extracting heat from the surrounding rock, resulting in rapid cooling and associated thermal contraction. The sudden temperature reduction develops tensile stresses within the rock matrix, which, when they exceed the tensile strength of the rock, lead to further fracture propagation and the formation of secondary fracture branches. This thermally driven mechanism enhances fracture complexity by promoting the extension of existing fractures into previously unstimulated zones and also by facilitating the nucleation of new fracture paths. , Additionally, the reduction in rock strength in the near-tip region contributes to more efficient fracture propagation under tight rock conditions. By repeating this process across multiple stages along the horizontal well, a high-stimulated reservoir volume (SRV) is achieved. The resulting fracture network significantly improves reservoir connectivity, facilitating gas desorption and matrix drainage into the wellbore. Compared with single-stage or purely hydraulic treatments, this approach leads to a more uniform and effective stimulation of the entire lateral section. This targeted thermochemical stimulation enhances the complexity and connectivity of the fracture system without requiring excessive fluid volume or proppant injection.

Results and Discussion

This section presents a detailed analysis of the results obtained from both the static and flow numerical simulations. The evaluation focuses on the stress distribution, the development of damage zones, fracture morphology, reservoir pressure profiles, gas saturation, and overall production performance across the five stimulation scenarios. Comparative results are discussed in terms of pressure depletion, gas saturation, stimulated reservoir volume (SRV), cumulative production, and the recovery factor.

Temperature Reduction Rate

The rate of temperature reduction due to the endothermic chemical reaction on Kentucky sandstone and Eagle Ford shale was monitored, with both core samples initially heated up to temperatures of 150 °C. The temperature reduction rate of both core samples is shown in Figure . It can be observed that upon interaction with endothermic chemicals at 44 s, there is a sharp decline in temperature for both types of rock, indicating the onset of thermal shock. Kentucky sandstone exhibited a temperature drop from 150 to 56.7 °C within 12 s, corresponding to an average initial cooling rate of 7.77 °C/s. On the other hand, Eagle Ford shale showed temperature decline from 150 to 57.8 °C in just 14 s, resulting in an average initial cooling rate of 6.58 °C/s. This rapid heat extraction from the rock surface is attributed to the highly endothermic nature of the NH4Cl-NaOH reaction, which absorbs substantial thermal energy from the surrounding rock matrix. After the initial cooling, both rocks experienced a small rise in temperature due to the internal heat conduction from the core sample to the surface. This was followed by the gradual decline in temperature, stabilizing at approximately 28 °C for both rocks. The slightly higher temperature reduction in Kentucky Sandstone can be attributed to its higher thermal conductivity (2.5–4.2 Wm–1 K–1) compared to that of Eagle Ford Shale (1.05–1.45 Wm–1 K–1), enabling more efficient heat transfer and faster thermal equilibrium. The enhanced thermal conductivity of sandstone allows for quicker redistribution of internal heat following the initial surface cooling, leading to more rapid stabilization.

These damaged zones form the basis for constructing the fracture networks, subsequently integrated into the reservoir-scale compositional simulation. The accurate representation of the fracture morphology is critical for evaluating the influence of thermochemical stimulation on long-term reservoir performance.

Case 1: Reservoir Performance without Stimulation

The base case represents the unstimulated reservoir conditions, serving as a reference for evaluating the effectiveness of conventional fracturing and thermochemical stimulation. In this case, the reservoir is modeled as a homogeneous ultratight formation with extremely low permeability and no induced fractures. Figure a shows the layout of the reservoir model that represents the entire formation with a centrally positioned horizontal wellbore. The wellbore geometry is embedded within a low-permeability formation without any artificially induced fractures. Figure b shows the reservoir pressure distribution after five years of production. The color scale indicates pressure in psi, with red representing the initial reservoir pressure (3000 psi) and blue showing the depleted zones. It can be observed that due to the lack of fracture, there is poor pressure propagation into the surrounding matrix, leading to limited drainage capability of the unstimulated reservoir. Figure c shows the corresponding gas saturation distribution at the end of the simulation period. The saturation scale reveals a confined region of increased gas saturation near the wellbore, while the remaining reservoir region exhibits negligible variation. This highlights the minimal stimulated reservoir volume (SRV) and confirms that gas production remains confined to a small area due to the ultralow permeability of the formation. Without any fractures to improve connectivity and flow, reservoir pressure remains high, and the gas recovery is the minimum.

Case 2: Single-Stage Hydraulic Fracture

In the single-stage hydraulic fracturing scenario, a single planar fracture was introduced at the wellbore to assess its effect on flow performance in the ultratight reservoir. As shown in Figure , the fracture is oriented orthogonally to the horizontal well and provides a localized high-permeability pathway to improve the connectivity between the wellbore and the matrix. This configuration enables enhanced gas drainage along the fracture plane compared to the unstimulated case, facilitating a slight improvement in pressure drawdown and gas mobility. However, because of the limited areal coverage and lack of fracture complexity, the stimulated reservoir volume (SRV) remains lower. The consistency of this trend across multiple stimulation scenarios confirms that the observed improvement is robust and not dependent on a single fracture configuration. The observed production enhancement is fundamentally controlled by the increase in fracture complexity and stimulated reservoir volume. In ultratight formations, matrix permeability is insufficient to sustain economic production without an extensive conductive fracture network. As fracture complexity increases, the average flow distance from the matrix to the nearest conductive pathway decreases substantially. This reduces flow resistance, accelerates pressure propagation, and improves gas drainage efficiency. Consequently, the production response observed in Cases 4 and 5 reflects not only increased fracture conductivity but also a more effective utilization of reservoir volume. Discussion

Thermally induced damage and fracture development were simulated at the rock scale using the CDP model in ABAQUS with parameters calibrated from experimental data and literature values. While ultratight formations may exhibit anisotropic and temperature-dependent behavior, incorporating such effects would significantly increase model complexity and computational cost. It is acknowledged that the assumption of homogeneous and isotropic rock behavior represents a simplification of real reservoir conditions. In practice, geological heterogeneity and anisotropy may influence the fracture propagation paths and connectivity. Future work will extend the current framework to include anisotropic elastic properties, natural fracture networks, and fully coupled thermohydro-mechanical processes to improve field-scale applicability. The numerical results provided spatial distributions of tensile and compressive damage, fracture initiation zones, and fracture branching patterns resulting from the thermal shock. The ABAQUS damage fields were then postprocessed to identify regions exceeding a critical tensile damage threshold, which were interpreted as thermochemically induced fractures. These fracture zones were abstracted into equivalent fracture properties including fracture length, spatial distribution, branching density, and relative transmissibility. The mechanical damage intensity was used as a proxy to assign a higher permeability and porosity to fracture regions relative to the intact matrix. The reliability of the thermomechanical model is supported by its consistency with experimental observations obtained during laboratory thermal shock testing. The experimentally observed rapid cooling behavior, development of surface cracks, and evidence of tensile damage were reproduced qualitatively by the numerical model. While quantitative fracture geometry validation was not possible because of the lack of three-dimensional fracture characterization data, the agreement between observed and simulated damage mechanisms provides confidence in the applicability of the model for evaluating thermal shock-induced fracture generation and its impact on reservoir performance.

These fracture geometries were subsequently embedded into the CMG-GEM compositional reservoir model using a dual-porosity, dual-permeability representation. Fracture cells were assigned enhanced permeability and reduced capillary resistance to represent their increased flow capacity, while the surrounding matrix retained its original ultralow-permeability properties. This approach preserved the strong contrast between matrix and fracture domains and enabled realistic simulation of matrix-to-fracture mass transfer. To assess the robustness of the proposed stimulation concept, five distinct stimulation scenarios with progressively increasing fracture complexity were evaluated under identical reservoir and operational conditions. The consistent improvement observed in production performance across these scenarios indicates that the reported trends are primarily governed by fracture connectivity and stimulated reservoir volume rather than by a single model realization. Future studies may extend the present framework through formal sensitivity and uncertainty analyses involving reservoir, thermal, and geomechanical parameters. The coupled workflow allowed the direct assessment of how thermally induced fracture complexity, as predicted by the thermomechanical simulations, influences reservoir-scale pressure depletion, gas saturation evolution, and production performance. It should be noted that the coupling between thermomechanical damage and reservoir-scale flow in this study is implemented using a sequential one-way approach. In this framework, fracture initiation and propagation are first simulated at the rock scale using the CDP model in ABAQUS, and the resulting fracture geometries are then incorporated into the CMG-GEM reservoir model without feedback from fluid flow. The results obtained from both thermomechanical and reservoir simulations consistently indicate that fracture complexity is the dominant parameter controlling production performance in ultratight formations. While conventional hydraulic fractures primarily create planar conductive pathways, thermochemical stimulation generates additional fracture branching and connectivity through thermal shock-induced tensile failure. This increased complexity enlarges the stimulated reservoir volume and improves matrix–fracture interaction, resulting in enhanced pressure depletion and gas recovery. The production trends observed in the present study are therefore consistent with fundamental transport mechanisms governing ultratight reservoirs, where reservoir performance is strongly controlled by accessible fracture surface area rather than matrix permeability alone.

Compared to previously reported thermal stimulation techniques, the proposed endothermic thermal shock approach offers a distinct mechanism for fracture generation. Whereas steam-based and exothermic thermochemical methods primarily rely on thermal expansion, pressure generation, or chemical reaction energy, the present method induces tensile failure through rapid cooling and thermal contraction. The resulting fracture complexity observed in the numerical simulations is consistent with the trends reported in cryogenic and thermal shock stimulation studies, supporting the physical plausibility of the proposed mechanism. The production improvements predicted in this study further indicate that cooling-driven fracture generation can provide meaningful enhancement of stimulated reservoir volume and reservoir connectivity in ultratight formations. The present study demonstrated an approximately 1.6-fold increase in the recovery factor compared with the multistage hydraulic fracturing case under identical operating conditions. This improvement is consistent with the general trend reported in previous thermal stimulation studies, which have shown enhanced hydrocarbon recovery through thermally induced fracture development and an improved flow conductivity. However, unlike most previous approaches that rely on heat generation or cryogenic cooling, the proposed method utilizes endothermic chemical reactions to generate a localized thermal shock through rapid heat extraction. This provides an alternative stimulation mechanism that can potentially be implemented by using conventional chemical delivery systems while avoiding some operational challenges associated with steam-based or cryogenic treatments. Furthermore, the present study differs from many previous investigations by integrating laboratory observations, thermomechanical fracture simulation, and field-scale reservoir forecasting within a unified framework. This allows direct evaluation of how thermally induced fracture complexity influences long-term reservoir performance and recovery efficiency. Table demonstrates that previous thermal stimulation studies have primarily focused on either laboratory-scale characterization of thermal damage, cryogenic stimulation, or exothermic thermochemical reactions. While these studies confirmed the ability of thermal stresses to alter rock properties and improve permeability, most did not establish a direct link between thermally induced fracture development and long-term reservoir production performance. The present study extends the existing body of knowledge by integrating laboratory observations, thermomechanical fracture modeling, and compositional reservoir simulation within a unified framework. Furthermore, unlike conventional thermochemical stimulation approaches that rely on heat generation and pressure pulses, the proposed method utilizes endothermic cooling to induce tensile failure through thermal contraction. The predicted recovery enhancement of approximately 1.6 times relative to multistage hydraulic fracturing indicates that cooling-driven stimulation has the potential to provide a viable alternative for ultratight high-temperature gas reservoirs.

It is acknowledged that the one-way coupling assumption limits the ability to capture dynamic interactions between flow and geomechanics, such as stress-dependent permeability, fracture closure during depletion, and potential flow-induced fracture propagation. These effects may influence fracture conductivity and long-term production behavior, particularly under significant pressure depletion. However, for the scope of this study, the dominant control on production performance is the fracture geometry and connectivity established during stimulation, which are adequately captured using the current approach. This approach is justified because fracture development occurs over a much shorter time scale compared to reservoir depletion, and the primary objective of this study is to evaluate the impact of the established fracture network on long-term production performance. While this assumption is reasonable for evaluating long-term production performance, future work may extend the framework to fully coupled thermohydro-mechanical simulations and incorporate anisotropic and temperature-dependent material behavior.

Summary and Conclusion

This study demonstrated a multiscale simulation-based evaluation of thermally induced fractures using endothermic chemical injection as a stimulation method for ultratight gas reservoirs. A key contribution of this study is the demonstration of a cooling-driven fracturing mechanism that is fundamentally different from conventional hydraulic fracturing and exothermic thermochemical stimulation methods. Rather than relying on pressure-induced failure or heat generation, the proposed approach utilizes rapid heat extraction through endothermic reactions to create thermal contraction, induce tensile stresses, and promote fracture initiation and propagation. A comprehensive workflow was developed by combining laboratory thermal shock experiments, finite-element simulation, and field-scale compositional flow modeling. Laboratory experiments confirmed that the sudden cooling of preheated rock samples using endothermic chemicals can generate thermal shock, leading to the development of tensile stresses and causing microcrack formation and mechanical weakening. These effects were modeled by using the CDP model in ABAQUS to simulate fracture initiation and propagation under thermal stress. Although temperature-dependent and anisotropic behavior could influence fracture patterns, and incorporating such effects will be a valuable direction for future work, this study assumes homogeneous and isotropic rock behavior, which is justified for the thermal shock impact on the near-wellbore study in the core samples. The resulting fracture morphologies, having branched and complex geometries, were integrated into the CMG-GEM compositional simulation to evaluate production performance across five stimulation scenarios.

Simulation results demonstrated that thermochemical fracturing can significantly enhance the gas recovery compared to conventional single- and multistage hydraulic stimulation, particularly in creating higher-conductivity pathways and increasing stimulated reservoir volume. Cases involving thermochemically induced fractures outperformed all other cases and showed higher cumulative gas production, more uniform pressure drawdown, and the best recovery factor over the 5-year production horizon. Among the thermochemical stimulations, the high-complexity thermochemical scenario yielded the best performance in terms of gas rate, recovery factor, and pressure distribution. It is inferred from this study that endothermic thermal shock stimulation is a viable and effective approach for improving reservoir contact and production in ultratight formations, offering a low-fluid, high-efficiency alternative to conventional hydraulic fracturing.
