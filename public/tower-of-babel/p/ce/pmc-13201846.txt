Assessing wheat irrigation practices among smallholder farmers in Kellem Wallaga Zone, Oromia, Ethiopia
Authors: Habtamu Rufe, Abu Regasa, Adane Edao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExpanding irrigation is widely recognized as a strategic pathway to enhance agricultural productivity and food security in Ethiopia. This study investigates the determinants of wheat irrigation adoption among smallholder farmers in Kellem Wallaga Zone, Western Ethiopia. Using cross-sectional data from 298 randomly selected households, we employ descriptive statistics and a probit regression model to identify factors influencing adoption decisions. The results revealed that wheat irrigation adoption is significantly shaped by gender, resource endowment, and institutional participation. Male-headed households, larger land allocation to wheat production, cooperative membership, participation in irrigation training, and shorter distance to irrigation plots significantly increase the likelihood of adoption of wheat irrigation. Beyond adoption determinants, irrigation users exhibit markedly improved welfare compared to non-adopters. Furthermore, wheat irrigation adoption is associated with improved household food security, increased income, enhanced self-sufficiency, and greater employment opportunities. These findings consider wheat irrigation not only as a productivity enhancing technology but also as a multidimensional rural development intervention that strengthens resilience to climate variability and reduces vulnerability to rainfall shocks. The study provides empirical evidence to inform irrigation policy and investment strategies in Ethiopia and similar agro-ecological contexts. Strengthening farmer cooperatives, expanding irrigation-specific training, improving extension services, and addressing gender disparities are critical for scaling adoption. Promoting wheat irrigation represents a viable pathway toward sustainable agricultural transformation, poverty reduction, and improved food security in smallholder farming systems.Keywords: Wheat irrigation, Food security, Probit model, Smallholder farmers, EthiopiaSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

Irrigation plays a vital role in enhancing agricultural productivity and ensuring food security worldwide. Globally, irrigated agriculture accounts for approximately 20% of cultivated land and contributes to about 40% of total food production, while its productivity is at least twice that of rain-fed agriculture1. In Ethiopia, widespread poverty and recurrent food shortages remain pressing challenges2. Reliance on rain-fed agriculture alone has proven insufficient to achieve food security, highlighting the critical role of irrigation in improving agricultural production and productivity at both household and national levels3.

Recognizing this, the Ethiopian government has made substantial investments in small-scale irrigation development, with the goal of enhancing irrigation infrastructure to mitigate household food insecurity4. Agriculture contributes approximately 70% of Ethiopia’s foreign exchange earnings, emphasizing its importance not only for feeding the population but also for driving the national economy5. Among cereal crops, wheat is a strategic crop prioritized for irrigation-based production due to its potential to improve livelihoods, accelerate agricultural transformation, and reduce rural poverty6.

Wheat is one of the most widely cultivated and traded cereal crops globally, covering 15% of the total cereal-cultivated area and ranking second after rice in global cereal importance7. In Sub-Saharan Africa, Ethiopia is the second-largest wheat producer after South Africa, both in terms of area coverage and production8. According to9, grain crops in Ethiopia occupy approximately 12.7 million hectares, producing over 315 million quintals annually from private peasant holdings. Cereals account for 87.97% of total grain production and 81.39% of the total cultivated area, with wheat contributing 13.73% of the cultivated cereal area, 15.33% of cereal production, and involving nearly 4.76 million farmers, with an average yield of 27.64 quintals per hectare. Wheat thus serves as a major source of income, employment, and household food supply, although a significant proportion is retained for home consumption10 (Fig 1).

Despite the favourable climatic conditions for wheat cultivation in Kellem Wallaga Zone, production is predominantly rain-fed and limited to the main rainy season. Nationwide, irrigated agriculture remains underdeveloped, with only a small proportion of the country’s irrigation potential utilized, mainly through small-scale and traditional schemes11. Expanding wheat irrigation could generate multiple benefits for rural households, including crop diversification and intensification, employment creation, improved access to credit services, enhanced livestock production, and increased household income. However, there is limited empirical research on the determinants of wheat irrigation adoption and its impact on rural livelihoods in the study area. Previous studies in Ethiopia12 have examined the general impacts of irrigation on crop and livestock production, income, and food security but did not specifically address the factors influencing wheat irrigation adoption.

According to Fida13, investigation, about 97% of Ethiopia’s food crops are produced under rain-fed conditions, while only 3% come from irrigated agriculture. This heavy reliance on rain-fed production contributes to persistent food insecurity and vulnerability to climate variability. Low participation in irrigation exacerbates the risk of agricultural production failure, forcing many rural households to rely on external food aid. Furthermore, rural farmers in Ethiopia often overlook the economic and food security benefits of dry-season irrigation, which can enable double cropping and significantly enhance household welfare14.

In Kellem Wallaga Zone, adoption to wheat irrigation is constrained by factors such as inefficient utilization of available manpower, high dependence on rain-fed agriculture, poor time and resource management in surface irrigation, and limited institutional support15. Despite the relevance of irrigation for wheat production, there is lack of comprehensive quantitative evidence on the extent of wheat irrigation adoption and key factors influencing farmers to wheat irrigation adoption practice. This knowledge gap limits the availability of policymakers and development practitioners to design targeted interventions. Accordingly, this study primarily aims to identify and analyze wheat irrigation adoption among farm households, providing empirical insights to inform future irrigation development strategies.

Methodology

Description of the study area

The study was conducted in Hawa Gelan and Seyo districts of Kellem Wollega Zone, Oromia Region, Ethiopia, located approximately 642 km west of the capital city, Addis Ababa15. Dambi Dollo serves as the administrative town of the zone. According to CSA9, Kellem Wollega Zone has a projected population of 1,040,585, with 95,785 and 148,341 inhabitants in Hawa Gelan and Seyo districts, respectively. The study area falls within the moist to sub-humid mid-highlands (1500–2200 m above sea level), characterized predominantly by Nitisols and Acrisols with moderate to high agricultural potential. The farming system is predominantly rainfed, mixed crop-livestock, with maize (Zea mays), sorghum (Sorghum bicolor), and finger millet (Eleusine coracana) as major staple cereals, often intercropped with pulses and perennial crops such as coffee (Coffea arabica). However, in recent years, smallholder farmers in the districts have increasingly supplemented rainfall with irrigation practices, particularly for wheat (Triticum aestivum) production, using water from perennial rivers and shallow wells. Irrigation is primarily practiced during the dry season (bona) using furrow and hand-dug well methods, enabling two production seasons per year and enhancing household food security and income. The favourable agro-ecological conditions, combined with access to surface water and groundwater, offer significant potential for expanding small-scale irrigation, with wheat emerging as a key cash and food security crop in the area.

Study design

A cross-sectional research design was employed, by integrating both quantitative and qualitative data collection methods. The study involved two groups of smallholder farmers: adopters and non-adopters of traditional and small-scale wheat surface irrigation.

Sampling procedure and techniques

A three-stage sampling technique was used. In the first stage, the 11 districts of Kellem Wollega Zone were assessed for irrigation potential, and two districts Hawa Gelan and Seyo were purposively selected based on potential for surface irrigation available. In the second stage, kebeles within these districts were selected based on irrigation potential. Finally, in the third stage, 298 households were randomly selected from the identified kebeles. Data collection techniques

Data sources included in this study was primary and secondary data. Primary data were collected using structured questionnaires, key informant interviews, and focus group discussions. Secondary data were obtained from the Bureau of Agriculture and Natural Resources, as well as published reports and journal articles related to crop and livestock production, credit access, and surface irrigation practices in the study area.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using both descriptive and econometric methods of analysis. Descriptive statistics, including means, frequencies, and percentages, were applied to summarize household characteristics and compare wheat irrigation adopters and non-adopters. Qualitative data from interviews and discussions were analysed thematically.

Econometric analysis

To identify the determinants influencing smallholder farmers’ adoption of wheat irrigation practices, this study employed a binary response econometric model. Dependent variable represents whether a household adopts wheat irrigation or not, it assumes a dichotomous outcome taking the value of ‘1’ if the household adopts irrigation and ‘0’ otherwise. In such cases, linear probability models are inappropriate due to heteroscedasticity and the possibility of predicted probabilities falling outside the [0, 1] interval. Therefore, a probit regression model was applied, which assumes that the error terms follow a standard normal distribution and provides consistent and efficient parameter estimates for binary outcomes17.

Goodness of fit and model validation

The goodness of fit of the probit model was evaluated using the likelihood ratio (LR) test, which examines the joint significance of explanatory variables. Additionally, the pseudo (R2) was reported to assess the model’s explanatory power. The classification accuracy of the model was evaluated using prediction success tables19.

Endogenity consideration

Certain institutional and socio-economic variables such as extension contact, training participation, credit access, and cooperative membership may potentially suffer from endogeneity due to reverse causality or omitted variable bias. To minimize this concern, careful variable selection and specification were employed. Furthermore, robustness checks were conducted to assess the stability of parameter estimates.

The adoption of wheat irrigation among smallholder farmers is significantly associated with key demographic and institutional characteristics (Table 2). Sex of the household head shows a strong statistical association at the 1% level. Male-headed households constitute 92.06% of adopters compared to 61.90% of non-adopters, whereas female-headed households are disproportionately represented among non-adopters (38.10%) relative to adopters (7.94%). This pattern suggests gender-based disparities in access to productive resources, labor, information, and financial capital, which influence adoption decisions27.

Institutional participation also plays a critical role. A significantly higher proportion of adopters (67.29%) are village-level model farmers compared to non-adopters (32.71%), indicating that preferential access to extension services, training, and improved technologies enhances adoption capacity. Similarly, cooperative membership is strongly associated with irrigation uptake, with 81.31% of adopters belonging to agricultural cooperatives. Cooperatives facilitate access to credit, input supply, market information, and collective learning, thereby reducing transaction costs and strengthening farmers’ ability to invest in irrigation technologies (Table 2).

Consequently, result in Table 2 show that, experience-sharing arrangements further reinforce adoption behavior, as 82.71% of adopters participated in such platforms compared to non-adopters. These mechanisms promote peer learning, knowledge diffusion, and confidence in irrigation-based wheat production. Participation in irrigation-related training also exhibits a statistically significant relationship with adoption. Approximately 78.04% of adopters received training, highlighting the importance of technical knowledge and managerial skills in facilitating technology uptake.

Access to credit services is another important determinant. A greater proportion of adopters (61.21%) had access to credit compared to non-adopters (33.33%). Financial access enables households to overcome liquidity constraints associated with irrigation investment, including the purchase of equipment and improved inputs. Overall, the descriptive results underscore that wheat irrigation adoption is not solely a function of farm-level characteristics but is strongly shaped by institutional support systems, social capital, human capital development, and financial inclusion (Table 2).

The results indicate that the educational level of the household head differs significantly between adopters and non-adopters at the 5% significance level. Adopters have a higher mean number of years of schooling (5.92 years) compared to non-adopters (4.57 years). This finding suggests that education enhances farmers’ ability to understand irrigation technologies and to manage water resources efficiently. Educated farmers are more likely to assess the benefits of irrigation accurately and to adopt improved production practices.

Similarly, the frequency of extension contact is significantly higher among adopters than non-adopters at the 1% significance level. Adopters reported an average of 2.45 extension contacts, whereas non-adopters reported only 1.56 contacts. This result underscores the critical role of extension services in disseminating information, providing technical support, and promoting irrigation technologies among smallholder farmers. Regular interaction with extension agents enhances farmers’ awareness, technical knowledge, and confidence in adopting new agricultural practices.

The tropical livestock unit (TLU) owned by farmers also shows a statistically significant difference between the two groups at the 5% significance level. Adopters possess larger livestock holdings than non-adopters. Livestock ownership often serves as a proxy for household wealth and asset endowment. Farmers with greater livestock assets are better positioned to finance irrigation investments, purchase necessary inputs, and absorb production risks associated with irrigated farming (Table 3).

Finally, the results in Table 3 reveal that the area allocated to wheat production differs significantly between adopters and non-adopters at the 1% significance level. On average, adopters allocate 0.23 ha more land to wheat production than non-adopters, who allocate only 0.03 ha. This finding suggests that farmers who prioritize wheat cultivation are more likely to invest in irrigation to stabilize production and enhance yield performance. The strong association indicates that crop-specific production orientation plays a substantial role in irrigation technology adoption decisions.

Association between irrigation adoption and food security

The results show that 99.24% of irrigation users reported being food secure compared to only 58.33% of non-users. Conversely, 41.67% of non-users were not food secure, compared to only 0.76% of users. The chi-square value (χ2 = 96.04) indicates a highly significant association between irrigation adoption and food security status. This suggests that irrigation adoption substantially enhances household food availability and stability. Access to irrigation likely reduces reliance on erratic rainfall, increases wheat productivity, and enables multiple cropping seasons, thereby ensuring consistent food supply throughout the year (Table 4).

Association between irrigation adoption and household Income

Similarly, 99.24% of irrigation users reported increased income compared to only 58.33% of non-users, with (χ2 = 92.03), indicating a strong association. This demonstrates that wheat irrigation adoption significantly contributes to income growth. Higher and more reliable yields, off-season production, and improved market participation likely translate into greater cash earnings. Irrigation may also enable diversification and commercialization, enhancing economic returns (Table 4).

Association between irrigation adoption and employment creation

In terms of employment generation, 99% of irrigation users reported creating employment opportunities compared to 58.33% of non-users, with (χ2 = 92.03). This statistically significant association suggests that irrigation intensifies labor demand for land preparation, watering, weeding, harvesting, and post-harvest handling. As a result, irrigation adoption contributes not only to household welfare but also to local labor market development, both through family labor utilization and hired labor (Table 4).

Sex of household head (SEXHH) The result of probit model shows, male-headed households have higher marginal probability of adoption. This disparity reflected in both the regression (coef. = 1.31) and earlier descriptive statistics suggests that being a male-headed household increases the probability of adopting wheat irrigation by approximately 40% compared with female-headed (Table 5). The result suggests gender-based disparities in access to productive resources such as land, irrigation infrastructure, capital, and extension services. Female-headed households may face cultural and institutional constraints in adopting intensive agricultural technologies. The result of the study was in line with the finding of21 that female-headed households are less participating in agricultural technology when compared to males.

Availability farm land for irrigation (AREHH) Availability of farm land for irrigation exhibits (coef. = 1.38) and positive effect on the probability of adoption to wheat irrigation at a 1% probability level. The marginal effect shows that as the land available for irrigation of wheat increases by one hectare, the likelihood of the adoption of irrigation increases by 30% (Table 5). This indicates that farmers with larger plots are substantially more likely to adopt irrigation technology. Larger plots may also provide greater flexibility in allocating land to irrigated production without jeopardizing subsistence needs. This finding is consistent with Gurmu et al.23 that justified in his study land allocated positively and significantly affect smallholder farmer adoption to newly introduced agricultural technology.

Distance of irrigation land from home (DISHH) The distance of the cultivated land from the homestead revealed a negative and significant association at 5% probability level. It indicates that a one-hour increase in round-trip walking distance reduces the probability of participation in wheat irrigation by about 5%, holding other factors constant (Table 5). Longer distances increase labour demands and time costs, discouraging smallholders from adopting irrigation. This is particularly critical in areas with minimal mechanization, where irrigation requires frequent monitoring and manual labour. This finding is consistent with28, who reported that commercialization and market participation by farmer was influenced by distance of farmers from certain activity plots.

Membership in farmer’s cooperatives (MEMHH) Member in farmer cooperative showed that it had positive and significant influences to the likelihood of adoption of wheat irrigation at a 5% probability level. Being a cooperative membership increases the probability of adoption to wheat irrigation by 16.0% probability level (Table 5). This emphasizes the role of social capital and institutional networks in facilitating access to information, input supply, credit services, and collective irrigation infrastructure. Cooperatives likely reduce transaction costs and enhance trust in new technologies. However, some studies26 noted that the effectiveness of cooperatives varies depending on their managerial capacity and service delivery.

Training in the use of irrigation (TRAHH) The result of probit model analysis showed that Participation in irrigation-related training positively and significantly affected adoption of wheat irrigation at the 5% probability level (Table 5). The marginal effect indicates that trained farmers are 21.0% more likely to adopt wheat irrigation than untrained counterparts. This finding highlights the importance of human capital development and extension systems in promoting technological diffusion. Training likely enhances technical knowledge, managerial capacity, and confidence in irrigation practices. This finding is consistent with Shiferaw et al. (2022) that capacity-building initiative improve the adoption of small-scale irrigation practices.

Conclusion

This study examined the determinants of wheat irrigation adoption among smallholder farmers in Kellem Wallaga Zone, Western Ethiopia, Using cross-sectional data from 298 households and a probit econometric model, the findings provide robust empirical evidence that wheat irrigation adoption is driven by a combination of demographic, resource, and institutional factors. Specifically, male-headed households, greater land allocation to wheat production, cooperative membership, participation in irrigation training, and proximity of irrigation plots to homesteads significantly increased the likelihood of adoption to wheat irrigation. These results underscore the central role of asset endowment, gender dynamics, institutional participation, and human capital development in shaping technology uptake decisions. Beyond adoption determinants, the study demonstrates that wheat irrigation is strongly associated in improving levels of food security, income, self-sufficiency, and employment generation compared to non-users. These findings highlight irrigation not merely as a yield-enhancing, but as a transformative rural development intervention that enhances resilience to climate variability, reduces vulnerability to rainfall shocks, and strengthens household economic stability. Promoting wheat irrigation adoption represents a viable pathway toward food security, poverty reduction, and sustainable agricultural transformation in Ethiopia. Strategic investment in institutional support systems and farmer capacity building will be essential to unlock the full potential of irrigation based wheat production in smallholder farming systems.

The results of the study policy implications is, expanding wheat irrigation adoption requires integrated interventions that address structural and institutional constraints. Strengthening farmer cooperatives, scaling up irrigation-specific training programs, improving access to extension services, and reducing physical access barriers to irrigation sites are critical entry points. Moreover, targeted strategies to enhance the participation of female-headed households are essential to ensure inclusive agricultural transformation.
