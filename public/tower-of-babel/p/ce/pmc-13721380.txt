Enhanced exosome biomanufacturing, precision isolation, and advanced engineering: toward mechanistic regulation and biomaterial integration for osteoarthritis therapy
Authors: Meipei Gao, Qiaotian Yin, Zejian Zhong, Zhuoxuan Song, Xinyue Song, Rui Zhao, Hong Ling, Fang He, Meng Zhang, Qiming Pang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExosomes (Exos) are nanoscale extracellular vesicles that have gained attention as promising therapeutic agents due to their unique biological attributes, immunomodulatory roles, and potential for targeted drug delivery. This review systematically explores their biological origins, secretion mechanisms, and cellular uptake dynamics of Exos. We explore strategies to enhance Exos production, including genetic modifications and optimized culture conditions, and compare purification methods such as ultracentrifugation and size-exclusion techniques, noting their advantages and disadvantages. Additionally, we discuss the engineering of Exos for drug delivery applications through methods like electroporation and physicochemical modifications. Focusing on osteoarthritis (OA)—a widespread degenerative joint disorder lacking effective clinical treatments for halting its progression or promoting cartilage regeneration—we elaborate on the therapeutic mechanisms of Exos in this context, including regulation of inflammatory responses, enhancement of chondrocyte survival, promotion of matrix synthesis, and inhibition of pathological angiogenesis via bioactive molecules like microRNAs and proteins. To address the challenges of rapid clearance and insufficient targeting in vivo, we propose innovative delivery systems using polymer-based and natural biomaterials to improve Exos retention and stability within the joint cavity. This approach aims to optimize therapeutic outcomes and bridge fundamental biological research with clinical applications, advancing next-generation OA treatment strategies.Graphical abstract Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Exosomes, Purification methods, Stem cells, Biomaterials

Introduction

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are nano-sized membrane-bound particles released by virtually all cell types, which have gained significant attention as promising therapeutic agents due to their excellent biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and efficient delivery of bioactive molecules [1, 2]. EVs include various subtypes such as microparticles, apoptotic bodies, and exosomes (Exos), with Exos attracting particular attention for their unique biological properties and therapeutic potential. For instance, studies have shown that Exos derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSC-Exos) can transport functional molecules such as miRNAs and proteins to specific target tissues, thereby modulating inflammatory responses, promoting chondrocyte repair, improving metabolic balance, and inhibiting pathological angiogenesis [3–10]. Furthermore, due to their inherent cell-derived characteristics, Exos can efficiently traverse biological barriers, such as the blood-brain barrier (BBB), to deliver nucleic acids, proteins, small molecules, and other bioactive components [11, 12]. These properties render Exos promising candidates as natural drug delivery vehicles.

Despite their remarkable therapeutic potential, the clinical translation of Exos faces significant hurdles related to production and isolation. The low secretion levels and yields of Exos limit their widespread application, even though they can be extracted from various bodily fluids and diverse cell sources [13, 14]. To enhance the production of Exos, one approach involves selecting naturally high-secretory cell types or utilizing genetic engineering techniques to boost the secretion capabilities of individual cells [15, 16]. Systematic optimization of the extracellular environment in cell cultures can further enhance Exo production, but achieving large-scale production that meets clinical demands often requires the integration of bioreactor technology and three-dimensional (3D) culture systems alongside engineering approaches [17]. Furthermore, the efficient separation and characterization of Exos from complex biological matrices remain key technical challenges. The choice of isolation techniques, such as ultracentrifugation and size-exclusion chromatography, profoundly affects the purity and consistency of the Exos, consequently impacting the efficacy of downstream applications [18, 19]. Therefore, selecting appropriate isolation methods is critical for obtaining high-quality Exos suitable for therapeutic use. Once high-quality Exos are obtained, engineering strategies can enhance their therapeutic efficacy. Loading strategies, either post-isolation (e.g., electroporation) or through parent cell engineering, facilitate the encapsulation of various therapeutic agents, including nucleic acids, proteins, and drugs [20]. Additionally, employing surface engineering techniques—whether through physical modifications or chemical alterations that enhance stability—can significantly improve the circulatory longevity of Exos and increase their specificity for target tissues, thereby optimizing their therapeutic efficacy in drug delivery applications [21].

Among the various pathological conditions where Exos show therapeutic promise, osteoarthritis (OA) represents a particularly compelling target due to its significant unmet clinical needs. OA is a common degenerative joint disease primarily causing chronic pain, progressive functional impairment, and substantial disability, particularly among the aging population [22–24]. Current clinical management remains limited to pharmacological treatments (such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and intra-articular corticosteroid injections) and end-stage joint replacement surgery, neither of which effectively halts disease progression or promotes cartilage regeneration, while carrying risks of adverse effects or revision surgeries [25–29]. Therefore, seeking innovative treatment strategies has become an urgent matter. In this context, Exos have emerged as promising candidates, efficiently transporting functional molecules such as miRNAs and proteins to joint tissues to modulate inflammatory responses, promote chondrocyte repair, restore metabolic balance, and inhibit pathological angiogenesis [4–10]. However, intra-articular application faces distinct challenges compared to systemic administration, including rapid clearance and insufficient targeting within the joint cavity. To overcome these, combining Exos with biomaterials can create delivery systems with sustained-release functions and microenvironment-responsive capabilities, significantly improving their retention and stability within diseased joints [11, 30].

In summary, this review provides a systematic overview that progresses from fundamental Exos biology to their therapeutic application in OA. We will examine: (1) the biogenesis, secretion and cellular uptake of Exos; (2) upstream bioprocessing and downstream isolation strategies for large-scale, high-quality production; (3) the construction of Exos-based drug delivery systems, with emphasis on drug loading, surface functionalization and their relevance to OA therapy; (4) the subsequent application to OA therapy, detailing their multifaceted mechanisms of action against joint pathogenesis; and (5) biomaterial-integrated strategies to overcome intra-articular delivery barriers and enhance therapeutic efficacy. By adopting this integrative perspective—from basic vesicle characteristics to clinical OA interventions—we endeavor to construct a coherent roadmap that elucidates synergies across developmental stages, pinpoints persistent translational hurdles, and anticipates the future trajectory of Exos-based nanomedicines.

Exos

Exos are a type of nanoscale EVs with an average diameter of approximately 100 nm and a density ranging from 1.11 to 1.19 g/mL, formed via the endosomal pathway [31]. Exos can be produced and released by nearly all types of cells [32]. Their structure is typically vesicular, encapsulated by a lipid bilayer that is rich in lipid components such as cholesterol, ceramide, and sphingolipids [33].

Origin of Exos

The production of Exos is not a random process but instead results from highly regulated cellular responses to specific stimuli or pathological conditions [34]. The classical biogenesis process involves four stages: initiation of endocytosis, endosome maturation, multivesicular body (MVB) formation, and release, as illustrated in Fig. 1 [35]. Following plasma membrane invagination, early sorting endosomes (ESEs) undergo maturation into late endosomes (LEs) through inward budding, generating intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) that accumulate to form MVBs [32, 36–38]. The formation of ILVs is driven primarily by two major classes of molecular mechanisms: ESCRT-dependent and ESCRT-independent pathways.

In the ESCRT-dependent pathway, the ESCRT protein family (including ESCRT-0, -I, -II, and -III) assembles sequentially and cooperatively to accurately identify and sort ubiquitinated protein cargoes, driving the inward budding and remodeling of the endosomal membrane [40]. This process culminates in the formation of ILVs within MVBs, orchestrated by the ESCRT-III complex and the VPS4 ATPase, which facilitates the final membrane scission event [40–44]. Alternative ESCRT-dependent pathways may play a significant role in the heterogeneity of exosomes. Specifically, the syndecan–syntenin–ALIX axis directly recruits ESCRT-III, circumventing upstream complexes. Additionally, components of ESCRT-III can facilitate the generation of Exos from non-canonical compartments, such as recycling endosomes, particularly under stress. This process results in a diverse array of vesicle populations, each characterized by distinct cargo profiles [45, 46].

In the ESCRT-independent mechanism, complex lipids such as ceramide play a crucial role. Ceramide, generated by sphingomyelinase, can self-assemble into lipid raft structures, driving membrane invagination and the formation of intraluminal vesicles (ILVs). However, the lipid-dependent regulatory pathway exhibits cell-type specificity; for instance, in certain melanoma cells, the generation of Exos is not reliant on the ceramide pathway [47].

Secretion of Exos

Regardless of whether they are generated via ESCRT-dependent or independent mechanisms, newly formed MVBs mature via a Rab5-to-Rab7 switch that sorts ubiquitinated cargo into ILVs and determines lysosomal degradation or plasma membrane fusion for Exos release [47]. Rab7 recruits adaptor proteins (e.g. RILP and PLEKHM1) that link to dynein and kinesin motors, transporting MVBs to the cell periphery, where Rab GTPases (e.g. Rab27, Rab35, Rab11) facilitate MVB docking through tethering factors like the HOPS complex. Subsequent SNARE activation allows v-SNAREs on MVBs to fuse with t-SNAREs on the plasma membrane, releasing ILVs as Exos [32, 36, 42, 48–51]. However, there are also studies indicating that Exos may directly bud from the plasma membrane [52]. Such diverse mechanisms of biogenesis and secretion inherently yield Exos populations characterized by varied membrane compositions and functional attributes, enriching their biological complexity.

Uptake of Exos

Once Exos are released into the extracellular environment, they can interact with recipient cells through various mechanisms, thereby regulating the physiological state of the recipient cells. The main pathways of action include: (1) delivering their contents into recipient cells through endocytosis or direct fusion with the cell membrane; and (2) initiating downstream signaling without internalization by specifically binding lipid or protein ligands on the vesicle membrane to receptors on the target cell membrane [53, 54].

Bioprocessing and isolation of Exos

Having understood the biological foundations of exosomes and their cellular uptake mechanisms, realizing their therapeutic potential requires overcoming the critical bottleneck of large-scale, high-quality production. This necessitates coordinated advances in upstream bioprocessing and downstream isolation.

Conventional cell culture systems produce insufficient yields for clinical manufacturing, necessitating enhanced secretory strategies—including cell line modification, environmental parameter optimization, physical stimulation and three-dimensional culture—to boost production whilst preserving therapeutic quality as illustrated in Fig. 2 [55–62]. Cell line modification

The yield of Exos demonstrates significant cell type dependence, with differences originating from the intrinsic sorting mechanisms and the regulation of secretion pathways in the cells [68, 69]. Current strategies for enhancing Exos production through cell line modification mainly involve screening for naturally high-secretion cell lines and enhancing the secretion capacity of cells through genetic engineering approaches.

Screening for naturally high-secreting cells

MSCs have emerged as core sources for the production of therapeutic Exos due to their advantages of low immunogenicity, tissue homing ability, and paracrine activity [70]. Yet Exos quantity and quality are fundamentally constrained by the proliferative behavior of their parental cells, which differs markedly across tissue origins [57, 71]. Studies have indicated that umbilical cord-derived MSCs exhibit a significantly shorter doubling time (approximately 4 days) compared to bone marrow or adipose tissue-derived MSCs (approximately 7 days), resulting in Exos yields that can be up to four times greater than those of the latter [63]. Notably, although certain cell types like Wharton’s Jelly MSCs (WJ-MSCs) have limited absolute Exos yields, they demonstrate exceptionally high single-cell secretion efficiency, with Exos output that can reach 5–25 times that of suspension cell lines such as HEK293 and CHO-K1, exhibiting superior therapeutic potential in functional assays such as wound healing experiments [64, 71]. Rather than pursuing the dramatic enhancements offered by genetic engineering, selecting naturally high-secreting populations such as UC-MSCs or WJ-MSCs presents a strategic compromise. This approach balances initial yield with biological fidelity, circumventing the safety complexities and regulatory burdens associated with extensive genetic modification while establishing a more robust foundation for clinical translation.

Genetic engineering

Exos biogenesis and release constitute a tightly orchestrated, multi-step process governed by discrete molecular checkpoints—including RAB GTPases, ESCRT complexes, SNARE machinery, and cytoskeletal networks—rendering specific nodes amenable to genetic optimization [72]. Targeting these regulatory hubs has yielded substantial production gains. Zhang et al. demonstrated that by knocking down the Rab4 gene, the transition of early endosomes to recycling endosomes could be inhibited, causing vesicles to remain at the MVB stage, subsequently increasing MVB numbers and ultimately enhancing Exos yield without affecting Exos cargo loading efficiency [73]. Another strategy involves transfecting specific nucleic acids into donor cells to achieve customized production. A plasmid system encoding STEAP3, syndecan-4, and L-aspartate oxidase fragments has achieved approximately a 40-fold increase in Exos release [74]. Viral vectors offer a complementary strategy to modulate Exos biogenesis by exploiting endosomal trafficking pathways. For example, lentiviral delivery of CD9-GFP into HEK293 cells—a platform favored for its high transfection efficiency and rapid proliferation—substantially increases yields relative to wild-type controls [71, 75].

However, gene manipulation may lead to unforeseen mutations within the cells, potentially altering the properties of the produced Exos [76]. Additionally, results from genetic effect studies in specific cell lines may not be applicable to other cell types [77].

Optimize the environmental parameters for culture

Beyond direct cell line modifications, optimizing the cellular microenvironment serves as a potent non-genetic strategy for enhancing Exos production. By chemically treating the cellular microenvironment—such as through nutrient deprivation, oxidative stress induction, pH adjustment, hypoxia, heat stress, and specific chemical reagent treatments—or by finely tuning physical conditions (such as cell seeding density and culture time), one can effectively alter cell membrane fluidity, modulate the expression of specific proteins, promote vesicle formation, and enhance the exocytosis process, thereby increasing Exos secretion [78, 79].

Adjust the composition of the culture medium and soluble inducers

The serum status of the culture medium fundamentally dictates exosome yield and purity. Serum-containing formulations introduce abundant bovine extracellular vesicles that co-purify with the target product, confounding functional assays and overestimating particle counts, even after ultracentrifugation (UC) of “Exos-depleted serum” (EDS) [80–82]. Eliminating serum not only reduces contamination but also activates stress-induced secretion pathways. Relevant studies have shown that under serum-free conditions, sphingolipid and ceramide biosynthesis genes are upregulated, and pharmacological inhibition of ceramide synthase (GW4869) or genetic silencing of SMPD3 attenuates Exos release by approximately 50%, establishing ceramide-dependent intraluminal vesicle generation as a key driver of nutrient-withdrawal-induced secretion [57, 83]. However, it is noteworthy that the effects of serum deprivation exhibit cell type specificity: the yield of Exos derived from UCMSC is unaffected, whereas yields from ADSC-Exos and BMSC-Exos are significantly reduced [84]. Moreover, when cell confluence reaches 70–80%, switching to serum-free media may induce unexpected cellular stress, autophagic flux, changes in cellular phenotype, and potential alterations in the packaging and release mechanisms of Exos [85].

Chemically defined media (CDM) may represent a more advanced solution. By providing essential nutrients like insulin and selenium, CDM maintains the continuity of the secretion phase cell cycle, thereby achieving sustained high Exos production [86]. For instance, Yong et al. reported that culturing hUCMSC in CDM can increase Exos yield by 8.5 times compared to serum starvation methods and enhanced their therapeutic efficacy in diabetic wound models [87]. Additionally, Yong et al. innovatively combined CDM with a three-dimensional (3D) culture system and cytokine preconditioning (TNF-α/IFN-γ (TI)), taking advantage of the of the defined environment and the absence of exogenous interferents to reduce toxicity and further boost yield [88]. In summary, the selection and optimization of culture media are key factors in balancing Exos yield, purity, and functionality.

Beyond basal formulations, soluble chemical inducers can selectively activate exosome biogenesis and cargo synthesis pathways. Metabolic stress inducers such as sodium iodoacetate (IAA) and 2,4-dinitrophenol (DNP) synergistically inhibit oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis, triggering adenosine-A2B receptor activation that increases Exos yield 3- to 16-fold; halving the dose and co-administering the β2-adrenergic agonist fenoterol mitigates cytotoxicity while maintaining a threefold gain in total yield and fivefold higher cell-specific secretion in HEK293 cells [64, 89]. Calcium ionophores like A23187 exploit the central role of Ca²⁺ in MVB–plasma membrane fusion, robustly triggering release, although precise dosing is required to prevent nonspecific vesiculation and cell damage [90–92]. Quantitative high-throughput screening (qHTS) has accelerated the discovery of small molecules that modulate Exos biogenesis: methyldopa combined with dextromethorphan triples MSC-exosome production via ceramidase-associated pathways without compromising angiogenic or immunomodulatory functions [16, 89]. Similarly, adiponectin activates endosomal sorting via t-cadherin, and TNF-α, upregulates ATG16L1 through PI3K/AKT signaling both enhancing secretion [62, 93, 94]. Notably, the effects of such chemical stimulation extend beyond merely increasing particle quantity; they can significantly optimize the “functional yield” of Exos. For instance, interferon-γ enriches Exos with immunomodulatory proteins through proteomic reprogramming without altering physical particle counts [95].

Nevertheless, induction efficiency varies across cell types, necessitating line-specific optimization rather than universal protocols. Challenges regarding metabolic dysregulation, intracellular drug accumulation, and safety at scale remain to be fully addressed [81, 96].

Modulation of physical parameters

Beyond chemical induction, the precise control of physical parameters offers a non-invasive and scalable means to enhance Exos production.

Hypoxia represents a potent physiological stimulus that activates HIF, PI3K/Akt, Wnt/β-catenin, and MAPK signaling, accelerating Exos secretion across diverse cell types while preserving their inherent stability and targeting capabilities [97, 98]. Notably, the increased secretion of Exos induced by hypoxia is not a phenomenon exclusive to tumor cells [99]. Ho et al. found that 3D spherical culture of hADSCs under hypoxic conditions upregulates HIF-1α and Bcl-xL while suppressing pro-apoptotic BAX, promoting sustained Exos release [100]. However, there may exist heterogeneity among Exos induced by hypoxic conditions, necessitating an evaluation of the quality and activity of these Exos.

Thermal stress represents another critical factor influencing Exos production. Research has shown that compared with normal culture conditions at 37 °C, heat stress (HS) treatment at 42 °C leads to a 13.2-fold increase in Exos yield from neural stem cells and a 16.1% increase in particle size without compromising vesicle integrity, mediated primarily by Rab7B and lipoprotein receptor upregulation [101, 102]. Although the protein diversity of HS-derived Exos is significantly lower than that of Exos derived from normal culture conditions (NHS), their components are enriched in anti-apoptotic and DNA repair-related proteins, exhibiting enhanced neuroprotective capabilities against oxidative stress and Aβ-induced neurotoxicity [101]. This suggest that thermal stress not only boosts quantity but can also functionally prime Exos. However, the associated reduction in protein diversity highlights a trade-off between yield and cargo complexity.

The pH of the culture environment is a key factor regulating Exos yield. In vitro studies have indicated that lowering the pH from physiological levels of 7.4 to a tumor microenvironment pH of 6.5 significantly increases the release of Exos from human cancer cells by as much as 65-fold [103]. However, while short-term low pH exposure can enhance Exos secretion, prolonged stimulation may yield complex effects. Culturing at pH 5 for 48 h reduces proliferation and absolute particle counts in CD63-GFP-HeLa cells but drives MVB accumulation at the basolateral membrane, ultimately increasing total exosome protein secretion during the culture period [104]. Additionally, acidic pH enhances exosome stability during isolation, improving recoverable yields fivefold [58]. Thus, timing and duration of acidosis are critical parameters to balance secretion enhancement against cellular fitness.

Cell seeding density and culture duration further govern Exos output through interdependent mechanisms. For adherent cells such as MSCs, moderate densities (~4 × 10³ cells/cm²) maximize per-cell production, likely reflecting optimal surface area availability and paracrine signaling, whereas higher densities (10⁴ cells/cm²) reduce yields by more than half [105]. Furthermore, Micropatterned surfaces that increase cancer cell density without contact inhibition similarly drive total production upward [106]. Extraction during the exponential growth phase generally optimizes yield, whereas late-exponential cultures show diminished output [105]. Moreover, serum-free collection windows of approximately 48 h appear to balance yield, purity, and size stability across multiple cell lines [107]. Collectively, these parameters must be optimized to avoid contact inhibition and nutrient depletion while preserving productive cell–cell signaling.

Although physical parameters such as hypoxia, heat shock and acidosis are widely reported to enhance Exos secretion, causal mechanisms remain unresolved and empirical findings from heterogeneous 2D systems lack cross-laboratory validation. Trade-offs between yield and cargo complexity remain poorly characterized mechanistically, limiting scalable bioprocessing. Rigorous standardization is therefore essential for clinical translation.

Physical stimulation

Beyond the realms of chemical and environmental modulation, direct physical stimulation emerges as a compelling, non-genetic strategy to activate Exos biogenesis pathways through mechanisms such as mechanotransduction and bioelectrical signaling. This approach harnesses carefully regulated mechanical, acoustic, or electrical energies to transiently and reversibly alter cellular states, with the goal of triggering a productive secretory response characterized by high temporal precision.

Mechanical stimulation

Mechanical stimuli mediated by mechanosensing pathways, such as cyclic stretch and shear stress, significantly enhance Exos output through YAP signaling activation [108]. However, the cellular responsiveness to such stimuli is notably heterogeneous, posing challenges at the single-cell level. Liu et al. demonstrated that applying 24-h cyclic stretch to periodontal ligament (PDL) cells resulted in a 33-fold increase in Exos yield, whereas gingival/dental pulp fibroblasts (GF/DPF) showed no response, underscoring the cell-specific limitations of mechanosensitivity [109]. However, 3D tissue engineering platforms can overcome this constraint; Guo et al. found that dental pulp stem cell (DPSC) scaffolds under shear stress of 0.5–1 mL/min produced 24–40 times more Exos, while MSC scaffolds achieved a concurrent 40.7-fold increase. Engineered muscle tissues subjected to cyclic stretch exhibited an 11-fold increase, enabling cell-type universal regulation, with the enhanced Exos maintaining their structural integrity and functional activity [108]. Therefore, mechanically activated three-dimensional bioreactors represent a scalable industrial production model, providing a new paradigm for the large-scale preparation of therapeutic-grade Exos.

Acoustic stimulation

Ultrasonic stimulation activates Exos secretion via the calcium-ALIX pathway: August et al. applied 4 W high-frequency standing wave ultrasonic (SRBW) stimulation to U87-MG cells (10 min of irradiation followed by 30 min of incubation) and achieved a 1.7-fold increase in Exos yield while maintaining cell viability at approximately 95%. Notably, after seven cycles of stimulation, the yield was amplified to 8–10 times without loss of cell activity, creating an efficient cell recovery strategy that offers a new avenue for the homogeneous production of therapeutic-grade Exos [110]. This cyclic, low-impact stimulation protocol establishes an efficient cell recovery strategy and offers a new avenue for the homogeneous production of therapeutic-grade Exos.

Electrical stimulation

Electrical stimulation (ET) achieves the non-destructive amplification of Exos by targeting cellular signaling pathways. Traditional low-intensity ET (0.34 mA/cm²) activates the Rho-GTPase pathway, synchronously enhancing the yield of Exos from cancer cells (B16F1) and normal cells (3T3), while maintaining their structural integrity and uptake efficiency [111]. Furthermore, Zhang et al. invented a triboelectric nanogenerator (TENG) that outputs optimized electrical signals (1800 V, 88 µA), promoting a 3.2-fold increase in the production of MSC-derived Exos through calcium ion influx, completely circumventing the electrolysis damage associated with conventional electrical stimulation, thus providing a new approach for the large-scale production of Exos [112].

In summary, mechanical, acoustic, and electrical stimulation represent a promising class of non-genetic, energy-based modalities under investigation for enhancing Exos production. Although each engages distinct mechanisms—such as mechanotransduction and calcium signaling—they collectively aim to perturb cellular states in a controlled manner to enhance secretion. The advent of enabling platforms, including 3D bioreactors, automated acoustic cycles, and advanced bioelectronic interfaces like TENGs, underscores the translational potential of these approaches for scalable manufacturing. Future efforts should focus on optimizing stimulation parameters, understanding long-term effects, and exploring potential synergies to achieve robust production of functionally consistent Exos.

Three-dimensional culture techniques

It is evident that changes in the culture matrix and cellular microenvironment significantly regulate Exos characteristics. Compared to 2D cell culture systems, 3D culture systems promote cell communication and cell-matrix interaction by simulating the in vivo microenvironment, effectively maintaining cell pluripotency and enhancing paracrine activity, ultimately achieving a synergistic optimization of Exos yield and stability [113]. Below, we categorize and discuss current common 3D technologies based on scaffold dependence.

Scaffold-free culture

Scaffold-free technologies rely on the self-assembly of cells into spheroids, with the core mechanism based on the recognition of the arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (RGD) motif by integrins, triggering adhesion, and leading to 3D construction mediated by cadherin-mediated cell aggregation [114]. Existing methods can be further divided into static and dynamic culture systems. A classic example of static scaffold-free culture, the hanging drop method standardizes initial spheroid size by controlling droplet volume and cell density, but limited nutrient diffusion and absence of extracellular matrix restrict long-term uniformity to approximately 3 days [115–117]. To overcome these bottlenecks, Zhang et al. developed a microfluidic hanging drop culture system that employs a tapered tube-micro-ring dual structure to enhance droplet stability and integrates a peristaltic pump to achieve dynamic medium perfusion, successfully extending the cultivation period of 500 μm WJ-MSC spheroids to 7 days while maintaining cellular phenotypes (e.g., CD90+/CD73+) and osteogenic differentiation potential, providing a new technological paradigm for the large-scale production of Exos [118].

In dynamic culture systems, stirring culture techniques rely on mechanical agitation to maintain cell suspension and promote cell aggregation into spheroids. While this optimizes nutrient and waste diffusion efficiency, shear stress poses a risk of cell membrane damage [119]. Conversely, vertical wheel bioreactors use computational fluid dynamics (CFD) modeling to customize low-shear biomimetic flow fields, combined with direct single-cell expansion processes, achieving a 32.3-fold efficient expansion and uniform micro-spheroid construction in hiPSC models, scalable to 50 L production systems [120]. Additionally, external physical stimuli such as magnetic forces can be introduced into these bioreactors to extend the effective culture period. The magnetic levitation technique (m3D) utilizes magnetic nanoparticles to label cells, driven by an external magnetic field to nonspecifically aggregate cells into spheroids [121]. This method supports long-term stable cultures (≥ 45 days) while maintaining > 90% cell viability and allows precise control over spheroid size [122]. Electron microscopy further revealed that the cross-sectional area of magnetic levitation spheroids is significantly smaller than that of hanging drop spheroids, with diameters stabilized between 200 and 360 μm, confirming that this mechanism constructs necrosis-free micro-spheroids through the dispersion of multiple spheroids, providing an ideal microenvironment for the maintenance of stem cell functions [123]. However, this method requires specialized equipment such as neodymium magnetic field generators, which increases operational costs [121].

In summary, the choice among these various 3D culture modalities hinges on the type of producer cells, the scale of production, and regulatory considerations regarding the equipment used. A promising direction for future research lies in the intelligent integration of multiple physical stimuli within advanced bioreactor systems, potentially combined with mild chemical cues, to establish fully controlled, scalable, and Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant platforms for the production of therapeutic Exos.

Scaffold-dependent culture

In contrast to scaffold-free technologies that rely on cell self-aggregation, scaffold-dependent culture utilizes exogenous biomaterials to actively construct biomimetic microenvironments. This approach not only precisely mimics the physicochemical properties of the natural ECM but also overcomes diffusion limitations, supporting tissue constructs greater than 1 cm³ while maintaining high yields continuously [124]. In basic static scaffold systems, hydrogels such as 5% concentration of methacrylated gelatin (GelMA) provides optimal mechanical stiffness for BMSCs, effectively delaying cellular senescence and promoting the release of Exos by approximately 2.1-fold, while enhancing the wound healing capability of BMSC-derived Exos [125, 126]. Additionally, microcarriers, spherical scaffolds with diameters of 100–300 μm, combine the biomimetic properties of static scaffolds with the dynamic control of bioreactors by providing a suspended adherent interface [127]. For example, Wang et al. employed biodegradable porous microcarriers in combination with a stirred bioreactor, achieving a 1.44-fold increase in cell population doubling levels in a model of hUC-MSCs while maintaining over 95% cell viability and reducing β-galactosidase activity by 60%, thereby establishing a metabolic basis for high Exos yields [128]. Importantly, the bioreactor system in this setup allows for precise, repeatable, and dynamic control and real-time monitoring of culture conditions such as temperature, pH, nutrient supply, and waste product removal, optimizing the growth environment for cells on scaffolds [129]. Hollow fiber bioreactors support MSCs in the continuous and efficient secretion of Exos over a period of 28 days, achieving a 5.7-fold increase compared to conventional 2D systems, whilst maintaining subpopulation stability and functional homogeneity by limiting excessive proliferation [130, 131]. Furthermore, compared to conventional bioreactors, microfluidic bioreactors exhibit superior environmental control capabilities, enabling fine-tuning of culture conditions, on-demand application of chemical and physical stimuli, and effectively limiting fluid shear stress-induced damage to cell membranes. This significantly enhances the yield and quality of Exos, which are nanometer-scale extracellular vesicles formed via endosomal pathways, averaging 100 nm in diameter, with a density ranging from 1.11 to 1.19 g/mL [31, 132]. For example, in a planar microfluidic bioreactor, precise control over calcium ion transport upregulated intracellular calcium levels to 1.57 times that of static cultures, stimulating Exos biogenesis pathways, including calpain, phosphorylated ERK, and β-catenin in human bone marrow stem cell (hBM-MSC) models [133]. Notably, Rima et al. developed a microfluidic Exo-SµBR cultivation system that enables quantitative and co-expression analysis of biomarkers at a single Exos level by precisely controlling the microenvironment, providing a powerful technical platform for deeper insights into the cellular origins and functional significance of Exos heterogeneity [134].

Although 3D culture, particularly scaffold-dependent techniques, significantly enhances the yield and quality of Exos, the complex culture conditions, potential damage to Exos membrane integrity due to enzymatic digestion, and increased heterogeneity in Exos secretion present additional complexities and challenges for Exos isolation [135]. Importantly, the microfluidic separation technologies (such as dielectrophoresis, size exclusion, and integrated platforms) offer very promising solutions to the challenges faced in 3D culture systems. For instance, a multi-channel microfluidic chip integrated with a hydrogel diffusion barrier enables enzyme-free, in-situ, continuous Exos collection, maximizing the preservation of Exos membrane integrity and biological activity. Hydrogel pore-size selection permits precise control over diffusing particle sizes, making it possible to investigate the functions of different Exos subpopulations and differentiate between Exos-mediated and soluble factor-mediated cell communication behaviors [136].

In summary, scaffold-dependent culture—especially when integrated with advanced bioreactor technologies—presents a powerful, controllable, and scalable strategy for producing high-quality Exos. The future of this field lies in further refining these sophisticated culture platforms alongside intelligent, non-disruptive harvesting technologies to create a fully optimized production pipeline for Exos.

Methods for the isolation of Exos

Downstream, efficient isolation from complex biological matrices is hindered by the nanoscale dimensions, low abundance and overlapping physicochemical characteristics of exosomes with other extracellular vesicles, compounded by intrinsic population heterogeneity that confounds therapeutic standardization [137–139]. To overcome these limitations, separation strategies have undergone multiple generations of technological iteration, as illustrated in Fig. 3. Current mainstream isolation methods, such as UC and size-exclusion chromatography (SEC), primarily rely on physical properties such as size and density for separation. However, while each has its advantages, they often fail to simultaneously address ease of operation, speed, cost, high sensitivity, and specificity, frequently resulting in compromised purity, activity, integrity, and yield of Exos [140].

Ultracentrifugation (UC)

UC is considered the gold standard for Exos isolation, accounting for approximately 80% of Exos processing [144]. UC separates Exos from complex biological samples based on size, density, and buoyancy characteristics, and can be classified into differential ultracentrifugation (DUC) and density gradient ultracentrifugation (DGC) [145]. DGC employs sequential increases in centrifugal force to deplete cells, debris, and larger vesicles before pelleting exosomes at high speed [18, 137]. Although DUC is simple to operate, can handle large volumes of samples, and provides higher concentrations of Exos, the purity of the resultant products is relatively low [146, 147]. Particularly in complex samples like serum, the abundance of soluble proteins and non-vesicular structures can co-sediment with Exos during UC, resulting in significant protein contamination in the final products [148]. Moreover, the extreme centrifugal forces can potentially cause mechanical damage to the Exos, reducing their biological activity [17]. DGC addresses these purity deficits by resolving particles according to buoyant density within continuous gradients—typically sucrose or iodixanol—with the latter offering superior biocompatibility and preservation of exosomal function [145, 149, 150]. DGC is primarily employed to enhance the resolution and purity of conventional ultracentrifugation techniques and is often used as a subsequent purification step following DUC [151]. Despite improving the purity of the final product by separating Exos from non-Exos contaminants through different density layers, DGC has limited effectiveness against overlapping-density contaminants, such as lipoproteins and liposomes in blood samples [150]. Consequently, while DGC markedly improves the purity of Exos relative to DUC, it lacks the specificity to distinguish Exos subpopulations based on surface markers or functional attributes.

Size-based separation of Exos

Size-based separation techniques leverage the principles of physical separation, focusing on parameters such as size to enable the quick and simple isolation of Exos. However, these methods, while effective for bulk enrichment, inherently fall short in addressing the molecular and functional heterogeneity that exists within the Exos population.

Size exclusion chromatography (SEC)

SEC, also known as gel filtration, is a chromatographic method that separates particles based on their size, exploiting the principle that small particles have a higher permeability within the stationary phase than larger ones [137]. During the passage of the sample through the chromatography column, substances larger than the gel particle pore size cannot enter the pores and elute earlier, whereas smaller molecules are retained in the gel pores and require longer elution times [150]. Consequently, SEC can rapidly and effectively remove soluble proteins from a wide range of biological fluids (such as synovial fluid), significantly enhancing the purity of Exos [150]. Given its advantages in removing non-vesicular contaminants, SEC is often combined with DGC to effectively isolate exosomes and lipoproteins from plasma or platelet concentrates, further improving the purity of the separation products [152].

Ultrafiltration (UF)

UF utilizes specialized membranes with specific pore sizes for the selective separation of Exos from other components in the sample, primarily aimed at isolating proteins and other unwanted contaminants from exosomal samples [137, 149]. This technique is advantageous due to the absence of sample volume limitations, making it applicable to large liquid samples such as cell culture media [146]. Research by Gao et al. indicates that the functional characteristics of Exos separated using UF and polymer precipitation techniques are comparable; however, losses may occur due to filter clogging or the adhesion of particles to the membrane, or due to the concurrent retention of similarly sized unwanted proteins, which can lower the concentration and purity of Exos [137, 146, 153]. Furthermore, the pressure and shear forces during filtration may lead to deformation and damage of Exos, thereby affecting their biological activity [150]. Consequently, UF may introduce a selection bias that enrich larger vesicles while neglect smaller subpopulations and co-concentrating undesirable contaminants. Thus, it may not be the optimal choice for investigations requiring a precise correlation between physical properties and biological functions of distinct Exos subpopulations.

Polymer precipitation method

In contrast to separation methods based on physical forces, polymer precipitation enriches Exos through chemical action. This method employs hydrophilic polymers such as polyethylene glycol (PEG)that decrease the solubility of Exos in the sample via competitive solvents, followed by low-speed centrifugal recovery [154, 155]. Compared to UC, polymer precipitation can protect the integrity of Exos, allowing for rapid and cost-effective enrichment and concentration of Exos from large-volume samples, which is scalable for clinical translation [150]. Notably, this technology can be used in conjunction with UC to separate co-precipitated protein contaminants and non-exosomal extracellular nucleic acids, thereby enhancing the purity of Exos [149, 154, 156]. However, a notable limitation of the polymer precipitation method is its low specificity, which often results in the co-precipitation of non-vesicular contaminants, leading to preparations with relatively low purity [154]. The method also indiscriminately precipitates Exos of varying sizes and surface characteristics, with residual polymers potentially interfering with downstream molecular and functional analyses [154, 157]. Consequently, while polymer precipitation serves as a rapid concentration tool, its lack of specificity highlights the necessity for combined methodologies to address these limitations effectively [158].

Separation techniques based on membrane properties of Exos

While size- and density-based methods facilitate bulk separations, techniques that exploit the membrane properties of Exos strive for higher specificity by targeting specific surface biochemistry. Methods such as affinity isolation, charge-based separation, and hydrophobic interaction chromatography offer enhanced purity for specific vesicular subsets; however, they introduce selectivity biases that warrant careful consideration.

Affinity isolation (AF)

AF leverages specific surface markers of Exos—including particular proteins, lipids, and polysaccharides—to achieve high-purity separation of distinct Exos subpopulations [137, 149]. The methods can be categorized based on the type of antibody substrate carrier, including magnetic bead immunoisolation, chromatography stationary phase methods, and other techniques [150]. Magnetic bead capture of Exos is a commonly used approach because magnetic particles can be easily functionalized with antibodies, such as those targeting tetraspanin proteins or tissue-specific markers [137, 149]. For instance, Kaige Yang et al. designed peptide ligand-functionalized silica microspheres (SiO2-pep) to selectively interact with phosphatidylserine (PS) on the surface of Exos, demonstrating that the purity of Exos isolated using this technique exceeds that of UF, while the yield surpasses that of DGC and UC [159]. This modification allows magnetic particles to attach specifically to Exos, facilitating their extraction from complex matrices via magnetism, which helps eliminate interference caused by the biological fluid matrix, thereby improving Exos purity [150]. However, not all Exos exhibit the same canonical protein markers, and maintaining the integrity of Exos during immunoisolation poses significant challenges, complicating the extraction process using traditional immunolabeling techniques [159]. To overcome this limitation, Yu JJ et al. designed a temperature-sensitive copolymer based on CD63 aptamers that captures Exos in a hydrophilic state below the lower critical solution temperature (LCST) and releases them in a hydrophobic state above the LCST, achieving efficient Exos separation [160]. In summary, affinity isolation represents the method of choice for isolating Exos subpopulations characterized by known surface markers with high purity. Nonetheless, the method’s exceptional specificity may compromise representativeness, making it less suitable for obtaining a comprehensive profile of total Exos heterogeneity.

Charge-based separation

Charge-based separation exploits the negative surface charge of exosomal lipid bilayers. Anion exchange chromatography (IEX) employs electrostatic interactions with positively charged resins, modulating salt concentration and pH to achieve selective binding and elution [137, 161]. Research by Malvicini, R et al. indicates that the size and distribution of MSC-Exos obtained through IEX and UC are similar, with comparable surface markers such as CD9 and CD63. However, IEX-isolated Exos are enriched in critical anti-inflammatory and immune-regulatory cytokines, exhibiting stronger anti-inflammatory activity [162]. This observation implies that IEX may preferentially select or preserve a unique subpopulation of vesicles based on surface charge properties, although it does so without specific molecular discrimination. Therefore, IEX lacks the specificity required to isolate Exos characterized by particular surface markers. To address these challenges, hybrid approaches are emerging. For instance, Hyunsung Kim et al. demonstrated an operation combining charge ion exchange with magnetic beads by functionalizing cationic polymer-coated magnetic beads with poly-L-lysine (PLL), which could be employed for the separation of plasma Exos, showing high yield and excellent reproducibility [163]. Future methodologies may persist in exploring multiparameter techniques that combine charge-based pre-enrichment with subsequent high-resolution sorting based on specific markers, thereby unraveling the complex interplay between surface charge, molecular identity, and functional characteristics within the exosomal landscape.

Hydrophobic interaction chromatography

Low-density lipoproteins (LDL) are adsorbed to the surface of Exos due to hydrophobic interactions caused by lipid accumulation defects in the Exos phospholipid bilayer structure. This leads to difficulties in effectively separating LDL from Exos using various existing separation techniques, such as UC and Sect. [164]. Based on the differences in surface hydrophobicity between Exos and LDL particles, Sisi Huang et al. investigated the use of hydrophobic interaction chromatography for the rapid and cost-effective separation of plasma Exos and LDL [142]. This technique can be combined with methods such as SEC and polymer precipitation to enhance the purity of the isolated Exos.

In summary, current isolation methods present fundamental trade-offs: physical property-based techniques (e.g. UC, SEC) inevitably co-isolate heterogeneous subpopulations, whereas immunoaffinity strategies enrich specific subsets at the risk of excluding functionally relevant vesicles (Table 2). Currently, most Exos separations utilize combined separation techniques to improve both the purity and yield of Exos [165]. Research by Lien-Hung Huan et al. found that the use of UC and SEC for the separation of hADSC-Exos resulted in differences in the types and quantities of differentially expressed proteins, indicating that distinct separation methods yield Exos preparations with divergent proteomic profiles [166]. Therefore, the selection or combination of various separation techniques should be based on the desired yield, purity, integrity, and downstream applications of the Exos.

Microfluidic platforms offer a compelling route toward integrated, efficient separation. This technology allows for precise manipulation of the physical properties of Exos, such as size and surface charge, as well as biochemical characteristics, including surface markers. It integrates functionalities like microelectrode dielectrophoresis and nanoporous membrane filtration, enabling the rapid acquisition of high-concentration Exos from low sample volumes and reagents, while separating Exos from protein contaminants, thus providing a reliable foundation for downstream proteomics analysis [143, 165]. However, most microfluidic technologies still rely on high-speed centrifugation for pre-treatment to remove large particle contaminants from the sample, limiting the widespread use of these systems due to their “off-chip” preparation [143]. Although on-chip pre-treatment remains a bottleneck, emerging architectures now unite multiplexed separation with high-sensitivity detection modalities—including fluorescence, colorimetry, and surface plasmon resonance—on a single chip, enabling one-step isolation and analysis [145, 167]. Ultimately, the transition from bulk preparations to defined, fit-for-purpose products hinges on our capacity to resolve and control heterogeneity at the single-vesicle level.

Co-incubation

The co-incubation method, a typical passive drug loading approach, involves the co-culturing of donor cells with target drugs, particularly hydrophobic drugs, allowing drugs to passively diffuse into the cells via concentration gradients and be endogenously encapsulated in Exos [174]. This method is simple and does not disrupt the integrity of Exos; however, the drug loading efficiency is limited by the lipophilicity of the drug and the concentration gradient, resulting in generally low efficiency and challenges in precise control [171, 175, 176]. In the case of OA, co-incubation may be suitable primarily for highly membrane-permeable therapeutic agents where ease of processing is favored over high dosages.

Electroporation

Passive diffusion frequently fails to achieve adequate encapsulation efficiencies, necessitating physical methods that transiently permeabilize the lipid bilayer. Conventional electroporation utilizes an external electric field to create transient pores in the phospholipid bilayer of Exos, allowing various small molecules to be passively loaded through electroosmosis [177]. However, this method can impact the stability and integrity of the Exos membrane, leading to aggregation and low drug loading efficiency [176]. Notably, Zhang et al. proposed a controlled nanopore electroporation (CNP) technique that integrates electroporation with cell transfection, generating controllable nanopores targeting donor cell membranes. This enables precise plasmid delivery and activation of the cellular stress response - HSP/[Ca²⁺]/p53-TSAP6 axis, simultaneously achieving enhanced Exos production and efficient endogenous mRNA loading fundamentally overcoming the limitations of traditional electroporation such as particle aggregation, low loading efficiency, and scalability issues [178]. This advancement positions CNP as a powerful strategy for delivering gene-regulating molecules (such as miRNA and siRNA) to modulate chondrocyte anabolic and catabolic processes in OA.

Ultrasonication

Mechanical permeabilization offers an alternative to electrical approaches. Ultrasonication employs acoustic shear to transiently weaken membrane integrity, facilitating efficient loading of hydrophilic molecules. Gao et al. found that ultrasonic drug loading increased the berberine loading capacity of Exos to 17.13%, a 3.4-fold improvement compared to co-incubation, while fully preserving sustained-release characteristics (71.44% release over 48 h) and brain-targeting delivery capability (accumulation at the injury site increased 18-fold), significantly enhancing repair after spinal cord injury [179]. Compared to electroporation, ultrasonication, while extending the drug loading time, achieves higher loading efficiency due to the cavitation effect [180].

Extrusion

Mechanical compression provides another route to membrane permeabilization. Extrusion forces Exos–drug mixtures through 100–400 nm apertures, inducing transient rupture and reassembly for uniform encapsulation, though this may alternative membrane properties [181]. Magnetic extrusion offers a refined alternative: iron oxide nanoparticles (IONP) purify endosomal membrane structures, in combination with mild extrusion through a 200 nm single aperture, yielding homogeneous Exos mimetics. Additionally, active drug loading using an ammonium sulfate gradient increased loading efficiency to 68% [182].

Freeze-thaw

Thermal permeabilization strategies present a stark contrast in biocompatibility. The freeze-thaw method utilizes cycles of liquid nitrogen freezing and room temperature thawing to induce phase transitions in the Exos membrane. While it can utilize the membrane fusion effect for drug loading, repeated freeze-thaw processes can irreversibly damage functional proteins in Exos, significantly reducing their natural targeting and immunomodulatory capabilities, thereby limiting therapeutic efficacy [181]. Due to these limitations, this method is often supplanted by more gentle alternatives. Han et al. developed a room-temperature ultrasound-extrusion approach that circumvents freeze-thaw-induced protein denaturation, enhancing Band-3/gp100 co-localization threefold and splenic accumulation 18-fold [183]. These findings reveal a broader principle: loading strategies that compromise surface protein integrity risk abrogating the intrinsic tropism that underpins exosomal therapeutic efficacy. For OA, where targeting of inflamed synovium or cartilage matrix depends on specific surface ligands, preserving the native protein corona is as critical as maximizing drug payload.

Genetic engineering

Biological loading strategies bypass physical membrane perturbation altogether. Genetic engineering enables modified donor cells to secrete Exos pre-loaded with therapeutic molecules or genetic material. Typical methods include transfection and viral transduction [176]. Du et al. engineered CD47-overexpressing Exos (ExosCD47) via liposome transfection in HEK293T cells, activating the CD47–SIRPα axis to reduce macrophage phagocytosis by 68% and increase tumor-targeting accumulation 2.1-fold [184]. Notably, recent studies have implemented a GFP-nanobody affinity system to achieve targeted loading of CRISPR-Cas9 RNPs, with an editing efficiency of 68%, resolving the uncontrollable loading issues associated with traditional transfection methods [185]. In viral transduction, for instance, Pastorakova et al. integrated a suicide gene (HSVTK) mediated by a retroviral vector into the genome of MSCs, driving the secretion of Exos efficiently loaded with HSVTK mRNA. After internalization by tumor cells, it expressed thymidine kinase, activating the prodrug ganciclovir (GCV) into cytotoxic metabolites, achieving over 50% tumor cell-specific killing. However, the clinical application of genetic engineering strategies faces three significant challenges: (1) the reliance on complex processes such as plasmid construction, stable cell transfection, and vesicle separation, resulting in high operational thresholds; (2) limitations in modifications to proteins or peptides coded by genetic material; (3) the overexpression of target proteins can easily lead to cytotoxicity, and inappropriate selection of donor cells may pose a tumor-promoting risk [176, 186, 187].

Liposome fusion

Hybrid strategies decouple cargo loading from biogenesis constraints. Exos, possessing a structural similarity to liposomes, can be fused to form hybrid vesicles that synergize the biological attributes of both systems while achieving enhanced drug-loading capacities. A notable strategy employs ethanol-mediated membrane fusion to generate hybrid nanoparticles (termed H-Hybrid) from TGF-β1-overexpressing Exos and lipid nanoparticles loaded with therapeutic agents. This method preserves the integrity of vesicular proteins and demonstrates significant therapeutic potential for OA [188]. These biomimetic hybrids function as modular platforms with independently tunable exosomal and liposomal components, enabling tailored strategies for OA pharmacotherapy.

Following the loading of therapeutics, the engineering of the exosomal membrane becomes paramount to ensure precise targeting to affected joints and to navigate in vivo barriers. Physical modification

Physical modification strategies enhance the exosomal membrane by exploiting non-covalent interactions such as hydrophobic insertion, electrostatic forces, and high-affinity ligand-receptor pairings. These methods typically maintain the native structure of Exos while imparting new functionalities aimed at improving delivery efficacy [190].

Functional molecules containing hydrophobic moieties (e.g., cholesterol, lipid-PEG conjugates) can be directly embedded into the Exos lipid bilayer. This approach is used to anchor nucleic acids or to confer “stealth” properties by grafting polyethylene glycol (PEG) onto the surface, which can extend systemic circulation time. However, potential immune responses to PEG upon repeated administration warrant consideration [191, 192]. Altering the surface charge of Exos is another potent strategy. For example, coating Exos with cationic polymers can neutralize their natural negative charge, thereby reduce inter-vesicle repulsion and enhance stability. For OA, conjugating cationic peptides to Exos can facilitate strong electrostatic adhesion to negatively charged glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) in cartilage, significantly improving intra-tissue penetration and retention [193, 194]. More versatile still, engineering universal adapter such as glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored affinity receptors, allows donor cells to secrete Exos that stably display biotinylated ligands. This approach achieved threefold enhanced uptake by carbonic anhydrase IX (CAIX)-positive tumor cells, and creates a flexible platform for the rapid screening and application of ligands specific to OA joint tissues [195].While physical modification strategies enhance the preservation of the natural structure of Exos and the functionalities of their surface proteins, this approach is still constrained by limitations such as prolonged balancing times and inadequate bond stability attributable to reliance on non-covalent binding [196].

Chemical modification

Chemical modification utilizes the inherent amino (-NH2), carboxyl (-COOH), and thiol (-SH) groups on the membrane components of Exos (transmembrane proteins and phospholipids) to covalently anchor ligand molecules while preserving the structural integrity of the membrane [165]. Among these functional groups, the prevalence of amino groups renders them the most common targets for modification, with bioorthogonal “click chemistry” emerging as a widely utilized strategy [197]. Traditional click chemistry (CuAAC) often relies on EDC/NHS chemical pre-activation of Exos carboxyl groups to introduce alkyne groups, followed by copper-catalyzed cycloaddition to connect azide ligands [198]. While efficient, the toxicity of copper catalysts and the risk of non-specific modifications present significant limitations for clinical applications [199, 200]. This challenge has catalyzed advancements in strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC), which permits efficient, catalyst-free conjugation of ligands—such as targeting peptides—to Exos through reactions between cyclooctynes and azides. This innovative method preserves vesicle integrity and function, thereby enhancing the therapeutic potential of engineered Exos [200, 201]. Covalent strategies extend beyond click chemistry. Dextran sulfate (DS) functions as a high-affinity ligand for scavenger receptor A (SR-A), which is markedly upregulated on pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages in the osteoarthritic synovium. DS-modified Exos exhibit significantly enhanced accumulation in inflamed joints and potently drive macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, showcasing precise immunomodulation as a therapeutic strategy for OA [202]. Integrated strategies further amplify these effects: concurrent anchoring of targeting peptides with PEG shielding—via physical insertion or covalent conjugation—enhances both targeting specificity and circulatory stability, underscoring the synergistic potential of multimodal engineering [203].

Nevertheless, these engineering approaches remain largely at the preclinical stage, lacking rigorous validation in complex OA microenvironments and standardized assessments of immunogenicity, off-target biodistribution and long-term safety after repeated dosing.

Regulation mechanism of Exos in OA via multiple pathways

Having established robust strategies for exosome production, purification and surface engineering, we now examine their therapeutic application in osteoarthritis. Although surface modification demonstrates precision-delivery potential, clinical translation requires elucidating how heterogeneous Exos populations modulate joint pathology. Exos cargoes including miRNAs, proteins, and lipids, as well as the resulting biological functions, can vary significantly due to factors such as cellular origin, the physiological or pathological state of the parental cells, and the methods of isolation previously described. This heterogeneity not only poses a central challenge in deciphering which specific subpopulations mediate particular therapeutic effects but also presents significant opportunities for developing more targeted treatment strategies. The diverse therapeutic effects discussed below (Table 4)—such as anti-inflammatory, cytoprotective, and matrix remodeling activities—are likely not derived from a single exosomal population but rather stem from a combination or synergistic action of different functional subpopulations within the isolated preparations. Keeping this perspective in mind will enhance our understanding of exosomal mechanisms and underline the necessity of employing advanced characterization and engineering strategies.

This section therefore reviews the molecular basis of Exos-mediated chondroprotection, immunomodulation and tissue repair, alongside current preclinical progress and the translational challenges posed by such heterogeneity.

The regulatory role of Exos in OA inflammation

In the progression of OA, the local microenvironment in the joints is characterized by a persistent low-grade inflammatory state, marked by elevated levels of various inflammatory mediators (such as IL-1β and TNF-α). These mediators significantly activate key signaling pathways (such as NF-κB), subsequently results in the disruption of the structure and function of the extracellular matrix through the inflammatory cascade [204, 205]. Additionally, proinflammatory factors in the inflammatory environment upregulate the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), promoting pathological angiogenesis and exacerbating osteoarthritis-related pain through the VEGFR1/FLT1 pathway, as well as incurring irreversible damage to joint structures [206]. Furthermore, immune cells (such as macrophages) in this inflammatory milieu release substantial amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS). The excessive production of ROS can disrupt the respiratory chain in chondrocytes, leading to oxidative stress, increased release of inflammatory mediators, and chondrocyte apoptosis, thereby creating an expanding inflammatory cascade that forms a self-propagating vicious cycle [207]. Consequently, improving the inflammatory environment at OA lesions is a prerequisite for the effective implementation of subsequent treatment strategies.

In recent years, Exos derived from various cell types, including MSCs and chondrocytes, have become a research hotspot due to their notable anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory capabilities, as illustrated in Fig. 6. Studies have shown that these Exos can not only directly inhibit the generation and release of key proinflammatory factors but also antagonize the activation of related signaling pathways, as well as modulate the polarization of macrophages, promoting he transition from proinflammatory M1-type macrophages to anti-inflammatory M2-type macrophages, effectively improving the inflammatory microenvironment in arthritis. For instance, Palamà et al. discovered through in vitro inflammatory models and computational simulations that Exos derived from human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hBMSCs) carry miR-21-5p, which can target thrombospondin 1 (THBS1), a protein known for its anti-angiogenic and anti-inflammatory effects. Additionally, the Exos also carry miR-145, which exhibits significant anti-inflammatory capabilities: its overexpression can markedly reduce the expression levels of proinflammatory factors such as IL-6 and IL-8 [208]. Direct evidence linking culture engineering to therapeutic mechanism comes from umbilical cord MSC-derived Exos produced under three-dimensional spheroid culture: these vesicles are enriched in miR-223-3p, which targets the NLRP3 inflammasome to suppress IL-1β and IL-18 maturation and block a central inflammatory cascade in OA pathogenesis [209]. This illustrates a broader paradigm: Exos therapeutic mechanisms in OA are not intrinsic constants but can be purposefully tuned through upstream engineering of the producer cell microenvironment. Integrating optimized 3D culture platforms with targeted molecular cargo loading thus carries substantial translational value.

Beyond miRNA components, Exos can also directly antagonize inflammatory signals and modulate the immune microenvironment by delivering other bioactive molecules. In an IL-1β-induced rabbit OA model, exosomes derived from platelets, which carry transforming growth factor β1 (TGF-β1) and endothelial growth factor, effectively suppressed the release of tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) [211]. As mentioned earlier, due to the high structural similarity between liposomes and Exos, they exhibit a high degree of fusibility, endowing the resultant fused Exos with new characteristics and therapeutic capabilities. For example, Kim et al. fused liposomes encapsulated with nicotinamide (NAM) and modified with Co12A1 antibodies with Exos derived from tonsil-derived mesenchymal stem cells (IMSCs) overexpressing TGF-β1 using ethanol-mediated fusion. This method effectively delivered NAM to block the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) pathway, significantly inhibiting the expression of interleukin 6 (IL-6) in a surgery-induced medial meniscus instability (DMM) rat OA model, while also enhancing the specificity and retention time of Exos in the lesion site [188]. Furthermore, engineered Exos also demonstrate potent anti-inflammatory effects: Zhan et al. utilized abundant inflammatory factors (IFN-γ, IL-6, and TNF-α) within OA lesions to precondition mesenchymal stem cells, yielding engineered Exos (iExos) with OA-specific immune memory. Notably, Exos derived from IL-6 preconditioning (IL-6-Exos) enhanced the expression of mt-ND3, improved mitochondrial function, and subsequently induced M2 macrophage polarization, thus exerting stronger anti-inflammatory and tissue repair effects [212, 213]. Collectively, these findings validate both liposome hybridization and microenvironmental preconditioning as viable engineering routes, while confirming that unmodified vesicles retain intrinsic anti-inflammatory capacity. However, the rapid expansion of techniques has outpaced systematic comparative evaluation. Head-to-head studies across methods are urgently needed to identify cost-effective, safe, and indication-optimized regimens for clinical translation.

In conclusion, Exos derived from various cell sources can participate in and regulate the signaling pathways of inflammatory cells related to OA, significantly influencing the expression and release of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory factors. This modulation facilitates the improvement of the inflammatory microenvironment at the lesion sites and creates conditions conducive to tissue repair.

Exos promote chondrocyte survival and anti-apoptosis

In normal articular cartilage, highly specialized chondrocytes not only contribute to the structural integrity of cartilage but also secrete a variety of cytokines that regulate the synthesis and degradation of the extracellular matrix, thereby maintaining cartilage homeostasis [214]. However, during the progression of OA, elevated levels of IL-1β within the inflammatory environment can induce chondrocyte apoptosis by modulating the expression of proteins such as Bax, Caspase 3/9, and Bcl-2 [215]. Additionally, autophagy in chondrocytes is inhibited in OA, leading to the accumulation of damaged intracellular materials that ultimately compromise chondrocyte survival [216].

Previous studies have shown that exosomes derived from infrapatellar fat pad mesenchymal stem cells (MSC IPFP-Exos) may significantly enhance chondrocyte autophagy and promote chondrocyte survival by partially inhibiting the mTOR pathway through the abundant internal miR-100-5p [217]. Furthermore, Li et al. reported that exosomes rich in circHIPK3 derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs-circHIPK3-Exos) significantly promote chondrocyte proliferation and migration while inhibiting the expression of Runx2, a transcription factor that drives chondrocyte hypertrophy, and MMP-13, thus protecting chondrocytes from apoptosis [218]. Similarly, neutrophil-derived exosomes (Neutrophil Exos) demonstrate comparable effects; when acting on human articular chondrocytes, they enhance the intracellular expression level of miR-455-3p, subsequently downregulating Runx2 and alkaline phosphatase (ALPL), an enzyme involved in cartilage degradation, thereby inhibiting chondrocyte apoptosis and preventing cartilage structural damage. Importantly, their findings indicate that neutrophil-derived Exos from rheumatoid arthritis (RA) patients are functionally similar to those from healthy donors, suggesting their potential clinical value as an autologous therapeutic strategy [219]. Building upon the genetic engineering and surface modification strategies discussed in preceding sections, engineered Exos exhibit marked chondroprotective potential. Exos bearing a CHP–LAMP2B fusion protein efficiently deliver chondrogenic miRNAs (miR-126-3p and miR-92a-3p) to residual chondrocytes, coordinately regulating proliferation, apoptosis, and matrix synthesis [209]. This targeted design ensures precise homing to diseased articular tissue, amplifying therapeutic efficacy while curtailing off-target exposure and preserving intrinsic biocompatibility. Collectively, advances in upstream bioprocessing—from optimized donor cell selection and 3D culture to precision isolation and covalent surface engineering—converge to position such integrated Exos platforms as increasingly robust therapeutic modalities for OA.

Exosomes promote cartilage extracellular matrix synthesis and remodeling

The ECM secreted by chondrocytes is primarily composed of type II collagen, which forms a network structure that not only provides cartilage with the ability to resist mechanical stress but also promotes water retention and lubrication through the aggregation and secretion of various proteoglycans [220]. Research indicates that in the pathological state of OA, pro-inflammatory factors activate the expression of MMPs and other ECM-degrading enzymes [221]. Concurrently, the expression of TLR4 in the lesion area is significantly increased, leading to the activation of the NF-κB pathway, which specifically recognizes and degrades fibronectin and hyaluronic acid, among other components of the ECM [221]. When these pathological changes disrupt the homeostasis of the ECM, the capacity to maintain cartilage protective functions diminishes, consequently impairing normal chondrocyte metabolism. Therefore, protecting and promoting the reconstruction of the ECM plays a crucial role in delaying the progression of OA and in therapeutic interventions [222].

Recent experimental results have demonstrated the significant potential of Exos from various sources in promoting ECM synthesis and repair. Hu et al. designed macrophage-derived Exos overexpressing Lacc1 (OE-Exos), which exhibited protective functions for maintaining ECM homeostasis in both the temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis (TMJOA) mouse model and in vitro models. Specifically, vitro experiments showed that OE-Exos could upregulate the expression of type II collagen and proteoglycans while inhibiting the expression of ECM-degradation-related genes, including MMP3 and ADAMTS18, in an IL-1β-stimulated environment. In the in vivo model, TMJOA mice treated with OE-Exos demonstrated a significantly increased synthesis level of glycosaminoglycan (GAG) in cartilage (GAG can combine with proteins to form proteoglycans), while immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining further confirmed the increased expression of type II collagen and proteoglycans [223]. Another study by Li et al. found that chondrogenically differentiated C-IPFP-MSC-derived Exos (CIE) could upregulate the expression of genes associated with ECM synthesis (e.g., ACAN, COL2A1, and SOX9), thereby promoting the secretion of COL2 and ACAN. More importantly, they discovered that CIE has a dual action: it can not only deliver exogenous SOX9 directly to chondrocytes but also transfer exogenous FOXO1 to chondrocytes, where it specifically binds to FOXO3A, promoting the transcriptional synthesis of endogenous SOX9 in chondrocytes [224]. This highlights the significant effect of CIE on promoting cartilage matrix synthesis.

Recently, TU et al. employed ultrasound to integrate KGN and TGF-B1 into exosomes derived from hUC-MSCs, which were then coated with succinylated chitosan. The engineered exosomes exhibited enhanced cartilage permeation in vitro and promoted the generation of smooth, seamlessly integrated cartilage with the subchondral bone when applied to full-thickness cartilage defects in SD rats, demonstrating superior cartilage repair capabilities [225]. Notably, low-oxygen preconditioned adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell (ADSC)-derived ApoExos (H-ApoExos) exhibited stronger therapeutic effects compared to those cultivated under normoxic conditions. This may be related to their mimicry of the hypoxic microenvironment in which most MSCs exist in vivo. Additionally, the richer protein components contained in H-ApoExos may be one reason for their diverse biological functions and enhanced reparative effects [226]. The superior performance of preconditioned Exos (e.g. those subjected to hypoxia or chondrogenic differentiation) further highlights how extrinsic manipulation can modify exosomal heterogeneity, leading to the generation of subpopulations with enhanced specific functionalities, such as promoting targeted ECM synthesis. This variability is crucial for tailoring Exos to achieve optimal cartilage repair.

In conclusion, Exos derived from diverse sources and subjected to engineered modifications have demonstrated significant capabilities in both in vitro and in vivo models. They promote the expression of anabolic genes (such as COL2A1, ACAN, and SOX9) while inhibiting the expression of catabolic genes, including MMPs and Adamts. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the potential mechanisms by which Exos can be utilized in the treatment of OA but also provide important theoretical guidance and supporting evidence for the future translation of these therapies into clinical applications for OA.

Exos inhibit angiogenesis

Angiogenesis can improve the blood supply and nutrient delivery to subchondral bone, thereby aiding its subsequent repair. However, related studies indicate that the progression of OA is often associated with the generation of numerous pathological blood vessels, such as H-type vessels. These aberrant vessels interact with mesenchymal stem cells, driving abnormal bone formation within the joint and leading to the degeneration of subchondral bone. These pathological changes contribute to the deterioration of the overlying cartilage and ultimately exacerbate the progression of OA [227]. Therefore, to prevent osteogenic lesions associated with angiogenesis, therapeutic strategies that promote angiogenesis must be precisely regulated. Indiscriminate induction of angiogenesis may lead to worsened conditions. In contrast, multiple studies support the idea that inhibiting angiogenesis can help slow the progression of OA [228, 229]. As a result, this section will focus on the mechanisms by which Exos inhibit angiogenesis.

Exos primarily inhibit angiogenesis through two pathways: on one hand, Exos exert anti-angiogenic effects indirectly through their anti-inflammatory properties. On the other hand, Exos can also directly inhibit angiogenesis through their contents. Research has shown that synthetic nanoparticles carrying anti-angiogenic agents can suppress the formation of sub-synovial blood vessels in osteoarthritis [230, 231]. For example, Liao et al. encapsulated axitinib within macrophage membranes to create bio-nanoparticles capable of penetrating the macrophage barrier within the joint synovium, targeting CD34 + cells, and continuously releasing axitinib, demonstrating powerful anti-angiogenic capabilities in vitro [232]. Similarly, nanoparticles prepared by encapsulating pazopanib in a polyethylene glycol-b-polycaprolactone amphiphilic diblock copolymer (PEG-b-PCL) exhibited targeted effects on VEGFR2, inhibiting CD31 + angiogenesis in the synovium in a mouse model of OA following partial medial meniscectomy (PMM) [206]. These nanoparticles, having a molecular size similar to Exos, along with the easily engineerable characteristics of Exos, suggest that they can also serve as carriers for these drugs [233]. Dong et al. demonstrated that CP05-mediated CD63 targeting enables precise loading of therapeutic cargoes such as KV11 onto HUVEC-derived Exos, potently suppressing pathological angiogenesis [234]. This peptide-based surface engineering exemplifies the modular functionalization strategies discussed in Sect. "Engineered modification of Exos for enhanced targeting and stability", wherein affinity ligands program both vesicle tropism and payload delivery. Given that aberrant synovial angiogenesis drives inflammatory infiltration and cartilage degradation in OA, such targeted anti-angiogenic Exos offer a translatable paradigm for joint therapy.

Enhancing Exos with biomaterials for OA treatment

As elucidated in Chap. 5, Exos demonstrate considerable potential to intervene in the pathological progression of OA through multiple mechanisms, translating these biological effects into durable clinical benefit critically depends on overcoming formidable intra-articular delivery barriers. The osteoarthritic joint constitutes a uniquely hostile microenvironment in which even locally administered Exos are subject to rapid clearance, enzymatic degradation, and insufficient retention at pathological sites, resulting in a markedly shortened therapeutic window. Indeed, unmodified Exos exhibit an extremely limited in vivo half-life—ranging from minutes to tens of minutes—severely constraining their therapeutic efficacy [246–248]. Biomaterials engineering offers an appealing solution and ideal platform for addressing these bottlenecks. By loading Exos onto carefully designed biomaterial carriers, precise regulation of their spatial distribution and release behavior can be achieved, significantly enhancing their therapeutic efficacy [249]. Biomaterial delivery systems for Exos in OA broadly comprise synthetic polymers and natural biopolymers. Synthetic matrices afford precise tunability of degradation kinetics, mechanical strength and surface functionalization through molecular design. Natural biopolymers intrinsically recapitulate cartilage extracellular matrix composition, providing bioactive, biomimetic microenvironments that synthetic materials struggle to replicate. Polymer-based materials: a tunable and functional engineering platform

Polymer-based materials, due to their tunable physicochemical properties (such as degradation rates, mechanical strength, and hydrophobicity), excellent biocompatibility, and mature processing techniques, have become ideal carriers for delivering Exos in OA treatment. These materials can be meticulously designed to form micro/nanoparticles, hydrogels, or fiber scaffolds to meet various clinical needs. Applying this general platform to osteoarthritis treatment, the high tunability of polymer-based materials can specifically address the challenges presented by the unique pathological environment of the joints. Synthetic copolymers such as polylactic acid (PLA) and its copolymer polylactic-co-glycolic acid (PLGA), polyethylene glycol (PEG), and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) have emerged as key materials in this field due to their distinctive properties.

Polylactic acid and its copolymers (PLA and PLGA)

PLA and PLGA are biodegradable polymers, with the final degradation products of PLGA being lactic acid and glycolic acid, which are naturally metabolic byproducts of the body, ensuring high biosafety [248]. Additionally, they possess excellent biocompatibility and mechanical properties, providing essential mechanical support at cartilage defect sites, which is beneficial for maintaining the local microenvironment required for Exos-mediated cartilage repair [250]. Duan et al. developed a bilayer porous PLGA scaffold that not only offered mechanical stabilization but also created spatially distinct microenvironments favorable for osteogenic and chondrogenic differentiation. The upper layer with smaller pore sizes (100–200 μm) promoted cell aggregation and hypoxia, while the lower layer with larger pores (300–450 μm) facilitated vascularization and subchondral bone formation. Bone marrow-derived stem cells (BMSCs) survived on the PLGA scaffold for at least 8 weeks and contributed to the formation of trabecular-like and cartilaginous tissues, demonstrating the scaffold’s capacity to support tissue regeneration relevant to Exos-based OA therapy [251]. A key advantage of PLA/PLGA for Exos delivery lies in their precisely tunable degradation rates, which can be adjusted by molecular weight, crystallinity, and lactic acid to glycolic acid (LA/GA) ratio. This tunability enables synchronization between carrier degradation and Exos release kinetics, allowing sustained intra-articular therapeutic effects. In OA treatment, this property directly addresses the short residence time of Exos following injection. For example, Yuan and colleagues embedded PLGA microspheres loaded with kartogenin (KGN) into a methyl chitosan–oxidized chondroitin sulfate hydrogel, forming an ultrasound-responsive composite system. Controlled degradation and release from PLGA microspheres maintained bioactive factor availability for at least 28 days, significantly enhancing type II collagen expression and cartilage repair [249]. These findings suggest that PLA/PLGA-based systems can serve as effective carriers to prolong and potentiate Exos-mediated therapeutic outcomes in OA.

Polyethylene glycol (PEG)

PEG, a water-soluble synthetic polymer, is renowned for its excellent biocompatibility and stealth properties that reduce protein adsorption. These attributes enhance the circulation half-life of drug-loaded particles, making PEG particularly beneficial when combined with Exos for OA treatment [252]. By minimizing recognition by the reticuloendothelial system, PEG facilitates the effective delivery of therapeutic agents to targeted sites in OA. Furthermore, PEG is capable of forming stable hydrogels and smart systems that facilitate controlled release and enhance the stability of Exos, contributing to improved outcomes in OA treatment [253]. Numerous studies have indicated that PEG holds significant therapeutic potential in OA treatment. For instance, a therapeutic platform was developed by a team led by Wang that utilizes M2 macrophage-derived exosomes with a surface modified by an MMP-cleavable PEG layer and oligo-lysine. After intravenous injection, PEG extended circulation time and facilitated Exos targeting to inflamed joints via CCL2. In the inflammatory microenvironment, MMP cleaves PEG, exposing positively charged oligo-lysine to clear pro-inflammatory free DNA while concurrently promoting M2 polarization of Exos to exert anti-inflammatory effects. In arthritis mouse and dog models, this PEGylated Exos therapy significantly inhibited joint inflammation and effectively protected cartilage and bone, demonstrating good therapeutic potential [254].

However, PLA/PLGA and PEG also exhibit limitations in practical applications: PLGA has significant hydrophobicity, which results in weak adhesiveness at the interface with hydrophilic cartilage tissue, and degradation may create a locally acidic microenvironment [255]. On the other hand, PEG is known for its bioinertness [256]. These challenges in constructing carriers have led to new material selection and modification strategies. To combine the advantages of different materials and compensate for their shortcomings, the copolymerization or blending strategy of PLA/PLGA with PEG has emerged as a hot research topic in OA treatment. Tao and colleagues developed a thermoresponsive gel based on poly(D, L-lactide)-b-polyethylene glycol-b-poly(D, L-lactide) (PLEL) triblock copolymer (D, L-lactide is a cyclic dimer of lactic acid). This hydrogel system loaded with small extracellular vesicles overexpressing circRNA3503 gelled at body temperature and achieved sustained release of Exos carrying circRNA3503 for over 30 days. Results indicated that this gel system alleviated inflammation-induced cell apoptosis and restored the balance between ECM synthesis and degradation, promoting chondrocyte renewal to mitigate progressive loss of chondrocytes, thereby significantly delaying the OA process [257]. Compared to using PLA or PEG alone, the combined use of multiple materials exhibited more robust sustained release characteristics and greater potential for continuous therapy.

Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)

PVA is a water-soluble synthetic polymer recognized for its excellent chemical stability, high elasticity, outstanding film-forming properties, and abrasion resistance. The abundance of hydroxyl groups along the PVA molecular chain imparts strong hydrophilicity and the ability to interact with biological tissues through hydrogen bonding, often making it a component in hydrogels or enhancing the mechanical properties of other materials. In the context of OA treatment, the core advantage of PVA lies in its superior lubrication properties. The micropores within PVA hydrogels can store significant amounts of water, allowing PVA to function as a lubricant on cartilage surfaces, directly reducing cartilage wear and pain caused by friction, which provides immediate relief of OA symptoms [258]. Importantly, when combined with Exos, PVA-based hydrogels can simultaneously function as sustained Exos carriers and biomechanical lubricants, offering a dual therapeutic strategy for OA management. Recent studies have highlighted the potential of PVA-containing hydrogels in osteochondral and joint-related applications, providing useful design references for Exos-loaded systems. For instance, the Thangprasert team combined PVA with gelatin in specific ratios to develop biomimetic hybrid hydrogels and investigated their biological properties in supporting bone tissue formation beneath the cartilage surface. The results indicated that a gelatin/PVA ratio of 30:70 led to good cell proliferation due to the presence of RGD amino acid sequences, and the hydrogel at this ratio exhibited high alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and calcium ion deposition, making it well-suited as a cartilage scaffold at the interface to facilitate subchondral bone formation [259]. Although this study did not directly involve Exos, the findings suggest that PVA-based composite hydrogels can provide a supportive interfacial microenvironment for osteochondral repair, which may be beneficial for enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of Exos in OA. In another study, Zhang et al. prepared PVA hydrogels through repeated freeze–thaw cycles and encapsulated mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived Exos within the hydrogel matrix. This system was further integrated into a microneedle platform for diabetic wound treatment, where sustained Exos release promoted tissue regeneration and favorably modulated the local microenvironment [260]. Although the disease model differed from OA, this work demonstrates the feasibility of using PVA hydrogels as stable Exos carriers capable of protecting bioactivity and enabling controlled release. These characteristics indicate that PVA-based hydrogels could be adapted for intra-articular Exos delivery in OA, combining lubrication, mechanical support, and sustained Exos-mediated therapeutic effects.

Natural biopolymers: high biocompatibility and biomimetic microenvironments

Natural biomaterials primarily originate from plants, animals, or the human body and include polysaccharides and proteins. Compared to synthetic materials such as PLA/PLGA, PEG, and PVA their most prominent advantages include inherent high biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and molecular structures that often contain specific bioactive sites recognizable and adhesive to cells, along with good biodegradability. Additionally, they can mimic the natural structure and function of the extracellular matrix ECM, providing an ideal biomimetic microenvironment for cell proliferation, differentiation, and tissue integration. Owing to their advantages, natural biopolymers are well suited to create biomimetic microenvironments that preserve Exos biofunction and enhance their therapeutic interactions with chondrocytes and surrounding joint tissues. Therefore, integrating natural biopolymers into Exos delivery systems represents a critical strategy for advancing OA therapy beyond passive Exos transport toward active regulation of cartilage repair and joint homeostasis.

Gelatin

Gelatin, a partially hydrolyzed product of collagen, retains a large number of RGD sequences and exhibits good biocompatibility, biodegradability, low immunogenicity, and water solubility, making it widely used in tissue regeneration and engineering applications [261]. Owing to its ECM-mimicking properties and abundant cell-adhesive motifs, gelatin-based hydrogels are particularly suitable for serving as carriers for Exos in OA therapy. Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA), a photocrosslinkable derivative of gelatin, has been extensively explored as a delivery platform for engineered Exos. Specifically, engineered Exos encapsulated with the cartilage-affinity peptide WYRGRL (W) conjugated to GelMA (W-Exo@GelMA) can effectively target chondrocytes, with in vitro studies indicating that it significantly inhibits catabolism and promotes anabolism. Moreover, W-Exo@GelMA shows advantages in terms of anti-OA activity and cartilage repair capabilities, significantly inhibiting OA-related inflammation and immune gene expression while enhancing retention time within the joint. This combination of gelatin with engineered Exos opens new avenues for OA therapy [262]. Building on this research, Chen and colleagues used GelMA as a carrier for MSC secretomes, enabling effective loading and retention of these secretomes while preventing degradation for slow release and local sustained action. Additionally, GelMA hydrogels exhibit good shear-thinning properties, allowing them to be smoothly injected into joint cavities and rapidly cured under UV light. This characteristic effectively addresses the limitations of traditional MSC secretomes, enhancing injectability and retention in joint cavities, which is crucial for cartilage repair in complex mechanical environments. Thus, the combination of gelatin and engineered Exosomes provides a more diverse and effective solution for OA treatment [263].

Hyaluronic acid (HA)

HA is a linear polysaccharide that serves as a core component of the extracellular matrix and is widely present in various tissues and bodily fluids [264]. Its molecular structure contains numerous carboxyl and hydroxyl groups, imparting strong hydrophilicity and lubricating properties. Biologically, HA plays a critical role in cartilage homeostasis by promoting chondrogenic differentiation of MSCs, maintaining chondrocyte phenotype, and stimulating ECM synthesis. Owing to its excellent biocompatibility and ECM-mimicking capability, HA has become a key material in cartilage tissue engineering and Exos-based OA therapy [265–267]. To enhance the therapeutic efficacy of Exos, researchers have developed HA-based delivery systems with targeting and sustained-release functions. For example, a mixed exosome system incorporating a cartilage-affinity peptide and an FGF18-targeting CRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing tool (CAP/FGF18-hyEXO) was encapsulated within methacrylated HA (HAMA) hydrogel microspheres to form an injectable microgel system (CAP/FGF18-hyEXO@HMs). This system established a self-renewing lubricating layer within the joint cavity, enabling prolonged lubrication, targeted gene activation, cartilage regeneration, inflammation reduction, and ECM preservation, thereby offering a multifunctional strategy for OA treatment [268]. Additionally, Pang et al. developed aldehyde- and methacrylate-modified HA (AHAMA), enabling both cartilage-surface adhesion via Schiff base reactions and photopolymerization-assisted microsphere fabrication. AHAMA microspheres were subsequently loaded with IFN-γ-pretreated MSC-derived ECM to construct an IFN-γ-ECM@AH system. These microspheres selectively adhered to OA-like cartilage while avoiding healthy tissue and promoted the release of chemokines such as CXCL12, thereby enhancing stem cell recruitment and aggregation. This work highlights the potential of HA-based microspheres as Exos-compatible delivery platforms with immunomodulatory and regenerative functions in OA therapy [269, 270].

Collagen and its mimetic peptides

Collagen is the most abundant structural protein in the ECM, and its unique triple-helix conformation confers excellent tensile strength, effectively maintaining tissue integrity and elasticity. Furthermore, its small particle size, large surface area, strong adsorption capacity, and stability in aqueous solutions make collagen an ideal carrier for drug delivery and tissue engineering applications [271]. In OA treatment, the value of collagen lies in its high similarity to cartilage ECM and its functionalization potential. Yi and colleagues developed a collagen-based hydrogel (Col-OHA) through Schiff base formation between oxidized hyaluronic acid (OHA) and type I collagen, enabling dexamethasone sodium phosphate (DSP) loading and sustained release. The OHA-induced collagen self-assembly significantly shortened gelation time and rendered the hydrogel sensitive to matrix metalloproteinases. In vivo studies demonstrated effective suppression of inflammatory mediators and long-term alleviation of OA symptoms. Although DSP was used as the therapeutic agent, this hydrogel design provides valuable insights into constructing collagen-based Exos delivery systems with enzyme-responsive release profiles for OA therapy [272]. To further enhance targeting efficiency, Liang et al. genetically engineered Exos by fusing a cartilage-affinity peptide (CAP) to the exosomal membrane protein LAMP-2B, enabling specific chondrocyte targeting and deep cartilage penetration. CAP-modified Exos loaded with miRNA-140 significantly increased intracellular miRNA delivery and effectively restored cartilage surface integrity in OA mouse models, demonstrating the therapeutic advantages of targeted Exos delivery [273]. Moreover, collagen mimetic peptides (CMPs) have been designed to replicate specific collagen functions. Huang and colleagues combined functional engineered Exos with fibrin-like CMPs and encapsulated them in RGD-containing photo-crosslinkable alginate hydrogels. CMPs significantly enhanced Exos retention and prevented burst release while preserving bioactivity. This strategy improved bone regeneration outcomes and offers valuable insights for anchoring Exos within biomimetic matrices for OA and other regenerative applications [274].

Carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC)

CMC is a water-soluble cellulose derivative obtained through the carboxymethylation of natural cellulose. Its molecular structure contains hydrophilic carboxymethyl groups, which endow it with excellent solubility, thickening properties, film-forming capabilities, and the ability to interact with other molecules through hydrogen bonds and ionic interactions. In OA therapy, CMC is particularly attractive due to its surface modification potential and ability to enhance carrier stability and cell interactions. Yang et al. modified poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) microspheres with CMC, significantly enhancing the hydrophilicity of the microspheres, dispersion stability, and electrostatic interactions with cell membranes. The increased negative surface charge enhanced microsphere stability and cell adhesion, providing an optimized platform for Exos loading and prolonged intra-articular retention, thereby improving targeted Exos delivery in OA joints [275].

Alginate

Sodium alginate is a natural anionic polysaccharide extracted from brown algae or bacteria, known for its excellent biocompatibility and convenient gelation capability (with divalent ions such as calcium ions), making it an important material in biomedical and tissue engineering fields [276]. Xu et al. and colleagues developed a sodium alginate hydrogel for delivering mesenchymal stem cell-derived Exos from the infrapatellar fat pad (IPFP). In a rat OA model, alginate-mediated Exos delivery significantly enhanced fibrocartilage formation and promoted macrophage polarization toward a reparative M2 phenotype, demonstrating its immunomodulatory and regenerative potential [277]. However, the limited tissue adhesion of native alginate necessitates further modification. Composite scaffold strategies incorporating alginate with other materials have shown enhanced mechanical properties and osteogenic performance, providing valuable design references for constructing Exos-loaded osteochondral repair systems in OA treatment [278, 279].

Chondroitin sulfate (CS)

CS is a key sulfated glycosaminoglycan in cartilage ECM that possesses anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-apoptotic properties [280]. CS is rich in hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amide groups, granting it some degree of tissue adhesion capability; however, this natural adhesive strength remains insufficient for clinical applications, requiring the introduction of additional groups to enhance adhesive properties [281]. In OA treatment, CS plays a multifunctional role by stimulating HA and collagen synthesis while inhibiting ECM-degrading enzymes. Wang et al. chemically modified CS by introducing methacrylate and aldehyde groups, using it as a “bridge” to integrate hydrogels with cartilage tissue. This strategy ensured long-term retention of hydrogels at defect sites and significantly improved cartilage repair outcomes, providing a promising approach for integrating Exos-loaded hydrogels with native cartilage tissue [282].

Chitosan

Chitosan is a deacetylated derivative of chitin and is the only naturally occurring cationic polysaccharide, known for its excellent hydrophilicity, biocompatibility, and biodegradability [283]. Unlike other polysaccharides, the free amino groups in chitosan impart a positive charge to its molecular structure [284]. This characteristic allows chitosan to bind electrostatically with negatively charged molecules, enabling the modulation of drug adsorption and release behaviors, thus serving as an outstanding drug carrier. Given that the phospholipid membrane of Exos is negatively charged, the positive characteristics of chitosan provide unique advantages in Exosome delivery systems [285]. Additionally, the amino groups in chitosan can react with aldehyde-containing polysaccharides to form dynamic covalent bonds through Schiff base reactions, making it an ideal choice for constructing self-healing hydrogels [286]. Wang and colleagues developed a self-healing hydrogel using coral hydroxyapatite, silk fibroin, glycolic acid, and bifunctional polyethylene glycol as materials, utilizing it as a delivery carrier for human umbilical cord MSC-derived Exosomes. The hydrogel demonstrated sustained Exos release, enhanced osteogenic differentiation, and improved cell proliferation, highlighting its potential for enhancing Exos-based regenerative therapies in OA [287].

Challenges and Future Directions for Natural Biomaterials: The primary challenges associated with natural biomaterials include significant batch variability and structural complexity, which hinder consistent performance. The extraction and purification processes can be cumbersome and costly. Additionally, the mechanical properties of natural materials are often relatively weak and degrade rapidly. To enhance functions such as adhesion, chemical modifications are frequently necessary; however, the effects of such modifications on the natural biological activity are still under-researched. As underscored by cross-material comparisons (Table 5), no single matrix satisfies the multifactorial requirements of osteoarthritis therapy. Future development should therefore focus on: (1) standardizing sources and processing of natural materials; (2) developing mild and targeted modification strategies to retain biological activity; (3) deeply investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying the interactions between materials, Exosomes, and host cells; and (4) creating composite or hybrid material systems to address the limitations of individual materials [288–290].

Conclusion

The exceptional biological properties of Exos position them as ideal candidates for OA treatment, while ongoing efforts to optimize isolation, large-scale production, and engineering modification strategies are progressively addressing initial bottlenecks to clinical translation. More importantly, we elucidate that Exosomes effectively intervene in the pathological progression of OA through synergistic mechanisms involving multiple pathways and targets (e.g., anti-inflammatory and promoting cartilage regeneration), showcasing therapeutic potential that surpasses traditional pharmaceuticals. However, translating this therapeutic potential into clinical reality requires overcoming the central challenge of low in vivo delivery efficiency. In conclusion, harnessing the superior properties of various materials, by providing protection, prolonging retention, and achieving targeted and controlled release, is crucial for maximizing the efficacy of Exos.

Despite recent advancements in enhancing therapeutic cargo loading and leveraging biomaterials, several interconnected systemic challenges persist. These include the development of standardized, potency-based characterization protocols and the long-term safety considerations of engineered exosomes. To bridge the gap between laboratory innovations and clinical applications, future research should focus on establishing a rigorous, product-centric development paradigm. This includes embracing transformative next-generation technologies, such as integrating computational and AI-driven rational design to predict optimal engineering strategies. Additionally, utilizing cell-free synthetic biology platforms can facilitate the assembly of standardized, fully defined artificial exosome mimetics with feedback-controlled therapeutic release. Converging basic research with these technological innovations will expedite the clinical translation of engineered exosomes, ultimately providing effective treatment options for patients with OA.
