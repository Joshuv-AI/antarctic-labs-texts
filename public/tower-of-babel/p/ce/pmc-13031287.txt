Evaluation of deformable image registration vs offline adaptive replanning in post-op oral cavity cancer treated with volumetric modulated Arc therapy
Authors: Shubham Dokania, Ashutosh Mukherji, Sambit S. Nanda, Sanju Sanju, Pritam Mondal, Ninad H. Patil, Ajay S. Krishnan, Ajay Choubey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anatomical changes during adjuvant radiotherapy (RT) for post-operative oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma (OCSCC) have greater dosimetric effects with highly conformal techniques, thereby necessitating plan adaptation. We compared conventional adaptive RT (cART) with deformable image registration (DIR)-based ART (dART). Post-operative OCSCC patients receiving adjuvant RT with a ≥ 5 mm change in skin contour on cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) were enrolled. After re-simulation, planning CT (pCT) and extended CBCT were imported into Velocity® to generate a synthetic CT (sCT) using DIR. Separate plans were created on the re-simulation CT (rCT) and sCT. The initial plan he was also projected onto the sCT anatomy and compared with the original plan on the pCT. Geometric (volume, Dice similarity coefficient [DSC], mean distance to agreement [MDA]) and dosimetric (mean dose, dose-volume histogram [DVH]) differences were evaluated between rCT- and sCT-based plans. Twenty-five patients were prospectively enrolled. Based on DSC and MDA, DIR showed acceptable geometric accuracy for all structures except the spinal cord (DSC = 0.75). Compared to pCT, most structures had significant volume reduction on sCT, except the low-risk planning target volume (PTVLR) (p = 0.14) and the larynx (increased, p = 0.04). Projection of the initial plan onto sCT revealed significant loss in PTVLR coverage (V95% p = 0.001; D98% p = 0.01) and a non-significant loss for the high-risk PTV (PTVHR). Organs at risk (OARs) doses increased non-significantly, except for the mandible (p = 0.007). Comparison of rCT and sCT volumes showed a 3.7% increases in PTVHR (p = 0.009) and 5.7% in PTVLR (p = 0.049), with non-significant OAR volume increases (2.4%–6.3%) except for the larynx (decreased). DVH comparison showed non-significant dose reductions to the parotids (0.6%), the mandible (0.5%), and the larynx (5.8%), but slight increases for the spinal cord (2.6%) and its planning organ at risk volume (PRV) (3.7%). Target coverage was significantly lower with sCT-based plans (PTVHR V95% by 3.2%, D98% by 3.8%, PTVLR V95% by 4.1% and D98% by 4.3%). dART improved OAR sparing but was compromised in a few target coverage parameters. Further enhancement of DIR accuracy is needed. Currently, DIR is primarily used in ART for dose accumulation to assess the need for plan adaptation.

Introduction

The standard of care for oral cavity squamous cell cancers (OCSCC) is surgery, followed by adjuvant radiotherapy (RT) or concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CTRT), depending on the risk factors in the post-operative histopathological report (HPR)1–3. Adjuvant RT reduces the risk of locoregional recurrence, resulting in a 30% increase in three-year disease-free survival (DFS)4. Over the last three decades, adjuvant RT for postoperative OCSCC has transformed from two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional conformal RT (3DCRT) to intensity-modulated RT (IMRT) and volumetric modulated RT (VMAT), with a significant improvement in the toxicity profile5–7.

Patients on adjuvant RT can undergo anatomical changes due to significant weight loss, muscle atrophy, inflammation, or resolution of post-operative edema/seroma during RT. In such a situation, both organs at risk (OARs) and target volumes undergo alterations, leading to overdosing and under-dosing respectively8. These consequences are more dramatic with IMRT and VMAT because of the sharp dose gradients between the edges of the target volumes and the critical OARs9. The execution of VMAT plans derived from a single planning CT (pCT) is inherently sensitive to positional and anatomical uncertainties, which may translate into unexpected normal tissue toxicities and/or marginal geographic misses of the target volumes10. Image-guided RT (IGRT) offers online correction of patient position and setup errors via rigid couch shifts. However, rigid couch shifts are not always sufficient to account for non-rigid geometric errors, such as volume and spatial position changes in the tumor and normal tissue11.

Adaptive RT (ART), pioneered by Yan et al. in 1997, provides a framework for modifying treatment plans in response to evolving anatomical and dosimetric changes, with the aim of maintaining the originally intended plan quality over the treatment course12. Various studies have indicated that between 11% and 65% of patients with head and neck cancer (HNCs) required adaptive radiotherapy (ART) and benefited from it in terms of dosimetry13,14. ART can be implemented on different temporal scales (offline, online, and real-time). Within these workflows, various adaptation methods can be employed, such as full replanning or DIR-based approaches. The full replanning method, referred to as conventional ART (cART), involves resimulation, recontouring, replanning, and implementation of a modified adaptive plan, which is time-consuming and can be resource-intensive for a department. Therefore, cART is adopted only when drastic anatomical changes are observed in the on-board imaging (OBI) during RT. The other method, deformable image registration (DIR)-based ART (dART), tries to overcome the time-consuming aspect of cART.

Image registration can be categorized into rigid, affine, and deformable (DIR) methods. Among these, DIR involves a non-rigid spatial transformation that aligns corresponding points between two datasets. DIR fuses the precisely calibrated voxel data of the initial pCT, with the latest patient anatomy data from the cone beam CT (CBCT) to generate a synthetic CT (sCT). Contours can then be readily propagated from the pCT to the sCT, followed by manual corrections as required. Various software packages are available for executing DIR and dART. Velocity® (Velocity Advanced imaging, version 4.0, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA) is a solution that aims to perform dART and reduce the time oncologists spend on resimulation and recontouring15. In this study, we hypothesized that dART is comparable to cART with respect to target and OAR volumes, target coverage, and achieved OAR doses.

Methods

Patient selection and treatment planning

This is a single-institution, prospective observational, dosimetric study for post-operative patients with OCSCC treated with adjuvant RT or CTRT using VMAT between June 2022 and December 2023. The Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) approved this study vide letter number OIEC/11,000,577/2022/00001. All research methods in this study were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

All patients signed a voluntary informed consent form specific to this study. Eligible patients included individuals of either gender, aged 18 to 70 years, with SCC histology and adequately resected oral cavity primary and nodal disease, requiring adjuvant RT or CTRT. These patients underwent kilovolt (kV) CBCT examinations at regular intervals (the first three fractions, followed by three times per week) during RT in accordance with institutional protocol. The CBCT images of each patient were routinely analyzed for the study’s inclusion criteria by the treating radiation oncologist and a patient was enrolled if a > 5 mm gap in skin contour between the pCT and CBCT was found. Patients with a history of head and neck radiation, metastatic disease, or pregnancy or breastfeeding were excluded. Patients with gingivobuccal complex tumors were treated with bite composite resection, while those with tongue primaries had partial or near total glossectomy depending on the extent of disease, along with selective neck dissection. Pectoralis major myocutaneous flaps (PMMC) were used most commonly. The CT images for treatment planning were obtained on a CT Simulator (Siemens Healthiness GmbH − SOMATOM Confidence, 80 cm bore) with the head-first supine position using an appropriate neck rest, a four-clamp head and neck thermoplastic sheet to immobilize the patient’s head, neck and shoulder, and intravenous iohexol contrast (1 ml/kg body weight) with a 3 mm slice thickness. The scans were exported to the treatment planning system (TPS) (Eclipse Treatment Planning System, Version 15.6, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA) for contouring of the clinical target volume (CTV). High-risk CTV (CTVHR) included the post-operative cavity with potential areas of microscopic spread and involved lymph nodal stations. As per our institutional policy, surgical flaps involving the buccal mucosa extending inferiorly up to the level of the hyoid bone are routinely included within the high-risk clinical target volume (CTVHR). These flaps are not delineated as OARs, and no specific dose reduction is attempted in this region. However, in cases of pedicled flaps, the vascular pedicle is contoured separately, and efforts are made to limit the radiation dose to the pedicle to minimize the risk of flap-related complications. Uninvolved nodal regions with a 5% to 15% chance of lymph node involvement in the adjoining areas were included in the low-risk CTV (CTVLR) for prophylactic nodal irradiation. Isotropic expansion of 5 mm was given to the CTVHR and CTVLR to generate the respective high-risk planning target volume (PTVHR) and low-risk PTV (PTVLR) respectively. The CTV-to-PTV margin was kept the same for the sCT and resimulation CT (rCT) plans as well.

Inverse planning was performed for all patients to deliver VMAT using 6 MV photons, to a dose of 60 Gy (Gy) to the PTVHR and 54 Gy to PTVLR, each in 30 fractions at 2 Gy/fraction and 1.8 Gy/fraction, respectively. Planning was performed using the Eclipse TPS. One or two arcs were used in the planning, depending on the extent of the target volume. The objective was to cover at least 95% of the PTV volume with 95% of the prescribed dose with the maximum dose not exceeding 107%. Standard dose constraints were used for the OARs. Plan evaluation involved the use of DVHs and slice-by-slice evaluation of the dose distribution to the PTVs and the OARs. The evaluated OARs included the brain stem, spinal cord, parotid glands, mandible, larynx, eyes, lenses and optic nerves. Having adequately met the set goals, the plan was finalized and approved for treatment delivery on Varian TrueBeam 2.7 Linear Accelerator (Model SDC, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA).

Extended CBCT acquisition and DIR

The CBCT images were acquired using the Varian On-Board Imager (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA), with a 2 mm slice thickness. A half rotation of the full fan bowtie filter was used as per the head protocol. All enrolled patients had an extended CBCT while maintaining the treatment position. Extended CBCTs have longitudinal scan lengths of 34 cm, compared to 18 cm for regular CBCTs. This includes the whole PTV and a 2–5 cm cranio-caudal margin. For each patient, the pCT and extended CBCT were imported into Velocity® to perform DIR and sCT generation.

Initial rigid automatic match based on the bony anatomy was followed by a DIR which created a voxel-to-voxel deformation vector field (DVF) between the two image sets. The theoretical degree of freedom (DOF) is up to three times the number of voxels in the original dataset; in practice, however, DIR doesn’t allow arbitrary free motion. This reduces the effective DOF, but it still remains orders of magnitude higher than that of rigid/affine registration. The DIR algorithm used in Velocity® is based on B-spline algorithm, along with a regularization function to ensure anatomically correct and smooth deformation. DIR of the pCT to the extended CBCT was performed using the Velocity® Navigator ACTOR (Adaptive Calculation and Tracking for Offline Plan Review) algorithm. The computed DVF was subsequently applied for sCT generation and for contour propagation from the pCT to the sCT. The sCT therefore represents a geometrically updated CT dataset that retains the calibrated Hounsfield unit (HU) information of the pCT, while conforming to the patient anatomy captured on CBCT at the time of treatment. The sCT dataset was exported in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format to the TPS for manual verification of the deformably propagated contours, correction of target and OAR delineations where necessary, and subsequent treatment planning. The initial treatment plan, with dose calculated on the pCT, was superimposed onto the sCT using the DVF obtained from pCT-CBCT registration. The resultant dose distribution was compared with the original dose distribution on the pCT, in order to assess the actual dosimetric need for ART. The workflow has been illustrated in Fig. 1.

Re-simulation CT acquisition and treatment planning

When a significant gap in the CBCT was noted, patients enrolled in the ART study were resimulated, preferably on the same day as the extended CBCT, as seen in Fig. 2(A). OARs and PTVs were recontoured on rCT slices (cART). Like the initial plan, the PTVHR was prescribed 60 Gy and the PTVLR 54 Gy, each in 30 fractions. The final treatment plan was, however, scaled down to treat only the remaining fractions. Apart from treatment for the remaining fractions, the rCT images were used as the benchmark to compare with sCT images. The terms dART and sCT-based, and cART and rCT-based, were used interchangeably throughout the manuscript.

DIR accuracy assessment

The accuracy of DIR achieved was evaluated using the American Association of Physicists in Medicine (AAPM) Task Group (TG)−132 parameters, namely mean distance to agreement (MDA) and dice similarity coefficient (DSC). These are referred to as conformality and surface distance metrics, respectively, in Velocity®. MDA is the mean distance (in mm) between all the respective points on two different contours16. A value of zero indicates that the two contours are entirely matched (i.e., excellent registration accuracy) while poor matching or registration is indicated by larger values of MDA. Lower MDA is especially required in high-dose gradient regions in close proximity to the OARs and PTVs, for better assessment of the maximum dose to the OARs. DSC quantifies the spatial coincidence of two contours, represented as segments A and B, and is mathematically expressed as DSC(A, B) = 2(A∩B)/(A + B)17. A DSC value of zero implies no overlap, while values closer to 1.0 indicate greater overlap and better registration. When used together, MDA and DSC provide a more comprehensive assessment of image registration accuracy.

Objectives

The primary objective of the study was to compare the volumes and DVH parameters of targets and OARs between dART/sCT-based and cART/rCT-based planning. Secondary objectives include comparison of various DVH parameters of targets and OARs in the initial plan versus the same plan superimposed on the sCT achieved via DIR. Additionally, the difference in volumes of target volumes and OARs between the pCT and sCT was evaluated. For dose-volume parameter analysis, PTV doses D2%, D98%, V95% and Dmean (D2% and D98% representing the minimum dose received by 2% and 98% volumes of PTV respectively, V95% representing the volume receiving at least 95% of the prescribed dose and Dmean being the mean dose received by a volume) were obtained from sCT-, rCT- and pCT-based plans as well as the from the same plan superimposed on the sCT. Conformity index (CI) and heterogeneity index (HI) were evaluated for sCT- and rCT-based plans. Similarly, for OARs, the maximum dose (Dmax) was evaluated for the spinal cord, planning organ at risk volume (PRV) spinal cord, brainstem, both eyes, optic nerves, mandible, and PRV brainstem. The Dmean was evaluated for both parotid glands (accounting for the PTV) and the larynx. These parameters were calculated from the same plans as described above.

Statistical methods

Data were presented as frequencies, absolute numbers, mean, and standard deviation, or percentages. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS Version 26. The distribution of target volumes, OAR volumes and dosimetric OAR coverages was analyzed for normalcy using skewness and kurtosis score. For normally distributed parameters, a paired t-test was used to compare the changes in mean volumes and doses. All statistical analysis was carried out for two-tailed tests at a 5% level of significance and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Volumetric differences between pCT and sCT

The mean volumes and differences in volumes of PTVs and OARs between the pCT and sCT are presented in Table 2. All volumes had a statistically significant decrease in sCT, compared to pCT, except the PTVLR (non-significant decrease in volume) and the larynx (significant increase in volume). Out of all statistically significant differences, clinically significant differences (> 5%) were found for all structures except the larynx.

Differences in DVH parameters between the initial plan on pCT and the initial plan superimposed on sCT

The initial plan (initial gantry angles, energies, MUs, etc.) when superimposed on the new anatomy of the sCT showed a decrease in four out of six target DVH parameters. A statistically significant impact was observed only for PTVLR V95% and D98%, as shown in Table 3. Four out of five OARs received higher doses compared with the initial plan. Statistically significant changes were noticed only for the mandible. Other OARs, such as the spinal cord, received a 14.8% higher dose, yet this was statistically non-significant. Plan superimposition did not exceed respective OAR tolerances for any OAR except the larynx. The larynx Dmean exceeded the threshold of 44 Gy in nine patients (36%). Thus, overall, there would be target underdosing and OAR overdosing if patients with shrunken target and OAR volumes are treated using the same plan.

Volumetric differences between rCT and sCT

The mean volumes and differences in volumes of PTVs and OARs between sCT (dART) and rCT (cART) have been tabulated in Table 4. For all OAR volumes, the difference was statistically non-significant. For both the PTV volumes, the difference was statistically significant. For PTVHR, the difference in mean volumes was − 18.3 ± 32.6 cc, and for PTVLR, −15.2 ± 34.2 cc. A lesser extent of shrinkage of PTVHR and body contour in sCT as compared to rCT has also been demonstrated in Fig. 2(B-D). Only the difference in PTVLR was clinically significant.

Differences in DVH parameters between rCT (cART) and sCT (dART) based plans

As shown in Table 5, planning directly on the sCT resulted in significantly reduced target dose coverage for six out of eight parameters, compared to cART using the rCT. Mean V95% of both the target volumes fell below 95%. Fourteen patients had their PTVHR and PTVLR V95% values below the threshold of 95%. Median CI in sCT and rCT plans was 0.95 and 0.97 (p = 0.07), respectively, whereas the median HI was 0.08 and 0.06 (p = 0.11), respectively. The dose of only one out of five OARs (the spinal cord) increased significantly. However, the increase was only 2.6% in Dmax, well within tolerance limits. Three OARs- the parotids, mandible and larynx- had lower doses, with a statistically significant reduction in the larynx, in sCT-based plans. Thus, dART was shown to be relatively superior in terms of OAR dose reduction, albeit with a mild decrease in target coverage.

Discussion

Multiple studies have been done on the anatomical and dosimetric changes occurring during the course of RT in HNCs. The shrinkage in body contour is associated with a decrease in parotid volumes along with a superomedial shift, leading to overdosing of the parotids and skin, as well as other OARs18. Brouwer et al. estimated the average parotid overdose to be 2.2 ± 2.6 Gy19. Studies on overdosing of the brainstem or spinal cord have reported inconsistent results with some studies describing considerable increases in the Dmax, while others show no change20–22. Currently, there is no clear consensus on the overall dosimetric impact of these anatomical changes on target volumes. A limitation of most of these studies regarding dosimetric changes is the basic assumption that dose accumulation in different regions of the PTV and OARs remains located in the same region throughout RT, which is not always the case23.

ART offers a practical solution, namely, developing a new plan that reduces the dose to OARs, while avoiding underdosing of targets. Multiple trials have demonstrated the dosimetric benefit of using ART on target volumes as well as OARs, including decreasing the dose to parotids and hastening recovery from xerostomia23–26. An ongoing Phase III trial for HNSCC, the ReStart trial under Laskar et al., compares standard IMRT (with a 5 mm PTV margin) with adaptive IMRT (using a 3 mm PTV margin, with two planned adaptive replans at the 10th and 20th fractions) in terms of reduction in late salivary toxicity27. Statistically significant DFS benefit has been noted in patients receiving ART, compared to those not receiving ART despite significant weight loss28. To overcome the barriers associated with cART, faster recontouring methods have been proposed, including dART, deep learning-based and atlas-based segmentation29. The DIR method of ART relates the corresponding points between the pCT and CBCT to estimate the DVF and propagate contours from the pCT to the sCT30.

In our study, we observed significant reductions in the volumes of most target structures and OARs in the sCT, compared to the pCT, thereby reinforcing the need for ART. Specifically, the mean volumes of PTVHR, spinal cord and bilateral parotids decreased by 9.9%, 14.3% and 8.5% respectively. These changes were all statistically significant. PTVLR showed a non-significant decrease of 3.9%, while the larynx exhibited a small but statistically significant increase of 3.5% in volume (p = 0.04). On the other hand, most structures showed increased volumes when the sCT was compared with the rCT, except the larynx. The unexpected decrease in laryngeal volume on the sCT aligns with findings from previous studies, which suggest that DIR algorithms struggle to accurately process air-containing structures31,32. The increase in volumes was statistically significant only for the two target volumes, as depicted in Fig. 2(B). For example, PTVHR volume was higher in the sCT in 22 patients, and the discrepancy was more prominent for volumes greater than 450 cc. This highlights the limited ability of the DIR algorithm to perform high-volume deformations, especially at the cranio-caudal extremities of the volumes. At these locations, sudden and considerable changes in volumes were observed which have also been reported in earlier studies31,32. The study by Jamema et al. demonstrated limited precision of volume propagation in large-volume structures with potential inter- and intrafraction volume changes, such as the bladder and prostate31.

The necessity for ART was confirmed dosimetrically by superimposing the initial plan on the updated anatomy captured in the sCT, which revealed notable underdosing of target volumes in nearly all DVH parameters except D2%. Specifically, there was a non-significant decrease in mean PTVHR V95% and D98%, (p = 0.2 and p = 0.12, respectively), with a significant decrease in mean PTVLR V95% and D98%, (p = 0.001 and p = 0.01, respectively). Simultaneously, the dose received by several OARs was noted to increase. The mean Dmax for the spinal cord and the mean Dmean for the parotids and larynx increased by 14.8% (p = 0.65), 4.2% (p = 0.43) and 0.5% (p = 0.79), respectively. The mandible experienced a smaller but statistically significant increase (p = 0.007) in Dmax. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that if patients continue treatment with the original plan after significant anatomical changes, there is a risk of target underdosing and OAR overdosing. We also compared cART (using rCT) and dART (using sCT), which showed that dART is better for avoiding OAR overdose, but less reliable in maintaining adequate tumor dose coverage. This shortcoming can potentially be addressed by improving DIR accuracy and ensuring better patient setup reproducibility. The large variability introduced in PTV volumes by DIR was reflected in the significantly lower values of V95% and D98%, ranging from 3.2% to 4.3%. Another contributing factor is the difficulty in avoiding positional differences between the sCT and rCT, as adaptive scanning may entail changes in the patient positioning devices to accommodate the altered patient anatomy. Nevertheless, D2% and Dmean were less affected by these factors. A similar study also showed that PTV Dmean decreased by only 0.6% to 1% in sCT, which was much smaller than the change seen in other parameters32.

The accuracy of image registration was evaluated following the AAPM TG-132 guidelines by qualitative visual inspection of every slice and by using MDA and DSC for quantitative evaluation. According to TG-132, acceptable tolerances are 2–3 mm for MDA and 0.8–0.9 for DSC33. We used both parameters, as MDA is more indicative of surface alignment and DSC is a better predictor of overlap between registered image sets. Thus, a combination of both provides a robust assessment of the accuracy and precision of image registration process30,34. In our study, the registration accuracy for all our structures was optimal except for the spinal cord. The DSC was significantly lower for the spinal cord, likely resulting from the change in neck flexion and shoulder position between the pCT and extended CBCT. Suboptimal deformation in a few regions has been illustrated in Fig. 2(C) and (D). These factors had a pronounced impact on three patients, whose DSC dropped to 0.5 or lower. This discrepancy was reflected in the dosimetric analysis, where the spinal cord received a higher estimated dose in dART compared to cART. This was unlike other OARs, whose estimated dose in dART was lower. However, these measures provide only limited local information and do not capture the quality of the underlying deformation. For contour propagation, combined use of these metrics is generally adequate for improved quantification of registration accuracy, but manual verification by experienced clinicians remains essential to ensure contouring accuracy30,34. In all our cases, the extent of manual correction required was minimal and was needed only for gross deformations near the terminal slices of volumes and in slices with sudden, significant change in volume. No case had to be excluded due to poor performance of the DIR.

In terms of workflow efficiency, the initial implementation of dART required three to four days per patient. However, as the team became more familiar with the process, the median turnaround time improved to two days. In comparison, cART in our high-volume center typically took three to seven days for implementation, with a median of four days. This suggests that dART, once streamlined, can offer a time-efficient alternative to cART in clinical practice. Additionally, it prevents additional radiation exposure to the normal tissues by avoiding the need for a repeat CT.

Clinical monitoring of weight loss and radiological monitoring of neck shrinkage using CBCT are practical approaches for determining the optimal timing of ART35. The magnitude of weight loss has been correlated with the extent of reduction in PTVLR D136. Change in neck diameter and neck separation have also been shown to be good predictors for the need of ART36,37. DIR can also be helpful in this regard by estimating the ‘dose of the day’. Specifically, the DVF estimated between CBCT at a given fraction and the pCT can be used to deform the dose according to the anatomy and determine the actual dose received by each voxel during that fraction. Consequently, daily CBCT imaging can be utilized to estimate the cumulative dose delivered throughout treatment, a process known as dose accumulation34,38. However, we were unable to apply dose accumulation in our patient cohort due to workflow constraints in our busy clinical setup. Moreover, accurate dose accumulation requires highly precise voxel-level mapping. DIR accuracy ranging from < 1 mm to 10 mm has been reported, varying with disease site, image quality and DIR algorithm39,40. Our study focused solely on the contour propagation aspect of DIR.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has compared cART and dART, both geometrically and dosimetrically, specifically in postoperative OCSCC patients. A few studies have compared the need for ART in postoperative and definitive settings14,36,41. While most of these studies reported a greater need for ART in definitive RT/CTRT patients, some found the difference to be non-significant; suggesting that analyzing both patient groups together may not yield reliable conclusions. Our study has several strengths. In contrast to previous studies, our study utilized extended CBCT to avoid missing the anatomical changes occurring outside the field of view (FOV) of limited-field CBCT, potentially preventing inaccurate results42. We also ensured that extended CBCT and rCT were acquired on the same day to avoid anticipated anatomical changes and minimize positional variations. Nevertheless, some positional changes between rCT and extended CBCT were inevitable. In postoperative OCSCC patients with pronounced anatomical changes, these changes are likely attributable to changes in body habitus. This, coupled with variations in neck flexion and posterior cervical muscle tension- even on the same day- could contribute to minor mismatches. However, frequent CBCT and online correction with well-trained RT technologist, as practiced in our study, may reduce this day-to-day variation. Extended CBCT has its own limitations, including HU instability and increased noise, especially at the periphery. Velocity® is able to mitigate these issues to some extent by down-weighing or excluding such regions. DIR is focused on anatomically relevant volumes where CBCT quality is acceptable. To reduce inter-observer variability, the same clinician performed contouring on both the pCT and rCT images for each patient.

The limitations of the study include the small sample size, limited efficacy of the DIR algorithm and the inability to adequately account for the differences in patient setup between CBCT and rCT. Incorporation of clinical parameters such as weight loss or dosimetric evaluation through exit dosimetry–based dose accumulation could have provided additional insights for decision-making regarding ART. The lower proportion of females in the study (8%) should not be a limitation as our findings are less likely to be influenced by sex. Moreover, the proportion of females with OCSCC is considerably lower than that of males; hence, the results can reasonably be extrapolated to female patients. Our DIR algorithm produced suboptimal deformation for large-volume PTVs leading to reduced PTV dose coverage, which was exacerbated by nearly inevitable positional variations. Looking ahead, structure-guided DIR has been shown to be more accurate, especially for large-volume PTVs, and holds promise for improving dose adaptation43. Future researches can also incorporate clinical parameters like weight loss and dosimetric aspects like dose accumulation to define ART thresholds, along with more robust DIR algorithm, to validate dART and further explore these findings.

Conclusion

dART improves OAR sparing; however, its utility is limited in terms of maintaining target volume coverage for certain parameters. Therefore, despite being a faster alternative to cART, dART cannot yet replace cART until technological advances address the limitations. These findings are specific to Velocity® and to our methodology and workflow. In the interim, DIR can supplement cART by tracking the actual dose delivered per fraction, thereby guiding the optimal timing of ART.
