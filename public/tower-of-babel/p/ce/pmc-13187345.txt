Development of a web platform for predicting fall risk in cardiovascular patients using machine learning
Authors: Jiayang Dong, Xinyue Yang, Zhiqiang Zhang, Jiayi Sun, Wenjuan Zhang, Huihui Wang, Cuihua Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to develop and validate a machine learning-based fall risk prediction model for middle-aged and elderly individuals with cardiovascular disease. Data were sourced from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study. Key predictive variables were identified using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator regression. Six machine learning algorithms were employed to construct predictive models. Model performance was assessed through both internal and external validation, with SHapley Additive exPlanation values used to interpret the optimal model. A total of 1784 participants were analyzed, of which 434 (24.3%) experienced falls during a two-year follow-up period. Nine predictive factors were incorporated into the model, with the Light Gradient Boosting Machine model demonstrating the best performance, achieving areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.839 (95% CI 0.804–0.874) for internal validation and 0.816 (95% CI 0.798–0.833) for external validation. This model provides a scientific basis for personalized fall prevention strategies aimed at reducing fall incidence and enhancing the quality of life for patients with cardiovascular disease. Finally, we formed a web platform based on the best model to predict the probability of falls in cardiovascular patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43482-z.Keywords: Fall, Cardiovascular disease, Machine learning, Prediction modelSubject terms: Cardiology, Health care, Risk factors

Background

Globally, falls represent a major public health concern. It is estimated that approximately 684,000 fatal falls occur each year, making falls the second leading cause of unintentional injury deaths, following road traffic injuries. More than 80% of fall-related deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries, and adults aged 60 years and older experience the highest rates of fatal falls1. A fall can be defined as “an unintentional event where the participant comes to rest on the ground, floor, or lower level”2. About 28.7% of older adults self-report having at least one fall in the past 12 months, and among those who fall, 37.5% report that at least one fall resulted in medical attention or activity limitation3. Falls not only significantly diminish an individual’s quality of life, leading to physical injuries, psychological issues, and social isolation, but they also impose a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems4. Specifically, in the United States, the estimated medical costs associated with falls reached $50 billion in 2015, with medical expenditures related to fatal falls amounting to $754 million5. With the aging of society, the issue of falls is expected to worsen, necessitating effective preventive measures to reduce the incidence of falls, enhance the quality of life for older adults, and alleviate the pressure on healthcare systems.

Concurrently, the burden of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) is also increasing due to population aging and extended life expectancy6. It is projected that from 2025 to 2050, the prevalence of CVD will increase by 90%, with crude mortality rates rising by 73.4% and disability-adjusted life years increasing by 54.7%. By 2050, deaths attributed to CVD are expected to reach 35.6 million7. In middle-income countries, the standardized incidence rates of ischemic heart disease and stroke—major components of CVD—are estimated to be twice that of high-income countries, with the number of CVD-related deaths in middle-income countries nearly quadrupling that of high-income countries6. Previous research indicates that elevated levels of cardiac biomarkers, such as high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T and N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide, which reflect subclinical cardiac dysfunction, are independently associated with increased fall risk in older adults8. This suggests that underlying cardiac issues may affect balance and functionality in older adults before clinical symptoms become apparent. Following a fall, patients with CVD may experience significant physiological stress responses, leading to acute fluctuations in heart rate and blood pressure, which further exacerbate cardiac workload and deteriorate cardiac function. These patients often sustain multiple injuries from falls, such as fractures, concussions, or internal injuries, increasing the risk of complications like deep vein thrombosis, pneumonia, and infections, which further complicate their existing cardiovascular conditions9. If a fall necessitates surgical intervention (e.g., fracture repair), the risk of postoperative complications is heightened; the trauma from surgery and the effects of anesthesia can complicate the recovery process. Consequently, the survival rate for patients with CVD is significantly reduced, particularly among older patients who face greater risks. Therefore, establishing a fall risk prediction model for patients with CVD is crucial. This not only aids in improving patients’ quality of life but also provides essential guidance for healthcare delivery, facilitating the development of targeted interventions to reduce fall incidence and associated healthcare burdens.

In the past, logistic regression and Cox regression were commonly used to build predictive models, which offer high transparency and interpretability. However, due to their oversimplification of complex real-world relationships, they may fail to capture all interactions and nonlinear features adequately, resulting in suboptimal model performance10. In contrast, machine learning can effectively handle nonlinear relationships in clinical practice, but its complexity makes the results difficult to interpret11. In recent years, methods to enhance the interpretability of machine learning models have emerged. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values provide transparent explanations of model predictions by quantifying the contribution of each feature, making complex models easier to understand12,13. This study developed a predictive model for fall risk in individuals aged 45 and older with CVD, based on interpretable machine learning techniques, and also launched an open-access web application to improve the accuracy and efficiency of medical decision-making.

Methods

Data and participants

Our study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Survey (CHARLS), which aims to collect a high-quality dataset representative of households and individuals aged 45 and older in China. This survey primarily assesses the social, economic, and health conditions of community residents. It officially began in 2011, conducting questionnaire surveys across 150 counties and 450 communities (villages) in 28 provinces nationwide, with follow-up visits to the baseline sample conducted in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 202014.

Our study utilized the CHARLS dataset from 2013 to 2015 to establish a model and conduct internal validation, incorporating a total of 1784 participants. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 45 years; and (2) individuals diagnosed with CVD, which included stroke, heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart-related issues. Participants with missing fall data in 2015 were excluded from the study. The whole research process is shown in Fig. 1. The CHARLS study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015). All participants provided informed consent, and all methods were carried out following the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Research variable

Outcome variable and predictive variables

The occurrence of fall events was designated as the outcome variable in our study, assessed by asking participants in 2015, “Have you fallen down since your last visit?” Based on prior published literature and the accessibility of data from the CHARLS database, we included a total of 40 variables categorized into several key areas. Demographic factors included age, gender, education (classified as illiteracy, primary school, middle school, and high school and above), marital status (classified as married and living alone, with married individuals defined as those living with their spouse), current residence, and insurance. Lifestyle factors encompassed smoking (including current smokers and those who have quit), drinking (defined as consuming alcohol more than once a month), social activity, good sleep quality (rated as rarely, somewhat, moderately, and most), and sleep duration at night (indicating the actual time spent sleeping). Health conditions included relevant comorbidities such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, cancer, chronic lung diseases, liver disease, kidney disease, digestive disorders, psychiatric problems, memory-related disorders, rheumatism, and asthma. Additional health indicators included pain (referring to any bodily pain), hearing, vision (including myopia and hyperopia), and self-assessment of health, which were categorized as poor, fair, and good. Mental health aspects included life satisfaction (rated as satisfied, somewhat satisfied, or dissatisfied) and depression. Functional indicators consisted of fall history, hip fracture, activities of daily living (ADL) score, cognition, grip strength, disability (encompassing physical disabilities, mental retardation, vision problems, hearing issues, and speech impediments), and walking speed. Finally, physiological measurements covered waist circumference (WC), body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP).

Data collection

Data were collected by trained staff through structured questionnaire interviews. Depression was assessed using the Chinese version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D), which consists of 10 questions, each scored from 0 to 3, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 30. A score of 10 or above indicates the presence of depression, with higher scores reflecting more severe depressive symptoms15. The ADL score was used to evaluate individuals’ ability to perform daily self-care tasks, including dressing, bathing, eating, getting up, using the toilet, and managing bowel and bladder control. Responses were categorized as “no, I don’t have any difficulty,” “I have difficulty but can still do it,” “yes, I have difficulty and need help,” or “I can not do it.” Responses of “no, I don’t have any difficulty” and “I have difficulty but can still do it” were scored 1 point, while “yes, I have difficulty and need help” and “I can not do it” were scored 0 points. Cognitive function was assessed through a comprehensive evaluation of memory, time orientation, visual-spatial abilities, and attention, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 2116. Walking speed was measured by asking participants to walk a distance of 2.5 m back and forth twice, with the average time recorded for both trips. Grip strength was assessed using a dynamometer, measuring each hand’s grip strength twice, and the average of the four measurements was calculated.

Statistical analysis

Data pre-processing

In this study, a total of 40 variables were included. To address the issue of excessive missing data, we opted to exclude variables with more than 30% missing values. We also removed near-zero variance variables to enhance the model’s performance and stability. To assess multicollinearity among the remaining variables, we calculated the variance inflation factor (VIF) and correlation coefficients, setting the VIF threshold at 10 and the correlation coefficient threshold at 0.917. The processed data were divided into a training set (80%) and a test set (20%). The training set was used to train the machine learning model, while the test set was utilized to evaluate the model’s performance. Testing on unseen data provided insights into the model’s generalizability. Subsequently, we employed multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) to fill in the missing values for the remaining variables. MICE not only preserved the overall distribution characteristics of the data but also made the dataset more complete for subsequent analyses18. Outliers in the data were addressed using the winsorization method. We first calculated the 2nd percentile (P2) and the 98th percentile (P98) of the dataset, replacing all values below P2 with P2 and all values above P98 with P98. This approach of replacing outliers rather than deleting them allowed us to better retain the original characteristics of the data and improved the quality and reliability of the analysis results. Given the imbalance in the dataset between fallers and non-fallers, which could lead to model bias toward the majority class and impact classification performance, we applied the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) to address this class imbalance. SMOTE generates new synthetic samples based on the characteristics of existing samples to increase the representation of the minority class, thereby significantly enhancing the classification accuracy of the minority class and improving the overall performance of the classification model19. The data in this study did not follow a normal distribution; thus, continuous variables were presented as medians and interquartile ranges, and comparisons were made using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and proportions, with comparisons conducted using the chi-square test. The significance level was set at 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2.

Model construction and evaluation

Initially, univariate analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between each variable and the outcome variable, identifying those with significant differences between the faller and non-faller groups. The variables demonstrating significant differences were then selected for inclusion in the model using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. LASSO regression employs L1 regularization to compress the coefficients of unimportant variables to zero, thus allowing for the selection of the most critical variables associated with falls. The optimal λ value was determined through ten-fold cross-validation to ensure the model’s generalizability20. We constructed models using the training set, including random forest (RF), light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), logistic regression (LR), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), support vector machine (SVM), and deep neural network (DNN). Each of these models possesses unique characteristics and advantages. XGBoost utilizes an optimized gradient boosting framework that enables faster model training, particularly with large datasets, and has demonstrated exceptional performance in numerous machine learning competitions, showcasing strong predictive capabilities21. LR is known for its interpretability, computational efficiency, and adaptability, particularly in cases with smaller sample sizes or linearly separable data22. SVM effectively handles both linearly separable and non-linearly separable classification problems by employing various kernel functions (such as radial basis functions) to map the input space, thereby identifying an optimal decision boundary. It enhances model generalizability and flexibly manages classification errors through the introduction of a penalty parameter, ensuring stability in the presence of noisy data and outliers23. RF is an ensemble model composed of multiple tree-structured classifiers, where each tree relies on independently sampled random vectors, thereby enhancing overall classification capability. Additionally, features are randomly selected at each node split, which improves the model’s robustness to noise and performs well with high-dimensional data24. LightGBM is an ensemble learning method that constructs a powerful predictive model by combining multiple weak learners (e.g., decision trees), building new trees based on the prediction errors of the previous tree, and progressively optimizing the model’s predictive capabilities. It effectively handles extremely large structured datasets, offering high training speed and low memory consumption25. DNN consists of a complex network structure formed by multiple layers of neurons, with numerous adjustable parameters. Through optimization algorithms such as gradient descent, optimal model parameters can be identified, enhancing predictive performance26. For each machine learning algorithm, hyperparameter tuning was conducted using repeated fivefold cross-validation, during which multiple hyperparameter combinations were evaluated to identify the optimal configuration. We calculated accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), F1 score, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) to evaluate the predictive performance of each model. Furthermore, decision curve analysis (DCA) for different models was plotted to visually compare their performance at specific decision thresholds, aiding in the selection of the optimal model.

Model interpretation

Due to the black-box nature of machine learning models, there is often a low level of trust in these models. SHAP values provide an effective tool for illustrating the specific contribution of each feature to the predicted outcome, as well as the interactions between features, thereby significantly enhancing model transparency and trustworthiness. SHAP values not only offer local explanations for individual predictions but can also aggregate multiple predictions to conduct global feature importance analysis, thereby addressing varying levels of interpretative needs27.

External validation

We conducted external validation using the CHARLS dataset from 2011 to 2013, ensuring that the inclusion and exclusion criteria for this dataset were consistent with those used for model development and validation. After excluding individuals with missing values for the variables used in model development, a total of 1,800 participants were included in the study. We evaluated the predictive performance of the six machine learning models using metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, F1 score, AUC, and AUPRC. Additionally, the DCA will be employed to assess the clinical utility of the models. This study aims to leverage data from different time periods to encompass a broader range of population characteristics and health conditions, thereby evaluating the generalizability and robustness of the models.

Development of the web application

Based on the optimal predictive model, we developed a web application that allows users to input specific values of individual predictive factors to directly generate the probability of a fall. This application is primarily built using the “shinydashboard” R package, providing a convenient tool for clinical applications to assist healthcare professionals in quickly assessing fall risk.

Results

Data processing results

Ultimately, our study included a total of 1784 participants, with falls accounting for 24.3% of the sample. After excluding variables with more than 30% missing values (disability and walking speed) and those with near-zero variance (insurance, cancer, psychiatric problems, memory-related disorders, and hip fracture), a total of 33 variables were included in the analysis. Notably, higher proportions of individuals in the faller group were characterized by older age, female gender, low educational levels, rural residency, non-smoking status, poor sleep quality, kidney disease, rheumatism, poor self-assessment of health, poor hearing, poor vision, pain, dissatisfaction with life, depression, fall history, lower cognitive levels, and reduced grip strength (Table 1). Moreover, the differences in these characteristics between the two groups were statistically significant (P < 0.05). We partitioned the data into a test set and a training set in a 2:8 ratio. Due to the imbalance in the proportions of fallers and non-fallers within each dataset, we applied the SMOTE to achieve balance. The training set ultimately comprised 2436 individuals, while the test set included 602 individuals, with falls representing 42.9% of both sets. Furthermore, there were no significant statistical differences in the majority of clinical characteristics between the training set and test set (Table S1).

Feature selection

Through LASSO regression, we generated two dashed lines; one represents the optimal lambda value selected based on the minimum binomial deviance, while the other indicates the minimum binomial deviance within one standard error (Fig. 2). We opted for the latter, as it incorporates a more stringent penalization criterion, resulting in fewer included variables and enhancing the model’s simplicity and generalizability. Ultimately, we included nine variables: age, education, good sleep quality, kidney disease, rheumatism, pain, life satisfaction, depression, and fall history. Among these, age is a continuous variable, while kidney disease, rheumatism, pain, depression, and fall history are binary variables, coded as 0 for absence and 1 for presence. Education, good sleep quality, and life satisfaction are treated as ordinal categorical variables. Education is represented by numerical values from 1 to 4, corresponding to illiteracy, primary school, middle school, and high school and above, indicating increasing educational attainment. Good sleep quality is coded from 1 to 4, representing rarely, somewhat, moderately, and most, reflecting progressively better sleep quality. Life satisfaction is denoted by values of 1–3 for dissatisfied, somewhat satisfied, and satisfied, indicating an increasing level of life satisfaction.

At the optimal Lambda value, the features with non-zero coefficients are produced. Model evaluation and comparison

Based on the variables selected by LASSO, we constructed six machine learning models to predict fall risk in patients with CVD. The models were trained using the training set, and upon completion of training, we evaluated them using the test set, calculating various performance metrics to assess their effectiveness in real-world application scenarios. The AUC values in the training set were 0.774 for RF, 0.950 for LightGBM, 0.723 for LR, 0.923 for XGBoost, 0.754 for SVM, and 0.763 for DNN. In the test set, the AUC values were 0.649 for RF, 0.839 for LightGBM, 0.617 for LR, 0.837 for XGBoost, 0.641 for SVM, and 0.627 for DNN (Fig. 3). Among these, the LightGBM model exhibited the highest AUC value in the test set, with its accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and F1 score significantly surpassing those of the other models, recorded at 0.804, 0.709, 0.875, 0.810, 0.801, and 0.756, respectively. Moreover, it also achieved the highest AUPRC value of 0.862, further demonstrating its superior performance (Table 2). In addition to relying on these traditional metrics for evaluation, we also plotted DCA curves, which illustrate the net benefits of different models at various threshold probabilities. This provides a more intuitive and practical method for assessing model performance in real-world applications, taking into account the associated risks and benefits. The “none” curve typically represents the net benefit of making no predictions for patients, while the “all” curve indicates the net benefit of predicting all patients as positive. In the training set, all models demonstrated net benefits greater than those associated with not making any predictions or predicting all patients as positive. In the test set, the LightGBM and XGBoost models showed significantly higher net benefits than the other models across most threshold probabilities (Fig. 4).

Model interpretation

We employed SHAP to provide both global and local explanations for the optimal machine learning model, LightGBM. The importance feature plot indicates that the most significant predictors of fall risk are age and fall history, while life satisfaction and depression have a minimal impact (Fig. 5a). The overall SHAP summary plot displays SHAP values on the x-axis and various predictor variables on the y-axis, with colors transitioning from purple to yellow representing increasing values of predictor factors. The plot reveals that fall history, kidney disease, rheumatism, depression, and pain are associated with an increased incidence of falls in patients with CVD. Conversely, higher education levels, better sleep quality, and greater life satisfaction are linked to a reduced risk of falls (Fig. 5b). In the SHAP force plot, yellow indicates predictor factors that promote falls in cardiovascular patients, while purple represents factors that reduce the likelihood of falls. The length of the arrows signifies the magnitude of each predictor’s influence. Figure 6a presents predictions for fallers, while Fig. 6b depicts predictions for non-fallers. Figures S1 and S2 are SHAP dependence plots. Figure S1 illustrates that somewhat good sleep quality, dissatisfaction with life, and education levels at or below primary school significantly increase the fall risk in cardiovascular patients. Additionally, cardiovascular patients aged 55–75 exhibit an elevated risk of falling. Figure S2 indicates that among patients with rarely good sleep quality or pain, there is a higher prevalence of depression.

External validation

Using the CHARLS dataset from 2011 to 2013 for external validation, we compared the baseline characteristics of the external validation cohort with those of the model development cohort (Table S2). Among the nine variables included in our model, six-age, rheumatism, pain, life satisfaction, depression, and fall history-demonstrated statistically significant differences between the two baseline datasets (p ≤ 0.05). This ensures that the model remains valid across different populations and time points. When predicting fall risk in the external validation dataset using the six machine learning models, the LightGBM model exhibited the best predictive performance, achieving an AUC of 0.816 (95% CI 0.798–0.833) (see Supplementary Fig. S3a online). Its AUPRC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and F1 score were 0.826 (95% CI 0.807–0.843), 0.780, 0.656, 0.871, 0.793, 0.772, and 0.719, respectively. Additionally, we plotted DCA curves for the six machine learning models, revealing that the LightGBM model yielded the highest net benefit across most threshold probabilities (see Supplementary Fig. S3b online).

Development of the web application

Based on the LightGBM model, we developed a web application that categorizes the included predictive factors into four sections: demographic factors, lifestyle factors, health conditions, and functional factors. Users can click on each section, input specific values for individual predictive factors, and then click the “Predict” button to obtain the probability of a fall. The web application can be accessed via the following link: https://djyzsr.shinyapps.io/FallApp/ (see Supplementary Fig. S4 online).

Discussion

We utilized the CHARLS public database to predict fall risk among middle-aged and older adults aged 45 and above with CVD in the Chinese population. We employed univariate analysis and LASSO regression to select nine predictor factors and trained six machine learning algorithms to validate the model. The test set was used to evaluate the performance of each model, with the LightGBM model demonstrating the best predictive performance. We applied SHAP to interpret the optimal machine learning model, facilitating a deeper understanding for clinicians. Additionally, we conducted temporal external validation of the model using the CHARLS dataset from 2011 to 2013 to assess its generalizability.

A meta-analysis comprising 183 studies found a significant positive correlation between common CVD and falls among adults aged 50 and older, particularly for conditions such as stroke, peripheral artery disease, atrial fibrillation, and orthostatic hypotension28. Additionally, Aisling et al. analyzed a large dataset from the Danish national administrative registry, focusing on the risk of falls and mortality within three years following emergency department visits among individuals aged 65 and older29. Their research indicated that, during a three-year follow-up, older adults who presented for care due to falls had twice the risk of subsequent falls compared to those seeking care for other reasons. While CVD did not directly predict the occurrence of falls, it did significantly increase the risk of mortality during the three-year follow-up. Therefore, accurately predicting fall risk in patients with CVD is particularly important. However, research in this area remains scarce, especially regarding analyses based on machine learning algorithms. To address this gap, the present study employed machine learning methods to predict fall risk among middle-aged and older adults aged 45 and above with CVD using data from the CHARLS database. This approach not only provides new insights into the relationship between CVD and falls but also offers critical evidence for risk assessment and intervention strategies in clinical practice.

The results of this study indicate that age and fall history are the most significant predictors of falls among patients with CVD, consistent with prior research30. Age is often indicative of frailty, accompanied by a multitude of comorbidities and cognitive decline, which increases susceptibility to falls31. However, our study showed that CVD patients aged 55–75 years were at a higher risk of falls. Aisling et al. also showed that among older adults who visited the emergency department at baseline for falls due to hip fractures, the risk of falls within 3 years was highest in the 65–74 age group. Considering that it could be that older people with CVD are less active and more cautious32, This suggests a need for further exploration of the non-linear relationship between age and fall risk in patients with CVD. While a history of falls may lead individuals to be more cautious during activities, it may also reflect deficiencies in balance, coordination, and muscle strength, along with the potential presence of multiple health issues such as chronic diseases, cognitive impairments, or visual disturbances. Moreover, individuals who have experienced falls may develop a fear of falling, resulting in reduced activity levels, which can lead to muscle atrophy and further deterioration of physical condition, thereby increasing the risk of subsequent falls33. Therefore, a comprehensive assessment of fall history in conjunction with relevant health issues is crucial for developing personalized intervention strategies.

Furthermore, the impact of nighttime sleep quality on fall risk warrants attention. Poor sleep quality can lead to fluctuations in blood pressure during the night and early morning, which may result in dizziness or loss of balance upon rising. Additionally, inadequate sleep quality can contribute to cognitive decline and impaired muscle health, as well as excessive daytime sleepiness, all of which can adversely affect alertness and balance in older adults, thereby increasing the risk of falls34. Interestingly, the somewhat good sleep quality had the highest fall risk, even higher than the rarely good sleep quality. One possible reason is that individuals with consistently poor sleep may become more cautious or adopt protective strategies35. In contrast, those with somewhat good sleep quality might carry on with normal activity levels, despite impairments in balance and attention. Moreover, subjective sleep quality does not always reflect true sleep patterns or functional capacity. It is also possible that the somewhat good sleep quality had a higher proportion of individuals with other fall risk factors (e.g., older age, higher waist circumference), underscoring the need to consider interactions among multiple variables in fall risk studies36. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) also elevates the risk of falls among patients with CVD. This is primarily due to CKD’s association with systemic complications such as muscle atrophy, decreased physical strength, and cognitive impairment37. Furthermore, CKD patients often face issues like renal osteodystrophy, which compromises bone health and increases the likelihood of fractures during falls38. CKD may also lead to electrolyte imbalances and anemia, further heightening the fall risk in individuals with CVD39. Rheumatic diseases are another factor that can increase fall risk in patients with CVD. Patients suffering from rheumatic conditions frequently experience joint pain and stiffness; some may even have vision impairments, all of which can adversely affect mobility and stability, increasing the likelihood of falls40. Pain is also a significant contributor to fall risk. Yao et al. examined the relationship between pain and fall outcomes using a multivariable logistic regression model in a cohort of 5340 middle-aged individuals (aged 45–65) residing in South Korea. The study found a positive correlation between baseline pain and the risk of fall-related injuries and injurious falls during follow-up, particularly among participants with ankle and toe pain41. Acute pain may lead to fluctuations in heart rate and blood pressure, increasing cardiac workload, while chronic pain can trigger persistent inflammation and stress responses that affect gait and mobility, leading to instability and an increased likelihood of falls42. Moreover, fear of pain may result in reduced physical activity, further compromising muscle strength and coordination43. Higher educational levels are generally associated with greater health and safety awareness, and better income levels can facilitate access to resources that may mitigate fall risk. Conversely, lower life satisfaction correlates with an increased risk of falls. A study conducted in southern Sweden assessed baseline fall risk and performed follow-up evaluations six years later among participants aged 60–93. The results indicated that fallers had significantly lower life satisfaction scores compared to non-fallers44. Additionally, a longitudinal cohort study involving older adults aged 40 and above in Germany found significant associations between falls and higher levels of negative emotions, lower levels of positive emotions, and diminished life satisfaction45. Patients with depression and CVD exhibit an elevated risk of falls, with severe depression manifesting as psychomotor retardation, slowed gait and cognitive processing speed, low energy, and reduced activity levels, all of which may contribute to fall incidents46. Furthermore, the use of antidepressants and sedatives has been significantly associated with falls and fractures in the elderly population47.

The variables included in our fall risk prediction model for patients with CVD demonstrate significant clinical relevance and predictive capability. These variables encompass not only physiological factors but also psychological and social factors, creating a comprehensive risk assessment framework. Among them, age, education, fall history, pain, and life satisfaction can be directly obtained through patient history inquiries. The presence of kidney disease and rheumatism can be assessed either through direct questioning or relevant laboratory tests and examinations. Depression can be evaluated using the CES-D. The simplicity and ease of assessment of these variables enhance the clinical utility and effectiveness of the prediction model. Beyond the predictors included in the current model, future studies could incorporate both comprehensive fall risk indices and objective functional assessments to further enhance predictive performance. For instance, the Silver Index provides an integrated evaluation of fall risk, while performance-based tests such as the Timed Up and Go test, Berg Balance Scale, and Sit-to-Stand test objectively assess dynamic balance, mobility, and lower-limb strength48,49. Combining these measures may provide complementary information to demographic and clinical variables, improving both the predictive accuracy and clinical applicability of fall risk models in patients with CVD.

In predicting fall risk among patients with CVD, the LightGBM model demonstrated significant advantages. The model achieved an AUC of 0.839 (95% CI 0.804–0.874) on the test set, reflecting its exceptional classification ability. Additionally, the model performed excellently across multiple key metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity and so on. In an external validation dataset, although the AUC slightly decreased to 0.816 (95% CI 0.798–0.833), LightGBM maintained optimal predictive performance. These results indicate that the LightGBM model possesses robust generalization capabilities and stability, effectively predicting fall risk in CVD patients across different datasets, thereby providing a reliable tool for clinical application. Furthermore, through the DCA of six machine learning models, LightGBM consistently yielded the highest net benefits across most thresholds in both the test set and external validation dataset, further substantiating its superiority in practical applications.

In this study, we utilized SHAP values to provide in-depth global and local explanations for the optimal machine learning model, LightGBM, demonstrating its advantages in predicting fall risk among patients with CVD. The importance feature plot ranks the nine variables based on SHAP values, revealing that age and fall history are the most significant predictors, while the impacts of life satisfaction and depressive symptoms are relatively minor. This finding offers critical guidance for clinical interventions. The overall SHAP summary plot illustrates how the predictor factors influence fall risk, aiding clinicians in better understanding the risk factors associated with CVD patients. The SHAP force plot provides explanations for individual patient predictions. For instance, despite a patient experiencing pain, depression, rheumatism, poor sleep quality, and a low education level, their age of 63 and absence of fall history significantly lower their fall risk. This underscores the importance of age and fall history in risk prediction and highlights the necessity of personalized assessments. The SHAP dependence plot not only reflects the impact of individual predictor factors on model outcomes but also analyzes potential associations among these predictors. We found that CVD patients aged 55–75 have a significantly elevated risk of falling. Furthermore, improving sleep quality, treating kidney and rheumatic diseases, alleviating pain and depressive symptoms, and enhancing life satisfaction can effectively reduce fall risk. In summary, the application of SHAP values enhances the understanding of the fall risk prediction model for patients with CVD, increases model transparency, provides a scientific basis for clinical applications, and supports the development of more effective risk management strategies.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to develop a web-based tool for predicting fall risk in middle-aged and older adults with CVD in China using machine learning. Unlike previous studies that focused on general older populations32, our model targets a high-risk group—individuals with CVD—and integrates easily accessible physiological, psychological, and social variables, enhancing its practicality. The best-performing model achieved an AUC of 0.839 in the test set, indicating strong predictive performance. To improve interpretability, we used SHAP to explain the contribution of each predictor. Finally, we deployed the model as an easy-to-use online platform, offering a practical alternative to traditional assessments and supporting timely, personalized fall prevention strategies.

Our study also has several limitations. First, we were unable to encompass all potential risk factors, such as the omission of laboratory test results, including HbA1c, serum creatinine, lipid profile, and relevant hormones (e.g., thyroid hormones, vitamin D)50,51. These markers reflect glycemic, renal, cardiovascular, and endocrine status, all of which can influence fall risk. Future research should incorporate these variables to enhance the predictive efficacy of the model. Second, the definition of falls was based on self-reported events occurring between consecutive survey waves, which may have introduced recall bias and been influenced by subjective interpretation or social desirability effects. In addition, variations in follow-up intervals among participants may have affected the temporal accuracy and clinical interpretability of the recorded fall events. Finally, while we performed temporal external validation of the model, three variables—education, kidney disease, and good sleep quality—did not show significant differences between the derivation and external validation cohorts, which may limit the model’s generalizability. Therefore, future studies should aim to validate the model in more diverse populations across different geographic regions and settings.

Conclusions

This study developed a fall risk prediction model for middle-aged and older adults with CVD based on data from the CHARLS. Utilizing interpretable machine learning methods, we identified key factors influencing fall risk, with the LightGBM model demonstrating exceptional performance across various metrics. External validation results further confirmed the model’s generalizability and stability, highlighting its potential for clinical application. This predictive model aids in formulating personalized fall prevention strategies, thereby reducing the incidence of falls and improving the quality of life for patients.
