Precision mitochondrial delivery for tissue repair in intervertebral disc degeneration
Authors: Jian-Bin Guan, Ren-Ji Wang, Si-Bo Wang, Ling-Jiang Li, Ying-Guang Wang, Shan-Xi Wang, Xiao-Fan Bai, Rong-Jin Luo, Xiao-Jun Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aims to develop a pathological microenvironment-responsive hydrogel system leveraging AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activation and mitochondrial delivery to regulate mitochondrial function, mitigate oxidative stress, and inhibit ferroptosis in nucleus pulposus cells (NPCs) for microenvironmental remodeling and tissue repair in intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD). Bulk RNA Sequencing (RNA-seq) on nucleus pulposus (NP) tissues from Sham and IVDD rats, intersected with tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP)-treated NPCs, identified key regulatory pathways. Treatment with AMPK modulators, A-769,662 and EX229, systematically evaluated AMPK’s regulatory impact on mitochondrial function and ferroptosis. FoxO3a interference via small hairpin RNA, coupled with molecular analyses, elucidated downstream targets and mechanisms. Mitochondria-derived microvesicles loaded with A-769,662, encapsulated in phenylboronic acid-modified gelatin hydrogel (GPVA), demonstrated enhanced mitochondrial function and efficient drug release behavior. In IVDD animal models, GPVA hydrogels significantly improved tissue structure, mitochondrial function, and mitigated oxidative stress and ferroptosis, with FoxO3a knockdown confirming the significance of AMPK-FoxO3a axis. This novel ROS/pH-responsive injectable hydrogel system offers precision delivery of mitochondrial therapy, effectively enhancing NPC function, promoting tissue repair, and showing promising translational potential for IVDD treatment.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04605-w.

Introduction

Intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) is the principal pathological basis of lumbar disc herniation, spinal stenosis, and chronic low back pain, and represents one of the leading causes of disability worldwide [1, 2]. The intervertebral disc consists of the nucleus pulposus (NP), annulus fibrosus, and cartilage endplates. Among these components, nucleus pulposus cells (NPCs) are essential for maintaining disc structure and function through regulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) metabolism [3, 4]. Under pathological conditions such as aging, mechanical overload, and chronic inflammation, NPCs frequently develop mitochondrial dysfunction and redox imbalance, resulting in excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis [5, 6]. Ferroptosis is a regulated form of cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, with the SLC7A11–GSH–GPX4 axis serving as its core protective machinery [6, 7]. Current clinical treatments mainly focus on mechanical correction or symptomatic relief and are unable to reverse disc degeneration at the level of cellular metabolism and homeostasis. Therefore, reconstructing mitochondrial homeostasis and suppressing ferroptosis have emerged as important therapeutic directions for IVDD.

Importantly, ferroptosis is not merely a consequence of oxidative injury, but a tightly regulated form of cell death, with the solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11)-glutathione (GSH)-glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) axis serving as its core defense system [8–10]. In the context of IVDD, ferroptotic activation in NPCs is closely associated with ECM degradation and degeneration progression, suggesting that ferroptosis may represent a critical and actionable pathological node [6, 11]. Mechanistically, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) can regulate the transcriptional activity of Forkhead box O3a (FoxO3a) and is functionally coupled to the SLC7A11/GPX4-associated regulatory network, indicating that the AMPK-FoxO3a axis may serve as a mechanistic bridge linking energy homeostasis to anti-ferroptotic regulation [12, 13].

Mitochondria are key organelles responsible for cellular energy metabolism and redox balance, and their function is tightly controlled by the energy sensor AMPK [14, 15]. AMPK phosphorylates FoxO3a and activates antioxidant- and autophagy-related genes, including superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2) and microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 beta (LC3B), which promote the clearance of damaged mitochondria and sustain metabolic homeostasis [16]. The AMPK-FoxO3a axis has been shown to play an important role in alleviating oxidative stress, preserving mitochondrial integrity, and inhibiting cellular senescence; however, its specific regulatory role in the pathological progression of IVDD remains insufficiently understood [17]. In addition, direct administration of AMPK agonists is limited by poor targeting and short duration of efficacy, particularly within the avascular and nutrient-deficient microenvironment of the intervertebral disc [18, 19]. Therefore, developing delivery systems capable of targeted and microenvironment-responsive release of AMPK activators has become a key research focus.

With the advancement of biomaterials science, injectable hydrogels have emerged as ideal carriers for tissue repair and drug delivery due to their excellent biocompatibility, moldability, and biodegradability [20, 21]. Recently, pathological microenvironment-responsive hydrogels that can sense local pH, ROS, or inflammatory cues and achieve on-demand release have attracted significant attention [20, 22]. Nevertheless, single-drug systems often fail to simultaneously achieve metabolic restoration and oxidative stress attenuation, thereby limiting their overall therapeutic efficacy [23, 24]. Therefore, constructing a multidimensional hydrogel system with dual ROS/pH responsiveness and mitochondrial functional delivery capacity may provide a promising strategy for remodeling the degenerative microenvironment and restoring tissue function at both molecular and cellular levels.

Mesenchymal stem cell-derived mitochondrial extracellular vesicles are capable of carrying functional mitochondria and metabolic enzyme complexes, thereby restoring bioenergetic metabolism and enhancing antioxidant capacity [25, 26]. Although previous studies have independently supported the involvement of mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis in IVDD progression, an integrated intervention combining mitochondrial functional compensation with metabolic signaling activation remains insufficiently validated. In particular, the direct relationship between the AMPK-FoxO3a axis and ferroptotic effector molecules remains to be clarified. In the present study, a ROS/pH-responsive injectable hydrogel delivery system, termed GPVA, was constructed for the local administration of A-769,662-loaded mitochondrial microvesicles (mitoMVs@A), with the aim of achieving controlled release and multilayered intervention within the degenerative microenvironment. We hypothesized that GPVA-mediated delivery of mitoMVs@A could activate AMPK-FoxO3a signaling, regulate ferroptosis-related molecules such as SLC7A11, GPX4, and acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), thereby improving mitochondrial homeostasis in NPCs and attenuating IVDD progression. To test this hypothesis, transcriptomic screening, material characterization, in vitro and in vivo experiments, and FoxO3a interference assays were performed to systematically evaluate the effects of this system on mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, ferroptosis, and tissue repair, as well as to elucidate its underlying molecular mechanisms. This study aimed to provide a locally deliverable, pathology-responsive, and mechanistically validated therapeutic strategy for IVDD.

Materials and methods

Establishment of animal model and tissue collection

To obtain transcriptomic data related to IVDD, eight-week-old male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (250–280 g, strain 101, Crl: CD(SD) IGS, purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd.) were randomly divided into the Sham and IVDD groups. The IVDD model was established by inserting a 21-gauge needle approximately 5 mm into the Co7/8 coccygeal disc and maintaining the puncture for 30 s before withdrawal. In the Sham group, the disc was exposed without puncture. On postoperative day 28, NP tissues were harvested and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA extraction and subsequent analyses [27]. The experiments strictly followed the institutional guidelines and regulations approved by the Ethics Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University and complied with the International Guidelines for Animal Experimentation.

Isolation and treatment of primary NPCs

Male SD rats weighing 150–200 g (strain 101, Crl: CD(SD) IGS; Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., China) were euthanized with an overdose of pentobarbital sodium. Under sterile conditions, lumbar vertebrae (L1-L6) were excised and intervertebral discs were isolated. Gelatinous NP tissue was carefully dissected under a stereomicroscope and digested in 0.1% collagenase (C6885, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 2 U/mL hyaluronidase (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 37 °C for 4 h. The digested tissue was transferred into high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; 11965092, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; 10099141, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (15140122, Gibco, USA), and cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Medium was replaced every 2 days. When cells reached 80–90% confluence, they were passaged using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (25200056, Gibco, USA). Passage 2 NPCs were used in all experiments, and six independent isolations were performed in total [27].

NPCs were treated with 100 µM tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP, 416665, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 24 h to induce oxidative stress [28], followed by co-treatment with the AMPK inhibitor EX229 (50 µM, HY-112769, MedChemExpress, USA) [29] or the AMPK activator A-769,662 (200 µM, HY-50662, MedChemExpress, USA) [30] for another 24 h. Four groups were established: Control, TBHP, TBHP + EX229, and TBHP + A-769,662.

Bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and analysis

Total RNA was extracted from NP tissues of Sham and IVDD rats (n = 5 independent animals/group) and from NPCs in the Control and TBHP groups (n = 3 independent samples/group) using TRIzol reagent (15596026, Invitrogen, USA). RNA purity and integrity were assessed by A260/A280 ratio and RNA integrity number (RIN > 8). Qualified samples were submitted to OE Biotech for sequencing and analysis. Libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in paired-end 150-bp mode, generating at least 40 million high-quality reads per sample.

Raw reads were filtered using fastp (v0.23.2) to remove adapters and low-quality sequences. Clean reads were aligned to the rat reference genome (Rnor_6.0, Ensembl release 108) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1), and gene counts were generated with FeatureCounts (v2.0.1). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (v1.38.3) with thresholds of |log2FoldChange| ≥ 1 and adjusted p < 0.05.

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using clusterProfiler (v4.6.2) with org.Rn.eg.db (v3.17.0) as the annotation database. P values were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, and adjusted p < 0.05 was considered significant. Enrichment plots were generated using ggplot2 (v3.4.4) and enrichplot (v1.20.0).

Small hairpin RNA (shRNA)-mediated FoxO3a knockdown and transfection

To silence FoxO3a, two shRNA sequences were synthesized by GenePharma (China): sh-FoxO3a-1, TCTCCCGCTAGTGTCTTTAAA; sh-FoxO3a-2, CCGACCTGTTGGAAGATTTAT. A non-targeting sequence (sh-NC: TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT) was used as the negative control. NPCs were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected at approximately 70% confluence using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000150, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at a DNA-to-reagent ratio of 1:3. Transfection was performed in antibiotic-free DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS. After 48 h, cells were collected for reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) and Western blot (WB) to evaluate knockdown efficiency.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (15596026, Invitrogen, USA). Reverse transcription was performed with PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan), and quantitative PCR was conducted using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, USA) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Primer sequences are listed in Table S1. GAPDH was used as the internal control, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

ChIP-qPCR

A ChIP Assay Kit (P2078, Beyotime, China) was used to assess FoxO3a enrichment at the SLC7A11 promoter in NPCs. Following treatment, cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde, lysed, and sonicated to generate chromatin fragments of approximately 500 bp. Equal amounts of chromatin were incubated overnight with anti-FoxO3a antibody (PA1-805, Invitrogen) or isotype IgG as the negative control. Immune complexes were captured using Protein A-Sepharose beads, and the precipitated DNA was purified and analyzed by qPCR. Primer sequences targeting the SLC7A11 promoter are listed in Table S1.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from rat NP tissues or cultured NPCs using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China) containing protease inhibitors (Roche, Switzerland). Protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg/lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). After blocking, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Table S2), followed by horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ab6721, Abcam, UK) or goat anti-mouse IgG (ab6789, Abcam, UK) for 1 h at room temperature. Bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and imaged with the ChemiDoc MP system (Bio-Rad, USA). Band intensity was quantified using ImageJ. Each experiment was independently repeated three times.

Establishment of the MSC-mitoMVs co-culture system

Five experimental groups were established: Control group (no treatment), MSCs group (TBHP-treated NPCs co-cultured with MSCs), MSCs + Rotenone group (co-culture with mitochondrial complex I inhibitor), mitoMVs group (TBHP-treated NPCs incubated with isolated mitoMVs), and mitoMVs + Rotenone group (mitoMVs pretreated with Rotenone before incubation).

In the MSCs group, NPCs in the lower chamber of a Transwell system were exposed to 100 µM TBHP for 12 h, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and then co-cultured with mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs; 5 × 105 cells/well) seeded in the upper chamber fitted with 0.4 μm inserts (Corning, USA). Co-culture was maintained in complete medium at 37 °C for 48 h. In the MSCs + Rotenone group, MSCs were pretreated with 1 µM Rotenone for 2 h, washed, and then co-cultured with TBHP-treated NPCs for 48 h. In the mitoMVs group, TBHP-treated NPCs were incubated with isolated mitoMVs resuspended in PBS at 3 × 108 particles/mL for 48 h. In the mitoMVs + Rotenone group, mitoMVs were pretreated with 1 µM Rotenone for 2 h before incubation with NPCs. All mitoMVs preparations were sterilized through a 0.22 μm membrane and used within 48 h [31].

Isolation and purification of mitoMVs

MSCs were cultured in T175 flasks containing α-MEM supplemented with 10% exosome-depleted FBS (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin until 80% confluence. After medium replacement, cells were cultured for another 24 h. Conditioned medium was collected and subjected to sequential centrifugation at 4 °C: 300 × g for 10 min to remove cells, 2,000 × g for 20 min to remove debris, and 10,000 × g for 30 min to eliminate large vesicles. The supernatant was then ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 70 min using a Beckman Optima L-100 K ultracentrifuge (USA). The pellet was resuspended in PBS, washed once by repeat ultracentrifugation under the same conditions, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and adjusted to 3 × 108 particles/mL for subsequent experiments [31].

Mitochondrial and membrane labeling of MSCs

To verify the presence of mitochondrial components in MSC-derived microvesicles, MSCs were dual-labeled with MitoTracker Red CMXRos and DiO. MSCs cultured in T75 flasks were incubated with 200 nM MitoTracker Red CMXRos (M7512, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37 °C for 30 min to label mitochondria. After three PBS washes, cells were incubated with 5 µM DiO membrane dye (V22886, Invitrogen, USA) at room temperature in the dark for 20 min. Following three additional washes, fresh medium was added and cells were cultured for 24 h. The conditioned medium was then collected and dual-labeled microvesicles were isolated as described above.

Flow cytometric analysis of dual-labeled MVs

Dual-labeled vesicles were analyzed by nano-flow cytometry (NanoFCM N30E, NanoFCM Inc., China) to quantify mitochondrial content. Samples were diluted to 1 × 106 particles/mL and excited with a 488 nm laser. Fluorescence signals were collected in the FITC channel for DiO and the PE channel for MitoTracker. Instrument calibration was performed using NanoFCM standard calibration beads (S16M-Exo). Each sample was analyzed for 60 s, and data were processed with NanoFCM Professional Suite v1.8.

Detection of mitochondrial transfer in transwell co-culture

To evaluate mitochondrial transfer from MSCs to NPCs via microvesicles, MSCs were pre-labeled with 200 nM MitoTracker Red CMXRos and NPCs were labeled with 200 nM MitoTracker Green FM (M7514, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C. After washing, red-labeled MSCs were seeded in the upper chamber of a Transwell insert (0.4 μm pore size, Corning, USA), and green-labeled NPCs were seeded in the lower chamber. After 48 h of co-culture, NPCs were washed, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), and imaged by confocal microscopy to assess mitochondrial transfer.

Preparation of A-769,662-loaded liposomes (ALPs)

ALPs were prepared by the thin-film hydration method. L-α-phosphatidylcholine (P5394, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), cholesterol (C8667, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and DSPE-PEG2000 (R-0057-2k, RuiXibio, China) were mixed at a mass ratio of 7:2:1 and dissolved in chloroform/methanol (2:1, v/v). A-769,662 (5 mg/mL; HY-50662, MedChemExpress, USA) was added, and the solvent was removed by rotary evaporation to form a thin lipid film. The film was hydrated with PBS for 1 h, followed by ultrasonication (VCX130, Sonics, USA) and extrusion through a 100 nm polycarbonate membrane (LiposoFast, Z373400-1EA, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) 10 times to obtain liposomes with uniform size distribution. The final ALPs were sterilized through a 0.22 μm membrane and stored at 4 °C in the dark until use [32].

Construction and membrane fusion of mitoMVs@A

To generate A-769,662-loaded mitochondrial microvesicles (mitoMVs@A), ALPs and MSC-derived mitoMVs were mixed at a protein mass ratio of 2:1 and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h to facilitate membrane fusion. The mixture was then extruded 10 times through a 100 nm polycarbonate membrane using an Avanti Mini-Extruder (610000-1EA, Avanti Polar Lipids, USA). The fused vesicles were purified by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g, washed twice with PBS, resuspended in PBS, and used as mitoMVs@A [32].

Characterization of particle size, zeta potential, and stability

The hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of mitoMVs@A were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Panalytical, UK). Samples were diluted to 1 × 108 particles/mL and analyzed at 25 °C in triplicate.

For stability testing, mitoMVs@A were suspended in PBS or PBS containing 10% FBS and incubated at 37 °C for 7 days. Samples were collected every 24 h to assess changes in particle size and PDI. Protein adsorption was quantified with a BCA Protein Assay Kit (23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to evaluate serum stability and resistance to protein fouling.

In vitro drug release study

To investigate A-769,662 release from mitoMVs@A under different pH conditions, PBS (pH 7.4) and mildly acidic buffer (pH 6.8) were used to simulate physiological and degenerative microenvironments. Equal amounts of ALPs and mitoMVs@A were loaded into Slide-A-Lyzer dialysis bags (10 kDa molecular weight cutoff; 66380, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and immersed in 25 mL of release buffer. Samples were incubated at 37 °C with gentle shaking (100 rpm). At predetermined time points, 1 mL of release medium was collected and replaced with an equal volume of fresh buffer. Released A-769,662 was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) as previously described [33].

Synthesis of gelatin methacrylate (GelMA)

GelMA was synthesized by dissolving gelatin (924504, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in PBS (pH 7.4) at 10% (w/v) and stirring at 50 °C until fully dissolved. Methacrylic anhydride (276685, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added dropwise at 0.8 mL/g gelatin, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 3 h at 50 °C in the dark. The reaction mixture was dialyzed against deionized water using dialysis tubing (8–14 kDa; YA1078, Solarbio, China) for 7 days with water changes three times daily. The product was then lyophilized for 48 h to obtain GelMA powder.

Carboxylation of GelMA (Preparation of GelMA-COOH)

For carboxyl modification, 1 g of GelMA was dissolved in PBS at 50 mg/mL. Succinic anhydride (0.5 g; 239690, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and triethylamine (1 mL; PHR2883, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) were then added sequentially, and the reaction proceeded at room temperature for 24 h. The mixture was dialyzed against deionized water using 3.5 kDa dialysis tubing (YA1078, Solarbio, China) for 72 h and lyophilized to obtain GelMA-COOH as a white powder.

Fabrication of GPVA hydrogel

To prepare the GPVA hydrogel, the GP precursor solution (10% w/v in PBS) was mixed with 0.5% (w/v) Irgacure 2959 (410896, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and dissolved at 37 °C. Preformed mitoMVs@A (3 × 108 particles/mL) were gently added with slow stirring to avoid bubble formation. The mixture was poured into cylindrical molds (8 mm diameter, 2 mm height) and irradiated with UV light (365 nm, 10 mW/cm²; XX-15 S, Spectroline, USA) for 180 s to complete photocrosslinking. The resulting hydrogel was soaked in sterile PBS overnight to remove unreacted small molecules and stored at 4 °C for further use [34]. GPVA refers to the GP-based injectable hydrogel loaded with mitoMVs@A, whereas GP denotes the corresponding empty hydrogel without active payload.

In vitro release kinetics of mitoMVs

To evaluate the release profile of mitoMVs from GPVA, DiO-labeled mitoMVs-loaded GPVA hydrogels were incubated in release media with different pH values (7.4, 6.5, and 5.5) or H2O2 concentrations (0, 0.1, 1, and 10 mM) at 37 °C with continuous shaking (100 rpm) for 96 h. Released mitoMVs were quantified by fluorescence nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) in fluorescence mode using 488 nm laser excitation and a 500 nm long-pass filter.

ABTS radical scavenging assay

ABTS radical scavenging capacity was assessed using an ABTS assay. The working solution was prepared by mixing equal volumes of ABTS stock solution (11684302001, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 10 mM potassium persulfate (60489, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), followed by incubation in the dark for 12–16 h. Before testing, the solution was diluted with deionized water to an absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.02 at 734 nm as measured using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (UV-2600, Shimadzu, Japan). Equal masses (10 mg) of freeze-dried GelMA, GP, and GPVA hydrogels were added separately to 2 mL of ABTS working solution and incubated for 10 min in the dark at room temperature. The absorbance of the supernatant was then measured at 734 nm. Establishment of rat IVDD model and hydrogel implantation

Eight-week-old male SD rats (250–280 g, strain 101, Crl: CD(SD) IGS; Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., China) were randomly divided into Sham, IVDD, GP, and GPVA groups (n = 5/group). Under 2% isoflurane anesthesia (R510-22-4, RWD, China), a puncture-induced IVDD model was established at the Co6/Co7 coccygeal disc using a 21-gauge needle inserted vertically into the disc center for 30 s. In the Sham group, only the skin was punctured without disc injury. Rats in the IVDD group received no further treatment. Rats in the GP and GPVA groups received 50 µL of uncrosslinked GP or GPVA precursor solution (10% w/v, containing 0.5% Irgacure 2959) injected into the punctured disc, followed immediately by in situ UV photocrosslinking (365 nm, 180 s). UV irradiance was measured using a radiometer (LS125, Linshang, China) at an irradiation distance of 5 cm, confirming an intensity of 10 mW/cm2 and an estimated energy dose of 1.8 J/cm2. Skin incisions were sutured, and gentamicin-soaked cotton swabs were applied locally to prevent infection. No obvious postoperative complications were observed.

To determine whether GPVA exerts its protective effect through the AMPK-FoxO3a axis, an additional cohort underwent the same IVDD modeling and GPVA implantation procedures and was further randomized into sh-NC and sh-FoxO3a groups (n = 5/group). FoxO3a shRNA lentivirus and scrambled control lentivirus were synthesized by Hanbio Biotechnology (China) at a titer of 1 × 108 TU/mL. At 48 h after GPVA implantation, rats received 200 µL of the corresponding lentiviral suspension via tail vein injection. The injection was repeated once 48 h later. At 4 and 8 weeks after surgery, rats were euthanized and Co6/Co7 discs were harvested under a dissecting microscope. NP tissue was carefully isolated by incising along the annulus fibrosus while avoiding contamination from the annulus fibrosus and cartilage endplate. One portion of the tissue was used for RT-qPCR to determine FoxO3a mRNA expression, and the other for WB to assess total and phosphorylated FoxO3a protein levels [20].

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining

At 4 and 8 weeks after surgery, Co6/Co7 disc segments were collected, fixed in 4% PFA (F8775, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 48 h, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, embedded in paraffin (EG1150C, Leica, Germany), and sectioned at 5 μm using a microtome (RM2016, Leica, Germany). After deparaffinization and rehydration, sections were stained using an H&E Staining Kit (G1120, Solarbio, China). Briefly, sections were stained with hematoxylin for 6 min, differentiated in 1% hydrochloric acid alcohol for 3 s, rinsed, and counterstained with eosin for 2 min. After dehydration and mounting, images were captured with an upright microscope (DM3000, Leica, Germany) equipped with a DFC450 C digital camera. Five random fields within the NP region were analyzed per slide by two blinded investigators.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Prior to group comparisons, each dataset was subjected to the Shapiro-Wilk test for normal distribution and Levene’s test for homogeneity of variance. Data meeting the assumptions of normality and equal variance are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical differences were evaluated using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test for comparisons between two groups, or a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for comparisons among multiple groups. For data that failed to meet the assumptions of normality or homogeneity of variance, appropriate non-parametric tests were employed: the Mann-Whitney U test was used for two-group comparisons, and the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison correction was applied for multiple-group comparisons. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA), and a p-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

RNA-seq Reveals the Involvement of the AMPK Pathway in IVDD Progression

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying IVDD, we first performed RNA-seq on NP tissue samples obtained from Sham and IVDD groups of rats (Figure S1A). A total of 134 genes were significantly upregulated and 165 genes were significantly downregulated in the IVDD group (Figure S1B). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses indicated that these DEGs were predominantly enriched in pathways related to oxidative stress and mitochondrial function (Figure S1C-D).

To mimic oxidative and mitochondrial stress in vitro, NPCs isolated from male Sprague-Dawley rat caudal discs were treated with 100 µM TBHP, followed by RNA-seq analysis of Control and TBHP-treated cells. Differential analysis identified 28 upregulated and 6 downregulated genes in the TBHP group (Figure S1E). Intersection of the in vivo and in vitro datasets yielded 11 overlapping genes (Figure S1F), which were predominantly enriched in AMPK signaling, mitochondrial metabolic processes, and cellular energy regulation pathways (Figure S1G). Given that mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress are closely linked to ferroptosis and that ferroptotic injury in NPCs contributes to IVDD progression, these findings suggest that AMPK signaling may participate in IVDD by connecting mitochondrial impairment, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis in NPCs.

Consistent with the transcriptomic results, WB analysis showed marked activation of AMPK signaling in NP tissues from IVDD rats compared with Sham controls (Figure S1H). A similar increase in AMPK activation was observed in TBHP-treated NPCs relative to Control cells (Figure S1I). Together, these data indicate that the AMPK pathway plays a pivotal regulatory role in mitochondrial function and ferroptosis within NP cells during IVDD progression.

AMPK activation alleviates TBHP-induced ferroptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction

Based on the RNA-seq results, we next examined whether AMPK activation could protect NPCs against oxidative stress-induced mitochondrial damage and ferroptosis. NPCs were exposed to 100 µM TBHP and divided into four groups: Control, TBHP, TBHP + EX229, and TBHP + A-769,662 (Fig. 1A).

CCK-8 assays showed that TBHP markedly reduced cell viability compared with the Control group, whereas both EX229 and A-769,662 significantly improved viability, with A-769,662 showing the stronger protective effect (Fig. 1B). Alcian blue staining and immunofluorescence analyses of ECM-related markers demonstrated that TBHP exposure substantially decreased proteoglycan, COL2, and AGG expression relative to Control, indicating ECM degradation. Conversely, both AMPK activators enhanced ECM synthesis, with A-769,662 treatment producing more pronounced increases in COL2 and AGG expression than EX229 (Fig. 1C-D).

Intracellular ROS, detected using DCFH-DA, were significantly elevated after TBHP treatment but were markedly reduced by EX229 and A-769,662, again with A-769,662 showing superior antioxidant activity (Fig. 1E). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that TBHP induced mitochondrial shrinkage, disrupted cristae, and loss of ultrastructural integrity, whereas both treatments preserved mitochondrial morphology (Fig. 1F). MitoTracker staining and JC-1 assays further confirmed that TBHP impaired mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm), while EX229 and A-769,662 stabilized Δψm and improved mitochondrial integrity (Fig. 1G-H). Consistently, RT-qPCR analysis showed that TBHP increased the fission-related gene DRP1 and decreased the fusion-related genes MFN1, MFN2, and OPA1, whereas both interventions partially reversed these changes (Fig. 1I). Overall, A-769,662 exerted a stronger restorative effect on mitochondrial homeostasis than EX229.

Ferroptosis-associated changes were then examined. FerroOrange staining and ferrous iron assays showed that TBHP markedly increased intracellular Fe2+ accumulation (Fig. 1J-K). TBHP also elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) levels and lipid ROS accumulation, as shown by MDA assays and oxidized BODIPY-C11 staining (Fig. 1L-M). WB analysis demonstrated increased expression of acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) and decreased expression of GPX4, two key ferroptosis-associated proteins (Fig. 1N). Both EX229 and A-769,662 significantly reduced Fe2+, MDA, and lipid ROS levels, suppressed ACSL4, and restored GPX4 expression, with A-769,662 again showing greater anti-ferroptotic efficacy (Fig. 1J-N).

These results indicate that TBHP induces severe mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis in NPCs, whereas pharmacological activation of AMPK effectively attenuates these injuries. Among the tested agents, A-769,662 exerted the more robust protective effect.

FoxO3a is involved in AMPK-mediated regulation of mitochondrial homeostasis and ferroptosis

Because AMPK has been reported to phosphorylate FoxO3a at Ser413 and thereby promote its activation and nuclear translocation, we hypothesized that AMPK protects NPCs from ferroptosis through FoxO3a-dependent regulation of mitochondrial ROS clearance (Fig. 2A).

WB analysis showed that TBHP significantly reduced total FoxO3a and p-FoxO3a levels compared with the Control group, whereas A-769,662 markedly increased both relative to TBHP alone (Fig. 2B). IF colocalization analysis revealed that under oxidative stress, FoxO3a was mainly distributed in the cytoplasm and mitochondria, accompanied by reduced mitochondrial abundance, whereas A-769,662 induced strong nuclear localization of FoxO3a (Fig. 2C). Fractionated WB analysis confirmed that TBHP increased mitochondrial FoxO3a accumulation, while A-769,662 promoted its nuclear translocation (Fig. 2D-F). These findings indicate that AMPK activation enhances FoxO3a phosphorylation and transcriptional activity.

To further determine the role of FoxO3a, shRNA-mediated knockdown was performed. Efficient knockdown was confirmed by RT-qPCR and WB (Figure S2A-B). FoxO3a silencing significantly reduced the expression of cytochrome c (Cyt c) and superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2), whereas heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) remained unchanged (Figure S2C), suggesting that FoxO3a exerts its antioxidative effects primarily through SOD2- and Cyt c-related pathways. In addition, FoxO3a knockdown increased DRP1 expression and decreased MFN1, MFN2, and OPA1 expression (Figure S2D), indicating aggravated mitochondrial fragmentation. Consistently, the GSH/GSSG ratio was reduced and oxidized lipid ROS were increased in the knockdown groups (Figure S2E-F), supporting a role for FoxO3a in maintaining mitochondrial redox balance and suppressing ferroptosis.

To further verify whether AMPK alleviates ferroptosis through FoxO3a activation, we conducted a rescue experiment. TBHP-treated NPCs were divided into three groups: PBS + sh-NC, A-769,662 + sh-NC, and A-769,662 + sh-FoxO3a-1 (Fig. 2G). FerroOrange staining was performed to detect intracellular Fe²⁺ levels (Fig. 2H); ferrous ion assay kits quantified Fe²⁺ content (Fig. 2I); MDA assay kits measured lipid peroxidation levels (Fig. 2J); and oxidized BODIPY-C11 fluorescence visualized lipid ROS accumulation (Fig. 2K). In addition, WB analysis evaluated the expression of ACSL4, GPX4, and SLC7A11 (Fig. 2L). The results showed that, compared with the PBS + sh-NC group, A-769,662 + sh-NC treatment significantly reduced Fe²⁺ accumulation, MDA content, and lipid ROS levels, while downregulating ACSL4 and upregulating GPX4 and SLC7A11. However, these protective effects were partially reversed in the A-769,662 + sh-FoxO3a-1 group, where ferroptosis-related indicators returned toward TBHP-induced levels (Fig. 2H-L). To verify whether FoxO3a can bind to the SLC7A11 promoter region, we performed a ChIP-qPCR assay. The results showed that, compared with the PBS + sh-NC group, the enrichment of FoxO3a at the SLC7A11 promoter region was significantly increased in the A-769,662 + sh-NC group; conversely, this enrichment was markedly decreased in the A-769,662 + sh-FoxO3a group (Fig. 2M-N). Furthermore, the IgG controls across all groups maintained low background signals, indicating that the immunoprecipitation exhibited good specificity (Fig. 2N).

These results indicate that activation of the AMPK-FoxO3a axis maintains mitochondrial homeostasis in NPCs, thereby conferring resistance to ferroptosis.

MSCs coculture enhances ECM anabolism and mitochondrial function in NPCs

MSCs have been reported to modulate NPC degeneration. To evaluate their protective effects, TBHP-treated NPCs were indirectly cocultured with MSCs in a Transwell system for 48 h (Figure S3A).

CCK-8 assays showed that coculture with MSCs significantly improved cell viability compared with the TBHP group (Figure S3B). Alcian blue staining and IF staining further demonstrated enhanced proteoglycan synthesis and increased COL2 and AGG expression in the TBHP + MSCs group (Figure S3C-D).

DCFH-DA staining showed a marked reduction in intracellular ROS after MSC coculture (Figure S3E). TEM analysis revealed substantial restoration of mitochondrial morphology and cristae integrity, which were severely disrupted by TBHP treatment (Figure S3F). MitoTracker and JC-1 staining confirmed improved mitochondrial activity and membrane potential in the presence of MSCs (Figure S3G-H). Moreover, RT-qPCR showed decreased DRP1 expression and increased MFN1, MFN2, and OPA1 levels relative to TBHP alone (Figure S3I). These findings indicate that MSC coculture enhances ECM anabolism and restores mitochondrial function in oxidatively injured NPCs.

1H NMR analysis confirmed the successful chemical modifications. GelMA, GelMA-COOH, and GP all exhibited characteristic methacrylate double-bond peaks at 5.3 and 5.6 ppm, whereas GP showed additional peaks at 7.5 and 7.6 ppm corresponding to the aromatic protons of phenylboronic acid (Fig. 5B). FTIR spectra further supported these structural transformations. The C–N stretching peak at 1160 cm− 1 and the C = O stretching peak at 1735 cm− 1 in GelMA-COOH and GP indicated successful carboxylation, while changes in the O–H deformation vibration near 1400 cm− 1 confirmed carboxyl introduction and subsequent amidation (Fig. 5C). When GP was mixed with alizarin red S (ARS), the maximum ultraviolet absorption peak shifted from 510 to 460 nm and the solution color changed from red to yellow, indicating boronate ester bond formation between GP and ARS and confirming the microenvironment-responsive nature of the hydrogel (Fig. 5D-E). Rheological analysis further showed that the GP precursor exhibited typical shear-thinning behavior, with viscosity decreasing as shear rate increased, indicating good injectability and suitability for minimally invasive administration (Fig. 5F).

Next, mitoMVs@A were incorporated into the GP hydrogel precursor solution, and the mixture was crosslinked under UV irradiation to construct the mitoMVs@A-loaded hydrogel (GPVA) (Fig. 5G). Scanning electron microscopy showed that GPVA retained a uniform interconnected porous structure comparable to that of unloaded GP, indicating that mitoMVs@A encapsulation did not disrupt the hydrogel microarchitecture (Fig. 5H-I). CLSM further confirmed that DiO-labeled mitoMVs@A were evenly distributed throughout the hydrogel matrix, suggesting successful and homogeneous encapsulation (Fig. 5J-K).

Given that the NP tissue primarily experiences compressive stress, we focused on evaluating the mechanical properties of GPVA hydrogels. Both GPVA and GP hydrogels exhibited compressive moduli higher than the physiological range of native NP tissue (3–6 kPa) (Fig. 5L-M), demonstrating their potential as biomechanically compatible NP substitutes. The hydrogels also exhibited rapid swelling behavior, reaching equilibrium within approximately 100 min (Fig. 5N). The equilibrium swelling ratio of GP and GPVA was significantly higher than that of GelMA, likely due to the increased hydrophilicity introduced by carboxyl groups. Under collagenase treatment, all hydrogels displayed controllable enzymatic degradation, with GP and GPVA degrading faster than GelMA, indicating favorable biodegradability in vitro (Fig. 5O).For in vivo tracking, indocyanine green (ICG) was covalently conjugated to the hydrogel using ICG-NHS. Fluorescence imaging performed on days 0, 3, 7, 14, and 21 after injection showed persistent signals within the intervertebral disc space, indicating relatively slow degradation and sustained retention in vivo (Fig. 5P–Q). Together, these data demonstrate that GPVA possesses suitable mechanical strength, swelling behavior, and biodegradability for intervertebral disc regeneration.

The dual ROS/pH responsiveness of GPVA was mainly attributed to APBA-derived phenylboronic acid groups. Under high ROS conditions, such as in the presence of H2O2, boronic acid-related structures undergo oxidation, thereby weakening the dynamic interactions within the hydrogel network. Under acidic conditions, shifts in the boronic acid/boronate equilibrium similarly destabilize these interactions, promoting network relaxation and accelerating drug release. Consistent with this design, release studies under simulated IVDD pathological conditions showed that A-769,662 release from GPVA increased progressively with rising H2O2 concentrations (Fig. 5R) and with decreasing pH (Fig. 5S). NTA results further showed that mitoMV release was relatively slow under neutral and low-ROS conditions but was significantly accelerated under acidic or high-H2O2 conditions. Although the overall release trend was similar to that of A-769,662, mitoMV release was more gradual than that of the small-molecule drug (Fig. 5T–U). Collectively, these results indicate that the GPVA hydrogel can achieve controlled and sustained drug release under high ROS and acidic conditions characteristic of IVDD, highlighting its excellent pathological microenvironment responsiveness.

Given the close association of IVDD with oxidative stress and inflammatory injury, the antioxidant capacity of GPVA was also evaluated. In the ABTS assay, the Trolox equivalent antioxidant capacity of GPVA was significantly higher than that of GelMA and GP, and the accompanying color change from cyan to colorless further confirmed efficient ABTS radical scavenging (Figure S6A-B). Similarly, in the DPPH assay, GPVA achieved a scavenging rate of 81.9%, markedly higher than the 9.5% observed in the GelMA group, with the solution color correspondingly changing from deep purple to light yellow (Figure S6C-D). These findings demonstrate that GPVA possesses strong free radical-scavenging activity in addition to its dual ROS/pH-responsive release behavior, highlighting its therapeutic potential for IVDD treatment.

GPVA hydrogel exhibits excellent in vitro biocompatibility

We next evaluated the biocompatibility of the GPVA hydrogel in vitro. CCK-8 assays showed that the GelMA group exhibited a slight reduction in NPC proliferation, whereas the GP and GPVA groups maintained proliferation levels comparable to those of the PBS control (Figure S7A). Live/Dead staining yielded consistent results. Only a few red fluorescent dead cells were detected on day 1, likely owing to mechanical stress during cell seeding, whereas by days 3 and 5, most NPCs in all hydrogel-treated groups displayed strong green fluorescence, indicating high cell viability (Figure S7B).

Alcian blue and safranin O staining further showed that NPCs cultured with the hydrogels preserved a normal phenotype and intact pericellular matrix architecture, supporting the good cytocompatibility of GPVA (Figure S7C-D). In addition, cytotoxicity control experiments showed no obvious increase in cell death in the Light only or GPVA + Light groups (Figure S7E), indicating that the photocuring conditions used in this study were well tolerated.

GPVA hydrogel demonstrates favorable in vivo biosafety

The in vivo biosafety of the hydrogels was further assessed by histological examination of tissues surrounding the implants after subcutaneous implantation (Figure S8A). H&E staining showed mild neutrophil infiltration in all groups on postoperative days 7 and 14, indicating a transient early inflammatory response. By day 30, neutrophil infiltration had largely disappeared, and the implanted materials were surrounded by fibroblasts, suggesting resolution of the acute inflammatory reaction. Serum inflammatory cytokine analysis was consistent with the histological findings (Figure S8B). At the early postoperative stage, serum TNF-α and IL-1β levels in the GelMA, GP, and GPVA groups were mildly elevated relative to baseline, with comparable increases among groups and no abnormal systemic inflammatory response. By day 28, both cytokines had decreased to near-baseline levels, and no significant intergroup differences were observed. These results collectively confirm the favorable tissue compatibility and in vivo biosafety of the GPVA hydrogel.

GPVA hydrogel significantly improves mitochondrial function and inhibits ferroptosis in NPCs

To investigate the protective effect of GPVA hydrogel on NPCs, TBHP-treated NPCs were divided into PBS, GP, and GPVA groups (Fig. 6A). CCK-8 assays showed that cell viability was moderately improved in the GP group and markedly enhanced in the GPVA group compared with PBS (Fig. 6B). Alcian blue staining and immunofluorescence analysis of ECM-related markers showed similar trends: protein levels of proteoglycans, COL2, and AGG increased slightly in the GP group but were significantly upregulated in the GPVA group (Fig. 6C-D), indicating that GPVA hydrogel effectively promotes NPC proliferation and ECM synthesis.

ROS levels, assessed using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe, were substantially elevated in the PBS group, slightly reduced in the GP group, and markedly decreased in the GPVA group (Fig. 6E). TEM further showed that GP treatment partially restored mitochondrial integrity, whereas GPVA preserved mitochondrial morphology and cristae structure more effectively (Fig. 6F). MitoTracker staining and JC-1 assays demonstrated that Δψm was more effectively maintained in the GPVA group than in the PBS and GP groups (Fig. 6G-H). Consistently, RT-qPCR results showed that DRP1 expression was significantly downregulated, while MFN1, MFN2, and OPA1 were significantly upregulated in the GPVA group relative to PBS, reflecting improved mitochondrial fusion-fission balance (Fig. 6I). These findings indicate that GPVA effectively alleviates oxidative stress, restores mitochondrial homeostasis, and promotes ECM synthesis in TBHP-injured NPCs.

FerroOrange staining revealed that intracellular Fe2+ fluorescence intensity was markedly weaker in the GPVA group compared with the PBS and GP groups (Fig. 6J). Consistently, iron quantification assays confirmed that GPVA treatment significantly reduced intracellular Fe2+ levels (Fig. 6K). MDA assays demonstrated a pronounced decrease in lipid peroxidation in the GPVA group (Fig. 6L), while oxidized BODIPY-C11 staining showed that lipid ROS (green fluorescence) levels were substantially lower in GPVA-treated NPCs than in PBS or GP groups (Fig. 6M). Furthermore, WB analysis indicated that ACSL4 expression was downregulated and GPX4 expression was upregulated in the GPVA group, with stronger modulation than observed in the GP group (Fig. 6N). These results collectively confirm that GPVA hydrogel exerts a potent inhibitory effect on ferroptosis.

To further validate the protective role of GPVA hydrogel, we employed an in vitro inflammatory model induced by TNF-α. CCK-8 assays showed that NPC viability increased modestly in the GP group and significantly in the GPVA group compared with PBS (Figure S9A). Alcian blue staining and IF analysis of COL2 and AGG showed a similar trend, with both ECM markers being slightly elevated in the GP group but markedly increased in the GPVA group (Figure S9B-E). These results indicate that GPVA remains effective in promoting NPC viability and ECM synthesis under inflammatory stress.

Collectively, under conditions of oxidative and inflammatory injury, GPVA exhibits pronounced antioxidative, anti-ferroptotic, and mitochondrial-protective effects, highlighting its strong therapeutic potential for IVDD intervention.

GPVA hydrogel effectively alleviates IVDD and reduces mitochondria-dependent ferroptosis

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of the GPVA hydrogel in vivo, SD rats were divided into Sham, IVDD, GP, and GPVA groups (Fig. 7A). Micro-CT analysis showed that the disc height index (DHI) was markedly reduced in the IVDD group compared with the Sham group. In contrast, both GP and GPVA partially restored DHI at 4 and 8 weeks after surgery, with GPVA showing the most pronounced recovery. By week 8, the DHI in the GPVA group was statistically indistinguishable from that in the Sham group. At both time points, DHI values in the GPVA group were also higher than those in the GP group, indicating superior preservation of disc structure (Fig. 7B-D).

Histological staining further supported the reparative effect of GPVA. Hematoxylin and eosin staining showed severe NP atrophy and structural disorganization in the IVDD group. The GP group retained partial NP tissue with a relatively loose architecture, whereas the GPVA group displayed nearly intact NP morphology with clear boundaries and minimal degeneration (Fig. 7E). Safranin O-Fast Green staining showed that although residual Safranin O-positive signals remained detectable in the IVDD group, the overall staining intensity and area were markedly reduced relative to those in the Sham group, accompanied by structural disruption and increased histological scores. These findings indicate a degenerative state characterized by partial proteoglycan loss rather than complete depletion. Both GP and GPVA enhanced proteoglycan and collagen preservation relative to the IVDD group, with GPVA showing the strongest staining and best-preserved ECM integrity. Histological scoring further confirmed these observations: the IVDD group exhibited the highest degeneration scores, whereas GP and GPVA significantly reduced tissue damage, with GPVA showing the lowest scores. Notably, degeneration scores in the GPVA group were further reduced at week 8 compared with week 4, suggesting progressive NP tissue repair, a phenomenon not observed in the other groups (Fig. 7G-H).

Immunohistochemical staining was performed to evaluate the expression of AGG and COL2. Compared with the Sham group, both markers were markedly reduced in the IVDD group. Relative to the IVDD group, both GP and GPVA increased AGG and COL2 expression, with the strongest staining observed in the GPVA group, indicating the most effective restoration of ECM anabolic metabolism (Fig. 7I).

Mitochondrial integrity and ferroptosis-related changes were further assessed in vivo. TEM showed severe mitochondrial damage in the IVDD group, slight improvement in the GP group, and clear restoration of mitochondrial morphology and cristae structure in the GPVA group (Figure S10A). Redox analysis showed that the GSH/GSSG ratio was significantly reduced and MDA content was markedly increased in IVDD tissues compared with the Sham group, indicating impaired antioxidant capacity and elevated lipid peroxidation. These changes were only mildly improved in the GP group but were significantly reversed in the GPVA group, which showed the most pronounced recovery of redox homeostasis (Figure S10B-C). WB analysis further showed increased ACSL4 and decreased GPX4 expression in the IVDD group. In comparison, the GP group showed only modest improvement, whereas the GPVA group exhibited marked downregulation of ACSL4 and strong restoration of GPX4, indicating the most effective suppression of ferroptosis (Figure S10D). Together, these results demonstrate that GPVA effectively attenuates IVDD progression and reduces mitochondria-dependent ferroptosis in vivo.

Knockdown of FoxO3a attenuates the therapeutic effects of GPVA Hydrogel

We next examined whether the therapeutic effect of GPVA depends on activation of the AMPK-FoxO3a axis. WB analysis of NP tissues from the Sham, IVDD, GP, and GPVA groups showed that AMPK, FoxO3a, and phosphorylated FoxO3a levels were significantly reduced in the IVDD group compared with the Sham group. No obvious difference was observed between the IVDD and GP groups, whereas the GPVA group showed markedly higher levels of AMPK, FoxO3a, and phosphorylated FoxO3a than the GP group (Figure S11A), suggesting that GPVA activates AMPK-FoxO3a signaling in vivo.

To investigate whether GPVA hydrogel exerts its effects through activation of the AMPK-FoxO3a axis, rats in the GPVA group were further randomized into sh-NC and sh-FoxO3a subgroups. WB analysis confirmed a significant reduction in FoxO3a expression following knockdown (Figure S11B). Micro-CT analysis showed that, compared with the sh-NC group, the sh-FoxO3a group exhibited a pronounced decrease in DHI at both 4 and 8 weeks post-surgery, indicating exacerbated IVDD (Figure S11C-D).

H&E staining revealed severe NP tissue shrinkage, structural disorganization, and indistinct nucleus-annulus boundaries in the sh-FoxO3a group, while the sh-NC group retained relatively intact NP structures (Figure S11E). SO-FG staining showed diminished proteoglycan (red) and collagen (green) signals in the NP region of the sh-FoxO3a group, indicating aggravated ECM loss, whereas the sh-NC group maintained abundant matrix distribution (Figure S11F). Histological scoring demonstrated that the sh-FoxO3a group had significantly higher scores than the sh-NC group at both time points, suggesting impaired tissue repair following FoxO3a knockdown. Moreover, the sh-FoxO3a group showed no reduction in scores at week 8, indicating a lack of regenerative progression (Figure S11G-H). IHC staining further revealed markedly lower COL2 and AGG expression in the sh-FoxO3a group compared with the sh-NC group, confirming reduced ECM synthetic activity (Figure S11I).

Collectively, knockdown of FoxO3a partially reversed the therapeutic effects of the GPVA hydrogel on IVDD, as evidenced by aggravated NP structural damage, decreased ECM synthesis, and limited regenerative capacity.

Discussion

In the present study, we developed a ROS/pH-responsive injectable hydrogel (GPVA) for the co-delivery of the AMPK activator A-769,662 and MSC-derived mitoMVs. This system effectively activated AMPK-FoxO3a signaling, restored mitochondrial function, suppressed ferroptosis in NPCs, and significantly attenuated IVDD. GPVA enhanced the antioxidant capacity of NPCs, restored Δψm, reduced ROS and Fe2+ accumulation, and upregulated SOD2 and Cyt c expression. Unlike conventional single-agent or passive carrier systems, GPVA integrates signaling activation, mitochondrial functional delivery, and pathology-responsive release into a unified platform, thereby providing a metabolic remodeling-based strategy for IVDD treatment.

AMPK is a master regulator of cellular energy sensing and metabolic homeostasis and has been widely implicated in oxidative stress, aging, and degenerative disorders [35–37]. However, most previous studies have focused on non-disc models or isolated cellular phenotypes, and the downstream role of FoxO3a in IVDD has remained insufficiently defined [38, 39]. Through AMPK activation by A-769,662, AMPK inhibition by EX229, and FoxO3a knockdown experiments, the present study demonstrates that the AMPK-FoxO3a axis is a central regulator of mitochondrial homeostasis and ferroptosis in NPCs. Notably, FoxO3a was found to regulate SLC7A11 expression and thereby influence lipid peroxidation-related ferroptotic injury, supporting a previously underexplored mechanistic link in IVDD. In the A-769,662-treated group, FoxO3a showed enhanced nuclear localization, whereas in the stress groups without effective activation it was predominantly retained outside the nucleus, suggesting that AMPK promotes FoxO3a nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity. This localization pattern was consistent with the downstream anti-ferroptotic molecular changes and functional recovery observed in the present study. At the same time, the upstream events controlling FoxO3a nuclear trafficking were not further dissected here and will require systematic investigation using pathway blockade and site-directed mutagenesis in future studies.

These findings expand the current understanding of the AMPK-FoxO3a pathway beyond general metabolic regulation and place it in a broader framework linking energy homeostasis to ferroptosis control. The newly added ChIP-qPCR results further strengthen this mechanistic model. Previous studies have shown that AMPK promotes FoxO3a activation and nuclear translocation, whereas SLC7A11 is a key component of the SLC7A11-GSH-GPX4 antioxidant defense axis [10]. In our study, A-769,662 treatment enhanced FoxO3a phosphorylation and nuclear localization, increased FoxO3a enrichment at the SLC7A11 promoter, and was accompanied by restoration of GPX4 and suppression of ACSL4. Under sh-FoxO3a conditions, promoter enrichment and anti-ferroptotic effects were both markedly attenuated. This continuous line of evidence, from upstream activation to promoter binding and downstream functional alteration, supports the conclusion that AMPK suppresses ferroptosis and improves mitochondrial homeostasis through FoxO3a-mediated transcriptional regulation of SLC7A11. However, the present evidence remains based mainly on binding and functional association, and the precise transcriptional hierarchy within this axis still requires clarification by FoxO3a binding-site mutation and SLC7A11 promoter reporter assays.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is recognized as a key pathological event in the early stages of IVDD [40, 41]. Although stem cell-derived exosomes have attracted considerable attention because of their ability to carry diverse regulatory molecules, most exert indirect effects and lack the capacity to directly restore mitochondrial structure and function [42, 43]. In contrast, mitoMVs used in the present study retain intact mitochondrial membranes and respiratory chain-related components, thereby enabling more direct bioenergetic compensation and metabolic reconstruction. Our data suggest that mitoMVs and A-769,662 act through mechanistically complementary routes: mitoMVs primarily support mitochondrial replenishment and functional restoration, whereas A-769,662 mainly activates the AMPK-FoxO3a-SLC7A11 axis and suppresses ferroptosis. Thus, the two components operate at the organelle and transcriptional signaling levels, respectively. Because this study did not include a formal dose-matrix design, it is more rigorous to define this effect as mechanistic complementarity with potential synergy rather than definitive pharmacological synergy. This combined strategy represents a distinct conceptual advance over conventional single-pathway interventions and provides a new framework for organelle-targeted regeneration in IVDD.

The material design of GPVA further strengthened this therapeutic strategy. The phenylboronic acid-modified hydrogel exhibited dual ROS/pH responsiveness and enabled controlled release under oxidative and acidic conditions characteristic of the degenerative disc microenvironment [44, 45]. Unlike conventional inert hydrogels, GPVA combined injectability, mechanical support, biocompatibility, antioxidant capacity, and intelligent release behavior, allowing sustained local retention and responsive cargo liberation in a poorly vascularized tissue environment [46, 47]. In vitro release experiments showed accelerated release at pH 6.8 and under elevated ROS conditions, supporting the concept that pathological cues can actively regulate therapeutic exposure. Accordingly, GPVA does not simply function as a passive carrier, but as a pathology-responsive platform that aligns drug availability with lesion-specific metabolic demands and provides a material basis for smart therapeutics.

Ferroptosis, a recently identified form of regulated cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, has been widely implicated in neurodegenerative diseases, cancers, and IVDD [6, 48]. In the present study, activation of AMPK-FoxO3a signaling reduced Fe2+ accumulation, lowered lipid ROS and malondialdehyde levels, suppressed ACSL4, and restored GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression. These findings indicate that AMPK not only regulates energy metabolism, but also modulates ferroptotic susceptibility through the cysteine-glutathione antioxidant system. In this context, the AMPK-FoxO3a-SLC7A11 axis provides a mechanistic bridge between mitochondrial protection and ferroptosis defense, thereby broadening the current understanding of metabolic regulation in disc degeneration.

The in vivo results further support the therapeutic potential of GPVA. Hydrogel implantation significantly improved disc height, preserved NP architecture, enhanced ECM reconstruction, restored redox homeostasis, and normalized ferroptosis-related protein expression. Compared with conventional local drug injection or cell transplantation, GPVA provided more sustained local efficacy and better integration of structural and metabolic repair. These data suggest that AMPK activation and mitoMV delivery can jointly interrupt the pathological cycle of energy deficiency, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis, thereby exerting protective effects from the molecular to the tissue level.

Current therapeutic approaches for IVDD, including anti-inflammatory drugs, protein injections, stem cell transplantation, and mechanical repair, often show transient efficacy, poor local retention, or limited survival of transplanted cells [42, 49]. Although hydrogel-based systems and exosome-loaded biomaterials have improved local delivery and reduced inflammation or senescence, most still focus primarily on anti-inflammatory effects or ECM preservation [50–52]. For example, hyaluronic acid- or kartogenin-related hydrogels can partially improve disc structure and the local microenvironment, whereas exosome-loaded hydrogels mainly enhance local retention and alleviate inflammation and cellular senescence [50–52]. However, these approaches provide only limited direct intervention in the metabolic pathological loop linking mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis. In contrast, the AMPK/mitoMVs co-delivery system developed in this study integrates metabolic signaling activation with mitochondrial functional restoration, supports pathology-responsive release, and exhibits favorable injectability and biocompatibility. Systematic investigation further identified the AMPK-FoxO3a-SLC7A11 axis as an important mechanism underlying ferroptosis regulation, thereby providing a mechanistic basis for metabolism-targeted intervention in degenerative disease. In this sense, the present study extends beyond conventional symptom-oriented repair and proposes a dual strategy of metabolic remodeling and structural restoration.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although the AMPK-FoxO3a-SLC7A11 axis was supported by phosphorylation, localization, ChIP-qPCR, and functional rescue data, the upstream regulation of FoxO3a nuclear trafficking and the exact transcriptional hierarchy within this pathway were not fully dissected. Second, although mitoMVs and A-769,662 showed clear mechanistic complementarity, formal dose-response matrices and quantitative synergy analyses were not performed within the same experimental system. Third, the in vivo experiments were conducted in rodents with a relatively limited observation period, which constrains extrapolation to long-term efficacy and safety. Fourth, systemic FoxO3a interference was used mainly for causal validation, and although target downregulation was confirmed in NP tissue, the tissue specificity and potential off-target effects of this approach remain limited and should be interpreted cautiously.

These limitations also indicate important directions for future work. Because the intervertebral disc is poorly vascularized and molecular transport depends largely on endplate-associated diffusion, future studies should prioritize local or tissue-targeted gene modulation strategies with improved controllability. In addition, large-animal models such as sheep and goats will be essential for evaluating long-term retention, dose-response relationships, repeat-dosing intervals, and safety under disc size and loading conditions closer to those in humans [53–55]. Future studies should also optimize mitoMV preparation consistency and drug-loading reproducibility, and explore whether this platform can be extended to other degenerative disorders characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis. Combining GPVA-like systems with stem cell-based regenerative strategies may represent another promising direction for enhancing tissue repair.

In summary, the ROS/pH-responsive GPVA hydrogel developed in this study enables the co-delivery of an AMPK agonist and mitoMVs, activates AMPK-FoxO3a signaling, restores mitochondrial function, suppresses ferroptosis, and promotes ECM reconstruction, thereby improving intervertebral disc structure and function. The major conceptual advance of this work lies in integrating metabolic signaling regulation, mitochondrial functional supplementation, and intelligent delivery into a single therapeutic platform linking energy metabolism, organelle homeostasis, and tissue repair. Mechanistically, the study identifies the AMPK-FoxO3a-SLC7A11 axis as an important component of ferroptosis defense in IVDD. From a translational perspective, GPVA provides an injectable, controllable, and biocompatible platform with promising potential for precision treatment of degenerative spinal disorders.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed a novel ROS/pH-responsive injectable hydrogel, GPVA, for the co-delivery of mesenchymal stem cell-derived mitoMVs and the AMPK agonist A-769,662. This system effectively activated the AMPK-FoxO3a signaling axis, alleviated mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis in NP cells, and promoted ECM reconstruction and tissue repair. By integrating mitochondrial functional supplementation, metabolic signaling activation, and pathology-responsive release, GPVA establishes a therapeutic strategy centered on mitochondrial restoration and ferroptosis modulation. These findings provide a promising biomaterial platform with substantial translational potential for precise and regenerative treatment of IVDD.
