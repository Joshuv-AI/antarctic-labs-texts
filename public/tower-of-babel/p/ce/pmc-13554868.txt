Physiology-Guided Atrial-Only Leadless Rescue of Pacemaker Syndrome

Persistent dyspnea after prior Micra AV implantation prompted physiologic evaluation despite preserved LVEF and normal prosthetic valve function. ECG showed ventricular pacing with retrograde P waves, and exercise testing identified preserved antegrade AV nodal conduction. Because transvenous upgrade remained undesirable and prior Micra extraction was avoided, an atrial-only leadless strategy was selected. Implantation of an Aveir atrial leadless pacemaker restored AV synchrony through the native AV node, reduced ventricular pacing to 0.1%, and resolved symptoms. AV = atrioventricular; ECG = electrocardiogram; LVEF = left ventricular ejection fraction.

History of Presentation

A 61-year-old woman was evaluated by electrophysiology because of persistent fatigue and dyspnea on exertion, particularly with routine activity and bending forward. Three years earlier, she required a prolonged postoperative hospital stay because of endocarditis of her prosthetic aortic valve. After redo aortic root and bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement, she had developed symptomatic junctional bradycardia requiring temporary pacing. At that time, a leadless pacemaker was chosen because of increased infection risk, for which she was on lifelong antibiotic treatment. The Micra AV leadless pacemaker was used as it was the only leadless option available at the time. Her course was complicated by stroke, frailty, and multiple infections that made a transvenous pacemaker system riskier. Despite subsequent recovery of left ventricular function, her exercise intolerance persisted until presentation to the electrophysiology office. Physical examination was unremarkable and did not identify an alternative structural explanation for her symptoms. Specifically, there were no signs of volume overload, no new murmur suggestive of prosthetic valve dysfunction, and no examination findings pointing to an acute pulmonary process.

Past Medical History

The patient has a history of complex valvular disease including a bicuspid aortic valve previously treated with mechanical aortic valve replacement. Twenty years later, she underwent redo surgery for aortic valve endocarditis and an aortic root pseudoaneurysm with implantation of a bioprosthetic aortic root and concomitant bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement. Her recovery was complicated by a postoperative stroke and recurrent infections. During that hospital stay, she developed symptomatic junctional bradycardia requiring temporary pacing and subsequently underwent implantation of a Micra AV leadless pacemaker to reduce infection and periprocedural complication risks. Her history was otherwise remarkable for heart failure with improved ejection fraction, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic anemia, and fibromyalgia with neuropathy requiring long-term opioid therapy.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for fatigue and exertional dyspnea in this case was broad and included worsening heart failure, bioprosthetic valve dysfunction, ischemia, arrhythmias, right ventricular pacing intolerance, and pacemaker syndrome with atrioventricular (AV) dyssynchrony.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography 4 years after her redo valve replacement and aortic root replacement showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 55% without native or prosthetic valve dysfunction. Interrogation of the Micra AV leadless pacemaker demonstrated no atrial or ventricular arrhythmias and showed 100% ventricular pacing. Electrocardiography (ECG) showed a ventricular paced rhythm with retrograde P waves, suggestive of intact retrograde conduction (Figure 1A). Exercise stress testing was subsequently performed. During exercise, an intermittent intrinsic junctional AV nodal rhythm competed with Micra-mediated ventricular pacing and subsequently suppressed ventricular paced beats entirely, demonstrating preserved antegrade conduction that was functionally obscured at rest (Figure 1B). When rate response was activated, ventricular pacing again predominated and suppressed the accelerated junctional rhythm (Figure 1C). Even with rate response activated, the patient exercised for only 3 minutes and 20 seconds before stopping because of dyspnea. During recovery, she remained ventricularly paced with premature ventricular complexes. These findings together supported symptomatic AV dyssynchrony from ventricular leadless pacing as the etiology of her symptoms rather than a structural cause. Exercise testing was the decisive physiologic study because it identified preserved antegrade AV nodal conduction, thereby establishing that atrial-only leadless pacing could restore AV synchrony without transvenous upgrade or prior device extraction.

Management

Management focused on how to restore AV synchrony without negating the reason leadless pacing had been selected initially. Options discussed included transvenous dual-chamber pacing, prior Micra retrieval or abandonment followed by dual-chamber leadless system implantation, atrial leadless pacemaker implantation with the existing Micra retained as ventricular backup, and atrial leadless implantation with the Micra programmed to ventricular paced, dual-chamber sensed, dual response to sensing tracking if ventricular pacing remained necessary. A transvenous upgrade was considered undesirable because leadless pacing had originally been chosen in the setting of frailty and major infectious risk.1,2 Chronic Micra retrieval was also avoided because late extraction of tine-based leadless pacemakers may be limited by fibrotic encapsulation, valvular or subvalvular adhesions, and tissue embedding.3 Because provocative testing had already demonstrated preserved antegrade conduction, an atrial-only leadless strategy was selected as the least invasive means of restoring AV synchrony. Given reliable exercise-induced and intraoperative 1:1 antegrade conduction, implantation of a ventricular leadless device was deferred and Micra reprogramming to ventricular paced, dual-chamber sensed, dual response to sensing tracking was not required. The Abbott Aveir atrial leadless pacemaker was implanted and programmed as atrial paced, atrial sensed, inhibited response, rate adaptive, and the prior Micra ventricular pacemaker was reprogrammed in ventricular paced, ventricular sensed, inhibited response mode as low-rate backup ventricular pacing.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Postoperative ECG demonstrated atrial pacing with intact AV conduction in atrial paced, atrial sensed, inhibited response, rate adaptive mode with the Micra programmed in ventricular paced, ventricular sensed, inhibited response mode as low-rate backup ventricular pacing (Figure 1D). Device interrogation showed 88% atrial pacing and 0.1% ventricular pacing, confirming near-complete suppression of Micra-dependent ventricular pacing. The patient reported rapid and sustained resolution of dyspnea on exertion after implantation.

Discussion

This case illustrates a clinically important cause of persistent dyspnea after ventricular leadless pacing. Pacemaker syndrome can occur from AV dyssynchrony, especially in patients with intact retrograde conduction. Minimizing unnecessary ventricular pacing while preserving AV synchrony remains an important principle in pacing practice.2,4 In this patient, preserved left ventricular ejection fraction, absence of native or prosthetic valvular dysfunction, lack of device-detected tachyarrhythmia, and an unremarkable examination made an alternative structural explanation less likely. The combination of 100% ventricular pacing, retrograde P waves on ECG, and exercise-induced re-emergence of preserved antegrade conduction instead suggested AV dyssynchrony as the cause of symptoms. Once atrial pacing restored a coordinated atrial-then-ventricular activation sequence through the native AV node, symptoms resolved and ventricular pacing virtually disappeared.

In 2021 when the patient required a leadless pacemaker, the Micra leadless pacemaker was the only Food and Drug Administration–approved device available. Micra AV is designed to improve AV synchrony by using accelerometer-based sensing of atrial mechanical activity from a ventricular leadless device, and several studies have shown meaningful AV synchronous pacing with this approach.5, 6, 7 However, this is not equivalent to true atrial sensing and pacing and AV dyssynchrony can still occur in a subset of patients.7 In our patient, if we increased the ventricular pacing rate on the Micra, she would have increased right ventricular pacing burden without restoration of AV synchrony. Fortunately, the Aveir atrial leadless pacemaker was approved by the Food and Drug Administration for clinical use in 2023. By implanting an Aveir atrial leadless pacemaker paired with a previously implanted Micra ventricular pacemaker, we were able to increase her heart rate by right atrial pacing only, significantly reduce right ventricular pacing burden, and restore AV synchrony while forgoing implantation of a second ventricular leadless pacemaker. There were concerns of the Micra oversensing the pacing spike from the Aveir atrial leadless pacemaker; however, in this case, intraoperative testing at maximal outputs did not demonstrate oversensing between devices. This suggests that the strategy may be feasible in carefully selected patients if the 2 independent leadless devices are positioned far apart enough.

In symptomatic patients concerning for pacemaker syndrome, provocative testing may be more informative than resting interrogation alone. In this case, exercise testing was the decisive physiologic study because it identified preserved antegrade AV nodal conduction and directly guided an atrial-only, nonextraction leadless rescue strategy. Atrial leadless pacing has now been shown to be feasible in appropriately selected patients.8 In addition, recent reports have shown that heterologous leadless systems can function together and that atrial leadless pacing can relieve pacemaker syndrome caused by ventricular leadless pacing.9,10 Our case extends that experience to a patient whose original indication for leadless pacing was substantial infection risk and whose previously implanted Micra was intentionally retained for backup ventricular pacing. The key physiologic principle was the use of preserved native AV conduction as the bridge between 2 independent leadless systems rather than direct device-to-device communication.

This approach is not applicable to all patients with ventricular leadless pacing–associated symptoms. The critical selection features in this case were preserved antegrade AV nodal conduction, a strong reason to avoid transvenous hardware, and an unfavorable risk-benefit profile for prior leadless device extraction.1, 2, 3 In such patients, atrial-only leadless rescue may restore AV synchrony while avoiding a more invasive, high-risk upgrade strategy.

Conclusions

In a high-risk patient with pacemaker syndrome after prior Micra AV leadless pacemaker implantation, implantation of an Aveir atrial leadless pacemaker restored effective AV synchrony, reduced ventricular pacing from 100% to 0.1%, and resolved exertional dyspnea without transvenous upgrade or prior device extraction.
