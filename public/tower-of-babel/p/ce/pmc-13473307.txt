Longitudinal metabolomics profiles reveal increased gut microbial protein fermentation during Spaceflight
Authors: Giorgia La Barbera, Jan Stanstrup, Sara R Zwart, Scott M Smith, Henrik M Roager, Lars Ove Dragsted
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrogravity is a condition that may affect gastrointestinal function and metabolism during spaceflight. Despite attempts to keep normal dietary habits at the ISS, the food selection and meal timing may also be perturbed during the confinement. To understand the impact of spaceflight on human metabolism, we characterized the plasma metabolome of astronauts (n = 52) before, during, and after missions on board the International Space Station (ISS) using liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry. Here we show that spaceflight affected around 40 circulating compounds. In this longitudinal assessment, the metabolic changes were already observed shortly after launch and subsided within a few days after landing. The flight-induced changes in metabolites reflected increased protein fermentation by the gut microbiota, possibly reflecting prolonged intestinal transit time caused by microgravity. Minor diet-related changes related to intakes of caffeine, fish, and some fats were also observed but affected less than one third of all metabolites responding to spaceflight. We did not observe major sex-specific differences in metabolism. Increasing the consumption of slow-fermented carbohydrates at ISS to reduce protein fermentation might improve gastrointestinal health in astronauts in future human long-term spaceflights.Subject terms: Metabolomics, Metabolomics

Introduction

Human desire for exploration is an innate trait, which since 1961 has brought humans to space. Spaceflight has provided insights into several biological phenomena, including the effects of microgravity on human physiology.

Biochemical change during spaceflight was first studied observationally in the Nutritional Status Assessment study, where blood and urine samples were collected for a wide range of endpoints examining the temporal effects of spaceflight on human biochemistry, primarily aspects of bone1 and eye2 health between 2006 and 2012. The Biochemical Profile study extended this work and collected samples from additional astronauts between 2013 and 2018, expanding aims also to include multi-omics reflecting the effect of microgravity on immune system stress3. The ProK dietary trial examined the effects of dietary influences on acid-base balance and effects on bone metabolism and bone loss. The net endogenous acid production during flight was affected by diet and shown to affect bone mineral content if calcium and resistance exercise were sufficient4.

As space missions increase in duration and distance from Earth, a key objective is to understand and mitigate the impact of microgravity on astronaut health, including changes in metabolism and the potential role of the gut microbiome5,6.

Untargeted plasma metabolomics can provide insight into the metabolic state and exposome of an individual7. In the NASA Twins Study, untargeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)-based metabolomics was performed on plasma samples collected from identical twin subjects before, during, and after a 1-year mission for one of the twins, and changes were observed in several metabolites, including some derived from the microbiota8. A metabolomics analysis of serum samples from 51 astronauts by NMR spectrometry found mainly sex-specific changes in energy-related metabolites before and after flight, but no consistent longitudinal changes before, during, and after flight9. Following these studies, another study has been performed to enrich the biochemical measurements collected in the Space Omics and Medical Atlas (SOMA) with metabolomics data, but it includes only flights of very short duration, and monitors metabolites only before and after flight10,11.

The current study provides an analysis of the metabolic changes comparing samples collected from previous studies before, during, and after 2–9 months of flights. Here, we provide a comprehensive, untargeted LC-MS-based metabolomics study on a large cohort of astronauts with repeated longitudinal sampling. By applying state-of-the-art liquid chromatography high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS) to 488 plasma samples collected from 52 astronauts, we report multiple metabolites changing in space deriving from gut microbiota, diet, and other metabolic changes, highlighting potential health implications in astronauts.

Results and discussions

In this work, 488 plasma samples were collected from 11 female and 41 male astronauts (aged 48 ± 5.4 y) at multiple time points before (n = 154), during (n = 233), and after flight (n = 101) on the International Space Station (ISS) (Fig. 1). The study subjects were astronauts with 2-9 months missions to the ISS between 2006 and 2018. Thirteen and 22 participated in the observational studies, Nutritional Status Assessment12 or Biochemical Profile13, respectively, while 17 participated in the ProK trial (Clinicaltrials.gov NCT01713634)4. The plasma samples were analyzed by LC-HRMS using an untargeted metabolomics workflow.

Spaceflight-induced changes in plasma metabolome

To investigate the spaceflight-induced changes in the plasma metabolome, we applied multivariate sparse PLS discriminant analysis (sPLS-DA) and a univariate linear mixed model (LMM). A 3-compartment sPLS-DA model including 81 features (positive and negative mode combined) was able to predict the 233 out of 488 samples that were collected during spaceflight with an error rate of only 2.6%, while samples obtained on Earth pre-flight were predicted with an error rate of 7.1%, possibly reflecting space simulation training and a larger dietary variability on Earth. The LMM identified 96 features that significantly changed in at least two time intervals after launch (after correction for false discovery rate). In total, 141 features were selected by sPLS-DA and LMM. As an additional quality step, we evaluated the signal linearity of the features by analyzing a serially diluted pooled quality control sample. A total of 72 features did not show a monotonous dose-response, possibly due to too low a signal or to detector saturation, and were not considered further. The remaining 69 features were investigated for identification and corresponded to approximately 40 compounds (Supplementary Fig. 1).

The identified metabolites that changed with spaceflight included food-, host-, and microbiota-derived metabolites. We observed no indication of metabolite changes with time in space, only features affected by launch and landing. The results from statistical analysis and metabolite annotations can be found in the Supplementary information (Supplementary Data 1, Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3).

Microbial proteolytic metabolites change with spaceflight

Among the spaceflight-associated metabolites, we consistently observed increased plasma levels of several markers of microbial metabolism during flight, including p-cresol sulfate, phenylacetylglutamine, and 4-ethylphenol sulfate. These host-microbial co-metabolites originate from bacterial fermentation of proteins (i.e., proteolysis) in the large intestine and subsequent phase II metabolism (i.e. sulfation) in the liver after absorption into the hepatic circulation (Fig. 2a). Other microbial proteolytic metabolites, i.e. 3-indoxyl sulfate and phenol sulfate, also showed an increased plasma concentration during flight (Fig. 2b). Although they were not selected by our statistical models, they were extrapolated from the pre-processed data and showed positive correlation with the other proteolytic metabolites (Supplementary Fig. 4), while we observed a decrease in their metabolic precursor, tyrosine. Three of these metabolites were also among the 245 differing metabolites in the NASA Twins Study when comparing the flight twin relative to the terrestrial twin8. Although these proteolytic metabolites have occasionally been linked to drastic dietary changes14, their production is highly variable between subjects even when consuming the same diet15, emphasizing that other factors than diet are likely to affect their levels.

Intestinal transit time has repeatedly been associated with proteolytic metabolites16,17, and we have previously documented that these metabolites are strongly associated with prolonged transit time18. Constipation is a common condition among astronauts19. We find it likely that the lack of gravitational pressure on the intestines during flight may prolong intestinal transit time in astronauts. With increased passage time, the fiber and carbohydrates that serve as primary substrates for the microbiota are depleted, and the gut microbes will switch to ferment protein (amino acids) to sustain energy production and growth. Protein fermentation leads to the characteristic pattern of metabolites observed in this study. Interestingly, pimelic acid, which is another metabolite previously associated with transit time and potentially derived from microbial biotin synthesis18,20, was also found to increase during flight (Fig. 2b). Pimelic acid may therefore be an additional microbial metabolite associated in slower passage of intestinal contents17, or the production of pimelic acid may serve as a marker of transit simply because pimelic acid- and biotin-producing bacteria reside longer in the gut lumen with increased transit time.

Gut proteolytic fermentation is accompanied by the formation of potentially detrimental compounds, including ammonia and hydrogen sulfide17. Phenylacetylglutamine was recently shown to be directly involved in cellular senescence and may be involved in mitochondrial dysfunction and DNA damage in humans21. Increased plasma levels of 4-ethylphenol sulfate, p-cresol sulfate, and indoxyl sulfate have been found in mice to alter brain activity and anxiety behavior, while indoxyl- and p-cresol sulfates are associated in humans with an increased disease risk in patients with compromised kidney function22. This points at microbial protein fermentation as potentially detrimental to health.

There is only one comparable animal study reporting on gut microbiomes of mice in space23; after 37 days, microbiomes were found to increase in measures of alpha-diversity. The same observation was made from astronaut microbiotas during six-month missions to the ISS23,24. Obviously, the mouse diet differs from that of human astronauts, but in both studies, there was an increased ratio of Firmicutes over Bacteroides, which is generally associated with slower transit time23, thereby supporting our findings. These findings also agree with gut microbial diversity being positively correlated with transit time16. Together, these observations suggest that increased gastrointestinal transit time may be common during spaceflights, may lead to a shift in gut microbiota activity, and could have implications for astronaut health during long-term missions unless countermeasures are taken.

Uric acid and fluid shift

Plasma uric acid decreased approximately 10% during spaceflight and returned to baseline values or higher at 10 days or more after landing, indicating a major change in purine or amino acid metabolism or disposition during flight. This might be due to a decreased dietary purine intake, an increased amino acid fermentation in the gut, increased renal or intestinal urate excretion, or a change in fluid distribution. Of these, evidence for fluid shifts reducing plasma volume while retaining total body fluid volume shortly after launch and until shortly after landing (as indicated by our data, Supplementary Fig. 3), is supported by evidence from a previous study25. Uric acid may therefore be removed by intracellular influx, by an increased glomerular filtration rate, or be reduced by increased microbial fermentation of amino acids. In agreement, some amino acids were also observed to change previously by NMR analysis, but the shifts were partially sex-specific in contrast to what was observed in the current analysis9.

Changes in diet-derived metabolites

An estimated 27.5% of the changing features were related to food intake. Two caffeine metabolites, including caffeine itself and paraxanthine, were reduced during space flight, suggesting lower caffeine intake during flight, a finding also published previously after NMR metabolomics9. The lower caffeine exposure might be due to lower intakes of coffee or to the use of instant coffee on the ISS; instant coffee is known to contain less caffeine than regular brewed coffee26. Although there are studies showing that coffee may increase large bowel motility, the effect of coffee on transit time is controversial. Therefore, we believe that the observed decrease in caffeine does not have any relationship with the prolonged transit time suggested by the proteolytic metabolites and pimelic acid. Coffee intake at the ISS was recently shown to affect astronaut sleep, but caffeine as such was not measured27.

Other food-derived metabolites decreased during flight, such as the fish intake biomarker, 3-carboxy-4-methyl-5-propyl-2-furanpropionic acid (CMPF), and several highly unsaturated lyso-PCs decreased; these compounds are typically also observed after intake of fatty fish or fish oils28. This likely reflects temporal changes in the use of fish products during flights. Several dishes with fish and other seafood are regularly offered during flights29,30. The astronauts are encouraged to consume these foods based on findings relating fish intake to protection against bone loss31. Notably, CMPF was the only sex-specific metabolite difference observed in the current study, as it was observed only in the females (Supplementary Fig. 5). A cluster of medium-chain acyl carnitines was also found to decrease during flight, indicating minor changes in fatty acid exposure or in their use for energy metabolism by the astronauts during flight32.

In conclusion, our study reveals that spaceflight induces changes in endogenous and gut microbial metabolism along with reflections of minor changes in dietary exposures, including a decrease in caffeine and in fish products among females. Prolonged transit time and formation of detrimental microbial metabolites may play a role in astronaut health during long-term missions. This emphasizes the importance of maintaining regular bowel habits during spaceflight, which may be supported by increasing the availability and intake of slowly fermented carbohydrates, i.e., fiber, to reduce protein fermentation.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study complies with all relevant ethical regulations. The protocols used for the original sample collections in the Nutritional Status Assessment12 or Biochemical Profile13, and/or in the ProK trial4 studies were reviewed and approved by the NASA Institutional Review Board. All astronauts provided written informed consent to participate in the original studies and again for the use of their samples for the analyses and research reported herein.

Study subjects

The study subjects were astronauts with 2–9 month missions to the ISS between 2006 and 2018. They participated in one of the observational studies, Nutritional Status Assessment12 or Biochemical Profile13, and/or in the ProK trial (Clinicaltrials.gov NCT01713634)4. These protocols were reviewed and approved by the NASA Institutional Review Board. All astronauts provided written informed consent to participate in the original studies and again for the use of their samples for the analyses and research reported herein.

Reagents and materials

For the UHPLC mobile phases, acetonitrile, methanol, and isopropanol were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Pittsburgh, PA, USA). For chemical synthesis and for reference standards: formic acid (CAS 64-18-6), uric acid (CAS 69-93-2), tyrosine (CAS 60-18-4), pimelic acid (CAS 111-16-0), CMPF (CAS 86879-39-2), cholic acid (CAS 81-25-4), paraxanthine (CAS 611-59-6), 3-indoxyl sulfate (CAS 2642-37-7), caffeine (CAS 58-08-2), phenylacetylglutamine (CAS 28047-15-6), p-cresol sulfate (CAS 3233-58-7), p-cresol (CAS: 106-44-5), phenol (CAS 108-95-2), 4-ethylphenol (CAS 123-07-9), 3-ethylphenol (CAS 620-17-7), octanoic acid (CAS 124-07-2), 7-octenoic acid (CAS 18719-24-9), 10-decenoic acid (CAS 14113-05-4), 3-decenoic acid (CAS 53678-20-9), thionyl chloride (CAS 7719-09-7), trichloroacetic acid (CAS 76-03-9), L-carnitine (CAS 541-15-1), LysoPC(17:1/0:0) (CAS 1246304-62-0), TRIS, NaOH, adenosine 3′-phosphate 5′-phosphosulfate lithium salt hydrate (PAPS) (CAS 109434-21-1), and pooled human liver S9 extract were ordered from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). For internal standard solutions: l-adenine-8-13C (Cambridge Isotope Lab), l-phenyl-d5-alanine-2,3,3-d3 (Cambridge Isotope Lab), caffeic acid 13C3 (Toronto Research Chemicals), caffeine 13C3 (Toronto Research Chemicals), l-tyrosine 13C9 (Sigma Aldrich), para-aminobenzoic acid (Sigma Aldrich), l-tryptophan-(indole-d5) (Sigma Aldrich), hippuric acid-[13C6] (IsoSciences), cortisone-d8 (Sigma Aldrich), and glycocholic acid-[2H4] (IsoSciences) were purchased. SiroccoTM plates for plasma deproteinization and 96-Well collection plates for collection and subsequent sample analysis were bought from Waters® (Hedenhusene, Denmark). The chromatographic column HSS T3 (C18, 100 × 2.1 mm2, 1.8 μm particle size) and the VanGuard HSS T3 C18 column (2.1 × 5 mm2, 1.8 μm) were purchased from Waters®. Reagent water was ion-exchanged and purified further by a Millipore (Billerica, MA, USA) unit to obtain an electrical resistance below 18 MΩcm.

Synthesis of reference standards

A biomimetic enzymatic synthesis33 was used for the conjugation of sulfate to 4-ethylphenol, 3-ethylphenol and phenol. One mmol of the substrate and 23 µL of PAPS (C=1mgmL−1) were added to TRIS C = 500 µM at pH 7.5 and stirred at 37 °C for 5 min. Twelve µL of chilled human liver S9 extract (C=2mgmL−1) was thereafter added, and the mixture was stirred again at 37 °C for 1 h. Ice-cold MeOH was added in a 2:1 (v/v) excess to terminate the reaction. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 4 min at 5 °C. The supernatant was collected and evaporated to dryness. The dried sample was redissolved in 200 μL H2O:ACN = 90:10 (v/v). Formation of p-cresol sulfate from p-cresol was used as a positive control to confirm the activity of the enzyme system. A negative control was also carried out by substituting the substrate by 5 µL of 500 µM TRIS (pH 7.5).

A chemical synthesis was used for the production of acylcarnitines34. Octanoyl carnitine, 7-octenoylcarnitine, 3-decenoylcarnitine, and 10-decenoylcarnitine were synthesized according to the following protocol: octanoic acid, 7-octenoic acid, 3 decenoic acid, or 10 decenoic acid (2 mmol) were added to thionyl chloride (0.05 mmol) and stirred at 60 °C for 1 h to obtain the acid chloride. Trichloroacetic acid (1.5 mmol) was added to L-carnitine (0.025 mmol) and stirred at 60 °C for 30 min. The carnitine solution was added to the acid chloride solution and stirred at 80 °C for 2 h. At the end of the reaction the solution was diluted ten times with isopropanol and stored at −20 °C. Before injection, the solution was diluted 100 times with water.

Blood sample collection

Study protocols and sample collection/sample management efforts were led by the NASA Nutritional Biochemistry Laboratory at the Johnson Space Center in Houston4. Blood samples were collected in ethylenediaminetetracetic acid (EDTA) gel separator tubes. Because preflight sampling sometimes occurred at the same time as blood collections for clinical purposes, some tubes were centrifuged, and the plasma was removed from the tube and aliquoted into separate tubes before freezing at −80 °C. For one of the preflight collections, the blood was collected and centrifuged, and the tubes were frozen at −80 °C to model the collection procedures used during flight, where it is not possible to remove the plasma from the tube before freezing, so the plasma is in contact with the gel separator while frozen for many months. Plasma that had been in contact with the separator gel while frozen showed characteristic chemical feature differences, which were taken into consideration during pre-processing and statistics (see below).

Plasma metabolomics

The plasma samples were shipped on dry ice and stored at −80 °C at the Department of Nutrition, Exercise and Sports, University of Copenhagen. The plasma was deproteinized, filtered, and processed by methods described previously35. Briefly, 40 μL of plasma and 10 μL of an internal standard solution (see Reagents and Materials) were added to 180 μL of methanol in a Sirocco TM plate (Waters®, Manchester, UK), vortexed for 5 min, and the liquid phase collected in a 96-well sample collection plate (Waters®) by filtering. An additional 90 μl of methanol were added to the Sirocco TM plate, vortexed for 5 min, and the liquid phase collected. This step was repeated twice. The collected liquid phase was dried, and the samples were reconstituted with water and 0.1% formic acid. Quality control (QC) samples were obtained by mixing 20 µl of each blood sample in each plate (plate pools) and by mixing 20 µl of each plate pool to create the global pool. In addition, plate pools were diluted three times and ten times in water. The plate pools, diluted plate pools, and global pools were treated with the same procedure as explained above for the samples. A total of 488 samples in random order, six plate pools, six diluted plate pools (3X), six diluted plate pools (10X), one global pool, six blanks (0.1% formic acid in water), and six blanks plus internal standards were injected into a UHPLC (Acquity I-class, Waters®) coupled with a QTOF mass spectrometer equipped with electrospray ionization (Synapt G2, Waters®). The injected samples (5 µl) were separated on a reversed-phase column (ACQUITY HSS T3 C18 column, 2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 µm) coupled with a pre-column (ACQUITY VanGuard HSS T3 C18 column, 2.1 × 5 mm, 1.8 µm). The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in 70:30 acetonitrile/methanol (solvent B). The duration of the analytical run was 7 min with the following flow rate: start condition (0.5 ml min−1), 1 min (0.5 ml min−1), 2 min (0.6 ml min−1), 3 min (0.7 ml min−1), 4 min (0.8 ml min−1), 4.5 min (1.0 ml min−1), 6.4 min (1.1 ml min−1), 6.6 min (1.0 ml min−1), 6.8 min (0.5 ml min−1), 7.0 min (0.5 ml min−1), and the following gradient: start condition (5% B), 1 min (8% B), 2 min (15% B), 3 min (40% B), 4 min (70% B), 4.5 min (100% B), 6.6 min (5% B) and 7 min (5% B). Electrospray settings were the following: the cone voltage was 2.5 kV and 3.2 kV; the collision energy was 6.0 and 4.0 eV; and the temperature of the ion source and desolvation nitrogen gas temperature were 120 °C and 400 °C for positive and negative ionization mode, respectively18. Mass spectrometry data were acquired in full scan mode with a scan range of 50–1000 mass/charge (m/z). Two blanks, one blank with internal standards, and one global pool were injected at the beginning and end of each plate (batch). The plate pools were injected every 8–10 samples, for a total of nine times per plate. The 3x and 10x diluted plate pools were injected in the middle of the sample sequence. Each unique sample was prepared and injected once (no technical replicates). Data was collected using Masslynx (version 4.1, Waters®).

Metabolome data pre-processing

The raw LC-MS data was converted into.mzmL files using ProteoWizard (version 3.0.24230-21f03f0)36. The R statistics (version 4.5.1, https://www.R-project.org) using the latest package for XCMS37 (version 4.9.2) was used for pre-processing. Peak-picking was performed using the latest version of the CentWave38 algorithm; peaks were grouped across samples using the peak density method and aligned using features common to most samples and finally, missing values were “gap-filled” by re-integrating samples for which values were missing for any of the features. Specific parameters can be found in the full set of analysis scripts available below. CAMERA39 (version 1.65.1) was used to group related features, to estimate the number of unique compounds, and to aid identification. Missing values (after gap-filling) represent values too low to detect; to maximize their variance, we performed feature-wise substitution with a random number between zero and the minimum detected intensity in the remaining samples. Each feature was first corrected for intra-batch drift by fitting a LOESS regression to the QC samples, and then the average of the QCs was used to correct inter-batch differences. Features where the ratio between the standard deviation of the QC samples and the experimental samples (D-ratio40) were below 1 were selected, and the remaining features were disregarded. PCA was used to visually inspect data uniformity (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7).

Statistics

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size since we used all data available from astronauts staying 2-9 months at the ISS within the specified time period. No data were excluded from the analyses. To compare plasma samples removed from the gel separator tube before freezing and plasma samples that had been frozen in the gel separator tube, a linear mixed model (LMM)41 was used. The features found to be significantly different between the two methods were excluded from the subsequent analysis.

To investigate the effect of space flight, the samples were divided into seven groups. Pre-flight (n = 154), 0–50 days in space (n = 98), 51–100 days in space (n = 47), 101–150 days in space (n = 54), more than 151 days in space (n = 34), within 10 days after returning to Earth (n = 49) and more than 10 days after returning to Earth (n = 52). An LMM41 was created that took into account person-specific random effects, in addition to the fixed effects of the 7 sample groups and the cofactors, collection tube type, age and sex of the astronaut, the age of the sample, and the analytical batch. The resulting p-values were corrected for false discovery rates (FDR)42,43 and features with resulting q-values below 0.05 in at least two time points during spaceflight were selected for investigation. In addition to the univariate analysis, an sPLS-DA method44 was also used to select the features discriminating pre-flight and in-flight samples. The samples were split 70:30 into training and test sets, 10-fold internal cross-validation was used, and the number of components was optimized. The test set was used for the evaluation of the model. Due to matrix effects, some features do not exhibit a dose-response relationship, meaning that the intensity does not decrease as expected in the diluted pool samples. We filtered such features out before interpretation by requiring that the undiluted pool must have higher intensity than the 10x diluted pool and that the 3x diluted pool must have less than 20% higher intensity than the undiluted pool.

All figures and statistics were prepared using R 4.5.145 and plots created using ggplot2 (version 4.0.3)46 and GGally (version 2.4.0)47, unless otherwise noted.

Metabolite annotations

Metabolites differing significantly between plasma collected on Earth and in space were listed in an inclusion list. Selected plasma samples were analyzed with data-dependent acquisition (DDA) at a collision dissociation energy ramp between 10 and 50 eV with inclusion list, using the same chromatographic method as used for the untargeted metabolomics profiling. For obtaining spectra with higher mass accuracy for some of the markers, DDA with inclusion list or targeted MS/MS was performed at three different collision dissociation energies, 10, 30, and 50 eV on a UHPLC system (Acquity H-class, Waters®) coupled to a higher resolution QTOF mass spectrometer (Vion IMS, Waters®). The samples were separated on a reversed-phase column (ACQUITY HSS T3 C18 column, 2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 µm, Milford, USA) coupled with a pre-column (ACQUITY VanGuard HSS T3 C18 column, 2.1 × 5 mm, 1.8 µm, Milford, USA) at a temperature of 50 °C. The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A), methanol (solvent B), 10 mM ammonium acetate in methanol (solvent C), and isopropanol (solvent D). The duration of the analytical run was 10 min with the following flow rate: start condition (0.4 mLmin−1), 0.75 min (0.4 mLmin−1), 6 min (0.5 mLmin−1), 6.5 min (0.5 mLmin−1), 8 min (0.6 mLmin−1), 8.1 min (0.4 mLmin−1), 9 min (0.4 mLmin−1), 10 min (0.4mLmin−1), and the following gradient: start condition (100% A), 0.75 min (100% A), 6 min (100% B), 6.5 min (70% C, 30 % D), 8 min (70% C, 30 % D), 8.1 min (70% C, 30 % D), 9 min (100% A), 10 min (100% A).

Initially, the precursor ions and fragments of each selected feature were matched with databases and spectral libraries such as HMDB (version 5.0) (https://hmdb.ca/), and mzCloud (https://www.mzcloud.org/), setting a tolerance of 0.01 m/z. When not matching, the MS/MS spectra of the remaining features were either manually interpreted or processed with in-silico fragmentation tools like Sirius48 (version 6.0) for metabolites annotation and NIST MS Search software (version 2.0)49 with the Lipid Blast library50 for the lipids annotation, setting a tolerance of 0.01 m/z. Finally, authentic commercial or synthesized reference standards were run together with the samples on the same platform and within the same batch to match retention times. The identification confidence level of the metabolites was assigned according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative as level I (confirmed by matching to a standard with two orthogonal measures (rt, m/z), level II (matching MS/MS fragmentation to a spectral library), level III (compound classification), or level IV (unknown)51. A list of all identified and unidentified compounds with spectral features is in Supplementary Data 1, and spectra of identified compounds are uploaded to Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.20591730).
