Determinants and long-term outcomes of treatment delays in young and middle-aged acute ischemic stroke patients: a prospective cohort study
Authors: HC Lin, ZX Liu, YC Wu, K Lin, ZL Liu, DL Chen, YX Zheng, MM Cai, SH Xie, ZY Jin, SQ Chen, H Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and purposeTreatment delays critically affect outcomes in acute ischemic stroke (AIS). This study investigates predictors of delayed hospital presentation and its long-term impact in young/middle-aged AIS patients.MethodsIn this prospective longitudinal study, 488 consecutive AIS patients (18–59 years old), admitted to our tertiary hospital (June 2021-February 2024), based on the onset-to-needle time for intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) ≤ 4.5 h or the onset-to-groin puncture time for endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) ≤ 24 h for inclusion in a timely-treatment group and other patients in a delayed-treatment group. Multimodal assessments included socioeconomic surveys, NIHSS scores, and followed up with modified Rankin Scale (mRS) and Stroke-Specific Quality of Life Scale (SS-QOL) at 1 and 6 months after onset. Multivariable logistic regression identified delay predictors.ResultsAmong 488 eligible patients (mean age 52.07 ± 6.68 years), 448 (91.8%) experienced treatment delays. Lower monthly income (OR = 3.76, 95%CI = 1.10–4.48), extended hospital distance (OR = 2.86, 95%CI = 1.31–6.24), and illness during non-working hours (OR = 5.85, 95%CI = 2.39–14.36). first visit medical facility is a non-three level hospital(OR = 3.87, 95%CI = 1.47–10.16), non-emergency admission (OR = 27.99, 95%CI = 3.55-220.86), and higher NIHSS score (OR = 0.81, 95%CI = 0.74–0.90) were associated with treatment delay. The negative impact of treatment delay on patient prognosis and quality of life shows a strengthening trend over time. Delayed presentation correlated with poorer 6-month outcomes: higher mRS scores (β = 0.41, P < 0.05) and reduced SS-QOL (ρ=-0.38, P < 0.05).ConclusionsOver 90% of young/middle-aged AIS patients experience treatment delays driven by socioeconomic barriers (low income, geographic constraints) and atypical symptom profiles. The negative impact of treatment delay on prognosis and quality of life strengthens over time. These findings advocate for targeted public health interventions addressing financial/geographic disparities and enhancing recognition of subtle stroke manifestations in the younger populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04803-x.Keywords: Ischemic stroke, Young and middle-aged, Treatment delay, Quality of life, Prognosis

The Global Stroke Report 2021 shows that the incidence of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) in young and middle-aged (age 18–59 years) people has increased by more than 40%, and stroke in youths is becoming increasingly severe [1–3]. Young and middle-aged people are the main force driving the economic development of the country, and the occurrence of stroke will bring a heavy economic burden to society and families. With the development of stroke medical technology, time has become the key to improve stroke prognosis. However, less than 20% of patients in China can reach the hospital within the 4.5 h time window, and the lack of awareness of stroke alertness in young and middle-aged people, who are the important subjects of medical decision-making, is the main reason for the treatment delay [4].Young and middle-aged people are mostly important players in the family and society, and their own dysfunction after stroke is very likely to cause their sense of shame, giving them severe anxiety and depression, making it difficult for them to feel social support. In addition, the long rehabilitation leads to difficulties for patients to return to work, preventing them from assuming their family roles and realizing their social values, as well as integrating back into society [5, 6].

Existing studies have identified several determinants of treatment delay in elderly stroke populations, predominantly focusing on individual characteristics and healthcare accessibility [7, 8]. Key factors associated with prolonged delays lasting three hours or more include prior stroke history, symptom onset at home, and self-recognition of initial symptoms. Conversely, protective factors against treatment delays encompass personal exposure to stroke cases through social networks, accurate symptom severity assessment, utilization of emergency medical services including prompt activation of emergency response systems such as calling 120, and shorter transportation distances from onset locations to healthcare facilities [9]. Notably, comorbidity profiles reveal significant differences in this population, with ischemic stroke patients exhibiting disproportionately high rates of cardiovascular comorbidities such as heart disease at 34%, diabetes mellitus at 16%, and atrial fibrillation at 9%, alongside a 22% prevalence of prior cerebrovascular events [10].

Most educational efforts have been traditionally focused on the older population, which may have led to significantly less awareness of stroke symptoms among young/middle-aged adults. Studies have found that treatment delay time of young/middle-aged patients was significantly longer than that of the elderly [11]. Unfortunately, Most current studies focus on the mechanisms of delay in the elderly stroke population, and there is a lack of systematic exploration of the factors affecting stroke in the young and middle-aged population, and even fewer studies have dynamically tracked the trajectory of delay in medical care on the quality of survival in the mid- and long-term. In addition, the currently used classifications lump patients with diverse and rare causes and underlying pathophysiological mechanisms into 1 other determined category, thereby ignoring the possible different long-term prognosis of stroke depending on the different causes. In this study, we proposed to reveal the key predictors of treatment delay in young and middle-aged AIS patients through a prospective cohort study, and to establish a quantitative relationship model between delay time and functional prognosis and quality of life, which would provide an evidence-based basis for the development of age-specific stroke emergency education strategies and full rehabilitation intervention programs.

Methods

Participants

This prospective cohort study consecutively enrolled 488 AIS patients, 18–59 years of age, from a tertiary stroke hospital (June 2021-February 2024). The primary time metric was the onset-to-needle time (ONT). Based on the criteria of delayed medical treatment access, patients were categorized based on their treatment status and eligibility: the timely-treatment group, which included those receiving intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) with an onset-to-needle time ≤ 4.5 h, or endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) with an onset-to-groin puncture time ≤ 24 h [12]; and the delayed-treatment group, comprising all other patients, specifically included those who received IVT or EVT beyond the above time windows and those who did not receive either IVT or EVT, regardless of the reasons (e.g., medical ineligibility, contraindications, patient/family refusal, or system delays). Inclusion criteria were based on the diagnostic criteria of the “Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute Ischemic Stroke 2018” confirmation by head CT or head MRI as ischemic stroke, age 18–59 years, consciousness, possession of a fixed telephone or mobile communication device, ability to cooperate with follow-up, provision of patient informed consent, and voluntary participation in this study [13]. Exclusion criteria were the combination of other severe organ dysfunction, eligibility for IVT or EVT but treatment refusal by the patient, incomplete data collection and dead patients. According to domestic reports [4], the rate of treatment delay among stroke patients was 80%. According to the sample size calculation formula, N = Z2α/2 × P (1-P)/δ 2, taking α = 0.05, Z α/2=1.96, δ = 0.05, P = 0.80, and considering a 20% dropout rate, the sample size was calculated to be at least 296 patients [14].

Rationale for the binary time classification

Contemporary stroke therapy utilizes both intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) within 4.5 h and endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) in selected patients up to 24 h from onset. This study focused on “delayed access” as a system and patient-level barrier, employing a binary classification (timely vs. delayed) anchored to the 4.5 h IVT and the 24 h EVT window [15]. This approach was chosen because it offers a clear and clinically relevant benchmark for assessing timely access to the most widely applicable acute reperfusion therapy, aligns with public health messaging emphasizing the “golden hours,” and reflects our cohort’s reality, only 2 patients received EVT within 24 h, precluding meaningful subgroup analysis. Thus, the binary classification pragmatically highlights that most young and middle-aged patients in our setting do not reach the hospital in time for first-line, time-sensitive treatment.

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Shantou University School of Medicine (Ethics Batch Number: B-2021-094).

Research tools

A self-designed questionnaire was used to collect patients’ demographic information and clinical characteristics, included age, gender, education level, smoking status (“Yes” stands for currently smoking or having a history of smoking, “No” stands for never smoking), alcohol consumption status (“Yes” stands for currently drinking or having a history of regular drinking, “No” stands for never drinking regularly), marital status, occupation, personal monthly income (patient’s approximate average monthly earnings in the year before stroke onset), distance to clinic (calculated as the distance from the patient’s location at symptom onset to the admitting hospital), residential status, place of residence, number of stroke episodes (“1” stands for first occurrence of stroke), time of onset (For patients with unclear onset time, the last known normal time, verified by patient/witness recall and as bedtime for nocturnal strokes—served as the operational onset time), illness duing working time (determined according to the working time of the patient at the time of onset, except the working time alone at home or other places), first visit medical facility (Place of first professional medical assessment), referral status, mode of onset, onset status, and emergency admission (“Yes” stands for the patient was evaluated and admitted to the hospital’s emergency department, “No” stands for the patient was admitted on an outpatient basis), combined with chronic disease (refers to previously diagnosed chronic diseases, such as hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, atrial fibrillation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, etc., but does not include prior stroke). Disease-related information included whether there was a family history of stroke, stroke frequency, comorbidities, initial symptoms, and National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score at admission [16]. Barthel Index (BI) involved assessments of patient daily self-care abilities, involving 10 items such as eating, bathing, dressing, and controlling bowel movements, and had a total score ranging from 0 to 100 points, with 0 to 20 points indicating complete dependence, 25–45 points indicating severe dependence, 50–70 points indicating moderate dependence, 75–95 points indicating mild dependence, and 100 points indicating independence [17]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale in this study was 0.952. The short version of the Stroke Specific Quality of Life Scale (SV-SS-QoL) for stroke patients was used for quality-of-life assessment. This scale is a quality-of-life assessment tool specifically designed for stroke patients. The Chinese version of the SV-SS-QoL scale includes two dimensions of quality of life related to physical health and social and psychological health, with a total of 12 items. Each item is scored on a 5-point scale (1–5 points), with a total score of 12–60 points [18, 19]. The higher the score, the better the quality of life. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale in this study was 0.879. The Likert 7-point scoring method was used for the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score to evaluate the degree of neurological deficit in patients after stroke, with scores ranging from 0 to 6 points. The higher the score, the more severe the neurological deficit. This scale has good reliability and authenticity, and has been widely used in clinical trials and prognostic studies for stroke patients [20]. An mRS score ≤ 2 is the threshold for determining whether stroke patients have disabilities. Therefore, in this study, an mRS score ≤ 2 represents good prognosis, while an mRS score > 2 represents poor prognosis [21]. Outcome assessments were conducted via telephone follow-up at two time points: 1 month and 6 months after AIS. Assessors performing these follow-ups were blinded to the patients’ treatment delay status.

Data collection and quality control methods

Within two weeks of patient onset of illness after admission, two investigators who had received training distributed a questionnaire to the patient and explained the requirements for filling out the questionnaire. General information, disease-related information, and related scales of the patient were collected. The questionnaire was distributed on the spot and collected after confirmation of accuracy. Disease information was obtained through the hospital medical record system. A follow-up with the patient and their family was performed before discharge to reduce the loss to follow-up rate. At 1 and 6 months after the onset of the disease, the investigator used mRS scores to assess the patient’s prognosis through telephone follow-up, and used the SV-SS-QoL to evaluate the patient’s quality of life. Dropout criteria were verbal refusal during follow-up or making two consecutive phone calls for 3 days without receiving a response. A total of 561 survey questionnaires were distributed, with 561 valid questionnaires and an effective response rate of 100%. During the 1-month and 6-month follow-up, there were 33 and 40 patients, respectively, who were lost to follow-up. Among them, 12 patients refused to follow up verbally and 61 patients were unable to answer the phone, with a loss to follow-up rate of 13.01%. Finally, 488 patients completed all follow-up.(Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

To ensure the accuracy of data entry, EpiData 3.1 software was used for two-person data entry, two-person cross-checking, and finding the original paper version of the questionnaire for verification if there was a discrepancy in the entered data. SPSS 26.0 software was used to analyze the data. Measurement data conforming to normal distribution was expressed as mean ± standard deviation (±s), and the independent samples T-test was used for comparison between groups. For data not conforming to a normal distribution, data was expressed as median (quartile) M (P25, P75), and the Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparison between groups. Categorical data was expressed as frequency and percentage (%), and comparisons between groups were made using the χ2 test. Both univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses were employed to identify factors associated with treatment delay in young and middle-aged AIS patients. Given the limited number of patients in the timely-treatment group, a clinically driven, parsimonious variable selection strategy was adopted to mitigate the risk of overfitting. Core predictors were selected from the univariate analysis for inclusion in the final logistic regression model based on clinical relevance and prior literature. The relationship between treatment delay and prognosis and quality of life was analyzed using univariate analyses of the relationship between treatment delay and prognosis and quality of survival. Correlation analysis was performed using Kendall and Spearman correlation coefficients with two-sided tests, with P < 0.05 being considered as a statistically significant difference.

Influencing factors of treatment delay for timely vs. delayed-treated patients

Treatment delay was defined as the dependent variable (timely-treatment = 0, delayed-treatment = 1). Given the limited number of patients in the timely-treatment group (n = 40), six core predictors were selected based on clinical relevance and prior literature to effectively mitigate the high risk of model overfitting. These predictors included: personal monthly income, distance to clinic, illness during working hours, First visit medical facility, emergency admission status, and NIHSS score.The final model contained these 6 independent variables, estimating a total of 8 parameters. This reflects the strategy adopted within the constraints of our sample size to prioritize clinical interpretability while addressing overfitting.The assignment method of independent variables is detailed in Table 3. Multiple factor binary logistic regression analysis showed that patient personal monthly income (OR = 3.76), distance to clinic (OR = 2.86), illness during working hours (OR = 5.85), first visit medical facility(OR = 3.87), emergency admission status (OR = 27.99), and NIHSS score (OR = 0.81) were associated with treatment delay in young and middle-aged AIS patients (P < 0.05). Interaction analysis was conducted on variables with statistical differences in multiple factor analysis, and showed no interaction between variables. Hosmer χ2 = 5.448, P = 0.709>0.05. The multifactor binary logistic regression model established in this study fits well with the actual data. (Table 4).

Impact of treatment delay on the prognosis and quality of life of young and middle-aged AIS patients

The univariate analysis results indicated that at 1 month after onset, there was no significant association between treatment delay and the prognosis or quality of life in young and middle-aged AIS patients (P > 0.05). At 6 months after onset, a significant association was observed between treatment delay and the prognosis and quality of life of the patients (P < 0.05). (Tables 5 and 6). Multivariable analyses revealed a consistent pattern regarding the impact of treatment delay. While the associations did not reach statistical significance, binary logistic regression indicated a strengthening negative effect on prognosis over time (OR decreased from 0.50 at 1 month to 0.28 at 6 months; P = 0.082 and P = 0.084, respectively). Similarly, multiple linear regression showed a negative trend between longer treatment delays and lower quality of life scores at both time points, with the effect approaching significance at 6 months (P = 0.088).(Tables 7 and 8). Correlation analysis of treatment delay with prognosis and quality of survival

Kendall correlation coefficient analysis showed that there was no significant correlation between treatment delay and prognosis within 1 month after the onset of the disease in young and middle-aged patients with AIS (P > 0.05), and it was positively correlated with prognosis at 6 months after the onset of the disease (β = 0.41, P < 0.05). There was no significant correlation between quality of life (P > 0.05) and negative correlation with quality of life 6 months after onset (ρ=-0.38, P < 0.05).

Discussion

This study addresses the critical issue of treatment delay, an urgent global public health priority. This problem is widespread and poses a particularly severe impact on young and middle-aged stroke patients. Stroke is the most common cerebrovascular disease in clinical practice, with the “four high characteristics” of high pathogenicity, high recurrence rate, high disability rate, and high mortality rate. Among them, the proportion of AIS is as high as 70% [22]. At present, the key emergency treatment for AIS is reperfusion therapy, mainly IVT, which has strict requirements for the duration of treatment [23]. We found that the incidence of treatment delay in middle-aged and young AIS patients was 91.80%, which is similar to previous research results [4]. The prolongation of treatment delay time is related to an increase in mortality and disability rates. The observed association between treatment delays and worsened 6-month outcomes aligns with ischemic cascade pathophysiology [24]. Prolonged hypoperfusion accelerates neuronal apoptosis in the ischemic penumbra through glutamate excitotoxicity and oxidative stress. Restoring cerebral blood flow beyond 4.5 h may exacerbate reperfusion injury, explaining the attenuated benefit in delayed patients.

Treatment delay for young and middle-aged AIS patients is associated with patient economic and geographical factors

This study identified low monthly income, extended distance to healthcare facilities, illness during non-working hours, first visit medical facility is a non-three level hospital, and non-emergency admission status as factors associated with treatment delays. These factors reveal structural barriers in terms of access to emergency medical services, health literacy, and the organization of local healthcare systems.

Financial constraints not only directly influenced medical decision-making through cost-related concerns but also reflected deeper disparities in health literacy-limited economic security restricts individuals’ capacity to engage in preventive healthcare practices such as regular health screenings and chronic disease management, aligning with existing evidence on how financial burdens reshape healthcare-seeking patterns [25]. Further analysis revealed that high-income groups demonstrate significant advantages in establishing health-promoting behaviors, partially explaining the association between socioeconomic status and clinical outcomes [3].

Notably, young adult patients exhibited distinct behavioral risk profiles, showing the higher prevalence of modifiable risk factors (such as smoking, hazardous alcohol use, obesity) than non-young adults [26]. This longitudinal study population frequently misattributed acute neurological deficits to lifestyle-related discomforts, such as interpreting limb weakness as exercise-induced fatigue, resulting in a median delay time from symptom recognition to care-seeking initiation. Such symptom misattribution patterns may critically mediate care-seeking delays in younger populations through impaired risk perception mechanisms [6]. Distance to consultation (> 10 km) is associated with an increased risk of treatment delay. which may be due to inconvenient transport or insufficient emergency care capacity of primary healthcare institutions, resulting in patients being transferred to other places for consultation. Notably, Our study revealed that stroke patients experiencing onset in workplace settings demonstrate higher rates of timely medical intervention, likely attributable to the increased visibility of acute symptoms by colleagues, acquaintances, or bystanders in occupational or public environments, prompting them to seek immediate medical attention [27].

The estimate for the “emergency admission” variable was imprecise (OR = 27.99, 95% CI: 3.55–220.86) due to the extremely low number of non-emergency cases in the timely-treatment group (n = 1). Although the exact effect cannot be quantified, this strong association suggests that access to the emergency pathway may be a critical system-level bottleneck. In support of this, admission without emergency department presentation is associated with delayed treatment for patients, suggesting that a rapid response mechanism in emergency access is critical for reducing the time to treatment. According to the standard process of stroke treatment, patients are admitted to the emergency department for triage, routine blood sampling and getting an electrocardiogram are completed within 10 min, a CT scan of the brain is completed within 25 min, and all of the test reports are obtained within 45 min to clarify the type of stroke, enabling patients to start receiving effective treatment within 60 min [28]. Currently, emergency departments of tertiary care hospitals are equipped with stroke green channels as well as stroke centers, resulting in lower risk of delay for patients admitted through emergency services. This difference further highlights the crucial impact of internal healthcare system organization and resource allocation on treatment timeliness.

Treatment delay for young and middle-aged AIS patients is associated with the severity of neurological deficits

In this study, low NIHSS scores were associated with treatment delay. Patients with low NIHSS scores may be under-alert due to the mildness or non-specificity of their symptoms, which further validates the clinical phenomenon of minor strokes are easy to be overlooked [29] .

Our findings indicate that certain young adults adopt self-management strategies for neurological symptoms such as limb numbness due to stroke-related manifestations presenting without significant functional impairment in daily activities, pathognomonic alterations in physical appearance, or discernible speech deficits-clinical features that often evade detection by observers while minimizing social interaction disturbances, compounded by their tendency to attribute such paresthesia to non-specific etiologies like fatigue reactions or cervical spondylosis, thereby opting for observational monitoring rather than seeking professional medical evaluation [30].

Impact of treatment delay on prognosis and quality of survival

In this study, we found that treatment delay had a negative impact on prognosis and quality of survival 6 months after the onset of the disease. Kendall’s correlation coefficient analysis showed that treatment delay in young and middle-aged AIS patients is positively correlated with prognosis 6 months after the onset of the disease, and Spearman’s correlation coefficient analysis showed that treatment delay was negatively correlated with quality of survival 6 months after the onset of the disease.

Multivariable analysis revealed a temporal gradient between treatment delay and prognosis, with the negative association strengthening over time (adjusted OR: 0.50 at 1 month to 0.28 at 6 months). This suggests delayed consequences may amplify during long-term recovery, possibly due to impaired neural repair [31]. Although statistical significance was not reached, likely due to limited sample size, the consistent trend remains notable.

This suggests that although the difference was not significant in the short term (1 month), patients in the delayed group experienced worsening neurological deficits, increased disability, and decreased quality of survival scores over time. This is closely related to the pathological mechanisms of stroke, where cerebral blood flow (CBF) decreases in patients in the delayed group, and neuronal cells within the ischemic penumbra undergo a series of physiological and biochemical changes, such as depolarization, oxygen radical damage, and excitatory amino acid toxicity, which can last for several days. If blood flow is not restored in time, cellular ATP synthesis is impaired, which further leads to neuronal death, and the ischemic penumbra progressively deteriorates into an irreversible infarct focus [31].

In addition, treatment delay may prolong the recovery cycle, aggravate patient psychological burden (such as anxiety, depression) and lead to loss of social functioning, further reducing patient quality of life [5]. According to a survey, for every day of treatment delay attention in elderly patients, patient hospital stay is prolonged by 2 days, patients have a higher risk of poor prognosis, and the mortality rate after intravenous thrombolysis in the elderly becomes 1.3%, which is 0.8% higher than that of young and middle-aged people [32, 33]. In this study, the proportion of patients with poor prognosis at 6 months of onset in the delayed group was as high as 16.96%, which was nearly 2.4 times higher than that in the timely group (5.00%), suggesting that the treatment delay significantly affects the neurological regression of the patients, which highlights the necessity of the optimization of the emergency stroke system.

In conclusion, while short-term (1-month) effects were not significant, the negative impact of treatment delay on prognosis intensified by 6 months, revealing a clear temporal gradient. Despite sample size limitations, this pattern underscores the need for future large-scale studies with long-term follow-up to confirm this dynamic risk, which is vital for shaping stroke care and public health strategies.

Significance and limitations

This study was a prospective cohort study designed to quantify how socioeconomic barriers (low income, geographic disparities) and clinical factors (atypical symptoms, mild deficits) interact to cause treatment delays in young and middle-aged patients with AIS, and included functional and quality-of-life outcomes at 1 month, and 6 months. The findings extend prior research focused on elderly populations by highlighting age-specific challenges, including occupational demands and under-recognition of minor strokes. In view of the current situation, it is still necessary to strengthen public education on stroke symptom recognition (especially in low-income and remote areas), promote the ‘FAST’ recognition method, improve the referral mechanism between primary care institutions and tertiary care hospitals, shorten the treatment chain, optimize off-hour resource allocation, and improve the efficiency of emergency response. However, there are some limitations in this study: firstly, the samples came from a single center, so there may be selection bias, additionally, the limited sample size of the timely-treatment group restricted the number of predictors that could be included in the multivariable model and may have affected the precision of some estimates, although this study addressed this through clinically driven variable selection and parsimonious modeling, future validation in larger samples is warranted, and the estimate for the “emergency admission” variable was imprecise (OR = 27.99, 95% CI: 3.55–220.86) due to the extremely low number of non-emergency cases in the timely-treatment group (n = 1). Although the exact effect cannot be quantified, this strong association suggests that access to the emergency pathway may be a critical system-level bottleneck, warranting priority investigation in future large-sample studies. Second, the effects of psychological factors (such as shame) on delay were not explored in depth. Future multi-center, large-sample studies incorporating dynamic psychological assessment and long-term follow-up (such as more than 1 year) are needed to fully reveal the evolutionary trajectory of delay effects. Finally, the binary time threshold (≤ 4.5 h vs. >4.5 h) used in this study provides a clear benchmark for assessing access to IVT but does not capture the extended therapeutic window for EVT or the continuous nature of treatment efficacy over time. Analysis based on the total onset-to-treatment interval could not distinguish between prehospital and in-hospital delays. Therefore, conclusions regarding “delayed access” reflect a combination of systemic, patient-related, and eligibility factors. Future research should employ finer time categories and process analysis to identify specific targets for intervention.

Conclusions

Treatment delay for young and middle-aged AIS patients is a serious phenomenon, which is affected by a combination of economic, geographic, and symptomatic factors, and impairs long-term prognosis and quality of life. Clinical intervention strategies need to be developed in accordance with the social background and symptom characteristics of patients, and public awareness of stroke emergency care needs to be strengthened to minimize the time window for treatment and improve patient outcomes.
