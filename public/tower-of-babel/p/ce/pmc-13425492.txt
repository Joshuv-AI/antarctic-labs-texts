Decoding Cellular Heterogeneity with Microfluidic Single-Cell Secretion Analysis Tools
Authors: Faqin Zhao, Xiaobei Chen, Yueyue Ma, Xianwei Liang, Anqi Liu, Chengjun Wu, Shuai Yuan, Jiu Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cellular heterogeneity is a fundamental determinant of diverse physiological and pathological processes, yet its functional manifestation, the dynamic secretion of proteins, remains challenging to interrogate on the single-cell level. Traditional methods, while invaluable, are constrained by limited multiplexing capacity, inadequate temporal resolution, and reliance on specialized infrastructure, impeding the comprehensive dissection of cellular diversity. Microfluidic technologies have emerged as transformative platforms that address these limitations, enabling high-throughput, multiparameter secretion profiling with spatiotemporal precision. This review systematically synthesizes recent advances in microfluidic single-cell secretion analysis. We first examined the conceptual framework underlying cellular heterogeneity and the inherent constraints of conventional approaches. Subsequently, we analyze core microfluidic principles for single-cell manipulation, focusing on microstructures for isolation and strategies for capturing and quantifying the secreted proteins. We then explored the expanding applications of these technologies across biological and biomedical research. Finally, we discuss emerging opportunities at the convergence of microfluidics with multiomics integration and artificial intelligence, outlining future trajectories for this rapidly evolving field.

Introduction

Cellular heterogeneity is an inherent characteristic of complex biological systems that profoundly regulates physiological activities and drives the occurrence and progression of various diseases. Even cells derived from the same tissue under steady-state conditions display distinct profiles in gene expression, metabolic activity, and biological function. For instance, hematopoietic stem cells exhibit nonuniform cell fate determination, which directly reflects functional heterogeneity at the single-cell level. In tumor tissues, inter- and intratumoral heterogeneity not only alters tumor initiation, differentiation, and metastatic potential but also greatly weakens the efficacy of chemotherapy and targeted therapy. Therefore, resolving cellular heterogeneity at high resolution is the key to exploring tumor dissemination mechanisms, screening drug-resistant cell subpopulations, and promoting the development of precision medicine. ,

At present, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has become a mainstream technical means to interpret cellular heterogeneity from the transcriptional perspective. This technology realizes cell subtype classification and cell lineage tracing by isolating individual cells and acquiring whole-genome transcriptome information. Nevertheless, scRNA-seq only captures static gene expression profiles and cannot directly reflect the dynamic functional output of cells. Unlike transcriptome analysis, single-cell secretion analysis focuses on the dynamic release of proteins, which is a direct manifestation of cellular functional heterogeneity. Relevant studies have confirmed that even cell populations with highly similar transcriptional characteristics can be divided into functionally distinct subgroups according to their cytokine secretion patterns, such as Jurkat T cells with differential expression of IFN-γ, IL-2, and TNF-α. , Secreted proteins represented by cytokines, antibodies, and exosomal proteins participate in immune regulation, tumor inflammatory response, and cell metabolic reprogramming and act as core mediators of intercellular communication. The dynamic changes of secretome can intuitively reflect the real-time response of cells to external stimuli, which makes single-cell secretion analysis an indispensable supplement to transcriptome research. ,

Traditional biological research mostly adopts population-level detection methods, and the average signal of a large number of cells will mask the functional differences between individual cells and rare cell subgroups. − Classic detection techniques, including enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent spot (ELISpot), are limited by technical defects such as low temporal resolution and poor dynamic monitoring ability: ELISA relies on population-averaged signals and completely ignores cellular heterogeneity; ELISpot can realize single-cell detection but only obtains static end point data after long-term incubation, failing to monitor the continuous dynamic changes of secreted proteins. − Flow cytometry needs to use secretion inhibitors in the detection process, which will interfere with the real secretion behavior of living cells, and also cannot track the dynamic process of protein release in real time. Although scRNA-seq can finely analyze transcriptional heterogeneity due to post-transcriptional regulation, translation efficiency, and other factors, there is often an obvious decoupling between mRNA level and final protein secretion volume, so it cannot replace functional detection of secreted proteins. , The typical technical process of scRNA-seq is shown in Figure A. After mechanical or enzymatic digestion of tissues, single cells are captured by fluorescence-activated cell sorting or microfluidic devices, followed by cell lysis, reverse transcription, library construction, and sequencing analysis, and finally, cell classification and status identification are completed through bioinformatics means.

In view of the above technical bottlenecks, traditional methods are difficult to meet the comprehensive requirements of high sensitivity, high throughput, multi-index parallel detection, and long-term dynamic tracking in single-cell secretion analysis, which seriously hinders the in-depth exploration of cellular functional heterogeneity. As an emerging interdisciplinary technology, microfluidics relies on the advantages of microscale fluid manipulation, miniaturization, high integration, and low reagent consumption and has become an ideal platform to break through the limitations of traditional technologies. Microfluidic chips can efficiently capture, isolate, and manipulate single cells through microchannels, microwells, droplets, microvalves, and other microstructures and encapsulate single cells in nanoliter-to-picoliter-independent microreaction units to complete high-throughput parallel detection. , Benefiting from precise fluid control and on-chip culture systems, microfluidic platforms can simulate the in vivo microenvironment of cells for a long time, realize real-time monitoring of cell morphology, proliferation, and secretion behavior, and also build coculture models to quantitatively analyze intercellular communication. , Among them, the droplet microfluidics combined with deep learning can realize automatic and label-free single-cell sorting (Figure B), which fully reflects the high-throughput and intelligent application potential of microfluidic technology in single-cell research. In addition, novel platforms such as plasmonic microwell arrays based on microfluidic technology combine optical sensing with micronano structures and realize long-term label-free dynamic monitoring of single-cell secretion, making up for the deficiency of traditional technologies in longitudinal dynamic detection.

In summary, microfluidic technology has promoted single-cell secretion analysis from static end point detection to dynamic, multidimensional, and high-content functional research and has been widely used in immunology, oncology, stem cell biology, and other fields. This review systematically summarizes the latest research progress of microfluidic single-cell secretion analysis tools. First, it expounds the biological significance of cellular heterogeneity and the inherent defects of traditional detection methods. Second, it sorts out the core principles of microfluidic single-cell isolation, manipulation, and secreted protein capture detection. On this basis, the application progress of this technology in basic biomedicine and translational research is discussed. Then, the research status of multiomics integration based on microfluidic platforms is analyzed. Finally, the existing technical challenges and future development directions in this field are prospected, aiming to provide a systematic reference for researchers in related fields and promote the further development and clinical transformation of microfluidic single-cell secretion analysis technology.

Core Technologies of Microfluidic Single-Cell Secretion Analysis

At the heart of microfluidic single-cell secretion analysis lies the synergistic integration of two foundational technologies. The first involves the precise isolation of individual cells from heterogeneous populations coupled with their controlled manipulation and downstream processing within microfluidic environments. The second encompasses the highly sensitive and specific capture and detection of minute quantities of proteins secreted by each isolated cell. Continuous innovation and convergence within these two interconnected domains have been critically important in advancing our capacity to decode functional heterogeneity on a single-cell resolution.

Single-Cell Isolation and Manipulation Strategies

Efficient, gentle, and precise isolation of individual cells from heterogeneous populations, coupled with stable manipulation and culture, constitutes a fundamental prerequisite for successful single-cell secretion analysis. The choice of manipulation strategy profoundly influences experimental throughput, cost, information dimensionality, and application specificity. Microfluidic platforms enable single-cell isolation and manipulation through a variety of mechanisms including droplet encapsulation, microwell arrays, optical tweezers, electric field manipulation, structure-based trapping, and probe pairing. Microporous-Array-Based Approaches

Microporous-array-based strategies constitute a mainstream approach for static single-cell capture. By fabricating micrometer-scale pore arrays in polymer substrates such as polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) with dimensions tailored to match cell sizes, these systems achieve physical isolation through gravity sedimentation or weak hydrodynamic forces, enabling individual cells from suspension to randomly settle into microwells. , This approach offers several advantages, including simple device architecture, ease of fabrication, high throughput, and low cost, permitting simultaneous processing of thousands of cells. The microwells provide a stable microenvironment with excellent process control and high functional integration, facilitating complex biochemical assays. Moreover, the fixed spatial configuration of the array ensures unique positional addressing for each microwell, enabling precise tracking of detection results across time points. , This spatial registration allows researchers to perform long-term dynamic imaging of individual cells confined within microwells, supporting the real-time observation of morphological dynamics, proliferative behavior, and kinetic profiles of secretory activities. Zhang et al. developed an innovative live-cell printing technique that combines PDMS relief molds with microfluidic hook traps to achieve high-precision, high-throughput, multicell-type single-cell array printing. This method has been successfully applied to investigate gap junction communication in heterogeneous cell pairs, characterize cancer cell membrane protrusion capabilities, and enable the controlled printing and long-term culture of primary cortical neurons. The technique thus provides an efficient platform for single-cell functional analysis, intercellular interaction studies, and high-throughput screening applications.

However, the inherent limitations of this technology warrant consideration. The core challenge stems from the Poisson-distributed random nature of cell loading, which typically results in substantial vacancy rates within microchannels, thereby constraining the experimental throughput and efficiency. Furthermore, the fixed dimensions of microchannels limit adaptability to cells of varying sizes and carry the risk of multiple cells occupying a single microwell, potentially introducing cross-contamination. Such occurrences can severely confound the interpretation of single-cell behavior and compromise data reliability. Addressing these limitations, Zhou et al. developed a hierarchical loading micropore chip (HL-chip) that achieves precise cell pairing based on size differences. This design offers key advantages, including high pairing efficiency, support for simultaneous multiparameter functional detection at single-cell resolution, and an open architecture facilitating cell recovery and downstream analysis. The HL-Chip has been successfully applied to comprehensively evaluate T-cell function in adoptive cell therapy (ACT), addressing the technical bottleneck wherein conventional methods cannot simultaneously satisfy the requirements for single-cell resolution, high throughput, and multiparameter detection. In a complementary approach focused on precise manipulation and electrophysiological analysis of individual cells, particularly neurons, Zhang et al. designed and validated an integrated chip platform combining microelectrode arrays (MEAs) with dielectrophoresis (DEP) technology. This innovation provides a novel experimental tool for in vitro studies of cell–cell interactions, neuronal function, and neurological disease model construction.

Droplet-Based Microfluidics

In contrast to the static analysis afforded by microporous arrays, droplet-based microfluidics represents a paradigm for ultrahigh-throughput single-cell analysis. This approach relies on the use of two or more immiscible fluids wherein the dispersed phase is partitioned into minute volumes within a continuous phase. Droplet formation is governed by the interplay of surface tension and fluid shear stress, enabling each droplet to function as an independent microreactor that confines individual cells and their secreted proteins within an extremely small volume. This process allows for the generation of reaction units on millisecond time scales.

The advantages of this technology include the production of uniform droplets ranging from nanoliters to picoliters in their volume. Each droplet serves as an autonomous “microlaboratory,” offering high throughput with minimal reagent consumption. The system enables precise manipulation of individual cells, features straightforward preparation, and is readily scalable, thereby addressing key limitations of conventional techniques. Banijamali et al. developed a droplet barcoding sequencing approach for protein analysis, examining 121 500 individual small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) derived from nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cell lines (H1975) and patient malignant pleural effusions (MPE). This work successfully identified distinct sEV subtypes, validating the technique’s utility in resolving sEV heterogeneity across samples, tracing sEV cellular origins, and screening potential diagnostic biomarkers for NSCLC.

Traditional droplet generation methods are broadly categorized into passive and active approaches based on their reliance on an external energy input. Passive generation requires no external energy, instead utilizing microchannel geometries, fluid shear forces, and interfacial tension to form droplets. Common configurations include cross-flow (T-junction), flow-focusing, and co-flow designs, which are well-suited for applications prioritizing simplicity, high throughput, and low cost. Active generation, by contrast, employs external energy inputs to modulate the droplet formation. The core advantage of this approach lies in overcoming the inherent limitations of passive methods, offering precise control, high speed, and operational flexibility. This makes active generation particularly suitable for applications requiring fine temporal control and customized droplet manipulation. ,

Droplet microfluidics provides a highly controllable microenvironment for single-cell analysis, with particular utility for screening applications and enzymatic activity studies. Mazutis et al. developed a standardized droplet microfluidic-based protocol for single-cell analysis and sorting. By coencapsulating individual cells, antibody-coated capture beads, and fluorescent detection probes within 50 pL droplets, they achieved detection of antibodies secreted by hybridoma cells within 15 min. Fluorescence-activated droplet sorting driven by dielectrophoresis (FADS) enabled sorting throughput of approximately 200 Hz, ultimately achieving 220-fold enrichment of antibody-secreting cells. The Wu team employed modular connectors to assemble three-dimensional cross-channel structures, enabling the flexible generation of both water-in-oil (W/O) and oil-in-water (O/W) droplets without requiring specialized surface modifications. This approach resolved the issue of droplet coalescence commonly encountered in the conventional two-dimensional PDMS chips. The generated droplets can be directly collected and processed within the capillaries. This system operates at higher generation frequencies than conventional chips and enables bidirectional droplet separation, accommodating diverse downstream processing requirements.

Despite its advantages, droplet microfluidic technology remains constrained by Poisson statistics during cell encapsulation, which inherently leads to the generation of empty droplets or droplets containing multiple cells. Additionally, the absence of solid support renders the system unsuitable for in situ analysis of adherent cells. To address these limitations, researchers have leveraged inertial effects in helical microchannels to achieve an orderly arrangement of high-concentration cells into uniform flow streams. This approach enables cells to enter the droplet generation zone at equidistant intervals, facilitating one-to-one encapsulation. Dean forces generated in curved channels focus cells to a single equilibrium position, preventing cell stacking and reducing the formation of multicell droplets while significantly increasing droplet generation rates, thereby balancing efficiency with precision. ,

Additional limitations include challenges associated with emulsification technology, constraints imposed by limited clinical sample sizes, and insufficient integration with multiomics approaches. Banijamali et al. addressed some of these concerns by using microfluidic chips with precisely designed channel geometries to generate uniformly sized microdroplets, reducing the probability of multiple-cell encapsulation and minimizing mixed signal rates. This approach simultaneously increases the number of droplets per unit volume, thereby enhancing detection throughput. Notwithstanding these limitations, the inherent scalability of droplet-based microfluidics renders it a powerful tool for investigating the cellular population heterogeneity. In recent years, droplet microfluidics have been further combined with intelligent sorting algorithms to realize automated single-cell screening. Guo et al. constructed an intelligent label-free droplet microfluidic sorting system, which achieves high-efficiency single-cell encapsulation and morphology-based cell screening. Zhang et al. systematically summarized the innovative progress of droplet microfluidics and pointed out that multidimensional droplet manipulation technology has become a core direction for resolving cellular and extracellular vesicle heterogeneity.

Microchamber- and Valve-Based Systems

For single-cell secretion studies requiring precise external intervention and dynamic observation, microchamber- and valve-based systems offer uniquely powerful control capabilities. These systems are typically fabricated using multilayer soft-lithography techniques, which embed microvalves controlled by pneumatic or hydraulic pressure within elastic polymer chips. By programmatically regulating valve opening and closure, researchers can achieve precise control over fluid flow and cell positioning, enhancing both the specificity and stability of single-cell capture while establishing a foundation for downstream mass spectrometry analysis. Shi et al. developed a three-layer microfluidic chip that integrates two complementary capture mechanisms: enrichment of circulating tumor cells (CTCs) via 50 μm pore filtration, followed by EpCAM-conjugated immunomagnetic bead capture, with subsequent single-cell trapping achieved through subchannel valve closure. This design enables in situ lysis and downstream RNA sequencing from captured single cells. The essential advantages of this approach lie in its high positional addressability, dynamic controllability, and capacity for fully automated, cross-contamination-free operation. Researchers can not only precisely confine individual cells within designated chambers but also achieve real-time, fine-grained control over the cellular microenvironment.

However, this level of precision entails trade-offs in the manufacturing complexity and throughput. The multilayer soft-lithography processes required for device fabrication are technically demanding, limiting widespread adoption. Moreover, constrained by physical space and the number of valve control lines that can be integrated on a single chip, a typical experiment permits parallel analysis of only tens to hundreds of cells, a throughput substantially lower than that achievable with droplet microfluidic approaches. , Addressing some of these limitations, Yang et al. developed an innovative technology platform termed AM-DMF-SCP, which utilizes a high-density, independently addressable active matrix digital microfluidic chip. This platform fully automates and integrates all protein sample preparation steps, including single-cell isolation, lysis, and enzymatic digestion, onto a single chip, thereby significantly enhancing the efficiency, sensitivity, and accessibility of single-cell proteomic analysis. In a complementary approach, Dashti et al. proposed a hybrid electro-microfluidic platform that integrates the manipulation advantages of hydrodynamics and dielectrophoresis with fluorescence-based immunoassays. This system successfully achieved single-cell protein secretion profiling and tumor cell typing, enabling ″high-throughput, low-damage, multitarget″ analysis of single-cell secretory activity. By addressing the challenges of manipulation precision and detection specificity that have constrained traditional approaches, this technology offers an innovative solution for single-cell biology research and precision medicine applications. The platform is particularly well-suited for in-depth, dynamic functional studies involving limited numbers of cells, where the unique control capabilities of valve-based systems provide critical advantages despite throughput constraints.

Other Strategies

Beyond the three major categories of mainstream technologies discussed above, numerous innovative single-cell manipulation strategies continue to be developed to address specific experimental requirements.

Hydrodynamic trapping captures single cells by exploiting fluid resistance differences generated through specialized microchannel geometries, such as cup-shaped microstructures or serpentine channels without requiring physical barriers. This approach offers high capture efficiency and rapid operation. , Pendharkar et al. developed a three-layer microfluidic flipping chip (MFC) that combines passive hydrodynamic capture with gravitational sedimentation. This system enables efficient pairing and electrofusion of leukemia cells (THP-1) with lung cancer cells (A549), providing a highly effective platform for hybridoma generation in cancer immunotherapy research.

Surface patterning techniques, including microcontact printing and photolithography, create defined chemical or topographical patterns on substrates to guide selective cell adhesion at predetermined locations. These approaches are particularly valuable for investigating relationships between cell morphology and secretory function. Debnath et al. employed photolithography to pattern biocompatible photoresist on glass substrates, constructing arrays of concave microwells with diameters ranging from 10 to 45 μm specifically designed for single-cell capture. This approach enabled efficient cell isolation, long-term culture, and automated detection.

Furthermore, noncontact manipulation techniques, including optical tweezers, acoustic tweezers, magnetic manipulation, and dielectrophoresis, are being increasingly integrated with microfluidic platforms. These methods enable targeted cell manipulation without physical contact, minimizing mechanical damage while permitting precise isolation of specific cells from heterogeneous populations. , Dashti et al. combined hydrodynamic capture with negative dielectrophoresis-based transfer to achieve a high-throughput single-cell array assembly. When coupled with fluorescence-based immunoassays, this approach enabled the single-cell resolution profiling of protein secretion patterns. This innovation addressed the longstanding challenge in microfluidics of balancing high throughput with minimal cellular perturbation, providing a versatile tool for cancer biology research. Acoustofluidics and dielectrophoresis (DEP) are two emerging noncontact manipulation technologies widely used in single-cell analysis. Liu et al. developed an acoustofluidic device for high-efficiency sample preparation and detection of small extracellular vesicles derived from single cells, which provides a new approach for analyzing vesicle-related cellular heterogeneity. Filippi et al. established an ODEP (opto-dielectrophoresis)-based robotic system, realizing precise in-flow manipulation and analysis of primary cells and effectively avoiding mechanical damage to fragile primary cells during single-cell processing. These noncontact microfluidic technologies complement traditional droplet and micropore systems, expanding the technical toolkit for decoding cellular heterogeneity.

Methods for Capturing and Detecting Secreted Proteins

Building upon successful single-cell isolation and manipulation, a second core challenge and a central focus of methodological innovation lies in achieving highly sensitive and specific capture and detection of the minute quantities of proteins secreted by individual cells. Technological advances in this domain have primarily centered on three interconnected areas: strategies for solid-phase capture of secreted molecules, modalities for signal transduction and readout, and approaches to enhancing detection sensitivity and multiplexing capacity.

Solid-Phase Capture

Solid-phase capture represents the most mainstream and well-established technical approach for detecting secreted proteins on the single-cell level. This strategy effectively prevents diffusion and dilution of secreted proteins within the local microenvironment, thereby significantly enhancing the detection sensitivity. The Hua team developed a three-dimensional microfluidic chip, featuring an array of spindle-shaped microcolumns combined with layer-by-layer-modified biotinylated gelatin membranes. This design substantially improved the capture efficiency of the secreted autophagosomes. Using two types of quantum dots conjugated with anti-LC Chemiluminescence detection and anti-HSP60 antibodies, they achieved sensitive detection of autophagosome surface proteins, demonstrating the chip’s potential utility in cancer diagnostics. On the basis of antibody immobilization strategies, solid-phase capture methods can be broadly categorized into two principal approaches.

The first is antibody microarray technology, which involves immobilizing multiple specific capture antibodies onto a chip substrate through spot-printing techniques to create micrometer-scale antibody spot arrays. When samples containing target proteins are incubated with the chip, multiple target proteins bind specifically to the corresponding antibody spots. Following incubation, unbound impurities are removed, and a mixture of fluorescently labeled detection antibodies is introduced. This forms sandwich immune complexes at each antibody spot, enabling parallel quantitative analysis of multiple target proteins through fluorescence signal readout. The resulting fluorescence patterns reveal the multiparametric secretion profiles of the individual cells. The key strength of this method lies in its multiplexed-protein-detection capability. In their work on single-cell proteomics, Bennett et al. highlighted that the integration of next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology with microfluidic platforms is driving continuous advances in multiplexed-protein detection. This approach converts protein detection signals to amplifiable nucleic acid signals by conjugating specific antibodies with oligonucleotides bearing unique identification tags (ADTs). When combined with microfluidic droplet systems enabling efficient single-cell encapsulation and multimodal analysis, this strategy has achieved simultaneous detection of dozens of protein biomarkers.

The second approach encompasses surface modification strategies such as uniformly coating the inner walls of microchambers or the interior surfaces of droplets with capture antibodies. Rather than constructing patterned arrays, the entire surface of microchambers or microwells can be uniformly functionalized with single or mixed capture antibodies. Through chemical modification of the chip surface, this method effectively reduces nonspecific adsorption of peptides or proteins, minimizes sample loss, and enhances the depth of single-cell protein identification. , This approach offers relatively straightforward preparation and is well-suited for quantitative analysis of single or a limited number of high-abundance secreted proteins. However, it typically constrains multiplex detection capabilities and may require integration with complementary techniques for comprehensive profiling.

Signal Detection and Readout

Captured proteins require quantitative detection through sensitive signal-readout systems. Fluorescence-based detection constitutes the most widely employed approach for signal readout in single-cell secretion analysis, with the sandwich immunoassay representing a canonical example. In this configuration, target proteins captured by solid-phase immobilized antibodies are subsequently bound by detection antibodies conjugated to fluorescent dyes or fluorescent nanomaterials, forming the classic “antibody–antigen-antibody” sandwich complex. Quantification is achieved by measuring fluorescence signal intensity, which correlates with target protein concentration. , This method offers broad applicability, high sensitivity, and compatibility with standard and confocal microscopy for spatial imaging. Dashti et al. successfully employed this system to capture and quantify low-abundance proteins secreted from single cells, addressing a longstanding challenge in single-cell analysis wherein conventional immunoassays lacked sufficient sensitivity to detect minute protein quantities. However, spectral overlap fundamentally limits the number of proteins that can be detected simultaneously in fluorescence-based approaches. When multiple fluorescent dyes are used to label different antibodies, overlapping emission spectra introduce significant signal crosstalk, severely constraining multiplexing capacity. Researchers have highlighted mass cytometry (cytometry by time-of-flight, CyTOF) as an effective solution to these limitations. This technique employs antibodies conjugated to stable heavy metal isotopes rather than conventional fluorophores. Mass cytometry routinely enables simultaneous detection of approximately 40 protein targets, with maximum multiplexing capacities reaching 30–45 parameters, substantially higher throughput than conventional fluorescence-based flow cytometry. Moreover, interference between isotope signals is minimal, effectively eliminating the spectral overlap challenges inherent to fluorescent dyes. Signal crosstalk is virtually absent, substantially enhancing both the dimensionality and accuracy of single-cell protein analysis. , It is necessary to strictly distinguish two detection modes in subsequent research: static end point assays collect secretory proteins after cell incubation for a fixed period and only obtain final secretion levels; dynamic longitudinal assays continuously monitor secretion signals in real time during cell culture and capture the temporal kinetics of secretory behavior. Most conventional microfluidic assays belong to static end point detection, while plasmonic microwell arrays and partially modified microporous-array systems support real-time dynamic monitoring. Beyond conventional detection modes, the plasmonic microwell array platform developed by Hatice Altug’s group represents a transformative solution for long-term, label-free dynamic monitoring of single-cell secretion. This integrated system combines gold nanohole plasmonic substrates with PDMS microwell structures, which confine individual cells and capture secreted proteins in situ. Leveraging surface plasmon resonance (SPR) spectral shifts and machine learning-assisted cell tracking, this platform eliminates interference from cell movement and enables continuous spatiotemporal recording of protein secretion kinetics over dozens of hours at high throughput. Different from short-term dynamic assays, this technology achieves true four-dimensional (space and time) monitoring of secretory behaviors and has been successfully applied to characterize antibody secretion from hybridoma cells and rare antibody-secreting primary human cells. It overcomes the bottlenecks of traditional end point assays and short-term dynamic tools and has become a benchmark platform for studying time-dependent cellular functional heterogeneity at single-cell resolution.

Strategies for Enhancing Sensitivity and Multiplexing

To overcome the inherent spectral overlap limitations of fluorescence detection that restrict multiplexing capacity and to address diverse application requirements, a range of alternative detection methods has been developed.

Integrating microfluidic single-cell processing platforms with high-sensitivity mass spectrometry is emerging as a mainstream approach in single-cell proteomics, enabling the systematic characterization of protein expression and secretion profiles at the individual cell level. As summarized by Bennett et al., the nanoPOTS (nanodroplet one-pot system) approach reduces the sample preparation volume to the nanoliter scale through microfluidic platforms. This miniaturization minimizes contact with container surfaces, boosting single-cell protein detection sensitivity by one to 2 orders of magnitude. The technique has been successfully applied to analyze diverse cell types, including stem cells and monocytes.

Rolling circle amplification (RCA) technology achieves exponential signal amplification by converting each protein recognition event into a DNA sequence capable of hybridizing with numerous fluorescent probes, significantly lowering the detection limits. This represents one of the key methods for enhancing sensitivity in single-cell-secreted protein detection. Gebreyesus et al. summarized advances in parallel single-cell multiomics detection. By designing mRNA-specific probes for cytokines and combining them with signal amplification strategies such as LAMP (loop-mediated isothermal amplification), they achieved highly sensitive detection of low-abundance transcripts. Concurrent protein-level detection revealed post-transcriptional regulation phenomena, wherein mRNA and protein expression were discordant in certain T-cell subpopulations, providing potential technical support for identifying immunotherapy-sensitive cell populations. Such multiomics integration approaches leverage the efficient sample processing capabilities of microfluidic technology to further enhance both detection sensitivity and resolution. Furthermore, traditional enzyme-linked signal amplification methods, such as horseradish peroxidase-catalyzed precipitate formation, are widely applied in contemporary workflows.

In enhancing multiplexing capabilities, DNA-barcoded antibody technologies play a pivotal role. Once individual cells have been isolated and captured in the microchamber of the chip by using microfluidic technology, the target proteins are released through secretion or lysis. These proteins then bind specifically to DNA-encoded antibodies on the bottom of the chamber, forming complexes with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies or detection probes. Finally, by reading the barcode site signals via fluorescence imaging and combining this with the DNA-encoded information, the expression status of multiple target proteins within individual cells can be accurately identified. Petrosius et al. comprehensively reviewed antibody-DNA barcoding strategies in their recent work. This approach converts protein detection to nucleic acid signal readouts by conjugating DNA oligonucleotides with specific antibodies. It enables high-throughput single-cell protein analysis through sequencing or mass spectrometry readouts and can be integrated with transcriptomic measurements for multimodal profiling. The technology has been successfully applied to immune cell typing and tumor microenvironment studies.

Another key strategy for enhancing both specificity and multiplexing is proximity extension analysis (PEA). This technique requires a pair of antibodies targeting distinct epitopes on the same protein to bind simultaneously and with sufficient proximity. Only upon dual binding can their respective DNA oligonucleotides hybridize and serve as templates for subsequent PCR amplification or sequencing. − Vervuurt et al. applied proximity extension analysis to biomarker screening for sporadic cerebral amyloid angiopathy (sCAA), enabling the large-scale targeted profiling of sCAA-associated biomarkers. This approach validated neurofilament light chain (NFL) as a biomarker for neurodegenerative damage in sCAA, providing a new screening indicator for early disease diagnosis. This ″dual recognition″ mechanism substantially reduces background signals arising from nonspecific binding, significantly enhancing detection specificity. It is particularly well-suited for detecting low-abundance proteins in samples with complex background interference, such as biological fluids.

Decoding Heterogeneity in Biology

Microfluidic analysis of single-cell secretion profiles offers a transformative approach to investigating fundamental biological questions across multiple disciplines. By overcoming the limitations of population-averaged measurements, this technology reveals previously obscured functional heterogeneity and provides novel insights into immunology, oncology, developmental biology, and therapeutic development.

Immunology

The immune system epitomizes functional cellular heterogeneity, relying on rare antigen-specific cells to orchestrate precise and adaptive immune responses. Microfluidic secreted protein analysis has emerged as an indispensable tool for deciphering this complex system at single-cell resolution.

T-Cell/B-Cell Functional Analysis

In studies of cell-mediated immune responses, T-cell activation status and cytokine secretion profiles constitute core functional readouts. To enable precise analysis of these parameters, Wang et al. developed a novel droplet-based single-cell immunoassay platform termed Drop-SCIA, which achieves highly sensitive multiplex detection of multiple cytokines at single-cell resolution. Compared to conventional ELISpot technology operating at solid–liquid interfaces, this platform exhibits superior target molecule enrichment capabilities. Historically, the functional quality of immune cells such as T cells has been assessed by evaluating their capacity for multiparametric cytokine production upon stimulation, a property termed ″multifunctionality,″ with multifunctional cells considered more protective or effector-rich. However, such assessments have typically been static, captured at single time points. Portmann et al. employed high-dimensional flow cytometry combined with a single-cell microfluidic platform to dynamically track cytokine secretion in human CD4+ T cells over extended periods of stimulation. Their study revealed that CD4+ T cells exhibit a continuous and temporally ordered cytokine secretion program following antigen stimulation. Individual cells sequentially produced effector molecules such as TNF-α and IFN-γ at different time points, demonstrating that the vast majority of effector T cells possess intrinsic temporal multifunctional potential. These findings underscore the necessity of integrating temporal dimensions and single-cell longitudinal tracking into immune monitoring, revealing unique secretory behaviors of heterogeneous cell populations under specific stimuli and facilitating a deeper understanding of immune dynamics during therapeutic interventions and disease progression. Breukers et al. successfully implemented a fully integrated microfluidic workflow for functional screening and recovery of rare antigen-specific B cells from postimmunization mouse spleens. Their system dynamically monitored secretion profiles of thousands of individual B cells over multiple days, enabling precise identification and nondestructive recovery of target cells using ″secretion of ovalbumin-specific antibodies″ as the selection criterion. Recovered cells exhibited robust viability. Subsequent single-cell RNA sequencing not only confirmed their B-cell identity but also comprehensively revealed their BCR sequences and clonal diversity, thereby establishing direct and precise correlations between antibody secretion function and B-cell genotype at single-cell resolution. In parallel with cytokine profiling technologies, Shana Kelley’s team developed a series of microfluidic immunomagnetic sorting platforms dedicated to functional screening and recovery of rare immune cells. , These platforms couple microfluidic manipulation with magnetic nanoparticle labeling, enabling high-throughput isolation of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) based on their real-time secretory activity. Unlike traditional cell sorting methods that rely merely on surface markers, this strategy screens cells according to their actual secretory function related to antitumor immunity. It efficiently enriches potent immune cell subsets with robust secretory capabilities, while retaining cell viability for downstream functional verification and cell therapy research. This work bridges single-cell secretion analysis and translational immunology, providing a powerful tool for screening functional immune cells for adoptive cell therapies.

Immune Cell Interactions

In the study of immune cell populations, measurements at the single-cell level enabled by microfluidic droplet technology provide critical advantages over conventional population-averaged assays. This approach more accurately captures the intrinsic heterogeneity of cellular responses within immune populations, revealing functional diversity that remains obscured in bulk measurements. This approach achieves high-precision single-cell capture and pairing with exceptional reliability and success rates. Representative microfluidic single-cell pairing strategies and their applications in immunology, cancer biology, and neurology are illustrated in Figure A. For instance, Xu et al. developed a novel microfluidic cell pairing method based on spatial trapping effects at capillary probe tips. The chip reliably captures and pairs individual immune cells, such as T cells or NK cells, with single cancer cells within the same microchamber, forming one-to-one interaction units that enable direct observation of intercellular crosstalk. Beyond immune-tumor cell interactions, microfluidic single-cell secretion analysis has proven to be instrumental in deciphering communication mechanisms between neurons and immune cells. Deng et al. developed a paired single-cell secretome analysis platform specifically designed for coculturing individual neurons with macrophages. By capturing secreted signaling molecules, including cytokines and chemokines, in situ, they successfully mapped secretion-mediated intercellular interaction networks. This study revealed that neurons regulate the macrophage polarization direction through the secretion of specific chemokines, while macrophages influence neuronal functional states by releasing inflammatory mediators. Such dynamic secretory interactions play crucial roles in maintaining homeostasis within the neuroinflammatory microenvironment. Complementary to these approaches, coherent Raman scattering imaging enables real-time, noninvasive monitoring of cellular processes without exogenous labeling. By detecting dynamic changes in lipid and protein signatures, this technique allows precise tracking of cancer cell death and enables an accurate determination of the timing and efficiency of target cell killing. When integrated with microfluidic platforms, this label-free imaging modality provides an unprecedented tool for decoding the real-time signaling dialogues that determine the cellular fate within the tumor immune microenvironment.

Cancer Biology

Tumors are not monolithic entities but complex ecosystems composed of diverse components, including malignant cells, vascular endothelial cells, immune infiltrates, and stromal elements. Cancer cells within the tumor microenvironment collectively determine disease progression, therapeutic response, and mechanisms of treatment evasion. Secretory signals within the tumor ecosystem serve as the core language coordinating intercellular behavior and driving heterogeneous evolution.

Tumor Heterogeneity and Drug Resistance

Circulating tumor cells (CTCs), tumor cells that detach from primary lesions and enter the peripheral circulation, represent critical intermediaries in metastatic dissemination. Cells within tumors exhibit extensive heterogeneity, manifested as diverse genetic mutations and phenotypic characteristics. Microfluidic single-cell approaches enable integration of morphological and functional analyses, providing valuable tools for investigating tumor heterogeneity. Lim et al. demonstrated that integrating the molecular heterogeneity of primary tumors with the presence of specific cell clusters, such as CTC-leukocyte aggregates, in the circulatory system enables unprecedented precision in prognostic stratification. Their findings revealed that patients harboring aggressive molecular subtypes combined with detectable CTC clusters exhibited the poorest clinical outcomes. This strongly suggests that a tumor’s malignant potential is jointly determined by its intrinsic molecular heterogeneity and the intercellular interactions manifested during systemic dissemination. Consequently, a comprehensive assessment of heterogeneity across both local and systemic dimensions represents an essential direction for future personalized cancer management. Complementing these findings, Ko et al. discovered that detecting specific proteins, such as PD-L1 and drug-resistance-associated membrane transporters, on extracellular vesicles (EVs) secreted by individual tumor cells enables identification of subpopulations with immune evasion or drug efflux capacity before genomic alterations become detectable. This functional profiling at the single-cell level provides early indicators of the therapeutic resistance mechanisms.

Tumor Microenvironment

The tumor microenvironment (TME) comprises complex components including nearby blood vessels, immune cells, fibroblasts, signaling molecules, and the extracellular matrix (ECM). Cancer cells actively manipulate their surrounding environment to support growth and suppress immunity, with the TME playing critical roles in drug resistance, intercellular communication, and disease progression. Paracrine interactions represent major communication modalities between tumors and stromal elements. Microfluidic chips provide unique platforms for deciphering complex cellular dialogues within the TME under controlled conditions. Ren et al. established a three-dimensional engineered tumor microenvironment by coinjecting cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) microtissues into stromal chambers and malignant epithelial breast cancer cell microtissues into tumor chambers. This configuration enabled real-time monitoring of tumor-stroma and tumor-ECM interactions, demonstrating that 3D fibroblast microtissues promote tumor cell invasion through paracrine signaling molecules. To systematically decipher intercellular communication networks within the TME, Yuan et al. developed a microfluidic-based single-cell signaling mediator profiling tool. By simultaneously capturing multiple signaling molecules secreted by tumor cells, fibroblasts, and immune cells, they successfully constructed a “secretory signaling interaction network″ of the tumor microenvironment. This study revealed that specific growth factors secreted by tumor-associated fibroblasts induce tumor cells to express drug-resistance-associated membrane proteins, while inflammatory factors secreted by immune cells reciprocally regulate fibroblast activation states. Such multidirectional secretory interactions collectively drive malignant tumor progression. Complementing these findings on soluble-factor-mediated communication, Lakins et al. demonstrated that immune suppression in the tumor microenvironment arises not only from paracrine effects of soluble factors but also involves direct cell-contact-mediated mechanisms. In pancreatic cancer, cancer-associated fibroblasts act as active effector cells by highly expressing Fas ligand, which binds to Fas-expressing antigen-activated CD8+ T cells, directly inducing antigen-specific apoptosis. This physical elimination of tumor-reactive T-cell clones represents a potent immunosuppressive mechanism. Using microfluidic coculture models, such inhibitory interactions can be observed in real time and quantitatively analyzed, providing unprecedented insights into the dynamic cellular interplay that governs antitumor immunity.

Stem Cells and Developmental Biology: Mapping Fate Decision Maps

The intricate architecture of multicellular organisms emerges from precise cellular fate decisions during stem cell differentiation and embryonic development. This process is not merely a deterministic execution of genetic programs but is dynamically shaped by interactions between gene regulatory networks and intercellular signaling. Understanding the heterogeneity inherent in these processes is essential for controlling them.

Pluripotent stem cell populations exhibit intrinsic functional heterogeneity, manifested in part through differential secretion profiles, both under basal conditions and in response to differentiation cues. Individual cells within a population display distinct differentiation potentials and trajectories. Microfluidic platforms address this heterogeneity by encapsulating single stem cells within nanoliter-scale droplets or microchambers, creating isolated microreactors that enable longitudinal tracking and high-throughput analysis of secreted molecules over extended culture periods. Building upon directed differentiation and self-organizing principles, stem cells have been harnessed to construct highly complex three-dimensional organ models, organoids, in vitro. Common organoid culture systems, including submerged Matrigel culture, air–liquid interface (ALI) culture, microfluidic organoids-on-a-chip, and 3D bioprinting-based methods, are summarized in Figure B. In skin organoids, by sequentially regulating key developmental signaling pathways such as Wnt, BMP, and FGF, Hong et al. have successfully cultivated multilayered structures comprising the epidermis, dermis, hair follicles, and even melanocytes. Despite these advances, organoid models continue to face challenges including a lack of vascularization and significant batch-to-batch heterogeneity.

Microfluidic technology offers innovative solutions for these bottlenecks. Precise fluid control enables the establishment of stable growth factor gradients that guide more uniform morphogenesis. Multichannel coculture systems facilitate the introduction of endothelial cells or neurons, enabling the construction of vascularized or innervated ″organ-on-a-chip″ models. Furthermore, the high-throughput capacity of microfluidic platforms permits the standardized, scalable production of organoids suitable for drug screening applications.

Therapeutic Antibody and Cell Therapy Development

Microfluidic single-cell secretion analysis has emerged as a pivotal technology in pharmaceutical development and biomanufacturing quality control, owing to its core advantages: precise microvolume manipulation, single-cell resolution, high-throughput integration, biomimetic microenvironment simulation, and operationally compliant workflows.

Antibody Discovery

The discovery of therapeutic antibodies from human donors or immunized animals typically requires screening millions of B cells to identify rare clones with the desired specificities. Droplet-based microfluidic platforms enable high-throughput screening and sorting of antibody-secreting cells, fundamentally transforming this discovery paradigm. Das et al. developed an ultrahigh-throughput antibody screening platform based on droplet encapsulation and fluorescence-activated droplet sorting (FADS). By coencapsulating single B cells with their secreted products and detection reagents within microfluidic reactors, they achieved direct and efficient isolation of functionally specific antibody clones from complex immune cell libraries. This approach provides a powerful tool for discovering neutralizing antibodies against rapidly mutating pathogens, such as HIV and influenza viruses, as well as rare tumor neoantigen-specific antibodies.

CAR-T/TCR-T Cell Therapy Evaluation

In quality control and efficacy assessment of CAR-T cell therapy products, traditional batch-based assays, such as ELISA for supernatant cytokine quantification, provide only population-averaged measurements, failing to capture the complex functional heterogeneity inherent in engineered cell populations. Xue et al. employed a microfluidic single-cell multifactor secretion analysis platform to capture multiparametric cytokine secretion profiles, including IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-2, and MIP-1α, from thousands of individual cells following antigen stimulation. This study visually demonstrated, for the first time, the functional response diversity within CAR-T cell populations at single-cell resolution. Results revealed highly heterogeneous cellular responses even within standardized production batches, spanning a continuum from nonresponsiveness to simultaneous secretion of three or more effector molecules. Crucially, the study established a significant correlation between the proportion of multifunctional T-cell subsets and in vitro cytotoxic activity, suggesting their potential as predictive biomarkers for product efficacy. To precisely analyze CAR-T cell killing potential at the single-cell level, Wong et al. developed a droplet microfluidic system that coencapsulated individual CAR-T cells with single tumor target cells, enabling time-lapse imaging over several hours. This technology achieved the first continuous, quantitative measurement of CAR-T cell killing kinetics at single-cell resolution, revealing substantial heterogeneity in cytotoxic efficacy even among cells derived from the same CAR-T product. This “one-on-one” precision assessment paradigm not only provides finer-grained functional readouts for optimizing CAR-T design but also establishes a robust in vitro analytical platform for predicting cell therapy product efficacy in preclinical settings.

The high-throughput screening capacity of droplet microfluidics is equally applicable to another critical area of cell therapy: the discovery of antigen-specific T cells. Wang et al. developed an integrated platform that coencapsulates individual T cells with antigen-presenting cells. By detecting IFN-γ secreted by activated T cells within droplets, captured using fluorescent reporter beads, the platform enables functional identification and sorting of antigen-specific T cells. This system processes thousands of droplets per second and physically recovers functionally positive single live T cells for subsequent expansion and TCR sequencing. Such technology enables efficient screening of rare T-cell clones targeting individualized neoantigens from tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), significantly accelerating the development of personalized TCR-T therapies. Furthermore, leveraging its highly integrated architecture, microfluidic platforms offer innovative solutions to the longstanding challenge in personalized immunotherapy: whether to prioritize neoantigen identification or TCR determination. Li et al. developed an ingenious integrated system enabling simultaneous discovery of functional neoantigens and their corresponding T-cell receptors. Using a ″tripartite coencapsulation″ approach, they packaged antigen-presenting cells loaded with diverse neoantigen candidates, patient-derived T cells, and Jurkat-NFAT fluorescent reporter cells within individual microfluidic droplets. When T cells recognize cognate neoantigen via their TCR, they activate the reporter system within the droplet, generating a fluorescent signal that enables functional droplet sorting. Following sorting, single-cell sequencing technology permits simultaneous reading of both neoantigen sequences and paired TCR sequences from the same droplet, achieving precise antigen-receptor matching. This technology establishes an efficient, controllable pathway for rapidly constructing personalized neoantigen-TCR pairing maps directly from patient tumor samples, thereby accelerating the development of individualized immunotherapies.

Integrating Multiomics: Beyond Secretomics

Single-cell secretome analysis reveals cellular heterogeneity and directly reflects the functional states. However, a comprehensive understanding of this heterogeneity necessitates multiomics integration strategies. Data derived from different molecular layers, including transcriptomics, proteomics, and secretomics, exhibit substantial differences while providing complementary information; no single omics approach can offer a complete view. , Secretory processes are characterized by high dynamism and intricate regulation, governed by complex networks that integrate extracellular signals with intracellular programs. Cells respond to environmental cues and release specific proteins through a precise spatiotemporal control. Consequently, reliance solely on static transcriptomic measurements or isolated secretome snapshots fails to capture temporal patterns of secretory behavior or real-time responses to microenvironmental perturbations, underscoring the necessity for integrated analytical approaches that transcend single-dimensional data. Microfluidic technology addresses this limitation by providing a powerful platform for integrated analysis of the secretome and transcriptome from the same single cell, thereby enabling elucidation of molecular mechanisms underlying observed secretory behaviors.

Linking Secretome and Transcriptome

The core scientific value of integrating secretome and transcriptome analyses lies in their capacity to directly investigate how cells translate their genetic blueprints into functional outputs. To achieve this, SEC-seq technology isolates single cells using nanovials and microcavity hydrogel particle arrays. A landmark technology bridging secretome and transcriptome at single-cell resolution is secretome-transcriptome sequencing (SEC-seq), proposed by Dino Di Carlo and colleagues. This platform utilizes hydrogel nanovials with microcavity structures to trap individual cells and locally capture their secreted proteins via antibody-functionalized surfaces. Secreted proteins are labeled with oligonucleotide-barcoded probes, which can be synchronized with single-cell RNA-library construction and sequencing workflows. Using SEC-seq, researchers can simultaneously obtain matched secretory profiles and whole transcriptome data from the same single cell. Multiple studies based on this technology have verified a weak correlation between gene transcription levels and final protein secretion abundance, fully demonstrating the prevalence of post-transcriptional and translational regulation in secretory processes. This platform has been applied to mesenchymal stromal cells, B lymphocytes, and other cell types, laying a solid technical foundation for dissecting heterogeneous secretory phenotypes and their underlying transcriptional regulatory networks. SEC-seq uses nanovials to capture secreted proteins and perform single-cell RNA sequencing simultaneously. , A fundamental insight emerging from such integrated analyses is that cellular secretion represents an independent and finely regulated phenotypic layer. Studies demonstrate that secretion intensity often exhibits a minimal correlation with corresponding gene transcription levels. For example, VEGFA protein secretion in mesenchymal stem cells shows no significant association with VEGFA mRNA expression, revealing the critical role of post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms. This integrated approach not only aids in discovering novel cell subpopulations and their functions in fields such as immunology but also provides direct validation of the ″organizer″ roles played by specific cell populations in developmental biology. In a recent study of human tooth germ development, such linked analysis compellingly demonstrated that dental epithelial cells drive secretion of factors including WNT5B through unique transcriptional programs, thereby directing mesenchymal cell fate and elucidating core mechanisms of tooth morphogenesis. In the context of paracrine signaling regulation, Deng et al. systematically deciphered the cellular diversity underlying paracrine communication within complex populations by integrating microfluidic single-cell secretion analysis with transcriptomic sequencing. This study identified three functionally distinct subpopulations with unique secretory characteristics, each participating in distinct phases, initiation, amplification, and termination of paracrine signaling pathways. This discovery further substantiates the independence of the secretome as a functional phenotype and provides a prototypical example of multiomics integration for deciphering molecular mechanisms of intercellular communication. Such strategies clearly demonstrate how correlating functional outputs with gene expression reveals hidden functional cell subpopulations and their regulatory networks during disease or developmental processes, thereby offering novel targets for decoding life processes and advancing disease diagnosis and therapy.

Coanalysis of Secretome and Surface Phenotype

The integrated analysis of the secretome and surface phenotype enables precise assignment of cellular identity and function at single-cell resolution. The technical principle involves sequential or parallel detection: following capture and labeling of secreted proteins, immunofluorescent staining is performed using antibodies targeting cell surface markers. By integrating fluorescence imaging with spatially encoded positional information, precise matching between secreted protein signals and surface phenotype signals is achieved, thereby establishing direct linkages between functional output and cellular identity. The efficacy of this strategy rests on a core biological principle: a cell’s surface proteome serves as a reliable indicator of its intrinsic state and functional potential. This concept has been robustly validated in studies of human pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hPSC-CMs), wherein immunophenotyping based on specific surface markers, such as SIRPA and VCAM1, represents a key strategy for addressing cellular population heterogeneity and identifying functionally distinct subpopulations. For instance, CD36 has been validated as a marker of cardiomyocyte maturity, with highly expressing subpopulations exhibiting more mature metabolic and electrophysiological characteristics. The technical capability for performing such multimodal correlation analyses has advanced substantially with microfluidic platform developments. Jung et al. developed an integrated microfluidic chip employing high-throughput single-cell capture combined with barcoded immunosensing technologies. This device, featuring 1376 parallel reaction chambers, enables simultaneous cell capture and multiplexed detection of both secreted and intracellular proteins at single-cell resolution (Figure A). Such technological advances validate the cross-disciplinary utility of this research paradigm for deciphering cellular functional heterogeneity through integrated surface phenotyping and secretory profiling. Combining surface phenotype readouts with direct measurements of secretory function, such as cytokine release, provides a powerful framework for precisely analyzing functional subpopulations, particularly within the immune system. This integrated strategy holds promise for elucidating the distinct functional response mechanisms of CD4+ T cell subpopulations under specific stimuli, enabling unprecedented resolution in the interpretation of cellular heterogeneity.

Integrative Analysis of Secretome and Cellular Metabolism

Single-cell proteomics and metabolomics offer an irreplaceable value for dynamically and comprehensively mapping cellular phenotypes and behavioral patterns. Proteins serve as the critical link between genetic information and biological function, not only executing vital processes but also regulating complex signaling networks. Protein expression exhibits broader dynamic ranges, greater stability, and higher tolerance to transcriptional noise compared to transcripts, enabling a more reliable reflection of cellular states. Metabolites, in turn, play pivotal roles in energy metabolism, signal transduction, and regulation of life processes, directly and sensitively reflecting cellular responses to environmental or internal signals that drive phenotypic transitions. , Consequently, systematic single-cell analysis of proteins and metabolites has emerged as a critical approach for unraveling mechanisms underlying key biological processes such as circulating tumor cell metastasis and stem cell fate determination.

Microfluidic technology provides a robust platform for the integrated analysis of single-cell secretomes and metabolomes. Its core advantage lies in isolating individual cells within independent nanoliter- or picoliter-scale microenvironments through droplet encapsulation or microcavity arrays, while enabling precise temporal control of culture conditions, such as drug stimulation, via integrated microvalves. ,, This fine-grained manipulation of cellular microenvironments establishes a foundation for studying cell–cell interactions and dynamic cellular responses to external stimuli. Because of its efficient single-cell manipulation and processing capabilities, microfluidic technology is now recognized as a key tool driving advances in single-cell proteomics and metabolomics.

The integration of microfluidic platforms with high-sensitivity mass spectrometry represents a pivotal strategy for achieving untargeted, multiomics detection of metabolic states and secretory functions at single-cell resolution. Single-cell operations (such as separation, capture, and metabolite extraction) are performed using microfluidic chips incorporating droplet microfluidics, hydrodynamic microfluidics, microporous arrays, and microvalves, combined with matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry (MALDI-MS), liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS), and capillary electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (capillary ESI-MS), to perform single-cell proteomics, metabolomics, and lipidomics analyses. Mass spectrometry, with its high sensitivity and untargeted analysis capabilities, has become indispensable for single-cell metabolomics and proteomics research. Microfluidic technology, through precise sample handling, significantly reduces sample loss and enables efficient interfacing with mass spectrometers, successfully extending the powerful analytical capabilities of mass spectrometry to the single-cell level. − This integration enables researchers to simultaneously obtain comprehensive metabolite and protein profiles, including secreted proteins, from the same cell populations, thereby directly deciphering molecular mechanisms by which intracellular metabolism drives specific secretory behaviors. For instance, in coculture models of tumor cells and tumor-associated fibroblasts, such integrated approaches precisely resolve the metabolic interdependencies established through metabolite exchange, including lactate and amino acids, and further reveal how this metabolic reprogramming dynamically influences changes in secreted protein profiles, such as cytokines and growth factors. , The application of microfluidic tools offers new perspectives for studying complex systems like circulating tumor cells, with multiparameter analysis capabilities aiding in the revelation of cellular heterogeneity and intercellular communication. This integration of multimodal data provides direct and systematic experimental evidence for identifying key signaling pathways that drive disease progression within the complex microenvironments.

Prospects for Multiomics Integration

The fundamental challenge in multiomics integration lies in the compatibility of sample processing requirements across different detection modalities. For instance, single-cell RNA sequencing necessitates cell lysis for RNA extraction, whereas secreted protein detection requires maintaining cell viability and integrity; simultaneously, immunostaining procedures may interfere with protein detection. To resolve these conflicts, microfluidic chips with spatially partitioned designs enable nondestructive capture of secreted proteins, followed by subsequent RNA extraction from the same cell, thereby achieving dual-omics integration. Furthermore, sequential analysis strategies effectively prevent interference between different chemical steps by processing samples in temporally staged workflows, representing a key innovation for enhancing methodological compatibility. Future advances will require the development of gentler and more universal sample processing methodologies to minimize the disruption of native molecular information within cells, thereby supporting more precise analysis of cellular heterogeneity.

Another significant advance in this field is the development of universal barcoding systems. Such technologies aim to assign uniform, readable labels, such as DNA barcodes, to molecules across different modalities (e.g., mRNA, DNA, and proteins), enabling integrated analysis on a single platform. For example, deterministic barcoding in tissue sequencing (DBiT-seq) successfully achieved spatial cosequencing of transcriptome (mRNA) and chromatin accessibility (ATAC-seq) on the same tissue section by constructing a two-dimensional barcode grid on the tissue surface. , Similarly, spatial cellular indexing of transcriptomes and epitopes by sequencing (spatial-CITE-seq) utilizes antibody-derived DNA tags (ADTs), followed by two rounds of spatial barcoding and in situ reverse transcription to generate cDNA suitable for paired-end sequencing. This approach converts protein detection into sequenceable signals, enabling in situ comapping of the entire transcriptome alongside highly abundant proteins (Figure B). These techniques demonstrate that “translating” diverse molecular information into universal DNA sequence information represents a key direction for achieving efficient, high-throughput multiomics integration.

From an applied perspective, the powerful potential of spatial multiomics integration has begun to unfold. By integrating spatial transcriptomics and metabolomics, researchers successfully revealed malignant progression from carcinoma in situ to invasive fronts exhibiting partial epithelial-mesenchymal transition characteristics in oral squamous cell carcinoma tissues derived from submucosal fibrosis, identifying key metabolic reprogramming events within these transitions. Furthermore, spatial multiomics single-cell profiling of the human maternal-fetal interface successfully deciphered the complete trajectory of trophoblast differentiation, deepening our understanding of mechanisms underlying pregnancy-related disorders. These studies collectively demonstrate the immense potential of spatial multiomics approaches for constructing high-resolution molecular atlases, decoding disease heterogeneity, and revealing intercellular interactions within native tissue contexts.

Current Challenges and Future Prospects

Despite the formidable capabilities of microfluidic single-cell analysis technologies, their potential to comprehensively resolve cellular heterogeneity remains constrained by several interconnected challenges. These primarily include limitations in detecting low-abundance secreted proteins from individual cells, the complexity of integrating high-throughput data generation with robust bioinformatics analysis, and the lack of standardized protocols for clinical translation. Overcoming these obstacles will not only deepen our systematic understanding of cellular functional diversity and microenvironmental interactions but will also profoundly reshape the landscape of precision medicine and fundamental biological research, driving a paradigm shift from “describing heterogeneity” toward “regulating heterogeneity”.

Technical Challenges

Several critical technical challenges persist in single-cell protein secretion analysis. Low-abundance or transiently secreted proteins are often rapidly diluted upon entering the extracellular environment, resulting in extremely low local concentrations that conventional detection methods struggle to capture and quantify effectively. For instance, while flow cytometry enables rapid single-cell analysis, its reliance on fluorescent labeling imposes fundamental sensitivity constraints, typically requiring high cell inputs (>5000 cells) that render it unsuitable for processing precious clinical samples such as circulating tumor cells. Mass spectrometry circumvents spectral overlap limitations but lacks the sensitivity required for reliable detection of low-abundance targets.

Recent advances have provided synergistic solutions spanning both the ″detection environment″ and the ″detection method″ to overcome these sensitivity limitations. At the environmental level, novel microporous scaffolds counteract signal dilution through physical confinement effects. Research demonstrates that microporous annealed particle gels fabricated using microfluidic technology can effectively segregate individual cells, trapping secreted factors within the interconnected pores between the microspheres. This spatial confinement achieves substantial local enrichment of secreted proteins, establishing a foundation for high-sensitivity detection. At the methodological level, innovations such as digital microfluidics (DMF)-Protein-seq have enabled transformative advances in detection sensitivity. By converting protein signals into amplifiable DNA sequences via DNA-barcoded antibodies, these techniques leverage the power of sequencing to push detection limits to the single-molecule level, approximately 50 protein molecules per cell. The incorporation of unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) to correct amplification bias enables precise quantification of low-abundance proteins with unprecedented accuracy. Critically, DNA-encoding strategies exemplified by DMF-Protein-seq simultaneously address both low-abundance detection challenges and the throughput bottleneck inherent in high-dimensional protein analysis. By tagging each target protein with a unique DNA barcode, this approach liberates detection from the constraints of finite spectral channels instead of exploiting DNA sequence diversity for multiplex identification. This enables simultaneous analysis of dozens or even hundreds of proteins in a single assay without sacrificing single-cell throughput, providing a powerful tool for constructing comprehensive single-cell protein secretion profiles.

Significant challenges also persist in achieving the dynamic monitoring of single-cell secretion. Conventional end point assays based on fixed-cell labeling provide only static snapshots, inevitably disrupting cellular physiology and failing to capture the temporal dynamics of protein secretion. Moreover, existing high-throughput platforms, while capable of large-scale single-cell encapsulation, predominantly rely on oil-phase droplet systems that preclude long-term real-time observation. Conversely, technologies with real-time imaging capabilities often face inherent trade-offs in throughput and cell capture efficiency. The microfluidic systems essential for dynamic monitoring face fundamental technical bottlenecks. Achieving long-term, automated real-time monitoring requires highly integrated fluid control, environmental maintenance, and detection modules. However, existing systems typically depend on bulky external pumps, microscopes, and computers, substantially hindering stable system integration and user-friendly operation, thereby rendering unattended long-term dynamic monitoring difficult to realize. Simultaneously, a key prerequisite for “nondestructive” monitoring is that chip materials must not interfere with cellular physiology or adsorb target molecules. Yet PDMS, the most commonly used material in academic microfluidics, exhibits strong adsorption of small molecules and proteins, severely compromising the fidelity of secreted signal detection. Thus, achieving nondestructive, real-time monitoring with high temporal resolution while maintaining cell viability and effectively correlating secretion dynamics with cellular phenotypes remains a formidable challenge.

Parallel to these technical hurdles, new challenges have emerged in the data analysis. The massive high-dimensional data sets generated by high-throughput single-cell technologies render it increasingly difficult to distinguish genuine biological signals from background noise. Furthermore, existing computational methods remain insufficient for fully deciphering complex biological processes such as cell–cell interactions and the spatiotemporal dynamics of signaling pathways. In immunology and high-throughput screening applications, single-cell-secreted protein data, including cytokines and antibodies, generated by microfluidic platforms exhibit characteristic high dimensionality and extreme sparsity. These properties pose unique challenges to conventional analytical tools, necessitating the development of novel bioinformatics algorithms and visualization strategies specifically tailored to these data characteristics. Collectively, these cross-domain and cross-data-type bottlenecks underscore an urgent need for more efficient and robust data integration and modeling strategies.

Future Outlook

In future development, the field of single-cell secretion analysis is poised for systematic breakthroughs spanning from technical principles to application paradigms. This trajectory is expected to unfold along four interrelated dimensions: deep technological integration, expanded clinical translation, enhanced spatial resolution, and the establishment of standardized technical ecosystems. Collectively, these advances will propel the field’s evolution from a specialized research tool toward a routine analytical platform.

Multidisciplinary convergence will serve as a core driver of technological innovation. Integrating microfluidic platforms with nanotechnology holds promise for surpassing current sensitivity limits through the design of novel nanoprobes, such as surface plasmon resonance-enhanced gold nanoparticle sensors, capable of capturing low-abundance, transient secretion events with high signal-to-noise ratios. Artificial intelligence (AI) is mainly applied to mine high-dimensional single-cell secretion data sets, automatically classify cell subpopulations based on secretory profiles, and screen disease-related secretory biomarkers. AI-assisted data analysis effectively solves the problem of the difficult interpretation of massive secretion data generated by high-throughput microfluidic platforms. Zhong et al. proposed a cell-specific causal network entropy method to identify critical functional states of heterogeneous cell populations, which can be combined with microfluidic secretion profiles to deeply interpret the dynamic changes of cell functions. The integration of microfluidic hardware and AI algorithms will greatly improve the efficiency and accuracy of the single-cell heterogeneity research. These technologies not only automate the identification of complex secretion patterns and cellular states but also extract underlying biological principles and disease biomarkers from massive data sets, transforming raw measurements into interpretable knowledge and substantially enhancing research depth and efficiency.

At the clinical application level, trends toward miniaturization, integration, and cost reduction will accelerate the penetration of these technologies into precision medicine and public health domains. The convergence of microfluidic technology with liquid biopsy concepts is driving the development of portable, integrated diagnostic devices. Such platforms can efficiently enrich rare targets, such as circulating tumor cells, and analyze their specific secretion profiles in situ, providing robust point-of-care testing solutions for early cancer detection, classification, and dynamic monitoring. This will enable precision medicine to transcend the limitations of traditional centralized laboratories, potentially benefiting broader patient populations.

Beyond analysis of individual cells, research perspectives are progressively expanding to encompass tissue and microenvironment scales, giving rise to ″spatially resolved secretomics.” This emerging field aims to overcome the spatial information blind spots of conventional secretion analysis by correlating quantitative secretion data with spatial distribution within native tissues or organoids. Through integration of microfluidic manipulation with high-resolution mass spectrometry imaging, it is becoming possible to visualize ″chemical maps″ of intercellular communication within tumor microenvironments, immune synapses, or developing organoids. , This approach opens fundamentally new avenues for understanding spatial coordination mechanisms of cell populations during physiological and pathological processes, substantially enhancing the biological relevance of the in vitro models.

To achieve widespread implementation of these advanced applications and transition single-cell secretion analysis from laboratory settings to broader research and clinical contexts, two major practical hurdles must be addressed: standardization and commercialization. Currently, the field lacks unified standards for chip design, experimental procedures, data generation, and data analysis. Results obtained from different platforms and laboratories often cannot be directly compared or integrated. Consequently, establishing a standardized framework encompassing the entire technological workflow is urgently needed to enhance the data reliability, reproducibility, and interoperability. Simultaneously, widespread technology adoption requires a mature product ecosystem. Only through deep collaboration among industry, academia, and clinical research can user-friendly, stable, and cost-effective integrated systems, including supporting instrumentation, consumable chips, and data analysis software, be developed. Such advances will substantially lower barriers to entry, enabling broader communities of researchers and clinicians to use these powerful tools. When single-cell secretion analysis becomes sufficiently convenient, reliable, and accessible, its full potential can be realized across basic research, disease diagnosis, and health-monitoring applications.

Conclusion

Microfluidic single-cell secretion analysis represents a paradigm shift in biomedical research, transitioning the field from static, population-averaged measurements to dynamic, single-cell resolution functional interrogation. This technology enables the precise quantification of secreted proteins at the individual cell level, systematically revealing cellular subpopulations and their functional diversity that remain obscured by conventional population-averaged methods. A key advantage lies in its seamless integration with complementary multiomics technologies, particularly single-cell transcriptomics, enabling correlative ″protein phenotype-genotype″ analysis within the same cell. This capability directly links real-time functional outputs to the underlying gene regulatory networks that govern them.

In complex biological systems such as the tumor microenvironment, this approach enables the direct correlation of specific genetic alterations with dynamic changes in cellular secretion profiles. Such integrated analysis not only deepens our mechanistic understanding of tumor heterogeneity but also provides experimental evidence for discovering disease-specific biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets. Furthermore, microfluidic secretion analysis demonstrates significant application potential in deciphering intercellular communication networks, dynamically monitoring signal transduction processes, and detecting trace biomarkers for an early disease diagnosis.

In summary, microfluidic single-cell secretion analysis establishes a powerful framework for interrogating secretory activity with functional, dynamic, and single-cell precision. By providing critical insights into cellular heterogeneity and functional diversity, this technology is poised to serve as a bridge connecting frontier life science research with clinical precision medicine, accelerating the translation of fundamental discoveries into diagnostic and therapeutic applications.
