Optimization and validation of a linear appraisal system for zoometric traits in dromedary camels
Authors: Carlos Iglesias Pastrana, Francisco Javier Navas González, Elena Ciani, José Manuel León Jurado, Juan Vicente Delgado Bermejo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLinear appraisal scales focus on the qualitative assessment of specific traits along a standardized scale across animal populations. By minimizing errors and optimizing measurement procedures, these scales streamline resource allocation, saving time and costs for livestock managers and breeders, thereby facilitating informed breeding decisions and promoting genetic progress. A total of 130 adult, healthy dromedary camels (Camelus dromedarius) of the Canarian Camel breed were evaluated across three representative locations in Spain (Huelva, Almería, and Fuerteventura). The study aimed to optimize and validate a linear appraisal system (LAS) for assessing zoometric traits in dromedaries. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) revealed that none of the zoometric variables considered were discardable due to their ability to explain variability in the sample. However, some zoometric variables lacked statistically significant representation across one or more levels within the linear appraisal scale. Therefore, a proposal for optimizing the linear appraisal scale involves reducing the number of initially proposed linear categories for these variables. Correlation analysis revealed high internal consistency (Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) ≥ 0.7), confirming significant agreement between zoometric variability-based scales and linear appraisal traits, thus validating the LAS. Notably, interobserver reliability varied across zoometric traits, with lower agreement (ICC < 0.5) observed in apical body regions, which are more prone to movement during evaluation. Overall, our findings underscore the potential of LAS as a robust tool for zoometric evaluation in dromedary camels, aiding in the selection of superior individuals for breeding. In this context, the LAS provides a valuable framework to improve phenotypic characterization of dromedary camels, a livestock species of growing global interest due to its resilience to climate change and its longstanding role as a socio-economic cornerstone in arid and semi-arid production systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11250-025-04628-9.Keywords: Phenotypic assessment, Zoometric evaluation, Linear appraisal, Camelus dromedarius

Introduction

The morphological conformation of livestock has gained increased attention due to the significant relationship between zoometric traits and productive functions (Anisimova et al. 2018; Kugonza et al. 2011; Sánchez-Guerrero et al. 2016). While aesthetic criteria have traditionally defined the harmony between body regions and phenotypic standardization within breeds (Holloway 2005), functional criteria may offer greater value for selection purposes, as they emphasize traits that enhance adaptation, productivityd, and overall performance (Dahiya et al. 2020; El-Gendy et al. 2014).

The linear appraisal scoring system collects predictive and objective information on morpho-functional traits through independent visual assessments, translating biological variability using a predefined point scale (Janssens and Vandepitte 2004). This scale must be sufficiently comprehensive to capture the full range of phenotypic expression while minimizing subjectivity (Breen 2009; Janssens et al. 2004; Vostrý et al. 2009). In this regard, Fernández Álvarez et al. (2020) proposed a simplification of the on-farm panel of zoometric variables and linear appraisal protocol while seeking the lowest loss of accuracy for dairy goats (Pares-Casanova et al. 2013; Vincent et al. 2014). Panel optimization and validation through the assessment of statistically significant correlations between zoometric measurements and linear trait scores can ensure the capacity of linear appraisal systems to reliably reflect actual phenotypic variability. Similarly, for sport horses, Gómez et al. (2006) demonstrated that zoometric measurements were significantly correlated with their respective linear type traits. However, such relationships have not been explicitly addressed in other livestock species such as cattle or sheep, despite the widespread use of linear appraisal schemes to inform breeding decisions (Dubey et al. 2014; Janssens, Winandy, Tylleman, Delmotte, Van Moeseke and Vandepitte 2004). This gap highlights the importance of validating whether linear traits effectively reflect the underlying phenotypic structure they are intended to represent —an essential step for their accurate and meaningful application in livestock improvement programs.

Within this context, the dromedary camel (Camelus dromedarius) is gaining prominence as an emerging livestock species due to its exceptional adaptation to arid environments, multifunctionality, and increasing socio-economic importance in the face of climate change (Abri and Faye 2019). Historically essential to the livelihoods of communities in arid and semi-arid regions, camels are now experiencing renewed interest as a sustainable source of meat, milk, transport, and cultural value. Despite this growing relevance, the species remains underrepresented in standardized phenotypic evaluation frameworks. Challenges related to animal temperament and handling, as well as the absence of validated, low-impact assessment tools, have limited objective selection and breeding strategies (Alhajeri et al. 2021). While isolated proposals for linear appraisal have been made—for example, for evaluating the mammary system (Ayadi et al. 2016) and various desirable milk production attributes (Dioli et al. 2023)—no studies to date have validated linear scoring systems in camels against objective morphometric data at the whole-body level. Developing such tools is essential for improving genetic selection, promoting conservation, and enhancing productivity in camel populations increasingly valued for their resilience in climate-stressed production systems.

Consequently, the present study aims to optimize and validate a linear appraisal system for assessing zoometric traits in dromedary camels of the ‘Canarian Camel’ breed. This camel breed, primarily selected for leisure riding purposes, is also increasingly being redirected toward milk production. Although originally from the Canary Islands, the breed is currently distributed not only across the archipelago but also in mainland Spain and in several Central and Eastern European countries. The study focuses on evaluating the replicability of linear trait scores and their correlation with objective zoometric measurements, in order to determine whether these visual assessments accurately capture phenotypic variability. By cross-validating subjective and objective indicators of morphology, we aim to reinforce the scientific robustness of this tool for practical use in farm conditions. To the authors’ best knowledge, this is the first report to establish and validate such a system in dromedary camels. Developing a species-specific LAS represents a significant step toward standardizing phenotype collection in camels, with implications for more informed breeding, enhanced productivity, and sustainable herd management under increasingly climate-challenged scenarios.

Materials and methods

Animal sample

Data collection occurred between September 2019 and August 2020 and involved 130 ‘Canarian Camel’ breed individuals (58 females and 72 males). These camels were situated in three representative locations where Canary camels are bred: Huelva (Doñana National Park, coordinates 36.972330, − 6.427498), Almería (coordinates 36.902180, − 2.429520), and Fuerteventura (coordinates 28.186777, − 14.158361), all within Spain. To ensure the suitability of the animals for the study, thorough clinical examinations were conducted, ensuring the animals’ well-being and eligibility. Birth data for all animals was provided by each owner and registered at inspection of the animals’ sanitary status prior to field experiences. Additionally, to mitigate potential bias stemming from the animals’ sexual status at the time of sampling, sexually immature individuals below 3 years of age were excluded. Furthermore, only non-pregnant female camels were included in the study to prevent potential bias in zoometric measurements in the thoracoabdominal region due to pregnancy (Yakubu et al. 2011). Data collection and preparation

Zoometric traits

A total of 30 zoometric measurements were taken from each animal as indicated by Iglesias Pastrana et al. (2022). Zoometric measurements included linear and tridimensional zoometric traits from head, neck, thorax, dorsum, hump, rump, tail, extremities, and feet. Once the quantitative values for the various zoometric variables were recorded across the study population, a 5-category scale was constructed for each variable. To create this scale, the difference between the maximum and minimum values obtained within the population was calculated. This difference was then divided by 5 to establish equal numerical distances for each of the 5 qualitative categories, ensuring that all categories had the same amplitude. Subsequently, for each individual, the corresponding category was assigned based on the qualitative value of each variable according to the specific scale developed.

These scales were developed with the objective of creating a dataset for statistical analysis to determine the correlation between the constructed 5-point scale, derived from the biological (phenotypic) variation present in zoometric traits within the study population, and the qualitative 5-point scale used for linear appraisal of zoometrics (see subsection Linear appraisal system (LAS)). This comparison will aid in determining the effectiveness of the linear appraisal scales in accurately reflecting the existing phenotypic variation for zoometric traits.

Linear appraisal system (LAS)

In light of the absence of a standardized protocol for linear appraisal traits specifically tailored for leisure camels, this study has undertaken the systematic selection of such traits (n = 29). The validity of the defined traits has been rigorously assessed throughout the optimization and validation process of the linear appraisal system (see subsections LAS optimization: dimensionality reduction and LAS validation: zoometric variability-based scales vs. linear appraisal traits). Each linear trait was evaluated on-field by 6 trained operators (appraisers), using a specific scale ranging from 1 to 5 points. Statistical analyses

A priori assumption testing

Firstly, the assumption of normality was tested to determine the most appropriate statistical approach to follow. The normality testing routine used was Shapiro-France (for samples 5 ≤ n ≤ 1000) from the Stata Distribution Graphics Software Version 15.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Common parametric assumptions were violated (p < 0.05); hence a nonparametric approach was suggested.

Sexual and reproductive status dimorphism

Sexual dimorphism in dromedary camels, like in many species, is evident in various aspects of their zoometry and morphology (Tandoh and Gwaza 2017). Male dromedaries or bulls tend to be larger and heavier, with more robust bodies and often have a distinctive hump shape. Their necks are thicker, and they may possess a more pronounced dewlap (a loose flap of skin) under their neck. In contrast, female camels generally have a slenderer build and smaller humps.

On the other hand, the reproductive status of the animals also conditions their zoometry. Castration can indeed influence the morphology of dromedary camels, although the extent of these changes may vary depending on the age of the animal when the castration is performed (Pigière and Henrotay 2012).

In the light of the aforementioned a priori assumptions violation, the occurrence of sexual dimorphism and reproductive status dimorphism in our study sample was tested in case differences may exist. Bayesian ANOVA was carried out across all the 30 zoometric traits to determine the need to consider their affectation by sex and reproductive status, thus the need to build separate 5-category scales for capturing the phenotypic variability existing for zoometric traits in each study subgroup (females (n = 58), bulls (n = 31), and castrated males (n = 41)). The Bayesian approach to ANOVA analysis developed in this study was carried out with the SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0, IBM Corp.

LAS optimization: dimensionality reduction

The optimization of the Linear Appraisal System (LAS) was carried out using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to discard confounding zoometric variables that contributed little to explained variability but increased the risk of type I errors. Component loadings below |0.5| were ruled out provided their confounding nature (Walde 2020).

PCA data were generated from the variance-covariance matrix, and the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were conducted to validate the dataset for structure detection. A KMO value above 0.5 and a p-value less than 0.05 for Bartlett’s test indicated that factor analysis was suitable (Jamil et al. 2015).

Communalities, representing the sum of squared factor loadings, were evaluated, with a cutoff factor loading of 0.30–0.33 (Field 2009). Varimax rotation, combined with Kaiser normalization, was applied to simplify component structure by maximizing the variance distribution across variables (Dien et al. 2005). Cronbach’s alpha was used to assess the reliability and internal consistency of the reduced variable set, with values above 0.7 considered acceptable for validity (George and Mallery 2003). All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics Version 25.0.

LAS validation: zoometric variability-based scales vs. linear appraisal traits

We calculated the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) based on multiple paired Cohen’s κ tests to assess the reliability (internal consistency) of the linear appraisal extrapolation from zoometric variability-based scales. Internal consistency, when applied to instrument comparison, estimates the average correlation among items within a test, with each item representing a measuring method (zoometric variability-based scales and LAS in our case) (Choudhary and Nagaraja 2005). Following the criteria set by Fleiss and Cohen (1973), we interpreted ICC values as follows: less than 0.4 indicates low reliability, 0.4–0.59 suggests reasonable reliability, 0.6–0.74 signifies good reliability, and values above 0.75 indicate excellent reliability. ICC calculation was performed using SPSS Statistics Version 25.0.

LAS repeatability and reproducibility: intraobserver and interobserver agreement

As suggested by Bunting et al. (2019), ICC serves also as a reference method for assessing the reproducibility and reliability of numeric measurements organized into groups. This includes scenarios where different operators measure the same variable across different animals (interobserver reliability) and where the same operator measures or scores the same animal multiple times (intraobserver reliability). ICC was computed using SPSS Statistics Version 25.0.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Summary of descriptive statistics for zoometric traits in dromedary camels across sexes and reproductive statuses (females, bulls, and castrated males) is reported in Supplementary Table S1.

Sexual and reproductive status dimorphism

Bayesian ANOVA suggested the existence of significant differences (p < 0.05) in dromedary camels’ zoometric traits across sexes and reproductive statuses (females, bulls, and castrated males). For this reason, a separate zoometric variability-based scale was develop for each study subgroup. A detailed description of these scales can be consulted in Supplementary Table S2.

LAS repeatability and reproducibility: intraobserver and interobserver agreement

The same strong agreement (ICC ≥ 0.7) was observed for each of the six appraisers’ judgments when assessing intraobserver reliability. Spearman’s correlation coefficients between almost all pairs of zoometric traits in dromedary females, bulls and castrated males were over |0.3|, which supported the performance of PCA. Discussion

The optimization and validation of a linear appraisal system as a methodological alternative to traditional on-field assessment of zoometric traits in dromedary camels, were performed using principal component and correlation analysis. As suggested by Reddy and Claridge (1994), such systematic approach to reducing the dimensionality of large datasets is fundamental to the pursuit of determining which are the reduced numbers of variables that explain the greater proportion of the variability found in a sample. Further, the level of resemblance between the measures within linear data cluster and zoometric traits was calculated to estimate the capacity of an artificially built linear appraisal system to capture the biological variability that exists in the study population for functionally and economically relevant zoometric traits.

First, ICC values revealed good-to-excellent agreement in our data set (ICC ≥ 0.7), both between zoometric variability-based scales and linear appraisal traits, and between raters. This suggests that the animal and rater study samples are reasonably capable to capture and explain the existing phenotypic variability for the zoometric traits assessed (Koo and Li 2016). In this sense, Melesse et al. (2022) remark the critical importance of sample size in morphological characterization researches given that it has to be large enough to differentiate genetic resources based on morphological traits in an accurate way and resemble the findings of molecular based studies when these last tools could not be feasible (Hassen et al. 2016).

In regard to the optimization of the scale, PCA identified the existence of nine reorganized major categories or dimensions (Supplementary Table S3). Concerning the variability that was explained by each model, values of 69.65%, 71.33% and 79.78% were accounted for females, castrated males and bulls, respectively. For the three subgroups, the sets of variables that mostly explain the variability found in the data are those that are related to body general corpulence and the proportionality of specific local regions. Globally, it could be also concluded that no variable is redundant and thus susceptible to be discarded from any of the models, that is, they are all explanatory to a greater or lesser extent depending on the percentage of variance that explains the dimension or dimensions in which each variable is clustered.

The qualitative aggrupation of explanatory variables slightly differs among study subgroups. This may be supported by the sex-separated breeding of the dromedary camel breed assessed for utilitarian reasons. For the particular case of dromedary females and castrated males, the first dimension practically includes the same morphometric variables that are related to body general corpulence (‘neck girth: caudal third’, ‘hump girth’, ‘thigh perimeter’, ‘hock perimeter’, ‘fore cannon bone perimeter’, ‘rear cannon bone perimeter’, and ‘foot perimeter’ for females; and the same variables but including ‘neck girth: middle third’ and discarding ‘hock perimeter’ for neutered males). For dromedary bulls, a similar dimension is at the third position and includes the traits ‘neck girth: middle third’, ‘hump girth’, ‘thigh perimeter’, ‘fore cannon bone perimeter’, ‘rear cannon bone perimeter’, and ‘foot perimeter’. The relative importance of such a dimension in both females and castrated males can be explained basing on the positive relationship between such morphological variables and milk yield (Pesántez-Pacheco et al. 2019) and riding performance (Faye 1997; Pigière and Henrotay 2012; Ucko and Dimbleby 2007) for females and castrated males, respectively. Certain regional body proportions, namely ‘head-neck junction’ and ‘neck-thorax junction’ for females, and ‘neck length and junction with thorax’ for castrated males, additionally support these results basing on their reported genetic correlation with body size and milk yield (Alphonsus et al. 2010; Khan and Khan 2016) and riding performance (Zechner et al. 2001) in other domestic species such as cattle and horse.

In contrast, dromedary bulls are generally less corpulent (Pigière and Henrotay 2012) and more difficult to handle for their often-strong temper (MacKay et al. 2022) when compared to castrated males. It may translate both into a lower functional resistance and breeders’ preference for riding activities (Fenner et al. 2019; Iglesias Pastrana et al. 2021), and the highest contribution of other morphological traits to explain greater fractions of the variability in their specific PCA dataset. Concretely, variables related to overall animal body proportion or harmony (‘neck girth: cranial third’, ‘neck girth: middle third’, ‘heart girth’, ‘body length’, ‘hump length’, ‘hump width’, ‘hump girth’, ‘rump length’, and ‘thigh perimeter’) explained the highest proportion of variation in bulls. Such specific traits can be encountered also for females in relatively less important positions (second position for ‘overall body proportion’ and third position for ‘body harmony’) but they are for castrated males. The underlying basis of such finding can be the impact of body size and harmony traits on milk production in females (Ahlborn and Dempfle 1992; Mingoas et al. 2017), and the correlations between harmonious body proportions and athletic performance in animals within the sport and leisure spheres (de Oliveira Bussiman et al. 2018; Gómez et al. 2016; Koenen et al. 2004).

For dromedary bulls, although the body harmony was the most explanatory dimension of within-subgroup variability, we additionally encountered that dorsal length (fifth dimension) and anteroposterior proportionality (sixth dimension), have a non-negligible explanatory potential. They may be further implicated in the motion and the maintenance equilibrium during locomotion (ninth dimension) in these animal species for anatomical reasons. Contrary to other quadruped mammals and equal to giraffes, camels are particularly long-legged, so that the length, width and mobility of the neck are adapted to counteract the dorso-ventral balance of the limb’s movements (Sharp 2012).

Contextually, for castrated males, the apical balance (‘neck girth: caudal third’ and ‘tail length’), in addition to neck length and body width at the anterior third (‘neck length: dorsal line’, ‘neck length: ventral line’, ‘chest width’, and ‘heart girth’), are the dimensions that accounted for a greater proportion of the variability encountered within this animal subgroup when compared to bulls. This suggests that these regional morphological traits have a pivotal influence on the performance of castrated males, the most preferred for riding activities, in account of the fact the animals have to maintain equilibrium while dealing with variable weight along their back. A major propulsion force is then also needed, as suggested by the greater relative importance of this dimension within this animal subgroup (sixth dimension) when compared to females and bulls (seventh dimension). Moreover, as they are disposed in caravans when performing leisure activities, the body language signals used by the animals for inter-individual communication are quite important for a secure experience and such condition can be ascribed to the identification of a specific dimension within PCA that comprises the size of some appendixes (‘ear length, ‘ear width’, and ‘tail length’) widely known to be involved in non-verbal communication in animal species (Czycholl et al. 2020; Proctor and Carder 2014; Westling 2014). Besides, the proportions of the hump (i.e., overall hump size) are expected to affect the apical balance being the difficulties in maintaining the balance greater as long as the hump proportions increase, similarly to the reported deleterious effects of rider’s size and position on equine gait balance and synchrony (Dyson et al. 2020, 2022). Indeed, the variables ‘hump-to-tail distance’, ‘hump length’, ‘hump width’, ‘hump height’, and ‘hump girth’, are clustered together in our results. These dimensions lastly condition a good functional performance of the camel during the rides, hence affects customer satisfaction and return intention probability within this entertainment business (Iglesias Pastrana et al. 2020).

Apart from these specific differences, both male subgroups share the same relative importance for dimensions comprising variables related to the disposition of gravity centre (second dimension), general equilibrium (fourth dimension) and aplombs (eight dimension). For females, these dimensions are inexistent. Indeed, despite sharing similar groups of morphological variables with both subgroups of males, female dromedaries appeared to display high inter-individual variability for morphometric traits that belong to general body proportion and some regional traits, namely the dimensionality of thoraco-abdominal region, but low variability for traits linked to aplombs and support. This is supported on the breeders’ preference for females to be selected for milk production and the larger functional specialization and selection of male dromedaries (especially castrated males) towards riding activities. Instead, bulls would be morphologically more suitable for beauty contests.

After mapping the high-dimensionality initial dataset to a lower dimension space, the optimization of the scale further requires to corroborate that the variability within each of the assessed zoometric traits has a statistically significant representativeness across the five levels of the linear appraisal scale for each animal subgroup. The resulting distribution of study data in quantitative histograms and the descriptive statistics (i.e., minimum and maximum values) offers an exploratory but reliable interpretation for this statistical association. It can be easily visualized which are the variables that maintain notable representativeness of animals within the five levels but also those zoometric traits that display the greatest representativeness of animals concentrated in a shorter number of categories and thus proposed to be reduced to a fewer number of levels of linear traits (see Supplementary Figure. 1). Specifically, a lack of sample representativeness for one or more levels within the linear appraisal scale does exist for the variables ‘ear length, ‘ear width’, ‘neck length: dorsal line’, ‘chest width’, ‘body length’, ‘hump-to-tail distance’, ‘rump length’, ‘tail length’, and ‘length of toe dorsal line’ for females; ‘ear width’, ‘neck length: dorsal line’, ‘neck girth: caudal third’, ‘heart girth’, ‘body length’, ‘hump-to-tail distance’, ‘hump height’, ‘sole length’, and ‘foot perimeter’ for bulls; ‘head length’, ‘ear width’, ‘height at withers’, ‘rump length’, ‘hock perimeter’, ‘heel height’, and ‘foot perimeter’ for castrated males.

Therefore, it can be summarized that all the morphometric variables considered in this study are maintained since they all have explanatory potential of the variability present in the sample. But, a proposal for the optimization of the scale goes through the reduction of the originally proposed number of linear categories for the above cited variables given the fact that there is not enough representativeness of the sample in some of them. Hence, the present attempt for scale optimization reported quite conservative results, similarly to Fernández Álvarez et al. (2020), who only omitted one variable (rump angle) from the model.

Lastly, basing on the results obtained for interobserver reliability, the lowest levels of agreement between raters (ICC < 0.5) (Koo and Li 2016) pertained to the linear traits ‘head profile’, ‘head width’, ‘forehead’, ‘rump angle, ‘rump width’, ‘tail length’, ‘width at the base of the tail’, ‘feet’, and ‘angularity of the trunk and posterior third’. Such finding can be mostly associated to the fact that these traits are mainly apical body regions and thus more likely to move during the animal handling and restraining for on-field zoometric assessment and linear appraisal. Similar results are reported by Zechner et al. (2001) for horses.

Conclusions

All zoometric traits assesed in this study were retained, as none showed redundancy and each contributed to explaining phenotypic variability within the sample. However, a reduction in the number of linear categories is recommended for certain traits due to limited statistical representativeness in specific scoring levels. PCA revealed distinct sets of traits explaining variability within each animal subgroup —dromedary females, bulls, and castrated males — reflecting their divergent functional roles and management goals (milk production vs. riding). In females and castrated males, variability was primarily associated with traits linked to body corpulence and apical balance, whereas in bulls it related to proportionality and general harmony. Overall, the results support the viability of the proposed LAS as a reliable and replicable tool for the zoometric appraisal of dromedary camels, contributing to more efficient on-farm selection and breeding strategies.
