Associations between extreme weather events, resource insecurities, and HIV vulnerabilities with self-reported urinary tract infection symptoms among adolescent girls and young women in Kenya
Authors: Carmen H Logie, Zerihun Admassu, Andie MacNeil, Aryssa Hasham, Lawrence Mbuagbaw, Humphres Evelia, Julia Kagunda, Beldine Omondi, Clara Gachoki, Mercy Chege, Mumbi Mwangi, Lesley Gittings, Caetano Dorea, Perry Hystad, Janet M Turan, Manjulaa Narasimhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionExtreme weather events (EWEs) and resource insecurities are linked to genitourinary infections, yet this is understudied in adolescent girls and young women (AGYW). We examined associations between resource insecurities, EWE exposure, HIV vulnerability factors, and self-reported urinary tract infection (UTI) symptoms among AGYW in Nairobi and Kisumu, Kenya.Material and methodsWe conducted cross-sectional analyses of survey data collected with a purposive sample of AGYW (16–24 years) in Nairobi (Mathare and Majengo informal settlements) and Kisumu (Ogal and Rota beaches) (October-November 2024). We conducted adjusted and unadjusted logistic regression analyses to examine associations between resource insecurities (food, water, sanitation, menstruation), EWEs, and HIV vulnerabilities (past 6-month transactional sex, past year multiple sex partners [MSP], past 6-month mobility), with self-reported UTI symptoms.ResultsAmong n=589 participants (mean age: 20.6 years; SD=2.6), 41.6% self-reported UTI symptoms. Regarding past 6-month EWEs, 11.5% (n=68) experienced one, 64.5% (n=380) experienced 2–4, and 24.0% (n=141) experienced ≥5. Most (81.3%, n=478) experienced multiple EWE types, with ≥1 occurring more than once. In multivariable analyses, odds of self-reporting UTI symptoms were highest among participants reporting: ≥5 (vs. 1) past-year EWE; water insecurity; food insecurity; sanitation insecurity; menstruation insecurity; and exposure to one, two, and ≥3 resource insecurities (vs. none). Transactional sex, multiple sexual partners, and mobility were also significantly associated with self-reported UTI symptoms.ConclusionsEWEs, resource insecurity, and HIV vulnerabilities were associated with UTI symptoms. Extreme weather and poverty-informed approaches are needed to reduce UTI risks and advance the sexual and reproductive health of AGYW in Kenya.Keywords: Urinary tract infection, Young women, Kenya, Resource insecurity, Extreme weather events

Introduction

Genitourinary infections, such as urinary tract infections (UTIs), cause significant morbidity among women and girls in sub-Saharan Africa [1,2]. A recent systematic review estimated the prevalence of UTIs in sub-Saharan Africa to be 32 % [1]. In Kenya, the health burden of genitourinary infections is exacerbated by numerous interrelated factors, including poverty [3], inadequate water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) [4,5], and increasing antibiotic resistance [3,[6], [7], [8]]. A burgeoning body of research has also described how extreme weather events (EWEs), such as drought, flooding, extreme heat, and heavy rain, and related resource insecurities, such as water and sanitation insecurity, may present new challenges for managing infectious disease in areas highly affected by climate change [[9], [10], [11]], such as Kenya [12]. However, research examining associations between EWEs, resource insecurities, and bacterial infections such as urinary tract infections with adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) in Kenya is limited.

Prior research in Kenya has noted UTI prevalence of 28 % in Kiambu [7] and 23 % in Kitui, with approximately half experiencing antibiotic resistance [13]. This high prevalence of antibiotic resistance was corroborated by a recent report on antibiotic resistance from the World Health Organization, which found that approximately half of UTIs in Kenya between 2018 and 2023 had antibiotic resistance (urinary tract Escherichia coli: 50.6 % resistance; urinary tract K. pneumoniae: 59.7 % resistance) based on national surveillance data [14]. When UTIs are not treated or the infection is not cleared, they can carry significant risks and complications, including febrile infection, ascension to the kidneys and ureters (pyelonephritis), and in rare cases, the bloodstream (urosepsis), which can be life-threatening [15,16].

Resource insecurity is linked with an increased risk of UTIs, particularly among young women living in poverty [3]. This is a key issue in Kenya, where in 2024 only 66 % of the population had access to at least basic water supplies, only 57 % had improved water available when needed, and only 41 % had access to at least basic sanitation services, with only 36 % accessing improved, non-shared sanitation facilities [17]. Water and sanitation insecurity are associated with a higher prevalence of UTIs [7]. As gastrointenstinal bacteria are a cause of UTIs, poor WASH access can increase infection risks, as can larger environmental factors including proximity to livestock and aquaculture that increase exposure to bacteria and pathogens [18]. To illustrate, in the Democratic Republic of Congo [19] and Kenya [20], unimproved water sources for bathing (e.g. rain, ground, river or lake water) were associated with increased risks of reporting urogenital infections. A recent study in Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania found that individuals with multi-drug resistant UTIs were more likely to have compounding social and environment disadvantage, including higher levels of chronic illness, lower levels of education, and to live in households with greater resource deprivation, including limited drinking water, washing water, and toilet access [3].

As climate change increases episodes of water insecurity in Kenya [21] and leads to more extreme weather events (EWE), including floods and drought [22] that constrain the ability to bathe and maintain adequate hygiene, this burden of UTIs may continue to grow. Warm weather is also associated with UTI risks; this is posited to be due to increased fluid lost and dehydration that in turn reduces bacterial clearance from the urinary tract [[23], [24], [25]]. Additionally, heavy rainfall is associated with increased pathogens in water sources and latrines in Kenya, including urinary tract pathogens [20]. Flooding in Pakistan was reported as increasing risks for UTIs among women in flood-affected communities through multiple pathways, including damage to sanitation and sewage infrastructure, leading to water contamination; reduced access to WASH, including during menses, leading to an inability to bathe and/or bathing in contaminated water; damage to latrines that increase exposure to bacterial pathogens; and road and infrastructure damage that reduce access to menstruation management products, leading to the use of unconventional products [26]. Thus seasons and EWE appear to affect UTIs, but exposure to multiple EWE and UTIs are understudied.

Climate change-related EWEs may also impact genitourinary infection risks through other mechanisms also related to HIV vulnerabilities, such as increased transactional sex to cope with resource scarcity [27,28]. Evidence suggests that sexual practices, including increased frequency of sexual intercourse (>2/week) may be linked to UTI risks, potentially via transmission of peri-anal and rectal flora to the urethral area [[29], [30], [31]]. A recent study on UTIs among women in Ethiopia found that more frequent sexual activity (>3 times per week) increased the likelihood of UTIs, while daily water intake of at least 1 to 2 litres decreased the risk of UTIs [32]. Yet increased water intake to offset UTI risks may not be possible for women experiencing water insecurity. Menstruation insecurity [33,34] and WASH insecurity [35,36] at large are associated with higher likelihood of reproductive tract infections, and there is also dearth of studies examining multiple resource insecurities and transactional sex with UTI risks. Mobility for livelihoods in East African contexts increases HIV infection risks among women via complex and dynamic pathways, including expanding sexual networks and increasing commercial and transactional sex [37,38]. There is also contrained access to WASH for mobile populations in African settings, whereby toilet insecurity is exacerbated outside of one’s home, including in public transport systems [39], yet AGYW’s mobility-related UTI risks are understudied.

Key knowledge gaps remain related to the associations between EWEs, multiple resource insecurities, HIV vulnerabilities, and urinary tract infections among young women in Kenya. To address these gaps, this study examined the relationships between resource insecurities, EWEs, HIV vulnerability factors, and self-reported UTI symptoms among AGYW in Nairobi and Kisumu, Kenya.

Methods

Design and participants

This analysis uses cross-sectional data collected as part of the cohort study "Rada Ya Weather" (loosely translated as “What's up with the weather?” in Swahili) collected between October and November 2024. Data were collected in Nairobi informal urban settlements (Majengo, population 577,980 and Mathare, population 804,990 [40]) and Kisumu’s fishing communities (rural and peri-rural areas along Ogal and Rota Beaches).

Purposive sampling was employed to select participants aged 16–24 who: self-identified as a girl/woman; owned or had access to a mobile phone; self-reported an HIV-negative serostatus at baseline; had the ability to communicate in English, Swahili, or Luo; and were able to provide informed consent. Additional inclusion criteria were: experienced in the past 14 days at least one form of resource insecurity (food, water, and/or sanitation) and/or an EWE (e.g., flood, drought), and any HIV vulnerability (i.e., parenting, transactional sex, intimate partner violence, condomless sex, multiple sex partners, having an HIV serodiscordant partner). Site-specific inclusion criteria were engagement in the fishing industry (e.g., selling cleaning fish) in Kisumu, and being a young parent in Nairobi. These criteria were applied to engage climate-affected AGYW populations at elevated HIV acquisition risks, including women in Kenya’s fishing industry [41] and AGYW mothers in East and South Africa [42].

Data were collected using SurveyCTO, a secure, multilingual, tablet-based application, by trained data collectors affiliated with community-based partner organizations. To ensure contextual relevance, we piloted tested the survey with peer navigators, data collectors, and local collaborators. Both the survey and consent documents were translated into Swahili and Luo to accommodate participants’ language preferences. Data collectors received comprehensive training and a daily data quality control check was conducted by the research team.

Measurement

Outcome variable: The study outcome was self-reported UTI symptoms. We assessed UTI symptoms following prior research [43], specifically the presence of any current symptoms, including pain or burning during urination, frequent urination, feeling the urge to urinate despite an empty bladder, blood in the urine, or pressure/cramping in the groin or lower abdomen. UTI symptoms were dichotomized as “Yes” if participants reported experiencing at least one listed symptom and “No” if they reported none.

Exposure variables: Extreme weather events (EWEs): Building on prior research [[44], [45], [46], [47], [48]], EWEs were assessed using the question: “In the last six months, have you experienced any of these with regards to weather?” with a range of events, including extreme rain, flooding, changes in expected rain patterns in a season, changes in expected temperature (heat, cold) in a season, drought, extreme heat, extreme wind, extreme cold, fire (home fire, wildfire), storm (e.g., hurricane), landslide, and/or earthquake. Responses were categorized into three groups: one EWE, 2–5 EWEs, and >5 EWEs. Past-year EWE frequency was evaluated with the question “Have you faced any of the weather event(s) more than once in the past six months?” Responses were categorized into experiencing: “1 type of EWE, once in last six months”, “1 type of EWE, more than once in last six months”, “more than 1 type of EWE, all only once in the last six months”, and “more than 1 EWE experienced more than once in the last six months.” These EWE items have been used in other research [[44], [45], [46], [47], [48], [49], [50]].

Resource insecurities: Water insecurity over the past six months was assessed using the Household Water Insecurity Experiences scale [51] (Cronbach’s alpha=0.93), and categorized as insecure for scores ≥12 and secure for scores <12 [51]. Past 30-day food insecurity was assessed using the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) [52] (Cronbach’s alpha=0.94); households were categorized as food secure (scores 0–1), mild food insecure (scores 2–7), and food insecure (moderate [scores 8–14], severe [scores 15–27]) [51], [52]. Past 30-day sanitation insecurity was assessed with the Sanitation Insecurity Scale [53] (Cronbach’s alpha=0.94). Menstruation insecurity during the last two menstrual cycles was evaluated using a validated scale [54] (Cronbach’s alpha=0.90). Each scale was scored by summing item responses, with higher scores indicating greater insecurity. For sanitation and menstruation insecurity, participants scoring above the mean were categorized as insecure, while those below the mean were categorized as secure [55,56]. A composite resource insecurity variable was created by summing four domains of resource insecurity (water, food, sanitation, menstruation) to capture the cumulative burden of multiple resource insecurities. Scores were categorized as follows: 0 insecurities, 1 insecurity, 2 insecurities, and 3–4 insecurities.

Sociodemographic variables: We assessed socio-demographic variables including age, highest completed education, relationship status, parenting status, household latrine location (e.g., dwelling, yard/compound), and source of water (e.g., dwelling, yard/compound).

HIV vulnerability factors: We assessed variables associated with increased HIV acquisition risks in sub-Saharan Africa [[57], [58], [59], [60], [61]]: a) past six-month transactional sex (yes/no); b) mobility, assessed using a single-item question (yes/no) regarding if the participant had traveled and stayed overnight away from their primary residence in the past six months; and c) number of sexual partners in past year (categories: none, one, multiple).

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome, self-reported UTI symptoms, was dichotomized into “presence” or “absence” of any UTI symptoms. To explore factors associated with UTI symptoms, descriptive and inferential analyses were conducted. The differences in the proportion of participants reporting UTI symptoms versus those without symptoms were compared across participant characteristics using the chi-square (χ²) test. Binary logistic regression was employed to assess unadjusted associations between potential explanatory variables and the presence of self-reported genitourinary symptoms. Variables with a significant association in univariate analyses were carried forward to multivariable logistic regression.

Multivariable logistic regression models were used to determine independent associations between participant characteristics and self-reported UTI symptoms. The model was adjusted for likely sociodemographic confounders (age, educational attainment, location). We present overall analyses in addition to site-specific analyses. The results of the regression analyses are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95 % confidence intervals (CI). All statistical analyses were performed using Stata SE version 17 (STATA Corp., Texas, USA). Statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance threshold of p<0.05.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the University of Toronto, Amref Ethics and Scientific Review Committee (Kenya), and the National Commission for Science, Technology, and Innovation (NACOSTI) in Kenya. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the study. Participants received an honorarium of 1,000 Kenyan Shillings for their time and participation.

Results

Participant characteristics

Sociodemographic characteristics of participants (n=589; mean age: 20.6 years; SD=2.6; n=243 Nairobi; n=346 Kisumu) are presented in Table 1. The self-reported prevalence of urinary tract infection (UTIs) symptoms among participants was high, with 41.6 % (n = 245) reporting self-reported UTI symptoms. Half of the participants (50 %, n=295) had access to a latrine within their compound/yard, 42 % (n=245) used a latrine located outside their yard/compound, and 8 % (n=45) had a latrine within their dwelling. Approximately one-third had engaged in past-6 month transactional sex (n=187, 32 %) and mobility (n=187, 32 %). Concerning past 6-month EWEs, 11.5 % (n=68) experienced one event, 64.5 % (n=380) experienced 2–4 events, and 24.0 % (n=141) experienced ≥5. Regarding the frequency of EWEs, most participants (81.3 %, n=478) experienced multiple EWE types, with at least one occurring more than once.

Socio-demographic variables: In adjusted analyses with the full sample, age was positively associated with self-reported UTI symptoms (Table 2). Each additional year of age was associated with a 13 % increase in the odds of self-reporting UTI symptoms (adjusted Odds Ratio [aOR] =1.13; 95 % Confidence Interval [CI]: 1.05–1.22). Participants with higher education (secondary and above vs. < secondary) had significantly lower odds of self-reporting UTI symptoms (aOR=0.52; 95 % CI: 0.36–0.75). Similarly, full-time students (vs. unemployed) were less likely to self-report UTI symptoms (aOR = 0.49; 95 % CI: 0.29–0.82). Participants with latrines located within their compound or yard also had reduced odds of UTI symptoms (aOR=0.52; 95 % CI: 0.34–0.80) compared to those with latrines located outside their compound.

Extreme weather event (EWE) related factors: Experiencing ≥5 EWEs in the past six months (compared to 1 EWE) was significantly associated with higher odds of self-reporting UTI symptoms (aOR=3.41; 95 % CI: 1.71–6.78).

Resource insecurity: Participants experiencing water insecurity (vs. water security) had a 79 % higher likelihood of self-reporting UTI symptoms (aOR=1.79; 95 % CI: 1.25–2.57). Menstruation insecurity (aOR=1.08; 95 % CI: 1.05–1.12), sanitation insecurity (aOR = 1.04; 95 % CI: 1.02–1.06), and food insecurity (aOR = 1.11; 95 % CI: 1.08–1.14) were each associated with higher odds of self-reported UTI symptoms. Compared to participants with no reported resource insecurities, those with one resource insecurity had 5.82 times higher odds of self-reported UTI symptoms (95 % CI: 1.99–16.92), those with two insecurities had 11.35 fold higher odds (95 % CI: 3.87–33.26) and those with ≥3 resource insecurities had 14.58 (95 % CI: 4.11–51.71) higher odds of self-reported UTI symptoms, indicating a dose-response relationship between multiple resource insecurities and self-reported UTI symptoms.

HIV vulnerability factors: Reporting past six-month transactional sex (aOR = 2.08; 95 % CI: 1.43–3.03), multiple past-year sexual partners (vs. none) (aOR = 2.20; 95 % CI: 1.22–3.94), and past six-month mobility (travel or relocation) (aOR = 1.70; 95 % CI: 1.17–2.46) were associated with higher odds of experiencing UTI symptoms (Table 2).

Discussion

Our study identified links between EWEs, resource insecurities, and HIV vulnerability factors with self-reported UTI symptoms among AGYW in Kenya. These findings highlight the need for gender-responsive, climate-informed strategies that integrate sexual and reproductive health (SRH) with genitourinary health [62,63], and for youth-focused research at the nexus of climate, SRH and UTI risks [64].

Our observed association between EWE exposure and self-reported UTI symptoms builds on growing evidence that climate-related environmental disruptions influence infectious diseases [9,10,12] and SRH outcomes [28,62,65]. Meteorologic factors such as temperature and precipitation may alter the ecology of uropathogens, including E. coli, that cause UTIs [66]. Multiple EWEs were associated with higher odds of self-reporting UTI symptoms, suggesting that cumulative environmental shocks can intensify exposures to unsanitary conditions, stress, and behavioral changes that heighten UTI risks. This aligns with evidence that converging social and environmental disadvantages, such as limited water for drinking and washing, chronic illness, and poor sanitation, contribute to multi-drug resistant UTIs [3].

Resource insecurities related to water, sanitation, food, and menstruation, were also associated with higher odds of self-reported UTI symptoms. Water insecurity constrains hygiene practices and increases exposure to contaminated sources, elevating UTI infection risks [[67], [68], [69]]. In Ethiopia, sufficient drinking water (1–2 litres per day) was linked to reduced UTI risk [32], underscoring water security’s role in urogenital health. Menstruation and sanitation insecurities further compromise vaginal and urinary health; lack of safe, private, and hygienic facilities and menstrual supplies increases exposure to pathogens [[70], [71], [72]]. Transactional sex was also associated with genitourinary symptoms, consistent with evidence that material need insecurities may drive expanded sexual networks and heighten vulnerability to urogenital infections and sexually transmitted infections (STIs) [37,38,57,73,74]. Similarly, our finding that food insecurity was correlated with self-reported UTI symptoms aligns with research linking severe food insecurity to HIV acquisition risks among women in sub-Saharan Africa via increased condomless and transactional sex [75].

This study offers novel evidence on the intersection of climate vulnerability and urogenital health, signalling that EWEs and resource insecurities are not only environmental or economic issues but also critical determinants of women’s health. Integrating urogenital health into youth-friendly SRH services can promote more holistic, patient-centered care and strengthen equity and trust in healthcare services. Addressing EWE-related urogenital health issues further requires gender-responsive adaptation and WASH strategies [76]. Emerging research underscores the importance of self-care strategies for SRH during EWEs, when formal healthcare may be disrupted [77]. For UTI prevention, such strategies could include increased psychoeducation on hydration, hygiene, and use of acccessible supplements such as D-mannose [78].

Study limitations include reliance on self-reported UTI symptoms. While there are cost-effective and valid UTI self-screening tools [43,[79], [80], [81], [82]] across multiple studies such as ours that center patients’ experiences [81,82], these self-reported findings may be affected by recall and reporting bias. Future studies should clinically validate symptoms and diagnoses. Sample size limited analyses by specific EWE type, and the purposive sampling restricts generalizability. Future longitudinal research should clinically validate UTI outcomes, examine diverse EWE types, and explore biological mechanisms linking environmental stressors, microbiome disruption, and infection susceptibility. Despite these limitations, our study is unique in examining EWEs as a risk factor for UTIs among climate-affected and HIV vulnerable AGYW in two Kenyan regions, revealing how EWE and multiple material-need resource insecurities converge to shape urogenital health.

Conclusion

Our findings underscore the urgent need for public health strategies and normative guidelines examining and addressing EWE and their interplay with social, economic and environmental determinants of UTIs and other urogenital health conditions among AGYW in low and middle income settings.
