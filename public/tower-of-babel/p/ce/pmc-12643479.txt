Rapid decline of SARS-CoV-2–specific salivary IgA antibody levels in people with hybrid immunity—data from the STOPCoV study
Authors: Sharon L Walmsley, Majid Nabipoor, Gary Chao, Leif Erik Lovblom, Lesley Ward, Rizani Ravindran, Karen Colwill, Anne Claude Gingras, Jennifer L Gommerman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHybrid immune individuals who experience an infection after a severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) mRNA vaccine series show evidence of salivary anti-spike and anti-receptor binding domain (RBD) IgA antibodies with broad specificity against different variants. It is unclear how long these antibodies persist and whether they offer protection against new SARS-CoV-2 infections. We compared salivary IgA levels to full-length spike protein and its RBD of the ancestral Wuhan SARS-CoV-2 virus and the Omicron BA.1 variant in a subset of persons participating in a longitudinal study of binding antibody responses to vaccination. We assessed the decay rate of the salivary IgA antibodies in those with hybrid immunity. In our heavily vaccinated population, low levels of salivary IgA to RBD and spike was variably detected in vaccine-only immunity to both Wuhan and Omicron BA.1, but antibody levels were an order of magnitude higher in those with hybrid immunity. In hybrid immune individuals, anti-spike/RBD salivary IgA rapidly decayed over a 4-month observation period. In a multivariate analysis, salivary IgA antibody to Omicron BA.1 was not associated with protection from a new SARS-CoV-2 infection over the subsequent 10 months during the Omicron XBB.1.5, EG.5, and JN waves of infection. In contrast, receipt of a new vaccine dose was significantly associated with protection.

Introduction

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) mRNA vaccines decrease death and hospitalization but do not completely prevent severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection.1–4 Hybrid immunity (combination of immunity provoked by vaccination and infection) provides greater defense.5 We among others have failed to determine a threshold value for either binding or neutralizing antibody levels to define protection against infection.6–10

Following inhalation, SARS-CoV-2 can infect cells from nares and mouth to gut. Interaction between the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of viral spike protein and angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) on mucosal epithelial cells is the first essential step in SARS-CoV-2 infection.11 Immunity in the oral and nasal mucosa thus serves as a first line of defense against viral entry.

Higher levels of mucosal anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies, particularly IgA induced by SARS-CoV-2 infection, could in part explain why hybrid immunity is superior to vaccine-only immunity against infection,12,13 providing a rationale for development of a mucosal vaccine.14–19 Saliva can be used as a biofluid for measuring the antibody response to SARS-CoV-2 spike and RBD.20

Our objective was to measure the salivary IgG and IgA antibody levels in a subset of persons participating in a longitudinal observational study of SARS-CoV-2 immunity. We compared responses in those with vaccine only to those with hybrid immunity and assessed antibody decay over 4 months. We investigated whether salivary IgA was associated with protection against a new SARS-CoV-2 infection over 10 months.

Materials and methods

Study design

The parent study, STOPCoV (Safety and Efficacy of COVID-19 vaccines),21 is a decentralized study that enrolled 1,286 participants beginning in May 2021 into 2 cohorts (those aged ≥70 years and those aged 30 to 50 years). All participants received the initial 2-dose mRNA vaccine series between May 26 and September 16, 2021. They prospectively contributed monthly electronic self-reported data on COVID-19 vaccine brands and doses and SARS-CoV-2 infections in a web-based portal and mailed in self-collected dried blood spots (DBSs) every 3 months to measure binding antibodies to SARS-CoV-2 spike, nucleoprotein (N), and RBD. The full protocol is available on the study website (www.stopcov.ca). The study flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

Study flowchart.

Salivary immunity study

In the fall of 2022, a random selection of 462 STOPCoV participants who consented to be contacted for future research were invited by email to participate in a substudy of blood neutralizing and salivary antibody responses with the goal of enrolling 126 in the older cohort and 54 in the younger cohort to maintain the age distribution of the parent study. Consenting participants with one or more documented SARS-CoV-2 infection were categorized as hybrid immunity, while those without any SARS-CoV-2 infections were categorized as vaccine immunity. SARS-CoV-2 infection was determined by report of a positive polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or rapid antigen test (RAT), or the development of anti-N (nucleocapsid) antibody levels above threshold on DBSs.

Participant consent

Consent for the salivary substudy was completed electronically. Study staff contact information (email and telephone) was provided for assistance. This substudy was approved by the University Health Network (UHN) Research Ethics Board (REB +22-5715). The study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards as laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards.

Sample collection

Participants were mailed saliva collection kits and instructed not to eat, drink, or smoke at least 30 minutes prior to collection. Saliva was collected using Salivette tubes (Sarstedt, Nubrecht, Germany).20 The self-collected specimens were submitted to a local laboratory or collected by courier at their home. They were transported at room temperature (RT) or on dry ice if delivery would be over 24 hours in barcoded containers to the UHN. Samples were sent to the laboratory of Dr Gommerman at the University of Toronto for processing. Salivettes were centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 5 minutes at RT, separated into aliquots, and stored at −80°C.

Blood collection kits were also sent by the study team to the enrolled participants. An external laboratory collected plasma and packaged specimen samples in barcoded containers, which were sent to UHN for processing. Binding antibodies to spike and RBD of Wuhan and Omicron BA.1 (the current circulating variant) were measured from these blood samples. Collection and shipping of specimens were performed at baseline (December 2022) and 4 months later (April 2023) simultaneously with the salivary collection.

Laboratory analysis

Binding antibody serological assays

Blood plasma samples were transferred to the Lunenfeld-Tanenbaum Research Institute at Sinai Health for processing (Sinai REB study 23-0069-E) by ELISA to test for IgG antibodies against the Wuhan spike trimer and RBD.22 The ELISA, developed in-house on serum and plasma, was optimized for sensitivity and specificity parameters and calibrated to the First World Health Organization International Standard 20/136 to report values in binding antibody units (BAU)/mL.

In addition, DBS samples were collected every 3 months as per the main protocol. Completed DBS cards were mailed to the research unit; checked for quality; registered in a REDCap database with the participant ID, date of collection, and date of receipt; and transferred to the Lunenfeld-Tanenbaum Research Institute at Sinai Health for processing (Sinai REB study 21-0112-E). Antibodies were eluted from DBS punches and tested by ELISA for antibodies (IgG) against the Wuhan spike trimer, its RBD, and the N protein. Correlations in the spike, RBD, and N assays between plasma and DBS were >0.95.

A new SARS-CoV-2 infection was determined either by self-report of a positive PCR or RAT for COVID-19 or a positive N antibody level above the threshold on testing. Determination of threshold was described previously.22 In brief, raw chemiluminescent values were normalized to a synthetic standard (anti-N IgG Ab from Genscript, catalog # A02039) included as a calibration curve on each assay plate to create relative ratios. A seropositivity threshold for anti-N antibodies was set at 99% specificity as defined by the receiver operating characteristic curve analysis: relative ratio of 0.642 at a 1:4 dilution of DBS eluate. The date of the infection was the first positive of either of the above.

Production of Wuhan and Omicron BA.1 SARS-CoV-2 spike and RBD antigens for the detection of anti-spike and anti-RBD IgA and IgG in saliva

The SARS-CoV-2 full-length spike trimer and RBD antigens used in these experiments were expressed, purified, and biotinylated as described.11,20 The RBD construct for both Wuhan and Omicron BA.1 was fused with a 6xHis tag followed by an AviTag. The soluble ectodomains of the spike proteins for Wuhan and Omicron BA.1 were fused with a phage foldon trimerization motif followed by a 6xHis tag and an AviTag. The resulting plasmids were used to stably transfect FreeStyle 293-F cells for the secretion of the RBD and soluble spike trimers using previously reported.20,21 The proteins were purified from the cell culture media using Ni-NTA affinity chromatography then site-specifically biotinylated in reactions containing 200 μM biotin, 500 μM ATP, 500 μM MgCl2, 30 μg/mL BirA, 0.1% (v/v) protease inhibitor cocktail, and not more than 100 μM of the protein-AviTag substrate. The reactions were incubated at 30 °C for 2 hours and the biotinylated proteins were then purified using size-exclusion chromatography to remove unreacted biotin. For the spike protein, negative stain electron microscopy was used to show evidence of high-quality trimers. Quality check of the RBD preparation was confirmed through binding to the CR3022 antibody using BioLayer Interferometry.

ELISA for detecting anti-spike and anti-RBD IgA and IgG in saliva to Wuhan and Omicron BA.1

An amplified (biotin/streptavidin) ELISA assay was used to detect IgG and IgA antibody levels to spike and RBD. Notably, we previously showed that salivary anti-spike and anti-RBD specific salivary IgA positively correlated with anti-spike/RBD specific salivary secretory IgA.20 In brief, streptavidin plates were coated with 50 μL per well of 2 μg/mL of biotinylated RBD or 20 μg/mL of biotinylated spike diluted in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Negative control wells were coated with 50 μL per well of sterile PBS. After coating with the antigen of interest and incubating overnight at 4 °C, the coating solution was discarded and plates were blocked with 200 μL per well of 5% BLOTTO solution (5% w/vol skim milk powder in sterile PBS [BioShop, catalog # SKI400.500]). Plates were incubated with the blocking solution at 37 °C for 2 hours, and the solution was discarded immediately prior to adding samples to each well. During the blocking incubation, frozen saliva samples were thawed and diluted using 2.5% BLOTTO at 1:5, 1:10, and 1:20 dilutions. Diluted samples were preincubated in separate streptavidin-coated plates with no antigen to reduce anti-streptavidin activity in the saliva for 30 minutes at 37 °C, after which 50 μL of each sample from the preadsorption plate was transferred to the corresponding wells of the antigen-coated plates and incubated for 2 hours at 37 °C. The plates were washed 3 times with PBS + 0.05% Tween 20 (PBS-T [BioShop, catalog # TWN510]). Fifty microliters of HRP-conjugated goat anti-human-IgG and IgA secondary antibodies (Southern Biotech, catalog # 2044-05 and 2053-05) were added to the appropriate wells at dilutions of 1:1,000 and 1:2,000 in 2.5% BLOTTO, respectively, and incubated for 1 hour at 37 °C. Plate development was done by adding 50 μL of 3,3′,5,5′tetramethylbenzidine Substrate Solution (Thermo Fisher, catalog # 00-4021-56) to each well. The reaction was then stopped by adding 50 µL/well of 1 N H2SO4, and optical density was read at a wavelength of 450 nm (OD450) on a spectrophotometer (Thermo Multiskan FC). We did not perform 570 nm background correction in our ELISA, which is typically done to correct optical imperfection of the plate. However, we always ran a separate PBS-only control for each sample and subtract that from all dilutions within that specific sample to account for background absorbance at 450 nm.

Analysis of saliva ELISA data

For each sample, the raw OD450 for the PBS control well was subtracted from the raw antigen-specific OD450 value, at each sample dilution (1:5, 1:10, 1:20). The adjusted OD450 value for each sample dilution was used to calculate the area under the curve (AUC) for each individual sample. This process was repeated for the positive controls, which consisted of pooled acute and convalescent COVID-19 patient saliva. We expressed each individual sample AUC as a percentage of the AUC for the preselected positive control—a relative antibody level designated as “% of positive control.” Each plate also included 1 to 3 negative controls, analyzed similarly. These negative controls consisted of seronegative samples, from a prevention trial conducted in early 2020.23 “Positive” cutoff values for each antigen-specific isotype were calculated using the following formula: average integrated scores of negative samples + 2 (SD of negative control integrated scores).

Statistical analysis

Cohort characteristics were compared based on hybrid or vaccine-only immunity using χ2 tests, Fisher exact tests, Student t-tests, or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, as appropriate. The salivary antibodies IgA and IgG to RBD and spike for the Wuhan and Omicron BA.1 at the baseline visit were compared between the hybrid and vaccine-only groups in 2 ways: (1) detection above the threshold for a negative control group and (2) % positive control (level).

The strength and direction of the association between the % positive control (level) of salivary antibodies and the log-transformed serum binding levels at the baseline and 4-month visits were measured using Spearman correlation.

The change in the levels (% of positive) of salivary antibodies in the hybrid immunity cohort was analyzed using longitudinal modeling between baseline and 4 months. We refer to this change as decay slope, and we used linear mixed models with subject-specific random intercepts and fixed effect covariates of age groups (30–50, ≥70 years), gender, comorbidity (any of diabetes cardiovascular disease, respiratory disease or cancer, yes/no) and the number of COVID-19 vaccine doses up to the baseline.

A Cox regression analysis examined protective factors against a new SARS-CoV-2 infection. The time from the 4-month follow-up visit date of this substudy to the first infection, or for those without a new infection up to February 24, 2024, was the outcome for the analysis. The analysis included fixed, time-independent predictor variables as above (detectable) salivary IgA against the Omicron BA.1 spike (yes/no), and vaccination (yes/no) after the 4-month follow-up until new infection or February 24, 2024.

Univariable and multivariable models were constructed. In longitudinal models, the coefficient represents the effect of the predictor on the average % positive control across all time-points, with positive values indicating higher levels and negative values indicating lower levels; the coefficient for time indicated whether % positive control increased or decreased over time. In these models, the reference value for the coefficients was 0. For the Cox models, we report hazard ratios for the covariates where the reference level was 1; tests of proportional hazards were conducted as was a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis for model robustness. A significance level of α = 0.05 was used for all statistical hypothesis tests. Data manipulation and analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4) and R software (version 4.2.2). The data sets are provided in the supplementary materials.

Results

One hundred and eighty participants were enrolled in the substudy (Fig. 1). Participants with a new SARS-CoV-2 infection or vaccination between the 2 sampling points were excluded. For the salivary substudy, we included 51 participants; 17 (33.3%) had a SARS-CoV-2 infection prior to the baseline visit and 34 (66.7%) were in the vaccine-only group. The demographics of study participants are shown in Table 1. The majority were female (67%) and aged >70 years (86%), had a White racial background (88%), never smoked (51%), had at least one comorbidity (71%), and received an average of 5 vaccine doses. The number of COVID-19 vaccine doses was the only characteristic differentiating the groups (P = 0.007), reflecting recommendations to defer vaccine for at least 6 months after a natural infection.

SARS-CoV-2 infection or vaccination before baseline visit

The median time from the most recent prior SARS-CoV-2 infection to the baseline visit for the hybrid group was 60 days (interquartile range [IQR], 32 to 75) and the median time from the most recent prior vaccine to the baseline visit for the vaccine-only group was 48 days (IQR, 34 to 75) (Table 1). More than 75% of the SARS-CoV-2 infections or last vaccine doses occurred within 90 days of the baseline sample time.

Salivary antibodies at baseline visit

The number of participants with detectable salivary IgA and IgG anti-RBD and anti-spike antibodies (Wuhan and Omicron BA.1) and the levels (% of positive control) were compared at baseline for hybrid vs. vaccine-only groups in Fig. 2. The proportion of hybrid participants with detectable IgA to Wuhan spike or RBD (94% and 76%, respectively) and to Omicron BA.1 spike or RBD (88% and 69%, respectively) was higher than that observed for vaccine-only participants with detectable IgA to Wuhan spike or RBD (59% and 65%, respectively) and Omicron BA.1 spike and RBD (18% and 71%, respectively). The level of these antibodies was a magnitude higher in the hybrid cohort.

Comparison of the IgA and IgG salivary antibodies to RBD and spike at baseline.

All participants had detectable salivary IgG levels against Wuhan spike and RBD. Of the hybrid immunity group, 94% had detectable salivary IgG levels against both Omicron BA.1 spike and RBD, while 91% of the vaccine-only group had detectable salivary IgG levels against both Omicron BA.1 spike and RBD.

For participants in the hybrid immunity group, there was a high variance in the level of spike and RBD-specific IgG and IgA at baseline (Fig. 2) and a trend in the correlation of the IgA against Wuhan RBD at the baseline visit with the number of days since the previous prior SARS-CoV-2 infection (regression coefficient value, 0.0078 [95% CI, −0.0008 to 0.0165]; P = 0.07; data not shown). The proximity between time of SARS-CoV-2 infection and the baseline timepoint may therefore account for the high variance in the level of spike and RBD-specific IgG and IgA at baseline in the hybrid immune group

Correlation analysis between salivary and serum binding antibody levels

We performed a correlation analysis of the levels of salivary antibody levels for those with detectable IgA and IgG antibodies to Wuhan and Omicron BA.1 RBD and spike with serum binding antibody levels against Wuhan RBD and spike (BAU/mL) (Figs. 3 and 4) for the hybrid immunity group. Salivary antibody levels against both Wuhan and Omicron BA.1 antigens were significantly and moderately positively correlated with anti-Wuhan serum binding antibody levels at both visits. Similar to what we previously observed,20 the correlation was stronger for salivary and serum IgG than for salivary and serum IgA (Fig. 5).

Salivary antibody decay analysis

As salivary antibody levels were low in the vaccine-only group, we focused on the decay slope for the hybrid immunity group. Univariable and multivariable decay analysis of salivary antibody levels between the 2 sampling time points was conducted (Fig. 6). The decay of slope over time for most salivary antibodies was significant. The decay slopes for IgA to spike and RBD to Wuhan and Omicron BA.1 in the hybrid subgroup were −0.37/−0.38 and −0.55/−0.52, respectively, and were not impacted by age, gender, presence of comorbidities, or the number of vaccine doses prior to the baseline evaluation (Table 2). New SARS-CoV-2 infection

Between the 4-month follow-up and up until February 24, 2024 (when there were sequentially Omicron XBB.1.5, EG.5.1, and JN circulating variants of SARS-CoV-2 infections in Ontario), there were 19 (37.3%) new SARS-CoV-2 infections of which 4 (7.8%) occurred in hybrid immunity participants. The median time to this infection was 163 days (IQR, 25 to 229) (Fig. 7). Only the receipt of an additional vaccine dose after the 4-month follow-up was significantly associated with protection with a hazard ratio of 0.19 (95% CI, 0.07 to 0.57) on multivariable or 0.21 (95% CI, 0.08 to 0.57) on univariable analysis. A positive IgA to the Omicron BA.1 spike was not associated with protection (P = 0.6; Fig. 8). We conducted a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, which demonstrated that the model coefficients remained largely stable, with no substantial influence from any single participant (data not shown).

Discussion

In our cohort of heavily vaccinated participants,21 the majority with hybrid immunity had detectable salivary IgA antibodies to RBD and spike proteins to Wuhan and Omicron BA.1 with a wide variability in the level. At the initial sampling, the level (% positivity of positive control) of salivary IgA was greater if the SARS-CoV-2 infection had occurred closer to the baseline visit. Although a sizeable proportion of those with vaccine-only immunity had detectable salivary IgA antibodies to RBD and spike proteins of Wuhan or Omicron BA.1, the levels of these antibodies were an order of magnitude lower than observed in the hybrid immunity group. From this, we conclude that recent SARS-CoV-2 infection rather than multiple vaccinations is more strongly associated with detection and the level of salivary SARS-CoV-2–specific antibodies. Our findings are similar to those of Havervall et al.,24 who detected spike-specific IgA and IgG in 62% and >99% of triple-vaccinated health care workers in Sweden. They similarly observed higher levels of spike-specific salivary IgA in participants with hybrid immunity.

In contrast to salivary IgA, the vast majority of both hybrid immunity and vaccine-only groups had detectable salivary IgG antibodies to Wuhan and Omicron BA.1, RBD and spike, although the hybrid immunity group had higher levels of these IgG antibodies. There was also a strong correlation between serum and salivary levels of spike and RBD in both vaccine-only and hybrid immunity subgroups, which was more marked for IgG than IgA, supporting the concept of an IgG spillover from the circulation to the mucosa.25

In previous work, we demonstrated the presence of IgG and modest levels of IgA to RBD and spike in the saliva after a single COVID-19 vaccine and a second mRNA vaccination boosted the IgG but not the IgA response.11 In the current study, our vaccine cohort had received an average of 5 vaccines prior to baseline and although a proportion had detectable salivary IgA compared to SARS-CoV-2–positive control samples, the levels of IgA were very low. Consistent with our findings, Cohen et al.26 demonstrated that although vaccines increase salivary antibody responses in infected persons, the change in antibody level is modest. Moreover, Lasrado et al.27 were unable to show augmented nasal IgA responses with the XBB.1.5 mRNA booster. Collectively these results demonstrate that multiple COVID-19 vaccines do not induce a mucosal immune response. This contrasts with a recent study by Declercq et al.,28 which showed that multiple vaccinations in the absence of infection were sufficient to sustain neutralizing antibodies to SARS-CoV-2 antigens in the mucosa using a mouse model; however, these antibodies are likely serum derived rather than locally produced.

Although our participants in the hybrid immunity cohort demonstrated high levels of salivary IgA to both Wuhan and Omicron BA.1, RBD and spike, the IgA level decayed between the baseline and 4-month sampling time. This decay was not impacted by age cohort, gender, presence of comorbidity nor the number of prior vaccine doses. This is consistent with our previous work in SARS-CoV-2–infected participants where salivary IgA levels strongly decayed over time.20 Similarly, Costantini et al.29 observed a peak of salivary IgA production 2 weeks after a SARS-CoV-2 infection with a rapid decrease after 9 weeks, and Cagigi et al.30 found that nasal IgG and IgA to spike protein fell to almost undetectable levels at 3 months. On the other hand, Gorochov et al.31 showed evidence of spike-specific salivary IgA in persons with hybrid immunity 1 month after vaccination with persistence over 180 days. In this case, however, infection occurred prior to vaccination rather than after a vaccine series. Thus, the order of exposure (infection followed by vaccination or vice versa) may dictate the persistence of anti-spike salivary IgA.

Mucosal anti-spike IgA has been previously shown to be associated with protection against breakthrough COVID-19 infections.24,32 Moreover, we have shown that salivary IgA to spike protein from hybrid immune participants can bind to different SARS-CoV-2 variants and even a distinct betacoronavirus (SARS-1),13 and intranasal vaccination against SARS-CoV-2 achieves the same cross-reactivity.33 However, while the increased level and breadth of specificity of salivary IgA in those with hybrid immunity would support the potential role for an intranasal vaccine, the decay in anti-spike/RBD salivary IgA levels over 4 months may result in a renewed risk of reinfection.

In our previous work, participants with breakthrough infections had lower levels of vaccine-induced serum IgA to spike and RBD at 2 to 4 weeks after the second vaccine dose compared to those without an infection, whereas IgG levels were comparable between groups.11,20 In that study, saliva was not available to confirm if the association seen with serum IgA was also observed in mucosal secretions. Moreover, the period of time between vaccination and breakthrough infections was short, with an outbreak occurring less than 28 days after the second vaccine dose in a long-term care Toronto cohort11 and after a median of 39 days in the Sheba health care worker cohort.24 Both cohorts were monitored during the pre-Omicron era. In this current study, over a 10-month period, we did not observe an association between the detection of IgA in saliva to Omicron BA.1 spike protein and protection against a new SARS-CoV-2 infection when Omicron variants were circulating. We believe this is due to poor IgA durability in the saliva. Although we were unable to specifically measure salivary antibodies to the new circulating variants (Omicron XBB.1.5, JN, and EG), it is reasonable to assume that there are cross-reacting IgA salivary antibodies.

The only association of protection from a breakthrough infection observed was receipt of a new COVID-19 vaccine booster (which was the Omicron XBB booster vaccine during the study period). Recent vaccination results in high levels of IgG in the serum that appear in the saliva. We hypothesize that salivary IgG that is matched to the variant is providing protection against breakthrough under conditions where salivary IgA is no longer present. It is also possible that both infection and vaccination are providing protection through alternative pathways—for example, other antibody-dependent effector functions or potent T cells responses that generate memory cells that protect against future infection. Alternatively, IgG antibodies at the mucosa of the respiratory tract could prevent viral infection by blocking virus attachment or function as opsonin as part of the local host defense in the airways.5

At first glance, these findings are in contrast to those of Haverall et al.,24 in Swedish health care workers where a correlation between low mucosal anti-spike IgA levels (>75%) and subsequent breakthrough infection over 8 months during the Omicron BA.2 and BA.5 dominance was observed.24,34 Zuo et al.32 also determined that salivary anti-RBD IgA levels (but not plasma anti-RBD IgG levels) were significantly lower in 9 persons with breakthrough infection than in 20 persons without such an infection. However, participants in the Havervall study with the highest mucosal spike IgA levels had experienced an infection prior to vaccination, which was not the case for our participants who instead experienced infections after vaccination. Moreover, the Haverall study used a different assay (Meso Scale) and had more Omicron breakthrough participants (n = 57), which may have allowed for an association to be observed.24

There are some limitations to our study: Our observations about protection are confined to the Omicron XBB.1.5, EG.5, and JN waves of infection and the results could vary with different SARS-CoV-2 variants. For example, the XBB variant has been shown to be relatively resistant to host defense mechanisms.27 Moreover, our failure to find an association could also be impacted by our modest sample size and infrequent observations. The study was designed with 2 time points: baseline and 4 months postintervention, during which a significant decline in salivary antibody levels was observed. Measuring at later time points would likely show continued decay, which would not add substantial new information. With our sample size and event rate if salivary antibodies had a protective effect, we would have expected a significant association in univariate analysis. We cannot account for unreported or asymptomatic infections that may not have been captured by self-reporting or in which antibodies to nucleoprotein did not increase above threshold or had decreased between sampling times. Further, there could be a misclassification bias with self-reporting, especially in the hybrid immunity group, although we only included those who documented a positive PCR or RAT as a new infection. Another limitation of our study was that the population consisted of people able and willing to volunteer for the research and who were mainly older and White. This may limit the generalizability of our findings and from this we cannot exclude certain forms of selection bias. On the other hand, some of the strengths of our study include the detailed data available on prior vaccines, infections, participant characteristics, and the prolonged follow-up. Remarkably, our cohort was more heavily vaccinated than previous work and yet levels salivary IgA levels remained low.

Conclusion

We demonstrated in a cohort of heavily vaccinated persons that salivary IgA antibody levels to spike and RBD are high in those with hybrid immunity but extremely low in those with vaccine-only immunity. SARS-CoV-2–specific salivary IgA waned over 4 months. We were unable to show an association of detectable salivary IgA to Omicron BA.1 spike with protection against a new SARS-CoV-2 infection during the 10-month observation period during which the Omicron XBB, EG.5.1, and JN variants were circulating in Ontario. Future work should contrast the impact of delivery of vaccines via different routes (intramuscular, intranasal, oral) on the durability of antigen-specific salivary IgA antibodies.
