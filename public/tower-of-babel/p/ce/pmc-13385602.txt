Machine learning-based incident duration prediction integrated with microscopic traffic simulation for expressway resilience evaluation
Authors: Ronnakorn Nakkliang, Noppakun Boongrapue, Ratthaphong Meesit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraffic incidents are a primary contributor to non-recurrent congestion on urban expressway networks, generating economic, safety, and operational impacts. Although machine learning has shown promise for incident duration prediction, a gap remains between predictive modelling and practical integration into traffic management workflows. This study proposes an integrated resilience-based decision-support framework that couples machine learning-based incident duration prediction with microscopic traffic simulation to support evidence-based incident management. A dataset of 2,772 incident records from the Expressway Authority of Thailand (EXAT) was used to train and evaluate four tree-based ensemble models: Random Forest, XGBoost, CatBoost, and LightGBM. LightGBM achieved the best test performance, with a test R2 of 0.506 and a mean absolute error (MAE) of 8.182 minutes for short-duration incidents of 0–40 minutes. The predicted incident duration was then used as an input to Aimsun Next simulation on the Chalong Rat Expressway, where four incident management strategies were assessed using the speed deficit area metric. To account for prediction uncertainty, three incident-duration cases were evaluated: 22, 30, and 38 minutes. The 30-minute case represents the LightGBM-predicted duration, while the lower- and upper-bound cases were defined using the short-duration MAE of approximately 8 minutes. The results show that reversible lane activation produced the strongest resilience performance across all tested duration cases, with the lowest deficit areas of 588, 809, and 1,038 km·min/hr. In contrast, shoulder-only restriction produced the poorest performance, with deficit areas of 1,376, 2,281, and 3,233 km·min/hr. Overall, the proposed framework demonstrates how incident duration prediction can be translated into simulation-based resilience assessment, providing traffic management centres with a structured, quantitative, and data-driven basis for comparing incident response strategies.Keywords: resilience assessment, expressway, incident duration prediction, machine learning, traffic simulation

1. Introduction

Traffic incidents represent one of the most significant sources of non-recurrent congestion in urban highway and expressway networks, contributing to an estimated 50–60% of total urban traffic delays. 1 The consequences of such events extend beyond travel time losses, encompassing broader economic, environmental, and safety impacts, including excessive fuel consumption, increased emissions, reduced operational efficiency, and heightened risk to road users.2,3 These concerns are particularly pronounced on expressways, where confined geometric configurations and high operating speeds can amplify incident severity and accelerate the propagation of congestion. Beyond immediate impacts, incident-induced disruptions can spread through surrounding road networks, resulting in widespread reductions in travel speed and degraded service levels. 3 In response, the concept of resilience has emerged as a central paradigm in modern transportation operations. Transportation resilience is generally understood as the capacity of a system to anticipate disturbances, absorb their impacts, adapt its operations, and restore service levels within an acceptable timeframe. 4 Key attributes include redundancy, robustness, and rapidity of recovery, with recovery rapidity being closely associated with incident duration. 5 Accurate prediction of incident duration from the early stages of an incident therefore provides an important foundation for traffic management agencies, enabling proactive resource allocation, timely traveller information dissemination, and the implementation of targeted traffic control measures to reduce system-wide impacts. 6

A substantial body of literature has examined incident duration prediction, with research gradually shifting from conventional statistical models to more flexible machine learning approaches. Ma et al. 7 applied gradient boosting decision trees to predict freeway incident clearance time, achieving MAPE values of 16.63% for incidents under 15 minutes and 33.15% for longer incidents, while also identifying incident response time as the most influential factor. Tang et al. 8 used an extreme gradient boosting model combined with K-means clustering and Bayesian hyperparameter optimisation, achieving MAPE values of 0.348 and 0.221 for shorter- and longer-duration clearance clusters, respectively, and outperforming SVR, random forest, and AdaBoost. Tang et al. 9 further emphasized the broader transition from conventional parametric models toward data-driven prediction methods. To address uncertainty in model specification, Zou et al. 10 introduced Bayesian model averaging and achieved an MAE of 5.97 minutes and RMSE of 15.65 minutes, demonstrating that combining multiple candidate models can improve prediction robustness.

Recent studies have further advanced incident duration prediction by improving model structures, integrating classification and regression tasks, and using richer data sources. Grigorev et al. 6 proposed a bi-level machine learning framework that first classifies incident duration categories before estimating duration, with the IEO-ML approach outperforming baseline models in 66% of tested scenarios and achieving MAPE as low as 9.34% for short-term incidents using LightGBM. Hamad et al. 11 evaluated eleven machine learning classifiers using more than 110,000 freeway incident records and found that SVM and k-nearest neighbour achieved the highest classification accuracy of 97%. Deep learning and explainable tabular models have also gained attention. Shang et al. 12 developed a hybrid topic modelling and BiLSTM framework for incomplete multi-source incident data, achieving RMSE of 15.72 minutes and MAE of 13.12 minutes. Li and Li 2 introduced an explanatory TabNet-based model, achieving MAE of 17.04 minutes and RMSE of 22.01 minutes, while maintaining interpretability. More recent work has incorporated dynamic contextual variables, such as real-time weather conditions, into hazard-based duration models, with Zeng et al. 13 showing improved model fit using a grouped random-parameters Weibull specification. Corbally et al. 14 further confirmed the practical effectiveness of machine learning for motorway incident duration prediction, with SVM achieving MAE of 40.66 minutes and outperforming ANN-based approaches. Among ensemble methods, LightGBM has become particularly attractive for structured tabular incident data because of its computational efficiency, ability to capture complex nonlinear feature interactions, and strong performance in operational prediction tasks.

Despite these advances, prediction accuracy remains a key challenge in incident duration modelling. Incident clearance time is highly variable because it depends on uncertain and interrelated factors such as incident severity, response time, roadway conditions, traffic demand, weather, and clearance operations. As a result, model performance differs considerably across datasets, incident types, and duration groups. Short-duration incidents are often easier to predict, while longer and more severe incidents tend to produce larger errors. Therefore, although advanced machine learning models improve accuracy compared with conventional approaches, incident duration prediction should still be interpreted as an operational estimate rather than an exact clearance time.

Parallel to these predictive modelling advances, a growing body of research has developed quantitative frameworks for evaluating transportation network resilience under unplanned disruptions. Pant provided an early foundation for transportation network resiliency by showing that recovery capacity depends not only on network structure but also on redundancy and demand-side behavioural flexibility. 15 Freckleton et al. examined transportation network resiliency following disruptive events and emphasized the need for performance-based measures that can quantify the degradation and recovery of transportation systems. 16 Balal et al. compared different measures for urban highway network resilience under traffic incidents and demonstrated the usefulness of area-based performance loss indicators for capturing cumulative disruption impacts. Subsequent studies extended resilience evaluation by incorporating dynamic traffic processes, network topology, and emergency management perspectives. 5 Wang et al. 17 applied a diffusion graph convolutional recurrent neural network to evaluate and predict transportation resilience under extreme weather events, showing that both spatial network dependencies and temporal traffic dynamics are important in resilience assessment. Yu et al. 18 proposed a Pressure–State–Response framework combined with dynamic Bayesian networks to model road operational resilience under emergency scenarios, identifying rescue resource availability and response disposal time as important factors affecting recovery. Yu et al. 19 further evaluated road network resilience to traffic accidents from an emergency management perspective, showing that resilience varies spatially across network segments and that timely coordinated response is critical for limiting cumulative performance loss. These studies demonstrate that resilience is not determined solely by the occurrence of disruption, but also by the system’s ability to maintain performance, adapt operationally, and recover rapidly after incident onset.

Despite these concurrent advances, most existing studies have addressed incident duration prediction and resilience assessment as separate research problems. Prediction-focused studies generally emphasize model accuracy, influential factors, or algorithmic comparison, but often stop short of translating predicted incident durations into operational response strategies. Conversely, resilience assessment studies commonly evaluate network performance under predefined incident scenarios, but do not always incorporate incident-specific prediction outputs into the assessment process. As a result, a critical gap remains between predictive model development and practical operational implementation. There remains a need for tools that translate machine learning predictions into structured decision-support processes for transportation agencies, 20 while the transportation sector has yet to fully leverage machine learning capabilities for evaluating and improving infrastructure resilience. 21 Based on these identified gaps, this research addresses the following questions.

• Which tree-based ensemble machine learning model provides the most accurate and generalisable prediction of traffic incident duration using real-world expressway data from the Bangkok Metropolitan Region?

• Which tree-based ensemble machine learning model provides the most accurate and generalisable prediction of traffic incident duration using real-world expressway data from the Bangkok Metropolitan Region?

• How can machine learning-based incident duration prediction and microscopic traffic simulation be operationally integrated into a unified decision-support framework for traffic management centres?

To address these questions, this study proposes an integrated resilience-based decision-support framework that couples machine learning-based incident duration prediction with microscopic traffic simulation for quantitative resilience assessment of expressway networks. The main contribution of this study lies not in proposing a new prediction algorithm, but in operationally linking prediction outputs with simulation-based resilience evaluation to support the comparison of incident response strategies. The practical applicability of the proposed framework is demonstrated through a case study on the Chalong Rat Expressway in Bangkok, Thailand.

2. Proposed resilience framework

In response to the challenges of traffic incident management, this research proposes a resilience-based incident management framework that systematically integrates a machine learning prediction model with a traffic simulation model for resilience assessment, as shown in Figure 1. The framework is structured around three interconnected modules: an incident duration prediction module (Module 1), a traffic simulation module (Module 2), and a resilience assessment module (Module 3). The process begins with data collection encompassing two streams: incident data for model training and simulation data for network development. In Module 1, the incident data undergoes preprocessing, model development, and model evaluation to produce a predicted incident duration. This predicted duration serves as the critical link to Module 2, where it is inserted into the simulation environment following network development, calibration, and scenario definition, enabling performance evaluation under realistic incident conditions. The outputs of Module 2 feed directly into Module 3, which performs resilience assessment across all evaluated strategies, identifies the best-performing strategy, and generates operational recommendations for traffic operators.

2.1. Data

The data used in this study consist of two main components corresponding to the two core modules of the proposed framework: incident data for machine learning model development and traffic data for simulation model construction. For Module 1, the incident duration prediction model was developed using traffic incident records obtained from the Expressway Authority of Thailand (EXAT). The dataset contains 2,772 incident records collected during fiscal year 2023 and includes information related to incident characteristics, environmental conditions, and operational response details. The dependent variable was defined as total incident duration in minutes, representing the time required for incident handling and clearance. The dataset was pre-processed and transformed into a structured format suitable for machine learning-based analysis. A detailed description of the variables used in the prediction model is provided in Section 2.2. For Module 2, traffic data were obtained from field sensors operated by EXAT and were used to support the development and calibration of the microscopic traffic simulation model. These data were used to define traffic demand inputs and to ensure that simulated traffic conditions realistically represent observed traffic behaviour. The integration of incident data and traffic data enables a consistent linkage between prediction outputs and simulation-based scenario analysis within the proposed framework.

2.2. Module 1: Incident duration prediction model

The incident duration prediction module was developed to estimate total incident duration as a continuous variable using machine learning-based regression techniques. The modelling workflow followed a structured pipeline consisting of data preprocessing, feature engineering, model development and performance evaluation. After preprocessing, the dataset was divided into training and testing subsets using an 80:20 split, where the training set was used for model development and the test set was used for out-of-sample evaluation. The model was trained using a comprehensive set of predictor variables representing multiple dimensions of traffic incidents. These variables include temporal, incident-specific, environmental, spatial, operational, and management-related characteristics (Table 1).

Temporal variables capture time-related patterns such as day of the week, time of day, month, and public holiday conditions. Incident-specific variables describe the nature and severity of the event, including incident type, driver condition, vehicle-related cause, visibility condition, accident severity, vehicle type involved, and casualty-related attributes. Environmental variables account for weather conditions at the time of the incident, while spatial variables represent roadway characteristics such as expressway route, lane type, and location. Operational variables describe response-related aspects such as assisting vehicle type and incident reporter, and management-related variables capture response timing, including initial notification time and response travel time. The target variable of the model is total incident duration in minutes.

In detail, four tree-based ensemble regression models were evaluated: Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost. Hyperparameter tuning was performed using GridSearchCV with 5-fold cross-validation, with the coefficient of determination (R2) used as the optimisation criterion. Model performance was evaluated using both R2 and Mean Absolute Error (MAE), where MAE provides a directly interpretable measure of prediction error in minutes. Additional diagnostic analyses, including real-versus-predicted comparisons, and residual plots, were used to assess model behaviour. To enhance model interpretability, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were employed to quantify the marginal contribution of each predictor variable to the model output. 22

2.3. Module 2: Traffic simulation model

The traffic simulation model was developed using Aimsun Next Version 24.0.2 under a student license, a widely recognized microscopic traffic simulation software for analysing transportation systems at various scales. The student license did not affect the simulation outputs because the tested network size, output resolution, and number of evaluated scenarios were within the permitted software capacity, and no functional limitations were encountered during the analysis. The model was constructed to represent the operational characteristics of the study expressway segment, including network geometry, lane configuration, and traffic control conditions. Traffic demand inputs were incorporated using field traffic data obtained from EXAT sensors to reflect observed traffic conditions under normal operating scenarios. The model development followed a structured workflow consisting of network development, demand input specification, scenario definition, parameter adjustment, and calibration (Figure 2). After constructing the network and defining simulation scenarios, key behavioural parameters, particularly those related to car following behaviour, were adjusted to reproduce realistic traffic flow dynamics. Calibration was performed by comparing simulated outputs with observed traffic data. Model parameters were iteratively adjusted until acceptable agreement was achieved between simulated and observed conditions. To quantitatively evaluate the calibration accuracy, the GEH statistic was employed as a goodness of fit measure for comparing simulated and observed traffic volumes. The GEH statistics provide a robust measure for assessing the consistency between modelled and real-world traffic conditions and has been widely adopted in traffic microsimulation studies. In general, GEH values below 5 indicate good agreement between simulated and observed data, while higher values suggest that further model refinement may be required. Calibration results indicate that the simulation model achieved a high level of agreement with observed traffic conditions. The GEH statistic was calculated using traffic volume data collected from four field sensors installed along the study expressway segment. The results show that all sensor locations satisfied the acceptance criterion (GEH < 5.0), with an average GEH value of approximately 0.19. This indicates that the simulated traffic volumes closely match the observed data, confirming that the model is sufficiently calibrated for subsequent scenario analysis. This calibration approach is consistent with established practices in traffic simulation. 23 Once the model was satisfactorily calibrated, it was used to simulate an incident scenario and different traffic management strategies. The resulting outputs were subsequently used as inputs for the resilience assessment module described in Section 2.4.

2.4. Module 3: Resilience assessment

The resilience of the traffic system under incident conditions was evaluated using a performance-based approach derived from time series traffic data obtained from the simulation model. In this study, average traffic speed was used as the primary performance indicator to represent network operating conditions over time. The impact of an incident was quantified as the cumulative loss in traffic performance relative to a predefined reference speed, which represents normal operating conditions. To quantify this performance loss (L), the concept of deficit area was adopted. The deficit area represents the cumulative deviation of observed traffic speed from the reference speed over the duration of the incident and captures both the magnitude and duration of performance degradation (Figure 3). Since simulation outputs are generated at discrete time intervals, the deficit area was calculated using a numerical approximation based on the trapezoidal rule. In Equation 2, Vk represents the average traffic speed at time step k , Vtarget is the reference speed under normal operating conditions, Δt is the time interval between observations (1 min in this study), and N is the total number of time steps. Lower values of the deficit area indicate better system performance and higher resilience, reflecting smaller deviations from normal operating conditions and faster recovery. Conversely, higher values indicate more severe and prolonged traffic disruption. This metric was used to compare the effectiveness of different incident management strategies within the proposed framework.

3. Results

3.1. Case study

The study area is defined at two levels corresponding to the two main components of the proposed framework. For the incident duration prediction model, the dataset covers the expressway network in the Bangkok Metropolitan Region, which is operated by the Expressway Authority of Thailand (EXAT) as depicted in Figure 4. This network-level dataset provides diverse incident conditions and operational characteristics necessary for developing a generalised machine learning model. For the simulation-based resilience assessment, a specific case study segment was selected from the Chalong Rat Expressway. The selected segment extends from kilometre post 8+100 to kilometre post 12+100 and consists of three lanes in each direction, including a designated reversible lane corridor. This segment was chosen due to its operational complexity, the presence of multiple on-ramp and off-ramp connections, and its suitability for evaluating dynamic traffic management strategies under incident conditions.

Expressway Authority of Thailand network (adapted from Wikipedia).

As illustrated in Figure 5, the reversible lane section is bounded by the end of the reversible lane at km 8+100 and the start of the reversible lane at km 12+100. A toll plaza ramp is located near km 8+000, which introduces additional merging traffic into the mainline. The incident location was defined at km 10+400, approximately at the midpoint of the study segment, in order to capture upstream congestion propagation and downstream recovery behaviour within the simulation. This configuration allows the model to realistically represent traffic dynamics under incident conditions and to evaluate the effectiveness of different management strategies, particularly those involving reversible lane operations. This segment also reflects typical expressway operating conditions in Bangkok, making it suitable for demonstrating the applicability of the proposed framework.

Chalong Rat Expressway infrastructure configuration.

3.2. Performance analysis of incident prediction model results

The performance of the incident duration prediction models was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R2) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) for both the training and test datasets. Table 2 presents the comparative results of four tree-based ensemble models, namely LightGBM, CatBoost, Random Forest, and XGBoost. Overall, LightGBM achieved the best predictive performance on the test dataset, with a test R2 of 0.506 and a test MAE of 10.251 minutes. This indicates that LightGBM was able to explain approximately 50.6% of the variance in incident duration while producing the lowest average prediction error among the evaluated models. CatBoost showed the second-best performance, with a test R2 of 0.495 and a test MAE of 10.816 minutes. Although its predictive accuracy was slightly lower than that of LightGBM, CatBoost demonstrated relatively stable performance between the training and test datasets, suggesting good generalization capability. Random Forest and XGBoost produced lower test performance, with test R2 values of 0.453 and 0.430, respectively. Their corresponding test MAE values were 11.494 minutes for Random Forest and 11.688 minutes for XGBoost, indicating larger prediction errors compared with LightGBM and CatBoost.

In practical terms, the test MAE of 10.251 minutes achieved by LightGBM means that the predicted incident duration differs from the observed duration by approximately 10 minutes on average. This level of error can be considered reasonably low for expressway incident management, where incident duration is affected by several uncertain factors, including incident severity, location, traffic conditions, emergency response time, and clearance operations. In addition, the comparison between training and test results also provides insight into model generalisation. LightGBM achieved the highest training R2 of 0.813 and the lowest training MAE of 6.784 minutes, but the reduction in performance on the test dataset suggests some degree of overfitting. Nevertheless, LightGBM still provided the best overall test accuracy and a practical level of prediction error. As a result, LightGBM was selected as the incident duration prediction model for integration into the proposed framework.

It is noted that the diagnostic plots in Figure 6 show that all models predict short- (0-40 min) and medium-duration (40-60 min) incidents reasonably well, as most points are close to the 1:1 reference line. However, for long-duration (60+ min) incidents, the points tend to fall below the reference line, indicating systematic under-prediction. The residual plots also show larger dispersion for higher predicted values, meaning that prediction uncertainty increases as incident duration becomes longer. This is likely caused by the imbalanced and highly right-skewed distribution of the incident-duration data. As shown in Figure 7, the majority of incidents are concentrated at relatively short durations, with most observations clustered around 20–50 minutes. The overall mean duration is approximately 38.1 minutes, with a relatively large standard deviation of 24.5 minutes, and the distribution exhibits a strong positive skew (skewness = 3.12). In contrast, incidents lasting more than 60 minutes account for only about 10.5% of the dataset, indicating that long-duration events are comparatively rare. Notably, the 60-minute threshold is close to mean + 1 SD (approximately 62.6 minutes), which further supports the view that long-duration incidents lie in the upper tail of the distribution rather than representing typical events. As a result, when a severe incident occurs, the model is more likely to pull the prediction toward the centre of the distribution, leading to systematic under-prediction of long durations.

For all models, the prediction error is relatively low for short-duration incidents, ranging from approximately 8.18 to 9.58 minutes, and increases slightly for medium-duration incidents, ranging from about 9.47 to 10.90 minutes. In both intervals, LightGBM gives the lowest MAE, suggesting that it performs best for the majority of common incident cases. 3.3. SHAP analysis results

The SHAP results (Figure 9) indicate that the LightGBM model predicts incident duration using a combination of operational, temporal, spatial, and incident-characteristic factors. Travel_duration_min has the strongest effect: higher values generally move SHAP values to the right, meaning longer response travel time increases the predicted incident duration, while lower values reduce it. Time_minutes, which represents the incident occurrence time measured as minutes from midnight, also affects the prediction; higher or lower values reflect different times of day, so the impact varies depending on when the incident occurs. For coded categorical variables, such as Expressway, Route, Vehicle_cause, Type_of_accident, Driver_condition, Assisting_vehicle, and Reporter, the numeric values represent category labels rather than a true ranking; therefore, higher codes do not necessarily mean a greater physical amount, but they show that some categories are associated with longer or shorter predicted durations. For Property_damage, higher values tend to increase predicted duration, suggesting that incidents involving property damage require more handling, inspection, or clearance time. Similarly, Month, Day_of_week, and Holiday capture temporal differences, where some months, weekdays, weekends, or holidays may be associated with longer incident durations due to changes in traffic demand, weather, or operational conditions. These findings enhance the interpretability of the model and provide a practical basis for traffic management centre operators to identify high-risk incidents and prioritise response resources accordingly.

3.4. Traffic simulation model results

3.4.1. Incident and mitigation strategies

To demonstrate the proposed integrated framework, a simulation case study was designed on the Chalong Rat Expressway. The incident scenario was defined using the predicted duration obtained from the LightGBM model and then implemented in Aimsun Next to evaluate alternative traffic management strategies. In all scenarios, the incident was located at km 10+400 and occurred at 10:30 during the off-peak period. The incident involved the closure of three lanes, including the shoulder lane, leaving only the innermost lane available for traffic. A 30-minute incident duration was applied in the simulation, with the incident cleared at 11:00. This duration was not selected arbitrarily, but was based on the predicted duration generated by the LightGBM model within the proposed framework. To account for prediction uncertainty, the model error was also considered using the MAE of approximately 8 minutes for short-duration incidents. Accordingly, three simulation cases were developed: a lower-bound case of 22 minutes, a base case of 30 minutes, and an upper-bound case of 38 minutes. This approach allows the simulation to represent a prediction-informed incident condition while also evaluating the sensitivity of traffic impacts to reasonable variation in the predicted clearance time. Four incident management scenarios were evaluated under the same prediction-informed incident condition. Scenario 1 represents the do-nothing baseline, in which no formal traffic management action is applied. However, the simulation allows limited shoulder use to reflect common driving behaviour observed in Thailand, where some drivers may move onto the shoulder under congested or blocked conditions to avoid queuing and maintain movement. Scenario 2 applies controlled shoulder closure to restrict shoulder use during the incident. Scenario 3 introduces reversible lane activation while allowing shoulder utilisation, with the reversible lane opened 10 minutes after incident onset at 10:40. Scenario 4 combines reversible lane activation at 10:40 with controlled shoulder closure. These scenarios enable the proposed framework to compare passive, restrictive, capacity-enhancing, and combined strategies under consistent incident conditions. The details of four evaluated strategies are depicted in Figure 10.

3.4.2. Simulation results

The simulation results are presented in Figure 11. During the pre-incident phase from 10:00 to 10:30, all four scenarios exhibited stable average speeds ranging from 80 to 84 km/h. The average speed during this period was approximately 82.5 km/h, representing the observed free-flow mean speed used as the reference speed for the resilience assessment. This pre-incident performance was closely aligned with Scenario 0, which represents the no-incident baseline condition. Following the incident at 10:30, all scenarios showed a rapid reduction in speed, but the recovery patterns differed clearly across the evaluated management strategies and incident-duration cases. Across the 22-, 30-, and 38-minute cases, Scenario 3 consistently demonstrated the fastest recovery after the reversible lane was activated at 10:40. The additional capacity provided by the reversible lane helped stabilize speeds shortly after activation and allowed traffic conditions to return to near-baseline levels earlier than the other management strategies.

Time-speed diagram across scenarios for 30 ± 8 minutes incident duration.

Scenario 1, representing the do-nothing condition with informal shoulder use, showed a moderate speed decline and gradual recovery. However, as the assumed incident duration increased from 22 to 38 minutes, the speed reduction became deeper and recovery was delayed. Scenario 2, which restricts shoulder use, consistently produced the most severe speed deterioration and slowest recovery, especially in the 30- and 38-minute cases. This indicates that shoulder restriction alone may reduce the system’s adaptive capacity under incident conditions when no additional capacity is provided.

Scenario 4, which combines reversible lane activation with shoulder closure, improved performance compared with Scenario 1 and Scenario 2, but its recovery remained slower than Scenario 3. This suggests that the benefit of reversible lane activation was partly offset by restricting shoulder use. Overall, the sensitivity analysis confirms that although longer incident durations lead to greater speed loss and delayed recovery, the relative performance pattern remains generally consistent: Scenario 3 provides the strongest recovery performance, followed by Scenario 4, Scenario 1, and Scenario 2.

It is noted that a secondary speed reduction was consistently observed across all four scenarios immediately following incident clearance. This post-clearance phenomenon reflects the merging dynamics induced by vehicles that had accumulated at ramp junctions during the blockage period, producing a transient merging bottleneck that temporarily suppressed average speeds before flowing re-equilibrated.

3.5. Resilience assessment results

The speed deficit area results indicate clear differences in resilience performance across the four incident management scenarios under the 30 ± 8 minute sensitivity cases (Figure 12). Scenario 3 consistently produced the smallest accumulated speed deficit across all tested durations, with deficit areas of 588 km·min/hr for the 22-minute case, 809 km·min/hr for the 30-minute predicted case, and 1,038 km·min/hr for the 38-minute case. Scenario 4 showed the second-best performance, with deficit areas of 741, 958, and 1,223 km·min/hr, respectively. In contrast, Scenario 1 produced higher deficit areas of 997, 1,571, and 2,188 km·min/hr, while Scenario 2 resulted in the largest deficits of 1,376, 2,281, and 3,233 km·min/hr. It should be noted that these results should be interpreted within the tested off-peak period at 10:30 AM and the specific incident location and blockage condition. Under peak-hour demand, the effectiveness of Scenario 3 may differ because limited spare capacity could reduce the benefit of reversible lane activation.

Deficit area comparison across scenarios for 30 ± 8 minutes incident duration.

Scenario 3 demonstrated the strongest resilience performance among all tested strategies across the three incident-duration sensitivity cases. Compared with Scenario 1, Scenario 3 reduced the accumulated speed deficit by approximately 41.0%, 48.5%, and 52.6% for the 22-, 30-, and 38-minute cases, respectively. The improvement was even greater when compared with Scenario 2, with reductions of approximately 57.3%, 64.5%, and 67.9%, respectively. Relative to Scenario 4, Scenario 3 also achieved lower deficit areas across all duration cases, with reductions of approximately 20.6%, 15.6%, and 15.1%, confirming that reversible lane activation alone provided the most effective recovery performance among the evaluated operational interventions.

Scenario 4 also improved system resilience compared with Scenario 1 and Scenario 2. However, it remained less effective than Scenario 3, suggesting that the benefit of reversible lane activation was partly offset by the simultaneous restriction of shoulder use. In contrast, Scenario 2 produced the largest deficit area in all duration cases, indicating that shoulder lane prohibition alone did not improve resilience and may worsen traffic performance under incident conditions. In the Thai expressway context, where drivers may adaptively use the shoulder under heavy congestion to avoid queuing and maintain movement, restricting shoulder utilisation without providing an alternative capacity-management measure can increase cumulative speed loss and reduce operational resilience.

Overall, the figure confirms the operational value of the integrated resilience assessment framework. In practical application, the predicted incident duration from the LightGBM model can be used to define incident conditions in the microscopic traffic simulation. The resulting deficit area can then provide traffic management centre operators with a quantitative basis for comparing alternative response strategies in near real time. This enables incident management to move beyond reactive decision-making and toward a proactive, data-driven approach for selecting strategies that minimise speed loss and improve expressway resilience.

4. Conclusion

This research developed and demonstrated an integrated resilience assessment framework for expressway traffic incident management by combining machine learning-based incident duration prediction with microscopic traffic simulation. Four tree-based ensemble models were evaluated using R2 and MAE, and LightGBM was selected as the most suitable model due to its best test performance, with an R2 of 0.506 and an overall MAE of 10.251 minutes. This level of prediction error suggests that the model can provide a practical operational estimate of incident duration for traffic management applications.

The simulation-based case study on the Chalong Rat Expressway demonstrated the usefulness of the proposed framework for comparing alternative incident management strategies. Within the scope of the tested off-peak incident scenario involving the blockage of two main lanes and the shoulder lane at the selected expressway location, reversible lane activation produced the strongest resilience performance across all tested incident-duration cases, with the lowest speed deficit areas of 588, 809, and 1,038 km·min/hr for the 22-, 30-, and 38-minute scenarios, respectively. Compared with the do-nothing baseline, this represents reductions of approximately 41.0%, 48.5%, and 52.6%, indicating a consistent improvement in system recovery. Scenario 4 also improved resilience, with deficit areas of 741, 958, and 1,223 km·min/hr, but remained less effective than Scenario 3. In contrast, the shoulder-only control strategy produced the worst outcomes across all duration cases. The results suggest that restricting shoulder use without providing complementary capacity-management measures may have limited effectiveness in improving operational resilience. However, this strategy may still offer potential safety benefits by reducing uncontrolled shoulder utilisation and improving lane discipline during incident conditions. Thus, future research should further examine the trade-off between operational resilience and safety performance.

Overall, the results confirm that integrating incident duration prediction with microscopic traffic simulation can support proactive and data-driven incident management. By using predicted incident duration as an input to simulation-based what-if analysis, traffic management centre operators can compare candidate response strategies quantitatively and select the option that minimizes cumulative speed loss and improves expressway resilience.

Future research should also be extended to additional expressway corridors, incident locations, lane-blockage configurations, traffic demand levels, and peak-hour conditions to further validate the generalisability of the framework. In addition, future work should incorporate safety analysis alongside operational resilience evaluation to assess whether a strategy that improves traffic flow also maintains or improves safety. Finally, real-time implementation challenges, including integration with traffic management centre data systems, automated scenario generation, and near-real-time what-if simulation pipelines, should be explored to support practical deployment.
