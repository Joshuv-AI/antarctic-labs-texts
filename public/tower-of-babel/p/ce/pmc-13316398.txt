Prospective Evaluation of a Simplified Discharge and Temporary Pacing Algorithm After Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement
Authors: Gulia Faizi, Edris Faiz, Fatima Sayid Ali, Lene Hüche Nielsen, Henrik Nissen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe increasing patient volume undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) necessitates procedural simplification and short admissions without compromising safety. The objective of this study was to prospectively evaluate a novel electrocardiogram (ECG)-based algorithm for discharge and the use of temporary pacemakers (TPMs) in pacemaker-naïve TAVR patients.MethodsPatients were stratified into next-day discharge, second-day discharge, or third-day discharge groups based on preprocedural right bundle branch block and immediate postprocedural ECG changes. Prophylactic TPMs were restricted to patients with pre-existing right bundle branch block or a PQ interval >250 ​ms.ResultsAmong 664 patients, the mean age was 80.8 ​± ​5.7 years, 46.0% were females, and 69.8% were discharged as planned or earlier. Self-expandable valves and balloon-expandable valves were used in 28.0 and 72.0%, respectively. The shorter the planned observation, the larger the fraction of discharge delays caused by nonconduction issues (next-day discharge 68.9%, second-day discharge 43.6%, and third-day discharge 10.2%, respectively, p ​< ​0.001). A permanent pacemaker (PPM) was implanted in 11.5%. Although consensus criteria suggested TPMs in 39.2% of cases, the algorithm reduced this to 4.5% without compromising safety. The 30-day readmission rate for PPMs was 2.0%, and no out-of-hospital deaths were related to rhythm disturbances.ConclusionsA discharge algorithm based on preprocedure and immediate postprocedure ECG in PPM-naïve patients undergoing TAVR, including a high threshold for TPM insertion, enables postimplant discharge planning in most cases and significantly reduces the use of TPMs without compromising safety during hospitalization or after discharge.Keywords: Conduction disturbances, Discharge, Discharge algorithm, Permanent pacemaker (PPM), Prospective temporary pacemaker (TPM), Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR)

Introduction

In transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), safe early discharge is increasingly prioritized.1, 2, 3, 4 Next-day or even same-day discharge is becoming increasingly feasible. However, early discharge can be compromised by conduction disturbances occurring during or shortly after the TAVR procedure, necessitating prolonged telemetry or permanent pacemaker (PM) (PPM) implantation. The expected rise in TAVR procedures due to both an aging population and a gradual shift toward using TAVR in younger patients necessitates discharge algorithms that safely allow for early discharge while maintaining a high safety level to avoid complications and readmissions for conduction issues. Previous studies have retrospectively looked at risk markers for PPM need, high-degree atrioventricular (AV) block (HAVB), and effect of prolonged postdischarge electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring resulting in American5,6 and European guidelines.7 Both retrospective8 as well as prospective evaluation9 of these guidelines have been conducted.

Since 2020, we have implemented an algorithm based on the presence or absence of right bundle branch block (RBBB) in the pre-TAVR ECG and findings on the immediate postprocedural ECG. This algorithm simplifies the 2019 consensus criteria and restricts temporary PM (TPM) indications to streamline workflow for ward physicians and optimize resource use. The aim of this study was to prospectively evaluate the safety of this algorithm and describe the reasons for deviations from the expected discharge timeline.

Methods

Patients

Data were collected from consecutive PPM-naïve patients having femoral TAVR procedures from January 1, 2020, when the discharge algorithm was implemented, until May 29, 2024, at Odense University Hospital in Odense, Denmark. Exclusion criteria were patients with a previously implanted PPM; those who underwent prophylactic PPM implantation before the TAVR procedure (combined RBBB and left anterior or posterior fascicular block or RBBB and first-degree AV block); or where decision to implant a PPM immediately after the TAVR procedure was made for either Mobitz type II AV block or third-degree AV block, developed and persisting during the procedure, regardless if a PPM was implanted in direct continuation of the TAVR procedure or, for logistical reasons, after TPM implantation. Data on excluded patients with prophylactic or periprocedural PM indications are given in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. Furthermore, patients were excluded if overall clinical condition rather than ECG findings was the primary determinant of discharge timing. This included acute procedures for cardiogenic shock or those performed during the index admission for acute decompensation, patients with complications leading to death during the procedure, vascular surgery needing general anesthesia, or pericardial tamponade (Figure 1).

Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation Procedure

All procedures were performed under local anesthesia in a simplified setup without anesthesia personnel, routine use of bladder catheters, or central venous lines. Ventricular pacing was via the implantation wire in the left ventricle. A transjugular prophylactic TPM set to 30 beats per minute was used in cases of pre-existing RBBB or PQ > 250 ​ms. Complications were evaluated according to Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 (VARC-3).10

Balloon-expandable valves (BEVs) were aimed at 90%/10% aortic/ventricular position and self-expandable valves (SEVs) (Portico/Navitor and Evolut series) at 3 to 5 ​mm implant depth in the cusp overlay projection. Accurate valves implanted in 3-cusp view with bottom of marker at annular level.

Postprocedure Follow-Up

Immediately postprocedure, a 12-lead ECG was taken, and patients were categorized into discharge groups (Figures 2 and 3) based on the presence or absence of pre-TAVR RBBB and post-TAVR ECG changes. This defined discharge timing as follows: next-day discharge (NDD) for group 1; second-day discharge (2ndDD) for groups 2, 4, and 5; or third-day discharge (3rdDD) for groups 3 and 6.

Postprocedure follow-up data, including ECG findings, reasons for delayed discharge, predischarge and postdischarge PPM, readmissions, and death, were systematically collected. Complete mortality follow-up was obtained via the Danish Civil Registration System. Standard definitions of conduction disturbances were used,6 albeit with Mobitz type II AV block or third-degree AV block with identical handling grouped as HAVB. Daily ECGs until planned discharge day determined if discharge was possible, in which case patients were discharged according to Figures 2 and 3 providing no other clinical or ECG issues warranted continued observation. Regardless of discharge group assignment, if ECG normalized to pre-TAVR ECG or a PPM was implanted early, discharge before planned day without home-monitoring was allowed.

Real-time ECG home-monitoring (Pocket ECG; Medicalgorithmics S.A., Warsaw, Poland) according to Figures 2 and 3 captured both symptomatic and asymptomatic events with immediate alarms to our department for relevant arrhythmias. Additional home-monitoring was conducted at the discretion of the physician at discharge.

Indications for Pacemaker

If ECG changes developed after the end of the TAVR procedure and were found on daily reevaluation until the day of planned discharge, these patients were upgraded to the next relevant discharge group for extended telemetry/PPM according to the flow-charts in Figures 2 and 3. If non–conduction-related issues dictated protocol deviations, extended telemetry or PPM implantation was evaluated on an individual basis.

Study Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the success rate of predicted discharge of the algorithm and proportion of TPM use compared to standard recommendations. Secondary endpoints included reasons for delayed discharge, the number of in-hospital PPM implantations stratified by algorithm groups, and the incidence of delayed PPM implantation or sudden death within 30 days following discharge.

Statistical Analyses

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ±SD or median (interquartile range or range) according to their distribution. Categorical variables are presented as counts and proportions. Differences between groups were compared using Chi-square test with p value ​< ​0.05 considered statistically significant.

Of 349 patients (52.4%) discharged as planned or earlier, most were in the NDD group (76.5%) compared to 2ndDD (64.8%) and 3rdDD (50.6%) groups (p ​< ​0.001). Of 281 patients in 2ndDD and 3rdDD groups, earlier discharge was possible in 115 patients (40.9%). Although discharge was mostly driven by normalization of ECG changes, six patients from the 3rdDD group had preplanned discharge facilitated by early PPM implantation (one with RBBB pre-TAVR had HAVB, and five without pre-RBBB had non-HAVB indications).

Delayed Discharge

Allocation to the 2ndDD and 3rdDD groups was associated with an increased risk of delayed discharge. Specifically, delays occurred in 23.5% of the NDD group, 35.2% of the 2ndDD group, and 49.4% of the 3rdDD group (Table 2). Early discharge groups had a significantly higher proportion of non–conduction-related delays (NDD: 68.9% [62/90], 2ndDD: 43.7% [31/71], and 3rdDD: 10.3% [4/39]; p ​< ​0.001; Table 3). Delays due to suspected infection (unspecific fever and elevated inflammatory markers) were often without a definite focus, rarely required treatment, and were typically attributed to a systemic reaction to the TAVR implant. Among the 36 patients categorized as “other,” three (8.3%) experienced gastrointestinal bleeding, six (16.7%) had non–rhythm-associated dizziness, and three (8.3%) were observed for minor anemia. (Only one qualified as VARC-3 minor). In addition, three (8.3%) had postoperative hypertension, three (8.3%) experienced congestive heart failure, and three (8.3%) had vascular issues, including two cases of planned surgery for pseudoaneurysms and one VARC-3 minor access-related complication. The remaining 15 patients (41.7%) had various uncommon causes.

Pre-TAVR RBBB

Pre-existing RBBB was present in 23 patients before TAVR (3.5%); of this group, 11 (47.8%) needed PPM versus 66 of 641 (9.7%) without pre-existing RBBB (p ​< ​0.001). PPM indications were found before discharge in all but one. The patient with pre-TAVR RBBB and postdischarge PPM had a rare “equal delay in both branches” phenomenon11 with disappearance of pre-TAVR RBBB under prolongation of the PQ interval missed before discharge. However, non–algorithm-driven home monitoring still found the HAVB. For patients in group 6 of Figure 3, PPM implementation was not based solely on lack of improvement of conduction delay but was indicated by HAVB in six patients, progressive PQ in three, alternating left bundle branch block (LBBB)/RBBB in one, and sick sinus node in one.

Temporary Pacemaker

Besides the 23 RBBB patients, seven had elective TPM for PQ ​> ​250 ​ms at procedure start. Only these 30 patients (4.5%) left the catheterization lab with TPMs. Thus, 260 patients (39.2%) in postprocedure ECG groups 2 and 3 and potentially needing guideline-directed TPM were all observed without. Notably, none of the patients in these groups who developed a predischarge PPM indication without a TPM in place experienced severe bradycardia, hemodynamic instability, or asystole requiring urgent PPM implantation. Due to suspected infection delaying PPM implementation, only three additional patients had a TPM after leaving the catheterization lab. Among these, one developed RBBB and increasing first-degree AV block, one an HAVB block, and one sick sinus syndrome with pauses.

After Discharge

In 21 patients, home monitoring at the physician’s discretion, mostly for atrial fibrillation evaluation, resulted in four PM implantations postdischarge. Following the discharge of 52 PM-naïve patients in the 3rdDD group, 20 received home-monitoring, which was omitted in 32 because their ECG had returned to preprocedural levels or they had undergone prolonged telemetry. However, one of these unmonitored patients was readmitted two ​days later with a symptomatic third-degree AV block. A TPM was placed but dislodged by the patient, and during attempts to re-establish, he suffered prolonged hypotension that led to severe ischemic brain injury and resulted in death. Two of the 20 3rdDD patients with home-monitoring had conduction issues: one third-degree nocturnal AV block and one 3-second pause in atrial fibrillation. Both were asymptomatic and had <2% pacing at follow-up. Eighty-seven patients (13.1%) were readmitted within 30 days. Of 601 PM-naïve patients at discharge, 12 (2.0%) had PPM indication (Table 4) and six identified via home-monitoring. HAVB was present in seven of these 12 patients, sick sinus syndrome in two, dizziness with PQ increased to 380 ​ms in one, syncope with developed LBBB and first-degree AV block in one, and syncope with intermittent LBBB/RBBB in one. In addition, six patients were readmitted for supraventricular tachycardia, nine for infection (not valve related), six with dizziness, 11 with minor groin/vascular problems, two with access site pseudo-aneurysms, and eight with stroke/transitory ischemic attack. Thirty-three patients were readmitted for rarer individual causes that were not apparently procedure related. Among patients discharged as planned, readmission within 30 days occurred in 36 of 293 (12.2%) in the NDD, 18 of 131 in the 2ndDD (13.7%), and 1 of 40 (2.5%) in the 3rdDD (p ​= ​0.15).

Table 2 shows postdischarge PPM rates for the individual discharge groups. There was no statistical difference in proportion of postdischarge PPM in the NDD (1.6%), 2ndDD (1.7%), and 3rdDD (5.7%) groups (p ​= ​0.37). Furthermore, there was no difference for late HAVB between patients discharged as planned (0/349) or earlier (3/115 [2.6%]) and those with delayed discharge (4/200 [2.0%]), p ​= ​0.11.

In addition to the aforementioned patient with postdischarge HAVB, 4 more patients without a PPM died within 90 days after TAVR. All deaths occurred during readmission, and none were related to arrhythmia or conduction disturbances. One patient was readmitted two days after discharge for declining neurological status and suffered multiple strokes, leading to death 12 days post-TAVR. A second patient died 63 days after TAVR following complications from drainage of a pleural abscess. A third patient, with LVEF 20% before elective TAVR, died 79 days post-TAVR from heart failure. The final patient suffered a fatal hemorrhagic stroke 31 days after TAVR.

Discussion

Aiming to safely reduce the length of stay and TPM requirements, we prospectively evaluated a new algorithm for monitoring, discharge, and TPM use in PM-naïve TAVR patients. By grouping patients according to post-TAVR ECG changes and pre-TAVR RBBB immediately following the procedure, we provided a clear discharge plan intended to simplify subsequent ward management. Building on existing consensus reports,5,6 we simplified the algorithms for discharge decisions and PM indications by reducing them to two flowcharts (based on the presence of pre-existing RBBB) and limiting TPM indications.

The patients included were within the typical age range for TAVR. With a mean EuroSCORE II of 3.5, most were considered low risk. In line with previous studies, preprocedural conduction delays were observed in 27.3% of patients (Table 1).12,13 With a predictive accuracy for discharge of 76.5% in the NDD group, 64.8% in the 2ndDD group, and 50.6% in the 3rdDD group, the algorithm proved operational. An overall accuracy of 69.8% is acceptable for predicting discharge and aiding the planning of ward capacity, as unavoidable random ECG- or non–ECG-related complications preclude significantly higher accuracy.

Prolonged rhythm monitoring was the primary driver of delayed discharge. For the NDD group, the prominent “other” category reflects the limited time available to ensure the resolution of minor clinical problems or address social barriers to early discharge. Most non–conduction-related prolonged admissions remain difficult to address given our institution’s discharge-to-home policy and the age and frailty of most patients.

The categorization into three discharge groups accurately stratified the risk of predischarge PPM. Notably, 34.2% of patients in the 3rdDD group required a predischarge PPM compared to only 2.9% in the NDD group. The observed overall 30-day PPM implantation rate was 11.5% (16.1% when including 36 prophylactic/acute periprocedural TAVR pacemakers; Supplementary Tables 1 and 2), which is consistent with the current literature.14,15

In the absence of extended follow-up, it remains uncertain whether all patients in groups 3 and 6 receiving PPMs were ultimately saved from HAVB, although many likely remained nondependent.16 However, in the current clinical and logistical context, prolonged admission without PPM implantation to await potential ECG normalization is unfeasible.

Pre-TAVR RBBB confers a high risk for PPM implantation.17 Regardless of the implantation technique, the risk of PPM in patients with RBBB would have been extreme had we included the numerous patients who received prophylactic or acute pacemakers during the TAVR procedure (see Supplementary Material). However, given the PM usage at follow-up, the indications appear to have been appropriate, overall.

The precise indications for home-monitoring remain elusive, but our restrictive use seems acceptable, as it identified 6 of 12 patients requiring postdischarge PPM among 601 PM-naïve patients. Even when omitting monitoring in 32 patients from the 3rdDD group whose ECGs had improved, only 1 case of HAVB was missed. Although the optimal duration of home-monitoring is debatable, we observed no additional clinically indicated PPM needs within 30 days, reflecting the fact that most HAVB cases tend to occur within the first 1 to 2 weeks.13,17

Expert panels recommend5,6 that TPM should be considered for all patients with pre-TAVR RBBB and for those with new LBBB—with6 or without5 pre-existing conduction disturbances—who experience a QRS/PQ increase >20 ​ms, maintained at least until the following day. This was tested in the Optimize Pro study,18 which showed similar safety to standard NDD protocols. Since our data collection period, a more conservative approach similar to ours that focuses on TPM use on RBBB has been proposed.14 Our data support this strategy by limiting preprocedural TPM to patients with pre-existing RBBB or severe first-degree AV block and utilizing LV wire pacing in all other cases. Previous consensus criteria5,6 would have suggested TPM in 260/664 patients (39.2%), but we reduced this to 30/664 (4.5%). Our approach proved safe with no urgent pacing requirements during follow-up, and it supports a minimalistic “pacing-on-the-wire” technique rather than routine TPM insertion. This setup significantly reduces TPM implantation and eliminates associated discomfort, perforation risk, and mobilization issues.19, 20, 21

The low incidence of postdischarge PPM implantation, particularly in the NDD group, could reflect out-of-hospital death. However, no cases of out-of-hospital death due to untreated HAVB were observed in our cohort. These findings are consistent with those from the original paper defining the ECG risk groups22 as well as more recent data involving 30-day extended postdischarge monitoring.13

Including the HAVB indication, our overall readmission frequency of 13.1% aligns recent studies.23,24 One might expect the short evaluation period in the NDD group to lead to more readmissions compared to the 2ndDD group, but this was not observed.

The very low readmission rate in 3rdDD patients discharged as planned (2.5%) likely reflects that prolonged in-hospital observation facilitated the identification and continued hospitalization of high-risk patients, as only 50.6% of 3rdDD patients were discharged as planned. Ultimately, an acceptable balance between length of stay and readmission rates must be defined by individual institutions.

Conclusion

The applied discharge algorithm based on pre-TAVR RBBB and immediate postprocedural ECG in PPM-naïve patients, as well as a conservative approach to TPM use, enables postimplant discharge planning in most cases and significantly reduces TPM use without compromising safety during or after hospitalization. With appropriate pre-TAVR preparation, including coordination with family or other caregivers, many patients in the NDD group could conceivably be safely discharged on the procedure day. This highlights the potential for streamlined TAVR pathways that prioritize safety while improving efficiency and patient experience. Although postdischarge syncope is never benign, in light of patient preference and the clinical drive for efficiency, the discharge strategy appears acceptable when the risk of rare adverse events is balanced against the burden of prolonged admission.

Limitations

Although prospectively performed, patients were managed by the physician in charge on the ward. Although this aligns with the routine at our and likely most other institutions, a dedicated electrophysiologist check of all patients at discharge would have supplied granularity to the decisions made and may have given different handling in some patients. In addition, the relatively low proportion of SEVs may not allow extrapolation of the result to sites with predominant SEV use. Finally, a policy of prophylactic PM implantation for RBBB with long PQ or hemiblock adds a layer of safety and, although rarely used in the study, it still affects translation to centers that do not adopt this approach.
