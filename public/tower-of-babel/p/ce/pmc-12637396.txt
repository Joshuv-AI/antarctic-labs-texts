Sites of metastases before systemic treatment influence progression patterns and survival in stage IV melanoma patients
Authors: I.A. Vergara, S.N. Lo, I. Li, J.W. Conway, X. Fu, A.M. Menzies, M.S. Carlino, R.A. Scolyer, G.V. Long, I.P. da Silva
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetastatic sites influence response rates to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) and survival, suggesting anatomical locations impact treatment outcomes. This study examines how baseline metastatic sites affect progression patterns and survival in melanoma patients receiving first-line ICI or BRAF/MEK inhibitors (BRAF/MEKi).MethodsMetastatic site presence and lesion counts at baseline and progression were captured chronologically for 347 ICI-treated and 210 BRAF/MEKi-treated patients using a novel graph representation. This novel approach enabled systematic comparison of progression patterns post-therapy failure across patients by providing a standardized representation of patterns of progression in patients with distinct clinical histories. Associations of baseline metastatic sites with progression patterns and progression-free survival (PFS) were assessed.ResultsPatients with baseline brain metastasis had higher progression rates in the brain (67% for ICI, 61% for BRAF/MEKi) than those without (21% for ICI, 32% for BRAF/MEKi). Among ICI-resistant patients (n = 91), brain progression occurred in 79.4% with baseline brain metastasis (n = 34) versus 19.3% without (n = 57). For both treatment modalities, faster progression in the brain occurred when present at baseline. First-line ICI patients with brain metastasis at baseline had worse PFS than patients without brain metastasis at baseline (hazard ratio 1.9, 95% confidence interval 1.31-2.78, P < 0.01) with patients with more than three brain lesions at baseline having worst 1-year PFS (28%) compared with patients with one to three brain lesions (49%) and with patients with no brain lesions at baseline (62%).ConclusionsBaseline metastatic sites, treatment modality, and resistance type modulate progression patterns and survival in melanoma patients treated with first-line ICI or BRAF/MEKi.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI), including anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) agents, and targeted therapies [BRAF and MEK inhibitors (BRAF/MEKi)], have greatly improved survival rates in patients with advanced melanoma, with 5-year overall survival (OS) of 44% with anti-PD-1 monotherapy, 52% with combination anti-PD-1 plus anti-CTLA-4,1 and 34% with BRAF/MEKi.2 Targeted therapy with BRAF/MEKi has early activity in almost all patients with melanoma harbouring a BRAF V600 mutation, but the majority subsequently become resistant to BRAF/MEKi, and <20% of patients will have a long-term response.2 Differently, the objective response rate (ORR) is lower with ICI (45% and 58% with anti-PD-1 monotherapy and with combination of anti-PD-1 plus anti-CTLA-4, respectively), but a higher proportion (29%-35%) of patients have long-term control of the disease with ICI.1 Nevertheless, the majority of patients with advanced melanoma are either innately resistant or develop resistance to these systemic therapies and die of melanoma.

Recent studies have indicated that specific sites of metastases can influence the response and survival of patients treated with ICI. Liver metastases have been associated with reduced response and survival rates when treated with ICI in melanoma and non-small-cell lung cancer.3 Lung metastases have been associated with increased response and survival rates in melanoma.4 Recent clinical models developed by our group combining factors measured at baseline, i.e. before starting treatment, including presence/absence of brain, lung and liver metastases, but also therapy type (monotherapy/combination ICI) and line of treatment (first or subsequent) have shown good performance for predicting response, progression-free survival (PFS) and OS with ICI.5 Phase III studies have shown differential benefits across clinical subgroups in patients treated with BRAF/MEKi, including number of metastatic sites, tumour burden and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels.2,6,7 In a phase II study,8 reduced response durability was reported in brain metastases compared with extracranial disease within a cohort of patients with BRAF V600E asymptomatic brain metastasis with no previous history of local brain-directed therapy at baseline, as well as compared with prior studies in patients without melanoma brain metastases. In one observational study with pattern of progression as primary endpoint and clinical benefit as secondary endpoint,9 patients stratified by LDH levels into limited disease versus bulky disease groups showed differences in prevalence of nodal disease from baseline to progression, as well as differences in survival, according to presence of brain and liver metastasis at baseline.

Whether there are specific sites of metastases at baseline associated with different types of resistance has not been assessed yet. Also, the patterns of anatomical progression—defined as metastatic sites of progression that emerge in patients who progress on ICI (innate and acquired resistance) and BRAF/MEKi—remain underexplored. In this study, we have investigated the impact that baseline sites of metastases have on the anatomical patterns of progression and their association with survival in stage IV melanoma patients treated with anti-PD-1-based ICI and BRAF/MEKi as first-line treatment. Specifically, we sought to investigate the association between sites of metastases at baseline and (i) sites of progression in patients who progressed on first-line ICI and first-line BRAF/MEKi, (ii) sites of progression in patients who progressed on first-line ICI with innate and acquired resistance, and (iii) PFS, from time of first-line treatment to time of first progression event or time to last follow-up.

Methods

Cohort selection

A cohort of 6226 patients with stage IV metastatic disease (American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Staging System, Eighth Edition) was extracted from the Melanoma Institute Australia (MIA) Research Database (MRD). Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (i) uveal melanoma, unclear treatment history or primary diagnosis date not available, (ii) no history of systemic therapy, (iii) first-line systemic therapy other than anti-PD-1 alone or anti-PD-1 combined with anti-CTLA4 and BRAF/MEKi, (iv) first-line systemic therapy anti-PD-1 alone or anti-PD-1 combined with anti-CTLA4 or BRAF/MEKi before first distant metastasis event, or (v) first-line systemic therapy started after 90 days of prior metastasis. This resulted in a cohort of 557 patients with stage IV melanoma treated between 2009 and 2021. Of these, 347 patients had anti-PD-1 alone (n = 189) or in combination with anti-CTLA4 (n = 158) as first-line therapy, whereas 210 had BRAF/MEKi therapy first line. This project was approved by the local institutional ethics review board.

Procedures

Patient demographics (age, sex) and disease characteristics [histological subtype, mutational status, sites of metastases, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS), and systemic therapy/therapies] were collected and analysed. Sites of distant disease were grouped as bone, liver, lung, brain, subcutaneous and soft tissue (SST), distant lymph nodes (LN), adrenal gland, gastrointestinal (GI) sites, genitourinary (GU) sites and spleen. Other sites included were thyroid, heart, ovary, gallbladder, orbit, cornea and mucosa.

Disease assessment

At baseline, all patients starting systemic treatment were assessed with staging scans, including imaging of the brain, most commonly magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or computed tomography (CT) scan if MRI was not possible. Tumour response to anti-PD-1 ± anti-CTLA-4 and to BRAF/MEKi therapy was assessed with regular scans as per standard of care and according to each institution’s protocols [in general 3-monthly (approximately every 90 days) CT or CT/positron emission tomography (PET) imaging] along with dedicated brain imaging (MRI or CT), and was determined based on RECIST v1.1.

For anti-PD-1 ± anti-CTLA-4 therapy, innate resistance was defined per RECIST 1.1 criteria as those with progressive disease (PD) as best response; acquired resistance was defined as those patients with partial response (PR) or complete response (CR) before PD. For assessment of resistance, patients with stable disease were excluded.

Modelling of clinical progression

For each patient, the date and site of all distant metastatic lesions and dates of administration of systemic therapies were retrieved from MRD, curated and sorted in chronological order (Figure 1A). Utilising the date of the earliest metastatic lesion as the beginning of the metastatic progression history, all subsequent metastatic lesions occurring within a 90-day time span were considered concurrent metastatic events (Figure 1B). Each metastatic site was represented as a separate node, with the node size proportional to the number of lesions detected in that site within the specified 90-day threshold. A new metastatic event was represented as a separate set of nodes upon the occurrence of metastatic lesions beyond 90 days of the first metastatic lesion in the previous metastatic event. A new treatment event was represented as a separate node upon administration of systemic therapy (Figure 1B). Association of metastatic events with progression and survival

For the assessment of spatial and temporal progression patterns in patients who progressed on first-line treatment, patients with more than one metastatic event before first-line therapy were still included to maximise the number of cases with progression. For associations of metastatic sites present at baseline with PFS, only patients who had first-line systemic treatment immediately after the first metastatic event with available follow-up (time from first-line systemic therapy to last follow-up >0) were included. Patients with a different second-line treatment modality before progression or at last follow-up without further progression were excluded.

Statistical analysis

Comparisons of categorical versus categorical variables were carried out using chi-square test. Comparisons of categorical versus continuous variables were done using Wilcoxon signed rank test and Kruskal–Wallis Test (if three or more categories). PFS outcome was summarised using Kaplan–Meier survival curves stratified by the number of brain metastases (0, 1-3, and >3). Associations between metastatic sites and number of lesions present at baseline with PFS were assessed using Cox proportional hazards regression models. Correction for multiple testing was pursued using Benjamini and Hochberg method. A P value >0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were done in R statistical software version 3.6.3.

Ethics approval

This study involved human participants and was approved by Sydney Local Health District Human Ethics Review Committee (Protocol No. X15-0454 & HREC/11/RPAH/444 and protocol no X17-0312 & HREC/11/RPAH/32). Participants gave informed consent to participate in the study before taking part.

The proportion of patients with brain, lung and SST metastases was different across treatment groups (Table 1). Sites of metastases at baseline influence the anatomical pattern of progression in patients who progressed on first-line systemic therapy

To accurately capture the anatomical progression history for each of the 557 patients and effectively compare the progression patterns across patients, a graph representation was constructed (Figure 1, see Methods). This novel representation effectively captures all metastatic and treatment events for each individual patient throughout progression, effectively enabling comparisons across patients with different progression patterns in their clinical history. For all subsequent analyses, patients receiving first-line anti-PD-1 therapy, either as monotherapy or in combination with anti-CTLA4, were pooled into a single group to ensure sufficient sample size and statistical power.

To explore the impact of metastatic sites of disease at baseline—i.e. before starting treatment —on the anatomical patterns of progression of patients who progressed on first-line anti-PD-1 (alone or in combination with anti-CTLA4, n = 144), as well as BRAF/MEKi (n = 126), treatments, we assessed the prevalence of metastatic sites present immediately after administration of first-line treatment based on the graph representation.

The brain was the most prevalent first site of progression in both treatment modalities, occurring in 40.5% of BRAF/MEKi progressors and 35.4% of anti-PD-1 ± anti-CTLA-4 progressors (Figure 2A and B, left). Within anti-PD-1 ± anti-CTLA-4 progressors, no significant differences in the rate of progression to different sites between patients with BRAF V600-mutant tumours (n = 26) and BRAF V600 wild type tumours (n = 111) were observed (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2025.101078). Patients with brain metastasis at baseline (n = 45 for anti-PD-1 ± anti-CTLA-4 progressors versus n = 38 for BRAF/MEKi progressors) had a significantly higher rate of progression in the brain compared with patients with no brain metastasis at baseline (n = 99 for anti-PD-1 ± anti-CTLA-4 progressors versus n = 88 for BRAF/MEKi progressors) (Supplementary Figure S2 and Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2025.101078). Of patients who progressed on anti-PD-1 with brain metastasis at baseline, 67% progressed in the brain, compared with 21% of patients without brain metastasis at baseline (adjusted P < 0.001). Similarly, 61% of patients who progressed on BRAF/MEKi with brain metastasis at baseline progressed in the brain, compared with 32% of patients without brain metastasis at baseline (adjusted P < 0.01). For both treatment modalities, presence of lung metastasis at baseline had the highest progression rate to the brain among extracranial sites of disease (41.9% in anti-PD-1 progressors and 48.5% in BRAF/MEKi progressors) whereas presence of bone metastasis at baseline had the lowest progression rate to the brain (27.9% in anti-PD-1 progressors and 33.3% in BRAF/MEKi progressors) (Figure 2A and B, right). Evidence for the presence of distinct extracranial sites of disease at baseline and for treatment modality to shape intraorgan and interorgan anatomical progression was identified (Supplementary Results S2, Tables S3 and S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2025.101078; Figure 2A and B, right).Figure 2Anatomical progression for patients that progressed after first-line systemic treatment. Within patients who progressed with anti-PD-1 first-line treatment, we further explored the patterns of anatomical progression in patients with innate (n = 91) and acquired (n = 36) resistance (Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2025.101078). Patients with innate resistance had an overall higher progression rate across sites compared with those with acquired resistance, particularly to the LN (33% innate versus 13.9% acquired) and brain (41.8% innate versus 25% acquired), albeit not reaching statistical significance (Supplementary Figure S3 and Table S5, respectively, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2025.101078). Strikingly, no patients in the acquired resistance group had progression to spleen (8.8% innate versus 0% in acquired). Adrenal gland was the only site with a higher progression rate in the acquired resistance group (7.7% innate versus 19.4% acquired).

Among patients with innate resistance to ICI who had brain metastasis at baseline (34/91), 79.4% progressed in the brain (Supplementary Figure S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2025.101078). In contrast, among patients with innate resistance without brain metastasis at baseline (57/91), only 19.3% progressed to the brain (adjusted P < 0.001, Supplementary Table S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2025.101078). This is an enhanced difference compared with the entire cohort of anti-PD-1 progressors described earlier (67% progression rate in the brain when brain is present at baseline versus 21% when absent). Significant differences in intersite progression rates within innate and acquired resistance patients were observed (Supplementary Results S3, Tables S6 and S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2025.101078).

Overall, these results suggest that the preceding sites of disease, the modality of the first-line treatment and the underlying type of resistance have a modulating effect on the anatomical progression of disease in patients immediately after first-line systemic treatment.

Progression after first-line systemic therapy is slower and more heterogeneous in patients who progressed on first-line BRAF/MEKi compared with anti-PD-1 progressors

The time of progression for patients who progress on first-line anti-PD-1 and BRAF/MEKi treatments was assessed by measuring the median time of progression to metastatic sites after administration of first-line treatment (Figure 3). Anti-PD-1 progressors had a faster overall progression rate across sites compared with BRAF/MEKi progressors (Figure 3A and B, left), likely driven by the higher proportion of patients with innate resistance in the former group. Within patients who progressed on first-line anti-PD-1, the fastest progression was to the brain (2.1 months). Patients who progressed on anti-PD-1 with brain metastasis at baseline had fastest progression in the brain (n = 30, median 1.3 months) (Supplementary Figure S5 and Table S8, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2025.101078). In contrast, anti-PD-1 progressors with no brain disease at baseline had a trend of slower progression to the brain (n = 21, median 2.6 months; adjusted P = 0.09). Similarly, BRAF/MEKi progressors with brain disease at baseline had faster progression in the brain (n = 23, median 4.5 months) compared with patients who progressed on BRAF/MEKi with no brain disease at baseline, albeit not reaching statistical significance (n = 28, median 7.8 months, adjusted P = 0.19) (Supplementary Figure S5 and Table S8, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2025.101078). For patients with innate anti-PD-1 resistance who had brain metastasis at baseline (n = 27), progression in the brain was 1.4 months compared with 2.3 months for patients with innate resistance and no brain metastasis at baseline (n = 11), with this difference being not statistically significant (adjusted P = 0.73; Supplementary Table S9, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2025.101078). Time of progression to specific sites was more homogeneous in anti-PD-1 progressors compared with those patients who progressed on BRAF/MEKi (Supplementary Results S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2025.101078; Figure 3A and B, right).

These results further support the idea that preceding site of disease and treatment modality modulate patterns of progression both spatially and temporally. This prompted us to assess if baseline metastatic burden and presence/absence of specific metastatic sites impact PFS.

Presence of brain metastases and number of brain lesions at baseline are associated with worse PFS in patients exposed to anti-PD-1

To understand whether total and site-specific metastatic burden before first-line treatment have an impact on PFS, the association between number of metastatic sites, number of lesions and presence of disease in distinct sites at baseline with PFS (defined as the time from first-line treatment to first metastatic site of progression, or to last follow-up if there was no progression event) was assessed within each treatment modality. For patients with anti-PD-1 as first-line treatment administered immediately after the first metastatic event (n = 276), a trend of association between total number of metastatic sites at baseline and PFS was observed [hazard ratio (HR) 1.09, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.99-1.21, P = 0.08) as well as a strong association between total number of lesions at baseline and PFS (HR 1.04, 95% CI 1.01-1.06, P = 0.002). Presence of brain metastasis (HR 1.9, 95% CI 1.31-2.78, adjusted P < 0.01), spleen (HR 2.23, 95% CI 1.27-3.9, adjusted P = 0.03) and genitourinary disease (HR 2.41, 95% CI 1.22-4.76, adjusted P = 0.04) at baseline were significantly associated with PFS (Supplementary Table S10, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2025.101078). A highly significant association between an increase in number of lesions in the brain and PFS was observed (HR1.13, 95% CI 1.06-1.21, adjusted P < 0.005). Specifically, the 1-year PFS rate in patients treated with first-line anti-PD-1 therapy was lowest for patients with more than three brain lesions at baseline (28%), intermediate for patients with one to three brain lesions (49%) and best for patients with no brain lesions at baseline (62%, Figure 4A). In patients with BRAF/MEKi as first-line treatment administered immediately after the first metastatic event (n = 169), the total number of metastatic sites at baseline was borderline significant in its association with PFS (HR 1.14, 95% CI 1-1.29, P = 0.045), whereas the association of total number of lesions at baseline with PFS was highly significant (HR 1.06, 95% CI 1.02-1.10, P = 0.002). Only the presence of SST disease at baseline (HR 1.74, 95% CI 1.18-2.58, adjusted P = 0.05) was borderline significantly associated with PFS, whereas an increase in the number of brain lesions showed a trend of association that did not achieve statistical significance (HR 1.18, 95% CI 1.05-1.33, adjusted P = 0.06) (Supplementary Table S10, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2025.101078). The 1-year PFS rate in patients treated with first-line BRAF/MEKi therapy was lowest for patients with three or more brain lesions at baseline (17%) and similar between patients with one to three brain lesions (41%) and patients with no brain lesions at baseline (40%, Figure 4B). Despite this trend, pairwise differences in PFS between patients with more than three brain lesions at baseline versus those with one to three brain lesions at baseline (P = 0.1) and no brain lesions at baseline (P = 0.07) did not reach statistical significance. Presence versus absence of brain metastasis at baseline did not lead to significant differences in PFS (HR 1.11 95% CI 0.74-1.67, adjusted P = 0.8) in patients treated with first-line BRAF/MEKi therapy.

These results indicate that a high total tumour burden present at baseline significantly impacts the PFS of patients treated with first-line anti-PD-1 and BRAF/MEKi. In contrast, while a high tumour burden in the brain at baseline (more than three lesions) significantly impacts the PFS of patients treated with first-line anti-PD-1, the trend of association observed in patients treated with first-line BRAF/MEKi did not reach statistical significance.

Discussion

In this large retrospective study with detailed clinical data on anatomical sites of progression in patients treated with first-line ICI or BRAF/MEKi therapy, we have utilised a graph representation to accurately map the progression pattern of each individual patient. This detailed individual representation allowed us to identify common patterns of progression within this cohort. Brain was the most common site of progression in both treatment types, with the presence of brain metastasis at baseline resulting in a faster and higher rate of progression in the brain. For both treatments, progression to the brain was more likely in the presence of lung metastases, but less likely in the presence of bone metastases at baseline. Still, differences between treatment modalities were observed for intrasite and intersite progression rates, and we have shown that progression with BRAF/MEKi is slower and more heterogeneous compared with progression with ICI. Also, the presence of metastatic sites of disease at baseline influences PFS; the presence of brain metastasis, as well as an increase in the number of brain lesions at baseline, is associated with worse PFS in patients treated with ICI, while an increased number of brain lesions at baseline, but not the presence of brain metastasis alone, is associated with worse PFS in patients treated with BRAF/MEKi.

Previous studies have identified cancer-specific metastatic patterns10 likely due to primary tumour phenotype, anatomical conditions, and similarities between the primary tumour and secondary sites.10, 11, 12, 13 How this cancer-specific network-like spreading to distant sites is impacted by systemic therapies is unknown. The brain has been described as the main site of progression with ICI in other cancer types, including renal cell carcinoma,14 and the association between brain and lung metastases has been reported in other cancer types, including colorectal cancer (CRC).15 These suggest that some anatomical sites have similar molecular mechanisms of metastatic organotropism.

There are two main patterns of resistance to immunotherapy: innate or primary resistance, when the cancer does not respond to immunotherapy; and acquired resistance, when the cancer progresses after an initial response.16 Innate and acquired resistance to immunotherapy may share the same biological mechanisms including lack of antigen expression, abnormalities in antigen processing machinery, human leukocyte antigen loss, abnormal interferon signalling, T-cell exhaustion, and increased density of immunosuppressive cells in the tumour microenvironment, among other mechanisms.17, 18, 19, 20 Previous studies have described the clinical characteristics of acquired resistance, and oligo-progression is the most common pattern.21 The percentage of acquired resistance was 19% with anti-PD-1 monotherapy and 12% with the combination of anti-PD-1 plus anti-CTLA-4,4 in two retrospective studies. However, the anatomical progression in innate versus acquired resistance to ICI was not previously known. We have shown that patients with innate resistance have a higher progression rate across sites than those with acquired resistance, with a predilection for progression in the brain when present at baseline. Furthermore, each resistance type shows distinct increases in progression rates depending on the site of disease present at baseline. Understanding the distinct patterns of progression specific to each patient, given individual anatomical sites of metastases at baseline and treatment type, will help guide the frequency and type of follow-up for each patient, towards a personalised follow-up. Also, the knowledge of the differences in progression patterns may inform us about the biology behind these two types of resistance.

We have also shown that baseline metastatic patterns are associated with PFS, and that different anatomical sites of metastases have distinct impacts on PFS depending on the treatment type. For example, the presence (versus absence) of brain metastases was associated with poorer PFS when treated with ICI but not with BRAF/MEKi, and the presence of SST metastases was associated with PFS when treated with BRAF/MEKi, but not with ICI. While a trend of association was identified between number of brain lesions at baseline (more than three) and worse PFS in patients with first-line BRAF/MEKi, this association did not reach statistical significance. A prior study has reported reduced durability of response in intracranial compared with extracranial disease within patients with BRAF V600E asymptomatic brain metastasis with no previous history of local brain-directed therapy at baseline, as well as compared with prior studies in patients without melanoma brain metastases.8 Assessments in cohorts with thorough characterisation of progression patterns like the one in this study that incorporate measures of intracranial tumour volume and segregate patients according to clinically relevant variables (asymptomatic versus symptomatic brain metastases, prior history of local brain therapy, mono- versus combination targeted therapy, LDH levels) would be needed to inform on the predictive value of the extent of brain disease in response to targeted therapy. Also, it is important to note that the results presented in this large retrospective cohort do not represent a head-to-head comparison, but an independent assessment of treatment efficacies based on the sites of disease present at baseline. Two clinical trials—ABC22 and CheckMate 20423—have shown durable responses in ∼50% of patients with melanoma brain metastases using anti-PD-1-based ICI (combination anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4), making it the treatment of choice for these patients.

Clinical models predictive of response and survival for patients with advanced melanoma treated with anti-PD-1 monotherapy or combined with anti-CTLA-45 developed by our group have shown that the presence of lung metastases was associated with better response, while the presence of brain and liver metastases was associated with poorer survival, similar to what we have seen in this study. Prognostic analysis of different metastatic patterns has been done in other cancers, with some similar results. In a large population study including patients with extensive-stage small-cell lung cancer, it was shown that liver and lung metastases were associated with poorer survival.24 Another study on patients with solitary CRC metastases showed that the prognosis is worse in patients with nervous system, bone and liver metastases, compared with those with lung metastases. Understanding the biology behind these differences might inform potential mechanisms of resistance to ICI and BRAF/MEKi and respective therapeutic targets. Additionally, prior studies have shown that baseline tumour volume is negatively associated with ICI response25 and carries predictive value when integrated with molecular markers.26 Future studies in cohorts like the ones presented here that evaluate the impact of tumour volume on patterns of progression, response and survival alone and in combination with molecular markers will shed light on the predictive role of this clinical variable. The association between anatomical sites of metastases with response and PFS suggests that, as the patient presents with new sites of disease and is administered new lines/types of treatment, the likelihood of progression and death changes. This prompts the need for clinical models that dynamically adjust in real-time to the new anatomical sites of metastases occurring throughout the patients’ journey. Our group is currently working on a dynamic and personalised time-dependent model of metastatic progression, and the models to predict the likelihood of developing brain metastases.27

This retrospective study has inherent weaknesses, including missing and possibly inaccurate data limited by the quality and completeness of medical records. Potential sources of inaccuracy include incomplete or inconsistent clinical documentation, manual data entry errors and variability in reporting. Furthermore, the presence of metastatic disease captured across patients is likely an underestimate due to limits of detection across imaging and other diagnostic technologies available at time of assessment. Biases in time of progression within brain in patients with brain metastases at baseline are expected to be present in our cohort due to increased imaging frequency in this group compared with standard of care, as brain imaging was carried out typically ∼6 weeks after treatment initiation in this group of patients (based on clinical judgement) compared with standard CT or CT/PET imaging every 3 months. Nevertheless, we have included a large cohort of patients, where the data needed for this analysis was carefully curated to increase their quality. Also, as far as we are aware, this is the largest study with this detailed clinical data at different time points, looking at patterns of progression.

In summary, this study has provided a detailed landscape of metastatic progression patterns based on the baseline anatomical sites of disease and treatment type, and their association with survival. These findings will be used to develop a dynamic and personalised time-dependent model of metastatic progression to help in personalising follow-up for individual patients. Knowledge of distinct progression patterns with ICI and BRAF/MEKi may be informative regarding potential mechanisms of resistance.
