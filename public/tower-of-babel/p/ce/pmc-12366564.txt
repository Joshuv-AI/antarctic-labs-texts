Intrinsic capacity and its change predict cardiovascular disease risk in early cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Fei Song, Meng-Jin Hu, Jinggang Xia, Chunlin Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases (CVD). Growing evidence has established that impaired intrinsic capacity (IC) served as a robust predictor of adverse health outcomes. However, its specific association with incident CVD in CKM syndrome populations has not been established.MethodsThe China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study is a nationally representative cohort study in China. First, the relationship between baseline IC and the incidence of CVD was analysed using Cox regression models. Subsequently, subgroup and interaction analyses were conducted among different groups. To investigate the potential non-linear relationship between baseline IC and CVD risk, restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression of HR was employed. Furthermore, IC trajectories over time were analysed using group-based trajectory modelling. The associations of the trajectory memberships with CVD risk were analysed using logistic regression models.ResultsAmong 5346 participants included, of whom 2712 (50.7%) were men and 2634 (49.3%) were women, with a mean age of 57.3 (9.02) years. The results of the fully adjusted Cox regression analyses indicated that there was an 8% decrease in risk for every 1-score rise in IC (HR=0.92, 95% CI: 0.87 to 0.98). The RCS regression analyses demonstrated a linear association between baseline IC and CVD risk in the CKM syndrome population. Subgroup and interaction analyses showed no interactions observed in any subgroups. Trajectory analysis identified three longitudinal patterns of IC with age: class 1, ‘low level’ (12%); class 2, ‘medium level’ (25%); class 3, ‘high level’ (63%). Logistic regression models indicated that both ‘low level’ and ‘medium level’ IC trajectories were independently associated with significantly higher CVD risk compared with the ‘high level’ reference group.ConclusionsThis prospective cohort study demonstrated significant associations between both baseline IC and its longitudinal changes with incident CVD risk among individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3.Keywords: Coronary Artery Disease, Risk Factors, Metabolic Syndrome

Introduction

Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome is a systemic disorder characterised by interconnected metabolic risk factors, chronic kidney disease (CKD) and cardiovascular disease (CVD).1,3 CKM is a chronic syndrome with a high prevalence, imposing a very large health and economic burden.4 5 Furthermore, accumulating evidence suggests that the greatest health burden of CKM syndrome is related to CVD.5 6 To capture the progressive nature of CKM, the American Heart Association (AHA) proposed a staging system (CKM stages 0–4), emphasising early risk stratification and intervention to prevent transition to advanced CVD (stage 4).1 Consistently, most existing studies evaluating CVD risk in CKM populations have focused on stages 0–3,7,10 as these represent the critical window for preventive strategies before irreversible organ damage occurs. This approach aligns with clinical practice, where interventions in earlier stages are most effective in altering disease trajectories.

Intrinsic capacity (IC) is a foundational concept proposed by the WHO in the ‘Global Report on Aging and Health’.11 It covers multiple dimensions of function with an emphasis on maintaining autonomy and quality of life throughout the ageing process. Extensive epidemiological studies have consistently demonstrated that impaired IC serves as a robust predictor of adverse health outcomes, including disability and mortality.12,14 Despite this established evidence base, the specific role of IC in modulating CKM syndrome progression remains unexplored.

What’s more, individuals may exhibit heterogeneous developmental patterns, with distinct subgroups following divergent paths of IC progression. A single assessment of IC at baseline fails to capture its dynamic longitudinal trajectory. Group-based trajectory modelling (GBTM) offers a valuable analytical approach by identifying these unique trajectories rather than relying on population-averaged estimates.15 Investigating longitudinal changes in IC may provide deeper insights into its potential associations with CVD risk in CKM population.

CKM syndrome plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of CVD. Notably, emerging evidence indicates that IC may be modified through targeted interventions.116,19 Understanding the relationship between IC and incident CVD across CKM syndrome is therefore crucial for developing early preventive strategies and reducing CVD burden in this high-risk population. In the current study, we used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative prospective cohort, to investigate how baseline IC and longitudinal IC trajectories predict CVD incidence among individuals with CKM syndrome (stages 0–3).

Methods

Data source and study population

CHARLS is a nationally representative longitudinal cohort study of individuals aged 45 years old and above in China. The design and cohort profile of the CHARLS study have been extensively documented in previous publications.20 In the CHARLS study, all fieldwork staff received systematic and professional training and conducted face-to-face interviews using standardised questionnaires. The datasets used in this study are publicly available from the official CHARLS website on reasonable request and completion of the required application procedures.

In examining the relationship between baseline IC and incident CVD events in the CKM population, wave 1 (2011) of CHARLS was regarded as the baseline and followed up in the years 2013, 2015, 2018 and 2020. In studying the relationship between dynamic changes in IC and incident CVD events in the CKM population. We used data from wave 1 (2011), wave 2 (2013) and wave 3 (2015) to evaluate the dynamic nature of IC status. Subsequent follow-up wave 5 (2020) survey was used to follow the outcome. The flowchart (figure 1) shows the inclusion and exclusion criteria of this study. Among 17 708 participants initially enrolled in wave 1 of the study, we sequentially excluded: (1) 323 individuals who completed their interviews in 2012, (2) 8802 participants with missing information on CKM stage, (3) 1040 participants who had CVD at baseline or with less than 2 years of follow-up, (4) 124 participants using cardiovascular medications, (5) 1957 participants with missing information on IC status at baseline and (6) 116 participants who had CKM stage 4 at baseline. Finally, a total of 5346 participants were included in the analysis.

Measurement of IC

The IC was assessed through six dimensions: movement, vitality, hearing, vision, cognition and psychology.21 22 Each dimension was scored as 1 for intact function and 0 for impaired function, for a total score of 0 to 6. Higher scores indicate better IC. In detail, movement: five independent sit-to-stand tests performed in 14 s or less were scored 1 point, and more than 14 s were scored 0 points. Vitality: 0 points for body mass index (BMI) ≤18.5 kg/m2 and 1 point for BMI >18.5 kg/m2. Sensory capacity was measured by self-report hearing and vision status. They received a score of 0 if they answered ‘poor/poor’ to any of the questions. Cognition: The Telephone Interview of Cognitive Status, including memory ability and mental status. Memory ability was assessed by immediate and delayed recall. Participants were asked to recall the words after approximately 2 and 4 min. When participants answered 20 words correctly, a total of 20 points was obtained. Mental status: Participants scored 5 points for correct orientation (day, month, year, day of the week and season), 5 points for accurate calculation (100–7 points for five consecutive repetitions) and 1 point for visual construction (copying pictures of two pentagram stars shown by the interviewer). The total score is 11. Cognitive function was rated 1 if neither memory nor mental status declined and 0 otherwise. Psychology was assessed using the Centre for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale. Scores <12 were considered intact and assigned a score of 1, while scores ≥12 were considered impaired and assigned a score of 0.

Definition of CKM syndrome stage

In accordance with the AHA Presidential Advisory Statement, the stages of CKM Syndrome from 0 to 3 are categorised.1 Stage 0: individuals with no CKM risk factors; Stage 1: individuals characterised by excess or dysfunctional adiposity; Stage 2: individuals with metabolic risk factors or CKD and Stage 3: individuals with subclinical CVD. Very high-risk CKD (stage G4 or G5 CKD) and a high predicted 10-year CVD risk by the Framingham risk score were employed as risk equivalents for subclinical CVD.23 Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the CKD-Epidemiology Collaboration formula equation and was classified into CKD stages according to Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes.24

Primary outcome

The primary endpoint of this study was the incidence of CVD. Information based on self-reported physician diagnosed heart disease (including angina, heart attack, congestive heart failure and other heart problems). The CHARLS study team implemented rigorous quality control measures for data recording and verification to ensure the reliability of the data.

Covariates

The covariates included age, sex, education, location, marital status, sleep time, BMI, smoking status, drinking, hypertension, diabetes, serum creatinine, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) and HbA1c. For consistency, location was divided into two categories: city/town or village. Marital status was divided into two categories: married or non-married. Education was classified into three levels: below high school, high school and college or above. Smoking status was categorised as never smokers and ever smokers. Ever smokers included former and current smokers. Drinking status was categorised as none, drink but less than once a month, drink more than once a month. Those participants who reported a history of hypertension or were receiving specific treatment for hypertension, as well as those with SBP ≥140 mm Hg or DBP ≥90 mmHg at baseline, were defined as hypertensive. Individuals who reported a history of diabetes or were undergoing treatment for this condition, as well as those with hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥6.5% at baseline,25 were considered to have diabetes.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as the mean±SD. For continuous variables that did not follow a normal distribution, the median and interquartile ranges were used for statistical description. The frequency of categorical variables was expressed as number (percentage).

The relationship between baseline IC and the incidence of CVD was analysed using univariate and multivariate Cox regression models. HR and the corresponding 95% CI were reported. Subsequently, subgroup and interaction analyses were conducted among demographic characteristics. To investigate the potential non-linear relationship between baseline IC and CVD incidence, restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression of HR was employed in the CKM syndrome stage 0–3 population.

GBTM was conducted using the ‘gbmt’ package in R software to identify distinct trajectories of IC. We fitted the models from one group trajectory to four group trajectories, with age in years serving as the timescale. To identify the model with optimal number of distinct trajectories, we compared the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) and Akaike’s information criterion (AIC) value to identify the best-fitted model. Additionally, an average posterior probability of assigning each participant to a group of approximately 70% or higher was indicative of a good fit, and models with greater than 5% membership in each trajectory group were selected. Subsequently, logistic regression analysis was used to estimate the association of IC trajectories with the CVD incidence. ORs and the corresponding 95% CIs were reported.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (V.4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were involved in the design, conduct, or dissemination of this study.

Results

Of the 5346 participants with CKM stage 1 to 3 in our analysis, of whom 2712 (50.7%) were men and 2634 (49.3%) were women, with a mean age of 57.3 (9.02) years. The prevalence of CKM was 96.20%. There were 590 (11.0%) patients with CKM1, 1484 (27.8%) patients with CKM2 and 3069 (57.4%) patients with CKM 3 at baseline. During the follow-up period, 925 (17.7%) patients developed CVD. Association of baseline IC with incident CVD

To evaluate the association between baseline IC and CVD incidence among participants across CKM syndrome stages 0–3, univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were developed (table 2). Model 1 identified that for every 1-score increase in baseline IC, there was a 10% decrease in CVD risk (HR=0.90, 95% CI: 0.85 to 0.95). After adjusting for risk factors such as age, sex, education, location, marital status, sleep time, BMI, smoking status, drinking in model 2, 1-score increase in baseline IC, there was an 8% decrease in CVD risk (HR=0.92, 95% CI: 0.87 to 0.98). When further correction of hypertension, diabetes, serum creatinine, LDL and HbA1c in model 3, the relationship remained significant with an 8% decrease in risk for every 1-score rise in IC (HR=0.92, 95% CI: 0.87 to 0.98). In model 4, we substituted individual comorbidities (hypertension and diabetes) with a composite measure of multimorbidity (≥2 chronic conditions) for further adjustment. The association remained consistent, with each 1-point increase in IC score associated with a 6% lower CVD risk (HR=0.94, 95% CI: 0.88 to 0.99). RCS Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to further examine the association between baseline IC and the risk of CVD among participants with CKM syndrome. The fully adjusted RCS regression model demonstrated (figure 2) a positive linear relationship between baseline IC and CVD risk among the overall participants in CKM syndrome stages 0–3 (p-overall=0.028, p-non-linear =0.658).

Stratified analysis

To further explore the relationship between baseline IC and the incidence of CVD, subgroup and interaction analyses were conducted across different age groups, genders, smoking statuses, drinking statuses, education, location, marital status, sleep time, hypertension, diabetes and multimorbidity. The results (figure 3) showed interaction effects among different groups, with no interactions observed in any subgroups (p for interaction >0.05).

Estimated CKM ageing trajectories

In order to better describe the dynamic change of IC score, the GBTM method was used to analyse the data of IC in the year of 2011, 2013 and 2015. We tested how many trajectories were optimal to explain the heterogeneity in the IC in this population. The BIC and AIC were lowest for the model with three trajectories (BIC=40 041.78, AIC=39 941.91) (table 3). Additionally, the average posterior probabilities were higher than 0.7 and had greater than 5% membership in each trajectory group. Although IC showed a downward trend with age, it could be clearly divided into three different classes: class 1 ‘low level’ (12%); class 2, ‘medium level’ (25%); class 3, ‘high level’ (63%). Relationship between IC trajectories and incident CVD

The association between IC trajectories and incident CVD was investigated using different logistic regression models (table 2). In the univariate logistic analysis (model 1), the ‘higher level’ was used as the reference group, IC trajectories were significantly associated with CVD incidence, with ORs (95% CI) of 1.09 (1.06 to 1.13), 1.05 (1.02 to 1.07) for the ‘medium level’ and ‘low level’, respectively. After adjustment for risk factors such as age, sex, education, location, marital status, sleep time, BMI, smoking status and drinking in model 2, the associations remained significant, with ORs of 1.08 (1.05 to 1.12) and 1.04 (1.02 to 1.07). In model 3, additional adjustments were made for potential confounding factors including hypertension, diabetes, serum LDL, serum creatinine and HbA1c. IC trajectories were independently associated with CVD incidence with ORs of 1.08 (1.04 to 1.12) and 1.04 (1.01 to 1.07). In model 4, the association remained consistent after adjustment for multimorbidity, with ORs of 1.07 (1.03 to 1.11) and 1.03 (1.01 to 1.06).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort analysis in middle-aged and older-aged adults, we investigated the associations between baseline IC and its longitudinal changes with incident CVD risks among patients with CKM syndrome. Our findings demonstrated that poorer baseline IC was significantly associated with elevated CVD risk across CKM stages 0–3. Moreover, participants exhibiting ‘medium level’ and ‘low level’ IC trajectories showed higher risks of incident CVD compared with those maintaining ‘high level’ trajectories.

IC comprised five core domains: movement, vitality, hearing, vision, cognition and psychology. Unlike conventional organ-specific health assessments, IC reflects the integrated function of multiple physiological systems,21 providing a more holistic measure of an individual’s physiological reserve. This comprehensive approach is particularly relevant in the context of CKM syndrome, where the complex interplay between multiple organ systems drives disease progression.3 26 Previous studies have widely confirmed that IC deficit scores are robust predictors of functional decline and susceptibility to disease.13 14 Of particular relevance to our findings, a recent study using UK Biobank data established that impaired baseline IC was significantly associated with higher risks of CVD in general population.27 More significantly, we specifically examined this relationship within the context of CKM syndrome, where the confluence of metabolic dysregulation, CKD and cardiovascular pathology creates a unique pathophysiological milieu. Furthermore, the RCS analysis demonstrated a linear dose-response relationship between IC and CVD risk. This linear association suggests two important implications for CKM populations: first, there appears to be no safe threshold for IC decline regarding cardiovascular risk; second, even moderate enhancements in IC may confer cardiovascular protective benefits. Additionally, our analysis included much younger age groups than most previous research.14 The associations observed in younger adults were consistent with findings in older groups. Although these findings should be interpreted cautiously, they suggest that assessment of IC may have value at earlier phases of the life course than previously proposed.

Given the frequent co-occurrence of IC impairment and multimorbidity,28 we conducted additional analyses to assess the potential confounding or mediating effects of multimorbidity on the observed outcomes. Importantly, the association between IC and CVD risk remained statistically significant even after full adjustment for multimorbidity status in the Cox proportional hazards model, suggesting that IC exerts an independent effect on CVD risk beyond comorbid disease burden. Several mechanisms may explain this independent association. IC impairment may contribute to CVD development by promoting adverse lifestyle modifications (reduced physical activity, poor nutrition),29 30 or directly through shared biological mechanisms including chronic inflammation, neuroendocrine dysregulation and autonomic dysfunction.31 32 Impaired IC may increase vulnerability to cardiovascular stressors that are not fully captured by disease counts or conventional risk factors.21 In stratified analysis, we observed a trend toward enhanced protective effects of IC in multimorbid individuals. While the non-significant interaction term precludes definitive conclusions about differential effects by multimorbidity status.

The WHO has suggested that the concept of IC should be considered a dynamic construct, evolving over time. This perspective is supported by a growing body of evidence highlighting the clinical significance of longitudinal IC trajectories.11 14 33 34 In addition to the baseline IC status, our study also investigated the associations of longitudinal changes in IC with incident CVD in the population with CKM (stages 0–3), which were not examined previously. The GBTM is particularly well-suited for capturing the dynamic trends in longitudinal data,35,37 making it particularly suitable for studying IC progression over time. In this study, we employed advanced GBTM models to analyse the ageing trajectories of IC. This approach helped identify groups of individuals who experienced similar levels and patterns of IC progression risk over time. In this longitudinal study, three IC trajectories were identified. Our findings showed that there were clear differences between the three trajectory groups. According to their distribution and trend, they were divided into ‘high level’, ‘medium level’ and ‘low level’. The ‘high-level’ trajectory group, maintaining relatively stable IC over time, had the lowest CVD risk. This supports the hypothesis that preserved IC may provide physiological resilience against cardiovascular insults. Interestingly, our medium trajectory group showed intermediate risk, suggesting that even moderate IC preservation may confer some cardiovascular protection. Our study contributed further knowledge of the association of IC trajectories with CVD risk in CKM patients. These findings underscore the importance of maintaining IC at a high level as a potential strategy for mitigating CKM progression.

Our findings may offer valuable insights for clinical practice and public health approaches. First, routine assessment of IC could enhance risk stratification in CKM syndrome, particularly for identifying individuals who might benefit from more intensive monitoring or intervention. Second, the trajectory findings imply that interventions to preserve IC might modify cardiovascular risk. These findings highlight the potential value of IC-based approaches in CKM populations, though future randomised controlled trials are needed to establish causal efficacy and optimise intervention strategies.

The strengths of our study include its longitudinal design, large sample size and comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders. The use of GBTM represents a methodological advance over previous cross-sectional analyses, allowing us to capture the dynamic nature of IC changes. However, the limitations of our study should not be overlooked. First, consistent with previous studies,7 38 39 the diagnosis of CVD was self-reported by participants in CHARLS, which may have introduced misclassification bias. Second, while our cohort represents an important Asian population, generalisability to other ethnic groups requires verification. Finally, although we adjusted for multiple confounders, residual confounding due to unmeasured or unknown factors might remain. Particularly relevant to medication effects, we excluded participants taking cardiac medications from primary analyses to minimise confounding, as baseline use could reflect undiagnosed CVD. While this conservative approach strengthens internal validity, it precluded direct medication effect analyses.

Conclusions

This prospective cohort study demonstrated significant associations between both baseline IC and its longitudinal changes with incident CVD risk among individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3. Our findings support the clinical integration of IC assessment into standard CKM management protocols for both middle-aged and older populations, as it may enhance early identification of high-risk individuals and inform targeted preventive strategies.
