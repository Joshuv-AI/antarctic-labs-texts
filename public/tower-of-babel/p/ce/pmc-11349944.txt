The effect of post-annealing on the performance of the Cu2ZnSnS4 solar cells
Authors: Mungunshagai Gansukh, Filipe Martinho, Moises Espindola, Sara Engberg, Jørgen Schou, Stela Canulescu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCu2ZnSnS4 (CZTS) solar cells commonly undergo post-annealing treatments to enhance their performance. However, multiple concurrent effects, including Cu–Zn disorder, Na diffusion from the back contact, CZTS grain boundary passivation, and elemental interdiffusion at the CdS/CZTS interface, can occur simultaneously during post-annealing, making it challenging to isolate their specific influence on device performance. To address this complexity, we have utilized Cu–Zn-disordered CZTS absorbers to eliminate the influence of Cu–Zn disorder in this study. Post-annealing experiments conducted at 120 to 300 °C in nitrogen and air atmospheres aim to decouple and assess the impact of different annealing conditions on the performance of CZTS solar cells. Our findings suggest that the optimal post-annealing might be a combined effect of two distinct processes: CZTS absorber surface passivation occurring at 325 °C and CdS crystallization at 225 °C. Hence, improvement in device performance could result from multiple concurrent mechanisms occurring at different temperatures.Subject terms: Solar physics, Solar cells

Introduction

Cu2ZnSnS4 (CZTS) is a promising earth-abundant, non-toxic p-type solar cell absorber material known for its high absorption coefficient, enabling efficient light absorption with absorber layers less than 1 µm thick1. With a bandgap of approximately 1.5 eV, CZTS is suitable for single-junction solar cells. Significant improvements over the past decades have been made, and the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of Cu2ZnSn(SxSe1 − x)4 solar cells has reached 14.9%2, while pure sulfide kesterite has reached 10.7%3. This performance is notably lower than that of other thin-film solar cells such as CdTe and Cu (In, Ga) Se2 (CIGS) with PCE values of 22.1%. and 23.4%, respectively4. The challenges in CZTS solar cell performance, including low open-circuit voltage (VOC) or high open-circuit voltage deficit, are attributed to various factors such as deep-level defects, cationic disorder, composition variations, sec- ondary phases, and low carrier lifetime 5,6.

Post-annealing after CdS deposition enhances device performance primarily through elemental interdiffusion of Zn and Cd, Na accumulation, and Cu depletion at the CdS/CZTS interface, leading to the formation of a CdxZn1−xS alloy that improves band alignment and device efficiency7. This performance improvement is particularly observed in devices that initially exhibited poor performance 8. Studies by Yan et al. demonstrated that post-annealing at 270 °C in an N2 atmosphere after CdS deposition yielded the best results, achieving a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 11%9. Similarly, Tajima et al. and Sousa et al. optimized their devices with post-annealing at 330 °C and 300 °C (in an N2 atmosphere), respectively10,11. A low-temperature heat treatment of the (Ag,Cu)2ZnSnSe4(ACZTSSe)/CdS heterojunction has enabled nearly 13% efficiency kesterite solar cells12. Thermal annealing has become a crucial step for enhancing the efficiency of kesterite solar cells. However, the post-annealing process is complex, as multiple effects occur simultaneously13. Despite extensive research, a systematic study exploring the influence of different post-annealing stages on device performance is still lacking.

In this work, we investigate the effect of post-annealing at three stages of CZTS solar cell production, i.e., on absorbers, after CdS deposition, and on finished cells, to evaluate their impact on device performance. Thermal annealing was performed in air and N2 inert atmosphere using fully Cu-Zn disordered absorbers at temperatures ranging from 125 °C to above 300 °C. This work aims to provide insights into optimizing CZTS solar cell fabrication processes by understanding the complex influence of post-annealing on device performance across different stages.

Materials and methods

CZTS precursors were deposited in Mo-coated soda-lime glass using co-sputtering of Cu, ZnS, and SnS targets. The films deposited were annealed in a graphite box with 50 and 5 mg of S and Sn powder at 575 °C for 45 min in a static atmosphere of 250 mbar N2 static atmosphere. The ramping rate was 20 °C per minute, and the cooling rate was 20 °C until 300 °C. The lid of the tube furnace was opened at 300 °C to cool the samples faster and induce fully Cu-Zn disordered samples. The CZTS absorber was etched in (NH4)2S solution for 5 min. Then, a 60 nm CdS layer was deposited using chemical bath deposition at 70 °C for 4 min 30 s, following the procedure described earlier14. A 50 nm i-ZnO layer and a 350 nm Al-doped ZnO layer were deposited by RF magnetron sputtering. The samples were post-annealed in air and N2 atmospheres at temperatures ranging from 125 to 300 °C, with additional treatments at 325 and 350 °C for specific sets. The post-annealing was carried out on CZTS, after CdS deposition (CdS/CZTS), and on full cells (AZO/i-ZnO/CdS/CZTS). Room temperature photoluminescence (PL) and Raman spectra were measured using a Renishaw in Via Reflex microscope with laser excitation at 532 nm and 785 nm. The laser beam was focused on the sample with a 50X objective (NA 0.75) to a spot size of 1.3 µm2 for the 785 nm laser and 0.9 µm2 for the 532 nm laser. The laser power for PL and Raman measurements were 0.05 and 0.5 mW, respectively. XPS core-level measurements were acquired at the Matline beamline in the ASTRID 2 synchrotron radiation source. Samples of air post-annealed CZTS were placed in an ultra-high vacuum chamber (<10−9 Torr). Measurements were performed with a beam size of around 1 mm2 and an electron energy analyzer. No heat treatments were applied to the samples due to the possible change in Cu–Zn ordering and surface composition. Instead, mild sputtering with an Ar ion gun at 0.5 keV energy and an area of 10 × 10 mm2 was used for cleaning the surface. External quantum efficiency (EQE) measurements were conducted using a calibrated QEXL system from 300 nm to 1000 nm under 0 V and − 1 V bias conditions. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was performed using a TM3000 tabletop electron microscope with a Bruker Quantax 70 system. J-V characteristics were measured using a Newport Sol2A ABA steady-state solar simulator under standard test conditions (AM 1.5, 1000 W/cm2, 25 °C, calibrated using a Si reference cell.

Results and discussion

In this study, we examine the effects of post-annealing at three different stages of CZTS solar cell production: on absorbers, after CdS deposition, and on finished cells. We aim to evaluate how these stages impact device performance. Thermal annealing was carried out in air and an inert atmosphere of N2, using fully Cu–Zn disordered absorbers.

Post annealing in air

Figure 1 illustrates the various configurations of solar cell devices subjected to post- annealing in the air for various series, i.e., absorber only—CZTS air, junction annealing—CdS/CZTS air and full cell annealing—AZO/CdS/CZTS air.

Device configurations. Schematics of the configurations used to study the effect of post-annealing in air and N2, namely (a) complete cell, (b) CdS/CZTS junction, and (c) CZTS absorber only. For simplicity, the i-ZnO layer in (a) was omitted.

Figure 2 illustrates the corresponding JV characteristics of the devices. The open circuit voltage (VOC) of CdS/CZTS and AZO/CdS/CZTS shows a monotonic increase with post-annealing temperature, reaching a plateau at approximately ≈ 225 °C. In particular, even at a low post-annealing temperature of 150 °C, there is a significant VOC increase of approximately 50 mV. In contrast, devices that use air post-annealed CZTS absorbers (CZTS air) exhibit a minimal change in VoC until they reach a temperature of 250 °C. Beyond this point, the VoC increases dramatically, eventually reaching a value comparable to that of the other devices around 300 °C.

The effect of the post-annealing in the air on the performance of CZTS devices. The change in the device parameters, namely open-circuit voltage (VOC) (a), short-circuit current (JSC) (b), fill factor (FF) (c), and efficiency (d) as a function of temperature for devices where the post-annealing was performed on CZTS (red curves), CdS/CZTS (green curves) and AZO/CdS/CZTS (orange-curves).

The short-circuit current (JSC) of CdS/CZTS and AZO/CdS/CZTS devices shows a slight increase with temperature, reaching peak values of 14 mA/cm2 between 250 and 275 °C. However, increasing the temperature to 300 °C significantly drops JSC. Finally, the fill factor (FF) of devices subjected to post-annealing after CdS deposition exhibits a slight decrease with increasing temperature; however, a pronounced drop occurs at a post-annealing temperature of 300 °C (Fig. 2c). In contrast, subjecting CZTS absorbers to a high- temperature post-annealing between 275 and 300 °C results in a notable increase in the FF from 55 to 60%, the highest values achieved in this work. Consequently, devices that underwent post-annealing after CdS deposition had the best power conversion efficiency in this temperature range (Fig. 2d).

Figure 3 shows the corresponding external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra. The air post-annealed AZO/CdS/CZTS devices display an increased EQE response with temperature in the 600 to 900 nm wavelength range (Fig. 3a). Additionally, the peak of the EQE curve shifts toward longer wavelengths as the post-annealing temperature rises. This is particularly noticeable for AZO/CdS/CZTS and CdS/CZTS series at post-annealing temperatures above 225 °C. In contrast, no shift is observed for the CZTS structure (Fig. 3c). This indicates that a significant change in the heterojunction occurs with thermal treatment. The observed shift in the EQE peak is probably due to the diffusion of Cu and Zn into the CdS buffer layer. Indeed, previous studies have shown that post-annealing of the Cd/SCZTS junction at 200–300 °C causes Cd and Zn atoms interdiffusion into CdS. This results in a Cu2CdxZn1−xSnS4 layer, which can lead to a change from a “cliff-like” band alignment to a “spike-like” band alignment15–17.

External quantum efficiency (EQE) curves of CZTS solar cell devices upon post-thermal treatments in air. Post-annealing of (a) complete AZO/CdS/CZTS cells, (b) CdS/CZTS junction, and (c) CZTS absorber. Insets show the section of the device subjected to post-annealing. For simplicity, the i-ZnO layer on (a) was omitted.

To confirm this hypothesis, Raman measurements were carried out on post-annealed AZO/CdS/CZTS, shown in Fig. 4. The background has been subtracted for clarity. The intensity of the CdS peak at 304.5 cm−1 increases with temperature to 225 °C, indicating the improved crystalline quality of the CdS buffer layer. Additionally, the peak shifts from 304.5 to 303 cm−1 at higher temperatures, suggesting that the CdS layer changes upon thermal treatment, possibly due to elemental interdiffusion with CZTS or crystallization of the layer. The higher intensity of the CdS peak compared to CZTS at elevated temperatures suggests an increased CdS bandgap due to enhanced crystallinity leading to a Raman resonance effect at the excitation wavelength of 532 nm (Fig. 4a). The sharpening of the CdS peak with temperature likely indicates improved crystalline quality (Fig. 4b)18. However, additional methods, such as XRD, would be required to confirm this. The Raman peak of CZTS shifts from 337.5 to 336.2 cm−1 above 250 °C, suggesting Cd diffusion from CdS to CZTS. The pure Cu2CdSnS4 crystal19 has the A mode peak at 331.3 cm−1. This indicates that the alloying of CZTS with Cd is small. Our findings align well with previous studies that have noted Cd diffusion in CZTS and CZTSe absorbers after post-annealing9,10. Given the 1.42 eV band gap of Cu2CdSnS4, alloying Cd with CZTS will lower the band gap, consistent with the observed shift of the EQE curve to longer wavelengths, as shown in Fig. 5d.

Notably, all studies were performed on fully disordered CZTS absorbers to minimize the influence of the Cu–Zn ordering. Near-resonant Raman measurements confirmed that the CZTS absorbers used in this study were fully disordered CZTS absorbers (see Supplementary Note 2, Figs. S2 and S3).

The photoluminescence (PL) spectra of air post-annealed AZO/CdS/CZTS devices at the excitation wavelength of 532 nm are shown in Fig. 5. The spectra exhibit two prominent peaks associated with CZTS (P1 at 1.35 eV) and CdS (P2 at 1.6 eV). The PL emission of CZTS resides at a lower photon energy than the bandgap of CZTS of 1.5 eV, as reported in the literature20. The P2 peak at 1.6 eV originates from the triplet state of Cu++ in CdS21. The peak P2 before post-annealing (shown as the reference spectrum in Fig. 5a) indicates its native association with CZTS. At a post-annealing temperature of 250 °C, a third peak at ≃ 2.25 eV (P3) becomes visible. The emergence of this peak suggests elemental diffusion of Cu and Zn from CZTS to CdS or the presence of VCd defects21 (Fig. 5b, c) shows the evolution of the peak positions as a function of temperature. Interestingly, the band gap of the CZTS extracted from the EQE spectra exhibits trends similar to the P2 peak (see Fig. 5d). Specifically, for CZTS post-annealed samples, the bandgap increases by 10 meV. However, after annealing in the presence of CdS, the bandgap decreases, and this can be attributed to Cd diffusion to CZTS altering the bandgap.

Post annealing in air versus N2

Post-annealing of the heterojunction involves thermal treatment of the CZTS layer, making it difficult to deduce effects solely from CdS post-annealing. Nevertheless, we hypothesize that the cumulative effects of CZTS and CZTS/CdS junction post-annealing could be beneficial. To test this hypothesis, we show the VOC of AZO/CdS/CZTS after annealing in N2 and CZTS after annealing in the air (Fig. 6). VOC increases up to 225 °C, which is consistent with an enhanced PL intensity (Fig. 5a). The drop in VOC at 275 °C is consistent with the formation of the defect state (peak P3). The optimal results were achieved with air post-annealing at 300 °C of bare CZTS due to CZTS surface passivation (see XPS data in Supplementary Note 1, Fig. S1). CZTS annealing in N2 did not enhance efficiency and degraded device performance (not shown). Based on these findings, we have post-annealed CZTS in the air at 325 °C, and then the finished cells were post-annealed in N2 at 225 °C. The corresponding J–V parameters, VOC, JSC, FF, and PCE, are plotted in Fig. 7. The increase in VOC and PCE indicates the cumulative effects of post-annealing. Therefore, the optimal annealing condition likely involves CdS crystallization and CZTS surface passivation.

J–V parameters of CZTS cells post-annealing temperature in air and N2. (a) VOC, (b) current JSC, (c) FF, and (d) PCE of CZTS cells as made (reference), CZTS air annealing at 325 °C and combined air annealing of CZTS in the air at 325 °C and annealing of full devices in N2 at 225 °C.

Conclusions

In this study, we have identified two beneficial processes during the post-annealing of kesterite CZTS solar cells. All studies were performed on fully disordered CZTS absorbers to minimize the influence of the Cu–Zn order effect on the I–V parameters. Firstly, post- annealing improves the crystalline quality of the CdS layer and shifts the absorption onset to lower energy levels. Secondly, post-annealing leads to the passivation of the CZTS surface upon exposure to oxygen, resulting in an oxygenated surface. However, annealing the bulk CZTS in an inert atmosphere does not enhance the CZTS efficiency. Finally, we have shown that the combined effect of CdS crystallization at 225 °C and CZTS surface passivation at 325 °C enhances cell performance.
