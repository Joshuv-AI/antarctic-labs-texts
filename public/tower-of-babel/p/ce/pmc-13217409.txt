Redefining Boundaries—Safety of Radiation Therapy in the Presence of Orthotopic Neobladder
Authors: Alexander Ho, Salah Dajani, Poonam Yadav, John Kalapurakal, Shilajit Kundu, Joshua Meeks, Sean Sachdev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPelvic radiation therapy in patients with neobladder reconstruction following cystectomy is rare but encountered in clinical practice. Its potential impact on neobladder function remains underexplored in the current literature.Methods and MaterialsWe conducted a retrospective study of patients with urothelial carcinoma who underwent cystectomy with neobladder reconstruction, followed by pelvic radiation therapy. Radiation dosimetric data pertaining to the neobladder were analyzed. Medical records were reviewed for evidence of urinary or neobladder dysfunction.ResultsTwenty-one patients were found to meet inclusion criteria at our institution and were subsequently analyzed. The median age at time of cystectomy and neobladder creation was 61 years. The mean equivalent dose in 2 Gy fractions (EQD2) using an α/β ratio of 10 of the prescribed total dose was 39.1 Gy. Three patients received multiple courses of pelvic radiation therapy, all other patients received a single course. Over half of the patients received hypofractionated radiation therapy; palliation was the most common indication. Among available radiation dose data, the median maximum dose (defined as D0.03 cc) in EQD2 using an α/β ratio of 10 to neobladder was 36.5 Gy (range, 5.2-98.8 Gy). The median mean dose in EQD2 using an α/β ratio of 10 to neobladder was 15.7 Gy (range, 0.5-49.6 Gy). The median maximum dose via EQD2 using an α/β ratio of 3 to neobladder (accounting for normal tissue effect) was 38.9 Gy (range, 4.6-102.9). Although 3 patients experienced acute genitourinary toxicity attributable to pelvic radiation therapy, none experienced grade 3 or worse toxicity. At median post-pelvic radiation therapy follow-up duration of 13.4 months, none of the 21 patients included on this study had neobladder dysfunction that could be attributed to radiation therapy.ConclusionsPelvic radiation therapy following neobladder reconstruction postcystectomy appears to carry minimal risk of radiation-induced neobladder toxicity. Larger studies are warranted to further validate the safety profile of pelvic radiation therapy in patients with neobladder reconstruction.

Introduction

Standard definitive surgical management of muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) involves performing a radical cystectomy, which necessitates urinary diversion after removal of the bladder. Urinary diversion techniques are broadly categorized into noncontinent and continent methods. A common instance of noncontinent diversion is the ileal conduit,1 in which the ureters are connected to a segment of small intestine that continuously drains urine through a cutaneous stoma. In contrast, continent methods include cutaneous diversions (such as the Indiana pouch),2 and noncutaneous diversions (such as the orthotopic neobladder).

Among available urinary diversion options, the orthotopic neobladder most closely replicates natural voiding function. Constructed from segments of the patient’s small bowel, the neobladder forms a reservoir that is anastomosed to the urethra, allowing volitional voiding and eliminating the need for a cutaneous stoma.3 However, orthotopic neobladders are associated with potential complications, including voiding dysfunction,4 stricture formation,5 and electrolyte/metabolic disturbances.5 Although relatively uncommon, some patients who undergo cystectomy with neobladder reconstruction may subsequently receive external beam radiation therapy (EBRT) to the pelvis. Indications for postoperative pelvic EBRT may include high-risk pathologic features warranting adjuvant treatment,6 locoregional recurrence requiring salvage or palliative therapy, or treatment of a separate pelvic malignancy, such as prostate cancer.

The question of whether pelvic EBRT delivered after neobladder creation may adversely impact neobladders has not been extensively explored in current literature, particularly in setting of hypofractionated treatment regimens. The goal of our study was to report and analyze outcomes of patients with neobladders who later underwent pelvic radiation therapy in the vicinity of the neobladder, focusing on dosimetric endpoints and potential radiation-induced toxicity to the neobladder.

Methods

A retrospective study was conducted following approval from the institutional review board to evaluate patients who underwent cystectomy with orthotopic neobladder reconstruction and subsequently received pelvic radiation therapy.

Eligible patients were adults with a documented history of MIBC, of any histologic subtype, who underwent radical cystectomy with orthotopic neobladder reconstruction. Patients must have subsequently received a course of EBRT directed to the pelvic region following neobladder creation. Documentation confirming that both the cystectomy and neobladder reconstruction were performed during the same surgical procedure was required.

For this study’s purposes, pelvic EBRT was defined as any radiation delivered to any area of the body between the axial plane of the L2 vertebra and the axial plane of the inferior aspect of the ischium of the pelvis. There was no restriction on the intent of radiation treatment (eg, adjuvant, salvage, palliative, etc). There were also no restrictions on target area of EBRT (including but not limited to: pelvic lymph nodes, soft tissue masses, osseous metastatic disease), fractionation scheme (conventional, hypofractionated, or etc), or radiation technique (eg, three-dimensional-conformal radiation therapy [3D-CRT], intensity modulated radiation therapy [IMRT], stereotactic body radiation therapy [SBRT]). Patients could also have received multiple courses of pelvic EBRT as long as all courses were delivered after cystectomy and neobladder creation.

Exclusion criteria included: patients who underwent cystectomy and neobladder creation for non-MIBC indications, patients who received pelvic irradiation via brachytherapy (eg, female patients with uterine cancer who underwent brachytherapy), and patients who underwent neobladder removal before administration of pelvic EBRT.

For each included patient, comprehensive clinical, and treatment-related data were collected. Patients were systematically screened for any acute genitourinary toxicities during treatment, and for posttreatment complications involving the neobladder and urinary function—such as hematuria, worsening incontinence or other relevant symptoms—following the administration of pelvic EBRT.

For patients for whom detailed 3-dimensional radiation dose data were available, the following neobladder-related dose data were recorded: equivalent dose in 2 Gy fractions (EQD2) assuming an α/β of10, of prescribed dose (in Gy), maximum dose (Dmax, defined as D0.03 cc) and mean dose (Dmean). EQD2 assuming an α/β ratio of 3 was also calculated for Dmax to account for normal tissue effect. If the neobladder was not contoured as an avoidance structure on the initial plan, the radiation plan was imported into MIM planning software, a neobladder contour was created, and dosimetric data were estimated. If a patient received multiple courses of pelvic EBRT, cumulative values of EQD2, Dmax to neobladder, and Dmean to neobladder were calculated. The following intervals were calculated: post-pelvic EBRT follow-up, defined as duration (in months) between the last date of pelvic EBRT treatment (if multiple pelvic EBRT courses were performed, then the last date of the first pelvic EBRT treatment was used) and clinical follow-up; duration (in months) between cystectomy and neobladder creation and date of death (if applicable).

Results

A total of 24 patients were initially included in this study. Three patients were excluded from the final analysis due to incomplete data regarding the timing of pelvic EBRT, resulting in a final cohort of 21 patients. For this cohort of 21 patients, the median age at time of cystectomy and neobladder creation was 61 years (range, 44-73). Median follow-up duration after pelvic EBRT (defined as the interval between the start date of pelvic EBRT and most recent documented encounter in the electronic medical record) for the cohort of 21 analyzed patients was 13.4 months. Of note, if a patient received multiple courses of pelvic EBRT, the follow-up duration was defined as the interval between the start date of the first course of pelvic EBRT and the most recent encounter in the electronic medical record. Four of the 21 patients were lost to follow-up post-pelvic EBRT; for the remaining 17 patients for whom complete follow-up information was available, 6 were alive (as of 2025) and 12 were deceased. For the 12 patients who were deceased, median duration between cystectomy and death was 2.1 years (range, 0.5-6.9 years).

For the cohort of 21 patients, the mean EDQ2 (alpha-beta ratio of 10) of the prescribed total dose was 39.1 Gy (range, 16.2-101.1 Gy). Ten of the 21 patients received pelvic EBRT via 3D-CRT, 4 patients received pelvic EBRT via IMRT, 1 patient received pelvic EBRT via SBRT, and the remaining 6 patients received pelvic EBRT via multiple techniques (ie, a combination of 3D-CRT, IMRT, and/or SBRT).

Three patients in the cohort received multiple courses of pelvic EBRT, whereas the remaining 18 patients underwent a single course. Of the 3 patients who received multiple courses of pelvic EBRT, 2 were treated with repeated hypofractionated palliative EBRT, whereas the third patient initially received conventionally fractionated salvage EBRT followed by additional courses of hypofractionated palliative EBRT.

Of the 18 patients treated with a single course of pelvic EBRT, 11 received therapy with palliative intent, and 7 were treated with nonpalliative intent, including salvage or adjuvant indications. Within this group of 19 patients, 5 patients received conventionally fractionated EBRT (1.8-2 Gy per fraction), 10 received hypofractionated EBRT (>2 Gy per fraction), and 3 received a combination of both fractionation schemes. The latter included cases where fractionation changed between the initial and boost plans or where a simultaneous-integrated boost technique was employed. Of note, 3 patients underwent pelvic EBRT for non-urothelial cancer indications. Two of these 3 patients received pelvic EBRT as part of prostate cancer management—1 underwent salvage EBRT for biochemically recurrent disease, and the other received adjuvant EBRT for node-positive disease; the remaining patient underwent pelvic EBRT for palliation of osseous metastatic disease secondary to lung carcinoma.

Detailed radiation dose data to the neobladder were available for 17 of the 21 patients. Among these, the median Dmax, normalized to EQD2 assuming an α/β ratio of 10, was 36.5 Gy (range, 5.2-98.8 Gy), while the median Dmean was 15.7 Gy (range, 0.5-49.6 Gy). The median Dmax to the neobladder, normalized to EQD2 assuming an α/β ratio of 3 (accounting for normal tissue effect), was 38.9 (range, 4.6-102.9 Gy).

Three of the 21 patients in the study experienced acute genitourinary toxicity that could be reasonably attributed to pelvic EBRT. Importantly, none of these toxicities were classified as grade 3 or higher. One patient reported transient slowing of urinary stream, another experienced temporary urgency, and the third noted a sensation of pressure at the onset of urination. Additionally, no acute or delayed neobladder-related complications were observed in any of the 21 patients that could be attributed to pelvic EBRT-induced toxicity.

Discussion

The intersection of pelvic EBRT and the presence of a neobladder represents a clinically uncommon but increasingly relevant scenario. Although rare, instances do arise where adjuvant pelvic EBRT is considered following neobladder reconstruction, particularly in patients with treated MIBC. Ballas et al7 previously reported a retrospective analysis of patients who underwent adjuvant pelvic radiation therapy after cystectomy and neobladder creation. In that cohort, the incidence of acute grade 3 toxicity was negligible, and no significant neobladder dysfunction was observed. Similarly, Orre et al8 included 12 patients with neobladders who received adjuvant radiation therapy in the setting of MIBC; 2 of those 12 patients experienced grade 3 genitourinary or renal toxicity. The phase 3 BART trial investigating the role of adjuvant pelvic EBRT after radical cystectomy for patients with high-risk MIBC included 5 patients with neobladders, none of whom experienced grade 2 or greater toxicity.9 Our study results are in line with these reported data, with no patients experiencing major acute genitourinary toxicity, and no patients experiencing neobladder dysfunction attributable to pelvic EBRT.

Neobladder reconstruction following prior pelvic EBRT has been previously described in the literature. A 2009 study included 25 patients who had undergone pelvic EBRT prior to neobladder creation; 19 of those patients were able to experience perfect urinary continence.10 A 2020 study evaluated 24 patients who had received pelvic EBRT targeting the bladder, prostate, or cervix prior to undergoing neobladder formation.11 Although 7 patients required postoperative interventions, the study concluded that neobladder creation after prior pelvic EBRT was feasible and generally well tolerated.

Our findings support the consideration of hypofractionated radiation therapy in the setting of a neobladder. Although data in this specific clinical context remain limited, a 2021 case report described a patient with a neobladder who underwent SBRT using an ultra-hypofractionated regimen to treat recurrent appendiceal carcinoma in the pelvis, without evidence of radiation-induced neobladder toxicity.12 Another case report from 2022 described a patient with a neobladder who also underwent SBRT using an ultra-hypofractionated regimen to treat locally-recurrent prostate cancer in close proximity to both neobladder and neobladder-uretheral anastomosis; using adaptive planning via a magnetic resonance imaging-linear accelerator, the patient did not experience either acute genitourinary toxicity or posttreatment toxicity at follow-ups at 3 months and 9 months post SBRT.13 Along this line, over half of the 21 patients in our study received hypofractionated EBRT regimens, in the setting of recurrent disease or palliative needs. As novel systemic therapies continue to improve survival outcomes for patients with recurrent or metastatic disease, the likelihood of requiring local treatment increases. The observation that these patients tolerated hypofractionated pelvic EBRT without clinically apparent neobladder toxicity is reassuring and suggests that such regimens may be safely employed in this population.

This study has several important limitations that warrant consideration. First, its retrospective design and small sample size—reflecting the rarity of the clinical scenario—substantially limit the generalizability of the findings. As a result, the conclusions drawn may not be broadly applicable across diverse patient populations or clinical settings. Additionally, there was marked heterogeneity in both the treatment modalities employed and the anatomic targets addressed. This variability, coupled with differences in the time intervals since neobladder reconstruction, introduces further complexity and may confound interpretation of outcomes.

Future studies with larger, more homogeneous cohorts and prospective designs are needed to validate our findings and enhance the applicability of the results across diverse clinical contexts.

Conclusions

In this study, pelvic EBRT administered following neobladder reconstruction after radical cystectomy was not associated with grade 3 or higher acute genitourinary toxicity and did not confer a significant risk of radiation-induced toxicity to the neobladder. Furthermore, hypofractionated EBRT regimens were well tolerated, suggesting their potential safety in this unique patient population. However, given the limited sample size and retrospective nature of the analysis, further research is warranted. Prospective studies involving larger cohorts are essential to validate these findings and to better inform clinical decision-making regarding the use of pelvic EBRT in patients with orthotopic neobladders.
