Deciphering tumor-intrinsic and immune characteristics in resectable non-small cell lung cancer treated with neoadjuvant pembrolizumab and chemotherapy
Authors: Yulong Chen, Yanjun Su, Ran Zhang, Song Wang, Jiangyan Zhang, Xiaoxuan Sun, Gang Zhao, Qiuxiang Ou, Hua Bao, Jian You
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy (NCIT) aims to improve surgical and survival outcomes of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients. However, the clinical application of NCIT still necessitates coordinated immune and molecular biomarker testing.MethodsWe conducted whole transcriptome sequencing on baseline and surgical samples from 26 patients with stage II–III resectable NSCLC who underwent neoadjuvant pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy followed by surgery. Gene expression and immune cell characteristics in the tumor microenvironment were assessed in relation to pathological complete response (pCR) and patient prognosis.ResultsBaseline pCR tumors were characterized by increased expression of genes related to antigen presentation (HLA-C, HLA-E, B2M), immune checkpoint regulation (BTN3A1, CD274), and innate immunity (HMGB1). Surgical samples from pCR patients showed elevated expression of immune-related genes (IGHD, LILRA6, and ICOSLG) but reduced CEACAM6 expression linked to cell adhesion and tumor progression. During NCIT, plasma cells and resting NK cells declined consistently across pathological groups. Non-pCR patients exhibited greater leukocyte-mediated immunity and higher CD8 + T cell infiltration (P < 0.01) post-treatment, suggestive of a sustained anti-tumor immune response. Baseline MGAT5B expression predicted pathological response (area under the curve: 0.86) and was associated with shorter disease-free survival (DFS; P = 0.03), with a consistent trend for overall survival (OS). Higher CEACAM6 expression in surgical samples was associated with both shorter DFS and OS (P = 0.03 and P = 0.04, respectively). The clinical relevance of MGAT5B and CEACAM6 expression for predicting NCIT response and prognosis was further validated in a clinical validation cohort.ConclusionsOur study identified molecular biomarkers for pathological and survival outcomes, which may inform optimal treatment decision-making, guide postoperative therapy, and improve survival outcomes for stage II–III patients with resectable NSCLC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-06965-7.Keywords: Neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy, Non-small cell lung cancer, Pathological response, Perioperative, Tumor microenvironment

Background

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related death, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) contributing to 80–85% of all reported cases [1]. Approximately one-third of NSCLC patients are diagnosed with resectable disease [2]. The standard care of these patients may involve surgery alone or induction chemotherapy or chemoradiation following surgery. However, limited tumor regression and disease downstaging have been reported, with a pathological complete response (pCR) rate of 5 to 14% and a 5 year overall survival (OS) rate of approximately 25% [3, 4]. Effective systemic treatments remain to be explored for non-metastatic NSCLC patients across perioperative contexts.

Inhibiting programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) or its ligand, PD-L1, has revolutionized the treatment landscape of NSCLC patients. There is a great interest in using immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), for resectable stage IB to IIIA NSCLC patients as a neoadjuvant treatment. This could be mainly due to the capability of the intact host immunity that increases the release of neoantigens from dying tumor cells, which then stimulates the priming and expansion of neoantigen-specific T cells before surgical resection. Notably, multiple phase 2 clinical trials have reported the promising efficacy of combining chemotherapy with immunotherapy in stage II–III resectable NSCLC patients [5–7]. The benefit was confirmed by CheckMate 816 [8], a randomized, phase 3 clinical trial, in which neoadjuvant nivolumab plus chemotherapy showed significantly improved event-free survival (EFS) (31.6 vs. 20.8 months, P = 0.005), with a notably higher rate of patients achieving pCR (24.0% vs. 2.2%, P < 0.001) for stage IB–IIIA NSCLC patients compared to the neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone group. More recently, the randomized, double-blinded phase 3 KEYNOTE-671 trial demonstrated that neoadjuvant pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy followed by surgical resection and adjuvant pembrolizumab significantly improved EFS, major pathological response (MPR), and pathological complete response as compared with neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone followed by surgery [9]. This trial justified the clinical use of pembrolizumab in combination with chemotherapy for resectable stage II–IIIB NSCLC patients regardless of baseline EGFR or ALK alterations.

While the initial results from these clinical trials are promising, response to neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy (NCIT) is not uniform, and a considerable proportion of patients with resectable NSCLC is refractory to treatment, likely leading to early tumor relapse and disease progression. Therefore, there is an urgent need to distinguish between patients whose tumors are more likely to achieve complete pathological response and those non-responders, as well as identify biomarkers that are associated with patient prognosis. Emerging prognostic and predictive factors associated with the efficacy of NCIT in NSCLC have been elucidated, encompassing tumor-intrinsic biomarkers, tumor microenvironment (TME) biomarkers, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) levels, and even microbiota and metabolic profiles [10–13]. However, owing to limited performance and controversial roles, there persists an ongoing pursuit for biomarkers of response to NCIT.

In this study, we performed whole transcriptomic sequencing on both baseline and surgical-resected tumor samples from a total of twenty-six patients histologically diagnosed with stage II–III resectable NSCLC who received neoadjuvant pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy followed by surgery. Our findings provide insights into the associations among gene expression profiles, pathological response, and survival outcomes of patients on neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy treatment.

Materials and methods

Patients and samples

This retrospective study comprised 26 patients histologically diagnosed with stage II–III NSCLC who received neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy at the participating hospital between January 2019 and June 2022. The primary inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) age ≥ 18; (ii) histologically confirmed stage IIa–IIIc NSCLC that was considered to be resectable after investigator assessment; (iii) an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status score of 0 to 1; (iv) had available primary tumor samples for whole transcriptome sequencing. Key exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) patients who have received prior systemic anti-cancer therapy including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1, anti-PD-L2 agent or an agent directed to other co-inhibitory T-cell receptors; (ii) patients had a diagnosis of immunodeficiency; (iii) patients deemed not suitable for surgery due to contraindications or tumor progression to an unresectable status after neoadjuvant therapy; (iv) patients who did not undergo a complete radical R0 resection. Clinical staging of primary tumors was done according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer guidelines (AJCC, 8th edition). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the chest and abdomen was performed for initial diagnosis, throughout all treatment phases, and during follow-up.

In the neoadjuvant phase, patients received 2–3 cycles of pembrolizumab at a dosage of 200 mg intravenous (IV) once every three weeks (q3w), with either cisplatin at 75 mg/m2 IV q3w and paclitaxel liposome at 135 mg/m2 q3w (in participants with squamous carcinoma), or cisplatin at 75 mg/m2 IV q3w and pemetrexed at 500 mg/m2 IV q3W (in participants with non-squamous carcinoma), as per the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines. Following complete surgery, the adjuvant therapy was initiated encompassing an additional 1–2 cycles of chemo-immunotherapy (a total of 4 cycles), followed by pembrolizumab monotherapy (at a dose of 200 mg q3w) continued up to one year. Baseline tumor biopsies and surgical tissue samples were collected for each patient for analysis. All study procedures were performed in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital (approval number: bc2020060).

Pathological assessment

Pathological response was independently assessed by two experienced board-certified pathologists blinded to clinical outcomes, treatment response status, and any survival information, based on the percentage of residual viable tumor in primary tumors and resected lymph nodes on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained slides [14–16]. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus discussion. In brief, tumor bed samples with a diameter of less than 6 cm were submitted in their entirety. For tumor bed samples that were 6 cm or larger in their greatest dimension, at least one slide per cm of greatest tumor diameter was evaluated for each specimen. The average percentage of viable tumor cells across all slides was reported for each patient. Pathological complete response (pCR) was defined as the complete absence of intact viable tumor cells in the resected primary tumor bed. If lymph node metastasis was present, the patient would be categorized as non-pCR.

Preparation and sequencing of RNA-seq libraries

Total RNA was isolated from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples using the miRNeasy FFPE Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quantity and quality were assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA), respectively. DV200 was used as the primary quality control metric, and only samples with DV200 ≥ 50% were included for library preparation. The median DV200 across included samples was 61.5% (interquartile range [IQR]: 57.0–66.0%). Libraries were prepared using the KAPA Stranded mRNA-Seq Kit (KAPA Biosystems, Wilmington, MA) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Pooled libraries were sequenced as paired-end reads on a NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA). Sequencing data underwent initial demultiplexing and FASTQ file quality control using Trimmomatic [17]. Next, BWA (version 0.7.17) was employed to remove reads corresponding to transfer RNA (tRNA) and/or ribosomal RNA (rRNA). Additionally, adaptor and primer sequences, as well as reads shorter than 20 bp were excluded. Qualified data were mapped to the human reference genome hg19 using STAR (version 2.7.3a) with default parameters [18], followed by gene and transcript level quantification using RSEM (version 1.3.0) [19].

Differential expression analysis

Differential expression analysis was performed for various subgroups using the R package “DESeq2” [20], which incorporates a built-in normalization algorithm. Normalized count data were generated by creating a “virtual reference sample” based on the geometric mean of counts across all samples for each gene. Each sample was then normalized to this virtual reference, yielding a single scaling factor per sample. The thresholds for identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were |log2FC|> 1, and a false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P value < 0.05 unless otherwise specified. The DEGs were subjected to pathway enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO, http://www.geneontology.org) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) pathways using the R package “ClusterProfiler”. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) [21] was performed to identify significantly enriched pathways between groups, with |normalized enrichment score |(NES)|> 1, P value < 0.05, and FDR < 0.25 as thresholds for statistical significance. The normalized expression of immune-related genes, calculated as Fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped fragments (FPKM), was compared across patient subgroups. This included genes involved in antigen processing and presentation, cell adhesion, co-stimulatory and co-inhibitory molecules, ligands, receptors, and others [22].

Immune characteristic analysis

The relative proportion of immune cell types was estimated using the CIBERSORT (cell-type identification by estimating relative subsets of RNA transcripts) method within the R package “Immunedeconv” [23]. In brief, CIBERSORT is an algorithm based on deconvolution to characterize cellular compositions of tissue samples by utilizing gene expression profiles [24]. The gene expression matrix, known as “LM22”, encompassing reference gene expression signatures, serves as the fundamental representation for each cell population. Immune activation status was quantified using the IKCscore (Immune-Keratin-Immune Checkpoint score), which integrates gene signatures related to cytotoxic immune responses, epithelial differentiation, and immune checkpoint expression. The score was calculated using the “IKCscore” R package (https://github.com/LiaoWJLab/IKCscore), as previously described [25].

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence assay

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining was performed on paraffin-embedded tumor tissues. Baseline PD-L1 expression was examined using the Monoclonal Mouse Anti-Human PD-L1 clone 22C3 (Dako, Carpinteria, CA). The tumor proportion score (TPS) was used to assess PD-L1 expression levels, which was calculated as the percentage of at least 100 viable cells exhibiting complete or partial membrane staining. Tumors with high PD-L1 expression were classified as those with a TPS ≥ 50%, whereas tumors with a TPS < 50% were deemed to have low PD-L1 expression.

MGAT5B and CEACAM6 protein expression levels were evaluated in baseline and post-treatment tissues, respectively, from an independent clinical validation cohort (N = 29). All patients in this validation cohort underwent the same treatment regimen as the study cohort. MGAT5B expression was assessed using immunofluorescence (IF) with the MGAT5B polyclonal antibody (16993-1-AP; Proteintech) at a 1:200 dilution, as well as a ready-to-use IHC staining kit (AXT38050031, Alpha X Biotech, China). Expression levels were quantified by counting MGAT5B-positive cells within each field, and patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the median count of MGAT5B-positive cells across all samples. CEACAM6 expression was evaluated by IHC on FFPE tumor sections. Tissue slides were stained using a primary antibody against CEACAM6 (ab275022, Abcam, USA) at a 1:2000 dilution, followed by detection with a Rabbit-specific HRP/DAB polymer detection kit (ab209101, Abcam, USA). CEACAM6 expression was quantified as the percentage of tumor cells exhibiting positive cytoplasmic staining among all nucleated cells within the examined microscopic fields. Patients were dichotomized into “high” and “low” expression groups using the median percentage of positive cells as the cutoff value.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.1.3). Comparisons of proportions between groups were performed using Fisher’s exact test. Comparisons between independent continuous data were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the date from surgery to disease recurrence or death by any cause. Event-free survival (EFS) was defined as the time from treatment initiation to any of the following events, including the first occurrence of local progression that precluded the planned surgery, unresectable tumor, progression or recurrence, or death. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from initial diagnosis to death from any cause. Survival analysis was performed using Kaplan–Meier curves, and the statistical difference between groups was assessed using the log-rank test. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was generated, and the corresponding area under the curve (AUC) was measured to assess the sensitivity and specificity of the relevant feature using the R package “pROC”. A two-sided P value of less than 0.05 was considered significant for all tests unless indicated otherwise (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001).

Results

Patient characteristics

This study included 26 patients with histologically confirmed stage II–III resectable NSCLC, all of whom had sufficient primary tumor tissues for RNA sequencing (Fig. 1). All patients underwent neoadjuvant pembrolizumab combined with chemotherapy, followed by R0 resection of primary tumors. The median age of patients was 64 years (IQR: 56.8–67.8 months), with 65.4% of them being above 60 years at the time of diagnosis (Table S1). The majority of patients were male (21/26; 80.8%) and presented with stage III disease (21/26; 80.8%). Histologically, there were 18 cases of squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and 6 cases of adenocarcinoma (ADC), while only two patients were diagnosed with large cell carcinoma or sarcomatoid carcinoma. Among all patients, 20 patients (76.9%) had a tumor proportion score (TPS) < 50% indicating low PD-L1 expression, whereas 6 patients (23.1%) showed TPS ≥ 50% (high PD-L1 expression). Surgical samples were subjected to pathological assessment to evaluate treatment response to NCIT. Consequently, 14 patients achieved a pathological complete response (pCR) as opposed to 12 patients who exhibited non-pCR. None of the clinical features reached statistical significance between the two pathological groups.

Baseline MGAT5B expression as a predictor of NCIT response and survival

We first analyzed baseline tumors to identify biomarkers potentially associated with complete pathological response in resectable NSCLC patients undergoing NCIT, followed by surgery (Fig. 1). Comparing pCR versus non-pCR tumors, we identified a total of 8 genes (1 upregulated and 7 downregulated) exhibiting differential expression between groups (Fig. 2A, B). Among these DEGs, the expression levels of MGAT5B, NKX2-1, and SFTA3 were significantly correlated with pCR (Fig. 2C). Further analysis with GSEA showed that pCR tumors were significantly enriched with immune-related pathways, including RIG-I-like receptor signaling pathway, B cell receptor signaling pathway, JAK/STAT signaling pathway, and NOD-like receptor signaling pathway (Fig. 2D). Meanwhile, several metabolic signaling pathways, encompassing citrate cycle, folate biosynthesis, and pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis, were also more enriched in pCR patients compared to non-responders.

Immunomodulators play a critical role in immuno-oncology by exerting influence on both immune cells and the TME, thereby unleashing antitumor immunity that is often suppressed within tumors [26, 27]. The greatest differences in gene expression between pathological groups were observed in genes participating in antigen presentation (HLA-C, HLA-E, and B2M), co-inhibitory pathways (BTN3A1 and CD274), and HMGB1 serving as a pivotal signal in the innate immune system (Fig. 2E; Fig. S1A). All these genes manifested a positive correlation with NCIT response, suggesting an activation of antitumor immunity in the TME. We also assessed the relative fraction of 22 immune cells within the TME and their association with treatment response. While no statistically significant differences were observed in major immune cell subsets, pCR tumors showed a non-significant trend toward a higher proportion of activated dendritic cells compared to non-pCR tumors (P = 0.06, FDR-adjusted P = 0.83) (Fig. 2F; Fig. S1B, C). Consistently, baseline IKCscore was slightly higher in pCR patients compared to non-pCR patients (P = 0.095) (Fig. 2G).

When assessing the individual ability of baseline DEGs for identifying patients most likely to benefit from NCIT, MGAT5B showcased the highest area under the curve (AUC) of 0.86 (95% CI 0.71–1) compared to others (Fig. 2H). At a specificity of 0.75, MGAT5B expression could reach an estimated sensitivity of 0.93 in predicting pathological response. Patients with high MGAT5B expression, dichotomized using the median expression value, exhibited significantly reduced DFS and EFS compared to those with low expression (both P = 0.03), with a consistent trend toward poorer OS (Fig. 2I). Neither clinical features nor the expression levels of the other DEGs in baseline tumors displayed a significant association with survival outcomes (Table S2). To validate these findings, we employed an independent clinical validation cohort comprising 29 NSCLC patients receiving NCIT followed by surgery (Table S3). MGAT5B protein expression was assessed in 26 patients with sufficient pre-treatment samples. Non-pCR patients showed a significantly higher proportion of MGAT5B-positive cells compared to pCR patients (P = 0.002) (Fig. 2J). Using the median positive cell count across all samples as the cutoff, MGAT5B-high expression was significantly enriched in the non-pCR group (P = 0.041). Consistent with the discovery cohort, MGAT5B-high patients tended to have shorter DFS and OS (P = 0.095 and P = 0.11, respectively), although these did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to limited sample size (Fig. 2K).

Prognostic associations of tumor immune response in post-NCIT samples

We have also analyzed samples collected at radical surgical resection for treatment response evaluation and their potential associations with survival outcomes. Three and seven genes were identified to show either up- or down-regulation in pCR compared to non-pCR surgical samples following NCIT (Fig. 3A). Notably, among genes showing elevated expression levels in pCR samples, immunoglobulin heavy constant delta (IGHD) could enable antigen binding and immunoglobulin receptor binding activities, while leukocyte immunoglobulin-like receptor A6 (LILRA6) may participate in the cytokine-mediated signaling pathway. Conversely, the expression levels of C–C motif chemokine ligand 13 (CCL13) were found markedly reduced in pCR samples. When comparing the expression of immunomodulatory genes, we observed an elevated expression of ICOSLG in pCR samples (P = 0.04) (Fig. 3B). ICOSLG is a costimulatory ligand predicted to play a role in the T cell receptor signaling pathway and the positive regulation of interleukin-4 production. At the pathway level, GSEA analysis revealed that pCR samples exhibited downregulation of pathways related to DNA damage repair, including homologous recombination, nucleotide excision repair, and the P53 signaling pathway (Fig. 3C). Regarding immune cell infiltration in surgical samples, the proportion of naïve B cells displayed a significant difference between the two pathological response groups (P = 0.03) (Fig. 3D, E). No significant difference in IKCscore was observed between the two groups following treatment (P = 0.41) (Fig. 3F). Nevertheless, we discovered a potential association between the expression of Carcinoembryonic Antigen Cell adhesion Molecule 6 (CEACAM6) and the risk of tumor relapse in these patients. Higher CEACAM6 expression in post-treatment surgical samples, classified based on the median expression level, was significantly associated with shorter DFS and EFS (P = 0.03 and P = 0.04, respectively), with a similar trend noted in the evaluation of OS data (P = 0.34) (Fig. 3G). None of the remaining DEGs between surgical samples with or without a complete pathological response exhibited a significant impact on the survival outcomes (Table S4). The predictive value of CEACAM6 expression for response to NCIT and prognosis was validated using post-treatment surgical samples from the clinical validation cohort (Fig. 3H, I). Patients were categorized into high or low CEACAM6 expression groups based on IHC staining, where expression was quantified as the percentage of tumor cells with positive cytoplasmic staining among all nucleated cells. Using the median percentage of positive cells as the cutoff, high CEACAM6 expression was predominantly observed in the non-pCR group. Furthermore, high CEACAM6 expression was significantly associated with shorter DFS and OS (P = 0.039 and P = 0.046, respectively), consistent with findings in the study cohort.

Transcriptional changes and TME infiltration patterns during NCIT treatment

We analyzed the alterations in gene expression and immune characteristics among 24 patients (12 pCR and 12 non-pCR) who had paired samples before and after NCIT. Among all upregulated genes, regulator of G protein signaling 1 (RGS1) showed the greatest difference in expression, followed by Krueppel-like Factor 6 (KLF6) (Fig. S2A). KEGG analysis unveiled that the upregulated genes in surgical samples were primarily involved in cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, the chemokine signaling pathway, and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway (Fig. S2B). The upregulation of the PI3K-Akt pathway may reflect adaptive survival mechanisms, immune activation, and compensatory signaling following NCIT. In contrast, surgical samples displayed a notable downregulation of pathways linked to cell cycle regulation, the Hippo signaling pathway, or tumor markers (Fig. S2C).

Notably, we identified over 5000 and 3800 DEGs in patients who achieved pCR and those who did not before and after NCIT (Fig. 4A–C). Among these, 979 upregulated and 1311 downregulated genes were commonly found, regardless of pathological response. GO analysis identified that genes commonly upregulated were predominantly involved in positive regulation of lymphocyte differentiation, cell activation, and cell–cell adhesion (Fig. 4D). In addition, genes related to positive regulation of cytokine production were also upregulated. Conversely, genes commonly downregulated in both groups primarily contributed to epidermal cell differentiation, cell junction assembly, and Wnt signaling pathway (Fig. 4E).

Furthermore, we analyzed the functions of DEGs that were exclusively upregulated or downregulated in each group. Compared to corresponding matched baseline tumors, surgical samples from pCR patients showed elevated expression of genes related to the muscle system process, positive regulation of MAPK cascade, and chemotaxis (Fig. 4F). Conversely, post-treatment samples from non-pCR patients showcased increased levels of genes related to leukocyte mediated immunity, B cell-mediated immunity, and immunoglobulin production (Fig. 4G). Meanwhile, pCR samples collected at surgery demonstrated lower expression levels of genes corresponding to epidermis development and positive regulation of cell cycle, while non-pCR samples were associated with decreased levels of neuron-to-neuron synapse and postsynaptic specialization genes (Fig. 4H, I). When examining the change of immune cell populations during NCIT, we found a significantly higher proportion of activated NK cells (P = 0.01), naïve B cells (P = 0.01), and resting memory CD4 + T cells (P = 0.02) in surgical samples of pCR patients, while notably a lower proportion of plasma cells (P < 0.001), resting NK cells (P < 0.001), and neutrophils (P = 0.04) compared to baseline samples (Fig. 4J; Fig. S2D, E). Interestingly, although non-pCR patients exhibited similar trends to pCR patients in plasma cell abundance, NK cell subsets (both resting and activated), and on-treatment IKCscore, a significant increase in CD8 + T cell infiltration was observed in post-treatment samples (P < 0.01).

Post-treatment immune activation in non-pCR patients and its potential association with sustained treatment efficacy

To further explore whether the immune activation was associated with disease progression in non-pCR patients, we then characterized the transcriptional changes in surgical and baseline samples from non-pCR patients with disease progression (N = 5) or without disease progression (N = 7). Patients with progression exhibited 36 upregulated and 48 downregulated genes, compared to 40 upregulated and 70 downregulated genes in non-progression patients (Fig. 5A–C). Notably, genes commonly upregulated in non-pCR patients, regardless of progression status, were primarily associated with immune activation processes, including lymphocyte differentiation and antigen receptor pathway (Fig. 5D). Conversely, genes significantly downregulated after NCIT in both groups were predominantly involved in cell cycle regulation (Fig. 5E). Interestingly, 12 genes were exclusively upregulated in non-progression patients, among which TYROBP, TNFSF13B, TNFSF14, IL6, and IKZF3 participate in lymphocyte proliferation, and IL6, IKZF3, EOMES, and CD3D were implicated in T cell differentiation (Fig. 5C). For uniquely downregulated genes in non-progression patients, nine were linked to apoptotic signaling pathway, including CX3CL1, CD44, TP63, IL1A, TNFSF10, MYC, S100A8, S100A9, and TNF. This could indicate a more stable and less aggressive TME in these patients. Cytotoxic immune cell populations displayed consistent trends between progression and non-progression groups, with post-treatment increases observed in CD8 + T cells and activated NK cell fractions (Fig. 5F). No significant changes in IKCscore were observed when non-pCR patients were stratified by disease progression (Fig. S2F).

Identification of patients at high risk of tumor relapse after NCIT following surgery

Non-pCR patients are at higher risk of tumor relapse and progression compared to pCR patients. In our study cohort, there were a total of seven cases of disease recurrence after surgery, with a tumor relapse rate of 41.7 (5/12) and 14.3 (2/14) in the two pathological response groups, respectively (Fig. S3A). To identify gene expression signatures that possibly affect the survival outcomes of non-pCR patients, we then conducted DEG and functional analysis between surgical samples of patients with or without disease progression. Six genes, including the tumor oncogene FURIN and potential metastatic marker genes BPIFA1, FGG, C1orf61, and NMNAT2, were significantly upregulated in progressed non-pCR samples (Fig. S3B). Univariate analysis identified five candidate genes in non-pCR samples, with their expression levels linked to the risk of tumor progression following complete surgery (Fig. S3C–G). Specifically, while the elevated expression levels of FGFR3, BMP7, and SIX2 were associated with extended intervals before tumor progression, the expression of BPIFA1 and IGHV1-2 indicated a poor prognosis for these patients. Nevertheless, further validation in larger cohorts is warranted.

Higher PD-L1 expression at baseline engaged in a more active immune response in the TME on treatment

In this study, baseline PD-L1 expression showed no significant association with pathological response or survival outcomes (Tables S1, S2). However, PD-L1 is a dynamic marker influenced by the TME and therapeutic interventions. Exploring the transcriptomic differences between patients with high versus low PD-L1 expression at baseline and after treatment can provide critical insights into the biological mechanisms underlying the immune response to NCIT.

At baseline, only a limited number of genes were differentially expressed between the PD-L1 high and low groups (Fig. S4A). Tumors from patients with high PD-L1 expression demonstrated a notably higher proportion of resting memory CD4 + T cells compared to those with low PD-L1 expression (P = 0.011) (Fig. S4B, C). Resting memory CD4 + T cells can differentiate into effector cells upon activation, secreting cytokines and engaging with other immune cells (e.g., B cells and cytotoxic T cells), thereby bolstering the overall immune response. These cells also play a pivotal role in sustaining immunological memory. At baseline, tumors with high PD-L1 expression exhibited significantly higher IKCscore (P = 0.023), suggesting greater immune activation in these tumors (Fig. S4D). We then assessed the impact of baseline PD-L1 expression on gene expression profiles and TME infiltration patterns in surgical samples following NCIT. Using a cutoff of |log2FC|> 1 and p.adjust < 0.05 for DEGs, only two genes, CXCL10 and CXCL11, were significantly upregulated in PD-L1 high patients (Fig. S4E). GSEA revealed that immune-related pathways, including those involved in chemokine and cytokine production, acute inflammatory response, and canonical NF-κB signal transduction, were predominantly upregulated in surgical samples from PD-L1 high patients (Fig. S4F). In contrast, the cell morphogenesis pathway was significantly downregulated. Although individual immune cell subset proportions did not differ significantly, baseline high PD-L1 expression was associated with a modestly elevated IKCscore, indicating a more immune-activated or inflammatory tumor microenvironment (Fig. S4G–I).

Discussion

The efficacy and safety of neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy in treating non-metastatic NSCLC patients have been established through multiple clinical trials [5, 6, 8, 9], leading to the FDA approval for standard of care therapy. The KEYNOTE-671 trial stands as a milestone for the clinical application of neoadjuvant pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy before surgery resection [9]. However, there is currently a lack of molecular studies to identify potential biomarkers for predicting pathological response and survival outcomes. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to utilize bulk RNA-seq for comprehensive gene expression profiling of NSCLC patients both before and after NCIT, in the clinical context of combining platinum-containing chemotherapy with pembrolizumab as neoadjuvant treatment. Our study allows for dynamic evaluation of tumor-intrinsic and immune-related changes during treatment, providing novel mechanistic insights into response heterogeneity. Key transcriptomic findings were validated at the protein level in an independent cohort, reinforcing the robustness and translational significance of our results.

Pathological response is a potential surrogate endpoint for survival. Identifying patients who are less likely to respond favorably enables more effective personalized therapeutic interventions, thereby potentially increasing the patients’ survival outcomes. The pCR rate observed in our cohort was relatively high compared to historical reports. This may be partly due to a higher proportion of squamous cell carcinoma cases, which have shown numerically higher pCR rates compared to nonsquamous histology in several randomized trials of NCIT [8, 28, 29]. For instance, SCC subgroups achieved higher pCR rates than non-SCC in the AEGEAN (21.3% vs. 13.3%), CheckMate 816 (25.3% vs. 22.8%), and CheckMate 77T (28.4% vs. 22.1%) trials. These consistent trends suggest a potential histology-dependent difference in treatment sensitivity, though such subgroup analyses remain exploratory. Additionally, the elevated pCR rate in our cohort may reflect real-world selection bias favoring patients with better performance status and fewer comorbidities, potentially enhancing responsiveness to therapy. Further studies are warranted to clarify the differential efficacy of NCIT across histological subtypes in more diverse populations. Unlike the previous study by Casarrubios et al., which focused on 395 immune-relevant genes [30], we analyzed the whole transcriptomic data to account for the intricate interplay of immune and metabolic pathways in the TME. Given the inclusion of a significantly larger number of genes in our study, the threshold for identifying DEGs was considered equally or even more stringent following correction for multiple comparisons. Here, we showed that pCR tumors tended to exhibit features suggestive of a more active immune environment at baseline compared to non-pCR tumors, including higher expression levels of immunomodulatory genes and a trend toward a greater proportion of activated dendritic cells in the TME. Among all the differentially expressed genes between baseline pCR and non-pCR tumors, the expression of MGAT5B outperformed the others for predicting pathological responses to NCIT assessed in surgical samples. Remarkably, baseline MGAT5B expression was negatively associated with survival outcomes of patients in both the study cohort and the clinical validation cohort. Interestingly, MGAT5B encodes β(1,6)-N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase-Vb (also known as GnT-IX (Vb)), which is homologous to GnT-V (encoded by MGAT5). Both enzymes catalyze the formation of β1,6-GlcNAc branching found on α(1,6)-linked mannose on N-linked glycans [31]. GnT-IX (Vb) also transfers GlcNAc to α(1,3)-mannose arms of the N-glycan and β(1,6)-mannose arms of the O-glycan, indicating the differential specificity between these two glycotransferases. Indeed, aberrant glycosylation is a hallmark of cancer and plays a critical role in cancer progression and metastasis [32–35]. Of interest, increased expression of MGAT5B was found in several malignancies, including prostate cancer, breast cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and colorectal cancer [36–39]. Lange et al. uncovered an increase in MGAT5B expression across multiple prostate cancer cell lines, with notably elevated levels observed predominantly in metastatic xenografts [38]. Similarly, Liu et al. revealed elevated transcriptional levels of MGAT5B in metastatic HCC tumors, further confirmed through validation in human HCC cell lines and orthotopic xenograft tumors [39]. In our cohort of stage II–III, resectable NSCLC patients receiving NCIT, we found that MGAT5B mRNA expression was associated with poor surgical outcomes and a higher risk of recurrence or progression. Although MGAT5B has not been functionally characterized in NSCLC, its known role in catalyzing β1,6-GlcNAc branching on N- and O-glycans implicates it in protein folding, cell–matrix interactions, and vesicle trafficking, which have been associated with immune evasion and therapy resistance [32]. Given the established roles of aberrant glycosylation in modulating antigen presentation and immune cell infiltration [33, 40–42], it is plausible that MGAT5B-mediated glycosylation contributes to an immunosuppressive TME, ultimately affecting response to chemoimmunotherapy. Furthermore, since MGAT5B is typically enriched in neural tissues and testes, its ectopic expression in NSCLC may suggest lineage plasticity and neuroendocrine differentiation, which has been associated with poor prognosis. Future mechanistic studies are needed to clarify its role in shaping antitumor immunity and treatment response.

We also delineated the transcriptional gene signature profiles of surgical samples to identify potential biomarkers related to tumor relapse and progression during perioperative pembrolizumab treatment. Here, we observed notably different expression levels of genes participating in the immune response between pCR and non-pCR patients. Genes upregulated in surgical samples obtained from pCR patients encompass IGHD, implicated in antigen binding and immunoglobulin receptor binding; LILRA6, functioning within the cytokine-mediated pathway; and ICOSLG, involved in the T cell receptor signaling pathway and positive regulation of interleukin-4 (IL-4) production. IL-4 is a pleiotropic cytokine that plays a key role in determining gene expression and preventing apoptosis of various stromal and immune cell types like epithelial cells, fibroblasts, lymphocytes, and macrophages [43]. These results were indeed consistent with Casarrubios et al. showing that upregulated pathways in post-treatment samples from responsive patients were mainly related to antigen processing and lymphocyte infiltrate [30]. Of particular interest, higher CEACAM6 expression in surgical samples was significantly associated with both shorter DFS and OS compared to low expression, and the finding was verified in the validation cohort. The CEACAM6 gene encodes a cell surface glycoprotein that belongs to the carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) family. Members of this family are overexpressed in many cancers and implicated in the adhesion of tumor cells to endothelial cells as well as tumor invasion [44]. Specifically, CEACAM6 overexpression has been associated with enhanced cell–cell adhesion, inhibition of anoikis, and promotion of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), all of which are biological processes closely linked to tumor cell survival, immune evasion, and resistance to therapy [45–48]. These observations suggest that CEACAM6 may mark therapy-persistent clones with increased tumor invasiveness. In our study, CEACAM6 was evaluated exclusively in post-treatment surgical specimens. Thus, its association with survival likely reflects a prognostic role in residual disease, rather than a predictive value for initial treatment response. Nevertheless, the involvement of CEACAM6 in immune-related pathways remains largely unexplored in NSCLC. Further mechanistic investigations are warranted to elucidate its functional contribution to immune modulation and therapeutic resistance.

Through assessment of genetic biomarker associations to survival outcomes, we identified five potential candidates whose expression in surgical samples was likely associated with tumor progression in non-responsive patients. Specifically, elevated levels of BP1FA1 and IGHV1-2 observed in surgical specimens were linked to an adverse prognosis. Conversely, increased levels of FGFR3, BMP7, and SIX2 were associated with prolonged intervals preceding tumor recurrence in non-pCR patients. Recognizing these prognostic biomarkers could inform clinical interventions, such as implementing close surveillance for individuals at high risk of disease progression. However, the independent association of these gene signatures with prognosis remains uncertain, mainly due to the compromised statistical power of the Cox proportional hazards regression model attributable to the small sample size.

There is also a need to understand the mechanisms employed by different subsets of immune cells to deploy, re-educate, and enhance their anti-tumor activities for overcoming immunosuppressive TME. In this regard, we analyzed paired samples obtained from 24 patients before and after NCIT, comprising 12 cases of pCR and 12 cases of non-pCR. The most notable difference in gene expression during treatment was observed in RGS1 and KLF6 genes; the latter encodes a member of the Kruppel-like family of transcription factors, known as a tumor suppressor [49, 50]. Both pCR and non-pCR patients exhibited increased expression of genes implicated in active immune response post-NCIT, including activation of lymphocytes and positive regulation of cell adhesion. Regarding the downregulated genes in both response groups, we noted a significant enrichment of genes associated with epidermis development. These compelling findings underscore the efficacy of combining chemotherapy and pembrolizumab as an effective treatment approach, stimulating the antitumor immune response in operable NSCLC patients. On the other hand, it is equally important to identify gene signatures and infiltration of immune cell subsets associated with pathological outcomes. Remarkably, through GO analysis, we found that surgical samples from non-pCR patients exhibited significantly higher expression levels of genes related to leukocyte-mediated immunity and immunoglobulin production. It is noteworthy that these genes were exclusively upregulated in non-pCR but not pCR patients post-surgery. In the estimation of immune cell infiltration proportions, both our data and those reported by Casarrubios et al. showed a higher proportion of memory resting CD4 + T cells in surgical samples after NCIT. Notably, we observed a significantly upregulated infiltration of CD8 + T cells from non-pCR patients. Furthermore, non-pCR patients without disease progression demonstrated slightly more pronounced immune activation responses compared to those with progression. Taken together, our preliminary results suggest that some non-pCR patients may exhibit a delayed but sustained antitumor immune response, potentially contributing to prolonged time to progression. We hypothesize that this could be attributed to the slower kinetics of ICIs, which require sufficient immune priming and reprogramming of the TME to overcome immunosuppressive elements such as regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells. Additionally, combining ICIs with chemotherapy or other therapies may also influence the speed of immune system priming. While additional time might potentially allow for further tumor regression in some non-pCR patients, definitive conclusions cannot be drawn without prospective studies rigorously evaluating the impact of extended neoadjuvant therapy on pathological response. Nevertheless, these findings highlight the importance of personalized treatment duration and timing. Future prospective investigations are warranted to determine whether modifications to neoadjuvant or adjuvant treatment strategies can improve outcomes for patients with residual disease.

Despite these promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while we applied FDR correction to address multiple testing, the small sample size necessitates cautious interpretation of findings. The limited sample size also reduces statistical power to detect subtle differences and may amplify the impact of intertumoral heterogeneity. Additionally, the use of median cut-offs for MGAT5B and CEACAM6 was data-driven and cohort-specific, which may limit generalizability and should be interpreted as exploratory. Larger cohorts will be essential to confirm the robustness and broader applicability of our findings. Second, our study relies solely on bulk RNA-sequencing data, which provides an averaged transcriptional profile across heterogeneous cell populations and lacks spatial or cellular resolution. Future studies incorporating multi-omics approaches, such as single-cell RNA-seq, spatial transcriptomics, or proteomics, would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the tumor microenvironment and the molecular mechanisms underlying response to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy. Finally, while MGAT5B and CEACAM6 were identified as potential biomarkers of response, these findings are exploratory and warrant cautious interpretation. Mechanistic insights into their biological roles in NSCLC remain limited. Further functional studies are needed to elucidate how glycosyltransferases like MGAT5B or adhesion molecules like CEACAM6 contribute to immune modulation, tumor progression, and resistance to chemo-immunotherapy, which may ultimately inform future therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we explored gene expression and immune cell populations at the transcriptional level, which may be associated with pathological and survival outcomes of stage II–III, resectable NSCLC patients who underwent the newly emerged neoadjuvant therapy with pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy. Our findings underscored the differing treatment responses attributed to alterations in gene expression and immune cell infiltration between pCR and non-pCR patients, highlighting the potential of personalized immunotherapy-based regimens in neoadjuvant or adjuvant settings.
