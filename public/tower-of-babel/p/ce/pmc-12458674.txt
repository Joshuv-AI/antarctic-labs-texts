Cardiovascular effects of incretin-based drugs in patients with and without a history of heart failure: a protocol for a systematic review, meta-analysis and trial sequential analysis of randomised controlled trials
Authors: Mohammed El-Sheikh, Christina Dam Bjerregaard Sillassen, Frederik Dencker Wisborg, Jens Dahlgaard Hove, Carsten Dirksen, Matthew Meng Yang Lee, Janus C Jakobsen, Johannes Grand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundIncretin-based drugs, including glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists (RAs) and dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP)/GLP-1 RAs, are increasingly used in the management of type 2 diabetes mellitus and obesity. While these agents have shown cardiovascular benefits, their effects on both cardiovascular outcomes and cardiac structure and function remain uncertain—particularly in patients with and without a history of heart failure (HF).Methods and analysisWe will conduct a systematic review and search major medical databases (Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online (MEDLINE), Excerpta Medica Database (EMBASE), Latin American and Caribbean Health Sciences Literature (LILACS), Science Citation Index Expanded (SCI-EXPANDED) and Conference Proceedings Citation Index-Science (CPCI-S)), as well as clinical trial registries from their inception and onwards to identify relevant randomised trials. The literature search is scheduled for July 2025. Two review authors will independently extract data and assess risk of bias. We will include randomised controlled trials assessing the effects of cagrilintide/semaglutide, liraglutide, semaglutide and tirzepatide in patients with and without a history of HF. The primary outcome will be cardiovascular mortality. Secondary outcomes will include HF hospitalisation, myocardial infarction, stroke, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide, left ventricular ejection fraction, left ventricular end-diastolic volume and left ventricular end-systolic volume. Data will be synthesised by aggregate data meta-analyses and trial sequential analysis. Risk of bias will be assessed with the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool, version 2, and the certainty of the evidence will be assessed by Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE).Ethics and disseminationAs this study is a systematic review based on secondary analysis of published data, ethical approval is not required. Findings will be published in international peer-reviewed scientific journals.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251003374.Keywords: Safety, Systematic Review, Meta-Analysis, Heart failure, Cardiovascular Disease, Drug Therapy

Introduction

GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) is an incretin hormone that enhances glucose-dependent insulin secretion, leading to significant therapeutic advancements for type 2 diabetes (T2D) and obesity, culminating in highly effective GLP-1 receptor agonists (RAs), available as both subcutaneous injectable and orally active formulations.1 Another incretin hormone, GIP (glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide), was initially considered ineffective in T2D until the advent of tirzepatide, a dual GIP/GLP-1 RA, which demonstrated superior glycaemic control and weight reduction, challenging previous assumptions about GIP’s metabolic role.2

GLP-1 RAs are an established class of glucose-lowering drugs that are increasingly used in the treatment of T2D and overweight/obesity. In patients with T2D, cardiovascular outcome trials (CVOTs) have demonstrated the efficacy of GLP-1 RAs in reducing major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), a composite of cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction and stroke, independent of glycaemic control.3,14 Liraglutide and semaglutide are both GLP-1 RAs. Tirzepatide is a combined GLP-1 RA and GIP analogue,15,17 and cagrilintide/semaglutide is a combined GLP-1 RA (semaglutide) and amylin analogue (cagrilintide).18 Other agents with ongoing phase III trials are the glucagon/GLP-1 RA survodutide, the triple glucagon/GIP/GLP-1 RA retatrutide and the GIP antagonist/GLP-1 RA maridebart cafraglutide (MariTide/AMG 133).19,21

Liraglutide, semaglutide and tirzepatide are approved by the US Food & Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) for the treatment of T2D, obesity (body mass index (BMI) of at least 30 kg/m2), or overweight (BMI of at least 27 kg/m2) with at least one weight-related comorbidity (eg, hypertension, dyslipidaemia or sleep apnoea).22,31 By April 2025, cagrilintide/semaglutide is not approved by neither FDA nor EMA, but several phase III trials are ongoing.32,37

Despite numerous large trials on incretin-based drugs, relatively few have specifically focused on heart failure (HF) and cardiovascular outcomes. While evidence on HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is growing, data on heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) remain limited, often relying on subgroup analyses.

Previous evidence

The effects of GLP-1 RAs on reducing HF events have been modest and inconsistent across T2D trials, with individual trials underpowered to assess their effect in patients with a history of HF, although they have generally shown a neutral impact or a reduction in HF hospitalisation.3,12 The prevalence of HF varied across trials from 9% to24%, and HF events were a secondary endpoint.3,12 Most of these studies do not report HF diagnostic criteria or background HF therapy, and only one trial reported left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) at baseline.10 Moreover, different GLP-1 RAs were used across the studies, with different structural homology (human GLP-1 vs exendin-4-based), durations of action, administration routes (subcutaneous vs oral) and weight-lowering effects, which may influence their efficacy.

In the recent STEP-HFpEF and STEP-HFpEF DM trials, which studied the efficacy of semaglutide among patients with obesity-related HFpEF without and with T2D, a reduction in the time to first HF events was demonstrated in the intervention arm, but this was a secondary endpoint with only a small number of events.38 39 In addition, in patients with HFpEF and obesity, tirzepatide reduced the risk of cardiovascular death or worsening HF, mainly by lowering HF events.40 Existing HF trials on GLP-1 RAs have primarily used surrogate endpoints, and no randomised controlled trials (RCTs) have been powered to assess their effects on hard outcomes such as cardiovascular mortality or hospitalisation for heart failure.41

A meta-analysis of nine RCTs including 8920 patients with HF reported a 13% reduction in MACE in the GLP-1 RA arm compared with the placebo arm.42 In contrast, GLP-1 RAs did not reduce the risk of all-cause death, HF hospitalisation or cardiovascular death.42 However, in that meta-analysis, no distinction was made between HF phenotypes (eg, with HFrEF vs HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF) vs HFpEF). Moreover, patients without a history of HF were not analysed, and incretin-based drugs such as cagrilintide/semaglutide and tirzepatide were not included.

A meta-analysis of 10 RCTs involving 68 653 patients in a broad population (with T2D and/or overweight/obesity and/or HFrEF and/or HFpEF) demonstrated that GLP-1 RAs did not reduce the risk of HF hospitalisation in the overall population compared with placebo.43 However, GLP-1 RAs significantly decreased HF hospitalisations and cardiovascular death in patients without a history of HF but did not provide these benefits in those with a history of HF. The analysis also highlighted that GLP-1 RAs effectively reduced MACE regardless of HF status. A limitation of this meta-analysis is the lack of cardiovascular function assessment and the absence of incretin-based drugs such as cagrilintide/semaglutide and tirzepatide in the analysis. This omission limits the ability to determine whether GLP-1 RAs influence both clinical outcomes and underlying cardiovascular structural and functional changes.

However, three other meta-analyses of primary cardiovascular or kidney outcome placebo-controlled RCTs in patients with T2D (with and without a history of HF) reported that GLP-1 RAs reduced the risk of HF hospitalisation.44,46

Why is this review important?

GLP-1 RAs were initially investigated for their potential to reduce the risk of MACE in patients with T2D, but concerns arose due to their chronotropic effects (eg, increased heart rate), particularly in HF.3,1047 Large CVOTs, designed primarily as safety studies in response to concerns with older anti-diabetic drugs (eg, thiazolidinediones) and increased risk of HF exacerbation and hospitalisations,48 have since provided largely neutral or positive (eg, sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors) findings for both HF prevention and/or treatment. This accumulating evidence has fuelled optimism about potential benefits in patients with HF. The key question remains whether these benefits stem primarily from weight loss or direct cardioprotective effects, challenging early concerns about their impact on HF.49

The potential for GLP-1 RAs to increase heart rate is a significant concern due to its implications for cardiovascular outcomes, including arrhythmias and other adverse events.50 51 The direct chronotropic effects may limit the use of GLP-1 RAs, especially in some patients with HF. For patients with HFrEF, increased heart rate predicts adverse cardiovascular outcomes.52

Small trials in patients with HFrEF, both with and without T2D, suggest that GLP-1 RAs may pose risks in this population due to a potential increase in HF hospitalisations and ventricular arrhythmias.41 47 However, major T2D trials have largely excluded patients with HFrEF, instead enrolling a small subset of patients with mildly symptomatic, stable HF, most of whom likely had HFpEF.10 While previous studies and meta-analyses provide valuable insights into the broader cardiovascular effects of incretin-based drugs, their findings on cardiovascular outcomes remain inconclusive, particularly when distinguishing between patients with and without a history of HF.4243 50 53,72

In recent years, data have accumulated, suggesting that GLP-1 RAs affect cardiac structure and function in patients across the HF spectrum.38

In this review, we will assess the effects of incretin-based drugs on cardiovascular outcomes, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) levels, LVEF, left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) and left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV). By integrating these outcomes, we aim to provide a comprehensive analysis of the cardiovascular impact of incretin-based drugs in patients with and without HF. To our knowledge, no systematic reviews or meta-analyses have specifically analysed RCTs comparing incretin-based drugs with control while distinctly focusing on cardiovascular outcomes alongside cardiovascular structural and functional changes.

Methods and analyses

The protocol is reported following the reporting guideline provided in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) statement73 74 and is registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420251003374).

Criteria for considering studies for this review

Types of trials

We will include RCTs (blinded and open-label), regardless of publication status (reported/published), year or language. We will exclude cluster-randomised studies, quasi-randomised studies (eg, allocation by alternation, date of birth or medical record number), non-randomised studies (such as case-control studies) and observational studies.

Types of participants

Participants will include all patients administered oral or subcutaneous incretin-based drugs, including cagrilintide/semaglutide, liraglutide, semaglutide and tirzepatide, as well as any future incretin-based therapies introduced during the study period, regardless of treatment indication (eg, T2D mellitus, overweight/obesity, HFpEF, chronic kidney disease, high cardiovascular risk, etc). We included liraglutide, semaglutide and tirzepatide, as they are the only GLP-1 RAs approved by both the FDA and EMA for use in treating both T2D mellitus and obesity. Cagrilintide/semaglutide is included in this review as it is being tested in individuals with T2D mellitus or obesity, and the drug is anticipated to be marketed as both an anti-diabetic and anti-obesity agent.

Types of interventions

Interventions will encompass all dosages of oral and subcutaneous incretin-based drugs. Placebo or ‘no intervention’ will serve as acceptable control interventions, while studies using active comparators (eg, other anti-diabetic or anti-obesity medications) as controls will be excluded.

Cointerventions

We will permit any cointervention, provided it is intended to be administered uniformly to both the intervention and control groups.

Search methods for identification of studies

Electronic searches

We will conduct comprehensive searches in several databases including the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online (Medline) (Medline Ovid), Excerpta Medica database (Embase) (Embase Ovid), Latin American and Caribbean Health Sciences Literature (VHL Regional Portal), Science Citation Index Expanded (Web of Science) to identify relevant trials, and Conference Proceedings Citation Index-Science (Web of Science). In addition, we will search indication-specific databases in cardiology and diabetes, as well as regional trial registries (eg, Asian and African registries) to minimise the risk of geographic publication bias. Searches will span from the inception of each database to the present. We will include trials regardless of language, publication status, year or type. For detailed search strategies for all electronic searches, see online supplemental material.

The literature search is scheduled for July 2025. Searches may be updated prior to final analysis/publication. The full and detailed search strategies for all databases, registers and websites, including any filters and limits applied, are provided in online supplemental file 1 and available in the online supplemental material.

Searching other resources

We will review the reference lists of relevant trials to find unidentified RCTs. To identify unpublished trials, we will search clinical trial registries such as clinicaltrials.gov, clinicaltrialregister.eu, who.int/ictrp and chictr.org.cn. Ongoing or terminated trials without reported results will be noted but not included in analyses. We will also assess retraction statements and errata for included trials to ensure data integrity and transparency. Preprint servers such as bioRxiv, medRxiv and Open Science Framework will also be searched for unpublished trials.

Data collection

Our review will adhere to the PRISMA guidelines.75 Data analyses will use Stata V.18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA)76 and trial sequential analysis tools.77 78

Selection of RCTs

Two review authors will independently screen titles and abstracts for trial selection. All relevant full-text studies will be retrieved, and the same two authors will independently review the full texts, recording reasons for excluding studies. Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion, or if required, consultation with a third author (JBG).

Data extraction and management

Two review authors will independently extract data from included trials using a predefined form. Discrepancies will be discussed with a third author (JG). The two review authors will also evaluate duplicate publications and companion papers of a trial to assess all available data concurrently (maximise data extraction, correct bias assessment). Trial data will be catalogued by the first author’s name and the year of the primary publication, with all secondary publications classified under that name. We will contact trial authors via email to clarify any missing data that may not have been reported sufficiently or omitted from the publications.

Trial characteristics

We will extract data on risk of bias components (as defined below), trial design (parallel, factorial or crossover), number of intervention groups, follow-up duration, sample size estimation, and inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Participant characteristics

Data extracted will include the total number of participants randomised, analysed, lost to follow-up, withdrawals and crossovers; age (mean or median); sex; race/ethnicity; primary diagnosis (eg, HF, T2D mellitus, overweight/obesity, hypertension, chronic kidney disease); haemoglobin A1c; BMI; systolic blood pressure; serum creatinine or estimated glomerular filtration rate; nutritional status using the prognostic nutritional index79; type of heart failure; LVEF; and NT-proBNP levels, where reported. Additional relevant baseline characteristics may be extracted as appropriate to enhance comparability across studies.

Experimental intervention characteristics

We will extract the following data: type of incretin-based drug administered, administration method (oral or subcutaneous), dosage and length of treatment period.

Control intervention characteristics

We will extract the following data: type of control (placebo, matching placebo, ‘active’ placebo or no intervention), dosage and length of treatment period.

Outcomes

We will extract all outcomes listed above from each RCT. For each outcome, we will note any deviations from intended interventions, missing outcomes, inappropriate measurement, or selective reporting as outlined in the ‘missing outcome data’ bias domain, the ‘risk of bias in measurement of the outcome’ bias domain, and the ‘risk of bias in the selection of the reported result’ bias domain.

Notes

We will investigate information regarding industry funding for personal or academic activities of each trial author. Publications will be considered at high risk of for-profit bias if the trial is industry-sponsored or if any author has an industry affiliation.80 We will note in the ‘Characteristics of included studies’ table if outcome data were not reported in a usable way.

We will evaluate each outcome result across the following domains: ‘deviations from intended intervention’, ‘missing outcome data’, ‘risk of bias in measurement of the outcome’, and ‘risk of bias in selection of the reported result’. This approach enables us to assess the bias risk for each outcome assessed in addition to each trial. The overall risk of bias for any given result or trial will be considered low if all domains are rated as low risk. Conversely, if any domain is rated as having some concerns or high risk, then the overall risk of bias will be judged as high. Our primary conclusions will draw from the results of our primary outcome that are determined to have a low overall risk of bias. Both primary and secondary conclusions will be detailed in the summary of findings tables.

Measurement of treatment effect

Continuous outcomes

We will calculate mean differences (MDs) and, if appropriate, standardised mean differences (SMDs) with 95% CIs for continuous outcomes. Data will be entered with a consistent direction of effect. If change-from-baseline values are available, we will analyse them. If multiple time-point measurements are reported, we will use the last available measurement from baseline and, where necessary, calculate the change from baseline. Our primary interest is in change-from-baseline values; we will derive these when not explicitly reported. When SDs are missing, we will calculate them from trial data or request them from authors. We will assess all outcomes at maximum follow-up.

Dichotomous outcomes

For dichotomous outcomes, we will calculate risk ratios with 95% CIs, as well as the trial sequential analysis-adjusted CIs (see below).

Dealing with missing data

We will preferentially use intention-to-treat data if provided by the trialists.82 As a primary method of handling missing data, we will attempt to contact all trial authors to acquire any relevant missing data, both for data extraction and for assessing the risk of bias as outlined previously.

Continuous outcomes

In our primary analysis, we will not impute missing values for any outcomes. However, in our sensitivity analyses (see paragraph below), we will impute data as part of our strategy to address potential data gaps.

Dichotomous outcomes

In our primary analysis, we will not impute missing values for any outcomes. However, in our sensitivity analyses (see paragraph below), we will impute data as part of our strategy to address potential data gaps.

Assessment of heterogeneity

We will primarily inspect forest plots for signs of heterogeneity. Second, we will assess statistical heterogeneity using the I² statistic, which quantifies the proportion of total variation across trials beyond random variation (0% indicating no heterogeneity, with higher values indicating increasing heterogeneity),81 83 84 and estimate heterogeneity variance using the restricted maximum likelihood method.85 86 We will only conclude that heterogeneity is high if the between-trial variance translates into clinically meaningful differences for patients based on effect sizes defined in the trial sequential analyses (see below). Third, we will investigate heterogeneity through subgroup analyses (see the Subgroup analyses and integration of heterogeneity section). Finally, if heterogeneity is substantial (defined as I² ≥50%), we will use a random-effects model for meta-analysis. If heterogeneity remains high despite this, we will explore sources through sensitivity analyses (eg, leave-one-out analysis) and may forgo meta-analysis if results are deemed too inconsistent to pool meaningfully.81

Assessment of reporting biases

We will assess reporting bias using funnel plots when a meta-analysis includes 10 or more trials. Funnel plots will be visually inspected to identify small trial effects, which may indicate potential publication bias. Recognising that funnel plots primarily assess bias related to small sample sizes, we will use this information to estimate the risk of publication bias. For both dichotomous and continuous outcomes, we will use the default Stata function to generate funnel plots for assessing publication bias.76

Unit of analysis issues

We will include only RCTs. For crossover trials, we will use data exclusively from the first period81 87 to avoid unit of analysis issues.

All identified pairwise comparisons will be assessed through meta-analyses, trial sequential analyses and subgroup and sensitivity analyses.

Meta-analysis

We will conduct meta-analyses following the guidelines outlined in the Cochrane Handbook of Systematic Reviews of Interventions,81 Keus et al88 and the eight-step assessment framework by Jakobsen et al.89 Data analysis will be performed using Stata V.18.76 For each treatment comparison, we will apply both random-effects90 and fixed-effect meta-analyses.91 We will primarily report the more conservative point estimate of the two (highest p value), while considering the less conservative result as a sensitivity analysis.89

Since we are assessing a single primary outcome, a p value of 0.05 or lower will be considered the threshold for statistical significance.89 Potential heterogeneity will be explored through subgroup analyses. The eight-step procedure will be used to determine whether significance thresholds are crossed and to evaluate the clinical relevance of the findings.89 For trials with multiple intervention arms, only the relevant groups will be included. If two comparisons are combined within the same meta-analysis, we will split the control group to prevent double counting.

Trial sequential analysis

We aim to control the risks of type I and type II errors. Therefore, we will perform trial sequential analysis for all pairwise comparisons to calculate the required information size—the number of participants needed in a meta-analysis to detect or reject a given intervention effect reliably—and to assess whether the cumulative Z-curve crosses the relevant trial sequential monitoring boundaries.7778 92,98 A more detailed explanation of trial sequential analysis is available in the Trial Sequential Analysis Manual77 and at http://www.ctu.dk/tsa/.

For dichotomous outcomes, we will estimate the required information size based on the observed proportion of patients with an outcome in the control group (ie, the cumulative proportion of patients with an event in the control groups relative to all patients in the control groups), assuming a 25% relative risk reduction or relative risk increase, a 5% alpha level for the primary outcome, a 20% beta level and the observed diversity as indicated by the included trials.

For continuous outcomes, we will use the observed SD, a mean difference equal to the observed SD/2, an alpha of 5% for all outcomes, a beta of 20% and the observed diversity as suggested by the trials in the meta-analysis.

Summary of findings table

We will generate a summary of findings table that includes all prespecified outcomes. To assess the certainty of the evidence, we will apply the five Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) considerations (bias risk of the trials, consistency of effect, imprecision, indirectness and publication bias).8999,101

Imprecision will be assessed using trial sequential analysis, with downgrades applied as follows: three levels if the accrued sample is below 33% of the diversity-adjusted required information size (DARIS); two levels if between 33% and 66% of DARIS; one level if above 66% of DARIS; and no downgrade if the cumulative Z-curve crosses the boundary for benefit, futility or harm.77 All decisions to downgrade the certainty of the evidence will be transparently justified in footnotes, with additional comments provided where necessary for clarity. We will present our findings in two stages: first, based on trials with an overall low risk of bias, and second, based on all included trials.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Ethics and dissemination

This systematic review does not require ethical approval and informed consent, as it does not use identifiable patient data. All data extracted from included studies will be curated and managed in accordance with established systematic review standards, ensuring accuracy, transparency and reproducibility. Findings of this systematic review will be published in international peer-reviewed scientific journals.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis will evaluate the cardiovascular effects of incretin-based drugs in RCTs, primarily focusing on cardiovascular function and cardiac structure in patients with and without HF. Given concerns about the potential impact of GLP-1 RAs on cardiovascular physiology—particularly their effects on heart rate—this review will provide a comprehensive assessment of their cardiovascular impact in both patients with and without a history of HF.

Recent European Society of Cardiology guidelines for the management of cardiovascular disease (CVD) in patients with diabetes state that GLP-1 RAs (eg, liraglutide and semaglutide) should be considered for glucose-lowering treatment in patients with T2D at risk of or with HF (class IIa recommendation) already taking SGLT2 inhibitors, without differentiating between HF phenotypes.102 The consensus statement from the American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists supports the use of GLP-1 RAs as first-line therapy in managing hyperglycaemia in patients with T2D with established (or at high risk of) atherosclerotic CVD, as well as in those with chronic kidney disease or a history of stroke or transient ischaemic attack, while recommending the administration of SGLT2 inhibitors to patients with HF.103 The American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association primary prevention of CVD guideline supports the use of GLP-1 RAs in patients with T2D and high atherosclerotic CVD risk, but does not mention existing HF.104

Direct tissue effects versus weight loss effects of incretin-based drugs

The cardiovascular benefits of incretin-based drugs are likely mediated by both direct tissue effects and indirect weight loss effects, although their relative contributions may vary across different outcomes.49 These effects include enhanced endothelial function, improved myocardial metabolism, natriuresis, anti-inflammatory properties and blood pressure reduction.105 Since these agents also promote weight loss—which itself impacts haemodynamics and cardiac reverse remodelling in patients with obesity—the extent to which they exert direct cardiovascular effects in patients with and without HF remains uncertain.

Strengths

This protocol has several strengths. It follows a predefined methodology aligned with the PRISMA-P guidelines,73 74 the eight-step assessment proposed by Jakobsen et al,89 trial sequential analysis77 78 and GRADE certainty assessments.99 These methodological frameworks help mitigate the risks of design errors, random errors and systematic errors. Including all relevant RCTs ensures a robust statistical power to detect meaningful effects. Furthermore, this will be the first systematic review and meta-analysis to comprehensively evaluate the impact of incretin-based drugs on cardiovascular outcomes, alongside key markers of cardiac function and haemodynamics.

Limitations

Certain limitations must be acknowledged. First, including diverse patient populations (with and without HF) increases the potential for statistical heterogeneity. Additionally, while CVOTs will provide relevant data on the primary endpoint, many included trials may primarily focus on metabolic effects such as glycaemic control and weight loss rather than detailed cardiovascular assessments, potentially limiting available data on the secondary outcomes. Another constraint is that this review will rely on aggregate trial data rather than individual patient data, which may restrict the ability to perform detailed subgroup analyses. Finally, many trials may have relatively short treatment and follow-up durations, potentially limiting the ability to assess long-term cardiovascular effects, particularly on mortality and hospitalisation outcomes.

Relevance of this review

Incretin-based drugs are increasingly used across a range of cardiometabolic conditions, and many patients may already be receiving these agents at the time of incident HF. Clinicians are therefore faced with the question of whether it is safe—or potentially beneficial—to continue incretin-based therapy in such patients. While previous meta-analyses have explored the general cardiovascular effects of incretin-based drugs, none have systematically assessed their impact on cardiovascular function and cardiac structure markers in patients both with and without HF.
