Small extracellular vesicles contain metals and transfer metal intercellularly
Authors: Adityas Purnianto, Celeste Mawal, Mitali M Kulkarni, Huaqi Su, Tiana F Koukoulis, Patricia Wongsodirdjo, Ya Hui Hung, Scott Ayton, Ashley I Bush, Kevin J Barnham, Laura J Vella
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCells have developed a highly regulated system for the uptake, transport, utilization, storage, and export of metals, ensuring the maintenance of cellular homeostasis. Small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) function as a mechanism through which a cell can export its cargo and transfer it to recipient cells. However, in contrast to the other molecular cargo associated with sEVs, the metal content of sEVs is not well characterized. To address this gap in knowledge, we measured the levels of nine essential metals (copper, iron, zinc, manganese, magnesium, potassium, calcium, chromium, cobalt) and six non‐essential metals (nickel, rubidium, titanium, aluminium, lithium, lead) in sEVs originating from multiple in vitro and ex vivo sources. Our findings reveal that, beyond containing redox‐active essential metals and those involved in redox reactions, sEVs also exhibit the capability to export and transfer non‐physiological, potentially toxic metals.Keywords: exosomes, intercellular transfer, metal, small extracellular vesicles

INTRODUCTION

Metals play a vital role in the normal functioning and maintenance of homeostasis in all living organisms. It is estimated that approximately one‐third of known proteins are metal‐binding proteins (Ibers & Holm, 1980). Calcium, for example, the most abundant metal in the human body, serves both structural purposes in biological structures like bones and teeth, and functions in cellular signalling. Other essential metals serve as cofactors for enzymes and other proteins. Some of these metals, like iron, copper, and manganese, are capable of accessing multiple oxidation states, which is crucial for the activity of proteins. For example, the oxygen‐binding function of haemoglobin and myoglobin relies on iron within their heme structures, and the detoxification of reactive oxygen species by superoxide dismutases (SODs) is catalysed by the copper, manganese, and iron cofactors of these proteins. If the regulation of these redox‐active metals is disrupted, it can lead to an overproduction of reactive oxygen species, resulting in oxidative stress‐related pathological conditions, including neurodegenerative diseases (reviewed in Acevedo et al., 2019; Barnham et al., 2004; Barnham & Bush, 2014).

Not all metals have known physiological roles in the human body. Exposure to some non‐physiological metals that are common in the environment, such as lead, cadmium, and mercury, can be toxic. Organisms have developed mechanisms to detoxify small amounts of these metals, for example by conjugation with thiol antioxidants like glutathione (Bi et al., 2007; Gupta et al., 2010; Kim et al., 2017; Sobrino‐Plata et al., 2014). However, when exposed to high doses or chronic exposure to these metals, cellular thiol depletion can occur. Since thiols also play a role in the cellular antioxidant system, their depletion can disrupt the redox balance within the cell, leading to oxidative stress. These non‐physiological metals can also interfere with the functioning of antioxidant enzymes and compete with essential metals for binding sites on proteins, leading to a loss of function (Faller et al., 2005).

Cells have evolved a tightly regulated system for the uptake, transport, utilization, storage, and export of metals to maintain cellular homeostasis. Since cells utilize small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) to export cellular cargo and transfer it to recipient cells, sEVs could potentially serve as a mechanism for the export and transfer of metals. Only a few studies have explored the role of sEVs in this context, all focused on the transition metal, iron (Brown et al., 2019; Palsa et al., 2023; van Meteren et al., 2020). Aside from iron, the metal composition of sEVs is currently unknown. Here we report the metal content of sEVs isolated from multiple in vitro and ex vivo sources and demonstrate transfer of non‐physiological metals between cells.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell line maintenance

The human neuroblastoma SH‐SY5Y cell line (Cell bank Australia, #94030304) and the SV40‐immortalized normal human fibroblast cell line GM2069 SV9 (Coriell Institute, Camden, NJ) were provided by The Oxidation Biology laboratory at The Florey Institute. All cells were maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubation atmosphere. SH‐SY5Y cells were grown in Gibco™ Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F‐12 (DMEM/F12) (1:1) media with 4‐(2‐hydroxyethyl)‐1‐piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) (ThermoFisher Scientific, #11330057) supplemented with 20% foetal calf serum (FCS) (Bovogen, #SFBSF) and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin. The immortalized human fibroblast cells were grown in Gibco™ DMEM with high glucose, GlutaMAX™ Supplement and HEPES (ThermoFisher Scientific, #10564029) supplemented with 10% FCS and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin.

Small EV isolation from cell culture conditioned media

Extracellular vesicle depleted FCS (EV‐depleted FCS) was prepared by ultracentrifugation of DMEM/F12 (1:1) media with HEPES containing 30% FCS at 120,000 × g average for 18 h at 4°C. The supernatant was collected and filtered using gravity force through 0.22 µm polyether sulfone (PES) filter cup.

SH‐SY5Y cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 (1:1) media with HEPES supplemented with 2% EV‐depleted FCS and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin. The immortalized human fibroblast cell line was cultured in Gibco™ Opti‐MEM™ reduced‐serum media (ThermoFisher Scientific, #31985088) supplemented with 2% EV‐depleted FCS and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin for sEV harvesting. Conditioned media (CM) was subjected to centrifugation at 500 × g for 5 min at 4°C, then 2000 × g for 10 min. Subsequently, the supernatant was collected and centrifuged at high speed (16,000 × g) for 35 min at 4°C. Clarified SH‐SY5Y CM was concentrated using a Centricon® Plus‐70 centrifugal filter unit with 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) regenerated cellulose membrane (Merck, #UFC701008). The immortalized human fibroblast line CM was concentrated using a tangential flow filtration (TFF) cassette with 100 kDa MWCO PES membrane (Sartorius, #VF20P4). SH‐SY5Y and human fibroblast CM were subjected to size exclusion chromatography (SEC) using qEV10/35 nm legacy column (iZON) performed at 4°C with 5–10 mL of concentrated CM (CCM). Eluted sEV fractions (20 mL after void volume) were concentrated with Amicon® Ultra‐15 centrifugal filter units with 10 kDa MWCO regenerated cellulose membrane (Merck, #UFC901096).

For the measurement of metal concentration with inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS), sEVs were pelleted via ultracentrifugation at 128,000 × g average for 70 min at 4°C, using a TLA‐55 fixed‐angle rotor (Beckman Coulter, #366725) in an Optima MAX‐XP tabletop ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter, #393315).

Small EV isolation from human caudate brain tissue

The caudate brain tissue was used for sEV metal analysis because of the significant role transition metals play in maintaining neuronal homeostasis. An imbalance in transition metal is associated with multiple neurodegenerative diseases. Post‐mortem caudate tissues, stored at −80°C, were obtained from the Victorian Brain Bank. All procedures were approved by The University of Melbourne Human Ethics Committee (#1750801) and in accordance with the National Health and Medical Research Council guidelines. Table 1 shows the demographic details of the cases included in this study. Fresh frozen human caudate tissue was sliced into ∼2 mm thick sections before incubating with extracellular matrix digestion buffer containing 50 U/mL of collagenase type 3 (Worthington, #LS004180) at 25°C in a water bath with agitation at a speed setting of eight for 20 min. Digestion was stopped by adding 10× inhibitor solution containing 0.5 M EDTA, 10× Roche cOmplete™ protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma‐Aldrich, #11697498001), and 10× Roche PhosSTOP™ phosphatase inhibitor (Sigma‐Aldrich, #4906837001) to achieve a final concentration of 1× inhibitor solution. Undigested tissue and cells were removed by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min at 4°C, then 2000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C. The supernatant volume was reduced to 500 µL using Amicon® Ultra‐15 or Amicon® Ultra‐4 centrifugal filter unit with 100 kDa MWCO regenerated cellulose membrane (Merck, #UFC810024). Small EVs were isolated by SEC using qEV original/35 nm legacy column (iZON), and sEV fraction (2.15 mL after void volume) was concentrated using Amicon® Ultra‐15 or Amicon® Ultra‐4 centrifugal filter unit with 100 kDa MWCO regenerated cellulose membrane. Samples were ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g average for 70 min at 4°C using aTLA‐55 fixed angle rotor, and the pellet was resuspended in Dulbecco's phosphate buffered saline (DPBS) without Ca2+ and Mg2+ (ThermoFisher Scientific, #14190250) containing protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor before storing at −80°C.

Small EV isolation from human plasma

Peripheral blood was collected with informed consent from healthy female adult donors. Blood handling and experimental procedures were approved by The University of Melbourne Human Ethics Committee (#24582) and in accordance with the National Health and Medical Research Council guidelines. Peripheral venous blood was collected from three healthy young female participants aged between 20 and 30 years old after 8 h fasting. Blood was collected into Vacutainer tube K2EDTA (Becton Dickinson) using winged blood collection set 21G. Blood was mixed continuously in the tube to avoid coagulation. Blood was processed within 1 h from collection. Platelet free plasma (PFP) was prepared by centrifugation at 1500 × g at 20°C for 20 min, followed by two spins at 2500 × g for 10 min at 20°C before storing at −80°C.

Small EVs were isolated from platelet‐free plasma using a method adapted from (Karimi et al., 2018). The platelet‐free plasma was laid on top a 40%, 30%, and 10% iodixanol (OptiPrep™) gradient in a SW41Ti tube, before ultracentrifugation at 178,000 × g average for 2 h at 4°C. The band (approximately 500 µL) at interphase between 10% and 30% of iodixanol was collected and applied to a qEVoriginal/70 nm legacy SEC column (iZON). Fractions of 500 µL were collected immediately and fractions seven–nine (sEVs fractions) were pooled and concentrated by freeze drying followed by resuspension in dH2O.

Small EV isolation from bovine milk

Raw unpasteurized whole cow milk was provided by Dr Bradley Coleman (Bega Group). Raw milk was centrifuged at 5000 × g for 30 min at 4°C, then 14,500 × g for 60 min at 4°C, followed by three spins at 22,600 × g for 60 min at 4°C. The supernatant from the final centrifugation was filtered consecutively with 0.8, 0.45, and 0.22 µm PES filters (Corning, #CLS430770). Glacial acetic acid was mixed with defatted milk to a final concentration of 1% and then centrifuged twice at 4500 × g for 30 min at 4°C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm syringe filter before diafiltration in DPBS using a Vivaflow 200 tangential flow filtration (TFF) cassette with 100 kDa MWCO PES membrane (Sartorius, #VF20P4) as per manufacturer's instructions. The supernatant was further filtered through a 0.22 µm syringe filter before loading onto a qEV10/35 nm Legacy SEC column (iZON). The sEV fraction was concentrated using Amicon® Ultra‐15 centrifugal filter unit with 100 kDa MWCO regenerated cellulose membrane (Merck) at 4000 × g at 4°C. Concentrated sEV eluate was supplemented, to a final concentration, with 2 mM tris(2‐carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP), 1x Roche PhosSTOP™ phosphatase inhibitor, and 1× Roche cOmplete™ protease inhibitor cocktail.

Immunoblotting

Cells and sEVs were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer and then sonicated in an ice water bath for 30 min. Cell lysate was further sonicated with a probe sonicator (Branson). Brain tissue was lysed in RIPA buffer with additional mechanical lysis in Bioruptor (Diagenode) and Bullet Blender homogenizer (Next Advance) with zirconium beads. Samples were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C and protein concentrations were measured using the Pierce™ BCA protein assay kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Samples (equivalent protein) in Bolt™ LDS sample buffer and/or Bolt™ sample reducing agent (ThermoFisher Scientific) were run on NuPAGE™ 4%–12% Bis Tris Gels (ThermoFisher Scientific) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Total protein staining was performed using Revert™ 700 total protein stain (LI‐COR, #926‐11011). Destained membranes were blocked in Blocker™ FL Fluorescent blocking buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific, #37665) for 1 h at room temperature (RT) prior to incubation in primary antibody for 12 h at 4°C. Antibodies used in this study were syntenin‐1 (#ab133267) and calnexin (#ab22595) from Abcam, TSG101 (#T5701‐200) from Sigma Aldrich, and flotillin‐1 (#610821), BiP/GRP78 (#610979), and CD63 (#556019) from BD Biosciences, and alix (#sc‐53540) from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. Membranes were washed three times in tris buffer saline containing 0.1% Tween®20, then incubated in corresponding IRDye® fluorescence tagged secondary antibodies (LI‐COR) for 1 h, before washing and the fluorescence signal measured using LI‐COR odyssey imaging system. Analysis of band intensity was done in Image Studio™ software (LI‐COR). Band intensity was normalized with the corresponding Revert™ total protein staining.

Transmission electron microscopy

Vesicles were fixed in 1% glutaraldehyde before being adsorbed to grids (ProSciTech). After 1 min, the excess liquid was absorbed, and each grid was washed twice with 10 µL of dH2O. Subsequently, each grid was incubated twice in uranyl acetate for 1 min, excess uranyl acetate was then removed, and the grid was air‐dried. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed on a FEI Tecnai F20 at The Ian Holmes Imaging Centre at The Bio21 Advanced Microscopy Facility, The Bio21 Molecular Science and Biotechnology Institute, The University of Melbourne.

Metal analysis

Nine essential metals (copper, iron, zinc, manganese, magnesium, potassium, calcium, chromium, cobalt) and six non‐ essential metals (nickel, rubidium, titanium, aluminium, lithium, lead) were measured in the cells, tissue, biofluids and their sEVs using ICP‐MS. The essential metals were selected based on their important roles in cellular homeostasis including iron, copper, and zinc. Some of the non‐essential metals, such as lead and aluminium, were chosen because exposure to them is known to be harmful to humans and can contribute to pathological conditions. Samples were lyophilized overnight prior to ICP‐MS. Samples that were in pellet form were immediately processed without lyophilization. Each lyophilized homogenate or pellet was mixed with 50 µL of 65% (v/v) concentrated nitric acid (Suprapur, Merck) to digest the samples overnight at RT. The partially digested samples were then heated in a heating block at 90°C for 20 min to complete the digestion. After digestion, each sample was mixed with 1% (v/v) nitric acid to final volume of 0.5 mL.

ICP‐MS measurement was performed in an Agilent 7700 series ICP‐MS instrument using a Helium Reaction Gas Cell in routine multi‐element operating conditions. Calibration of the instrument was performed using certified multi‐element ICP‐MS standard calibration solutions (ICP‐MS‐CAL2‐1, ICP‐MS‐CAL‐3, and ICP‐MS‐CAL‐4, Accustandard) that contain a range of concentrations of multi‐elements (0, 5, 10, 50, 100 and 500 parts per billion). As an internal control, a certified standard solution that contains 200 parts per billion of Yttrium (Y89) was used (ICP‐MS‐IS‐MIX1‐1, Accustandard).

Loading experiments and EV‐mediated metal transfer

SH‐SY5Y cells were seeded at 125,000 cells/cm2 in DMEM/F12 (1:1) with HEPES media, supplemented with 10% FCS and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin. After overnight incubation, cells were treated with either 100 µM lead (Pb) acetate, 100 µM 57Fe isotope, or vehicle solution for 24 h. After 24 h, media containing unincorporated Pb or 57Fe was removed, and the cells were washed extensively with Locke's buffer three times. Fresh DMEM/F12 (1:1) with HEPES media, supplemented with 2% EV‐depleted FCS and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin, was added to the cells which were then incubated for 96 h before sEVs were harvested from the CM. Recipient cells (SH‐SY5Y) were seeded in a 24‐well plate in DMEM/F12 (1:1) with HEPES media, supplemented with 10% FCS and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin. The next day cells were washed with Locke's buffer twice before changing into DMEM/F12 (1:1) with HEPES media, supplemented with 2% EV‐depleted FCS and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin. Small EVs isolated from vehicle or Pb acetate‐treated cell CM (Veh‐sEVs or Pb‐sEVs) were incubated with the cells for 24 h. Cells were washed extensively with DPBS without Ca2+ and Mg2+, then detached from the plate by incubating them with trypsin for 5 min at 37°C, followed by two washes in Locke's buffer. Cells were then harvested for immunoblotting or metal analysis.

RESULTS

Characterization of sEVs isolated from cell culture, human caudate and plasma and bovine milk

Small EVs were isolated from multiple sources including the conditioned media of two cell lines (human neuroblastoma cell line SH‐SY5Y and human fibroblast cell line), human caudate brain tissue and plasma, and bovine milk. Small EVs were isolated using different methods optimized for each source of sEVs. SEC was used to isolate sEVs from the SH‐SY5Y and the fibroblast cell lines. Bovine milk sEVs were isolated by differential centrifugation followed by serial filtration and SEC. Human caudate sEVs were isolated using a protocol we previously developed (Huang et al., 2020; Vella et al., 2017). Human plasma sEV isolation was performed with both density gradient ultracentrifugation and SEC to deplete lipoprotein particles and highly abundant plasma proteins (Karimi et al., 2018).

The sEVs were characterized according to the minimal information for studies of extracellular vesicles (MISEV) 2023 guidelines (Welsh et al., 2024). Immunoblotting was used to confirm the presence of general sEV markers as well as the absence of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) proteins BiP and calnexin, casein in bovine milk sEVs, or low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) marker ApoB100 and high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) marker ApoA‐1 in human plasma sEVs (Figure 1). Transmission electron micrographs of negatively stained sEV preparations showed cup‐shaped structures with a size of 40–200 nm in diameter, which are typical of the morphology and size of sEVs (Figure 2).

Small EVs contain many essential metals

To determine the metal content of sEVs, ICP‐MS was utilized to quantitatively measure the concentration of metals in the parental cell line, tissue/fluid, relative to the same concentration (of total protein) of sEVs. Copper and iron were detected in sEVs from all in vitro and ex vivo sources (Figure 3A,B). The concentration of copper was significantly higher in sEVs isolated from cell lines relative to the parental cells, and also in human plasma and bovine milk sEVs relative to the neat biofluids (Figure 3A). Iron was enriched in human plasma sEVs and bovine milk sEVs relative to the neat fluids (Figure 3B). Human caudate tissue sEVs contained significantly less copper, iron, and zinc than the parental tissue.

Zinc was present in all sEVs, except for sEVs isolated from human plasma, and was present at a significantly lower concentration than the parental cell homogenate, tissue, or fluid (fibroblast, caudate, bovine milk) (Figure 3C). Manganese was also present in all sEVs, except for bovine milk sEVs, and was either present at a significantly lower concentration than in the parental cell homogenates, tissue, or neat fluid (SH‐SY5Y, fibroblast, human caudate and plasma) or as was the case with sEVs in human plasma, contained significantly more manganese relative to human PFP (Figure 3D). Magnesium was present in the sEVs derived from cell lines and caudate tissue at significantly lower concentrations than their parental cell lines or tissue, respectively (Figure 3E).

Other essential metals and elements were also measured including macro minerals such as potassium and calcium, and essential trace metals such as chromium and cobalt as well as the essential element phosphorus (Figure S1).

To further investigate the incorporation of trace metals into sEVs, SH‐SY5Y cells were preloaded with the non‐radioactive stable isotope of iron (57Fe) (Figure 4). This isotope represents ∼2% of all iron found in nature where the predominant isotope is 56Fe. Uptake of 57Fe by SH‐SY5Y cells was demonstrated by an enrichment of this isotope in the cells treated with 57Fe compared to the cells treated with vehicle (Figure 4B,C). The sEVs isolated from the conditioned media of SH‐SY5Y cells treated with 57Fe also showed an enrichment of 57Fe relative to the sEVs isolated from SH‐SY5Y cells treated with vehicle (Figure 4B,C). This result suggests that the essential trace element iron, is packaged intracellularly and then released from cells via sEVs. Taken together, these data show that sEVs export metals essential for biological systems including the redox active transition metals iron and copper.

Small EVs contain non‐physiological and toxic metals

Many metals have no physiological role in mammals (e.g., nickel, rubidium, titanium) and can be toxic to organisms especially in high doses or following chronic exposure, such as aluminium, lead, lithium, mercury and cadmium. Exposure to these metals, however, is inevitable due to their ubiquity in the environment. Organisms have developed mechanisms to detoxify and remove these metals from cells, but whether exosomes, a subtype of sEVs formed inside the cell and known to export unwanted molecular cargo, are one such mechanism, is unknown.

To examine whether sEVs contain non‐physiological metals, we measured nickel, rubidium, titanium, aluminium, lithium, and lead concentrations in cells, tissue, biofluids, and their associated sEVs (Figure 5). Nickel, rubidium, and titanium were detected in all sEVs (except for rubidium in SH‐SY5Y sEVs and titanium in human plasma sEVs) (Figure 5A–C). All sEVs were enriched in nickel, and four out of five sEVs contained less rubidium relative to their parental cell homogenate, tissue or biofluids (Figure 5A and B). Aluminium was detected in all human samples, and lithium and lead in cell lines and their sEVs (Figure 5D–F).

Lead is released via sEVs and transferred to recipient cells

Small EVs can transfer cargo, including proteins and RNA, between cells to mediate intercellular communication. Research into metal transfer via sEVs has been limited to indirect studies that have not directly shown transfer of sEV‐associated metal. Here we used lead, as a metal tracer, to examine whether sEVs could transfer metal between cells (Figure 6A). Parental SH‐SY5Y cells were treated with either vehicle (Veh) as a control, or lead acetate (Pb) to preload the cells with lead, followed by isolation of sEVs (Veh‐sEVs and Pb‐sEVs) (Figure 6B). Recipient cells were treated with increasing doses of either lead acetate solution, Veh‐sEVs, or Pb‐sEVs (Figure 6A and C). A dose‐dependent increase in lead levels was detected in lead acetate‐ and Pb‐sEVs‐treated cells (Figure 6C). While sEVs did successfully transfer lead, the transfer was not as efficient as non‐EV mediated delivery (Figure 6C). This result provides direct evidence that sEVs can transfer metal between cells.

DISCUSSION

Metals play a crucial role in the intricate balance of biological systems, governing processes such as uptake, transport, and storage in a tightly regulated manner. Any disturbance in metal homeostasis can potentially contribute to the development of various diseases, including neurodegenerative conditions (reviewed in Acevedo et al., 2019; Barnham et al., 2004; Barnham & Bush, 2014). The export of metals from cells is a complex and tightly regulated process crucial for maintaining homeostasis. Many proteins involved in the uptake and efflux of metals are localized to the plasma membrane. These proteins can traffic between intracellular compartments and can undergo recycling via the endosomal pathway (Harding et al., 1983; Pascale et al., 2003; Tabuch, 2010). The biogenesis of small extracellular vesicles, called exosomes, occurs within the endosomal system. Thus, proteins involved in the metal uptake and efflux, along with their associated metals, could potentially be incorporated into intraluminal vesicles (exosome precursors) during their biogenesis, before being exported out of the cell. However, unlike other biomolecular components of sEVs, the metal content of sEVs is not well characterized. This is the first study to measure the levels of multiple metals simultaneously in sEVs from several in vitro and ex vivo sources. We demonstrated that, in addition to containing many redox‐active essential metals, sEVs export and transfer non‐physiological metals.

We corroborated previous findings that sEVs isolated from in vitro sources and plasma contain iron (Atipimonpat et al., 2021; Brown et al., 2019; van Meteren et al., 2020). Additionally, we measured the iron content of sEVs from brain tissue and bovine milk sEVs. We showed that both human plasma and bovine milk sEVs were significantly enriched in iron relative to the neat fluids. Iron is a potent redox active metal which can catalyse the generation of reactive oxygen species via the Fenton reaction. Iron is rarely found in a bioavailable labile form but is bound to proteins and other biological molecules. An example of an iron binding protein is ferritin, the major iron storage protein in the cell. Using cryo‐electron microscopy, electron dense structures typical of ferritin‐iron cores have been shown inside EVs (Truman‐Rosentsvit et al., 2018). Moreover, sEVs carry iron‐containing proteins on their surface via iron‐binding receptors, such as the transferrin receptor, CD163, and CD91. During infection, host EVs can sequester iron in iron‐binding proteins to prevent bacterial acquisition of host iron (Kuang et al., 2023).

The mechanism of iron export via sEVs is not well characterized. One study suggests that an iron‐responsive element (IRE) is present in the 5′ untranslated region mRNA of CD63, a canonical EV marker (Yanatori et al., 2021). This study also showed that under iron repletion, cells deliver ferritin to CD63‐positive intracellular vesicular structures via the ferritin receptor Nuclear Receptor Coactivator 4 (NCOA4), which are released from cells as sEVs (Yanatori et al., 2021). Similarly, during ferroptosis, a programmed cell death mechanism, prominin2 was shown to induce the translocation of ferritin to multivesicular bodies and subsequent release of the iron‐containing ferritin via exosomes (Brown et al., 2019).

In comparison to the other studies showing iron in sEVs (Atipimonpat et al., 2021; Brown et al., 2019; van Meteren et al., 2020), here we utilized a rare non‐radioactive stable iron isotope 57Fe to demonstrate that iron was incorporated into sEVs after preloading the parental cells with 57Fe. This experiment demonstrated that an essential metal is exported from cells via sEVs and suggests a mechanism for the incorporation of metals into intraluminal vesicles, the sEV precursors.

We measured copper, another potent redox‐active transition metal in sEVs, and found that copper was present in all tested sEVs and significantly enriched in four out of five samples. This metal has never been measured before in sEVs isolated from biological fluids. A study showed that Gram negative bacteria utilized EVs to export copper, particularly under elevated copper conditions (Lima et al., 2022). The concurrent increase in the levels of a copper chaperone, CopM, in bacterial EVs correlated with the treatment of bacterial cultures with copper (Lima et al., 2022). Furthermore, the loss of a functional protein involved in the copper efflux system resulted in an increase in copper‐containing EVs (Lima et al., 2022), suggesting that EV release serves as an alternative pathway for intracellular copper detoxification in these bacteria. Similar to iron, secretion of copper in eukaryotic EVs may also provide a potential mechanism of detoxification against copper toxicity, particularly during an oxidative stress event. Several copper‐binding proteins are associated with eukaryotic sEVs, such as ceruloplasmin (Gangoda et al., 2017), copper chaperone for superoxide dismutase (CCS) (Liang et al., 2013), and antioxidant 1 copper chaperone (ATOX1) (Reinhardt et al., 2012), all of which are potential sources of copper in eukaryotic EVs. Additionally, eukaryotic sEVs contain the copper transporters ATPase copper transporting alpha (ATP7A) and ATPase copper transporting beta (ATP7B) (Safaei et al., 2005), which may mediate sequestration of copper to EVs.

Other essential metals which are involved in redox reactions were also detected in sEVs. For example, zinc, manganese and magnesium, albeit in mostly lower concentrations than the parental cells, or neat tissue/biofluids. Unlike iron and copper, zinc is not a redox active metal, but is recognized for its antioxidant properties. Zinc serves as the cofactor of copper‐zinc SODs (SOD1 and SOD3), which are important antioxidant enzymes that catalyse the dismutation of superoxide into H2O2. Both SOD1 and SOD3 have been identified in EVs (Abdelsaid et al., 2022; Silverman et al., 2019).

Similarly, manganese is a crucial component of manganese SOD (SOD2), which is exclusively located in mitochondria. Mitochondrial components have been identified in EVs including SOD2 (Zhang et al., 2019), and these components increase in sEVs under conditions associated with oxidative stress (Kim et al., 2020).

In addition to essential metals, we identified non‐physiological metals in sEVs including nickel, titanium, and rubidium. Notably, these metals have never been measured before in eukaryotic EVs. We showed that non‐physiological metals in the environment are taken up by cells and then exported from cells via sEVs. It has been reported that bacteria use EVs to export the non‐physiological metal cadmium as a way to resist its toxicity (Wang et al., 2023). In eukaryotic cells, sEVs may serve a comparable role. This idea is supported by the findings showing that cancer cells export the platinum metal‐based chemotherapeutic agent, cisplatin, through sEVs, conferring resistance to this drug (Safaei et al., 2005, Khoo et al., 2019). This defense mechanism could be a double‐edged sword if sEVs laden with toxic metals deliver their cargo to neighbouring or distant cells. Indeed, we have demonstrated for the first time that sEVs can transfer the non‐physiological metal, lead, between cells. This finding corroborates a previous EV transfer study suggesting EVs deliver iron intercellularly (van Meteren et al., 2020). The ability of sEVs to transfer potentially toxic metals implies that sEVs could be involved in spreading non‐physiological metal toxicity and toxicity related to redox‐active essential metals. Small EVs, enriched in iron, have been shown to propagate oxidative stress between hepatocyte cells leading to cell death (van Meteren et al., 2020).

In summary, we measured the metal content in sEVs isolated from diverse in vitro and ex vivo sources. The sEVs contained many macro minerals, essential trace elements, including redox‐active trace metals, as well as non‐physiological metals. The inclusion of non‐physiological metals in sEVs suggests a potential protective mechanism against toxic metal accumulation. Additionally, we provided direct evidence for sEVs transfer of metals between cells. This observation suggests that sEVs may play a role in the intercellular transport of essential nutrient metals under normal physiological conditions, while also potentially contributing to the propagation of oxidative stress through the delivery of redox‐active or toxic metals in pathological conditions. Our findings also suggest sEVs could be considered a source of biomarkers for diseases or conditions associated with metal dysregulation.
