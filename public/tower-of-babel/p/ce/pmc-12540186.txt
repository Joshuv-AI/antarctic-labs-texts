Value of diversity characteristics in predictive modeling: ACS screening as a case study
Authors: Gabrielle Bunney, Kate Miller, Keejeong Ryu, Rana Kabeer, Anna Graber-Naidich, Melissa A Pasao, Marium Rizvi, Maame Yaa A B Yiadom
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe sought to improve the subgroup performance variability of a model that identifies arriving ED patients at high risk for ACS, to receive an ECG within 10 minutes of arrival, to detect STEMI. We compared a Base Model using age, sex, and chief complaint alone to (1) an Interactions Model adding interactions among the 3 variables, and (2) a Diversity-Sensitive Model including race, ethnicity, language, as well as identity interactions. We quantified human performance and combined it with each of the 3 models simulating use as practice augmenting AI predictions. With sensitivity as our primary outcome, we found humans at 72.8% were bested by the Diversity-Sensitive Model at 82.8%, and by the human-augmented Diversity-Sensitive Model at 91.3%, improving ACS predictions in all demographic subgroups. However, there was residual variation among subgroups (range of sensitivity: 62%−98%). Given risk distribution differences, subgroup-specific ECG-testing thresholds may further equitize ACS prediction performance.Subject terms: Myocardial infarction, Unstable angina

Introduction

Equity and fairness are at the forefront of the question on whether to include race and ethnicity in a predictive model1–3. The question remains unanswered as the use of these demographic characteristics has both improved and harmed modeling performance within these groups2,4. Though not universally validated, its suspected that the inclusion of race and ethnicity is more likely to be beneficial in models that uncover an unmet health need rather than determining healthcare resource allocation1,4.

We address this question in the context of screening for acute coronary syndrome (ACS) upon arrival to the Emergency Department (ED). Patients with potential ACS must receive an ECG within 10 minutes of arrival to identify the severe sub-diagnosis of ST segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI)5,6. Failure to diagnose STEMI within 10 minutes is associated with a 50% higher mortality7. There are several clinical scores that predict the risk of adverse events among patients who have already received a diagnosis of ACS, the Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI)8 and Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE)9 score, and those who are undergoing further testing for ACS, the HEART10 score. However, these scores do not predict ACS and require the results of initial diagnostic testing to be used, such as an ECG and troponin levels. As a result, these scores fail to address the screening challenge of identifying who needs immediate consideration of ACS upon arrival to the ED such that these patients receive an ECG within 10 minutes to identify STEMI.

Current screening is often performed manually by non-clinical registration staff, the only staff who reliably contact patients within 10 minutes of arrival, using simple screening criteria6. Oversimplified screening, however, under-selects certain subgroups and leads to disparities in timely interventions for STEMI11–15. A prior investigation describing the outcomes of current staff screening has shown that Black patients have a 51% greater chance of delayed ACS diagnosis than white patients, and female patients have a 36% greater chance of delay than male patients6. We previously built a logistic regression model based on variables available within 10 min of ED arrival: age, sex, and chief complaint16. Compared to screening by registration staff, this model correctly detected more patients at risk for ACS overall, but systematically disadvantaged Black, Hispanic/Latino, and Native American, Alaskan, Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander (NAAH/PI) patients17. In our investigation of why this occurred, we found the incidence of ACS in these subgroups occurred at younger ages. Our model heavily weighted age, leading to uneven performance17. In addition, certain chief complaints were also more predictive of ACS in particular subgroups, such as the experience of chest pain in men compared to women.

Using what we learned from this prior work, we explored modifications to our prediction approach to address these inequities. We first modified the ACS predictive model by (1) allowing for interactions between variables, and then (2) including demographic information such as race, ethnicity, and language. Finally, we also tested the combination of the registration staff screening and the models’ predictions to evaluate human-model combined performance, simulating augmented intelligence. With these iterations, we aimed for the screening with these approaches to better account for diversity and, in turn, to have more consistent sensitivity within demographic subgroups.

Performance of ACS Screening Approaches to Identify STEMI: Human Alone, Models, and Model-Augmented Human Practice

Screening performance

Building on the base model, each progressive predictive approach had higher overall sensitivity than the prior. The largest increase in sensitivity was between the base model (77.5% 95% CI: 76.2−78.7%) and the interactions model (81.7%, 95% CI: 80.6−82.9%) (Table 2). The Diversity-Sensitive Model performed the best at 82.8% sensitivity (95% CI: 81.7−83.9%). Specificity hovered around 78% for each of the models, with PPV just over 3%. The proportion of missed STEMIs ranged from 18.0% in the base model down to 13.0% in the diversity-sensitive model. We see that the models are very well calibrated with R2 ranging from 99.8%–99.9% (Fig. 2). This is both above and below the screening threshold as denoted by the vertical line on the graph.

Calibration curves for 3 ACS screening models to identify STEMI: base, interactions, and diversity-sensitive models.

The first panel (red) shows the calibration of the Base Model, the second panel (green) shows the calibration of the Interactions Model, and the third panel (blue) shows the calibration of the Diversity-Sensitive Model.

The model-augmented human practice showed greater sensitivity than the predictive models alone, ranging from 89.8% (95% CI: 88.8-90.7%) in the base model-augmented human practice to 91.3% (95% CI: 90.4-92.1%) in the Diversity-Sensitive Model-augmented human practice (Table 2). Across all 3 model-augmented human practices, specificity was approximately 68% and PPV was consistently 2.5%. The percentage of patients requiring ECGs increased to roughly 33%, up from about 22% observed among humans and all models alone. The percentage of missed STEMIs ranged from 5.9% in the Interactions Model-augmented human practice to from 7.3% in the Base Model-augmented human practice. This is lower than the 13−18% observed among humans or with the models alone.

Performance across subgroups

Figure 3 shows the sensitivity and specificity of four screening approaches within 17 demographic subgroups. The grey series represents the observed human practice, which we used as a reference. The base model alone (light green series) showed higher sensitivity than human screening for some subgroups, such as men and White or Asian patients, but lower sensitivity for others such as Black, NAAH/PI, and Hispanic/Latino patients. Generally, the Base Model was less equitable than human screening because its sensitivity was more variable across subgroups. Compared to the base model, the Diversity-Sensitive Model (dark green series) had equal or higher sensitivity for all subgroups, with gains in sensitivity in Black and NAAHPI patients. This model mitigated some of the inequity in the Base Model by sex and race, though similar less pronounced inequities remained across ethnicity and language subgroups.

The two blue series in Fig. 3 present results for the model-augmented human practice. Compared to human performance alone and the base model alone, the Base Model-augmented human practice (light blue) showed higher sensitivity for all subgroups. For most subgroups, the Diversity-Sensitive Model-augmented human practice (dark blue) had the highest sensitivity. Compared to the predictive models alone, the model-augmented human practice had improved equity in sensitivity, with less variability across subgroups. Specificity, which was a secondary outcome, showed an expected inverse relationship with sensitivity. The two predictive models had similar specificity within each subgroup, except among Black and NAAHPI patients, where the base model had markedly higher specificity. The model-augmented human practice had the lowest specificity across all subgroups. Overall, the interactions, diversity sensitive, and model-augmented human practice had more variation across subgroups resulting in worse performance equity compared to human performance.

Discussion

Overall, our model-augmented human practice approach demonstrated superior performance at identifying patients with ACS using information known upon ED arrival compared to the predictive models alone. Specifically, the augmentation of human screening was more sensitive, and without prohibitive decreases in specificity, since this screening is for a very high-risk disease and the risks of performing this screening are very low. This result is consistent with our previous findings of synergistic human and model predection17 and continues to support the literature illustrating the “collaborative intelligence” resulting from combining humans performance with AI18.

Despite these improvements in sensitivity and not worsening predictions in any subgroup in comparison to human performance, all models reduced equity by widened variation across subgroups. While sensitivity increased with each incremental model, the largest improvement came from capturing the relationships between variables in the Interactions Model. Upon further exploration of our data, we found that the disease prevalence within subgroups varied widely. As a result, any single threshold favors majority subgroups, and underpredicts in subgroups with inherently lower risk, e.g. due to younger age distributions. This is not surprising as differing characteristics of disease across demographic subgroups has been previously documented19. With noted inequity there are two possible solutions: change the predictive model itself or modify the application of the prediction. Post-model processing, where the objective is to modify the application of the predictions to better target the desired impact, is an innovation that is being developed to solve this equity problem20,21. Due to the recognized importance of the equitable application of AI models, fairness has been a natural area of exploration for these methods22,23.

Adding the diversity characteristics in the Diversity-Sensitive Model only minimally improved sensitivity over the Interactions Model. We previously hypothesized that adding interactions into the model may improve its sensitivity17. The interactions are likely partially capturing variation associated with the diversity characteristics in ACS screening, particularly with chief complaint and age variation across sex, race, and ethnicity subgroups24–26. We structured our base model to include data available within 10 min of ED arrival. In practice race, ethnicity, and language are not collected by all EDs within this window. Therefore, it is more feasible to mathematically optimize the model whose variables are universally available, as with the Interactions Model, and avoid needing to negotiate workflow changes.

Our study has several limitations that should be considered in the interpretation of our results. First, our population has a lower percentage of Black and higher percentage of Asian patients compared to the general US population, which may affect generalizability of model performance. However, the concepts derived from the trends we observed are generalizable to modeling methods for ACS and other important predictions. Second, to avoid multiple testing, we did not perform any formal statistical tests between groups in this descriptive study, although the 95% CIs do allow for a general assessment of variance in the estimates. Third, the dataset was not split into training, validation, or test sets. The modeling exploration was not conducted to derive and validate a model for use. Rather, this paper shares the results of a conceptual exploration where we simulate the potential outcome of 3 models with different variables and their interactions.

Having the model augmented human staff screening had the best performance in identifying patients with ACS compared to the other predictive modeling approaches. Adding interaction terms and diversity characteristics improved the performance of a predictive model for ACS screening and avoided worsening performance in any subgroup. However, it increased variation in performance across subgroups, raising concerns about performance equity. Examining the predicted risk distribution of disease among demographic subgroups may unlock a way to not only improve the performance of the model but also impose algorithmic equity.

Methods

Study design

This is a descriptive retrospective cohort study simulating the prediction performance of 4 different predictive as the base of a planned AI intervention to identify arriving ED patients with highest risk for ACS needing an ECG within 10 minutes.

IRB approval

The Stanford Institutional Review Board reviewed the study and granted a waiver of informed consent (IRB Ethics approval number: 60010).

Human practice

ACS screening in current human practice by registration staff was our comparator for screening by model and model-augmented human practice. We considered a patient to be screened positive under human practice if they received an ECG within 10 min of ED arrival.

The models

The Base Model Approach is a logistic regression model predicted final hospital-diagnosed ACS using only variables available within 10 minutes of arrival to the ED: age, sex, chest pain, and “other ACS-associated chief complaints”. “Other ACS-associated chief complaints” was present when one of 15 non-chest pain ACS symptom categories, previously identified as a presenting symptoms among ACS patients, were reported16. Details on this modelling approach, and the selection of ACS-associated chief complaints, have been previously described16.

To account for the complex interactions between age, sex, chief complaints, and ACS risk we then derived an Interactions Model Approach. The approach used “chest pain” as a presenting ACS chief complaint, and the 15 other ACS-chief complaint categories, with each included as individual binary variables. We first ran bivariate regression models for each chief complaint variable to examine their independent association with ACS with a level of significance for inclusion in the Interactions Model of p < 0.10 in the bivariate analysis27. Second, we transformed age by squaring it (age2) to better capture the nonlinear relationship between age and ACS incidence. Last, for each pair of variables X and Y, we fit a small model predicting ACS from three terms: X, Y, and X*Y. Any interaction term achieving a p < 0.10 level of significance in this context was selected for inclusion in the full interactions model. We did this to account for the complex interactions between age, sex, chief complaints, and ACS risk.

Finally, we modified the Interactions Model to create the Diversity-Sensitive Model Approach by adding three new characteristics: race, ethnicity, and language. Race was included as White, Asian, Black/African American, Native American, Alaskan, Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander. Ethnicity comprised Hispanic/Latino. Language consisted of English, Spanish, and Other. The levels within these demographic variables were each included as binary variables. Just as with the Interactions Model, we ran bivariate analyses for each variable including any demonstrating an association with ACS at a p < 0.1 level of significance. The same was done for all permutations of variable pairings where we included their interactions as described above. Only two-way interactions were included. Higher-order interactions were not.

We combined the predictions of the models with human practice to create a Model-Augmented Human Practice Approach: any patient screened positive by either model or human practice was considered a screen positive in the augmentation approach. The purpose is to simulate how a model may be implemented in practice as the model acting as a fail-safe to human practice. Specifically:

1. Concordant Decisions: The agreed-upon decision was retained when both the model and human concurred (ECG performed or not performed).

2. Human-Driven ECG Decision: If the human recommended an ECG while the model did not, the ECG was performed.

3. Model-Augmented ECG Decision: If the model recommended an ECG despite the human not doing so, the model augmented the human’s decision, leading to an ECG being performed.

Data source and inclusion/exclusion criteria

We obtained data for an adult ED, co-located with an adult hospital, from the hospital’s electronic health record (EHR) system. We included all ED encounters from January 1, 2015 to December 31, 2022, and excluded encounters for patients <18 years of age, with unknown sex, or missing ICD codes needed to determine an ACS vs non-ACS diagnosis. The outcomes of ACS and STEMI were defined by a set of ICD-9 codes and ICD-10 codes in all models as has been previously established and can be found in the Supplementary Tables 1 and 2, respectively16,28,29.

Performance measures

Our primary performance measure for ACS screening was sensitivity (95% confidence interval [CI]). Secondary measures included specificity (95% CI), precision (positive predictive value [PPV]) (95% CI), the percentage of patients requiring screening ECGs, and the percentage of STEMI cases missed during screening. Our models were set to have a threshold of performing ECGs on 22% of patients, reflecting the current rate of obtaining ECGs in the ED upon patient arrival16.

Model calibration

For each ED visit in the dataset, we compared the predicted risk of ACS with the observed risk in a calibration curve to understand model reliability.

Analysis among subgroups

We identified 17 demographic subgroups of interest based on permutations of sex, age, race, ethnicity, and language that had at least 30 patients as a threshold for a meaningful sample per the central limit theorem30. We calculated sensitivity and specificity for these subgroups across five selected screening approaches: human practice, the base model, the diversity-sensitive model, the base model-augmented human practice and the diversity-sensitive model-augmented human practice. We excluded subgroups with unclear demographic meaning (e.g., race marked as “unknown/refused”) and any subgroups with fewer than 5 ACS cases.
