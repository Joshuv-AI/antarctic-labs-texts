Wetland science, ecosystem services and protection actions in China
Authors: Zicheng Yu, Ming Jiang, Fahu Chen
Published: 2023

Wetlands are widely recognized as among Earth's most vital ecosystems. They occupy a mere 5%−8% of our global landmass, but provide unparalleled ecosystem services per unit area compared to any other habitats. Wetlands provide not only sustenance and shelter for livelihoods but also essential ecosystem functions, including biodiversity preservation, climate regulation, hydrological flow regulation, and water purification. For example, wetlands serve as biogeochemical hotspots, actively participating in greenhouse gas exchanges between the Earth's surface and the atmosphere [1], and peatlands—peat-accumulating wetlands—contain the highest soil carbon density and the largest biosphere carbon pool among all ecosystems [2]. Often referred to as the “kidney of the world”, wetlands excel at removing pollutants and purifying water.

Despite their immense significance, wetlands are disappearing at an alarming rate worldwide. Over half of the world's wetlands have vanished, with much of this loss occurring in the 20th century due to land-use changes and environmental pressures. Recognizing the myriad benefits of wetlands, numerous countries are now prioritizing wetland conservation. For example, in 2022, China implemented the Wetlands Protection Law and released the National Wetlands Protection Plan (2022–2030). China also hosted the 14th Session of the Conference of the Parties to the Ramsar Convention (COP14) in Wuhan in November 2022. Recognizing the pivotal role of wetlands, especially peatlands, in mitigating climate change, a team of international researchers collaborated under the guidance of the United Nations Environmental Programme and the Global Peatlands Initiative, producing the Global Peatlands Assessment: The State of the World's Peatlands [3] as a contribution to the 26th UN Climate Change Conference of the Parties (COP26) held in Glasgow, UK in late 2021. Additionally, the recent publication of The Mires of China [4] provides a comprehensive update on knowledge and information regarding wetlands in China.

Wetland science provides the scientific basis for the conservation, restoration, and management of wetlands. Wetland ecosystems are delicately balanced, driven by intricate interactions among plants, water, soil, and microbial processes [1]. The outcome of these interactions shapes the ecosystem services that wetlands provide. Microbes, in particularly, play a central role in many processes in wetland soils, mediated by hydrological and chemical conditions. Microbial respiration and activities are fundamental in decomposing organic matter, recycling nutrients, and sequestering carbon in wetland soils.

To uncover fundamental scientific questions for wetland conservation and restoration, the National Natural Science Foundation of China (NSFC) organized the “Shuangqing Forum” in Beijing from October 18 to 20, 2021, themed “Wetland Protection and Restoration: Fundamental Theories and Critical Technologies”. Over 40 experts attended the forum to discuss a variety of subjects including wetland change and regional ecological security; key processes and mechanisms in wetlands; technological systems and models of wetland restoration; and wetland function maintenance and regional sustainable development.

This special section in the journal of Fundamental Research is an extension of the aforementioned “Shuangqing Forum” and extends invitations to the global scientific community devoted to wetland science, restoration, and management. In this issue, we present seven papers encompassing various natural wetland types, including peatlands, freshwater wetlands (including shallow lakes), coastal wetlands (salt marshes and mangroves), as well as restored and constructed wetlands. These papers primarily delve into understanding the intricate processes and mechanisms, with a focus on microbial composition and activity, soil organic matter decomposition and protection, and carbon storage.

Among the seven papers, two papers investigate the role of iron minerals in protecting soil organic carbon from mineralization and decomposition. Huang and coauthors evaluate the significance of iron-bound organic carbon in peatlands, known as the “rusty carbon sink”, by examining iron-bound organic carbon abundance across six peatland profiles in southwestern and central China [5]. Their findings reveal no significant differences between surface and sub-surface peats, challenging previous observations. Yuan and coauthors explore the role of iron minerals in regulating the rates and pathways of organic matter mineralization in shallow lakes with varying degrees of eutrophic levels and influences from nearshore wetlands [6]. Another paper by Liu and coauthors investigates microbial metabolic functions in the deep soils of freshwater wetlands in Northeast China, shedding light on the often-overlooked role of soil carbon metabolic activity in deep soil in wetland carbon cycling [7].

Two papers focus on microbial processes in coastal wetlands, a critical aspect of “blue carbon”. Zhang and coauthors evaluate the impact of salinity on soil microbial communities and organic matter decomposition in coastal salt marshes in the Yellow River Estuary [8]. Their controlled experiments unveil a threshold effect of salinity stress on organic matter decomposition and temperature sensitivity. Wang and coauthors investigate bacterial communities, their metabolic functions, and soil organic carbon storages in mangrove forests restored at various points over several decades (“chronosequence”) along the southeastern coast of China [9].

The remaining two papers in this collection are dedicated to wetland restoration and construction. Zou and coauthors use water isotope techniques to probe water sources and movement in degraded wetlands in semi-arid regions, offering insights into hydrological dynamics—a prerequisite for restoring these degraded wetlands [10]. Tian and coauthors investigate the efficacy of iron substrates and ions in pollutant removal in constructed wetlands from paddy field drainage in Northeast China [11].

This collection of articles explores a wide array of research topics within the realm of wetlands, covering diverse wetland types. They highlight the intricate ecological interactions and biogeochemical processes inherent in these transitional ecosystems, bridging terrestrial upland ecosystems and aquatic ecosystems. Furthermore, these papers emphasize the indispensable role of wetlands in providing essential ecosystem services crucial for both human well-being and ecological security.
