Low Incidence of Relapses After Vaccination in Anti‐Aquaporin‐4 Antibody‐Positive NMOSD
Authors: Sean J. Pittock, Jin Nakahara, Becky Parks, Kerstin Allen, Sami Fam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPatients with neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) may experience increased signs and symptoms of their underlying disease when vaccinated against meningococcal disease before receiving complement component 5 inhibitor therapies. This retrospective analysis indicated an overall low relapse incidence (mean [range], 3.3% [0.7%–10.6%]) of physician‐reported relapses occurring within 4 weeks of meningococcal vaccination in those screened in CHAMPION‐NMOSD (NCT04201262), randomized to eculizumab or placebo in PREVENT (NCT01892345), and from the Japanese postmarketing surveillance of eculizumab in patients with anti‐aquaporin‐4 antibody‐positive NMOSD. Further studies are needed to determine if postvaccination relapses are attributable to vaccination or inherent relapse risk among these patients.

Introduction

Anti‐aquaporin‐4 antibody‐positive (AQP4‐Ab+) neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) is a rare autoimmune disease of the central nervous system characterized by repeated, unpredictable relapses that lead to irreversible neurological disability, including vision loss and paralysis [1, 2]. The complement component 5 inhibitor therapies (C5ITs) ravulizumab and eculizumab have been shown to reduce the frequency and severity of NMOSD relapses and are approved for AQP4‐Ab+ NMOSD in several countries and regions, including Europe, Japan, and the United States [3, 4, 5].

Patients with complement deficiencies or those who are treated with complement inhibitor therapies are at increased risk of Neisseria meningitidis (Nm) infection [6, 7]; patients initiating C5ITs are advised to be vaccinated against meningococcal disease at least 2 weeks beforehand [8, 9]. Because vaccinations may further activate the complement system, patients with complement‐mediated diseases, including NMOSD, may experience increased signs and symptoms of their underlying disease when vaccinated [8, 9, 10]. Therefore, informing on the risk of relapse after meningococcal vaccination prior to starting C5IT in patients with NMOSD is of clinical importance; here we report on relapses occurring within 4 weeks of meningococcal vaccination in 2 clinical trials and a real‐world postmarketing surveillance (PMS) setting in patients with AQP4‐Ab+ NMOSD.

Methods

This analysis included patients with AQP4‐Ab+ NMOSD with vaccination data who were screened in CHAMPION‐NMOSD (NCT04201262), irrespective of screening outcome; patients who failed to meet CHAMPION‐NMOSD inclusion or exclusion criteria or relapsed during the screening period were considered screen failures. The analysis also included patients randomized to placebo or eculizumab in PREVENT (NCT01892345) and patients with AQP4‐Ab+ NMOSD enrolled in the Japanese PMS and treated with eculizumab from November 2019 (Japanese approval) to October 2023 (data cutoff). Eligibility criteria and methodology of these studies have been reported [3, 4, 5].

Patients in CHAMPION‐NMOSD were vaccinated against Nm within 3 years prior to or at the time of study drug initiation. In PREVENT, patients had to be vaccinated at least 14 days prior to the first dose of study drug. In both studies, patients vaccinated less than 14 days prior to study drug initiation were to receive appropriate antibiotics until 14 days after vaccination. Vaccination data were not available for screen failures from PREVENT. Patients enrolled in the ongoing Japanese PMS were treated per Japanese guidelines and received meningococcal vaccination at least 14 days prior to the first dose of eculizumab; concomitant antibiotics may have been given if vaccination was less than 14 days prior to the first dose. Nm serotype B vaccine is currently unavailable in Japan [4].

All analyses were descriptive and based on observed data, presented as counts and percentages. The primary outcome was the number of patients with a physician‐reported relapse occurring within 4 weeks of any pre‐C5IT meningococcal vaccination; the first dose of C5IT could be included in the 4‐week period. Only physician‐reported relapses were analyzed, as relapses occurring in the Japanese PMS and before C5IT initiation in CHAMPION‐NMOSD and PREVENT were not adjudicated. The proportion of patients receiving oral corticosteroids (prednisone, prednisolone, and methylprednisolone [CHAMPION‐NMOSD and PREVENT]; prednisolone [Japan PMS]) at the time of vaccination was examined.

Physician‐reported relapses within 4 weeks after meningococcal vaccination were infrequent (Figure 2A). In CHAMPION‐NMOSD, 2 of 70 (2.9%) patients, both screen failures, relapsed within 4 weeks after meningococcal vaccination; neither patient received ravulizumab. The relapses occurred 12 and 19 days after meningococcal vaccination; relapse type was not captured for screen failures. Data for corticosteroid use at the time of vaccination were available for 66 of 70 patients in CHAMPION‐NMOSD, of whom 34.8% (23/66) were receiving concomitant corticosteroids at the time of vaccination. In PREVENT, 4 of 96 (4.2%) patients randomized to eculizumab had a physician‐reported relapse within 4 weeks of meningococcal vaccination (range, 5–17 days; partial transverse myelitis [n = 3]; brainstem syndrome and partial transverse myelitis [n = 1]). Three occurred prior to receiving eculizumab; one occurred in a patient vaccinated on the same day as first eculizumab dose. Of patients randomized to placebo, 5 of 47 (10.6%) experienced a physician‐reported relapse 2–20 days after vaccination (partial transverse myelitis [n = 2]; cerebral syndrome [n = 1]; brainstem syndrome and partial transverse myelitis [n = 1]; bilateral optic neuritis and partial transverse myelitis [n = 1]). Data for corticosteroid use at the time of vaccination were available for 91 of 96 patients randomized to eculizumab and 46 of 47 randomized to placebo, of whom 58.2% (53/91) and 47.8% (22/46) of patients, respectively, were receiving concomitant corticosteroids at the time of vaccination. Three patients (2 of 4 randomized to eculizumab and 1 of 5 to placebo) who relapsed were receiving concomitant corticosteroids at the time of vaccination.

In the Japanese PMS, 150 patients received a meningococcal vaccination; only 1 (0.7%) patient had a physician‐reported relapse within 4 weeks of meningococcal vaccination. The relapse was classified as transverse myelitis and occurred 12 days after meningococcal vaccination; eculizumab was initiated after the relapse. The patient was not receiving concomitant corticosteroids at the time of vaccination. The 150 patients included in this analysis received at least 1 meningococcal vaccination before initiating eculizumab (Table 1). Excluding cases with unknown corticosteroid use or timing, patients were receiving concomitant oral corticosteroids for 87.2% (129/148) of vaccinations.

Figure 2B illustrates the time course of meningococcal vaccination and physician‐reported relapse for the 12 of 363 patients included in this analysis who relapsed within 4 weeks after meningococcal vaccination. The average time between vaccination and these 12 physician‐reported relapses was 1.6 weeks.

Discussion

This retrospective analysis of clinical trial and real‐world data indicates a low percentage of patients who experienced a physician‐reported relapse (mean [range] 3.3% [0.7%–10.6%]) within 4 weeks of meningococcal vaccinations. While these data cannot determine whether the relapses observed can be directly attributable to meningococcal vaccination or inherent relapse risk, the data illustrate a low risk of relapse among patients with AQP4‐Ab+ NMOSD within 4 weeks of receiving a meningococcal vaccination. A recent retrospective study of patients with AQP4‐Ab+ NMOSD treated with eculizumab in Germany and Austria found a higher postvaccination relapse rate of 15.6% (7/45) before eculizumab initiation [10]. However, 3 of the 7 patients with postvaccination relapses had received plasma exchange ≤ 16 days before meningococcal vaccination, suggesting that these patients may have experienced a relapse shortly before vaccination with the postvaccination relapse representing a pseudo‐relapse [10]. The authors suggested that oral prednisone may have been protective in their patients at the time of vaccination [13]. Our results may be indicative of a protective effect of oral corticosteroids at the time of vaccination; however, the number of relapses in our analysis was too small to formally detect any effect.

As relapse prevention is a principal goal of NMOSD management [1], determining the most effective therapy for each patient is critical. Eculizumab and ravulizumab have been shown to be effective therapies for the long‐term treatment of patients with AQP4‐Ab+ NMOSD [3, 5]. In PREVENT and CHAMPION‐NMOSD, relapse risk was reduced by 94.2% and 98.6%, respectively, with C5IT treatment relative to placebo [3, 5]. In previously published data from the Japanese PMS (N = 151), 7 patients experienced 11 physician‐reported relapses (as of October 2023), translating to a lower relapse rate of 0.06 relapses/patient‐year with eculizumab compared with 0.57 relapses/patient‐year in the same patients in the 2 years before eculizumab initiation [14].

Limitations of this analysis include the retrospective and observational nature of the data and the lack of comparator or control group. As prior research has suggested a possible increase in relapse following vaccination, there is a need to explore this association. The data presented here cannot determine whether the relapses observed can be attributable to meningococcal vaccination or inherent relapse risk but suggest that the risk is low. Future prospective studies or retrospective analyses are needed to further examine this possible relationship.

Given the heightened risk of Nm infection among patients treated with C5ITs, critical risk mitigation strategies include infection awareness, proper vaccination, or the use of prophylactic antibiotics until vaccination. Patients should receive meningococcal vaccinations according to current national vaccination guidelines [8, 9]. While vaccinations may cause patients to experience increased signs and symptoms of their underlying disease [8, 9], these findings demonstrate a low incidence of relapse within 4 weeks of meningococcal vaccinations among patients with AQP4‐Ab+ NMOSD. The use of prophylactic antibiotics in patients starting C5IT less than 2 weeks after vaccination may allow for prompt initiation of treatment [8, 9, 10, 15].
