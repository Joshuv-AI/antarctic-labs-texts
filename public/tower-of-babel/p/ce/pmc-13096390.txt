Multifunctional chitosan/polyvinyl pyrrolidone/iron vanadate nanocomposites: insights into structural, optical, electrical, and dielectric properties for sustainable applications
Authors: Amani M. Al-Harthi, A. Rajeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, the polymer nanocomposites (PNC) films, which consist of chitosan (CS), poly (vinyl) pyrrolidone (PVP), and iron vanadate (FeVO4) nanoparticles (FVO NPs) were prepared utilizing the solution casting method. Numerous investigations, including structural, vibrational, electrical, dielectric, and optical tests, demonstrate promising properties of the generated polymer nanocomposites. Up to a 1.5 weight% FVO concentration, the sample’s amorphous character improved, according to the Xray diffraction (XRD) examination. The existence of intermolecular interactions in the nanocomposite was confirmed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and UV-Visible spectroscopy. Experimental results showed that the nanocomposites’ optical bandgap decreased as the FVO concentration grew. The investigative values of the ε′, ε′′, and σac show notable differences in frequency. It is found that the values of the ε′ and ε′′ rise with frequency. It is shown that when the frequency increases, so does the σac. The σac value increased with frequency, reaching 4.14 × 10− 6 S·cm− 1 at 104 Hz from 5.42 × 10− 10 S·cm− 1 at 100 Hz. An increase in σac values is observed when the loading of FVO NPs is increased. Dielectric analyses showed that the CS/PVP/1.2%FVO nanocomposite sample had the best boost. Additionally, the energy density rose from 4.2 × 10− 7 J/m3 for CS/PVP to 1.35 × 10− 6 J/m3 for CS/PVP/1.2%FVO. The results show that the CS/PVP/FVO films can be used in energy storage applications because of their dielectric properties, which are successfully improved by increasing the FVO concentration.

Introduction

Scientists have recently concentrated their efforts on creating room-temperature, highly electrically conducting solid polymer electrolytes (SPE) with desired characteristics including electrode compatibility, mechanical flexibility, and chemical stability1. Fuel cells, dye-sensitized solar cells (DSSCs), electrochemical capacitors, batteries, and other electrochemical devices are using these materials as the most promising electrolytes for energy storage and conversion2,3. Additionally, the suitability of SPE for energy storage devices in a variety of applications-such as fuel cells, sensors, credit cards, electrochromic display devices, batteries, etc.ــ has been thoroughly investigated4. Among the host polymers used in the manufacture of natural SPE are natural biopolymers, such as chitosan and its derivatives, cellulose, polylactic acid, and starch. Synthetic polymers include polyethylene, polyvinyl chloride, polyvinyl pyrrolidone, polycarbonate, polyacrylonitrile, and polyvinyl chloride5,6. More researchers were drawn to natural or biopolymer-based electrolytes in particular because of their unique qualities and benefits, such as their high mechanical and biocompatibility, nontoxicity, availability, abundance in natural sources, electrical qualities, and low cost of production7,8. On the other hand, synthetic polymers are a major issue and pollute the environment. Therefore, it is crucial to replace non-biodegradable polymers with biodegradable ones9. A simple method for producing novel polymeric products with improved properties and broader applications than those of a single polymer is to blend two or more polymers. These organic macromolecules can also be combined with inorganic nanofiller (metals and/or metal oxides) to generate novel PNC that differ from the original compounds10,11. The main obstacles to the intended development of PNC are the inorganic nanofillers’ type, size, shape, correct dispersion, strength of interactions with the polar groups in the polymer matrices, and capacity to create complex structures and hydrogen bonds12. Additionally, the physico-mechanical and chemical properties of PNC may significantly alter when two types of nanofillers are added to a blend13,14. The literature review makes clear that functional groups linked to the backbone of polymers and bio-polymers can readily interact with nanofiller and change their chemical structure15,16. Because of their exceptional qualities, including high affinity, antibacterial activity, film-forming ability, biocompatibility, and nontoxicity, some naturally occurring biopolymers, such as starch, cellulose, and CS, are being utilized extensively in research17. Due to its various polar functional groups, such as C–O–C, NH2, O–H, and OH, CS can be used in food packaging, biomedical applications, and other fields. However, it has certain drawbacks, such as brittleness and low electrical conductivity at room temperature (~ 10− 10 S/cm)18,19. The heteroatom’s lone pair electrons cause components of other polymers or additives to interact with one another, which is nitrogen or oxygen linked to the CS backbone. Poly (vinyl) pyrrolidone is a water-soluble polymer with a number of desirable characteristics, including high dielectric constant, strong environmental stability, amorphousness, and ease of processing. Various approaches, such as polymer blending, plasticizer addition, and nano-fillers, were utilized to enhance the mechanical flexibility and room temperature (RT) ionic conductivity17,20. Therefore, in the polymeric matrix, CS and PVP are a perfect combination. The polymers become miscible due to the H bond attraction between the C=O groups associated with PVP and the NH2 and OH groups of CS. This results in the creation of a novel homogenous biocompatible blend that can be applied in various ways21,22. Additionally, the nanoparticles were included into the polymeric matrices to create a novel composites films made of CS, PVP, and NPs in order to enhance its inclusive qualities. It is currently unclear how the nanofiller affects the optical, conduction, mechanical, thermal, and electrochemical characteristics of the SPE23. TiO2, Al2O3, SiO2, MgO, and other nanoparticles are the most often used. It has been discovered that adding these nanoparticles to SPE is one of the best ways to achieve balanced ionic r and mechanical stability qualities24. In order to explain the ionic conductivity of PV-based SPEs, Malathi et al.25 and Prajapati et al.26 recently took into account complex permittivity characteristics. Dielectric analysis was also employed by Pradhan et al.27 and Sengwa et al.28 to comprehend the behavior of nanocomposite SPE. The dielectric characteristics of ion-conducting SPE and PNC have not received much attention, according to a thorough review of the literature. Even the mechanism of ion conduction in SPE remains poorly understood. Understanding the ion-transport behavior in PNC is therefore crucial. In ion-conducting polymers, where the dielectric constant is a key indicator of a polymer material’s capacity to dissolve filler, the dielectric characteristics related to relaxation processes are especially important29. Iron vanadate (FeVO4), which is less expensive but has a superior redox reaction and comparatively greater chemical and thermal stability than most vanadates, is another option. Because of these characteristics, FeVO4 is used in photocatalytic, electrochromic, and energy storage applications30. However, because the FeVO4 with ideal shape, structure, and electrochemical characteristics has not yet been synthesized, its full potential as a supercapacitor electrode material has not yet been realized. FeVO4’s visible spectrum bandgap of about 2 eV allows it to effectively excite charge carriers and absorb a significant amount of sunlight31. Because CS and PVP have several chemical groups that enable practical, effective, and robust interactions between the polymer chains as well as with nanofiller, a binary mix made of these two materials is anticipated to be highly miscible. It has not been documented how FeVO₄ as a nanofiller affects the physical characteristics of CS, PVP, or CS/PVP. To the best of our knowledge, there are no studies or papers on CS/PVP/FeVO₄ nanocomposites in the literature. In this work, the intriguing physical and chemical characteristics of FeVO4 and the CS/PVP blend are combined in composites, and their interactions, complexation, and optical, electrical, and dielectric characteristics are investigated. The results demonstrated that the addition of FeVO₄ NPs improved the structural, electrical, dielectric, and optical properties while altering the mix structure. Additionally, it enhanced charge flow and energy density. Because of this, the resulting PNC show promise for the development of certain electrochemical, optoelectronic, and energy-storing devices.

Experimental works

Materials

We purchased iron vanadate (FeVO4), polyvinyl pyrrolidone (M.W. 40,000 Da, 102652840, CAS-No.: 9003-39-8), and chitosan (high molecular weight, 102537320, CAS-No.: 9012-76-4) from Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany. Throughout the whole synthesis process, acetic acid and double-distilled water (DDW) were used as solvents.

Preparation of CS/PVP/FeVO₄ nancomposites

50%CS/50% PVP/x wt% FVO polymer nanocomposites were made using the solution casting method. To make a CS/PVP blend, 1.2 g of CS and 1.2 g of PVP for each film were separately dissolved in 70 ml of DDW and acetic acid at 70 °C. FeVO4 nanoparticles (0, 0.5, 0.8, 1.2, and 1.5 wt% for polymer) were distributed in distilled water and subjected to a 12-minute ultrasonication. After that, the CS/PVP blend was combined with the nanoparticle aqueous solution and ultrasonicated once more for thirty minutes. Until the solution was homogenous, the mixture was forcefully agitated. After that, the mixture was put into a petri dish and dried for three days at 50 °C in an oven. After being removed from the petri plates, the films were stored at RT in a vacuum desiccator. The PNC films that were produced ranged in thickness from 0.23 to 0.25 mm.

Characterization details

The PVP/CS and FVO/PVP/CS X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were measured using a PANalytical X’Pert PRO diffractometer. A Cu Kα source with a wavelength of λ = 1.5418 Å at room temperature was used to directly scan the samples at 2θ angles between 50° and 80°. A Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy analysis in the 500–4000 cm⁻¹ range was conducted using a Shimadzu IR Tracer-100. A Spectronic CamSpec M550 double-beam UV–Vis spectrophotometer was used to record the absorption spectra of the as-prepared thin films in the 200–1100 nm range. The dielectric and impedance properties of PVP/CS -x wt.%FVO nanocomposite films were evaluated at RT using the Novocontrol Turnkey 40 Conceptual model.

Results and discussion

XRD

Figure 1 illustrates the XRD data of the pure blend and the blend filled with different weight fractions of FeVO4 NPs nanofiller. Halo peaks at 2θ = 8.5°, 11.8°, 19.9°, and 22.0° show the amorphous nature of chitosan32. While PVP has 2 peaks at 2θ = 10.4° and 21.2°33. Diffraction peaks for the CS/PVP mix are seen at 2θ = 8.8°, 11.9°, 18.5°, and 22.1°. These peaks represent the structural properties of the two polymers, and their shifting confirmed the interaction between the two polymers34. Strong interactions between the (O–H and N–H) groups of CS and the (C=O) group of PVP result from mixing CS and PVP; as a result, the length of intramolecular and intermolecular H bonds reduced, the CS and PVP became miscible, and the film stiffness rose. The intensity of the diffraction peaks further decreased at higher content of the FVO NPs; however, the present XRD pattern revealed no discernible changes in the locations of the three peaks. This could be the consequence of the blend’s interaction with FeVO₄ NPs, which reduces intermolecular contact and the degree of crystallinity in the mix chains19. The degree of crystallinity and peak intensity were found to be related by Hodge et al.35. They found that when a dopant is added, the diffraction pattern’s intensity diminishes as the amorphous nature grows. Higher concentrations of FeVO4 NPs in the polymer did not show any distinct peaks in the current study, indicating the predominance of the amorphous phase36. In amorphous polymers with a flexible main chain, this amorphous character results in increased ionic transport and enhanced ionic conductivity37. Additionally, the characteristic peak of the CS/PVP blend becomes more intense and broader as the FeVO4 concentration rises. This implies that the blended composite is becoming more amorphous and less crystalline. This reduction in amorphous phases is necessary to increase the conductivity of the polymer blend, which is beneficial for its application in dielectric capacitors. Strong intermolecular adhesion and the effective integration of FVO into the CS/PVP cause the blend with the primary crystalline peaks of FVO to undergo this transition from a crystalline to an amorphous structure. M. A. Awad et al.30 stated that pure tramadol emerged at 2θ = 23.35°, 23.61°, 25.21°, 27.35°, 28.31°, 28.85°, 29.53°, 30.27°, 31.12°, 31.51°, 32.41°, 33.63°, 34.81°, 35.39°, and 42.53°, the steep peaks at roughly 2θ = 25.21°, 27.35°, 33.63°, and 35.39°, corroborated the presence of crystalline FeVO4. Each of the constituents is present as a crystalline material.

FTIR

Figure 2 shows the FTIR spectra of the PVP/CS composite and PVP/CS/FVO incorporated with various concentrations of FVO nanoparticles. The spectra of the CS/PVP/FVO PNCs contain a wide and broadband in the range of 2995–3550 cm–1, which can be divided into two bands. The asymmetrical stretching of the NH2 group in the PVP/CS blend is responsible for the first band, which has a center of 3350 cm–138, while the OH stretching vibration overlaps with the ring of carbohydrate in the chitosan39. The asymmetric stretching vibration of C–O–C and the stretching of C–O in the C–O–H groups are responsible for the three absorption bands at 1149 cm–1, 1063 cm–1, and 1030 cm–138. At 2931 cm− 1 and 2860 cm− 1, respectively, the C–H symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations may be observed. The bond vibrations of amide I and amide II are located at 1649 cm− 1 and 1557 cm− 1, respectively. Furthermore, the stretching vibration of –C=N of amide-III in chitosan is observable at 1387 cm–1. The absorption band at 1280 cm–1 is caused by the frequencies of the (CH + OH) combination40. The blend’s twisting vibrations could be the cause of the tiny band at 653 cm⁻¹. The spectra of all the films are comparable, and there is no noticeable modification in the peak position as a result of FVO incorporation. Nevertheless, it is seen that the width and intensity of the peaks grew when the filler ratio was increased to 1.2 and 1.5 weight%, but dropped after loading 0.8 weight% FVO. This finding suggests that the best ratio for limiting the vibrations of CS/PVP blend functional groups is 1.2 weight%.

Optical properties

The optical absorption findings for the pure blend and the blend filled with different weight fractions of FVO NPs nanofiller are shown in Fig. 3. The absorbance spectra of all blends show two distinct absorption peaks at 221 nm and 250 nm. The identified peaks are associated with the R-band, which results from n → π* and π → π* transition of the C=O groups in the polymer blend41. Since no clear peaks are visible right away, all developed films are transparent. Nonetheless, the data shows a clear relationship between the inclusion of nanoparticles and improved material absorption. It is clear that the samples’ absorption intensity increases along with the doping concentration. The film doped with 1.2% FVO NPs is especially notable since it exhibits the highest intensity and absorption peaks between 190 and 600 nm. Over the whole wavelength range, the doped blend with x = 1.2 weight% FVO showed the highest absorption. This, by encouraging better charge transfer interactions and electronic transitions, FVO nanoparticles effectively improve the optical properties of the polymer matrix, indicating the optimal nanoparticle loading for enhanced optical features42. The research of CS/PVA doped with ZnO NPs showed similar outcomes43. The creation of H bonds between the blended polymer chains and the nanofiller could account for variations in the absorption edge36. The CS/PVP-FVO nanocomposite exhibits a redshift in absorption when compared to the CS/PVP-FVO nanocomposite. The growth of the CS/PVP-FVO nanocomposite results in a shift in crystallinity and a red shift in the fundamental absorption edge toward a longer wavelength. The redshift of the absorption band would facilitate the effective transfer of charge carriers from the valence band to the conduction band, resulting in high electrical conductivity.

AC impedance analysis

Conductance spectra

Figure 7 displays the frequency dependent real part (σ’) of the complex alternating current (ac) electrical conductivity (σ*) at room temperature for both pure blend and blend filled with different weight fractions of FVO nanofiller. In contrast to the real parts, which show two distinct linear dispersion regions in the lower (audio frequency (AF) region: 0.1 Hz −7 MHz) and higher (radio frequency (RF) region: 20 kHz − 7 MHz) frequency ranges of these spectra, the frequency dependent behavior of σ’ in this figure demonstrates two are linear over the entire frequency range. The semicrystalline structure of the examined samples is the cause of these linear zones with varying slopes. The power law relation σ′(ω) = σdc + Aωn, where n is a fractional exponent, A is a pre-exponential factor, and σdc is the direct current (dc) conductivity, is used to match the σ′ values for the AF area50. The obtained σdc and n values of these samples are recorded in 1. The charge motion is the hopping mechanism with a long-range translational movement, as indicated by the n values being much less than 151. Additionally, the σdc values are found to be higher than those of the pure CS/PVP blend. Following the inclusion of FVO NPs, σdc values improved because of the creation of three-dimensional conductive routes through their interactions with the polymeric matrix’s functional groups and each other52–54, this might have facilitated the flow of charge carriers. It is discovered that the AC conductivity of the nanocomposites rises as the concentration of FVO nanoparticles increases up to 1.2 weight%, after which it falls as more NPs are added. The concentration of nanoparticles at which conductivity stays constant after subsequent nanoparticle addition is the composite’s percolation limit, sometimes referred to as critical nanofiller loading55. In this study, the percolation limit is determined to be 1.2 wt% of loading. At this concentration, the nanocomposite’s conductivity suddenly increases by magnitude several orders. This percolation threshold in the CS/PVP-FVO nanocomposite is caused by strong interfacial contacts between the nanoparticles and blend segment, which lead to the creation of a conductive network inside the blend matrix. The polarity of the nanofiller and the chains of polymer, aspect ratio, surface area, size, shape, nanofiller aggregating propensity, and nanofiller dispersion in the CS/PVP blend all effect the nanocomposite’s conductivity at the limit of percolation56. At greater concentrations, the uneven distribution and poor orientation of the FVO nanoparticles in the blend can lead to poor conductivity. Higher loading also reduces the particle-to-polymer contact, which hinders hopping between the chains and lowers conductivity. The picture illustrates how conductivity for each sample varies with frequency. The production of surplus charge carriers in the polymer composites is suggested by the increase in polymer conductivity with frequency.

Dielectric properties

Figure 8a,b depicts the ε′ and ε′′ spectra of CS/PVP composite and filled samples at room temperature, where the values of ε′ and ε′ increase upon the addition of FVO to the polymer blend and drop nonlinearly with increasing frequency. For all CS/PVP-FVO PNCs, it has been found that the true dielectric permittivity falls with increasing frequency. Numerous applications benefit from CS/PVP-FVO PNCs’ strong actual dielectric permittivity at low frequencies. These materials will probably be utilized in even more applications in the future, including microwave filters, sensors, optical communications, and optical sensing, as research into them progresses. The limited capacity of the dipoles in the polymeric films to align with the applied electric field is the cause of the observed reduction in ε′ with increasing frequency. At higher frequencies, dipole alignment is hampered by the field’s fast alternation, which lowers ε′ values. As a result, there is less polarization and a lower dielectric constant, mostly because of the suppression of electrical polarization at high frequencies49. Additionally, the blend/1.2 weight% FVO sample’s ε′ and ε′′ values are comparatively high, suggesting that it can be used as a flexible-type dielectric material with a high dielectric permittivity value57. When compared to the pure CS/PVP mix film, the ε′ and ε′′ values of nanocomposites samples are found to dramatically rise at low frequencies (AF region). This finding shows that the dispersion of FVO nanofiller within the polymeric blend matrix considerably enhances the interfacial polarization (IP) effect52,53. The majority of composite materials with different conductivity ingredients exhibit this effect. The comparatively high ε′ value of FVO in comparison to the polymer mix can be attributed to the enhancement of ε′ values with the inclusion of nanofiller inside the CS/PVP blend47. The presence of electrostatic interactions between the functional groups of the CS/PVP polymer and the nanofiller structures that enhance the dipolar ordering is reflected in the improvement for the ε′ and ε′′ values.

Electric modulus spectra

Figure 10 shows the spectra of the real M′ and imaginary M′′ components of the electric modulus M*(ω) for the filled samples and pure CS/PVP blend at RT. These samples’ M′ values initially rise nonlinearly as frequency increases. Additionally, the nanocomposite samples’ M′ values (Fig. 10b) are found to be lower than the pristine blends. There are two relaxation peaks in the intermediate and RF sections of the M′′ spectra. In comparison to CS/PVP sample, the first peak may be explained by the cooperative dynamics of the polymeric chain and moves to higher frequencies because the polymer-nanofiller interactions lessen the obstruction to the segmental dynamics of chains within the nanocomposite samples46. The second peak, which vanishes in the nanocomposite spectra, might represent the reorientation of the functional polar groups connected to the side chains in the high frequency area. The modulus relaxation time (τM) values for these samples were computed using the formula τM = 1/(2πfp(M)), where fp(M) is the frequency value that corresponds to the M′′ peak. When nanofiller is added to increase the cooperative polymer chain dynamics of nanocomposite samples, the τM values decrease. The modulus peak’s intensity decreased as the concentration of nanofiller increased, according to the imaginary portion of the modulus spectra (Fig. 10b). The intensity of the CS/PVP-1.5wt.%FVO PNCs was lower than that of the pure CS/PVP. According to the formula M*= 1/ε*=M′ + jM″, this was caused by the high dielectric permittivity of the aforementioned films60.

Cole-Cole plot

Figure 11’s Cole-Cole plot of the electric modulus (M″ vs. M′) of the virgin CS/PVP blend and filled samples at room temperature displays curves that illustrate how the material relaxes as a result of charges and dipoles moving inside the polymeric matrix. Because of its strong resistance to dipolar alignment, a semicircular arc is visible in each of these spectral plots, suggesting the presence of considerable relaxation processes61. It is evident that two semicircles with centers that do not finish on the real part of the M′ axis describe the patterns for every film. This behavior implies that non-Debye model relaxation mechanisms exist in the materials under investigation62. The CS/PVP-1.2 weight% FVO’s semicircle radius is clearly smaller than that of the CS/PVP blend and the other films, indicating that the ionic conductivity has been improved. Additionally, by making the polymer chains less rigid in some places, which enhances the movement of their segments, the dielectric characteristics of the FVO NPs enable it to react to changes in the electric field more rapidly63. This finding suggests that, in comparison to the other samples, the CS/PVP-1.2 wt.%FVO sample has the largest capacitance storage capacity. As a result, these resultant nanocomposites have potential uses in optoelectronic devices for energy storage.

Conclusions

Using the solution casting process, the different ratios of FVO nanoparticles were effectively loaded into the PNCs made of CS and PVP to create new CS/PVP/FVO nanocomposite films. The doping of FVO nanofiller increased the blend nanocomposite’s degree of amorphousness, according to the XRD. All of the polymers’ reactive functional groups, which were more constrained at 1.2 weight% FVO loading, were verified by FTIR. The strong intermolecular interaction between the nanofiller and the CS/PVP is demonstrated by the shift and increase in intensity of the UV peaks of mix nanocomposites when compared to pure CS/PVP. For the 1.2 weight% nanocomposite, the blend’s straight bandgap energy (Eindg) decreased from 4.42 eV to 2.99 eV. Electrical conductivity is enhanced when FVO nanoparticles are added to a polymer matrix like CS/PVP. This characteristic, which allows for better charge transfer within the composite, is essential for flexible conductors and electrodes in many electrical devices. The dielectric measurements demonstrated that the impedance and dielectric characteristics significantly improved with an increase in the FVO content in the composite. The dielectric constant, energy density, and charge transport behavior of CS/PVP are all enhanced by the addition of FVO nanoparticles. The energy density of CS/PVP rises from 4.2 × 10− 7 J/m3 to1.35 × 10− 6 J/m3 as the FVO increases from 0.0 weight% to 1.2 weight%, respectively. The structural and chemical characteristics of PVP/CS films are altered when the FVO filler is added, opening the possibility of combining these flexible materials with more conventional materials to create solar cell devices. These findings open the possibility of the use of CS/PVP/FVO nanocomposites in energy storage devices, supercapacitors, and some optoelectronic applications such as photovoltaic solar cells and light-emitting diodes.
