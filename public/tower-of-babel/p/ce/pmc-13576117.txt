Can social media videos meet patients’ information needs for benign prostatic hyperplasia? A repeated cross-sectional study
Authors: Chengyang Tian, Weibo Wang, Junyue Tao, Guangyue Luo, Jun Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBenign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a common urological condition among older men and can cause lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS). As patients increasingly seek disease-related information through social media, evaluations of the quality, reliability, and transparency of patient-facing BPH content remain limited.MethodsRepeated cross-sectional searches were conducted on Bilibili and TikTok on December 1, 16, and 31, 2025. Basic video characteristics were extracted. Video reliability, quality, and transparency were assessed using the modified DISCERN (mDISCERN), Global Quality Score (GQS), and the Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) benchmark criteria, respectively. Exploratory supplementary assessments of content coverage and accessibility-related features were conducted using the content coverage checklist (CCC) and video accessibility checklist (VAC). Potentially misleading or harmful information signals were also descriptively assessed. Spearman correlation analysis and ordinal logistic regression were performed to examine associations between video characteristics and assessment scores.ResultsA total of 217 videos were included. Overall, the median (IQR) scores were 3.00 (2.00–3.00) for mDISCERN, 3.00 (2.00–3.00) for GQS, and 2.00 (2.00–2.00) for JAMA. In exploratory supplementary analyses, the median (IQR) scores were 3.00 (2.00–4.00) for CCC and 6.00 (5.00–6.00) for VAC. Potentially misleading or harmful information signals were observed in 29 videos (13.4%). Videos published by professional individuals and professional institutions had significantly higher mDISCERN, GQS, and JAMA scores than those published by non-professional individuals (all P < 0.001). Spearman correlation analysis and ordinal logistic regression indicated that engagement metrics were not independent predictors of assessment scores.ConclusionsOverall, BPH-related videos on social media showed moderate reliability, quality, and transparency, but consistently high-value content remained limited. A minority of videos also contained potentially misleading or harmful information signals. Videos from professional sources tended to provide higher-quality information, whereas user engagement was not a reliable indicator of content quality. These findings suggest that urologists and healthcare institutions may have an important role in providing more structured, understandable, and patient-oriented digital education for men seeking BPH information online.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12894-026-02234-x.Keywords: Benign prostatic hyperplasia, Social media videos, Patient education, Health information quality, Digital health communication

Introduction

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a common urological condition that predominantly affects middle-aged and older men, with prevalence increasing with age [1, 2]. It can lead to lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), including urgency, nocturia, and weak urinary stream [2, 3], which not only impair quality of life but also increase the healthcare burden [3, 4]. Taking China as an example, there were approximately 3.2 million incident cases and 23.1 million prevalent cases of BPH in 2021, with age-standardized incidence and prevalence rates of 229.1 and 2,315.2 per 100,000, respectively [5]. In addition, internet use among middle-aged and older adults in China is increasing, with the China Internet Network Information Center (CNNIC) reporting that the proportion of internet users aged over 50 years increased from 32.5% in December 2023 to 34.1% in December 2024, suggesting that more older adults may be exposed to health-related information online [6]. Consistent with this trend, recent research on Chinese older adults has suggested that digital technology represented by short videos is becoming an essential part of older adults’ lives [7]. These data suggest that middle-aged and older adults may increasingly encounter health-related information through short-video platforms. Meanwhile, treatment options for BPH are diverse, including lifestyle adjustments, pharmacotherapies, minimally invasive surgeries, and standard surgical interventions [1, 2, 8]. Given the high prevalence of BPH, the diversity of treatments, and the growing online exposure of older adults, evaluating the quality of BPH-related video content is of increasing practical importance.

With increasing awareness of healthcare and the rise of social media platforms, people are no longer confined to traditional medical information sources [9, 10]. Previous studies have shown that many patients use social media to obtain health-related information before seeking medical care, and exposure to such content may influence their health beliefs and decision-making [11–13]. In addition, healthcare professionals and institutions can also use social media to disseminate health-related information [10, 14]. However, due to the openness of the internet, misleading, incomplete, or inaccurate information remains relatively common on social media, which may lead to potential health risks [15, 16]. This concern is particularly relevant to BPH, as it primarily affects middle-aged and older men, who may face greater challenges in accessing, evaluating, and identifying misleading health information online [17–19].

Although previous studies have assessed BPH-related videos on English-language platforms, evidence from large non-English-language video environments remains limited [20]. As a case example of such an environment, we examined Bilibili and TikTok, two widely used social media platforms in China. Accordingly, this study aimed to assess the reliability, quality, and transparency of patient-facing BPH videos on two major Chinese social media platforms, and to examine their informational value and potential implications for urologists and healthcare institutions involved in patient education.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was not required for this study in accordance with the local legislation and institutional requirements, because all data were obtained from publicly accessible videos on Bilibili and TikTok and no human participants, human tissue samples, or laboratory animals were involved.

Search strategy and data extraction

Bilibili and TikTok were selected to represent two distinct Chinese video-sharing ecosystems, with Bilibili generally featuring longer-form and community-based videos and TikTok primarily focusing on short-form video content.

On December 1, 2025, December 16, 2025, and December 31, 2025, repeated cross-sectional searches were conducted on Bilibili and TikTok using the keyword “前列腺增生”, which translates to “benign prostatic hyperplasia”. This keyword was selected because it is one of the most commonly used Chinese terms for this condition in both public and medical contexts and allowed a standardized, reproducible, and comparable search strategy across platforms and sampling rounds. To further explore the representativeness of this single disease-term search strategy, an additional descriptive supplementary search was conducted using “前列腺增生” (“benign prostatic hyperplasia”), “前列腺肥大” (“prostate enlargement”), “前列腺问题” (“prostate problems”), “尿频” (“urinary frequency”), “尿不尽” (“incomplete emptying”), and “排尿困难” (“difficulty urinating”).

To reduce potential bias introduced by personalized recommendation algorithms, each search was conducted using newly registered accounts on both platforms. Consequently, a total of eight independent accounts were used. For each platform, three accounts were used for the three main sampling rounds, and one additional account was used for the supplementary keyword search. Neither TikTok nor Bilibili required users to provide or modify personal profile information during account registration. Therefore, all accounts retained the default platform settings and profile configurations. In addition, before the searches were performed, none of the accounts had any prior browsing history, search history, likes, comments, collections, shares, or subscriptions to other accounts. Videos were obtained in each platform’s default order without applying any additional filters, and the same retrieval conditions were maintained across all three sampling rounds. At each sampling round, the top 50 videos were retrieved from each platform. After three sampling rounds, 150 videos were collected from each platform, resulting in 300 videos overall. For the supplementary keyword search, the top 20 videos for each keyword were retrieved from each platform, resulting in 240 additional videos. These videos were descriptively categorized as clearly related, partially related, or unrelated to BPH and were not included in the main scoring analyses. The sample size of 150 videos per platform was selected with reference to prior cross-sectional studies using similar methods, rather than a formal statistical power calculation [21, 22]. Previous studies have also shown that users often focus on the top search results rather than browse the full list [23, 24].

All retrieved videos were independently screened by two reviewers according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria on each search date. Advertising videos, duplicate or similar videos, and irrelevant videos were excluded according to predefined criteria. Detailed criteria for identifying these videos are provided in the Supplementary Methods 1. Basic information on the included videos was extracted, including platform, engagement metrics (likes, collections, comments, and shares), duration, publication date, and days since publication (calculated as the time interval from the publication date to the search date).

Video classification

Video sources were classified into four categories: (1) Professional individuals, (2) Non-professional individuals, (3) Professional institutions, (4) Non-professional institutions.

Professional individuals were further divided into: (1) BPH-related specialists, (2) Non-BPH-related specialists, (3) Other healthcare practitioners, (4) Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) practitioners.

Detailed definitions of video sources and professional individual subcategories are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Video source classification was performed independently by two reviewers. In cases of disagreement, a third reviewer was involved to resolve discrepancies through discussion until consensus was reached. The identities of professional individuals and institutions were determined based on platform verification information and platform-specific verification badges. Additional details on how professional verification information and platform-specific verification badges were used to determine video source categories on Bilibili and TikTok are provided in the Supplementary Methods 1.

Main assessment tools

The included videos were evaluated using the modified DISCERN (mDISCERN), the Global Quality Score (GQS), and the Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) benchmark criteria.

The modified DISCERN, a simplified version adapted from the original DISCERN instrument, was used to assess the reliability of online health information [25]. The mDISCERN was selected because it has been widely used in previous evaluations of online health information. Compared with the original DISCERN instrument, mDISCERN provides a more concise and practical framework while retaining core domains related to reliability, making it suitable for evaluating a large number of short-form videos. It consists of five questions, with each scored as 1 point for a “yes” and 0 points for a “no”. Higher scores indicate better reliability. Detailed items of the mDISCERN are shown in Supplementary Table 2. With reference to previous video-based health information studies, total mDISCERN scores were descriptively categorized as poor (0–2), moderate (3), and good (4–5) reliability [26, 27].

The Global Quality Score (GQS), first described by Bernard et al., was used to assess the overall quality of the videos [28]. It uses a 5-point scale, with higher scores indicating better quality, ranging from poor (1) to excellent (5). Detailed items of the GQS are shown in Supplementary Table 3.

The JAMA benchmark criteria, originally proposed by Silberg et al., were used to assess transparency-related features, including authorship, attribution, disclosure, and currency [29]. Each of the four domains was scored as 0 or 1, with a total score ranging from 0 to 4. Detailed items of the JAMA benchmark criteria are shown in Supplementary Table 4. With reference to previous video-based health information studies, total JAMA scores were descriptively categorized as low (0–1), moderate (2), and high (3–4) transparency to aid interpretation [30, 31].

A summary table of the scoring systems for mDISCERN, GQS, and JAMA scores is provided in Supplementary Table 5. Three reviewers with a urology background and more than three years of clinical experience participated in the video assessment. Before formal scoring, all reviewers jointly reviewed the scoring criteria to standardize the application of the assessment tools. Two reviewers independently scored all included videos, and in cases of disagreement, a third reviewer was involved to resolve discrepancies through discussion until consensus was reached.

Exploratory supplementary descriptive measures

To provide an exploratory descriptive overview of content coverage and accessibility-related features in BPH-related videos, we additionally used the content coverage checklist (CCC) and the video accessibility checklist (VAC) with reference to previous studies and relevant guidance documents. These two measures were intended only to describe commonly observed content domains and accessibility-related items, rather than to provide a comprehensive or validated assessment of content completeness or accessibility.

The content coverage checklist (CCC) was developed with reference to content coverage frameworks previously used in video-based health information studies across multiple diseases [32, 33]. We adapted these frameworks to the context of BPH-related videos and selected seven key domains to assess: basic concept, etiology, symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, prevention, and prognosis. One point was assigned for each domain mentioned, with the total score ranging from 0 to 7. A higher score indicates broader coverage of relevant content domains. Detailed items of the CCC are shown in Supplementary Table 6.

The video accessibility checklist (VAC) was developed with reference to the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2, the A + E Closed Captioning Style Guide, and Federal Communications Commission (FCC) recommendations [34–36]. To provide a brief descriptive overview rather than a comprehensive assessment of accessibility, we selected several commonly used accessibility-related items for assessment. It consisted of two major domains, hearing-friendly features and vision-friendly features, comprising six individual items: audio clarity, caption synchronization, caption coverage, text readability, text contrast, and obstruction. Detailed items of the VAC are shown in Supplementary Table 7. For descriptive purposes, one point was assigned for each item present, with the total score ranging from 0 to 6. A higher VAC score may reflect broader coverage of selected accessibility-related features.

We also conducted an exploratory descriptive assessment of potentially misleading or harmful information signals. Five items were evaluated for each video: confusion of BPH with other diseases, such as prostatitis or prostate cancer; exaggerated treatment claims; recommendation of non-standard or unproven treatments; advice that may delay appropriate medical evaluation or standard treatment; and misleading, fear-inducing, or absolute statements. Each item was recorded as present or absent. A video was considered to contain a potentially misleading or harmful information signal if any of the five items was present. This supplementary assessment was descriptive and was not intended to provide a validated measure of clinical accuracy or patient impact.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using R (version 4.4.2). Categorical variables were summarized as n (%) and compared using Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR) due to non-normal distributions. After exclusion of duplicate or similar videos, each included video was treated as an independent unit of analysis. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test, and comparisons among three or more groups were first assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. These tests were used to compare overall distributions between groups. When significant, Dunn’s multiple comparisons test was used for pairwise comparisons. P values from multiple pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Inter-rater agreement for total scores was assessed using Cohen’s kappa. According to the Landis and Koch criteria, kappa values were interpreted as slight (0.00-0.20), fair (0.21–0.40), moderate (0.41–0.60), substantial (0.61–0.80), and almost perfect (0.81-1.00) agreement [37]. Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate associations between video characteristics and assessment scores. According to the absolute value of Spearman’s r, correlations were interpreted as very weak (0.00-0.19), weak (0.20–0.39), moderate (0.40–0.59), strong (0.60–0.79), and very strong (0.80-1.00).

To further explore associations between video characteristics and score distributions across the assessed domains, ordinal logistic regression was performed with mDISCERN, GQS, and JAMA scores as ordinal outcomes [38]. To improve interpretability, duration was expressed in minutes and days since publication was converted to months since publication (assuming 30 days per month). Covariates included platform, source group, duration, months since publication, and log-transformed engagement metrics (log1p-transformed likes, collections, comments, and shares). Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated to assess multicollinearity among covariates. Given the observed multicollinearity among engagement metrics, sensitivity analyses were conducted by fitting models that included only one engagement metric at a time (likes, collections, comments, or shares), while retaining platform, source group, duration, and months since publication in the model. In addition, a robustness analysis was performed for the JAMA model after excluding videos with extreme duration values. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Exploratory supplementary descriptive findings

As exploratory supplementary measures, CCC and VAC were also applied to the included videos. Inter-rater agreement for total scores was high for both measures, with Cohen’s kappa values of 0.839 (95% CI, 0.783–0.895) for CCC and 0.852 (95% CI, 0.793–0.911) for VAC.

The overall median (IQR) CCC and VAC scores were 3.00 (2.00–4.00) and 6.00 (5.00–6.00), respectively, indicating moderate content coverage and relatively broad accessibility-related features. When stratified by platform, the median (IQR) CCC was 3.00 (2.00–4.00) on both platforms (P = 0.612) and the median (IQR) VAC score was 6.00 (4.00–6.00) on Bilibili and 6.00 (5.00–6.00) on TikTok (P = 0.071), with no significant between-platform differences in score distributions. In subgroup analyses, similar source-related differences were observed. Videos from professional sources showed higher CCC and VAC scores than those from non-professional individuals. These detailed results are shown in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2. In addition, the pooled sensitivity analysis also indicated higher assessment scores for professional sources (all P < 0.001).

Regarding video content coverage, the three most frequently mentioned domains were basic concept, symptoms, and treatment on both platforms. Specifically, the proportions of videos mentioning these domains were 75/104 (72.1%), 70/104 (67.3%), and 61/104 (58.7%) on Bilibili, compared with 80/113 (70.8%), 71/113 (62.8%), and 66/113 (58.4%) on TikTok, respectively. Overall, diagnosis was the least frequently covered domain. Regarding accessibility-related feature coverage, the three least frequently observed features on Bilibili were audio clarity, caption coverage, and text contrast. Specifically, the proportions of videos showing these features were 70/104 (67.3%), 77/104 (74.0%), and 81/104 (77.9%), respectively. On TikTok, the three least frequently observed features were audio clarity, caption synchronization, and caption coverage, with corresponding proportions of 88/113 (78.6%), 95/113 (84.8%), and 101/113 (89.4%), respectively. Detailed item-level results are shown in Supplementary Fig. 3.

In the exploratory descriptive assessment of potentially misleading or harmful information signals, 29 of 217 videos (13.4%) contained at least one such signal. The most frequent signals were exaggerated treatment claims (16/217, 7.4%) and misleading, fear-inducing, or absolute statements (14/217, 6.5%). Recommendations of non-standard or unproven treatments were observed in 8 videos (3.7%), and advice that may delay appropriate medical evaluation or standard treatment was observed in 3 videos (1.4%). No video confused BPH with other diseases such as prostatitis or prostate cancer. When stratified by source category, any potentially misleading or harmful information signal was identified in 19 of 147 videos (12.9%) from professional individuals, 9 of 44 videos (20.5%) from non-professional individuals, 1 of 18 videos (5.6%) from professional institutions, and 0 of 8 videos (0.0%) from non-professional institutions. Among professional individuals, the most common signal was exaggerated treatment claims (11/147, 7.5%), whereas among non-professional individuals, the most common signal was misleading, fear-inducing, or absolute statements (7/44, 15.9%). Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Table 9.

Spearman correlation analysis and ordinal logistic regression

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to examine associations among video characteristics and assessment scores. As shown in Fig. 5, strong positive correlations were observed among video engagement metrics, including likes, collections, comments, and shares. Moderate to strong positive correlations were also observed among the three main assessment scores. However, no meaningful correlations were identified between engagement metrics and the assessment scores.

Discussion

Main findings

With increasing public health awareness and the rapid growth of social media, more people are turning to video-based online content for health-related information [9, 10]. However, misleading, incomplete, or inaccurate information remains widespread and may lead to potential health risks [15, 16]. This concern is particularly relevant to BPH-related content because BPH primarily affects older men, who may be more vulnerable to problematic online health information [39]. To address the limited evidence on patient-facing BPH information in non-English-language video environments, we conducted a repeated cross-sectional assessment of BPH-related videos on two major Chinese social media platforms. Overall, the included videos demonstrated moderate reliability, quality, and transparency according to mDISCERN, GQS, and JAMA. In addition, we exploratorily assessed content coverage and accessibility-related features using the content coverage checklist (CCC) and video accessibility checklist (VAC). The median CCC and VAC scores were 3.00 (2.00–4.00) and 6.00 (5.00–6.00), respectively, indicating moderate content coverage and relatively broad accessibility-related features. We also found that potentially misleading or harmful information signals were present in a minority of videos, most commonly exaggerated treatment claims and misleading, fear-inducing, or absolute statements. Notably, videos from professional sources showed higher assessment scores than those from non-professional sources, and this pattern remained consistent in source-adjusted regression and pooled sensitivity analyses.

Informational value and patient implications

Our findings suggest that BPH-related videos generally demonstrate moderate reliability, quality, and transparency. This may indicate that severely poor informational quality may not predominate in BPH-related video content and that patients are often able to access videos of at least an average informational standard when searching online. However, obtaining videos with consistently high informational value may still be challenging for patients. Importantly, these scores should be interpreted as indirect indicators of informational value rather than direct measures of clinical accuracy or patients’ actual information needs. BPH patients may require not only reliable and transparent information, but also practical explanations about symptom interpretation, treatment selection, and other practical concerns. Therefore, even videos with relatively high assessment scores may not fully address the specific concerns that patients face during real-world decision-making.

Our exploratory supplementary descriptive findings suggested moderate content coverage and relatively broad accessibility-related features. In addition, potentially misleading or harmful information signals were identified in a minority of videos. The moderate level of content coverage further indicates that obtaining relatively comprehensive disease-related information from social media may still be challenging for patients. Diagnosis was among the least frequently covered domains, suggesting that some videos may provide symptom or treatment information without adequately explaining how BPH should be clinically evaluated. This may limit patients’ ability to judge when further medical evaluation is needed. Although potentially misleading or harmful information signals were identified only in a minority of videos, their presence deserves attention. The most common signal was exaggerated treatment claims, which were mainly observed in videos discussing newer therapeutic approaches. Some videos tended to emphasize potential benefits while giving limited attention to possible adverse effects, treatment limitations, or the need for careful individualized decision-making. In addition, misleading, fear-inducing, or absolute statements may increase patients’ anxiety about their symptoms. Therefore, patient-facing BPH videos should avoid overstating treatment effects and provide balanced explanations of treatment benefits and possible adverse effects. Meanwhile, the relatively broad accessibility-related features suggest that many videos incorporated presentation features that may facilitate understanding. This may be particularly relevant for BPH, because patients with BPH are often older and may have lower eHealth literacy [17, 39]. For older viewers, features such as subtitles, clear cover text, clear audio, and plain-language explanations may reduce cognitive burden and make health information easier to follow. The increasing attention to short-video platform use among older adults further highlights the importance of accessible presentation [7]. In this context, well-designed accessibility-related features may be especially important for older men seeking BPH-related information through short videos.

For urologists, these findings may have practical implications for routine clinical care. When patients obtain fragmented, incomplete, or even potentially misleading information from social media, such information may influence their treatment expectations, trust in physicians, and even the quality of physician-patient communication. Clinicians may therefore need to play a more active role in clarifying misconceptions and directing patients toward reliable, balanced, and accessible educational resources.

Engagement-informational value mismatch

Our findings suggest a mismatch between conventional engagement metrics and informational value. Spearman correlation analysis and ordinal logistic regression consistently indicated that engagement metrics were not independent predictors of these scores. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that commonly used engagement metrics should not be interpreted as markers of content quality [21, 22]. This mismatch may have important practical implications. Because highly shareable content can spread farther and faster on social media, patients searching for disease-related information may be more likely to encounter content that is popular rather than content that is reliable or helpful [21, 22, 40]. The wide IQRs observed for engagement metrics also suggest substantial heterogeneity in video dissemination. This heterogeneity may be partly explained by differences in follower base, time since publication, and platform recommendation mechanisms. In general, videos posted by creators with larger follower bases may receive greater exposure and therefore higher engagement metrics, regardless of their informational value.

For BPH-related content, this mismatch may be particularly important. First, because patients with BPH are often older, they may face greater challenges in accessing, evaluating, and interpreting online health information than younger individuals [17, 39]. Second, as BPH is a chronic condition that often requires ongoing symptom management and repeated treatment-related decision-making, patients may be more likely to seek information from the internet over time. Therefore, the mismatch between popularity and informational value may contribute to misunderstanding, unrealistic expectations, and even poor health decisions.

Clinical relevance for urologists and healthcare institutions

Our subgroup analyses showed that videos from professional sources tended to have higher assessment scores than those from non-professional sources. This source-related pattern was further supported by multivariable ordinal logistic regression models, suggesting that professional sources remained associated with higher assessment scores after accounting for platform and basic video characteristics. From a practical standpoint, this finding suggests that urologists and healthcare institutions are well positioned to improve the online BPH information environment. First, clinicians may consider participating more actively in patient-facing digital education, particularly by producing short videos that explain basic disease knowledge, indications for seeking medical evaluation, treatment options, treatment limitations, and common misconceptions in plain language. Such videos should avoid overstated treatment claims and present information in an objective, accurate, and balanced manner. Second, healthcare institutions may contribute to standardizing online health communication by developing concise evidence-based content templates, review procedures, or patient education video series for common urological conditions such as BPH. Third, because professional expertise alone does not guarantee effective health communication, training in short-form digital communication may help clinicians present accurate information in a format that is understandable, engaging, accessible, and clinically useful for patients [11, 12]. Fourth, the relatively lower scores observed among TCM practitioners should be interpreted cautiously. This pattern may partly reflect a mismatch between the explanatory framework and presentation logic of some TCM-related videos and the criteria emphasized by these tools, rather than indicating that TCM itself is inherently problematic. Nevertheless, TCM-related BPH communication should also translate TCM-specific professional concepts into plain language and provide objective, accurate, and balanced information. Greater caution is needed when discussing traditional remedies or approaches with limited rigorous clinical validation. Together, these measures may help narrow the gap between content popularity and informational value in the digital environment.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to evaluate the reliability, quality, and transparency of BPH-related content on major Chinese social media platforms. We selected two representative Chinese social media platforms, Bilibili and TikTok, for analysis. We applied mDISCERN, GQS, and JAMA to assess informational value from complementary perspectives. We performed Spearman correlation analysis and source-adjusted ordinal logistic regression to explore the relationships between video characteristics and assessment scores. In addition, we conducted multicollinearity diagnostics and sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the regression findings. We also conducted exploratory descriptive analyses of content coverage, accessibility-related features, and potentially misleading or harmful information signals, providing a broader overview of the characteristics and potential concerns of BPH-related videos on social media. High inter-rater agreement across the main and exploratory assessment tools further supported the consistency of the evaluation process. Based on the key problems identified in this study, we proposed practical directions to improve patient-facing BPH information quality.

This study also has several limitations. First, although we performed repeated sampling at three different time points, content on Bilibili and TikTok is continuously updated. Therefore, our findings reflect only a repeated cross-sectional snapshot and should not be interpreted as definitive platform-wide conclusions. In addition, only two platforms were assessed, and the findings may therefore not fully represent the broader Chinese online health information environment. Second, only a limited number of top-ranked videos were included and a single search term was used, both of which may have influenced the results. Although the supplementary keyword search suggested that the standard disease term retrieved the highest proportion of clearly related videos, relying primarily on this term may still have missed some relevant videos retrieved through lay disease-related expressions. Meanwhile, the sample size was determined based on prior studies and the practical feasibility of detailed manual assessment rather than a formal statistical power calculation. Third, although the main assessment tools used in this study have been applied in many similar studies, they remain subjective and have not been specifically validated for Chinese content. At the same time, these scores should be interpreted as indirect indicators of informational value rather than direct measures of clinical accuracy, patient comprehension, behavioral impact, or patients’ actual information needs. Similarly, the supplementary measures used in this study were not formally validated and should be regarded as descriptive rather than conclusive. Fourth, video source classification relied on platform verification information. Therefore, independent verification of all professional credentials was not possible, and some degree of source misclassification cannot be excluded. Fifth, this study focused only on Chinese-language BPH-related videos, and future research could extend these findings through cross-language comparisons between Chinese and international content. Finally, the sample sizes of some source subgroups were relatively small. This may reduce the precision and reliability of subgroup comparisons involving these categories. Although we performed a pooled sensitivity analysis comparing professional and non-professional sources, these subgroup findings should still be interpreted cautiously. This limitation may be addressed in future studies by expanding the sample size.

Conclusions

Overall, BPH-related videos on social media demonstrated moderate reliability, quality, and transparency, with supplementary findings suggesting moderate content coverage and generally accessible presentation features. Videos with consistently high informational value remained limited, and a small proportion contained potentially misleading or harmful information signals. Notably, videos from professional sources tended to provide higher-quality information than those from non-professional sources, whereas user engagement was not a reliable indicator of informational value, highlighting a mismatch between popularity and quality. For urology practice, these findings highlight the need for more active, structured, and patient-oriented digital education on BPH. Greater involvement of urologists and healthcare institutions may help improve the quality of online information available to patients with BPH.
