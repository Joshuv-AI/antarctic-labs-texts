In-dwelling microfluidic device for precise and reliable intranasal drug delivery during freely moving behavior
Authors: Maria F Ramirez, Abhishek Gour, Sarah E Sniffen, Emma K Watson, Abhisheak Sharma, Daniel W Wesson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMany substances and drugs are administered intranasally. These include opioid overdose reversal drugs, anti-epileptic medications, migraine medications, hormone treatments and medicines to treat/prevent allergies, colds and flus, including nasally administered vaccines, corticosteroids, antihistamines and decongestants. In addition, intranasal (IN) administration is the preferred route of entry by users of illicit drugs. Despite the widespread use of the IN route of administration, there is no established paradigm to access this route of administration preclinically to yield precise and reliable control over delivery. This poses major gaps in therapeutic discovery/testing, establishing pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic relationships of therapeutics and understanding the mechanisms of actions of therapeutics. Here we developed an in-dwelling microfluidic device that, when implanted upon the nasal bone in mice, accesses the nasal cavity to allow reliable and precise IN fluid delivery during freely moving behavior. We validated this device, called the nasal access port (NAP), to confirm it allows rapid and precise control of fluids. We further exemplified the application of the NAP for studying outcomes of IN cocaine in mice, including its pharmacokinetic profile, and both the rapid release of dopamine and behavioral effects upon IN cocaine. By achieving precise and reliable access to the IN route of administration, the NAP represents a substantial methodological advance with broad applicability in the biomedical and life sciences, especially in the neuroscience, pharmacology, medicinal chemistry and physiology domains.Subject terms: Animal disease models, Animal biotechnology

Main

Many substances and drugs are administered into the nose. These include opioids, anti-epileptic medications, migraine medications, hormone treatments and medicines to treat/prevent allergies, colds and flus, including nasally administered vaccines, corticosteroids, antihistamines and decongestants1–6. In addition, the nasal route of administration is the preferred route of entry by users of illicit drugs. Indeed, many drugs of abuse are inhaled by users to achieve a more immediate or intense high, including cocaine, amphetamine, prescription stimulants (for example, adderall), heroin, prescription opioids, benzodiazepines, ketamine and ecstasy7. Nasal inhalation of these drugs (viz., ‘snorting’, ‘taking a bump’, ‘sniffing’ or ‘blowing’) holds substantial public health and treatment implications8, in addition to destruction and necrosis of oral, nasal and facial structures9–12.

Intranasal (IN) drug administration confers multiple advantages. For one, IN delivery is ideal for drugs intended to act locally in the nasal passages, like for treatment of nasal congestion, infections and allergies. Second, IN delivery allows drugs to have a rapid time course of action due to the nasal cavity being highly vascularized and permeable13. Relatedly, drugs delivered IN have privileged access to the brain because they can bypass the blood–brain barrier—resulting in IN drug delivery being conceptually ideal for therapeutics intended to enter the brain. Indeed, inhalation of drugs into the nose exposes them to distinctive entry points whereby they can reach the central nervous system, including a specialized enzymatic milieu that will impact drug metabolism and unique plasma exposure. Drugs delivered intranasally also bypass hepatic metabolism, further enhancing the rapid time course of action and bioavailability13. Altogether, these factors influence the total amount of drug that reaches the brain in addition to the kinetics of how it does so. IN delivery is less invasive and is associated with fewer systemic side effects when administering therapeutics intended to target mechanisms in the nasal passages or brain14. Finally, from a research and development as well as commercialization standpoint, IN delivery allows therapeutic targets and goals to be reached with less waste of valuable drugs that would otherwise be metabolized or bind to unintended bodily compartments.

Well-developed and regulated15 infusion devices, designed to dispense precise and reliable doses IN, are available for testing of candidate drugs and therapies in humans. No devices are available, however, for precise and reliable IN substance administration in preclinical animal models, posing a substantial barrier for early-stage therapeutic design, validation and testing. Current preclinical methods for IN drug administration in animal models have done so by delivering the fluids using pipette tips directly onto the nares while the animal is restrained, sometimes in a supine position, and manually distributing the doses across both nostrils (for example, refs. 16,17). Other methods have resorted to attaching polyurethane (PU) tubing to a syringe and delivering the fluids into the nose while the animal is anesthetized18,19. While these methods have highlighted the importance and viability of the IN route to deliver drugs, they do not achieve precision and reliability of drug delivery. Furthermore, sedation and differences in animal handling during restraint can introduce variability in the accuracy of drug delivery, not to mention that this procedure can lead to expulsion of some of the fluid due to pressure from the pipette or syringe. In addition, these methods have limitations that require the experimenter to deliver higher volumes, which can lead to fluid being inhaled into the lungs, thereby altering the kinetics of the nasal route. Drugs can also be vaporized or nebulized and passed into gas chambers for administration, yet here again accuracy of dosage is difficult as is it impossible to rule out that outcomes of drugs are not due to absorption through the skin, eyes or even mouth20–23.

The lack of a reliable and accurate IN drug delivery paradigm presents a major void that has debilitated the drug development industry to design and test drugs that could be used to treat multitudes of medical conditions—in both human patients and companion animal patients. This void also yields uncertainty in the mechanisms of actions of drugs when delivered intranasally. To overcome these gaps, a paradigm is needed that achieves reliable and precise IN delivery of drugs in preclinical models.

We developed a simple-to-use surgical device, called the nasal access port (NAP), that, when implanted upon the nasal bone of mice, accesses the nasal cavity to allow reliable and precise IN drug delivery during freely moving behavior. We validated the NAP through several methods and further utilized cocaine to exemplify the application of the NAP for studying outcomes of IN drug delivery, including its rapid and precise delivery, its pharmacokinetic profile and both the release of dopamine (DA) and robust behavioral effects upon IN cocaine delivery. This includes evidence that some animals will self-administer IN cocaine. The NAP represents a substantial methodological advance with broad applicability in biomedical and life sciences research, especially in the neuroscience, pharmacology, medicinal chemistry and physiology domains and one we predict will be a cornerstone approach for investigating IN drug delivery and its effects on genomic, molecular, cellular, systems and behavioral consequences—all of which remain unexplored so far.

Results

Design and fluid dynamics of the NAP

We were inspired by prior research24,25 that utilized in-dwelling cannulae to access air pressure within the dorsal nasal recess of rodents, and worked to adapt this approach to achieve reliable IN fluid delivery. We had several criteria to reach this goal. First, the device must be able to extend into the nasal passage in a fluid-tight manner. Second, the device must be small enough to fit onto the nasal bone. Third, the device must not obstruct respiration or block the front of the nostrils, thereby allowing animals implanted with the device to freely breathe and explore their environments, which rodents do prominently by protruding their snouts. Fourth, the device must have a mechanism to allow it to temporarily attach to a mating connector for fluid delivery, which can be connected and removed with little force to not risk dislodging the device from the animal. Finally, the device must have a small footprint on the cranium to allow concurrent cranial implantation of the animals with other devices for measures and/or manipulations of physiology simultaneous with IN fluid delivery (for example, in-dwelling fiber optic implants for light transmittance/collection (fiber photometry and optogenetics), in-dwelling drug delivery cannulae, head-fixation devices, optical windows and so on).

Given the importance of the size factor in most of the above criteria, we worked to consider how to accomplish IN fluid delivery in the smallest of all mainstream preclinical animal models—the house mouse (Mus musculus). Like other mammals, the nasal bone covers the nasal passages, which in their more posterior regions house the nasal turbinates; these vascularly rich structures serve as the primary substrate for substances to enter the circulatory system and therefore represent targets of interest during IN administration13. The entire surface area of both dorsal nasal bones in an adult C57BL/6J mouse occupies only approximately 2.8 mm × 6.5 mm (medial–lateral × anterior–posterior)26. Underlying the nasal bones are the lateralized nasal passages separated by the nasal septum. Furthermore, a single nasal bone represents a restricted amount of cranium (approximately 1.35 mm × 6.5 mm (medial–lateral × anterior–posterior)).

With the above criteria in mind, we designed the NAP. The NAP is a small-footprint in-dwelling fluidic device, the body of which is constructed predominately out of the polybutylene terephthalate thermoplastic Hydex with a 25-gauge stainless-steel tube that extends throughout the NAP to achieve a channel for fluid delivery. The NAP design has four main components: (1) a connection port for fluid-tight securing of a female mating connector for subsequent fluid delivery, (2) a small magnet to achieve secure temporary fastening to the female mating connector for fluid delivery and/or a dust cap when not in use, (3) a stainless-steel tube that spans the dorsal–ventral extent of the entire device, and extending beyond the base of the device, whereby the fluids can travel for unilateral infusion into a single nasal cavity, and (4) an exaggerated plastic perimeter that permits for securing the NAP to the cranium. Figure 1a displays an image of the NAP and accompanying schematic. The largest dimensions of the NAP body span 9.9 mm (anterior–posterior), approximately a third of which is devoted to the exaggerated plastic perimeter for securing to the cranium, and 10 mm (dorsal–ventral), most of which is encumbered by the connection port for fluid delivery. Importantly, overall skull morphology is conserved across animals, with minimal variation in distance between cranial landmarks and skull thickness across ages and sexes26.

The selection of the 25-gauge stainless-steel tube, with an outer diameter of 0.5 mm, was based on its inner diameter (0.029 mm), which allows IN delivery of small fluid boluses with minimal resistance, enabling reliable flow. Importantly, the 0.5-mm outer diameter fits within the medial–lateral span of a single dorsal nasal bone of the mouse (2.8 mm). To record the rapid fluid dynamics achieved by the NAP, we connected a NAP to female mating connector that was attached to a computer-controlled syringe pump via flexible PU tubing (0.43 mm inner diameter × 0.94 mm outer diameter, 25 gauge). The female mating connector and flexible infusion line were filled with the fluorescent dye, fluorescein. By positioning the NAP under a wide-field epifluorescence microscope and exciting with 455-nm light, we were able to image fluid dynamics as fluid was expunged from the NAP with temporal precision by a syringe pump (Fig. 1b). We found that fluorescence was detected from the NAP within 58 ms (±0.003 ms) of the pump being triggered (Fig. 1c; mean of 10 replicates, 0.045–0.078 ms across replicate means). Through identifying the rise (10%) and near-peak intensities in infusion dynamics (90%), we found that fluid boluses are released from the NAP in <250 ms (Fig. 1c; mean of 10 replicates 0.12–0.40 ms across replicate means, 0.29 ± 0.019 ms). As in the example in Fig. 1b (inset), given the pump rate and tubing used, each bolus exited the NAP as a spherical droplet rather than an aerosolized spray. Importantly, for this purpose we chose the same syringe, pump and approximate length of infusion line as would be appropriate for use in subsequent in vivo experiments, therefore supporting that the NAP would yield rapid IN fluid delivery in vivo.

The steps for implanting the NAP for delivery of fluids into the nasal cavity are simple, following standard cranial implantation methods routinely used in physiological and behavioral neuroscience laboratories. As indicated in Fig. 2a, the first step is to identify the mesial aspect of one of the dorsal nasal bones, particularly along the dorsal–ventral axis, superior to the highly vascularized nasal turbinates. In commonly used C57BL/6J mice, this is approximately 1 mm anterior of the frontal–nasal fissure. Following aseptic surgical conditions, the nasal bone should be leveled by adjusting the stereotaxic incisor bar. Next, a 1-mm-diameter hole should be drilled through one of the nasal bones, 1 mm anterior to the frontal–nasal fissure and 0.5 mm lateral. The stainless-steel tube of the NAP should be lowered to extend through the craniotomy, and terminate immediately upon the nasal turbinates, as illustrated by micro-computed tomography (µ-CT) scans that we performed in a NAP-implanted mouse (Fig. 2b). Finally, the NAP should be secured with an orthopedic-compatible adhesive (for example, traditional polymer-based dental cements, ultraviolet (UV)-curable adhesives and so on) to the proximal nasal bone and remainder of the skull. Following recovery, and even upon connection of the mouse to the infusion tether, the NAP rests upon the nasal bone in a manner that does not obstruct the nostrils nor the eyes (Fig. 2c).

As confirmation of implantation site, following anesthetic overdose, we infused a subset of mice with 1 µL of the dye methylene blue through the NAP, dissected the nasal cavity along the midline, and confirmed that methylene blue was visible on the ipsilateral turbinates following IN delivery (Fig. 2d). Qualitatively more area of the turbinates was covered with methylene blue when larger boluses (2 or 4 µL) of the dye were injected. Finally, to support utility of this approach to achieve reliable IN infusion, we investigated the possibility that IN infusion of fluids would entail escape of fluids from the nose. As above (Fig. 2d), a subset of mice was tethered and placed into a behavioral chamber which was lined with a clean piece of white paper and subsequently infused either with 1 µL of methylene blue through the NAP, or, after changing the paper with a new clean piece, four rapid successions of 1 µL methylene blue. As anticipated, four rapid successions of 1 µL dye resulted in a small amount of blue dye detectable upon the papers in two of the mice, whereas the single 1-µL infusion left a clean paper except trace evidence in one mouse (Fig. 2e). These results support that the NAP can achieve precision infusions; notably, when the volume of a given bolus is kept to a minimum, fluids are retained in the nasal passages. In these same animals, we also verified no detectable methylene blue in the trachea or esophagus, confirming sequestration of delivery to the nasal passages.

Because mice are obligate nasal breathers, we sought to validate that being implanted with the NAP into the nasal cavity does not impair the basic ability to respire or to perceive odors. We used whole-body plethysmography, wherein breaths are detected by a pressure transducer that accesses the inside of the air-tight plethysmograph (Fig. 3a) to assess possible differences in respiration in NAP-implanted mice versus control non-implanted mice. After two habituation days (30 min each), we monitored respiration on a subsequent day as the animals were in the plethysmograph, which revealed qualitatively similar respiratory dynamics (Fig. 3b). We calculated the average percentage of time the mice spent in low-frequency respiration (1–5 Hz), indicative of a quiescent behavioral state, and high-frequency respiration (6–10 Hz), observed during exploratory behavior24,27,28. Comparisons of NAP-implanted versus control mice indicated that both groups exhibited similar respiratory frequencies, suggesting that the fundamental breathing behavior, at least at the level measured here, was not altered (Fig. 3c; two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), NAP versus control: F(1,6) = 0.21, P = 0.67; NAP versus control × average % time at low versus high respiratory frequency: F(1,6) = 0.38, P = 0.56).

Furthermore, in the same set of mice, we screened olfactory function by examining odor investigation behavior. For this, we relied upon the intrinsic motivation of mice to approach and investigate (‘sniff’) novel odors that they had not previously experienced by presenting them with four contiguous novel odors and monitoring the time spent investigating them (as defined by snout-oriented investigation within 1 cm of an odor port; Fig. 3d). Both NAP-implanted and control mice reliably oriented toward the novel odors, displayed investigation of the odors and did so at a comparable level (Fig. 3e; two-way ANOVA, NAP versus control: F(1,6) = 0.21, P = 0.67; NAP versus control × average % time at low versus high respiratory frequency: F(1,6) = 0.38, P = 0.56). Taken together with prior literature from rodents with IN implants to monitor respiration, which report normal displays of even highly sophisticated cognitive, social and motivated behaviors (for example, refs. 24,25,27–32), these results confirm that NAP implantation does not alter some fundamental aspects of rodent behavior.

Application of the NAP to reliably deliver substances in vivo

Next, we sought to test the capability of the NAP for IN delivery of substances in awake, freely moving mice. To do so, we selected to quantify known chemical readouts of IN cocaine hydrochloride exposure. Despite the widespread use of the nasal route of administration by human drug users, there is no established preclinical model that accurately captures this method of administration. Cocaine is frequently administered IN, contributing to its reinforcing effects via recruitment of the brain’s dopaminergic system, and subsequently to its high abuse liability. However, the distinct pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of IN cocaine administration in an awake mouse remain poorly understood. This knowledge gap limits our ability to develop interventions for cocaine addiction, particularly for individuals who primarily use the IN route. Furthermore, despite the high prevalence of IN drug use, preclinical research has largely relied on intravenous, intraperitoneal or sometimes oral routes of administration. This discrepancy limits our ability to translate findings from animal models to human drug use and hinders the development of effective prevention and treatment strategies.

To address this issue, we used the NAP to administer cocaine IN in freely moving mice and accomplished measures of cocaine and its metabolites in brain and plasma post mortem; and in separate mice, we performed real-time measures of DA levels in the brain utilizing in vivo fiber photometry33 with a genetically encoded DA biosensor. Importantly, in all subsequent experiments, we validated the patency of the NAPs through infusion of a small bolus of saline and confirming that fluid was emitted from the ipsilateral nares in all mice.

NAP-implanted mice received a cocaine bolus consisting of four separate 1-µL infusions, achieving a cocaine dose of 8 mg/kg while they were awake and freely exploring a cage. Plasma and brain samples were collected at 1, 3, 6 or 12 min post-infusion for quantitative analysis of cocaine and its metabolites (benzoylecgonine and ecgonine methyl ester) using ultraperformance liquid chromatography–triple quadrupole mass spectrometry (UPLC–MS/MS) (Fig. 4a; compound parameters for quantification of analytes using UPLC–MS/MS are represented in Table 1). Both plasma and brain samples from treated mice contributing data to all time points were included, as well as samples from control mice infused with physiological saline. To prevent the metabolism of cocaine by esterase, samples were stabilized with 2% potassium fluoride. IN administration of cocaine resulted in detectable levels of cocaine in the brain within just 1 min post-infusion (Fig. 4b), with the greatest levels of cocaine in the brain (compared with saline-infused mice) being detected at 6 min post-infusion (Tukey’s multiple comparisons, P < 0.0001) and levels starting to decline by the 12-min time point (Fig. 4b). IN infusion of cocaine resulted in greater levels of cocaine detected in the brain compared with plasma (Fig. 4b; two-way ANOVA, F(1,38) = 20.89, P < 0.0001). Consistent with its primary metabolism in the liver and plasma34–37, levels of benzoylecgonine, the secondary metabolite of cocaine, were detected at higher concentration in the plasma in comparison with the brain (Fig. 4b; two-way ANOVA, F(1,38) = 35.4, P < 0.0001), with the greatest levels detected at 6 and 12 min post-infusion (Tukey’s multiple comparisons, 6 min P = 0.0015; 12 min P < 0.0001).

Next, we used real-time measures to examine neurochemical outcomes following IN cocaine. Cocaine exerts its effects on neural circuitry and behavior primarily by inhibiting the reuptake of biogenic amines, including DA in a key ‘brain reward’ center, the nucleus accumbens38–40. To monitor possible changes in DA levels upon IN cocaine delivery, we injected mice with the DA sensor GRABDA (AAV9.hSyn-GRAB_DA1h)41 into their nucleus accumbens shell (NAcSh), and subsequently implanted an optical fiber in the same region for subsequent fiber photometry-based monitoring of DA in freely moving mice (Fig. 4c). All mice were also implanted with a NAP as described above. On separate days, mice were intranasally delivered boluses of either physiological saline or cocaine hydrochloride in a counterbalanced manner. A total of four boluses were delivered (2 mg/kg each, 1 µL each infusion, 15 s inter-infusion interval), over three total repetitions separated in time by approximately 5 min, spanning 15 min total, yielding a cumulative amount of 24 mg/kg cocaine per mouse (Fig. 4d). As shown in Fig. 4e,f, we observed elevations in the GRABDA signal upon the second and especially the third cocaine bolus, indicating that IN cocaine via the NAP results in elevations in central nervous system DA levels, at least as detected by a biosensor, within <15 min. Increases in nucleus accumbens DA levels were detected across repetitions of cocaine boluses in GRABDA mice (Fig. 4f; repeated measures (rm)ANOVA, F(1.566, 6.263) = 5.059, P = 0.054), with the greatest increase in DA levels after the third bolus (Tukey’s multiple comparisons, baseline versus bolus 3, P = 0.015). This increase was specific to cocaine and DA in that no such increase was detected in the same mice on the day they were administered IN saline (Tukey’s multiple comparisons bolus 3 versus saline, P = 0.046), nor in separate mice injected with IN cocaine, yet monitored by means of a GRAB sensor mutated to be unresponsive to DA, GRABDA-mut (Fig. 4f, Tukey’s multiple comparisons bolus 3 of cocaine in GRABDA versus GRABDA-mut mice P = 0.024; Methods). Altogether, these results indicate that, following IN infusion via the NAP in freely behaving mice, cocaine is detected soon after, and brain DA levels are elevated.

IN cocaine rapidly and robustly drives behavioral changes

Having established that the NAP achieves rapid accumulation of cocaine in both plasma and brain, as well as dopamine in the brain following IN cocaine delivery, we next sought to determine whether the NAP permits drug delivery concurrent with behavioral monitoring of drug-induced changes. As a starting point, we monitored locomotion in mice to identify that IN cocaine rapidly drives hyperlocomotion. NAP-implanted mice were placed in chambers with infrared cameras for later tracking of distance traveled42 and connected to a flexible infusion line attached to an infusion pump (Fig. 5a). Each day, following 15 min of acclimation to the chamber tethered to an infusion line, the mice received a single bolus (1 µL infusion, 4×, every 15 s) of saline or cocaine (Fig. 5b), with increasing concentrations to achieve increasing doses of cocaine across separate days. Across sexes, we found an increase in locomotion in cocaine-treated mice at the 4 mg/kg per infusion dose, which escalated with increasing doses on subsequent days (Fig. 5c; mixed-effects model, F(4.597, 88.59) = 6.998, P < 0.001; Tukey’s multiple comparisons 4 mg/kg per infusion dose versus saline P = 0.024). Increases in locomotion were observed in both sexes (Fig. 5d). In a subset of mice used in the experiments above, we allowed cocaine to wash out for at least one week and then examined the time course over which IN cocaine induced hyperlocomotion by analyzing locomotor changes following delivery of a highly concentrated cocaine bolus (48 mg/kg). This uncovered that significant elevations in locomotion occur within just 5 min post-infusion of IN cocaine (Fig. 5e; two-way rmANOVA, F(4.552, 81.94) = 6.984, P < 0.001; Tukey’s multiple comparisons cocaine treatment versus saline at 5 min P = 0.048).

Validation of the NAP for use during instrumental behaviors

Intravenous self-administration models, in which mice actively seek and take cocaine using instrumental procedures, are widely used to study the behavioral effects of cocaine, define its neuropharmacological mechanisms, and have provided insights into cocaine-induced plasticity and circuitry that differ from those observed with passive cocaine administration43–46. Through these paradigms, models have been established in which, much like human users, some rodents show high levels of responding to obtain cocaine, whereas others exhibit low levels of responding47–51. Owing to these factors, cocaine self-administration paradigms are thought to have high face validity for recapitulating cocaine use disorder in humans. Having established the NAP, we sought to test the applicability of the NAP for use in drug self-administration. Would mice with an implant on their nasal bone engage in an operant behavioral assay? Will some mice escalate in cocaine taking across sessions as reported in intravenous paradigms? Furthermore, because irritation induced by IN cocaine itself would provide a cue to the animal as an IN stimulus, will mice work to take cocaine intranasally in the absence of drug-paired or contextual cues that are otherwise needed for animals to develop self-administration in intravenous paradigms52–55?

To test these questions, mice of both sexes were placed into operant chambers containing nose-poke ports equipped with infrared photodiodes that signal port entry and NAPs connected to flexible infusion tethers attached to infusion pumps to develop IN cocaine (Fig. 6a; 0.3 mg/kg per infusion, infusion of 1 µL volume delivered over a 350-ms period). Before the first self-administration session, active (cocaine-delivering) and inactive ports (no outcome, monitored to assay poking behavior independent of achieving IN cocaine) were randomly assigned for each chamber and mice allowed to self-administer for up to 2 h daily (Fig. 6b). NAP patency was verified pre- and post-testing by IN saline resulting in fluid exit from the ipsilateral nares. Consistent with prior intravenous work, we found that some mice escalated in their taking of IN cocaine across behavioral sessions. Across all mice, 27% (4/15) displayed high-responding behaviors as defined by >70% of pokes occurring in the cocaine-paired port for at least two consecutive sessions (Fig. 6c). Among these mice, they displayed on average 85% of pokes for the cocaine-paired port compared with the inactive port on their last three behavioral sessions (Fig. 6d; 70–97% interanimal range). High-responding mice displayed significant escalation in poking for the cocaine-paired port across sessions compared with intermediate- and low-responding mice. (Fig. 6d; two-way rmANOVA, F(2,12) = 11.99, P = 0.001).

As expected from prior work investigating cocaine-taking in intravenous rodent models56–61, population differences in drug-responding vulnerability also manifested from IN cocaine self-administration, which we have defined here as intermediate- and low-responding mice (those not displaying bias toward poking in the active port across sessions and those displaying decreases in poking in the active port across sessions, respectively; Fig. 6c,d). Further validation of IN cocaine self-administration was achieved in some mice: we inspected behaviors of high-responding mice to uncover that several of the mice took infusion boluses at respectively high numbers (Fig. 6e), achieving considerably large doses of cocaine intake within sessions, and they largely displayed consistent motivation within individual sessions as indicated by an across mouse inter-infusion interval average of 67 s (Fig. 6f; 14–119 s interanimal range within the session with the greatest number of pokes).

Consistency of in vivo fluid delivery

A common issue of intravenous jugular catheter drug delivery in preclinical models is that the catheters fail from mechanism failure or become clogged/lose patency for fluid entry into the body62–64. From the mice used throughout all the above in vivo experiments, we verified patency upon end of the experiments by means of either validating fluid emission from the ipsilateral nares, or by means of observing methylene blue staining of the nasal turbinates. Mice survived for varying durations following NAP implantation, depending on the requirements of each experiment. Across all experiments, including even the behavioral assays that involved daily connecting and disconnecting of the animal to infusion lines for weeks, the NAP yielded a >91% success in maintaining patency for an average of 2 months (62/68 mice). Notably, this outcome was independent of any maintenance to the implant—we did not provide solutions to clear the NAPs following implantation nor did we administer any antibiotics. To emphasize the feasibility of this approach, it should also be noted that the sole surgeon and in vivo experimenter (M.F.R.) was a first-year graduate student and novice to the surgical procedure for NAP implantation.

Discussion

Here, we present an in-dwelling microfluidic surgical device that achieves reliable and long-term (up to months) delivery of drugs into the nasal cavity. This represents a tractable solution to a long-standing challenge in preclinical drug delivery and discovery, the IN delivery. The NAP we present here enables IN delivery in freely behaving animals as small as mice.

Our findings support multiple uses of the NAP. First, our work indicates that the NAP is suitable for research requiring precise drug delivery to the nasal epithelium, allowing controlled investigation of drug effects, including pharmacokinetics, behavioral responses and neurochemical/circuitry changes and beyond, relevant to a wide range of research areas, drug discovery and so on. The NAP offers an innovative platform for the biomedical community for future testing of the effects of other drugs that are provided intranasally, even mandated by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in some cases, including candidate analgesics, anti-epileptics and so on, and their neural mechanisms. Second, the NAP proves appropriate for studies needing to examine outcomes of IN substance administration on the body, including bodily organs such as the brain, at different scales. By facilitating IN delivery, the NAP bridges the gap between human drug use patterns and preclinical models (which otherwise administer drugs systemically or through jugular catheters, neither of which are used by human drug users). This capability allows directly translatable research on drug-induced brain changes. In addition, the small scale of the NAP enables simultaneous physiological measures and manipulations. We exemplified that by showing the amenability of the NAP for simultaneous cocaine administration during in vivo fiber photometry. Moreover, the NAP enables precise and controlled drug administration in behaving animals, enabling the study of behavioral outcomes of IN drugs. Highly relevant to the study of behavior, we demonstrated that, while the NAP resides in the nose, it does not impair the ability of animals to maintain basic breathing or olfaction. These findings are consistent with a wealth of prior literature examining rodents with IN implants of comparable sizes and placement, while engaging highly sophisticated cognitive, sensory-guided, social and motivated behaviors (for example, refs. 24,25,27–32). Furthermore, animals can freely move and even engage in highly coordinated instrumental behavioral assays while implanted with the NAP and connected to an infusion system (as in Fig. 6). There are a few key differences in the application of the NAP for use in vivo versus traditional in-dwelling jugular catheters, which are the mainstream approach for in vivo drug delivery other than manual injections by means of hypodermic needles. First is that jugular catheters directly access the circulatory system, presenting the risk of infection, and that these catheters must be closed to the environment by a solution that often contains antibiotics, anticoagulants and glycerol. The maintenance of the jugular catheters, recommended by many labs to be performed daily, is not an issue with the NAP because it does not require maintenance. Despite not performing any maintenance on the NAP, in this study we achieved a >92% patency success rate in mice until their predetermined endpoint. A total of 72 mice remained implanted for 8 weeks, upon which patency was still observed. We experienced patency loss in six mice. In one mouse, the NAP was determined to be clogged immediately following surgery due to dental cement precipitating through the craniotomy (experimenter error). A different mouse lost patency owing to dislodging of the NAP implant from the skull 14 weeks after implantation. In the remaining four mice, loss of patency was determined >8 weeks after implantation. Those mice had undergone locomotion monitoring with cocaine administrations (as in Fig. 5), so it is possible that the repeated cocaine delivery resulted in buildup of nasal mucus/scar tissue as part of the healing process associated with potential cocaine-evoked irritation of the nasal mucosa. Also, while in-dwelling jugular catheter patency is often assayed by monitoring rapid loss of motor tone following injection of propofol or xylazine, both of which are somewhat costly and subject to strict regulations by research institutions and most countries, patency of the NAP can be verified by simple observation of saline or any other innocuous fluid being released from the ipsilateral nostril following a small infusion.

The NAP promises scalability from mice to larger preclinical models. We anticipate that a comparable approach would achieve reliable and precise drug delivery in rats, rabbits, guinea pigs, pigs and other preclinical models. These adjustments would simply involve (1) providing a greater surface area of the NAP to increase strength of the surgical connection to the nasal bone, (2) increasing the strength of the magnetic attachment configuration or include a latching device to ensure the device stays attached to the nasal bone, and (3) increasing the length of the infusion tube so it can pass through thicker nasal bones. The later adjustment is especially important since the location of the infusion in the nasal cavity would impact the location where substances would be distributed—a factor also impacted by IN dynamics (for example, nasal airflow) that may be unique in different animal models65,66.

Despite the advantages stated, the NAP requires surgical implantation This entails aseptic surgical supplies and surgical equipment, making it a potentially costly procedure for a lab not already equipped. The implant also extends beyond the skull cap, and ultimately for fluid delivery requires a tether, which also may slightly restrict normal head movements; this suggests caution when interpreting results if applying the NAP for studies on certain behaviors, such as orofacial behaviors, including facial grooming. While we show proof-of-concept data that the NAP lends itself for use while animals engage in complex instrumental/operant behavioral assays, more work is needed to establish IN self-administration paradigms. This work includes more concretely establishing cocaine self-administration parameters (dose/infusion) and to further study IN self-administration in animals seeking to obtain other psychoactive drugs.

In conclusion, the NAP represents an important methodology with broad applicability in biomedical and life sciences research, especially in neuroscience, pharmacology, medicinal chemistry and physiology domains. We anticipate the NAP will be a cornerstone approach for investigating IN drug delivery and its genomic, molecular, cellular, systems and behavioral consequences, all of which remain unexplored. This paradigm is expected to enable preclinical research and development of IN therapies to extend beyond in vitro models, as currently required and closely regulated by both the FDA and the European Medicines Agency (EMA)15, into whole-animal systems.

Methods

Animals

Male and female C57BL/6J mice, 3–6 months of age, originating from breeder stock at Jackson Laboratory (strain #000664; RRID: IMSR_JAX:000664), were bred and maintained at the University of Florida. Mice were housed in a temperature-controlled vivarium on a 12:12 h light–dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. All behavioral testing occurred during the light cycle. Mice were single housed following surgical procedure for chronic implants. Animals were housed in standard shoebox-sized plastic cages (Allentown Jaw 75 micro-vent system; length 29.2 cm, width 18.5 cm, height 12.7 cm) in a rack for individually vented cages. Corncob bedding and a Nestlet (Ancaes) were in each cage along with manzanita sticks (Bio-Serve) for enrichment. All experimental procedures were conducted within the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC) animal research program of the University of Florida in accordance with the guidelines from the National Institute of Health and were approved by the University of Florida Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol no. 202300000126).

The NAP

To achieve microfluidic delivery into the nasal cavity, we developed the NAP. The NAP consists of an infusion port that protrudes 10 mm from the Hydex plastic base, which expands 2.8 mm mediolaterally and 9.9 mm anteroposteriorally to provide sufficient surface area and maximize adhesion to the mouse’s cranium. A 2.54-mm magnet is embedded in the base for temporary fastening to a female mating connector for subsequent fluid delivery and/or a dust cap when not in use. Extending 1.3 mm dorsoventrally beyond the base of the NAP is a 25-gauge stainless-steel tube (0.5 mm outer diameter and 0.029 inner diameter) that is placed inside a small craniotomy over the nasal bone of the animal and permits IN delivery of microliter boluses of fluids with minimal resistance unilaterally in the nasal passage.

Surgical procedures

For all surgical procedures, under aseptic conditions, mice were anesthetized with 2–4% isoflurane in oxygen (IsoFlo, Patterson Veterinary) and mounted on a stereotaxic frame while body temperature was maintained on a 38 °C heating pad underneath their body. The analgesic Meloxicam (20 mg/kg, Patterson Veterinary) was subcutaneously (s.c.) administered followed by the local anesthetic lidocaine (lidocaine, 3 mg/kg, s.c., Patterson Veterinary) to the scalp before incision and exposure of the skull.

For NAP implantation, the nasal bone was leveled by adjusting the stereotaxic incisor bar. Next, a 1-mm-diameter hole was drilled through the right nasal bone, 1 mm anterior to the frontal/nasal fissure and 0.5 mm lateral. These coordinates were selected to position the NAP’s stainless-steel infusion tube immediately upon the vascularized turbinates of the nasal epithelium in mice. Following drilling, the NAP was lowered through the craniotomy, and secured with dental cement (Teets cold cure) to the proximal nasal bone and remainder of the skull. After the cement dried, a removable aluminum cap with a magnetic insert was placed over the implant to protect it while not in use.

For in vivo monitoring of DA levels, we injected viruses to transduce the GRABDA biosensor. For this, craniotomies were drilled to specifically target the medial shell region of the nucleus accumbens (NAc; +1.25 mm anterior–posterior/0.8 mm medial–lateral/−4.40 mm dorsal–ventral). A pulled micropipette containing 100 nL AAV9.hSyn-GRAB_DA1h (Addgene 113050, titer: 1 × 1013 vg/mL)49 or 100 nL of the inert GRABDA sensor AAV9.hSyn-GRAB_DA-mut (Addgene 140555, titer: 1 × 1013 vg/mL) was slowly lowered into the region of interest, and 100 nL of viral solution was delivered unilaterally using a Nanoject III (Drummond Scientific) at a rate of 2 nL/s. Following a 10-min wait, the pipette was slowly withdrawn from the brain. Subsequently, a 400 µm outer-diameter, 0.39 numerical aperture (NA) optical fiber terminating in a 2.5 mm outer-diameter ferrule was lowered to the same coordinates as the injections and cemented to the skull for later fiber photometry-based monitoring of DA levels. Animals were allowed to recover for at least 3 weeks before any fiber photometry to allow sufficient viral transduction.

Upon completion of surgeries (whether NAP alone or NAP along with methods for monitoring DA levels) the mice were removed from the stereotaxic frame and placed in a clean home cage with on a heating pad to aid recovery. Mice were given Meloxicam (20 mg/kg, s.c., Patterson Veterinary) for a 3-day postoperative period following the surgery.

Fluid-delivery kinetics

We used time-lapse microscopy, using an Olympus epifluorescence microscope (ThorLabs), to demonstrate the fast dispensing of small volumes of fluid from the NAP. The access port of the NAP was connected to a female mating connector attached to a line backfilled with 1% fluorescein (Thermo Fisher Scientific), a fluorescent dye, driven by a syringe pump (R-210, Razel Scientific Instruments) controlled to deliver 1-µL aliquots. Time lapse was acquired using a compact scientific digital camera (CS235CU, ThorLabs) and analyzed through the ThorCam software (version 3.7.0.6, ThorLabs, Newton, NJ) across ten infusion replications. Upon triggering the pump, we were able to validate the fast dispensing of 1 µL of the dye with short latency (tens of milliseconds) measured as changes in fluorescence intensity.

Patency testing

Patency of NAPs was tested a day before any behavioral testing occurred. Mice were briefly anesthetized under 2–4% isoflurane in oxygen and a 1-mL syringe attached to a PinPort injector (Instech Laboratories) was utilized to infuse 0.1 mL of saline through the NAP to ensure a small amount of fluid could travel through the NAP and into the nasal cavity as indicated by a visible drop exiting the ipsilateral nostril.

NAP implantation site confirmation

To verify the NAP implant and subsequent infusion site, the skull of a NAP-implanted mouse was carefully dissected and the fur removed following overdose with sodium pentobarbital (Fatal-Plus, Patterson) and transcardial perfusion with cold 0.9% saline followed by 10% phosphate-buffered formalin. The skull was placed in a mouse cassette with styrofoam inserts (Bruker) and scanned in a Skyscan 1276 µ-CT (Bruker, Billerica, MA). Then, 16-µm optical slices were obtained at 60 kV, 200 µA, Al 0.5 mm, and images were processed off-line using DataViewer (Bruker). As an additional measure, a subset of mice were placed in a 15.24 × 15.24 × 15.24 cm (length × width × height) clear acrylic chamber and tethered to a magnetic female connector attached to PU tubing contained within a 30.48-cm spring connected to a 25ga swivel tethered with the addition of a clean white paper lining the floor of the chamber, and received a 1-µL infusion of methylene blue dye. The paper was exchanged for a clean piece, and the mice consecutively received 1-µL infusions, ×4, in a 5-s succession. The papers were scanned for visible residues of the dyes. In addition, a subset of mice underwent NAP-mediated infusions of 1 µL, 2 µL or 4 µL of methylene blue dye, and the nasal cavities were dissected along the midline guided by the frontal incisors for confirmation of ipsilateral infusion site and surface area covered by bolus infusion.

Assessment of breathing and odor perception

To monitor breathing, mice (four NAP-implanted, four non-implanted) were placed in a whole-body plethysmograph (Data Sciences International). Respiratory transients were detected using a pressure transducer (Data Sciences International) and digitized at 300 Hz (0.1–12 Hz band pass; Tucker Davis Technologies), following 500× gain amplification (Cyngus Technology). Positive pressure of clean room air was continuously applied to the chamber using a stable-output air pump (Tetra Whisper). Inhalation peaks were detected offline in Spike2 (Cambridge Electronic Design), and breathing frequency was calculated by comparing the interbreath intervals.

For assessment of the basic ability to perceive odors, the same mice used above to assay breathing were presented with novel odors (n = 4; 2-heptanone, isoamyl acetate, limonene, ethyl butyrate, Millipore Sigma) diluted in mineral oil to standardize the vapor pressure across odors (1 torr). As previously described67, a cotton applicator tip was saturated with each odor and placed inside a hollow plastic tube before inserting it into a port on the side of the animal’s clean home cage, the ‘odor port’, to prevent contamination of the testing chamber. During testing, the food rack and water bottle were removed to minimize distraction. Odors were presented for 20 s separated by 30 s between each odor presentation. The duration of time spent investigating was defined as snout-oriented investigation (‘sniffing’) within 1 cm of the odor presentation port and recorded by a single observer (M.F.R.).

Cocaine

Cocaine hydrochloride was generously provided by the Drug Discovery Program at the National Institute on Drug Abuse and was dissolved in sterile physiological saline (0.9% NaCl). Concentration was calculated w/v and adjusted to achieve the desired concentration per infusion based on body weight of the animal (mg/kg per infusion dose).

UPLC–MS/MS

For cocaine and metabolite detection, mice were placed in the clear acrylic chamber and tethered as previously described. In this instance, the line was backfilled with cocaine and attached to a syringe pump triggered by a microcontroller to deliver a single bolus of 8 mg/kg of cocaine (1 µL, ×4, every 15 s) for tissue collection at 1, 3, 6 and 12 min, post-dose. Following infusion, mice were deeply anesthetized in a chamber filled with isoflurane at T − 60 s from the goal time point. After 45 s, a lack of response to a toe pinch was confirmed, and tissue collection proceeded. Blood was collected from cardiac puncture and added to EDTA test tubes containing pre-aliquoted potassium fluoride (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a final concentration of 2% w/v. Subsequently, blood was centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the plasma was collected and then stored in −80 °C until analysis. Following blood collection, the brain was perfused using 0.5 mL of the same potassium fluoride solution through the heart. The brain was extracted and immediately stored in −80 °C for later analysis (Fig. 4). Plasma and brain samples were stabilized with potassium fluoride to prevent the esterase-mediated metabolism of cocaine.

UPLC–MS/MS was used to quantify cocaine and its metabolites (benzoylecgonine and ecgonine methyl ester (Millipore Sigma)) in plasma and brain homogenates using a Waters Acquity I-Class system coupled with a Xevo TQ-S Micro triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Waters) and running MassLynx (version 4.2, TargetLynx XS; Waters). Samples were analyzed against freshly prepared calibration and quality-control standards in respective matrix following the FDA guideline for bioanalytical method validation68. These samples were processed via simple protein precipitation method by addition of 80 μL of acetonitrile (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing internal standard (cocaine-d3; IS, 2 ng/mL, Millipore Sigma). Samples were vortex mixed, filtered via a multiscreen Solvinert (Millipore Sigma) 96-well filter plate (0.45 μm), centrifuged at 1,500 rpm, 4 °C for 5 min, and injected onto Waters Acquity UPLC coupled with Xevo TQ-S micro triple quadruple mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was achieved in gradient mode using a Waters Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (1.7 µm, 2.1 × 100 mm) with a VanGuard pre-column of a similar chemistry (Waters). The optimized linear gradient program for quantitation of cocaine and its metabolites was as follows: 0–0.8 min, 5% B; 0.8–1.5 min, 5–95% B; 1.5–2.5 min, 95% B; 2.6–3.5 min, 5% B, where mobile phase A was ammonium acetate buffer (2.5 mM; pH: 3.5) and mobile phase B was acetonitrile. The weak needle wash was comprised of water, methanol and acetonitrile (2:1:1 proportion; 0.1% formic acid). The strong needle wash was comprised of water, acetonitrile, methanol and isopropyl alcohol (1:1:1:1 proportion; 0.1% formic acid). The column oven and autosampler temperature were set at 50 °C and 10 °C, respectively. Quantification of cocaine and its metabolites was performed using TargetLynx software. Behavioral experiments

Locomotor activity test for dose response

The locomotor activity test was conducted in a custom-made, open-top, acrylonitrile butadiene styrene 30.48 × 30.48 × 30.48 cm (length × width × height) dark acrylic chamber. For the dose response, the mice were handled and habituated to the behavior room before experimentation. On day 1, the animals were gently scruffed, the protective aluminum cap was removed, and the mice were tethered. The line was empty to allow habituation to the apparatus. On days 2 and 3, baseline locomotion was recorded. The lines were backfilled with sterile saline and the swivel connected to a 1-mL syringe in a pump powered and controlled by an Arduino Uno microcontroller running a custom script. The Arduino generated a transistor–transistor logic pulse to trigger a pump that delivered 1-µL infusions via the NAP into the nasal epithelium every 15 s, for a total bolus of 4 µL. The mice were placed in the chamber for 15 min to acclimate each day tethered to the swivel, and at the 15-min mark, they received saline infusions and locomotor activity was recorded for 15 min post-infusion. At the end of the 15 min, mice were gently restrained, the tether was removed, and they were placed back in their home cage. On days 4–15, mice received cocaine hydrochloride in increasing doses daily (0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 1, 1.25, 1.5, 1.75, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 mg/kg per infusion dose) and followed the same recording schedule previously mentioned. To study the time course whereby IN cocaine results in hyperlocomotion, a subset of mice used for the locomotor dose–response study above received eight 1-µL infusions (15-s interval) via the NAP into the nasal epithelium of a highly concentrated cocaine bolus (48 mg/kg). All behavioral data were recorded using an infrared video camera (12-Hz frame rate) located above each chamber. Locomotion was extracted from the videos off-line in ezTrack42.

Cocaine self-administration

Self-administration studies were conducted in custom-made open-top 60 × 30 × 30 cm (length × width × height) acrylonitrile butadiene styrene operant chambers69 housed in noise-attenuating cabinets. Each chamber consisted of a guillotine-style door and a wall that contained an extruded panel with three nose-poke ports equipped with 880-nm infrared photodiodes that generated a transistor–transistor logic pulse when broken to signal port entry. For IN self-administration sessions, a swivel was held by a counterbalanced lever arm anchored to one of the chamber walls. The swivel was connected to a 1-mL syringe in a pump and attached to flexible PU tubing contained in a 30.48-cm-long spring that would be attached to the NAP. The operant chambers were powered and driven by an Arduino microcontroller to digitize the occurrence of beam breaks (nose-pokes, 200 Hz sampling rate). Mice underwent 7 days of IN self-administration. Before the first session of self-administration, animals were gently scuffed, the protective aluminum cap was removed, and the mice were tethered for 1 h to an empty line to allow habituation to operant chamber. On the next day (session 1), one of the nose-poke ports was randomly assigned as the active port, which would result in cocaine IN infusions, while the other port was deemed as the inactive port and would result in no consequences. This assignment was kept consistent throughout sessions for each mouse. During each 2-h-long session, mice were tethered to the drug line which would deliver 1 µL infusion of cocaine (0.3 mg/kg per infusion dose) upon entry of the active nose-poke port. No other cues were present in the chamber, except for the nasal sensation evoked by the delivery of the drug into the nasal epithelium.

Fiber photometric recordings of DA

Mice were injected into the nucleus accumbens with AAV9.hSyn-GRAB_DA1h49 or the inert GRABDA sensor AAV9.hSyn-GRAB_DA-mut and implanted with an optical fiber into the NAcSh as described above for later fiber photometry as we have described (see surgical methods)27. At least 3 weeks later, 465-nm (GRABDA/GRABDA-mut excitation wavelength, driven at 210 Hz) and 405-nm (UV excitation wavelength, driven at 330 Hz; control) light-emitting diodes (LEDs; TDT, RZ10x) were coupled to a fluorescent minicube (Doric lenses) using 200-µm, 0.22 NA optic fiber patch cords. Excitation and emission light were directed through a single 400-µm, 0.57 NA patch cord connected to the animal by the implanted 2.5-mm metal ferrule. Emission light was directed through the minicube and coupled to femtowatt photoreceivers to monitor GFP and UV signals (TDT, RZ10x). The emission signals were low-pass filtered (6th order), pulse demodulated, and digitized (1 kHz) using a Tucker Davis Technologies processor (TDT). All parameters for imaging and acquisition of photometry data were consistent across all groups.

Behavioral sessions for acquiring photometry data lasted approximately 25 min. The mice were tethered to the patch cord; the NAP was attached to the infusion line and then placed in the clear chamber previously mentioned for behavioral monitoring. The animals received saline and cocaine (2 mg/kg per infusion dose) intranasally on individual sessions in consecutive days. Each session consisted of a 5-min acclimation period, followed by computer-controlled and user-initiated infusions via a pump. Each bolus cycle consisted of 1-µL aliquots delivered every 15 s, ×4 for a total volume of 4-µL bolus. The session consisted of three bolus repetitions every 5 min to allow monitoring of DA transients.

Histology

At the end of the experiment, mice were overdosed with sodium pentobarbital (Fatal-Plus, Patterson) and transcardially perfused with cold 0.9% saline followed by 10% phosphate-buffered formalin (SF100-4, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Brains were removed and stored at 4 °C in 30% sucrose formalin for cryoprotection and postfixation for at least 24 h before they were coronally sectioned at 40 µm on a freezing microtome (Leica). Sections were slide mounted and coverslipped with a 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI)-counterstained mounting medium (DAPI-fluoromount-G, SouthernBiotech). The location of the nucleus accumbens, the target region for fiber photometry, was identified using a mouse brain atlas while referencing the DAPI label70. Viral injections and optical fiber placements were verified on a Nikon Ti2e inverted fluorescent microscope, and only the mice with on-target injections/fiber implants contributed to the dataset.

Statistical analysis

All data were processed using semi-automated steps whenever possible, including processing data from mixed treatment groups at the same time. Statistical comparisons were analyzed in GraphPad Prism, which included rmANOVA and/or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison corrections when needed. All data are reported as mean ± s.e.m. unless noted otherwise.
