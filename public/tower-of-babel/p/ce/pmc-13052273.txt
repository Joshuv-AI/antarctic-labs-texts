Supporting the Synergy Between School Meal Programs and Food Banks to Combat Food Insecurity Among Children: A Bayesian Mindsponge Framework Analysis
Authors: Ni Putu Wulan Purnama Sari, Chenaimoyo Lufutuko Faith Katiyatiya, Adrino Mazenda, Chido Chakanya, Minh‐Hoang Nguyen, Quan‐Hoang Vuong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsFood banks play a major role in the food aid sector by distributing donated and purchased groceries directly to food‐insecure families, which helps reduce food insecurity. On the other hand, school meal programs are implemented in many countries to combat food insecurity among school‐aged children. The synergy between school meal programs and food banks has great potential to combat food insecurity among children effectively. This study aimed to examine the impacts of experts' involvement, such as nutritionists, farmers, and private sector actors, and community engagement, including parents and others, on the linkage between food banks and school meal programs.MethodsThe Bayesian Mindsponge Framework, combining the reasoning strengths of the Granular Interaction Thinking Theory (GITT) and inference advantages of Bayesian analysis, was employed on a dataset of government representatives who manage large‐scale school meal programs in 126 different countries.ResultsFindings showed that the involvement of nutritionists and the private sector was positively related to the linkage between food banks and school meal programs, while farmers' involvement showed an ambiguous relationship. The engagement of parents and the public in general also showed an ambiguous relationship with this linkage.ConclusionFindings underscore the importance of increasing the involvement of nutritionists and the private sector to link food banks with school meal programs in implementing countries, which can help improve food security among school‐aged children.Keywords: child nutrition, expert involvement, food bank, food insecurity, school meals program

Introduction

Food insecurity remains a critical issue that affects millions of individuals and households globally, with particularly devastating effects on children and low‐income families [2]. According to Endale et al., food insecurity is characterized by the lack of consistent access to enough food for an active, healthy life [3]. Food insecurity affects millions of children globally, limiting their access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food [4], with far‐reaching consequences on their well‐being and development. The 2025 edition of the State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World (SOFI) Report from FAO, IFAD, UNICEF, WFP, and WHO showed that 673 million people faced hunger in 2024 (8.3% of the global population), and 2.3 billion people experienced moderate or severe food insecurity [5]. Children disproportionately affected; stunting among children fell to 23.2% in 2024, but progress remains uneven across regions [5]. Sub‐Saharan Africa (SSA) has the highest global food insecurity rate (66% of the population) [6]. Statistics have shown that food insecurity in SSA has risen since 2015, when more than one in five people were affected [5], and the situation has worsened due to the Ukraine conflict and the ongoing implications of the COVID‐19 pandemic [7]. In addition, studies have shown that marginalized populations are most affected by food insecurity, including racial/ethnic minorities, low‐income households, women‐headed households, and displaced communities, linking them to poverty, poor health outcomes, and limited educational achievement [8, 9, 10, 11].

Literature shows that children facing food insecurity are more likely to experience anxiety, behavioral issues, and developmental delays, along with diminished academic performance [8, 9, 10, 11]. These children often struggle with concentration in class [12], attend school less frequently [13], and consequently, suffer from poor educational outcomes [14]. To address these challenges, various strategies have been employed across both developed and developing nations. These include government‐funded school meal programs, community‐led food banks, faith‐based feeding schemes, and private food pantries [10, 15, 16].

Food banks, typically non‐governmental and community‐based, redistribute surplus food from donors to families in need [10, 17, 18]. Such interventions are more common in high‐income countries [19], where stronger synergies between food banks and school meal programs can offer a more comprehensive response to food insecurity [20, 21, 22]. School meal programs, especially in low‐income settings, are often supported by international agencies and are crucial in enhancing child nutrition, school attendance, and academic performance [23].

Nowadays, approximately 80% of countries have a school feeding policy; one in every two school children, or 388 million children from all income levels, receives school meals every day in at least 161 countries [24]. Several international agencies and coalitions play central roles in supporting and scaling global school meal programs, such as the World Food Programme (WFP), School Meals Coalition, Global Child Nutrition Foundation (GCNF), UNICEF, WHO, Multilateral Development Banks, and Philanthropic Organizations. In addition, the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA), through the McGovern‐Dole International Food for Education and Child Nutrition Program, managed by the Foreign Agricultural Service, supports school meal programs in low‐income, food‐deficient countries by awarding funds, selecting commodities, and purchasing and shipping U.S.‐produced commodities (exclusively) for direct feeding [25].

School feeding has consequences for at least four sectors, such as social protection (being a social safety net), education (increasing school attendance), agriculture (promoting local markets), and health & nutrition [24]. A systematic review of free nutritious meal programs found that school meal programs enhance food availability, accessibility, and utilization, particularly in low‐income communities [26]. By reducing household food expenditure, these programs ease financial pressure and stabilize food access for vulnerable families. Empirical evidence shows that school meal programs contribute to healthier dietary patterns, reduce micronutrient deficiencies, and promote sustainability practices, such as integrating local produce, which strengthens both nutrition and food systems resilience [27]. Additionally, evidence from the International Food Policy Research Institute (IFPRI) highlights that school meals are more than just food; they prevent hunger, support cognitive development, and improve school attendance [28]. Children receiving regular meals show better concentration, reduced absenteeism, and improved learning outcomes, linking nutrition directly to educational achievement.

Despite the recognized crucial role of school meal programs in improving child nutrition and supporting educational outcomes [20], ensuring sustainability and adaptability to external challenges, such as economic crises or global pandemics, remains unclear. In particular, the global COVID‐19 pandemic exposed the limitations of relying solely on school meal programs. School closures prevented millions of children worldwide from accessing daily meals during the pandemic [11, 17, 29, 30]. This disruption highlighted the need for a more integrated approach, where school meal programs are linked with community‐based initiatives, such as food banks, to provide consistent and reliable access to nutritious food, even during periods of crisis. However, certain areas were committed to delivering school meals to homes during the pandemic. Evidence from the US showed that the summer meal programs, such as the Summer Food Service Program (SFSP) and Seamless Summer Option (SSO) program funded by the USDA, provided emergency free school meals for children at home during the pandemic [31, 32].

Food security initiatives such as school meal programs and food banks can be a novel approach in alleviating the negative outcomes of food insecurity. However, without proper expert involvement (nutritionists, farmers, and private sector actors) and community engagement (parents/guardians and community members, the public in general), these food security initiatives may not become sustainable. A nutritionist is considered an expert in child nutrition because they are a registered dietitian. A study in Indonesia found that nutritionists can influence the food service delivery system; the food service on‐site model with a nutritionist is better in school meal service, especially in terms of menu quality, hygiene, and sanitation [33]. To effectively play their roles in school meal programs, nutritionists also express their needs. A study in Brazil among nutritionists involved in school meal programs showed that they need more effective communication, increased participation, autonomy in administrative decisions, and full support for their mandatory activities to enhance their involvement and job stability in public services [34].

On the other hand, a farmer is considered an expert in the agricultural sector. School meal programs are market access for smallholder farmers, especially in rural areas. A study in South Africa showed that farmers' participation in school meal programs includes supporting local food production, nutrition education, and community participation [35]. Encouraging schools to engage directly with local farmers, ranchers, fishers, and other food producers offers various benefits, such as strengthening agricultural communities, providing healthier food choices, reducing costs, and creating a more sustainable school meals program by serving local food that children like [36]. Literature has shown that undernutrition has improved following agricultural development [37]. Thus, farmers' involvement in school meal programs is highly encouraged.

In addition, there are various private sector stakeholders involved in the implementation of school meal programs, which support operational modalities, multiple stakeholders' participation, and public–private sector relationships [21]. They are all experts in their respective fields, such as food and beverage businesses, transportation, marketing, quality assurance, and so forth. The school meals scheme is a big business, so many business actors are involved. Big corporations and food service companies are making millions of dollars from public school meal programs [38]. A study in Vietnam showed that these business actors were involved in the development of fineLunch, a new and unique business model for a school meal catering service, to improve the quality of school meals [39]. Many schools look to the private sector when they are lacking resources to build new kitchen and cafeteria facilities [38]. Thus, the roles of the private sector in school meal programs are essential.

Beyond experts' involvement, many community stakeholders are involved in successful school meal programs, such as public leaders, nongovernmental organizations (NGOs), parents, and so forth. A study in India showed that the success of the Mid‐Day Meal (MDM) scheme is driven by passionate initiatives taken by public leaders and institutions, such as religious organizations, NGOs, Panchayats/Municipalities, Parliament, the Supreme Court, and elected leaders at various levels, from Prime Ministers and Chief Ministers to local government officials and bureaucrats [40]. A study about the Free and Reduced Price School Meals (F&RM) program implemented in the US found that parents had largely negative views of school meals, but agreed that they met students' needs [41]. Another piece of evidence from the US showed that more positive parental perceptions about school meals and their benefits to families were associated with greater student meal participation [42]. Thus, including parents in meal‐related experiences and decision‐making could improve their involvement in school meal programs and alter their perceptions into more positive ones, influencing better educational outcomes in their children.

Research underscores the critical role of expert involvement in mitigating disruptions to traditional school meal systems [18, 30, 41]. Effective food security programs increasingly rely on community stakeholders—parents, farmers, nutritionists, and private businesses—for sustainable impact [3, 10, 16, 30, 43]. These stakeholders contribute key resources, such as produce, labor, and funding to strengthen food banks and school meal systems during crises. Parental involvement enhances program outcomes through volunteerism, feedback mechanisms, and advocacy, fostering accountability and community ownership [18, 21, 30]. Participation in food literacy programs also empowers parents with knowledge on nutrition and resource management, offering long‐term benefits for food security [41]. Local farmers aid by donating surplus or imperfect produce, reducing waste while ensuring access to fresh, affordable foods [43]. However, challenges such as a lack of incentives and poor logistics impede local food procurement [43]. Nutritionists support households by promoting dietary knowledge and cooking techniques, which is particularly impactful in low‐income settings [21, 41]. Meanwhile, private sector engagement through donations, funding, and operational support strengthens public–private partnerships in food security [3, 4, 10, 18].

School meal programs and food banks can potentially ensure a more comprehensive food security solution for children by applying a holistic approach to resource use and mobilization, as well as knowledge and community support. The study assesses the extent to which the involvement of experts and community engagement supports the synergy between food banks and school meal programs. This study aimed to examine the impacts of experts' involvement—such as nutritionists, farmers, and private sector actors—and community engagement, including parents and others, on the linkage between food banks and school meal programs among implementing countries.

Materials and Methods

Theoretical Foundation and Hypotheses

We employed the Granular Interaction Thinking Theory (GITT) in the study's conceptualization, model construction, hypothesis formulation, and findings explanation [44, 45]. GITT expands upon the Mindsponge Theory (MT) [46], incorporated by concepts of quantum mechanics [47], and Shannon's information theory [48, 49], to elucidate how macroscopic behaviors in complex systems, including human cognition, arise from the interactions of discrete information units at a microscopic level [50]. As an extension of the MT, GITT preserves its fundamental ability to describe how individuals acquire, process, and internalize information from their surroundings [44, 46]. Thus, important to understand how experts' involvement and community engagement influence the synergy between food banks and school meal programs. The granular interactions thinking mechanism is the key point of GITT to propose an entropy‐based notion of value for enhancing and strengthening its ability to explain the complexity of human behavior [44, 45].

MT is a mind's information processing theory that considers the subjective cost‐benefit judgment in information multi‐filtering processes as the key point [46], the mindsponge mechanism [51]. The mindsponge mechanism determines information absorption and ejection processes, aiming at maximizing perceived advantages and minimizing constraints. MT offers a dynamic framework for understanding the cognitive operations of the human brain, emphasizing information as the core of mental processes [46]. This cognitive model rests on entrenched beliefs that guide information processing and behavioral responses aligned with the system's priorities. MT has been used as a guiding framework and validated in several studies on school meal programs [52, 53, 54]. Recent developments, the GITT, are enhancing MT's capacity to explain complex behaviors and information dynamics [44, 45], increasing its potential to be an effective guiding framework for this study.

Within MT, information‐processing involves both the presence of information in the environment (availability) and an individual's ability to recognize and retrieve it (accessibility), which jointly shape psychological and behavioral outcomes [46, 55]. Optimal information use leads to responses that are better aligned with external stimuli. The mind, seen as an information collection‐cum‐processor [46], is not limited to individuals but may be extended to collective entities, such as nations [56, 57, 58]. In this view, a nation operates as a collective mind that processes information through internalized belief systems. MT's granular perspective helps explain how information units interact to form a mindset, core values deemed essential for the system's persistence [45]. These values influence input filtering and output behavior. As information is assimilated, the mindset evolves, progressively mirroring the system's subjective understanding of reality [59].

Effective food management in the food banks and schools by actors of both food security initiatives is crucial in ensuring optimal food production and comprehensive nutrition strategies for school‐aged children. The off‐school food bank's services and the in‐school meal programs tackle food insecurity among children from a two‐sided approach. The synergy between actors involved in both food security initiatives determines the successful linkage between food banks and school meal programs.

In the current study, successful linkage between both initiatives can be deemed an outcome of the nation's information process. For this synergy to emerge and persist within the mind (i.e., implemented in the country as a program synergy), several conditions need to be met. First, the nation needs to have adequate access to information regarding food banks and school meal programs executed in the country, and second, the nation needs to justify this information as beneficial for the whole system. The multi‐filtering system is mainly based on the mindset to determine whether the information is beneficial, neutral, or costly to the whole system. If the information is deemed beneficial after interacting with the nation's mindset, such as the regulations guiding both initiatives, it will be given a higher probability of being stored and used within the mind (i.e., upheld and implemented in the country). If it is deemed costly, the information will be discarded. If it is deemed neutral, the information will be kept within the mind's buffer zone for later evaluation. Based on this rationale, we have outlined the following Hypotheses (H):

Experts' involvement, that is, nutritionists, farmers, and the private sector actors, is associated with the linkage between food banks and school meal programs.

Community engagement by parents/guardians and community members, the public in general, is associated with the linkage between food banks and school meal programs.

Model Construction

Dataset

This study utilized a dataset of government representatives who managed large‐scale school meal programs in 126 different countries, encompassing geographical areas of the Middle East/North Africa, Sub‐Saharan Africa, South Asia/East Asia/Pacific, Europe/Central Asia/North America, and Latin America/Caribbean, and diverse socioeconomic development levels, such as low‐, lower‐middle‐, upper‐middle, and high‐income countries [60]. The dataset is about the results of a global survey on school meal programs in 2021, which can be accessed publicly at the Global Child Nutrition Foundation (GCNF) Global Survey of School Meal Programs database. Results of the GCNF survey were validated by experts in school nutrition and related fields. This dataset can be accessed freely by researchers worldwide for conducting various studies, supporting policy formulation, and informed decisions in school meal programs. The dataset has been analyzed in several studies of secondary data analysis, aiming at analyzing national policies in supporting food fortification practices [54] and analyzing the impact of supply sourcing on feeding modalities in school meal programs [52], and how these sourcing strategies influence the dietary diversity of school meal supplies [53].

GCNF is a non‐political and non‐profit entity. The GCNF global survey on school feeding was partly funded by the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA). This survey asked about national or large‐scale school feeding programs (or school meal or school nutrition programs), including programs that are managed or administered by the national, regional, or local government, as well as large‐scale school‐based feeding programs that are managed by a non‐governmental entity but in coordination with the national government. It also includes programs that do not involve the government but reach a substantial proportion of students in the country.

A standardized questionnaire was used in data collection. This instrument was developed by GCNF. This survey included 11 sections. Four sections contain national‐level questions, meaning that the respondents only need to complete these sections once for each country. The remaining seven sections contain program‐level questions, meaning that the respondents completed these sections separately for each large‐scale school feeding program in each country. Compared to the 2019 GCNF Global Survey of School Meal Programs, this 2021 global survey gathered updated information regarding (1) the scope of school feeding in each country in the most recently completed school year (2020–2021), (2) government financing of, and involvement in, school feeding, (3) nutrition‐, education‐, and gender‐related aspects of school feeding, (4) agricultural and private sector engagement, (5) related health and sanitation topics, and (6) the impact of emergencies. Among all variables, there were only seven variables employed in the current study's statistical analysis to achieve the study objective (see Table 1).

Data Analysis and Validation

Bayesian Mindsponge Framework (BMF) analytics was employed in the current study for several reasons [55, 59]. First, the analytical method integrates the logical reasoning capabilities of MT with the inferential advantages of Bayesian analysis, exhibiting a high degree of compatibility [59]. Second, Bayesian inference is a statistical approach that treats all the properties (including the known and unknown ones) probabilistically [61, 62], enabling reliable prediction of parsimonious models. Nevertheless, utilizing the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) technique still allows Bayesian analysis to deal effectively with various intricate models, such as multilevel and nonlinear regression frameworks [63]. Third, Bayesian inference has various advantages in comparison to the frequentist approach. One notable advantage is the ability to utilize credible intervals for result interpretation instead of relying solely on the dichotomous decision based on p‐values [64, 65]. BMF has been used and validated in several studies on school meal programs, such as to examine various national policies guiding school meal programs in supporting food fortification practices [54], analyzing the impact of national and international sourcing strategies in enhancing feeding modalities in school meal programs [52], and how these sourcing strategies influence the dietary diversity of school meal supplies [53].

In Bayesian analysis, selecting the appropriate prior is required during the model construction process. Due to the exploratory nature of this study, uninformative priors or a flat prior distribution were used to provide as little prior information as possible for model estimation [66]. The Pareto‐smoothed importance sampling leave‐one‐out (PSIS‐LOO) diagnostics were employed to check the models' goodness of fit [67, 68]. LOO is computed as follows:

ppost(−i)(θ) is the posterior distribution calculated through the data minus data point i. The k‐Pareto values are used in the PSIS method for computing the LOO cross‐validation in the R loo package. Observations with k‐Pareto values greater than 0.7 are often considered influential and problematic for accurately estimating LOO cross‐validation. When a model's k values are less than 0.5, it is typically regarded as being fit.

If the model fits well with the data, we will proceed with the convergence diagnoses and results interpretation. In the current study, we validated the convergence of Markov chains using statistical values and visual illustrations. Statistically, the effective sample size (n_eff) and the Gelman–Rubin shrink factor (Rhat) can be used to assess the convergence. The n_eff value represents the number of iterative samples that are not autocorrelated during stochastic simulation, while the Rhat value is referred to as the potential scale reduction factor [69]. If n_eff is larger than 1000, it is generally considered that the Markov chains are convergent, and the effective samples are sufficient for reliable inference [70]. As for the Rhat value, if the value exceeds 1.1, the model does not converge. The model is considered convergent if Rhat = 1. Visually, the Markov chains' convergence was also validated using trace plots.

The Bayesian analysis was performed on R using the bayesvl open‐access package, version 1.0.0, which provides good visualization capabilities [71]. The code snippets and this statistical analysis were deposited in a public repository (Zenodo) for transparency, public evaluation, and reuse. The data that support the findings of this study are openly available in Zenodo at https://zenodo.org/records/13907906, reference number 13907906.

Discussion

The Bayesian Mindsponge Framework (BMF) was employed to assess the impacts of experts' involvement—such as nutritionists, farmers, and private sector actors—and community engagement, including parents/guardians and community members, on the linkage between food banks and school meal programs. The BMF is useful for this study because it helps clarify that integrated approaches, ranging from national policy‐based programs, school meal programs, to community‐based initiatives, food banks, can support improved food security outcomes among children. The framework also provides nuanced insights into how knowledge, values, and stakeholder perspectives contribute to the success of integrated food security initiatives.

Findings showed that the involvement of nutritionists and the private sector actors was positively related to the linkage between food banks and school meal programs. This finding aligns with Masset & Gelli, who highlighted that school children could benefit from involving nutritionists and the private sector in school feeding programs [72], as these initiatives may improve nutrition, health, education, and food security. In community practice, Pizzi suggests that expert‐driven public health interventions, such as the school meal programs, will help develop community programming and interventions through increased funding or grants from the government or even private donors [73]. Funding is one of the essential components to ensure a sustainable school meal program. Therefore, experts' involvement may address the gaps that arise when traditional school meal systems are disrupted by providing more resources [18, 30, 41].

GITT views this positive relationship as an outcome of the nation's information‐processing system. The information regarding the involvement of nutritionists and the private sector actors in food banks and school meal programs execution is adequately available and accessible to the nation, enabling proper information acceptance and multi‐filtering activities at the collective level. In addition, this information is deemed beneficial for the country, enabling positive results of information benchmarking with the nation's mindset or core values, such as the regulations guiding both food security initiatives. Therefore, this information is accepted, given a higher priority of being stored in the nation's mindset and used by the country for improving the food‐related practices that emerged in food banks and schools. Consequently, the involvement of nutritionists and the private sector actors in both food security initiatives is upheld and implemented in the country to ensure the synergy between food banks and school meal programs.

Findings also revealed an ambiguous relationship between farmers' involvement and the linkage between food banks and school meal programs. This is not aligned with the argument made by Wineman et al., who stated that farmers' participation in school meal programs fosters local food purchases, particularly for school meal supplies [22]. Across the entire food value chain, farmers' initiatives often create new markets for farm products and generate employment opportunities [21]. Evidence from the US related to the Farm‐to‐School programme (FTSP) showed that since the early 2000s, this program has helped bring local fruit and vegetables to schools and associated nutrition‐related programs like school meal programs [74], demonstrating a successful farmer‐school partnership. Moreover, local sourcing is frequently employed to ensure that school menus include a variety of nutrient‐dense foods, addressing both health and nutrition objectives [22, 75]. The World Bank and World Food Programme also emphasize the importance of long‐term, sustainable solutions that leverage local resources and capacity [76]. While many studies support possible positive relationships between farmers' involvement and food security initiatives, the findings of this study open up a new perspective on the complexities of this relationship.

From the mindsponge perspective, GITT considers this ambiguous relationship also as an outcome of the nation's information‐processing system. Farmer's involvement indicates that the existence of information and knowledge provided by farmers can contribute to the implementation of school meal programs in the country. However, the relationship between farmer involvement and the linkage between food banks and school meal programs is ambiguous, suggesting that information and knowledge provided by farmers have either contributed to or negated existing information related to food bank practices or school meal program implementations. In terms of contribution, the information related to farmers' involvement may be buffered until a comprehensive evaluation is conducted by the nation, suggesting a need for comprehensive information from the farmer side to become available and accessible to the nation. Once this information undergoes thorough multi‐layered filtering and evaluation, it is more likely to be recognized as beneficial for the nation, increasing the chances of its adoption and support by the government. In terms of negation, this information is considered unfavorable or deemed costly for the nation, indicating low support from the government for the farmers to be involved in both food security initiatives in the country.

The engagement of parents/guardians and community members also showed an ambiguous relationship with the linkage between food banks and school meal programs. This is not aligned with the argument made by Flores, who reported a positive relationship between school meal programs and parental involvement [77]. Evidence from Brazil even showed that a family farming program managed by parents can reduce ultra‐processed foods in school meals [78], demonstrating a successful parent‐school partnership. Zuercher et al. postulated that parental perception of school meal programs influenced not only their participation or engagement but also their children's participation as students [42]. Evidence has shown that some parents have a negative perception of school meal programs [41]. Communities require full and updated knowledge and information on how their engagement can foster food security among children. Formulating strategies to improve community engagement of parents and others in linking food banks and school meal programs is essential. In addition, policy actions centered around parental or community engagement, food banks, and school meal programs should be increased.

Similar to the ambiguous relationship of farmers' involvement, the information and knowledge provided by parents and the public in general do not adequately affect the processing of information related to food banks and school meal programs. Thus, the relationship with the linkage of food banks and school meal programs is ambiguous. GITT considers this phenomenon to be the need for a thorough assessment of information related to parental and public engagement in general, representing their inadequate involvement in both initiatives. The “mind” at the collective level, or the nations, requires more comprehensive information from the community side to be filtered and evaluated by using its core values, that is, regulations. After further information‐processing activities, there is a possibility that this information may be accepted and become favorable for the nation, so that community engagement in both initiatives may be encouraged and supported by the government to improve the synergy between initiatives. Adequate community engagement in both programs' implementation represents full support for the government's national programs to effectively combat food insecurity among children.

The study findings are useful for existing literature because they highlight the importance of synergizing school meal programs and food banks in the dietary and health improvement strategies for children. Forrestal et al. reported that school meals contribute significantly more to the energy intake and diet quality of food‐insecure and marginally secure students compared to their food‐secure counterparts [79]. Jones et al. highlighted a notable reduction in overweight risk among food‐insecure girls participating in multiple food assistance programs [80]. Holler and Mason, and Ezekekwu et al. reported reductions in food insecurity and improvements in nutrition behaviors in multicomponent interventions [80, 81]. Targeted access and utilization of school meal programs among the vulnerable population is also essential. Mcloughlin et al. noted that access to school meals and community food resources is critical, especially for children in high‐poverty, minority, and urban populations [82]. The study also reported that program utilization was highest among food‐insecure populations, emphasizing the importance of targeted outreach and flexible delivery mechanisms. Overall, the findings from this study add to the literature, highlighting the potential for synergy between school meal programs and food banks, and underscoring the possible value of expert involvement and theory‐driven approaches in designing effective interventions.

This study contributes uniquely to food security research by exploring experts and community engagement in child‐focused nutrition incentives, specifically evaluating the synergy between food banks and school meal initiatives for program effectiveness. We emphasize the importance of social networks and human capital in program success. By assessing how stakeholder engagement predicts and enhances the linkage between food banks and school meal programs, this study presents a nuanced, collective‐level analysis, largely absent from previous studies. It may inform how specific stakeholder participation influences program synergy and long‐term sustainability.

This study is not without limitations. The first major limitation is the dataset. This secondary data analysis utilized a dataset from the global survey of school feeding. There were 126 government representatives who managed large‐scale school meal programs who participated in the survey, revealing how the implementation of school meal programs in their respective countries. In this dataset, countries were not classified according to the standards of the World Bank, as the data of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) was not available, which may influence the presence and operation of food banks, and other food security initiatives within the country. There was also no data on the specific location of food banks among countries, whether they were available in the urban or rural areas only, which may influence the linkage between food banks and school meal programs; potential to be a confounding variable.

In addition, there are certain differences in how food security initiatives are structured among each country's categories based on their GDPs. For instance, the National School Lunch Program (NSLP) in the United States (US) is the most extensive anti‐hunger program besides the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program. In 2019, NSLP successfully served US students almost 15 million breakfasts and 30 million lunches daily for free [83]. In addition, the commodity surplus has led the US government to conduct a food donation program for food banks guided by federal policies, supporting food justice for all citizens [84, 85]. It is more likely to see effective program synergy in the US as resources and regulations are well‐established. In comparison, the Student Nutrition Improvement Program (SNIP) in China has successfully served 40 million students in 1762 counties across 29 provinces in 2020 [86]. However, the poverty amid growth was the reason for the Chinese government to establish food banks [87], not the commodity surplus. There is a possibility to observe various extents to which food banks link with SNIP in China due to resource limitations. It is important to acknowledge the diversity and complexities of issues influencing the linkage between food banks and school meal programs in this cross‐country analysis.

The second limitation is that the applied cross‐sectional design in the primary survey generated the dataset, making it unable to measure how the variables' values under investigation changed over time. While a particular scenario may be shown in this study to illustrate a trend of events, it might not depict the dynamic shifts in the situation on the ground. The survey that is being used is designed to be self‐reporting. For measuring variables, it could be less objective. Understanding the impacts of community engagement and experts' involvement on the linkage between food banks and school meal programs to enhance food security among children requires a qualitative study using in‐depth interviews with all school meal program actors.

Conclusions

The experts' involvement has a significant positive association with the linkage between food banks and school meal programs among implementing countries. The successful linkage between food banks and school meal programs relies heavily on the involvement of nutritionists and private sector stakeholders. In contrast, the involvement of farmers and the engagement of parents and the public in general show ambiguous relationships with this linkage. Therefore, it is essential to formulate strategies to increase the effects of farmers' involvement and community engagement on the linkage between food banks and school meal programs. Further investigation is also needed to determine the parental perception of school meal programs and possible moderating variables on the relationship between farmers' involvement and the linkage between both food security initiatives to combat food insecurity among children successfully. Effective strategies for improving farmers' involvement and community engagement in both initiatives are also needed to clarify further their relationships with the linkage between food banks and school meal programs in implementing countries.

Practical Implications

This study offers valuable insights for various stakeholders involved in successful synergy between school meal programs and food banks to combat food insecurity among children. The findings highlight the pivotal role of expert involvement—particularly nutritionists and private sector stakeholders—in strengthening the linkage between food banks and school meal programs. For nutritionists, this underscores the need to actively design menus, ensure nutritional adequacy, and provide evidence‐based guidance that aligns donated food with children's dietary needs. Their expertise can transform food bank contributions into balanced, health‐promoting school meals. Private sector actors, including food companies and distributors, are encouraged to formalize partnerships with schools and food banks, offering logistical support, surplus food, and innovative supply chain solutions that enhance sustainability.

For farmers, the ambiguous relationship suggests opportunities to clarify and strengthen their roles. Direct farm‐to‐school initiatives, cooperative models, and transparent procurement systems could help farmers contribute more effectively to the linkage between food banks and school meal programs. Parents and the public, while showing mixed associations, remain crucial in shaping demand and accountability. Awareness campaigns and participatory school committees can channel their engagement into constructive oversight and advocacy.

Schools serve as the operational hub, where coordination between food banks, nutritionists, and private partners materializes into daily meals. Strengthening school‐level management capacity and fostering openness to multi‐stakeholder collaboration are essential. Food banks should prioritize structured partnerships, aligning donations with school meal requirements rather than ad hoc distributions. NGOs can act as facilitators, bridging gaps between stakeholders, piloting innovative models, and advocating for inclusive policies.

Finally, government agencies play a decisive role in institutionalizing these linkages. By setting clear guidelines, incentivizing private sector participation, and integrating nutritionists into policy frameworks, governments can ensure consistency and scalability. For the public at large, the implication is to view food banks not merely as charitable outlets but as integral components of national food security and child nutrition systems. Collective responsibility, guided by expert involvement, is key to sustaining effective linkages between food banks and school meal programs.

Policy Recommendations

The national policies guiding, supporting, and protecting the involvement of nutritionists and the private sector actors need to be strengthened and increased in terms of policy actions. Strengthening monitoring and evaluation efforts on their involvement is crucial for ensuring the successful linkage of food banks and school feeding initiatives. Implementing a universally recognized monitoring framework with standardized indicators across program areas can facilitate this process. Formulating policies to increase the involvement of farmers, parents, and the general public is crucial to clarify their influence in supporting the synergy between food banks and school meal programs.

At the national level, measures should be taken to ensure that those responsible for executing school meal programs and food banks are familiar with relevant policies. Additionally, implementation support should be provided as needed. It is essential to equip schools with the resources to avoid reliance on external parties while fostering participation and commitment within the community for further reinforcement. Collaboration between external and internal stakeholders, along with efforts to strengthen cross‐sectoral relationships, should be prioritized. Training tailored to the specific needs of stakeholders and their interactions will also enhance program effectiveness.

Sufficient funding for food banks and school meal programs is essential for the effective execution of relevant policies in and out of the classroom. To increase food availability for these programs, community networks across the food supply chain, such as domestic in‐kind donations as a manifestation of community engagement in both initiatives, should be developed. Ultimately, building strong relationships between schools and their communities will enhance food security among children, supporting policies that promote their access to nutritious food through food banks and school meal programs.
