Pedoturbation matters beyond soil science
Authors: Hao Long, Aimin Zhang, Fei Yang, Ganlin Zhang
Published: 2026

Main text

Soils are often conceptualized as horizontally layered and slowly evolving systems, in which material properties change primarily through in situ processes such as organic matter decomposition, mineral weathering, and horizon differentiation. Within this traditional framework, soil horizon structure is typically treated as quasi-static, and deviations from stratigraphic order are often regarded as noise introduced by biological or physical disturbance. Recent advances, however, challenge this long-standing view by demonstrating that pedoturbation (soil mixing)—particularly the vertical redistribution of soil particles and organic matter—is not a secondary complication but a fundamental process shaping how soils function, record environmental change, and interact with the Earth system.1,2,3

The broader significance of pedoturbation has deep intellectual roots.4 As early as the late 19th century, in Darwin’s final scientific work, the humble activity of earthworms was recognized as capable of reshaping landscapes over geological timescales, highlighting the power of continuous biological transport to generate planetary-scale geomorphic effects.5 Today, new analytical advances are forcing a reappraisal of this old insight. Luminescence signals from mineral grains permit quantitative constraints on the depth structure of soil mixing,1,3 while radiocarbon measurements of organic matter offer a complementary tracer of vertical transport integrated over biogeochemical timescales.2 Together, these tools expose a critical disconnect between how soils actually function and how they are represented in numerical models spanning carbon cycling, landscape evolution, and paleoenvironmental reconstruction. Here, we examine this disconnect and its far-reaching implications through the lens of recent advances.

The physical rules of mixing: Depth-dependent pedoturbation

Pedoturbation encompasses a suite of processes—including bioturbation by roots and fauna, cryoturbation, shrink-swell dynamics, and other episodic disturbances (Figure 1)—that collectively redistribute soil constituents in the vertical dimension.5 Importantly, these processes do not operate uniformly with depth. Biological activity, energy availability, and physical forcing typically decline downward, producing a characteristic decrease in mixing intensity with depth rather than an abrupt cutoff. Direct empirical validation of this depth dependence has been demonstrated via novel luminescence-based profiling.1 Because luminescence signals record the exposure-burial history of mineral grains, they serve as highly sensitive, diagnostic indicators of vertical particle migration induced by pedoturbation.3 For instance, recent luminescence-based quantification from a Mollisol profile in Northeast China reveals that while the upper 80 cm of the soil body is actively undergoing mixing, the intensity peaks at the surface and systematically diminishes downward3—a mathematical scaling pattern remarkably consistent across diverse global climate zones.1 The observed consistency of depth-dependent mixing across diverse climatic and ecological settings suggests that pedoturbation follows general physical-biological rules rather than being an idiosyncratic feature of specific soils. This insight reframes soils as vertically dynamic systems in which structure emerges from continuous redistribution, not simple accumulation.

The biogeochemical consequences: Rethinking radiocarbon ages

The implications of pedoturbation become even more consequential when soil carbon is considered. Radiocarbon (14C) measurements are commonly interpreted as indicators of carbon stability, with older ages at depth taken to imply slower turnover and long-term sequestration. However, in a vertically mixed soil, 14C ages primarily integrate the time required for organic carbon to be transported downward and the duration it resides at a given depth rather than the intrinsic resistance of that carbon to decomposition.2 Vertical transport—mostly mediated by the same pedoturbation processes that mix mineral grains—moves carbon in both directions: it can deliver relatively young carbon to deep horizons while simultaneously exposing older carbon to surface conditions that enhance decomposition. When such transport is ignored, models tend to portray deep soil carbon as inert. In fact, neglecting vertical transport may cause Earth system models to significantly underestimate the dynamic turnover rate of deep soil carbon, thereby erroneously treating deep horizons as a permanent, long-term carbon sink rather than a highly dynamic carbon source under future warming.2 The recognition that 14C ages encode migration-residence histories rather than simple stability fundamentally alters how soil carbon data should be interpreted and how soil-climate feedbacks are assessed.

Implications for the Earth system: Carbon, archives, and models

The significance of pedoturbation extends far beyond pedology. First, it directly affects global carbon cycle assessments. Deep soils host a substantial fraction of terrestrial carbon stocks, and their response to warming depends critically on vertical transport rates. Misrepresenting pedoturbation therefore propagates uncertainty into predictions of future atmospheric CO2 concentrations.

Second, pedoturbation influences how environmental signals are archived in soils. Climate shifts, vegetation changes, and anthropogenic impacts recorded in soils are filtered through depth-dependent mixing. The significance of this filtering effect depends strongly on timescale. Environmental signals operating over periods shorter than characteristic mixing times may remain relatively well preserved, whereas centennial-to-millennial scale variations can become progressively smoothed or redistributed by ongoing pedoturbation. As a result, the assumption that soil profiles represent static, layered archives becomes increasingly problematic over long timescales. Without accounting for this internal filter, paleoenvironmental reconstructions risk conflating external forcing with soil-intrinsic dynamics.

Third, pedoturbation has direct implications for Earth system and land surface models, which increasingly underpin policy-relevant projections. Most current models employ simplified representations of vertical transport or neglect it entirely below the topsoil. Incorporating depth-dependent pedoturbation offers a mechanistic pathway to reconcile observed soil carbon profiles, isotopic gradients, and model predictions. However, a key challenge in scaling from site-level observations to regional or global models lies in accounting for spatial and temporal heterogeneity. Spatially, pedoturbation regimes can be modulated by topographic settings; for instance, contrasting mixing rates and depths are expected between erosional hillslopes and depositional depressions, which significantly alter local carbon stabilization. Temporally, pedoturbation is far from a steady-state process. Its intensity varies across multiple timescales, from long-term glacial-interglacial climate shifts to rapid, transient pulses driven by anthropogenic land-use change, such as agricultural tillage. Failing to capture these transient dynamics and steady-state mixing limits our ability to accurately predict long-term soil carbon storage capacity under fluctuating environmental forcing.

Finally, pedoturbation connects soil science to ecology, geomorphology, and Earth’s critical zone research. The depth distribution of roots, fauna, and physical disturbance links ecosystem structure to subsurface material fluxes, positioning pedoturbation as a unifying process across traditionally separate disciplines.

From noise to signal: A process-based view of soils

A key message emerging from recent work is that pedoturbation should no longer be treated as random noise that obscures stratigraphy but rather as a quantifiable signal of ecosystem functioning. Luminescence methods constrain the intensity and depth structure of soil mixing, while radiocarbon integrates the migration-residence history of organic carbon shaped by that mixing. Together, these tracers provide complementary windows into soil system dynamics. Recognizing pedoturbation as a first-order process invites a broader conceptual shift: soils are not merely layered archives but vertically dynamic transport-reaction systems. To bridge the remaining gap between these observational insights and numerical model implementation, land surface components—including dynamic global vegetation models and soil biogeochemical models—must transition toward process-based representations of soil mixing. This can be practically achieved by introducing a depth-dependent vertical transport coefficient (e.g., a diffusion-advection framework parameterized by luminescence constraints) directly into soil physics modules. Furthermore, coupling these tracer-derived mixing rates with existing carbon turnover and microbial modules will allow models to explicitly simulate how physical particle movement competes with metabolic decomposition. Embracing this view will improve interpretations of soil age data, reduce uncertainty in carbon cycle projections, and strengthen links between soil processes and Earth system dynamics.

Concluding remarks

Pedoturbation matters—not only for understanding soils but for interpreting biogeochemical cycles, reconstructing environmental change, and predicting future climate feedbacks. By explicitly accounting for the depth-dependent and spatio-temporally heterogeneous nature of soil mixing, recent studies challenge entrenched assumptions and open new avenues for interdisciplinary research. To elevate this conceptual shift into an actionable research agenda, three interdisciplinary priorities merit particular attention: (1) can we construct a universal pedoturbation function parameterized by readily available macro-scale climatic, topographic, and ecological variables? (2) How can quantitative representations of depth-dependent soil mixing be efficiently integrated and computationally optimized within the next generation of land surface models? (3) Regarding the architecture of deep soil organic matter, what are the respective, competing roles of stochastic physical disturbance events versus continuous diffusive biological mixing? Incorporating dynamic, depth-dependent pedoturbation into the core of Earth system thinking is therefore not an incremental refinement but a necessary step toward a more realistic representation of the living skin of the planet.
