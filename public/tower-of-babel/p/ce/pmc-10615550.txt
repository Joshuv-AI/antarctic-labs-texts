Blood coagulation, risk factors and associated complications in COVID-19 patients in Saudi Arabia: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Haifa Mohammed Al Nafea, Mohammed Tahani Al-Qahtani, Farjah Hassan Al Gahtani, Hajera Tabassum
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
A good understanding of the possible risk factors for coronavirus disease 19 (COVID-19) severity could help clinicians in identifying patients who need prioritized treatment to prevent disease progression and adverse outcomes. COVID-19-linked coagulopathy is one of the life-threatening severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 infections. Growing evidence indicates a correlation between abnormal coagulation and increased risk of venous thromboembolism; in COVID-19-infected patients, yet a clear understanding of the role of coagulopathy in the severity of COVID-19 illness is still unresolved. This retrospective cohort study was thus undertaken to investigate the role of coagulation dysfunction with COVID-19 mortality/severity. Blood samples from 1000 hospitalized patients with COVID-19 pneumonia were collected. The study participants were both male and female in equal ratios with a mean age of 48.94. Patients were followed-up until discharge either for recovery or death. All biochemical investigations-complete blood count and coagulation profile including D-dimers, prothrombin time, partial prothrombin time, and international normalized ratio was performed in COVID-19 survivors and in non-survivors admitted in intensive care unit. In the survivor group, all coagulation parameters were within normal limits, and 8.7% had a low red blood count. The most common risk factors associated with COVID-19 patients were diabetes mellitus (2.8%), hypertension (10.8%), and heart disease (3%). In the non-survivor group, the coagulation parameters were above the normal range (prothrombin in 31.5%, PTT in 10.5%, international normalized ratio in 26.3%, D-dimer in 36.8%) with thrombocytopenia in 21.04% of patients. Other complications were pulmonary embolism in 21.05% and venous thromboembolism in 15.7% of non-survivors. A significant association was found between increased markers of coagulopathy and the severity of SARS-CoV2 infection. Furthermore, the severity of infection was observed to increase with risk factors such as age, heart disease, hypertension, and DM eventually affecting COVID-19 prognosis and mortality.

1. Introduction

World is under pandemic stress due to an increased mortality rate owing to the outbreak of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2 or 2019-nCoV). At the dawn of the pandemic though, severe forms of coronavirus disease 19 (COVID-19) are complicated by thromboembolism. As of May 16, 2022, there have been 519,105,112 confirmed cases of COVID-19 reported to World Health Organization guidelines, with 6266,324 fatalities. 11,660,363,722 vaccine doses had been delivered as of May 14, 2022.[1] Mortality rates in the KSA was around 1.7% comparatively lower than in other countries due to strict precautionary measures and effective lockdown implemented during the outbreak. Whilst the effective measures, the developed countries with the best healthcare facilities have even complied with the unprecedented damage and mortality caused by COVID-19. The symptoms of patients infected with SARS-CoV-2 range from minimal signs to severe respiratory failure with multiple organ failure.[2] COVID-19 patients may be asymptomatic. Fever, dry cough, and shortness of breath are common symptoms. Besides these, patients suffer from gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, stomach discomfort, diarrhea, and neurological symptoms such as anosmia, hyposmia, and dysgeusia.[3] Multiorgan complications in SARS-CoV-2 patients include but are not limited to cardiac injury, acute respiratory distress syndrome, arrhythmias, thromboses, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), infarcts, and secondary infections been studied in these patients.[4]

Owing to COVID-19’s extremely high mortality rate, there is an urgent need to identify the clinical features associated with its progression. Greater attention is drawn toward investigating risk factors and exploring the pathophysiology underlying this disease. Of interest are the coagulation markers as coagulopathy is increasingly gaining significance in the etiology of the Sars-CoV2 infection. While the underlying pulmonary pathophysiology remains incompletely unresolved, presumably severe COVID-19 infection is found associated with a marked alveolar inflammatory cell infiltrate, and cytokine storm response causing vascular endothelial damage, activation of the coagulation system, and inhibiting the fibrinolytic and anticoagulating systems.[5–7] Disseminated intravascular coagulation can result due to excessive thrombosis in the microvascular system, microcirculatory disorder, and consequently serious multiple organ dysfunction syndrome. Moreover, emerging data suggest that severe COVID-19 is also associated with a significantly increased risk of developing venous thromboembolism (VTE) including deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE).[8,9] Consequently, early detection and correction of coagulation dysfunction could effectively reduce mortality. A number of potential indicators like coagulation markers including D-dimers (DD), and Troponin may constitute prognostic tools that could eventually lead to the development of point-of-care testing for this disease.

The coagulopathy associated with COVID-19 is characterized by mild thrombocytopenia, slight prolongation of the prothrombin time, high DD, elevated fibrinogen levels, factor VIII, and Von Willebrand factor. The levels of DD, a breakdown product of cross-linked fibrin, correlate with disease severity and predict the risk of thrombosis, the need for ventilatory support, and mortality.[10] Higher mortality with increased age and coagulopathy have also been reported.[11] Similarly, abnormal coagulation elevated DD and fibrin degradation product, was reported common in deaths with novel coronavirus pneumonia.[12] Coagulopathy and thrombotic events are serious complications that occur in some COVID-19 patients. However, whether coagulopathy is linked to the severity of COVID-19 illness is unknown. Further, the role of coagulopathy in COVID-19 and its relation to death and severity have been poorly understood.

The current study thus aims to identify the association of coagulopathy with COVID-19 mortality/severity and study the correlation between VTE/EP and Covid-19 mortality/intensive care unit (ICU) admission. Furthermore, assessment of these biomarkers in point-of care testing could be useful for COVID-19 patients triaging, reassurance and follow-up management.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and patient population

This retrospective cohort study was carried out in the Department of Clinical Laboratory Sciences, College of Applied Medical Sciences, King Saud University, in collaboration with King Khalid University Hospital, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. One thousand patients were diagnosed with SARS-CoV-2 from January 2020 to January 2022. All patients were analyzed according to the “pneumonia diagnosis protocol for novel coronavirus infection (trial version 5).” They were subjected to tests including clinical examination, computed tomography, and real-time reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction for SARS-CoV-2. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board College of Medicine, King Saud University Medical City, Saudi Arabia (Study Approval number E-20–4832). The information of patients participating in the study is treated with strict confidentiality and undeclared.

2.1.1. Definitions

Coagulopathy is a bleeding disorder resulting from derangement of hemostasis resulting in either excessive bleeding or clotting. Thrombocytopenia is defined as a platelet count of <150,000 per cubic millimeter. VTE is a condition that occurs when a blood clot forms in a vein. VTE includes DVT and PE. A pulmonary embolism is a blocked blood vessel in the lungs.

2.1.2. Inclusion criteria

COVID-19-positive patients aged > 16 years admitted to hospital/ICU/ward.

2.1.3. Exclusion criteria

Patients with a family history of VTE or history of recent VTE (3 months), on anticoagulation therapy, extended major pelvic or abdominal surgery (e.g., cesarean, hysterectomy), history of superficial phlebitis.

2.2. Data collection and laboratory method

Data on coagulation parameters were obtained from all 1000 confirmed COVID-19 patients. Real-time reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction for SARS-CoV-2 was performed according to guidelines from the World Health Organization guidelines (2021) using a SARS-CoV-2 detection kit (Bioperfectus, Taizhou, China).

Demographic characteristics including age, gender, comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, an renal disease) and laboratory investigations including complete blood count (CBC) including white blood count (WBC), red blood count (RBC), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), hemoglobin (Hgb) and Coagulation profile including activated partial thromboplastin time, prothrombin time (PT), international normalized ratio (INR), and DD levels were retrieved from electronic patient records. All biochemical investigations were performed using an SYSMEX and Stago STA Compact Max Coagulation Analyzer respectively using proprietary reagents. Patients were followed-up until discharge either for recovery or death.

2.3. Statistical analysis

Qualitative variables are presented using frequency distribution and percentages. Quantitative variables are presented using the mean and the standard deviation if they are normally distributed or the median and the interquartile range if they follow a non-normal distribution. The comparison between qualitative variables was analyzed using the chi-squared test. Data was analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 24; IBM Corp).

3.1. Coagulation profile

Table 3 depicts the coagulation profile between COVID-19 survivors and non-survivors. Assessment of the coagulation profile demonstrated a normal range of PT, INR, DD, and partial thromboplastin time in a majority of COVID-19 patients. Among the non-survivor group, an increase of PT in 31.5%, PTT in 10.5%, DD in 36.8%, and INR in 26.3% was observed (Fig. 1). Intriguingly, levels of PT, PTT, INR, and D-dimer varied significantly between the 2 groups (P < .05). Notably, levels of PT were higher in non-survivors compared to the survivor (P < .001). Increased percentages of PT, PTT, and INR were observed in non-survivors compared to the survivors group (P < .01). Further, the non-survivor group exhibited higher levels of the D-dimers (P < .01) reflecting a strong association between DD and increased COVID mortality.

4. Discussion

The main hallmark finding of the current report was the abnormal coagulation observed in non-survivors and patients admitted to ICU compared to COVID-19 survivors. In the non-survivor group, the coagulation parameters were above the normal range (PT in 31.5%, PTT in 10.5%, INR in 26.3%, DD in 36.8%) with thrombocytopenia in 21.04% of patients and with complications-PE in 21.05%, and VTE in 15.7%. A significant association was found between increased markers of coagulopathy and the severity of SARS-CoV2 infection.

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, more than 81% of COVID-19 deaths have occurred in individuals over 65 years. In the current report, 94.7% of deaths and patients infected with COVID (44.8%) were aged above 50. This is in line with previous reports. Adults over the age of 50 were reported to have a higher chance of developing severe COVID-19.[13]

Altered levels of WBC, RBC, ESR, RDV, MCV, Hgb, and platelets were in the normal range in the majority of the COVID-19 survivors. The levels of these parameters varied significantly with non-survivors (P < .05). In non-survivors, 21.05% of patients had RBCs below the normal range, RDW low in 36.8% of patients, Hgb low in 31.5% of patients, and an increase in WBC in 21.05%, the platelet was below average range in 21.05% patients. The data obtained is in accordance with the previous findings.[14,15] Contrarily, in a study in Iraq by Al-Saadi et al[16], 2022 subtle changes were recorded in the hematological data in COVID infection, nevertheless with an altered coagulation profile as observed here.

Amongst the various studies undertaken to evaluate the severity of COVID-19 and biomarkers, IFCC Guidelines on COVID-19 strongly suggested DD testing in COVID-19 patients. An increased correlation between the severity and outcome of COVID-19 with increased levels of DD was reported.[17,18] Baroiu et al[19], 2022 reported a strong correlation between DD and COVID-19 severity. Hui Long et al[20], 2020 investigated the role of coagulation indicators like DD, PT, and PTT, in predicting the severity and prognosis of COVID-19. About 43.5% (50/115 patients) with abnormal levels (>0.55 mg/L), in the first investigation of DD, was reported. Of the 28 critically ill patients, 17 were > 0.55 mg/L, accounting for 60.7%. (17/25), and 14 cases had 2 times more than the normal reference value. Hence, early identification of the severity is very important to the clinical diagnosis and treatment of COVID-19.[14] Severe and critically ill COVID-19 patients mostly succumbed to coagulation dysfunction. COVID-19-related coagulopathy had been evidenced by numerous studies. An increase in D-dimer around 3.75% to 68.0% was reported in COVID-19 patients.[21] D-dimer > 1 μg/mL is considered one of the risk factors for mortality in adults in patients with COVID-19.[11] Intriguingly, elevated levels of DD were reported in severe COVID-19 illness compared to mild infection emphasizing its prognostic value in COVID-19 infection.[19]

Parallel to CBC, an altered coagulation profile was observed between the 2 groups. Here, we found that elevated coagulation parameters are associated with poor prognosis, with an increase in PT (31.5%), PTT (10.5%), INR (26.3%), and DD (36.8%) among non-survivors. In the same line, we observed lower platelet counts in (21.05%) of non-survivors. Elevated levels of these factors observed in the present study are similar to previous findings. Tang et al[12], 2020 in a retrospective study in China similarly reviewed the coagulation profile between survivors and non-survivors of Sars-CoV2 infected patients with higher PT, PTT, and DD observed in the non-survivors group. Recently, Buffart et al[22], 2022 observed COVID-19 -related coagulopathy is marked by significant alteration in the hypercoagulable thrombin generation test profile. A significant variation in DD and thrombin time between mild and severely infected COVID patients has also been reported.[23] More recently, in a study in Poland, an increased risk of mortality was reported with an increase in DD levels in COVID patients.[24] To note, coagulation marker- DD has been suggested as a vital prognostic tool in point-of-care testing in Sars CoV2 infection.[25] Intriguingly, data on coagulation profile in patients admitted to ICU varied and reported to have higher DD between the severe group admitted to ICU and the mild group. The finding of our study concurs with Wang et al[26], 2020.

The COVID mortality rate is influenced by associated risk factors. Risk factors include older age and underlying medical diseases, such as heart disease, blood disorders, hypertension, cancer, diabetes Miletus, and renal failure.[27] DM has been linked to an increased risk of severe COVID-19 infection. In the present study, the most common comorbidity was DM-12.8% and hypertension −10.8% of COVID-19 patients. In the non-survivor group, hypertension was 47.3%, DM in 42.1% and heart disease in 26.3%. Notably, type 1 diabetes leading to a higher risk of COVID-19 has been reported.[28] The increased risk of comorbidity observed here was similar to the finding of Zhou et al[11], 2020 where comorbidity was reported in 91 (48%) of the patients, with hypertension being the most common (30%), DM (19%), and coronary heart disease (8%) of infected patients.

SARS-CoV-2 infection has been linked to an increased risk of VTE, which occurs in roughly 20% of COVID-19 patients and is more prevalent in critically sick patients.[26] Research from a tertiary care hospital in France investigated cases of PE in 106 confirmed COVID-19 patients. PE was discovered in 30% of 106 individuals. Patients with PE had greater D-dimer levels than those who tested negative.[29] In a study of 7 patients who had VTE in a German autopsy series of 12 consecutive COVID-19 fatalities, PE was deemed the direct cause of death in 4.[30] A study of 21 autopsies from the United States found a comparable high prevalence of pulmonary microthrombi, and severe pulmonary thromboembolism was a common fatal condition.[31] The Complications in our study of COVID-19 patients were PE in (1.6%) and VTE in (2.6%). The prevalence of PE (21.05%) and VTE (15.7%) for COVID-19 patients appeared to be in the higher range compared with patients admitted to ICU for other disease conditions.

Data obtained in the present study reported a significant correlation between coagulation dysfunction and COVID-19 severity or mortality. Furthermore, risk factors including increasing age and preexisting chronic conditions (DM, heart disease, and hypertension) identified were found significantly associated with mortality from confirmed COVID-19 infection. Abnormally increased coagulation parameters in COVID-19 patients lead to complications such as VTE and PE reflecting a strong association between coagulation dysfunction and increased COVID-19 severity.

5. Conclusion

Thus, it is conclusive that severe COVID-19 infection is associated with coagulopathy that correlates with a poor prognosis. Moreover, the risk factors such as older age, heart disease, hypertension, and diabetes are associated with severe illness in COVID-19 patients and increased mortality.

6. Study limitations

The findings are limited to the accuracy of record keeping, given its retrospective design. The correctness of overlapping comorbidities cannot be established other than what is available in the checklist of medical history and medications taken based on hospital records. Further studies to explore the mechanisms of this coagulopathy are required, including an analysis of the fibrinolytic system and clotting factor. Another limitation of the study was the detailed demographic characteristics and the effect of underlying treatment of high-risk patients with anticoagulants for example who have heparin for DVT or Warfarin for prior mechanical heart valve were not analyzed, the data of which could add to the clear elucidation of the role of COVID-19 induced coagulation in presence of anticoagulation therapy.

7. Recommendation

Assessment of the coagulation profile and risk for VTE and PE is highly recommended. These tests at the point-of care testing could be useful for COVID-19 patients triaging, reassurance, and follow-up management. In addition, it might be crucial to identify patients who should be admitted to hospitals meant for COVID-19 treatment and further beneficial for determining disease progression and prognosis.
