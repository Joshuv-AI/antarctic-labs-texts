Choice of colon capsule or colonoscopy versus default colonoscopy in FIT positive patients in the Danish screening programme: a parallel group randomised controlled trial
Authors: Gunnar Baatrup, Thomas Bjørsum-Meyer, Lasse Kaalby, Benedicte Schelde-Olesen, Morten Kobaek-Larsen, Anastasios Koulaouzidis, Rasmus Kroijer, Issam Al-Najami, Niels Buch, Anders Høgh, Niels Qvist, Marianne Kirstine Thygesen, Ulrik Deding, on behalf of the CareForColon2015 study group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColonoscopy is among the standard tests for colorectal cancer (CRC) screening. However, uptake varies, and alternatives such as colon capsule endoscopy (CCE) are available. The uptake and detection rate of clinically significant neoplasia with CCE, compared with colonoscopy, remain unclear in this setting.ObjectiveThe primary objective of this study was to compare the detection rates of advanced neoplasia between CCE and colonoscopy, using a pathway in which the study group could choose between the two procedures, while the control group was offered only colonoscopy.DesignA randomised, intention-to-treat trial was conducted among Danish CRC screening participants who tested positive with a faecal immunochemical test (FIT). The trial compared the detection rate of advanced neoplasia (primary outcome) and the uptake rate of both approaches between the two arms.ResultsA total of 473 684 invitations were sent to 396 676 individuals, with 62.6% returning the test. Among them, 11 075 tests were positive (4.5%), with no significant differences between the two study groups. Among FIT-positive cases, the uptake for colonoscopy was 91.1% in the control arm and 91.7% in the study arm, where participants had a choice of methods. In the study arm, 45.8% preferred CCE, 11.4% preferred colonoscopy and 42.8% had no preference and underwent colonoscopy. Ultimately, 69.9% of patients who initially opted for CCE were later referred for colonoscopy. The rate of advanced neoplasia detection was similar between the groups: 0.67% in the study arm versus 0.64% in the control arm.ConclusionOffering CCE as an alternative to colonoscopy did not significantly alter the detection rate of advanced neoplasia, nor did it increase uptake in a screening programme with high adherence to colonoscopy following a positive FIT test. Instead, it led to a very high rate of secondary colonoscopies. Therefore, CCE cannot be recommended in this setting.Trial registration numberNCT04049357 (ClinicalTrials.gov)Keywords: COLORECTAL CANCER, SCREENING, COLORECTAL ADENOMAS, DIAGNOSTIC AND THERAPEUTIC ENDOSCOPY

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Several published trials, reviews and meta-analyses have evaluated/described the performance of second-generation colon capsule endoscopy (CCE). It is being realised that resection of diminutive polyps does not change the patients’ short- or long-term outcomes. Previous studies evaluating CCE using colonoscopy as the reference standard have been criticised due to interval cancer rates after colonoscopy, leading to falsely low specificity and accuracy rates of CCE.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Based on our study, CCE is a safe and accurate alternative to colonoscopy. Artificial intelligence algorithms can enhance quality and reduce costs for specific patient groups with a low frequency of positive findings needing therapy, such as those undergoing colorectal cancer screening, without a faecal immunochemical test (FIT) triage. In the setting of FIT-based screening programmes, CCE does not seem to be a cost-efficient or patient-preferable alternative to colonoscopy given the rate of reinvestigations from the current trial.

Introduction

Colonoscopy is recognised for its high efficacy, sensitivity and low complication rate, and is widely accepted as the reference standard for colonic investigations; it is the method of choice in stool-test-based screening programmes once these tests are returned positive. Despite significant investment, the capacity for colonoscopies is being stretched in many countries. Additionally, the fear of discomfort, anxiety about the procedure’s invasive nature and potential complications are consistently cited as barriers to participation.1,8 Notably, approximately 8–10% of Danish screening participants with a positive faecal immunochemical test (FIT) for occult blood do not proceed with the recommended colonoscopy.9 10 In other countries, these rates may vary: 14% in Spain,11 15% in Italy,12 13% in Ireland,13 but 69% in China;14 furthermore, it may correlate with several factors such as sex, age and socioeconomic status.15 This suggests a need to look for potentially easier and more attractive alternatives for colon imaging.

Colon capsule endoscopy (CCE) is a potential alternative for selected patient groups; it is minimally invasive but lacks therapeutic capabilities. Therefore, it must be followed by a colonoscopy if polypectomy or biopsies are required. A CCE service can be established with minimal capital investment in out-of-hospital facilities.16 17 Despite its enhanced safety profile and non-invasive nature compared with colonoscopy, CCE has not been widely adopted in routine diagnostics of neoplastic disease,18 19 and its attractiveness when offered broadly is not known. The test sensitivity for colorectal cancers (CRCs) and advanced adenomas remains a significant concern when considering the implementation of CCE in clinical practice. Another concern is the high number of patients who will require a follow-up colonoscopy after CCE due to either an incomplete investigation or the detection of adenomas that necessitate resection. This has implications for patient preference and healthcare economics.

The primary objective of this multicentre randomised trial was to compare the detection rate of clinically significant, advanced neoplasia using optional CCE as a filter test before colonoscopy, versus primary colonoscopy in a large high-risk population. The results are presented both as the intention to treat outcome and as the per protocol outcome.

Methods

The Danish CRC screening programme

The national CRC screening programme in Denmark extends biennial invitations to all citizens aged 50–74 since 2014. A FIT is used to quantify haemoglobin in the stool. Measurements were performed using OC-Sensor Pledia instruments (EIKEN, Japan). The analytical coefficients of variation for FIT are: 3%, 4% and 3% at levels 13 µg hgb/g faeces, 22 µg hgb/g faeces and 29 µg hgb/g faeces, respectively. Citizens receive an invitation letter along with a FIT kit and instructions. The return of the FIT sample is deemed as acceptance of participation. If the FIT result is positive (≥20 µg hgb/g faeces), the participants are invited for a diagnostic colonoscopy within 14 days, free of charge, including bowel preparation. All observed polyps are removed during the initial colonoscopy or referred for advanced resection at a subsequent expert endoscopy session. Suspected cancers are biopsied. Diminutive (<6 mm) polyps in the rectum and sigmoid colon, of hyperplastic appearance, may be left behind. The following definitions are used in the Danish screening programme to determine treatment: non-neoplastic polyps (not registered), low-risk adenomas (<3 adenomas, smaller than 10 mm), intermediate-risk adenomas (3–4 adenomas<10 mm or one adenoma 10–19 mm, or more than 25% villous histology or high-grade dysplasia), high-risk adenomas (more than four adenomas or one adenoma>19 mm, or piecemeal resection). Following polypectomy, risk stratification is conducted. Depending on the risk level, participants are either offered a surveillance endoscopy (for intermediate or high-risk polyps), returned to the biennial bowel cancer screening programme (for low-risk adenomas), or quarantined for 8 years (for no colonic pathology). More details appear from the published trial protocol.20

Study design

The study was an intention-to-treat, multicentre, open-label, randomised, parallel group-controlled trial conducted at all four hospitals in Southern Denmark’s Healthcare Region (population of approximately 1.3 million).

The study population included all citizens invited for CRC screening from 3 August 2020 to 19 December 2022, irrespective of previous participation. The study population consists of a mixture of first, second, third and fourth invitation rounds. At the start of inclusion, the Danish Screening had entered the prevalence period, and the number of positive findings has proportionally decreased.

Citizens invited for CRC screening were randomised between the control group offering the standard screening pathway of colonoscopy following a positive FIT and the intervention pathway offering optional CCE as a filter test following a positive FIT (figure 1).

Randomisation and masking

On a weekly roll, citizens were randomised in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention or control groups. During the trial, individuals invited for screening in the first week were assigned to the control group, while those invited in the subsequent week were assigned to the intervention group. This pattern of weekly alternation continued throughout the duration of the trial. The specific individuals invited for screening in a given week were randomly drawn from the pool of citizens eligible for screening in the upcoming months. This identification process was automated and managed by the invitation and administration module of the screening system. Therefore, it was not possible to predict who would be invited in an odd week and who would be invited in an even week. The researchers were blinded to the randomisation and they had no part in the invitation process.

Participation course

Citizens in the control group were handled as usual in the Danish screening programme, receiving written information in the mail, including an invitation letter, a leaflet on screening from the Danish National Health Authorities and the FIT kit. Citizens in the intervention group additionally received a one-page information sheet (included in online supplement 1) describing the optional choice in case of positive FIT. They could express interest in CCE or colonoscopy by attaching a green or red sticker (provided in the one-pager), respectively, to the FIT sample vial. The absence of a sticker led to a reminder about the CCE option and a referral for a colonoscopy.

Citizens in the intervention group received additional detailed digital mail about the study, their rights and the National Screening Programme for CRC following positive FIT. CCE participants were managed at four local healthcare centres. The expected number of invitations needed to obtain the planned sample of 2015 CCE investigations was exceeded by 280% due to a decrease in FIT positive rate (from 6.9 to 4.4%), overestimation of screening participation, a higher exclusion rate of CCE participants and a high number of individuals not choosing between CCE and colonoscopy.21

Certified healthcare professionals performed colonoscopy and CCE. The colonoscopy teams from the region’s four surgical departments underwent formal postgraduate training, and their adenoma detection rates were monitored and published annually by the Danish Colorectal Cancer Screening Group.9

An external contractor, Corporate Health International, carried out CCE. The process involved patient communication, video prereading by nurses and final reading by certified gastroenterologists. A report was made available to doctors and citizens within 3 days. An independent expert assessed 1% of the reports for quality assessment. The capsule used throughout the trial was the PillCam COLON 2 (Medtronic, Minnesota, USA).

All citizens aged 50–74 were invited for screening and included in this study based on their first invitation. Those who chose CCE were screened at first nurse contact and could be excluded based on the following criteria:

Bowel preparation regimens

Two different colon cleansing regimens were used during the study. For colonoscopy, the standard screening programme cleansing protocol was followed. The regiment was a 2-day polyethylene glycol-based protocol (Movicol and MoviPrep, Norgine Danmark A/S, Herlev, Denmark) combined with a seedless diet for 3 days. A more extensive cleansing protocol was used for CCE with 4 days of polyethylene glycol before and a sulphate-based saline booster (Eziclen, Ipsen Pharma, Boulogne-Billancourt, France) after capsule ingestion. After the interim analysis, 2 mg prucalopride (Resolor) was added 45–60 min before capsule ingestion to improve cleansing and transit time.21 22 Individuals whose CCE showed more than two polyps of any size or one polyp>9 mm, suboptimal CCE recording (ie, incomplete transit within battery lifetime, inadequate bowel cleansing (Leighton-Rex Scale grade 1) for any colonic segment or technical error), were referred for colonoscopy within 2 weeks.

Due to discrepancies between the assumed rates of screening participation, FIT positivity, CCE exclusion and proportion choosing CCE while preparing the protocol and the actual rates observed during the trial inclusion, the inclusion period was extended to 2 years and 5 months,21 exceeding the 2 years for repeat invitations. Therefore, some citizens were invited twice but were only included in the analyses based on their first invitation.

Data collection

The Danish civil registration system enables the identification of>99.5% of all eligible persons in the region at any given time by their individually assigned registration number.23 The civil person’s registration number enables us to invite all citizens in the given age group and to match information from different sources for everyone. Data on trial group (determined by the date of screening invitation), sex, age, screening participation, FIT results and conclusion from screening (ie, findings of cancer, intermediate-risk or high-risk adenomas) for all individuals included in the analyses (ie, all invitees of both groups) were collected from the Danish Colorectal Cancer Screening Database.24 All registered neoplastic lesions were histologically verified after resection. Non-neoplastic polyps were not registered. Data on the CCE performance (ie, bowel preparation grade, completion of transit and technical errors) were collected prospectively during the trial from the CCE reports.

Complications after CCE were closely monitored through questionnaires 3 days after the intervention and from patient files monitoring hospitalisation. Complications after colonoscopy were registered by the National Screening Database only.

Sample size

The CareForColon2015 trial was designed to include 2015 CCE investigations to determine the sample size. The number of invitations depended on factors including the FIT positivity rate, patient participation, choice of CCE over colonoscopy and exclusion rate.

Assuming a 38% prevalence of the main outcome (the proportion of individuals with advanced neoplasia (cancers and intermediate or high risk as defined above) identified) in the control group and a 4% absolute difference as the minimum clinically relevant difference, a total sample size of 4704 was determined. This calculation used the binomial test with a significance level of 0.05 and power of 0.8.

Interim analysis revealed that the proportion choosing CCE was lower than expected, while the exclusion rate was higher. Thus, the actual trial included 10 122 FIT-positive individuals from both groups attending either CCE or colonoscopy. The FIT positive rate decreased from the expected 6.9 to 4.4% as the screening programme had entered the third round of invitations and passed the prevalence peak.

Statistics

The trial was conducted as a parallel group randomised trial. Simple comparisons of the proportions of cancers and intermediate to high-risk adenomas between the groups were made using the χ² test. The χ² test was also used to compare the underlying outcomes (cancers, intermediate-risk adenomas and high-risk adenomas) separately, as well as baseline characteristics (age and sex). The level of statistical significance was set at 5%. Details of the statistical analyses, including variable definitions and information sources, can be found in the statistical analysis plan (online supplemental 2).

Sensitivity analysis

We identified 138 individuals who had wrongfully marked their vials with stickers, even though invited to the control arm without stickers. Therefore, sensitivity analyses were conducted to investigate the effect. The first excludes them from the analyses, and the second reallocates them to the intervention arm. Further, because 2/3 of participants in the intervention arm had a primary colonoscopy and no CCE, we have described the outcome distributions based on the primary investigation (colonoscopy or CCE), including multivariate logistic regression models estimating the odds of the main outcome adjusted for sex, age and FIT concentration in parallel to the intention-to-treat analysis.

Results

The flow of screening individuals

Screening invitations were sent to 396 676 individuals, equally divided between trial groups. In the control arm, 63.5% returned the FIT vial, and 61.7% did so in the intervention arm. This left 5585 and 5490 FIT-positive eligible samples in the two arms, respectively (figure 2).

Detection of cancers and adenomas

Advanced neoplasia was found in 1272 (0.64%) individuals in the control arm and 1326 (0.67%) in the intervention arm. This corresponds to 22.8% and 24.2% of FIT positives. The intervention group had 0.03% more advanced adenomas (absolute difference) for all invitees and 1.7% for FIT positives only. The difference in advanced adenoma detection in FIT positives was significant (p=0.027) (table 3). The sensitivity analyses showed no significant influence from excluding individuals applying a sticker to the vial in the control arm or reallocating them to the intervention arm. Histology was unavailable for 336 polyps.

Complications and adverse events

No major procedure-related complications or deaths related to CCE were registered. Three patients visited the emergency unit during the first 10 days following CCE. They were all handled in the out-clinic with no treatment. Five individuals could not swallow the capsule, and six expelled it after ingestion.

Discussion

This study failed to prove a difference in the detection rate of advanced neoplasia between the two arms of the investigation. Based on earlier studies, the experimental arm was expected to be superior to colonoscopy. Although the observed detection rate in CCE was higher than the control, the difference was lower than the minimal clinically relevant difference. It did not reach the level of significance in the intention-to-treat study. The study is thus a negative study.

The per-protocol analysis might, however, be subject to bias. It seems very unlikely that CCE detection rates are inferior to that of colonoscopy. This is also seen in the recently published paper by Professor Turvill et al who demonstrated how complete CCE identified a matched lesion in all patients with CRC who had an adequately prepared colon.25

CCE is not suited for CRC screening in FIT-based programmes due to the high reinvestigation rate in this population. Factors like patient preference, feasibility and cost are important when considering CCE for routine examinations. Neither cost nor patient preference is in favour of CCE in screening.

The first generation of colon capsules was introduced in 2006,26 27 but it was less effective than colonoscopy in detecting clinically significant findings or polyps.27 In 2009, the second-generation colon capsules were launched, featuring technological enhancements that improved diagnostic accuracy.28 29 Subsequent trials have reported a high sensitivity of CCE for polyp detection.30 31 Sensitivity of CCE to polyps and adenomas has been tested in several trials and described in multiple systematic reviews.30,38 The sensitivity of CCE is ranging between 82% and 90% in these trials. It should be remembered that the sensitivity of colonoscopy has been set to 100%. Colonoscopy is, however, a poor standard with significant miss rates of both cancers and adenomas. Colonoscopy and CCE detect partly different populations of adenomas and cancers which lead to a sensitivity for CCE of<100%. This study shows that the detection rates are fully comparable between CCE and colonoscopy. This is the first large study to investigate the performance of CCE compared with colonoscopy regarding advanced neoplasia. This study on CCE performance on relevant endpoints was tested in a population with a high frequency of pathological findings, to reduce the sample size and cost and make the trial feasible. For this trial, we employed a 4% absolute difference as the minimum clinically relevant difference. Ethical concerns related to the severity of missed advanced neoplasia make it difficult to suggest a non-inferiority margin for future non-inferiority trials, but in our recommendation, this should be no higher than 2%, given that the trial is conducted under the same circumstances.

Given the high occurrence of significant growths in this group, CCE will not be a practical substitute for colonoscopy. With a 70% reinvestigation rate, the costs of the diagnostic procedure will probably be close to doubled, and patients will likely prefer primary colonoscopy.19 To our best estimate, CCE will not be cost-efficient unless the reinvestigation rate can be held below 30%.39 Since CCE’s ability to detect advanced growths is not influenced by prevalence, this population is suitable for testing, and results can be applied to other groups with lower prevalence. This assumption depends on investigating for similar elements in these other groups.

Only 58% made an active choice between CCE and colonoscopy, most probably due to over-information provided. This is very likely because the information related to the trial had to be combined with the general information on CRC screening, making the handouts overwhelming. This has led to a high proportion of participants not indicating preference and a large proportion undergoing primary colonoscopy in the intervention group, which led to a smaller difference in prevalence between the two groups than expected and has likely reduced the difference to a non-significant level. No severe complications were found with CCE, as was reported earlier.17 40

In the UK, CCE has been introduced as a routine service (>10 000 investigations) to reduce the demand for colonoscopies during the COVID-19 pandemic. This initiative showed that implementing large-scale CCE-based diagnostic pathways is feasible, and the complication rate is low.41 42 However, the high number of patients requiring a colonoscopy after CCE is one major obstacle to broader implementation. A high reinvestigation rate negatively affects patient preference and healthcare economics. Nonetheless, the reinvestigation rate due to causes other than positive findings has been reduced significantly during the last 4–5 years, and it is currently lower than what is seen in this study.22 43 Unpublished data indicate that evaluating bowel cleanliness with artificial intelligence (AI) will further improve this.

CCE could develop into an attractive supplement in one-tier screening programmes employing primary colonoscopy, where the rate of positive findings is low. Other patient groups with a low rate of positive findings could also be considered for CCE. Large-scale trials from Scotland, England and from our unit have shown that a technical completion rate, with acceptable and complete colonic visualisation of about 80%, is feasible. Population with less than 30% positive findings would then potentially be of interest according to earlier studies on patient preference.19 A major cost analysis is currently ongoing, and with the addition of AI for image analysis. Preliminary, unpublished data from the ScotCap trial indicate that the CCE first strategy might come out with realistic costs for some patient populations.

Even though individuals were randomised in groups (clusters), they were not in the same physical environment, eliminating contamination risk. Further, the number of clusters throughout the trial exceeded 120, which limits any risk of seasonal differences between the two groups. Therefore, the statistical analysis approach was a standard randomised controlled trial with no adjustments for clusters or covariates.

During trial planning, we expected quite a larger proportion of the intervention group to undergo CCE than was realised. This could potentially have decreased the interventions’ effect on the primary outcome. However, as the trial included more than double the number of projected FIT-positive screening participants, the statistical power to detect a relative difference of 10% as determined clinically relevant was even larger than planned.

Weaknesses: 16% of the patients were offered investigation twice, and the intended sample of 2015 first-time invitation CCEs could not be reached. Unexpectedly, many individuals did not respond to the participation offer; therefore, only 1/3 of individuals in the intervention arm had CCE. The trial was not designed for a per protocol analysis, and there is a risk of bias.

In conclusion, optional CCE can replace default colonoscopy as a cancer and advanced adenoma detection method in terms of efficacy and complication rate. With the current reinvestigation rate, CCE is not suited for CRC screening. Other factors such as patient preference, feasibility and cost are essential when considering CCE for visualising the large bowel. A follow-up study on interval cancers in the two groups has been planned.
