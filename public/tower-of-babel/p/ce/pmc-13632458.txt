Cell pellets promote periodontal bone regeneration in furcation involvement by regulating macrophage polarization through TGF-β1 secretion
Authors: Fangfang Xu, Le Bai, Xiang Liang, Sina Ahmadi, Xin Yang, Huan Liu, Xinyi Li, Xinyi Hou, Junbo Tu, Xinpeng Dai, Sijia Na
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFurcation involvement, a severe complication of chronic periodontitis, poses significant challenges for clinical regeneration because of failure to remodel the inflammatory microenvironment. Current strategies such as guided tissue regeneration are limited by two-dimensional architecture and insufficient immunomodulation, resulting in failure to restore functional periodontal tissues. Cell pellet, a novel scaffold-free technology, features self-assembled three-dimensional structures and endogenous extracellular matrix storing growth factors; however, its immunomodulatory mechanism, particularly with respect to macrophage polarization, remains uncharacterized.ObjectiveTo investigate the effects of cell pellets on macrophage polarization and its molecular mechanisms and to validate their in vivo efficacy in promoting periodontal bone regeneration by reshaping the inflammatory microenvironment in FI.MethodsIn this study, a coculture system involving cell pellet and Raw 264.7 cells was established to elucidate the impact of the cell pellet on macrophage polarization. rTGF-β1 and an inhibitor of the TGFβI/II receptor (LY2109761) were used to investigate the underlying mechanisms of macrophage polarization. Finally, in vivo studies were conducted by implanting the cell pellet into a rat tooth root fenestration defect model to assess its regenerative potential and immunomodulatory effects.ResultsWhen cocultured with macrophages, the cell pellet was observed to polarize the macrophages toward the M2 phenotype. This effect could be counteracted by the use of a TGF-β receptor inhibitor. In vivo studies have shown that the cell pellet effectively transitions proinflammatory M1 macrophages into a pro-healing M2 phenotype, restores the M2/M1 ratio to a physiological balance, efficiently resolves inflammatory responses, and ultimately augments osteoblastic differentiation and promotes bone regeneration.ConclusionCell pellets promote periodontal regeneration by orchestrating M2 macrophage polarization via paracrine TGF-β1, suggesting a scaffold-free strategy that integrates immunomodulation and structural support for FI treatment.Clinical significanceThis study provides a novel scaffold-free therapeutic option for furcation involvement, addressing the clinical bottleneck of insufficient immunomodulation and limited regenerative efficacy of traditional treatments. The TGF-β1-mediated immunomodulatory and regenerative effects of cell pellets offer a promising approach to improve functional periodontal tissue restoration and tooth preservation in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09226-1.Keywords: Furcation involvement, Cell pellet, TGF-β1, Mesenchymal stem cells, ECM, Macrophage

Introduction

Furcation involvement (FI), a severe complication of chronic periodontitis, is characterized by the pathological destruction of periodontal tissues within the inter-radicular space of multirooted teeth [1]. The complex anatomical morphology of the furcation area impedes effective plaque control, thereby compromising long-term tooth prognosis. While regenerative strategies such as guided tissue regeneration (GTR) and guided bone regeneration (GBR) are frequently applied in clinical practice, their efficacy in treating FI remains inconsistent. These conventional methods generally fall short of completely reconstructing the natural three-dimensional architecture of the periodontium, often achieving only partial regeneration of the periodontal attachment structures. Furthermore, GTR and GBR commonly fail to establish a functional reconnection between the newly formed alveolar bone and the periodontal ligament, yielding regenerated tissues with structural and mechanical properties that differ from the native periodontium [2]. Consequently, achieving predictable and comprehensive regeneration of the periodontal apparatus in FI defects remains a major clinical challenge.

A pivotal factor underlying the suboptimal outcomes of conventional regenerative therapies is their inability to effectively remodel the dysregulated inflammatory microenvironment within periodontal defects [3]. Macrophages, central components of the innate immune response, exhibit high phenotypic plasticity and play a pivotal role in periodontitis progression [4–6]. In the chronic inflammatory milieu of FI, these cells predominantly polarize toward the classically activated M1 phenotype, which accelerates tissue destruction by secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines and stimulating osteoclastogenesis. Consequently, strategies aimed at biasing macrophage polarization toward the alternatively activated, anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype are considered a promising therapeutic approach to attenuate inflammatory damage and support tissue repair. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have emerged as potential candidates for such interventions due to their capacity to orchestrate immune microenvironments [7]. Through both direct cell-to-cell contact and paracrine signaling, MSCs have been reported to modulate macrophage behavior, suppressing the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators while enhancing the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines [8, 9].

To harness the reparative potential of MSCs, scaffold-free tissue engineering, particularly cell sheet technology, has been extensively explored [10, 11]. However, traditional cell sheets are inherently two-dimensional and often exhibit suboptimal mechanical properties, which restrict their clinical application in large, complex spatial defects such as FI. To overcome these limitations, three-dimensional cell pellet technology has emerged as an advanced alternative. Derived from the assembly of cultured monolayer cell sheets, cell pellets are further cultivated to promote self-assembly, forming dense, multicellular structures with robust mechanical integrity [12–14]. Distinct from exogenous biomaterial scaffolds that may elicit foreign body reactions, this scaffold-free 3D construct provides essential spatial support for FI defects while spontaneously accumulating abundant extracellular matrix (ECM). Crucially, this endogenously generated ECM functions as a biological reservoir, enriching and retaining MSC-secreted bioactive molecules, most notably transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1) [14]. The retention and sustained release of TGF-β1 within this ECM-rich niche are postulated to create a favorable microenvironment that supports cell viability and facilitates prolonged immunomodulatory and regenerative signaling.

Accordingly, while the immunomodulatory potential of MSCs is well-recognized, whether 3D cell pellets can effectively reshape the macrophage-driven inflammatory niche in FI, and whether TGF-β1 acts as a primary mediator in this process, remains to be fully elucidated. In this study, we hypothesized that MSC-derived cell pellets facilitate periodontal bone regeneration by secreting TGF-β1, which biases macrophage polarization toward a pro-healing M2 phenotype. The objective was to characterize the molecular signaling underlying this paracrine interaction and to evaluate the regenerative efficacy of these scaffold-free constructs in vivo. For verification, a rat mandibular root fenestration model was utilized; although inter-species anatomical variations exist, this model provides a relevant platform to recapitulate the inflammatory and surgical challenges inherent to furcation defects [15, 16]. By integrating immunomodulatory control with structural support, this work seeks to establish a foundational proof-of-concept for cell pellet therapy as a promising, scaffold-free strategy for the treatment of furcation involvement.

Method

The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Materials

Fetal bovine serum (BI, Israel); α-MEM (Servebio, China); penicillin‒streptomycin (Zhongqiaoxinzhou, China); phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Servebio, China); ascorbic acid (Solarbio, China); trypsin (Glico, USA); rTGF-β1 (Solarbio, China); LY2109761 (MCE, China); TUNEL apoptosis detection kit (Boster, China); anti-TGFβ1 antibody (Abcam, UK); anti-iNOS antibody (Abcam, UK); anti-CD86 antibody (CST, USA); anti-CD206 antibody (Servebio, China); anti-Arg-1 antibody (Abcam, UK); anti-mouse CD86-FITC (eBioscience, USA); anti-mouse CD206-PE (eBioscience, USA); CO₂ incubator; centrifuge; laminar flow hood; inverted phase-contrast microscope and imaging system (Olympus, Japan); and flow cytometer (Beckman-Coulter, USA).

Isolation of primary rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs)

The rats were euthanized via cervical dislocation and soaked in 75% ethanol for 5 min for disinfection. Under sterile conditions, the femur and tibia were isolated from each rat. Any adhering muscle tissue was removed, and the bones were washed with sterile PBS. A 10 mL syringe with a needle was used to flush out the bone marrow from the femur and tibia. The mixture was flushed with complete culture medium containing 20% FBS to maintain cell viability. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min to pellet the cells. The supernatant was aspirated, and the cells were resuspended in fresh complete medium. The cells were plated at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL in complete culture medium and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator. After 24–48 h, the medium was changed to remove nonadherent cells. Subsequent media changes were performed every 2–3 days. The cells were observed for growth and morphology. Once the cells reach approximately 80% confluence, they can be passaged via trypsin-EDTA for detachment and reseeded at a 1:2 to 1:3 ratio for further expansion.

Construction of the BMSC-CP

Primary rat BMSCs at passage 3 demonstrating optimal growth were selected as the seed cells to ensure stable biological activity. The cell monolayer was washed twice with sterile PBS to remove residual serum and impurities. A 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA solution was added, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for approximately 2 min. Upon observing cell detachment under an inverted microscope, complete medium was rapidly added to terminate digestion. The cells were pipetted to a single-cell suspension, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, and resuspended in fresh complete medium.To ensure batch-to-batch reproducibility and standardize the number of cells per pellet, the cell suspension was seeded into 90 mm culture dishes at a precise initial density of 2 × 10⁵ cells/dish. The cells were incubated for 48 h to allow for full adherence. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with a cell sheet-inducing medium supplemented with 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid. This medium was refreshed every 3 days to maintain microenvironmental stability. After approximately 10 days of continuous induction, a white, velvet-like membrane structure formed at the bottom of the dish. This intact BMSC sheet was carefully detached along the edges using a sterile pipette tip, transferred to a dry culture dish, and repeatedly pipetted to aggregate the membrane into a clump. This aggregated mass was then transferred to the edge of a sterile 90 mm culture dish to allow for adherence, with fresh medium supplied daily. After 5 days of continuous culture, the mass gradually condensed into a compact, three-dimensional BMSC-CP and was selected for subsequent experiments to guarantee structural consistency across all batches.

Characterization of the BMSC-CP

For the TUNEL assay, the cell pellet was prepared first and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for more than 4 h. The samples were dehydrated through a graded alcohol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. The samples were separated, flattened in 45 °C water, and dried at 60 °C. The slides were incubated at 60 °C for 2 h, dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. The samples were digested with Proteinase K in 0.01 M TBS at 37 °C for 5–10 min and then washed with TBS. The TUNEL reaction mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 2 h, followed by washes with TBS. The samples were incubated with blocking solution for 30 min and then with biotinylated anti-digoxigenin antibody at 37 °C for 30 min. The samples were washed, and SABC was applied at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by additional washes with TBS. The sections were counterstained with DAPI, washed, and mounted with anti-fade reagent. The samples were analyzed via a laser confocal microscope. For hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining, the cell pellet was prepared, and sectioning was performed. The slides were baked at 60 °C for 2 h. The samples were deparaffinized in xylene and rinsed with PBS. The samples were rehydrated through a descending gradient of alcohols for 3 min each. After being washed in PBS for 3 min three times, the sections were stained with hematoxylin for 10 min and then rinsed with PBS for 10 min. They were differentiated in a mixture of hydrochloric acid and alcohol for 30 s and then rinsed with PBS. The samples were stained with eosin for 15 min and rinsed with PBS, then dehydrated through an ascending gradient of alcohols and rinsed with PBS. They were washed twice with xylene for 10 min. Finally, the samples were mixed with neutral resin and observed under a microscope. For scanning electron microscopy observation, the cell pellet was prepared, and sectioning was performed. The culture medium was aspirated, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. The samples were fixed with scanning electron microscopy (SEM) fixative for 4 h. They were rinsed with PBS and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series and dried then coated with gold. The samples were observed under a scanning electron microscope.

Measurements of the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G′′)

Within a sterile biosafety cabinet, a sterile biopsy punch with a 2 mm inner diameter was applied vertically to the dense core region of the BMSC-CP to extract cylindrical samples, yielding standardized, uniform pieces with a precise diameter of 2 mm and a thickness of approximately 0.5 mm. The pretreated BMSC-CPs were then placed in a sterile culture dish and gently pre-wetted by applying pre-chilled PBS dropwise to the sample surface. To prevent sample dehydration and shrinkage, all subsequent testing was conducted within 2 h. Dynamic viscoelastic measurements of the cell pellets were performed using an Anton Paar MCR 302 rheometer (Graz, Austria). Prior to testing, the cell pellets were prewetted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). A dynamic frequency sweep (1% strain) was conducted over 0.1–10 rad/s at 23.7 ± 0.1 °C. The plate gap was set to 0.355 mm, and the samples were equilibrated for 10 min at the target temperature. The storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G′′) were recorded via RheoCompass, and the data were subsequently analyzed via GraphPad Prism 10.

ELISA of the cell pellet culture supernatants

The supernatants from days 3, 5, and 7 were collected and centrifuged for 10 min to remove debris. TGF-β1 levels were measured via ELISA kits following the manufacturer’s protocols: standards and samples were added to precoated plates, which were subsequently incubated, washed, and reacted with detection antibodies. After adding the substrate and stopping solution, the absorbance was read at the specified wavelength, and the cytokine concentrations were calculated on the basis of standard curves.

Immunofluorescence staining for TGF-β1 in cell pellets

The cell pellets were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 4 h, dehydrated, cleared, and embedded in paraffin. The samples were cut into 4-µm slices, dewaxed in xylene, and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by boiling the sections in 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 15 min. Nonspecific binding was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS at room temperature for 30 min. The sections were incubated with primary anti-TGF-β1 antibody (1:200, Abcam) at 4 °C overnight. After the samples were washed with PBS, they were incubated with an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (1:500, Invitrogen) at room temperature for 1 h in the dark. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000) for 10 min. The slides were incubated with anti-fade reagent and analyzed via a laser confocal microscope. The fluorescence intensity was quantified via Image-Pro Plus 6.0.

Coculture of Cell pellets with macrophages

RAW 264.7 murine macrophages were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were maintained in a 37 °C incubator with a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere, and only cells in the logarithmic growth phase demonstrating optimal viability were selected for experiments. To accurately isolate the immunomodulatory effects of the cell pellets, the experiment was divided into four distinct groups: (1) Control group (RAW 264.7 alone), (2) CP group (RAW 264.7 co-cultured with BMSC-CP), (3) LPS group (LPS-induced RAW 264.7), and (4) LPS + CP group (LPS-induced RAW 264.7 co-cultured with BMSC-CP). RAW 264.7 cells were detached using 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA, resuspended, and seeded into 6-well plates. The cells were cultured for 24 h to ensure uniform adherence. For groups requiring inflammatory induction (LPS and LPS + CP groups), the original medium was discarded and replaced with 2 mL of fresh complete medium containing 500 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS). These cells were incubated for 24 h to induce polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. Prior to co-culture, mature BMSC-CPs cultured for 5 days post-aggregation were gently washed twice with sterile PBS to remove residual medium. For the co-culture groups (CP and LPS + CP), the medium in the 6-well plates was removed, and the macrophages were gently washed once with sterile PBS. Then, 2 mL of fresh complete medium was added to each well, and a BMSC-CP was carefully transferred to the edge of the well. The pellets were completely submerged in the medium to facilitate direct cell-to-cell contact and soluble factor exchange, without mechanically disturbing the adherent macrophages. The co-culture system was maintained undisturbed for 12 h.

Following the experiment, the culture supernatants were collected, and the BMSC-CPs were removed. The adherent RAW 264.7 cells were digested with 1 mL of 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA for 2–3 min. Upon microscopic confirmation of cell detachment, 2 mL of fresh medium was added to neutralize the trypsin. The wells were gently pipetted to ensure complete detachment, and the cell suspension was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. The resulting cell pellets were washed twice with sterile PBS and prepared for downstream Western blot, immunofluorescence, and flow cytometry analyses.

Recombinant TGF-β1 (rTGF-β1) and LY2109761 treatment of macrophages

RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured overnight to allow adherence. For induction of M1 polarization, the cells were stimulated with 500 ng/ml LPS for 12 h. Subsequently, recombinant TGF-β1 (Solarbio, China) was added to the culture medium at a final concentration of 10 ng/ml. After rTGF-β1 was added, the cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator for another 12 h. For the groups treated with both rTGF-β1 and the TGF-β receptor inhibitor LY2109761, LY2109761 was added 1 h prior to rTGF-β1 treatment at a final concentration of 10 µM to block TGF-β signaling. Following the incubation period, the cells were harvested for subsequent analyses, including Western blotting, immunofluorescence staining, and flow cytometry, to evaluate the expression of macrophage polarization markers.

Western blot analysis of Raw264.7 cells

For Western blot analysis of RAW 264.7, after coculture with cell pellets (with or without LPS stimulation and the TGF-β receptor inhibitor LY2109761), the cells were lysed, and the proteins were extracted. The protein concentration was quantified, followed by SDS‒PAGE, transfer to PVDF membranes, and blocking. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against M1 markers (iNOS and CD86), M2 markers (Arg-1 and CD206), and GAPDH (loading control) and then with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Bands were visualized via enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL), and relative expression was quantified via normalization to the level of GAPDH.

Immunofluorescence of RAW 264.7

For immunofluorescence of Raw264.7 cells after coculture, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA. The samples were incubated with primary antibodies against the M1 marker CD86 and the M2 marker CD206 overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 594- and 488-conjugated secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were captured via confocal microscopy, and positive cell ratios were quantified via Image-Pro Plus 6.0.

Flow cytometry analysis of RAW 264.7 cells

Adherent Raw264.7 cells were detached with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA, and the resulting cell suspension was centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 2% fetal bovine serum to terminate trypsin activity. This washing step was repeated twice to remove residual culture medium and debris. The cells were resuspended in 100 µL of blocking buffer (PBS with 5% BSA) and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min to block nonspecific antibody binding sites. After blocking, the cells were centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min, and the blocking buffer was discarded. The cells were resuspended in 100 µL of staining buffer (PBS with 2% FBS) containing the following fluorescently labeled primary antibodies: anti-mouse CD86-FITC (1:100, eBioscience) and anti-mouse CD206-PE (1:100, eBioscience). Isotype-matched IgG antibodies (FITC- and PE-conjugated) were used as negative controls. The cells were incubated with antibodies at 4 °C in the dark for 30 min. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with cold staining buffer (300×g, 5 min, 4 °C) to remove unbound antibodies. The stained cells were resuspended in 300 µL of cold staining buffer and filtered through a 40-µm cell strainer to remove cell aggregates. The samples were analyzed immediately via a Beckman‒Coulter flow cytometer.

Rat mandibular root fenestration model

All experimental procedures involving Sprague Dawley (SD) rats were conducted in strict compliance with the ethical principles for animal experimentation, approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University (approval NO.XJTUAE2024-2788), and adhered to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Research Council, USA). The sample size for the in vivo experiment was determined based on preliminary pilot study results, which showed that the bone volume to total volume ratio (BV/TV) in the cell pellet (CP) group was approximately 30% higher than that in the control group at 6 weeks post-implantation. A total of 40 healthy SPF-grade SD rats (male, 8–10 weeks old, weight 220–250 g, purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Xi’an Jiaotong University) were enrolled and randomly divided into two groups (n = 20 per group): the control group (no implantation) and the CP implantation group. To balance potential environmental confounders from cage location, cage allocation was randomized using a random number table. Each group was randomly assigned to 3–4 cages with cages labeled only with unique numeric codes to maintain blinding of the staff. In addition, cages were distributed across different shelves of the animal rack in a randomized manner, ensuring that environmental conditions were evenly distributed between the control and CP groups.

The rats were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at a dosage of 45 mg/kg.

After sterilization with iodophor and alcohol, a 1.5 cm longitudinal incision was made along the mandibular margin. Using an ultrasonic bone cutter under a surgical microscope, a standardized 3 mm × 2 mm × 1 mm (W × H × D) bone defect was created at the furcation area of the first and second molar, mimicking the anatomical destruction of furcation involvement. Constant saline irrigation was used to minimize thermal damage. To simulate an inflammatory microenvironment, 10 µL of LPS (10 µg/mL) was slowly injected into the defect and allowed to infiltrate for 5 minutes [15]. In the CP group, a pre-treated, mature BMSC-CP was trimmed to match the defect dimensions and vertically applied directly into the defect area. Precise placement ensured that the pellet remained in close contact with the defect walls without displacement. Notably, as a scaffold-free strategy, no exogenous membranes, carriers, or additional scaffolds were utilized to isolate the biological effects of the cell pellets. The control group received no implantation. The surgical site was meticulously closed in layers using 5 − 0 absorbable sutures. Rats received buprenorphine (0.1 mg/kg) twice daily for 72 h for analgesia and were maintained on a soft diet for one week. Animals were humanely euthanized via an overdose of pentobarbital (120 mg/kg) at 2, 4, and 6 weeks post-surgery (n = 5 per time point) for subsequent radiological and histological analysis.

Micro-CT, histology, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence analyses

Rat mandibles were subjected to micro-CT scans at a resolution of 10 μm via a µ-CT35 imaging system (Scanco Medical). The samples were reconstructed and segmented with Mimics software 10.01 (Materialise, Belgium). The samples were labeled with random numbers before scanning. After scanning, the bone volume (BV) to total volume (TV) ratio at the defect site was analyzed via Scanco Medical software. Then, the rat mandibles were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 48 h. The tissues were subsequently washed in PBS for 1 h and demineralized in 15% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid for 4 weeks at room temperature. After decalcification, the samples were dehydrated via an ascending ethanol series and embedded in paraffin. Consecutive horizontal sections (4 μm) obtained from the middle third of the mandibular defect were used for histology and immunofluorescence analyses. Hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining was carried out for histologic observation. For immunochemistry, after dewaxing, rehydration, and heat-induced antigen retrieval with sodium citrate buffer, the sections were blocked with 5% BSA, incubated with primary antibody against TGF-β1 at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with HRP-labeled secondary antibody. Then DAB chromogenic reaction and hematoxylin counterstaining were performed. Finally, images were captured via an optical microscope, and the integrated optical density (IOD) value was quantitatively analyzed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 (Media Cybernetics). Immunofluorescence staining was conducted as previously reported. The sections were double-stained with primary antibodies against F4/80, CD86 and CD206 to detect M1 or M2 macrophages, respectively. The images were acquired via a confocal laser scanning microscope. For semiquantitative analysis, the number of F4/80 + cells per square millimeter and the ratios of CD86 + F4/80 + cells/F4/80 + cells, CD206 + F4/80 + cells/F4/80 + cells, and M2/M1 macrophages in the defect area were calculated via Image-Pro Plus 6.0.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted via SPSS software, version 16.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Statistical results are presented as the mean value ± standard deviation, providing a measure of central tendency and variability. For data from each experimental group, normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visualized via Q-Q plots, where data points approximating a straight line indicated compliance with normality; For multi-group comparisons and pairwise comparisons, variance homogeneity was assessed using Levene’s test, with a non-significant result (P > 0.05) indicating equal variance across groups; To assess the significance of differences across the four experimental groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed. For pairwise comparisons between two groups, Student’s t test was utilized. For non-normal data, data transformation was first attempted to normalize the distribution; if transformation failed, non-parametric alternatives were used (Kruskal-Wallis H test instead of one-way ANOVA for multi-group comparisons, Mann-Whitney U test instead of Student’s t test for pairwise comparisons); For unequal variance (Levene’s test, P < 0.05), Welch’s ANOVA was used for multi-group comparisons and Welch’s t test for pairwise comparisons to adjust for variance heterogeneity. The threshold for statistical significance was established at a P value of less than 0.05, indicating a 95% confidence level in the observed differences.

Results

Characterization of the cell pellet

To evaluate the feasibility of utilizing BMSC-CPs as a scaffold-free regenerative therapy for furcation involvement, it was first necessary to verify that the fabrication method successfully produces a three-dimensional, viable structure mimicking the natural extracellular microenvironment. Rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) were seeded in a medium-sized dish. After 5 days of culture until cell confluence, 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid was added to the culture medium, and the cells were further cultured for approximately 14 days to form cell sheets. Under the microscope, the cells grew in layers and were arranged in a vortex-like pattern with polarity. Macroscopically, the cell sheets had a certain thickness and good ductility (Fig. 1A). The cell pellets were prepared by wrapping the cell sheets with a pipette tip. After 5 days of continuous culture, the cell pellets became milky white, with a dense structure and good elasticity. Hematoxylin‒eosin (HE) staining revealed that on day 3, the extracellular matrix (ECM) was sparse and loosely distributed. By day 5, the cell pellets had a complete structure with a relatively dense morphology and were rich in cells and ECM. The cells were uniformly distributed throughout the structure, forming a highly integrated network. This uniform distribution indicated the formation of an ideal microenvironment conducive to tissue development and function. On day 7, the ECM remained dense, but the cell count decreased slightly (Fig. 1B, F, G). TUNEL apoptosis staining revealed that the cell pellets cultured for 3 days exhibited almost no apoptosis. However, by day 5, the number of apoptotic cells within the cell pellets had slightly increased, and by day 7, the degree of apoptosis had significantly increased (Fig. 1B, D). These results fully demonstrate that the cell pellets have a three-dimensional structure and dense ECM, mimicking the in vivo microenvironment for cell growth and making them ideal materials for tissue regeneration.

Mechanical properties, ECM architecture, and TGF-β1 secretion kinetics of cell pellets

Successful periodontal regeneration in furcation defects requires a graft that provides both physical stability against masticatory forces and the sustained release of immunomodulatory factors. Therefore, the dynamic viscoelastic properties of the developing cell pellets were subsequently assessed, and their capacity to store and secrete TGF-β1, a crucial cytokine for tissue repair, was quantified. A rheometer was subsequently used to evaluate the mechanical properties of the cell pellets during remodeling. The results revealed that at days 3, 5, and 7, both the storage modulus and loss modulus of the cell pellets gradually increased with prolonged culture, indicating increased rigidity and viscosity. The modulus values on days 5 and 7 were greater than those on day 3, with a more significant increase on day 7, further confirming the dynamic evolution of mechanical properties during collagen remodeling (Fig. 2A-C). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that on day 3, only a small number of collagen fibers were present in the cell pellets, which appeared relatively thin, short, and scattered without obvious directional arrangement, indicating a loose distribution state within the ECM. By days 5 and 7, the collagen fibers in the cell pellets had undergone significant remodeling. SEM images clearly revealed thickened and elongated collagen fibers that were closely packed, with a more regular and consistent orientation, forming a dense network structure that wrapped around the surrounding cells. TEM further confirmed that these collagen fibers exhibited a uniform diameter, with their longitudinal axes aligned in a parallel manner, indicating enhanced structural order. This dynamic evolution of collagen fibers—from dispersed and underdeveloped on day 3 to thickened, densely packed, and uniformly oriented on days 5 and 7—further demonstrates the ability of cell pellets to construct a physiologically mimetic ECM microenvironment, which is crucial for supporting cell‒cell interactions and exerting sustained biological functions (Fig. 2D).

Cell pellets may exhibit paracrine effects, and TGF-β1 is an important paracrine factor. Currently, the time-dependent expression pattern of TGF-β1 during the culture period of cell pellets remains unclear. To determine the optimal time point for subsequent coculture experiments, this study measured the content of TGF-β1 within the cell pellets, which was secreted into the culture supernatant on days 3, 5, and 7 of culture. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that the TGF-β1 content in the cell pellets was low on day 3, peaked in the extracellular matrix (ECM) on day 5, and decreased on day 7 (Fig. 2E, F). ELISA of TGF-β1 in the culture supernatant revealed that the factor levels remained similar between day 3 and day 5 but increased sharply on day 7 (Fig. 2G).

These results suggest that TGF-β1 was mainly stored and gradually accumulated in the ECM during the first 5 days of cell pellet culture. From day 5 to day 7, TGF-β1 was released from the cell pellets into the culture supernatant. Therefore, cell pellets cultured for 5 days were selected for the next coculture experiment to maximize their paracrine effects.

Coculture of cell pellets with macrophages promotes macrophage polarization

Given that BMSC-CPs possess a robust ECM architecture capable of secreting TGF-β1, the next step was to determine if these pellets could reshape the inflammatory microenvironment in vitro. A direct co-culture system was established, and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) was utilized to induce an inflammatory state. Western blot analysis demonstrated that LPS stimulation increased the protein expression of iNOS and CD86, while decreasing the expression of Arg-1 and CD206. In contrast, following co-culture with the cell pellet, the LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells exhibited a significant reduction in iNOS and CD86 expression, coupled with increased Arg-1 and CD206 expression (Fig. 3A). Immunofluorescence staining corroborated these protein-level observations, revealing decreased CD86 and increased CD206 fluorescence signals in the co-culture group compared to the LPS-only group (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, cytokine quantification via ELISA showed that LPS stimulation elevated the concentrations of IL-6 and TNF-α and reduced IL-10 and TGF-β1 in the supernatant. Upon introduction of the cell pellet to the LPS-treated macrophages, the concentrations of IL-6 and TNF-α significantly decreased, while IL-10 and TGF-β concentrations were elevated (Fig. 3C). Collectively, these changes in surface marker profiles and cytokine secretion support the biological interpretation that the cell pellet facilitates macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype.

Recombinant TGF-β1 promotes macrophage polarization

While the co-culture system demonstrated the pellets’ anti-inflammatory capacity, the specific molecular driver responsible for this effect needed to be isolated. Thus, it was investigated whether the direct application of recombinant TGF-β1 could replicate the observed shifts in marker expression. Compared with the LPS-only treatment group, macrophages treated with 10 ng/mL recombinant TGF-β1 displayed significantly lower protein expression levels of iNOS and CD86, and higher expression of Arg-1 and CD206. The addition of the specific TGF-β type I/II receptor inhibitor LY2109761 reversed these changes, leading to the recovery of iNOS and CD86 expression and a reduction in Arg-1 and CD206 levels (Fig. 4A). Immunofluorescence (Fig. 4B) and flow cytometry analyses (Fig. 4C, D) further confirmed these population-level marker changes, showing a decrease in CD86-positive cells and an increase in CD206-positive cells under TGF-β1 treatment, which was abrogated by the inhibitor. These results indicate that TGF-β1 signaling is a primary driver in shifting the macrophage marker profile toward an M2 state.

The immunodulatory effect of the cell pellet is blocked by a TGF-β receptor inhibitor

To definitively confirm that the immunomodulatory effects exerted by the BMSC-CPs are fundamentally mediated through their paracrine TGF-β1 secretion, a specific TGF-β I/II receptor inhibitor (LY2109761) was introduced into the co-culture system to observe if the M2 polarization could be reversed. The TGF-βI/II receptor inhibitor LY2109761 was introduced into the coculture system to clarify the signaling pathway involved. Western blot, immunofluorescence, and flow cytometry analyses consistently demonstrated that the inhibitor successfully blocked the previously observed effects of the cell pellet. Specifically, in the presence of LY2109761, the co-culture system showed elevated CD86 expression and reduced CD206 expression compared to the co-culture without the inhibitor (Fig. 5A-F). These findings confirm that the cell pellet’s ability to induce M2 macrophage polarization is fundamentally dependent on the TGF-β1 signaling pathway.

The cell pellet exerts regenerative and immunomodulatory effects in the rat mandibular root fenestration model

Finally, to validate the in vitro mechanistic findings within a complex physiological system, the therapeutic potential of BMSC-CPs was evaluated in an in vivo rat mandibular root fenestration model, specifically assessing their ability to couple targeted immunomodulation with functional periodontal bone regeneration. Subsequent animal experiments confirmed the immunomodulatory and bone regeneration effects of the cell pellet in vivo. We established a rat mandibular root fenestration model by randomly dividing the rats into a cell pellet implantation group and a control group (n = 20). In the cell pellet group, the pellet was implanted into the defect site, whereas in the control group, nothing was implanted, followed by layered suturing of soft tissue and skin (Fig. 6A). Micro-CT analysis demonstrated that by 2 weeks, early bone formation was observed within the defect area in the CP group, whereas the control group exhibited only minimal new bone formation at the defect margins. By week 4, osteogenesis was more active in the CP group, characterized by a significant increase in the BV/TV (24.174 ± 0.372%), a thickening of the Tb.Th (0.1100 ± 0.0062 mm), and a reduction in the Tb.Sp (0.3497 ± 0.0040 mm) compared with the control group at the same time point. By week 6, the control group still presented small areas of unfilled defects, whereas the defect sites in the CP group displayed advanced bone healing, exhibiting higher BV/TV (49.096 ± 0.351%) and Tb.Th (0.1681 ± 0.0027 mm) values, along with further reductions in Tb.Sp (0.1281 ± 0.0015 mm). Histological assessment via HE staining revealed that at 2 weeks, newly formed bone tissue began to emerge in the CP group ; at 4 weeks, the amount of bone tissue increased and showed signs of mineralization ; and at 6 weeks, the bone tissue structure had matured, resulting in substantial defect fill. Masson’s trichrome staining further showed that collagen fibers in the CP group gradually accumulated and formed an orderly network structure closely integrating with the newly formed bone, whereas the control group exhibited slower bone tissue repair with minimal and disorganized collagen fiber deposition (Fig. 6D).

To further validate the core role of TGF-β1 in in vivo immunomodulation and bone regeneration, TGF-β1 immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed on mandibular defect sections. In the control group, only weak and scattered TGF-β1-positive signals were observed in the cytoplasm of cells within the defect area at 2 and 4 weeks post-surgery; no concentrated positive signals were found in regions related to tissue regeneration (Fig. 7A). In contrast, the cell pellet (CP) group exhibited a clear temporal and spatial pattern of TGF-β1 expression. At 2 and 4 weeks post-implantation, concentrated and strong TGF-β1-positive signals were detected, and these signals were mainly localized in the cytoplasm of endothelial cells in newly formed blood vessels within the defect area. Quantitative analysis of integrated optical density (INtden) confirmed that the INtden of TGF-β1 in the CP group were significantly higher than those in the control group (Fig. 7C). To further detect the immunomodulation ability of CP, immunofluorescence staining was then used to observe macrophage polarization within the tissue. The macrophages were labeled with F4/80 (green fluorescence), while CD86 and CD206 were used to label M1-type and M2-type macrophages (red fluorescence), respectively. The percentage of CD86- or CD206-positive cells relative to F4/80-positive cells was quantified. In the CP group, the proportion of F4/80 + CD206+ M2-type macrophages significantly increased, whereas the proportion of F4/80 + CD86+ M1-type macrophages was relatively low, indicating that the CP group promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype. In contrast, the control group primarily contained M1-type macrophages, with a lower percentage of M2-type macrophages. Quantitative analysis further validated this trend, demonstrating that the CP group facilitated bone tissue regeneration by creating a favorable immune microenvironment through macrophage polarization (Fig. 7B, C). These findings indicate that the cell pellet promotes periodontal tissue regeneration by secreting TGF-β1, which induces M2-type macrophage polarization.

Discussion

Furcation involvement, a severe complication of chronic periodontitis, is characterized by pathological destruction of periodontal tissues at tooth root bifurcations, leading to attachment loss, alveolar bone resorption, and eventual tooth mobility, which seriously threatens tooth preservation and oral function [17]. Its complex anatomical structure, coupled with difficulties in plaque control within the furcation area and persistent dysregulation of the inflammatory microenvironment, poses significant challenges to traditional regenerative therapies. Current clinical standards for treating furcation involvement (FI), such as Guided Tissue Regeneration (GTR) and Guided Bone Regeneration (GBR), primarily utilize passive barrier membranes to prevent epithelial downgrowth and promote bone formation. However, these techniques exhibit significant limitations in the complex anatomical environment of FI, often recovering only 30%–50% of periodontal attachment structures. The success of GTR/GBR is frequently hampered by high technical sensitivity and a notable failure rate in defects with deep periodontal pockets or complex morphologies. Furthermore, traditional membranes serve as inert barriers that cannot functionally reconnect the periodontal ligament to alveolar bone, nor can they actively modulate the persistent inflammatory microenvironment that characterizes chronic periodontitis. This clinical dilemma underscores the urgency of developing innovative therapeutic strategies targeting the unique pathological mechanisms of FI, particularly those addressing both inflammatory regulation and structural regeneration. The novel scaffold-free cell pellet constructed in this study, which is based on the principle of self-assembly between cells and the ECM, exerts synergistic regenerative effects by enhancing intercellular connections and cell–ECM interactions. In contrast to the passive nature of GTR/GBR, BMSC-CPs represent an active biological strategy. As a scaffold-free construct, the BMSC-CP eliminates concerns regarding the uncontrollable degradation rates or foreign-body reactions associated with exogenous membranes.

Its ability to regulate macrophage polarization to establish an anti-inflammatory microenvironment provides a potential solution for FI treatment that integrates immunomodulation and structural support, with promising regenerative effects demonstrated in in vivo experiments, offering a new perspective to overcome the bottlenecks in FI therapy.

The BMSC-CP constructed in this study via physical remodeling demonstrates significant advantages in structural integrity and physiological biomimicry compared to traditional two-dimensional (2D) cell sheets. While 2D monolayers are limited by their planar architecture, this physical aggregation method successfully transcends these spatial constraints, recreating a three-dimensional (3D) microenvironment that closely mirrors the physiological conditions of tissue growth in vivo. Microscopic characterization and histological evaluation confirm that the BMSC-CP possesses a highly dense internal structure and remarkable elasticity, properties that are essential for maintaining mechanical stability within the complex anatomical environment of furcation defects. Furthermore, a 5-day cultivation period was identified as the optimal equilibrium point between structural maturation and cellular vitality. At this specific juncture, the bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells are uniformly distributed throughout the construct, supported by a highly integrated and dense endogenous extracellular matrix (ECM). This state of biological and physical harmony establishes a robust foundational scaffold, ensuring that the BMSC-CP is primed for subsequent paracrine activity and targeted immunomodulatory functions required for functional periodontal regeneration.

The dynamic enhancement of the mechanical properties of BMSC-CPs is intrinsically linked to the ultrastructural remodeling of the internal extracellular matrix (ECM). Rheological characterization revealed that both the storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G’’) increased progressively from day 3 to day 7 of culture. Specifically, the values recorded on days 5 and 7 were significantly higher than those on day 3, signifying a marked evolution in structural rigidity and viscoelastic stability. This mechanical reinforcement reflects a fundamental transformation of the collagen architecture within the pellet. While collagen fibers on day 3 appeared thin, short, and scattered in a loose distribution, electron microscopy confirmed that by days 5 and 7, these fibers had developed into a thickened, elongated, and densely packed network. The resulting matrix is characterized by a regular, consistent orientation with fibers aligned in a parallel manner, creating a physiologically mimetic microenvironment. This dense, endogenous collagenous network serves a critical functional role by providing the physical scaffolding necessary to withstand masticatory forces within the complex anatomical landscape of furcation defects. Beyond simple structural support and space maintenance, the increased mechanical stiffness of the ECM acts as a biomechanical signal to the resident cells. Such a rigid microenvironment is likely to influence cellular biological behavior through mechanotransduction pathways, potentially including the YAP/TAZ signaling cascade, which may activate genes related to mesenchymal stem cell proliferation and osteogenic differentiation. Consequently, the BMSC-CP functions not merely as a passive filler but as a biomechanically active unit that promotes tissue regeneration by coupling physical stability with cellular signaling.

Mesenchymal stem cells can secrete many growth factors, such as TGF-β1 and VEGF, during tissue development, which play corresponding roles in various stages of tissue development and regeneration to promote tissue regeneration [18, 19]. TGF-β1 is an important factor secreted by mesenchymal stem cells that plays a key role in tissue regeneration and immunomodulation. Some researchers have reported that TGF-β1 is mainly stored in the ECM in the form of latent complexes (latent TGF-β, LTGF-β) after being secreted by mesenchymal stem cells and that latent TGF-β binding protein (LTBP) anchors the complex to the fibrous structure or proteoglycans in the ECM through covalent bonds [20]. However, the precise mechanisms governing the latency and release of TGF-β1 within cell pellets remain underexplored. Addressing these questions is critical for optimizing the therapeutic potential of cell pellets by precisely controlling TGF-β1-mediated immunomodulation and tissue regeneration in the context of furcation involvement. In summary, the unique integration of the self-assembled 3D architecture, abundant ECM with optimized TGF-β1 storage, and enhanced mechanical stability not only overcomes the limitations of traditional 2D strategies but also establishes a functional microenvironment tailored to the pathological demands of FI. The evaluation of TGF-β1 secretion kinetics reveals a distinctive paracrine profile unique to the BMSC-CP construct. Empirical observations demonstrating a peak accumulation of TGF-β1 within the endogenous extracellular matrix (ECM) on day 5, followed by a subsequent release into the supernatant by day 7, indicate that the ECM serves a dual role: providing essential structural support and acting as a natural reservoir for growth factors. This reservoir effect distinguishes the BMSC-CP from conventional single-cell suspensions or exogenous factor delivery systems. Facilitated by the physical anchoring provided by matrix proteins and the high-density interactions between cells and the ECM, the construct enables the efficient in situ accumulation and controlled release of bioactive molecules within the recipient area [21]. Such a mechanism ensures the maintenance of sustained biological activity even within the complex and pathological microenvironment characteristic of furcation involvement, thereby establishing a stable molecular foundation conducive to functional periodontal regeneration.

The reconstruction of the periodontal inflammatory microenvironment is recognized as a critical prerequisite for achieving functional tissue repair. In direct co-culture assays, the BMSC-CP demonstrated superior immunomodulatory capacity, evidenced by the significant downregulation of pro-inflammatory M1 markers (iNOS and CD86) and the concurrent upregulation of anti-inflammatory M2 markers (Arg-1 and CD206). This phenotypic shift was further supported by the restoration of anti-inflammatory cytokine levels, specifically IL-10 and TGF-β1, within the co-culture environment. Mechanistically, this regulatory effect was confirmed to be mediated by paracrine TGF-β1. The status of the TGF-β1 receptor-mediated signaling pathway as the central hub for driving macrophage phenotype conversion was substantiated by both recombinant TGF-β1 (rTGF-β1) simulation experiments and blocking assays using the specific inhibitor LY2109761. By effectively facilitating the polarization transition from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to a pro-healing M2 phenotype, the BMSC-CP resolves the cytokine storm and clears the persistent inflammatory obstacles necessary for successful periodontal regeneration.

The regenerative potential of BMSC-CPs in vivo was validated using a rat mandibular fenestration model. The sustained, high-abundance expression of TGF-β1 observed in the recipient area of the CP group, coupled with a significant increase in the bone volume to total volume (BV/TV) ratio, reflects substantial bone filling and the promotion of periodontal ligament-like fiber formation. However, several limitations of the current study must be acknowledged. Significant anatomical, mechanical, and oral microbiome differences exist between this rodent models and human clinical conditions. While a 6-week observation period demonstrated significant early repair, it was insufficient to evaluate long-term tissue stability or the ultimate metabolic fate of the implanted materials. Due to the constraints of current detection methodologies, it remains challenging to definitively distinguish whether the TGF-β1 within the recipient area originated entirely from the implanted BMSC-CPs or was partially produced by host cells through paracrine induction. Future research incorporating large animal models and in vivo tracing technologies is warranted to further elucidate the molecular crosstalk between donor and host cells, thereby facilitating the clinical translation of this scaffold-free strategy.

In future research, more specific immunomodulatory mechanisms of cell pellets still need to be further explored. Notably, although the TGF-β receptor inhibitor LY2109761 could partly counteract the M2 polarization effect induced by cell pellets, the blocking effect was not entirely complete. For example, via western blot analysis, the expression of M2 markers (Arg-1 and CD206) in the inhibitor-treated group was only significantly lower than that in the cell pellet coculture group (Fig. 5A). As a complex three-dimensional cell structure, cell pellets may exert immunomodulatory effects through multiple mechanisms in addition to secreting TGF-β1. For example, mesenchymal stem cells within cell pellets can directly interact with macrophages through cell‒cell contact, influencing macrophage polarization via surface molecule interactions [22]. Additionally, extracellular matrix components in cell pellets may also interact with macrophages to regulate their functional states [23]. Cell pellets may further release extracellular vesicles carrying various bioactive molecules, such as miRNAs and proteins, which can be taken up by macrophages to regulate their gene expression and functional polarization [24]. In subsequent studies, multiomics joint analysis methods can be employed to comprehensively identify molecules and signaling pathways related to immunomodulation in cell pellets to explore the diversity of their immunomodulatory mechanisms in detail. Moreover, the fate and outcome of cell pellets after implantation in vivo remain to be investigated. It is crucial to clarify the survival time of cell pellets in the host, the process of their integration with surrounding tissues, whether they differentiate into specific cell types involved in periodontal regeneration, and how their structural and functional characteristics change over time. Overall, cell pellets represent a novel scaffold-free tissue engineering material with promising potential in periodontal regeneration. In the future, as research advances and technological breakthroughs emerge, cell pellets are expected to become safe and effective new therapies for periodontitis and FI, offering new hope to patients.

Conclusion

Cell pellets address the core deficits of furcation involvement through the TGF-β1-mediated polarization of M2 macrophages. As a scaffold-free strategy, it circumvents the limitations inherent to exogenous scaffolds, presenting a promising therapeutic approach that integrates immunomodulation and structural support to address FI. This synergy of functions positions cell pellets as robust candidates for advancing FI treatment.
