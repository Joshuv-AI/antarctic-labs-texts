More Than a New Heart: Psychological Adjustment and Quality of Life Following Heart Transplantation, a Mixed‐Methods Study
Authors: Tao Zheng, Elaine A Thompson, Kristoffer Rhoads, Michael V Vitiello, Hyo Chan Park, Cynthia M Dougherty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionHeart transplantation (HTx) offers life‐saving treatment for advanced heart failure. While physical recovery is well‐documented, psychological recovery and health‐related quality of life (HRQOL) during early post‐discharge remains less well understood. This mixed‐methods study aims to describe changes in depression, anxiety, and HRQOL in HTx recipients from hospital discharge to three months, integrated with qualitative perspectives.MethodsThe study enrolled 18 HTx recipients. Patient Health Questionnaire‐8 (PHQ‐8), Generalized Anxiety Disorder‐7 (GAD‐7), and the 36‐Item Short Form Health Survey (SF‐36) were administered at both time points. For scaled responses, change and associations were evaluated using paired t‐tests and correlations; semi‐structured interviews were examined using thematic analysis. Findings were integrated with a convergent matrix approach.ResultsParticipants (mean age 49 ± 15.2) reported no significant changes in depression or anxiety at 3 months. HRQOL improved significantly in SF‐36 physical functioning (p = 0.001), physical role limitations (p = 0.007), bodily pain (p < 0.001), and social functioning (p < 0.001). Qualitative findings characterized emotional recovery as early vulnerability and gradual adjustment.ConclusionsHRQOL improved significantly after discharge, while psychological recovery was unchanged. Integrated findings identified medication‐induced mood changes and social isolation as barriers to the strengthening of emotional well‐being during early recovery.Keywords: anxiety, depression, heart transplantation, mixed methods research, quality of life

Background

Heart transplantation (HTx) is a critical life‐saving intervention for individuals with advanced heart failure (AHF), a condition that poses significant morbidity and mortality risks. Advances in surgical techniques, immunosuppressive therapies, and postoperative care have transformed the field of organ transplantation, resulting in a notable increase in the number of HTx procedures performed globally [1]. This increase underscores the growing recognition of HTx as a viable treatment option for patients with end‐stage heart failure (HF), enabling individuals to achieve not only longer survival but also substantial improvements in health‐related quality of life (HRQOL).

While HTx alleviates the physical symptoms of HF, recipients frequently confront psychological challenges, including depression [2, 3] and anxiety [4, 5] that adversely impact HRQOL. Although the prevalence of these symptoms generally decreases over time following transplantation [4, 5], distress often persists in the early recovery phase, exacerbated by medication side effects and the stress of transitioning into independence [6, 7, 8]. Most existing data are derived from variable time points ranging from discharge to 14 years post‐transplant [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16], rather than fixed intervals during the acute post‐discharge phase of recovery. Consequently, the progression of psychological symptoms and HRQOL during the critical three‐month window immediately following hospital discharge remains underexplored.

In light of these research gaps, the present study aims to address the following objectives, (1) describe changes in psychological symptoms and HRQOL from hospital discharge to 3 months after HTx, (2) examine relationships between changes in psychological symptoms and changes in HRQOL at 3 months post‐discharge after HTx, and (3) explore in‐depth patient experiences, challenges, and perspectives on changes in psychological symptoms and HRQOL during 3 months post‐discharge after HTx. Characterizing these outcomes and their inter‐relationships provide valuable insights into the complexities of post‐transplant recovery, inform management strategies to optimize long‐term outcomes, and enhance HRQOL for HTx recipients.

Methods

Study Design

This descriptive study used a prospective, concurrent mixed‐methods design. Data were collected at two time points: hospital discharge and 3 months later. The mixed‐methods approach integrated subjective and objective outcomes while simultaneously integrating participant experiences and perspectives.

Setting and Procedure

Participants were recruited between May 2023 and June 2024 at a large academic quaternary medical center in the Pacific Northwest. HTx recipients were screened for inclusion/exclusion criteria using electronic health records (EHR). Inclusion criteria included: (1) 18 years or older, (2) receipt of an HTx with planned hospital discharge to home, and (3) English language proficiency needed to complete questionnaires and participate in interviews. Exclusion criteria were a diagnosis of stroke post‐HTx. All participants were invited to participate in a 1‐1 interview at each time point to discuss their experiences, challenges, and perspectives on psychological symptoms and HRQOL. Baseline interviews characterized participants’ experiences, challenges, and perspectives regarding selected outcomes, and follow‐up interviews assessed changes at 3 months post‐discharge. The medical center and university Institutional Review Board approved the research protocol. All participants signed informed consent prior to data collection.

Quantitative Data Collection and Analysis

Demographic and clinical variables were collected at both time points using standardized data abstraction from EHR, including age, sex, race, years of education, and relevant clinical information. Data collection visits coincided with routine clinic visits to reduce participant burden.

Outcome Measures

Patient Health Questionnaire‐8 (PHQ‐8) [17], a valid tool for measuring depression in HF [18] and HTx [19, 20] consists of 8 items with total scores ranging from 0 to 24. Higher scores indicate greater symptom severity.

General Anxiety Disorder‐7 (GAD‐7) [21], a validated screening tool for anxiety in HTx recipients [20] consists of 7 items, with total scores ranging from 0 to 21. Higher scores reflect higher anxiety.

HRQOL was measured using the 36‐item Short Form Health Survey (SF‐36), which yields 8 subscales: Physical Functioning, Role Physical, Bodily Pain, General Health, Vitality, Social Functioning, Role Emotional, and Mental Health. These are aggregated into Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) scores. All scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores reflecting better health or fewer limitations. The SF‐36 has been used in previous studies of HTx recipients [22, 23].

Quantitative questionnaire data were analyzed using IBM SPSS v29 (IBM Corporation, Somers, NY). Descriptive statistics were used to describe demographic and clinical characteristics. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± SD), and categorical variables were expressed with frequency and percentage (n, %). Paired sample t‐tests were used to compare repeated measures at hospital discharge (Time 1) and 3 months after discharge (Time 2). Changes in outcome measures were computed by calculating the difference between the 2 time points. Pearson correlations were used to evaluate interrelationships between changes in psychological symptoms and changes in HRQOL. A significance‐value was set at p ≤ 0.05 (two tailed).

Qualitative Data Collection and Analysis

Semi‐structured interviews were conducted within one week of the in‐person study visits via phone or in person, based on participant preference. All interviews were audio recorded in a private space, using study identification numbers to ensure privacy. Immediately following each interview, the audio files were uploaded to Otter.ai (Mountain View, California, USA) for initial transcription. During the initial review of transcripts, all identifiable information was removed to protect confidentiality. The transcriptions were reviewed for completeness and corrected for accuracy. The final versions were imported to Atlas.ti 25 (Atlas.ti, Berlin, Germany) for coding and qualitative analysis.

Qualitative data were processed in accordance with best practices as outlined by Creswell [24]. The transcribed interviews were organized by baseline interview or follow‐up and analyzed using thematic analysis. Each interview was read repeatedly to gain an overall understanding and to identify prominent themes and patterns within predetermined categories (psychological symptoms, HRQOL, and their interrelationships). Data were coded by labeling shared concepts and codes were grouped into larger categories and refined into major themes. To enhance confirmability and minimize personal bias, all interviews and the initial coding scheme were independently reviewed by a second investigator. Discrepancies were discussed until consensus was reached. Throughout the analytic process, memos were maintained to document and organize emerging thoughts, ideas, and reflections, thereby facilitating reflexibility and rigorous analysis. Finally, summaries of each interview were constructed from the memos to build coherent narratives and draw case‐specific conclusions.

Quantitative and Qualitative Data Integration

Integration of quantitative and qualitative data was guided by recommended methods [25], using triangulation to discover changes in reported psychological symptoms and HRQOL, and explore their interrelationships. An informational matrix was based on two categories captured by the research measures: psychological symptoms and HRQOL. Qualitative codes and overall impressions were incorporated into the matrix under the respective outcome category for each case. Next, quantitative results were systematically compared with qualitative findings to identify convergence and divergence. Then comparisons were made between time points to assess change over time. Triangulation of the quantitative and qualitative data enabled a deeper understanding of individual experiences, challenges, and perspectives related to these health outcomes. Rigor was maintained by keeping a detailed audit trail throughout the integration process.

Results

Participants’ Characteristics

A total of 69 HTx recipients were screened. Recipients with prolonged hospitalization exceeding one month (n = 15), medical complications (n = 11), or logistical barriers (n = 13) were not recruited to minimize cofounding. Of the 30 eligible candidates approached, 12 declined participation, attributing their refusal to the overwhelming burden of care and recovery demands. Of 18 recipients (mean age 49 ± 15.2) enrolled, 17 completed data collection at both time points; one participant was lost to follow‐up. Most of the sample were male (66.7%, n = 12); the predominant etiology of HF was nonischemic cardiomyopathy (66.7%, n = 12). Heightened clinical acuity was evident, with 55.6% classified as New York Heart Association functional class IV at the time of transplant, and 33.3% requiring mechanical circulatory support devices prior to transplant. Pertinent comorbidities are presented in Table 1. At baseline, documented history included depression in two (11.1%) and anxiety in 4 participants (22.2%). Three participants (16.7%) were prescribed antidepressant medications, increasing to 5 participants (27.8%) at follow‐up, while no participants were prescribed anti‐anxiety medications at either time points.

Changes in Depressive Symptoms

Mean PHQ‐8 scores decreased non‐significantly from 5.24 ± 3.60 at hospital discharge to 5.18 ± 0.23 (p = 0.95) at 3 months (Table 2), suggesting that depressive symptoms remained relatively stable during the immediate recovery period post‐discharge. At baseline, 38.89% reported minimal or no depression (score < 4) and 50% exhibited mild depression (scores 5–9). A smaller proportion of participants reported moderate depression (n = 1) and moderately severe depression (n = 1). These findings highlight that most participants experienced either minimal or mild depressive symptoms at the time of discharge.

At 3 months, a slight reduction in depressive symptoms was observed. The number of participants with minimal or no symptoms increased to 8 (47.06%), while those with mild symptoms decreased to 8 (47.06%). The number of participants reporting moderate depression remained unchanged (n = 1), and no participants reported moderately severe symptoms. Notably, antidepressant use increased from 3 participants (16.7%) at baseline to 5 participants (29.4%) at follow‐up, suggesting that some participants may have initiated pharmacologic treatment during this period. The increased usage of antidepressant prescriptions may reflect clinical efforts to address persistent or emerging depressive symptoms, even in the absence of significant group‐level change in PHQ‐8 scores.

Changes in Anxiety Symptoms

Mean GAD‐7 scores decreased from 3.71 ± 3.26 at hospital discharge to 3.24 ± 3.62 at 3 months (p = 0.58), suggesting that anxiety symptoms were relatively stable over time. At baseline, 11 participants (61.11%) reported minimal anxiety (scores < 4), 6 participants (33.33%) had mild anxiety (scores 5–9), and 1 participant (5.56%) reported severe anxiety (scores 15–21). Importantly, no participants reported moderate anxiety at baseline, indicating that most experienced relatively low levels of anxiety.

At the 3‐month follow‐up, there was a slight reduction in the proportion of participants with minimal anxiety, which decreased to 58.82% (n = 10). Meanwhile, the percentage of participants reporting mild anxiety remained consistent at 35.29% (n = 6). Only one participant indicated moderate anxiety, and severe anxiety was no longer reported. The overall trend suggested that anxiety levels remained relatively stable between discharge and the 3‐month follow‐up, with most participants with minimal to mild anxiety.

Changes in Health‐Related Quality of Life

Significant improvements were observed in subscales of the SF‐36 quality‐of‐life measure from hospital discharge to three months. Statistically significant improvements were observed in physical function (t = 3.94, p = 0.001), role limitations due to physical problems (t = 3.08, p = 0.007), bodily pain (t = 4.24, p < 0.001), and social functioning (t = 4.42, p < 0.001). The general health, vitality and mental health subscales showed slight improvements, though not statistically significant. The PCS improved significantly at 3 months (t = 4.35, p < 0.001), reflecting enhanced physical well‐being. In contrast, the mental MCS scores remained slightly below general population norms and exhibited a small, non‐significant increase (t = 0.79, p = 0.45) over time, indicating relatively stable mental health over time. No change was observed in role limitation due to emotional problems.

Associations Between Changes in Psychological Symptoms and Changes in HRQOL

Correlational analysis was used to describe associations between the degree of change in psychological symptom scores and in HRQOL over time (Table 3). For instance, the amount of decrease in depressive symptoms was significantly associated with the degree that vitality (r = −0.59, p = 0.01) and MCS (r = −0.51, p = 0.04) increased, indicating that over time HTx recipients with enhanced vitality or overall improved mental HRQOL reported greater reductions in depressive symptoms as well.

In terms of anxiety symptoms, a significant negative association was observed with the role limitation physical subscale (r = −0.58, p = 0.02) and the role limitation emotional scale (r = −0.61, p = 0.01), suggesting that decreases in anxiety over time was linked to perceptions of fewer limitations in daily activities as related to physical or emotional health. Theme 1. Transplant‐Related Medication Caused Mood Changes

Participants attributed emotional volatility to prednisone rather than depression. Respondents described uncharacteristic emotional lability, noting that “everything makes me cry,” including “regular commercials,” and that “emotional swings” only resolved once prednisone dosages were reduced.

Theme 2. Ongoing Anxiety and Stress Persisted Throughout Recovery

Immediate fears of cardiac failure and transplant rejection were replaced by logistical anxieties. Financial strain was a primary stressor, with participants worrying about “where am I going to get the money” while unable to work. In addition, medical post‐traumatic stress symptoms persisted for some participants. One individual noted that “biopsy day” triggered traumatic memories of prior ventricular tachycardia storms.

Theme 3. Isolation After Returning Home From Temporary Housing

The transition from temporary housing (“healing houses”) back to private residences precipitated loneliness. Participants contrasted the “safe and secure” healing house environment, filled with family against being “home alone” and lonely after returning to their permanent residences.

Theme 4. Emotional Fluctuations During Recovery Related to new Conditions

Distress also stemmed from non‐cardiac limitations. Tremors, a side effect of immunosuppressants on fine motor skills, caused frustration in social settings, such as inability to hold a spoon. One participant described this as an emotional “roller coaster.” Others reported disappointment over new orthopedic issues that prevented them from playing with grandchildren and going on outings with the family.

Qualitative Findings on Health‐Related Quality of Life After Hospital Discharge

Qualitative themes that informed the significant quantitative improvements in physical and social functioning included: 1) improved physical strength and mobility, 2) resuming hobbies and social activities, 3) easier completion of daily tasks, and 4) reductions in pain, discomfort, and stress.

Theme 1. Improved Physical Strength and Mobility

Consistent with the significant increase in SF‐36 physical scores, participants described a clear trajectory of gaining strength. One individual indicated progressing from “considerably weaker” to standing for “an hour” to wash dishes and clean the kitchen. Others described progressing from limited movement to being “all over the place,” including walking at the mall, grocery shopping, and engaging in structured exercise using treadmills and bikes during cardiac rehabilitation.

Theme 2. Resuming Hobbies and Social Activities

Participants reported a return to meaningful leisure activities that signified a restoration of normalcy. Narratives included the ability to engage in physically demanding hobbies, such as playing “18 holes of golf” in the heat, an activity that previously would have caused the individual to “pass out by hole nine.” Others highlighted getting out more to fish or spend time with family, noting they were “looking at life at a different way now.”

Theme 3. Easier Completion of Daily Tasks

The burden of basic self‐care and domestic tasks diminished over time. Participants described moving beyond the “early stage” of requiring constant assistance to a point at which they “don't even think about” performing daily tasks. Return to independence was further supported by descriptions of renewed energy for creative pursuits, such as weaving in a studio, rather than “just sitting.”

Theme 4. Reductions in Pain, Discomfort, and Stress

The overall sense of bodily pain and situational stress improved by 3 months. Participants expressed relief at leaving the immediate area of the transplant center and noting that the fear and stress associated with navigating an urban environment had dissipated. Even those with residual issues, such as atrophy requiring a cane, acknowledged that compared to pre‐transplant, their physical condition was “better than it has been for years.”

Integration of Quantitative and Qualitative Findings

The quantitative results indicated minimal and statistically insignificant changes in depressive and anxiety symptoms from hospital discharge to 3‐month follow‐up, suggesting relative stability in psychological symptoms during early recovery. The qualitative data corroborated this trend. While some participants reported feeling calmer and less anxious due to improved cardiac function and reduced fear of sudden cardiac events, persistent stressors such as financial concerns, loneliness, and medication‐related mood fluctuations limited broader psychological improvement (Table 4). These narratives align with the observed lack of significant change in mental health subscales and the MCS score of the SF‐36, reinforcing that physical recovery alone does not guarantee parallel gains in emotional well‐being. Conversely, qualitative accounts strongly support the significant quantitative improvements in physical domains of quality of life, as participants consistently reported enhanced mobility, pain relief, and engagement in valued activities. The convergence underscores the multidimensional nature of post‐transplant recovery, in which physical gains are evident and measurable, whereas psychological adaptation lags behind physical improvements.

Discussion

This study found small, non‐significant reductions in depression and anxiety, with average scores consistently remaining in the minimal to mild range, from hospital discharge to 3 months follow‐up. Statistically significant improvements were found in physical function, role limitations due to physical problems, bodily pain, and social function. Recovery of physical function post‐transplant was dramatic, whereas psychological recovery was slower, cofounded by complex medical treatments and procedures. Individual narratives revealed a trajectory of emotional recovery characterized by early vulnerability, gradual adaptation, and emerging psychological recovery. These findings provide important insights into the interplay between physical and psychological recovery in the early post‐discharge period.

Compared to the existing literature on AHF populations, the current HTx sample reported a lower level of depressive symptoms. While Hammash et al. [26] note that depressive symptoms are frequent in the HF populations, the current cohort reported mean PHQ‐8 of 5.24 ± 3.60 at discharge, indicating minimal to mild symptom burden. This difference may reflect not only a distinct psychological context following successful transplant but also the potential benefit of HTx in restoring vitality, which was significantly correlated with reduced depressive symptoms (r = −0.59, p = 0.01). Similarly, comparisons with published GAD‐7 scores in AHF revealed a stark contrast in anxiety burden. While Tully et al. [27]. reported mean GAD‐7 scores of approximately 14.16 ± 5.30 in HF cohorts with comorbid psychiatric needs, the current study sample demonstrated a substantially lower level of anxiety 3.71 ± 3.26 at discharge. Low anxiety levels may be attributed to physical improvements, symptom relief, and the reassurance of surviving a high‐risk procedure, supported in the qualitative narratives on regained control and gratitude. Consequently, the lack of statistically significant changes in depressive and anxiety symptoms likely reflects a combination of limited statistical power due to the small sample size and the low baseline symptom burden, which left minimal room for measurable improvement (floor effect). However, the low scores at baseline may also reflect selection bias, as individuals with prolonged hospitalizations or medical complications, who likely harbor higher level of distress, were excluded from enrollment. Thus, while findings suggest that HTx confers immediate psychological benefits that distinguish this population from individuals with advanced HF, these results may be most applicable to recipients with uncomplicated recovery trajectories.

The positive trajectory in physical health observed in the study aligns with prior research in HTx, though the magnitude of improvement appears influenced by pre‐transplant clinical acuity. When compared to the study by Kugler et al. [22], the current cohort reported lower physical function scores at 3 months post‐discharge (approximately 16 weeks post‐HTx) than Kugler's cohort assessed at only 6 weeks post‐transplant. This disparity, despite the longer recovery period in the current study, is likely attributable to broader inclusion criteria that retained participants with significant comorbidities, such as diabetes and mechanical circulatory support. Similarly, PCS scores at 3 months remained below those reported by Rolid et al. [23], whose sample was assessed at 11 weeks post‐surgery and consisted of recipients capable of performing high‐intensity interval training. Despite these physical disparities, the current cohort demonstrated notably higher social functioning than Rolid's group, supporting qualitative findings regarding the protective role of family involvement during early recovery. Collectively, these comparisons reinforce that while early physical recovery is rapid, it is heavily dependent on baseline comorbidities. In contrast, psychological adjustment often progresses more gradually.

Qualitative findings from this study further illuminate the psychological challenges experienced by HTx recipients, particularly during transitional periods after hospital discharge. The stress associated with relocation to transplant housing, resumption in independent living, and prolonged unemployment can significantly impact mental health. These transitions may evoke feelings of uncertainty and anxiety, suggesting that there is a critical window of time following HTx where recipients may benefit from additional psychological and social support. The significant associations observed in this study between anxiety symptoms and physical role functioning underscore the intricate link between psychological well‐being and physical recovery, highlighting the necessity for integrated care models that address both domains.

Future Research

While the study highlights substantial improvements in physical HRQOL domains, it also raises important questions regarding the psychological aspects of recovery. The lack of significant improvement in mental health scores, alongside qualitative reports of isolation and financial stress, suggests mental health support may not be keeping pace with physical rehabilitation. To bridge this gap, future research should explicitly examine these unmet psychological and psychosocial needs to guide evidence‐based practice. Consequently, clinical interventions must be prioritized, specifically the implementation of peer support networks and stage‐specific psychosocial programs designed to better prepare recipients for the emotional complexities of recovery. Further descriptive work to outline recovery 6 and 12 months after HTx can identify important timepoints in the psychological processes.

Qualitative findings of isolation suggest a need for transitional interventions. Future studies should evaluate if peer mentorship can mitigate the loneliness experienced when moving from temporary housing to home. Additionally, findings indicated individuals were surprised by “emotional swings” from steroids and new physical limitations. Future research should assess stage‐specific education to manage expectations regarding medication side‐effects and the distinction between cardiac success and physical recovery. While our small sample size provides valuable preliminary data on early recovery, future multi‐site studies utilizing longitudinal follow‐ups (for example, at 6, 9, and 12 months) are needed to fully capture the psychological impacts of rejection episodes, biopsy‐result anxiety, and immunosuppression adjustments.

Strengths and Limitations

This study described recovery in psychological symptoms and HRQOL in HTx recipients from early hospital discharge to 3 months. Both qualitative and quantitative measures were employed to understand important trajectories of early recovery. A key strength is the prospective longitudinal design with fixed intervals, capturing the underexplored critical windows immediately post‐discharge. This approach distinguished this study from existing research based cross‐sectional data or variable follow‐up time points, ranging from discharge to several years post‐transplant. Second, the concurrent mixed‐methods design allowed for a rigorous integration of data. While survey scores suggested statistical stability in mental health, qualitative interviews revealed that this was a complex balance between financial strain, isolation, and medication side effects. The finding contextualized the discontinuity of physical and psychological recovery. Third, despite the population's medical complexity, 94% of participants remained engaged and completed the study at follow‐up.

Study limitations must likewise be considered. First, the small, single‐center convenience sample limits generalizability and statistical power to detect moderate effect sizes. Second, the repeated‐measures design was limited to two time‐points that may not capture the longer‐term trajectories of psychological recovery or fluctuations in HRQOL. Lastly, the baseline assessment, referred to as the “discharge” time point, was conducted during the first post‐discharge clinic visit, rather than on the exact day of hospital discharge. While this timing closely reflects the early recovery period, the interval between discharge and data collection may have influenced participants’ perceptions of psychological symptoms and HRQOL.

Conclusions

In the first three months following HTx, physical health and HRQOL improved between hospital discharge and 3 months, while depressive and anxiety symptoms remained stable at notably lower levels that previously reported. Qualitative findings indicate that this period involves complex emotional transitions, and that physical recovery does not automatically confer improved psychological well‐being. These results highlight the critical need for integrated care models and targeted psychosocial interventions to address transitional stress and emotional vulnerability. Future research should prioritize larger, diverse samples to identify stage‐specific strategies that promote holistic, patient‐centered outcomes in this medically complex population.
