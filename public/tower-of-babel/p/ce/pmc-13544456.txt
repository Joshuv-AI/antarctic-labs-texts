Chronic Rhinosinusitis and Nasal Polyps in Eosinophilic Granulomatosis With Polyangiitis: A Scoping Review
Authors: Alisha Sharma, Alyssa Y Li, Teruko Kishibe, Dennis Wong, Kevin M Sanders, Maggie Jiang, Heedo Lee, Christine Song, Kieran Mcintyre, Christian Pagnoux, Yvonne Chan, John M Lee, Daniel J Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) is one of the most common manifestations of eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA), often preceding the diagnosis of systemic vasculitis by several years. The sinonasal presentation of EGPA typically resembles CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP), making it clinically indistinguishable from those without EGPA. This overlap frequently contributes to significant diagnostic delays. This scoping review summarizes the prevalence, diagnosis, management, and outcomes of CRSwNP and CRS without nasal polyps (CRSsNP) in EGPA.MethodsWe searched MEDLINE, Embase, CENTRAL, CINAHL, and Web of Science from inception to June 4, 2025, for studies reporting CRS in patients with EGPA. Eligible designs included trials, cohorts, case series, and narrative reviews. Non‐English studies without translation and articles without primary data were excluded. Outcomes included CRS prevalence, diagnostic modalities, management strategies, and sinonasal outcomes. This study is registered with the Open Science Framework (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/DA5CK).ResultsEighty‐three full‐text and 26 abstracts were included, for a total of 109 studies, of which 88 studies (74 full‐text and 14 abstracts were included in the analysis). Across the analyzed studies, 1087 patients with CRS and EGPA were included. Median age was 53 years (range: 14.5–75; n = 154), and 189 (48.5%; n = 390) were male. CRSwNP and CRSsNP occurred in 753 (69.3%) and 334 (30.7%) of patients, respectively. The median time from CRS onset to EGPA diagnosis was 66 months (range: 1–120; n = 35). Among 857 patients, EGPA was classified using ACR 1990 criteria (51.7%), ACR/EULAR 2022 (2.7%), and combined frameworks (45.6%). Diagnostic modalities included CT sinus (97.6%), nasal endoscopy (94.7%), and sinus biopsy (91.8%). Biopsy‐proven vasculitis was confirmed in three (1.3%) patients. Systemic steroids were used in 362 (91%; n = 398) patients, immunosuppressants in 290 (86.6%; n = 336) patients, and biologics in 154 (58.9%; n = 262) patients, primarily mepolizumab (73.3%), dupilumab (23.4%), and benralizumab (11.7%). Complete CRS remission was achieved in 106 (67.9%) patients following medical therapy and in 11 (18.2%) patients after combined surgical and medical treatment. Among those receiving dupilumab (n = 15), mean SNOT‐22 improved by 25.2 (n = 9), with complete remission in six (83.3%) patients.ConclusionsCRSwNP is the predominant sinonasal phenotype of EGPA. Management is shifting toward targeted biologics with evidence supporting mepolizumab and benralizumab for systemic disease control. Standardized outcome reporting and prospective comparative studies are needed to optimize management and reduce diagnostic delays.Keywords: Churg–Strauss syndrome, nasal polyps, rhinosinusitis, sinusitis

Introduction

Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) affects approximately 8.7% of the population worldwide [1]. It affects approximately 8%–12% of the US adult population, with chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) representing 1%–2% prevalence, translating to approximately 3 million individuals [2].

Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) is a rare, systemic anti‐neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)‐associated vasculitis (AAV) characterized by eosinophil‐rich granulomatous and necrotizing inflammation involving small‐ to medium‐sized blood vessels [3, 4]. While AAV collectively has estimate of up to 42.1 per 100,000 in the United States [5], EGPA is the rarest subset with a global prevalence of 15.3 cases per million (95% confidence interval: 11.9–19.6), based on a systematic review and meta‐analysis [6].

EGPA is distinguished from other AAVs by its nearly universal association with adult‐onset asthma and peripheral eosinophilia. Although systemic vasculitic involvement often affects the heart, peripheral nerves, and kidneys, upper and lower airway manifestations are common and often precede other systemic symptoms [3, 7]. Sinonasal involvement is nearly ubiquitous: 97.4% of patients have CRS or rhinitis, and 52%–86% have CRSwNP. This represents approximately 7000 EGPA patients in the United States with nasal polyps, or 0.2%–0.3% of all CRSwNP cases [7, 8, 9, 10]. Only 2.6% have normal nasal findings [8]. Despite the rarity, patients with EGPA and CRSwNP often experience a highly refractory disease course, necessitating the review of current practice and identification of ongoing clinical challenges and unmet therapeutic needs.

The sinonasal phenotype typically resembles eosinophilic CRS (eCRS) or CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP), making it clinically indistinguishable from non‐vasculitic eCRS [11], or severe eosinophilic asthma, both of which are nearly universal in EGPA.

This symptomatic overlap contributes to substantial diagnostic delay with siloed treatment for each symptomatic manifestation rather than a holistic approach [7]. Despite the high prevalence of sinonasal involvement, existing literature on CRS in EGPA remains fragmented, with inconsistent definitions and variable reporting of diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

Recent developments in biologic therapies have transformed the management paradigm. Mepolizumab demonstrated improved remission rates in a landmark randomized trial for relapsing or refractory EGPA [12]. Likewise, a pivotal multi‐center, double‐blind randomized controlled trial phase 3 study also demonstrated that benralizumab is non‐inferior to mepolizumab for inducing remission in relapsing or refractory EGPA [13].

While existing reviews have addressed systemic EGPA management [14, 15] and eCRS/CRSwNP separately [16, 17], no comprehensive synthesis has examined CRS specifically within the EGPA population, encompassing its prevalence, diagnostic approach, and management. This gap leaves clinicians without clear, evidence‐based guidance on the evaluation and treatment of sinonasal disease in EGPA.

Therefore, we conducted a scoping review to map the current evidence on CRS specifically in EGPA, with a particular focus on its prevalence, diagnostic approaches, and management, including systemic EGPA treatments, local sinonasal treatments, and biologic options.

Methods

Protocol

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the methodological framework outlined by the JBI [18] for scoping reviews and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) [19]. The study protocol was registered with the Open Science Framework (DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/DA5CK).

Information Sources and Search Strategy

A comprehensive search strategy was developed by an experienced information specialist (T.K.) in consultation with the research team. The following electronic databases were searched from inception to June 4, 2025: MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (Ovid), CINAHL Complete (EBSCO), and Web of Science (Clarivate).

Search strategies combined controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH, Emtree) and free‐text terms for the two core concepts, CRS and EGPA, linked with Boolean operators. No date, language, or geographic restrictions were applied. The full search strategy for each database is provided in Appendix A.

The final search was peer reviewed by an independent information specialist using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) checklist [19]. Search results were exported to EndNote (Clarivate) for deduplication and then imported into Covidence for screening and data management. Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify additional eligible sources.

Eligibility Criteria and Selection of Sources

Eligible sources included primary studies (prospective or retrospective cohorts, cross‐sectional studies, case series, and case reports), systematic or scoping reviews, clinical guidelines, and relevant grey literature (e.g., conference abstracts, theses) reporting on a primary focus of or subgroup of patients with CRS with or without nasal polyps in the context of EGPA.

Studies were included if they involved human participants diagnosed with EGPA based on the 1990 or 2022 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria, the 1994, 2002, or 2012 Chapel Hill Consensus Conference nomenclature, or equivalent diagnostic criteria, and explicitly reported CRSwNP or CRSsNP within EGPA populations. Studies describing EGPA with nonspecific otolaryngologic or sinonasal involvement were excluded. These studies reporting nonspecific otolaryngologic manifestations in patients with EGPA were instead summarized and described separately in a dedicated section of this scoping review.

Exclusion criteria included non‐human studies, non‐English publications without available translation, editorials, letters, or opinion pieces lacking primary data, and studies focusing exclusively on CRS without reference to EGPA.

Title/Abstract and full‐text screening were performed independently and in duplicate by two reviewers (A.S. and A.Y.L.). Conflicts were resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer (D.L.). A PRISMA‐ScR flow diagram was generated to document study selection (Figure 1).

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

Data extraction was performed in parallel by two reviewers (A.S. and A.Y.L.) using a pretested data extraction form, with discrepancies resolved by a third independent reviewer (D.L.). Extracted data included study characteristics (e.g., author, year, country, study design, and sample size), population details (e.g., EGPA diagnostic criteria, demographics, comorbidities, CRS phenotype), diagnostic approaches (e.g., imaging modalities, nasal endoscopy findings, sinus biopsy or cytology, laboratory markers), management strategies (e.g., local therapies, systemic therapies), and outcomes (e.g., sinonasal symptom improvement, disease remission or recurrence, endoscopic or radiologic scores, and quality‐of‐life measures). All extracted data are available on request to the author. Consistent with JBI and PRISMA‐ScR guidance, no formal critical appraisal was performed, as the objective of this scoping review was to map the breadth and nature of available evidence rather than to assess study quality or synthesize effect estimates.

Synthesis

A descriptive synthesis of the analyzed data was performed to address the review's key domains, including prevalence and clinical characteristics of CRS in EGPA, diagnostic workup and investigative modalities, and management strategies and outcomes.

Quantitative data (e.g., frequencies, percentages, medians, ranges) were summarized in tabular format, supplemented by narrative synthesis to describe key findings, identify trends, and highlight gaps in the literature. Given growing clinical interest in targeted biologics, a subgroup analysis was performed focusing on patients treated with dupilumab for CRS in the context of EGPA [20].

Results

A total of 1603 studies were identified in our literature search, of which 109 met inclusion criteria (Figure 1). Study types included retrospective cohort studies (n = 49, 44.9%), case reports (n = 20, 18.3%), case series (n = 15, 13.8%), prospective cohort studies (n = 9, 8.3%), cross‐sectional studies (n = 9, 8.3%), narrative reviews (n = 4, 3.7%), one case–control study (0.9%), one randomized controlled trial (0.9%), one systematic review (0.9%), and one systematic review and meta‐analysis (0.9%) (Table S1).

Studies were mostly full‐text (n = 83, 76.1%), and 26 were abstracts (23.9%). Of these studies, 88 were included in the final analysis, of which 74 were full‐text and 14 were abstracts. Geographic distribution was broad: Europe (n = 50, 46%), North America (n = 19, 17.4%), Asia (n = 14, 12.8%), multinational (n = 10, 9.2%), Middle East (n = 7, 6.4%), South America (n = 1, 0.9%), and Oceania (n = 1, 0.9%). Over half (58.7%) were published in 2020 or later. Only 29.4% (n = 32) explicitly focused on CRS in EGPA as a primary objective (Table S1).

Patient Characteristics

Across all studies, 1087 patients with EGPA and CRS were identified (Table 1). The median age was 53 years (range: 14.5–75; n = 154). Among 390 patients with reported sex, 189 (48.5%) were male. Race and ethnicity were rarely reported (n = 139); one (0.7%) was Asian, five (3.6%) were Black, 14 (10.1%) were Central Asian, one (0.7%) was East Asian, and 118 (84.9%) were White.

CRS phenotype was documented for all 1087 patients: CRSwNP 69.3% (n = 753) and CRSsNP 30.7% (n = 334). Among patients with symptom reporting (Table 1), the most frequent were nasal congestion (75.6%), nasal discharge (71.8%), postnasal drip (86.7%), facial pain/pressure (69.6%), and hyposmia (53.2%). Asthma was present in 98.9%, and 20% had aspirin‐exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD), also known as Samter's Triad, which consists of nasal polyps, asthma, and respiratory reactions to nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs.

Prior sinonasal surgery was reported for 243 patients, of whom 219 (90.1%) had undergone endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS) and/or nasal polypectomy. The median number of prior sinonasal surgeries was one (range: 1–3; n = 235). The number of surgeries for the remaining patients was not reported. Median reported follow‐up was 22 months (range: 1.8–56.4) (Table 1).

Diagnostic Approaches

Classification Frameworks

Among 849 patients with available data, ACR 1990 criteria were used in 51.7%, ACR/EULAR 2022 in 2.7%, and combined frameworks (i.e., more than one of ACR 1990, ACR/EULAR 2022, Lanham's criteria, MIRRA trial, CHCC 1994, CHCC 2012) in 45.1%. The most common combinations were ACR 1990 and Chapel Hill 2012 (22.9%) as well as ACR 1990 and Lanham's criteria (10.6%) (Table 2).

Investigations

In studies where data were available, imaging and endoscopy were performed: CT sinus in 97.6%, nasal endoscopy in 94.7%, and sinus biopsy in 91.8% of reported cases.

Reporting of specific findings was inconsistent. On CT, sinus opacification was noted in 11.3%, while 86.7% did not describe radiologic details. Nasal endoscopy reported the presence of nasal polyps in 15.3%, with no specified findings in 83.5%. Reported histopathology from sinus biopsies showed eosinophilic infiltration in 15.6% (35/224), neutrophilic infiltration in 6.3% (14/224), vasculitis in 1.3% (3/224), granulomatous inflammation in 0.4% (1/224), and fungal bodies in 0.4% (1/224). Remaining biopsy results were not reported (76%, 170/224). Biopsies were most often obtained from nasal polyps (21.4%; 48/224), nasal mucosa (13.4%; 30/224), or inferior turbinate (12.5%; 28/224).

In reported cases, laboratory evaluation of the clinical presentation of patients (Table 2) showed ANCA positivity in 33%, predominantly p‐ANCA (12.7%), c‐ANCA (1.7%), and a non‐reported subtype (18.6%). ANCA negativity was in 67%. Peripheral eosinophilia was reported in 76.5% of reported cases. Elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 36%, ESR 45.5%) and IgE (15.2%) were variably documented. The median duration of CRS prior to EGPA diagnosis was 66 months (range: 1–120; n = 35).

Management Strategies

Surgical and Medical Therapies

Among 64 patients with surgical details, 87.5% underwent ESS. All patients undergoing ESS had concurrent medical regimen, while those patients who did not undergo ESS received medical therapy instead. In reported cases, medical therapy included systemic steroids in 91% of patients, immunosuppressants in 86.6% of patients, intranasal corticosteroids (INCS) in 88.3% of patients, and biologics in 58.8% of patients. Systemic corticosteroids (n = 362) included methylprednisone (35.4%), prednisone (23.5%), and betamethasone (1.4%). Immunosuppressants (n = 290) most commonly included methotrexate (53.4%), cyclophosphamide (42.8%), azathioprine (31%), and mycophenolate mofetil (17.6%). Intranasal corticosteroids (INCS) (n = 181) were frequently used: beclomethasone (15.5%), mometasone (13.3%), fluticasone (11.6%), and budesonide (10.5%). Biologic therapies (n = 154) included mepolizumab (73.3%), rituximab (11.7%), benralizumab (12.3%), dupilumab (23.4%), omalizumab (1.9%), and tezepelumab (0.6%) (Table 3).

To examine temporal trends in treatment patterns, studies were stratified by publication year: before 2000, 2000–2009, 2010–2019, and 2020 onwards (Figure S1). Systemic corticosteroids were the most consistently reported treatment modality across all years. INCS use was reported sporadically in earlier years, with increased use in publications post‐2020. Immunosuppressant use was prevalent in publications from before 2000, through to 2010, however declined in frequency in studies published from 2020 onwards. A few isolated cases reported use of biologic therapy in the 2016–2019 period, with marked increase in use post‐2020.

Outcomes

Among patients receiving combined surgical and medical therapy (n = 11), 18.2% achieved complete remission of CRS, with no documented recurrences. With medical therapy (n = 156), remission occurred in 67.9%, recurrence in 23.1%, and outcomes were unreported in 9% (Table 4). Patients who achieved complete remission had symptoms that were well‐controlled through medical treatment and continued to stay on medical treatment.

Mepolizumab Subgroup

A total of 113 patients received mepolizumab, with a median age of 48 years (range: 13–76). Among 64 patients with reported sex, 29 (45.3%) were male. CRSwNP was present in 28 (24.8%) patients, CRSsNP in 22 (19.5%), and the specific CRS phenotype was not reported in 63 (55.7%) patients. Asthma was documented in 55 (94.8%) patients (n = 58). Of those with reported data, all patients experienced nasal congestion (100%, n = 10), nasal discharge (100%, n = 2), postnasal drip (100%, n = 2), and hyposmia (100%, n = 4).

Among 62 patients with reported ANCA status, 51 (82.3%) were ANCA‐negative and 11 (17.7%) ANCA‐positive. Eosinophilia was present in 50 patients (92%; n = 46), and IgE was elevated in all four patients. CRS‐specific diagnostic investigations were inconsistently reported. On CT (n = 8), sinus opacification was present in all these patients, nasal endoscopy findings (n = 16) reported polyps in three (18.8%) patients, and sinus biopsy (n = 8) demonstrated eosinophilic inflammation in four (50%) patients and vasculitis in one (12.5%).

Combination therapies were frequent, with some therapies overlapping with one another, or being used separately intermittently to treat various different symptoms. Systemic steroids were used in 103 patients, most commonly methylprednisone (9.7%), with the majority unreported (78.6%). Intranasal corticosteroids, predominantly beclomethasone (20.4%), were documented in 103 patients. Twenty‐five patients received immunosuppressants, and 66 received another biologic. The biologics were most often dupilumab (6.1%), benralizumab (1.5%), or not reported (89.4%).

Median follow‐up was 34.2 months (range: 12–56.4) for 23 patients with available data. Among 27 patients with CRS outcome data, 24 (88.9%) achieved complete CRS remission with medical therapy, while three (11.1%) experienced recurrence. Symptom scores improved significantly. In medically managed patients (n = 10), mean SNOT‐22 decreased by 48.3 points. In two patients undergoing combined surgery and medical therapy, mean SNOT‐22 decreased by 68 points. Asthma control also improved, with mean ACT increasing by 8.9 points.

Benralizumab Subgroup

A total of 18 patients with CRS and available details on CRS outcomes received benralizumab, with a median age of 57 years (range: 51–57). For the two patients with reported sex, the distribution was equal (50% male, 50% female). All patients with reported sinus manifestations (n = 17) had CRSwNP. Details regarding sinus manifestations were not systematically described, and only two patients reported nasal congestion (n = 2), and three patients reported asthma (n = 3).

Among the six patients with available laboratory data, all (100%) had elevated eosinophils. Elevated IgE was reported in 33.3%, and of the two patients with available data, all of them were ANCA‐positive with the specific subtype unknown.

CRS‐specific diagnostic investigations were inconsistently reported. One patient had a CT with results not reported. One patient had a nasal endoscopy with results not reported. The two patients who underwent sinus biopsy reported the location as sinus mucosa, but the biopsy results were not specified.

Two patients underwent ESS with and/or polypectomy following EGPA diagnosis. Concomitant therapies were frequent. Systemic steroids were used in 16 patients, but the specific steroid used was not reported. Sixteen patients received immunosuppressants, but the specific immunosuppressant was unreported for all. INCS were documented in 16 patients, including fluticasone alone (6.2%), and the majority were unreported (93.8%). Concomitant biologic therapies were reported in 16 patients, including dupilumab (n = 3).

As benralizumab‐treated patients were grouped with cohorts of patients on various other treatments, data on outcomes could not be retrieved from these studies.

Dupilumab Subgroup

Thirty‐six patients received dupilumab, and the median age was 53 years (range: 14.5–75; n = 28 with reported age). CRSwNP was present in 23 patients (63.9%), with the rest not having a specific CRS phenotype reported (36.1%, n = 13). Among 24 patients with reported sex, 58.3% were male. All patients reported nasal congestion, nasal discharge, postnasal drip, hyposmia, and asthma.

Prior to EGPA diagnosis, in reported cases, 25% had undergone ESS and/or polypectomy. After diagnosis, FESS was performed in two of the reported patients. In reported cases, ANCA positivity was found in 57.1%, all p‐ANCA. Laboratory data from reported small subsets demonstrated eosinophilia in 83.3% of patients and elevated IgE in 18.2% of patients.

CRS diagnostic workup was variably reported. Sinus biopsy was performed in all 13 of the reported patients, nasal endoscopy in all of the two reported patients, and CT sinus in 75% of reported patients. Among patients with available findings, CT demonstrated complete sinus opacification in all four cases. Nasal endoscopy (n = 2) identified polyps in one patient. Biopsy findings (n = 13) most commonly showed eosinophilic infiltration (46.1%), with vasculitis (7.7%) and granulomatous inflammation (7.7%) also described.

Concomitant treatments included systemic corticosteroids in 23 patients with available data, most commonly prednisone (n = 10), immunosuppressants in eight, and INCS in 25 (primarily mometasone and budesonide). Among 17 patients with available data, these patients received additional biologics alongside dupilumab, including mepolizumab (n = 4) or benralizumab (n = 2).

Dupilumab was most commonly started after EGPA diagnosis (88.2%), and was mostly started after other biologics had already been used (91.2%) (Table 7). Reasons for starting dupilumab were most commonly due to asthma (52.9%), refractory CRSwNP (38.2%), eosinophilic CRS (eCRS) (5.9%), and eCRS and eosinophilic otitis media (3%) (Table 7).

Among six patients with reported outcomes, complete CRS remission occurred in five (83.3%) and recurrence in one (16.7%) after a median 12‐month follow‐up (n = 9). SNOT‐22 scores improved from 57.6 ± 11.5 pre‐treatment to 32.4 ± 7.3 post‐treatment (n = 9).

Dupilumab‐related side effects were also reported. Eleven patients (33.3%) developed eosinophilia, and 16 (64%) experienced elevated eosinophils without causing eosinophilia; 66.7% (n = 3) had an elevated IgE, 66.7% (n = 3) had an elevated CRP, and 40% (n = 5) had an elevated MPO‐ANCA. Two patients reported worsening paresthesia.

Multiple and Global

Several studies, included in the current review, reported the use of more than one biologic agent, either sequentially or concurrently, for the management of refractory CRS in EGPA. Sequential use of biologics was most common, typically driven by inadequate disease control or adverse effects, including one case of alternating mepolizumab and dupilumab due to dupilumab‐induced neuropathy [21]. Concurrent dual‐biologic therapy was reported in 10% of patients (8/80) in the largest cohort documenting this practice, with combinations including mepolizumab with dupilumab and mepolizumab with rituximab, initiated primarily for poorly controlled CRSwNP and corticosteroid‐sparing purposes [22]. In the pediatric setting, dupilumab added to mepolizumab was an effective rescue strategy in two children with ANCA‐negative EGPA refractory to prior biologic and surgical therapy [23]. These strategies reflect individualized clinical decision‐making rather than evidence‐based recommendations, and prospective comparative data remain lacking.

Sinonasal Manifestations in Major EGPA Cohorts and Clinical Trials

While this scoping review was restricted to studies explicitly defining CRS phenotypes, several landmark EGPA cohorts and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that report “sinonasal abnormalities” or “ENT manifestations” are summarized separately as complementary evidence (Table S2).

Large Cohort Studies

In the French Vasculitis Study Group cohort (n = 383), sinonasal symptoms were among the most frequent clinical features: 160 patients had sinusitis or polyposis, and 65 had rhinitis [24]. Sinonasal involvement was persistent, with 72 patients experiencing sinusitis flares over follow‐up, and nasal/sinus mucosal biopsies had limited sensitivity (52%) for detecting disease [24].

In the Vasculitis Clinical Research Consortium longitudinal cohort (n = 354), “ENT manifestations” were present in 201 patients at diagnosis, including 123 with documented nasal polyposis and 188 with “sinus involvement” [25]. Sinonasal disease contributed substantially to long‐term damage, with the Vasculitis Damage Index score related to “chronic sinusitis” rising from 1.7 at baseline to 3.35 over 7 years [25]. Notably, 21% of patients were classified as having “ENT‐only” disease, underscoring the potential for upper airway involvement to drive morbidity even in the absence of major systemic vasculitic flares [25].

Randomized Controlled Trials (MIRRA and MANDARA)

In the MIRRA trial of mepolizumab for relapsing or refractory EGPA, 128 of 136 patients (94%) had a “sinonasal abnormality” at baseline [12]. Mepolizumab led to greater improvements in sinonasal quality of life, with mean SNOT‐22 reductions of approximately 7.5–10 points over 1 year, compared with 2.5–5 points in the placebo group [12].

In the MANDARA head‐to‐head non‐inferiority trial (n = 140), “sinonasal abnormalities” were reported in 129 patients (92%) [13]. At 1 year, SNOT‐22 scores improved by six points with mepolizumab and five points with benralizumab, indicating comparable symptomatic benefit from anti‐IL‐5 and anti‐IL‐5Rα therapies for upper airway disease in EGPA [13].

Collectively, these large cohorts and RCTs demonstrate that sinonasal inflammation is a near‐universal feature of EGPA that contributes meaningfully to chronic damage and relapses, yet is responsive to eosinophil‐targeted biologic therapies, even though CRS phenotypes are not consistently defined.

Discussion

This scoping review of 109 studies encompassing 1087 patients provides the most comprehensive map of CRS within EGPA to date. Sinonasal disease, predominantly CRSwNP, emerged as a frequent airway manifestation of EGPA, consistent with prior large cohort studies [7, 24]. Although only a small subset of studies reported timelines of symptom evolution, available data indicate that CRS often precedes the recognition of systemic vasculitis in some patients. During this period, patients are often treated for refractory eCRS or severe asthma while the underlying systemic vasculitis remains unrecognized, leading to the unfortunate progression of irreversible cardiac, neurological, or renal sequelae. These findings reinforce the need for heightened clinical suspicion of EGPA in patients presenting with severe, recalcitrant CRSwNP or CRSsNP in the setting of asthma and peripheral eosinophilia.

A key challenge in evaluating sinonasal disease in EGPA relates not to the diagnosis of CRS itself, which was generally well characterized through CT and nasal endoscopy [26, 27, 28], but rather to the limited diagnostic utility of sinonasal biopsy for establishing the diagnosis of EGPA. Although biopsies were performed in most reported patients, histopathologic findings were specified in only a minority, and vasculitic features were identified in only 1.3% of cases. This likely reflects the patchy distribution of sinonasal vasculitis as well as the absence of standardized recommendations regarding the indications for biopsy or optimal site of biopsy in suspected EGPA [26, 29]. Consequently, sinonasal tissue sampling often demonstrates nonspecific eosinophilic chronic rhinosinusitis rather than definitive vasculitic changes. These findings underscore the importance of improving clinician awareness of EGPA as a potential cause of refractory CRSwNP/CRSsNP and support the need for an integrated diagnostic approach that synthesizes clinical presentation, laboratory markers, and systemic evaluation rather than relying solely on local histopathology [8, 29].

ESS remained a commonly reported component of management, performed in 87.5% of patients within the limited subset that provided surgical data. However, its contribution to outcomes could not be clearly determined, as surgery was almost universally combined with systemic steroids, immunosuppressants, intranasal corticosteroids, and biologics. This is likely due to the severe systemic effects of EGPA among these patients. Despite available treatments, recurrence occurred in 23.1% of patients, reflecting the chronic and relapsing nature of EGPA‐associated CRS. These findings highlight the need for studies that specifically evaluate CRS outcomes in EGPA and clarify the respective contributions of surgical and medical interventions.

Systemic corticosteroids and immunosuppressants remain central to EGPA management, and this review demonstrates increasing adoption of biologic therapies, with an increase in reported biologic use for CRS in EGPA amongst studies published in 2020 onwards.

Mepolizumab, an anti‐IL‐5 monoclonal antibody with proven efficacy for systemic disease control and steroid‐sparing benefit [12], was the most frequently reported biologic (73.3%), consistent with its regulatory approval and robust evidence base in EGPA [12].

Benralizumab is an anti–IL5‐Rα with a similar therapeutic strategy as mepolizumab, which is an anti‐ IL‐5. This small subgroup (n = 18) was characterized by a uniformly severe, Type 2 inflammatory phenotype, as all 17 patients with reported sinonasal manifestations had CRSwNP (100%), and all five patients with available data presented with eosinophilia (100%). Conclusions regarding its efficacy are severely constrained by the small sample size and the absence of reported CRS outcome data, such as SNOT‐22 scores or remission rates, in this subgroup. However, it is important to note that the MANDARA study did report non‐inferior outcomes for patients with sinonasal abnormalities in the context of EGPA as compared to mepolizumab [13]. Importantly, benralizumab was also frequently utilized in combination, with dupilumab being the most common concomitant biologic therapy (17.6%), potentially supporting the emerging real‐world strategy of layering biologics to target severe, refractory sinonasal disease in EGPA patients.

Of particular interest was the use of dupilumab, which targets the IL‐4/IL‐13 pathway central to Type 2 inflammation [30]. Dupilumab is not approved for EGPA and carries cautions related to potential eosinophilia or systemic vasculitic flares [31]. In our series, there was a subset of mepolizumab‐treated patients (n = 113) who received combination biologic therapy with dupilumab. Among 36 dupilumab‐treated patients with CRSwNP, symptom outcomes demonstrated clinically meaningful improvement, with a mean SNOT‐22 reduction of 25.2 points (n = 9) and complete CRS remission in 83.3% of those with follow‐up data. Importantly, dupilumab was frequently used in combination with mepolizumab or benralizumab, reflecting a pragmatic, phenotype‐directed approach. While mepolizumab and benralizumab address systemic vasculitic activity by suppressing IL‐5, dupilumab may target an alternate Type‐2 inflammatory pathway to aid with severe, refractory CRSwNP. Although formal evaluation of combination biologic therapy was limited, this pattern potentially suggests real‐world use of adjunctive or layered biologic strategies for refractory disease, either to enhance systemic control or to better address severe airway manifestations. These findings highlight an emerging clinical consideration. However, it is crucial to consider the side effects of dupilumab. In fact, side effects from dupilumab were notable in this cohort. Eosinophilia was observed in 34.4% of patients, with elevated eosinophils in 64%. Additional inflammatory markers were also elevated, with IgE and CRP becoming elevated in 66.7% of patients, and MPO‐ANCA elevation in 40%.

While there was only a report of one patient being treated with tezepelumab, this is another key biologic important in the management of CRSwNP. Tezepelumab is a human monoclonal antibody targeting thymic stromal lymphopoietin, an upstream epithelial cytokine implicated in Type 2 and non‐Type 2 inflammation in CRSwNP and asthma [32]. Tezepelumab does not have a formal approval for EGPA [32]. There are no data supporting tezepelumab for EGPA‐associated sinonasal disease, and its use in this context remains investigational.

Multiple case reports document the development or unmasking of EGPA following dupilumab initiation in patients treated for asthma or CRS, typically characterized by worsening airway symptoms, neuropathy, and vasculitic features [33, 34, 35]. These adverse events often manifested within weeks to months of starting therapy, frequently accompanied by marked hypereosinophilia (exceeding 1500 cells/µL) or rising peripheral eosinophils in patients with pre‐existing MPO‐ANCA positivity [35]. Specific complications reported in these series included the exacerbation of underlying asthma or CRS, the onset of eosinophilic otitis media [33], and in one notable case, the development of periaortitis [35], all of which necessitated the discontinuation of dupilumab and the initiation of corticosteroids or alternative immunosuppressive agents [33, 35]. Therefore, there is much caution and more data needed for combination therapy, particularly involving dupilumab.

Distinct from typical vasculitic presentations, one case report described a patient treated for eosinophilic esophagitis who developed a multisystem granulomatous hyperinflammatory state, characterized by granulomatous lesions across multiple organs [36]. Collectively, these findings highlight that while rare, serious adverse events such as EGPA unmasking, severe hypereosinophilia, and systemic granulomatous inflammation can occur during dupilumab therapy. Consequently, these reports underscore the critical need for vigilance and routine monitoring of eosinophil counts and vasculitic symptoms in patients receiving IL‐4/IL‐13 blockade.

These findings highlight a nuanced therapeutic landscape. For patients with active systemic vasculitis with EGPA, anti–IL‐5 or anti–IL5‐Rα therapy remains the most evidence‐based biologic option. However, for selected patients with controlled systemic disease but persistent, burdensome CRSwNP, dupilumab, particularly when used in conjunction with mepolizumab, may offer meaningful quality‐of‐life benefits. However, further evidence is required to optimize the combination therapy. Optimal management of EGPA‐associated CRS therefore demands close collaboration among rhinology, rheumatology, pulmonology, and allergy/immunology specialists to balance airway and systemic disease control, tailor therapy to patient phenotype, and guide safe use of emerging adjunctive biologic strategies.

Reported CRS remission rates and symptom improvements within biologic subgroups should also be interpreted with caution. Many patients received concurrent medical therapy or surgery, making it difficult to attribute outcomes to biologic therapy alone. Heterogeneity in outcome definitions further limits comparability across studies. Therefore, subgroup findings should be contextualized accordingly. Additionally, with the limited information provided in the studies, we cannot ascertain the extent of surgery performed, which could have affected sinonasal outcomes [37, 38]. Future prospective studies should therefore control for concurrent medical and surgical therapies, define the extent of surgery performed [38], and include biologic‐specific subgroup analyses to enable more definitive conclusions regarding treatment efficacy in EGPA‐associated CRS.

Despite the high prevalence and morbidity of sinonasal involvement, standardized reporting of disease severity and outcomes remains inconsistent. Where data were available, baseline Lund–Mackay and SNOT‐22 scores indicated moderate to severe disease burden, yet such measures were documented in only a minority of studies. The absence of uniform symptoms, imaging, and endoscopic scoring systems limits the ability to compare outcomes across studies or evaluate treatment efficacy. Adoption of standardized metrics such as SNOT‐22, endoscopic polyp scoring (e.g., Lund–Kennedy Endoscopy Score), and the Lund–Mackay system should be prioritized in both research and clinical care to enable consistent disease characterization and assessment of treatment response [12, 14, 31, 39].

Future studies should adopt a minimum core outcome set spanning four domains: patient‐reported outcomes (e.g., SNOT‐22), endoscopic assessment (e.g., Lund–Kennedy Endoscopy Score or a validated polyp grading scale), radiological staging (e.g., Lund–Mackay CT score at baseline and follow‐up), and systemic disease activity (Birmingham Vasculitis Activity Score or Five‐Factor Score). Biologic outcome reporting should additionally specify treatment duration, concomitant therapies, time to response, and the definition of remission used, given the considerable variability across included studies.

The strengths of this review include a comprehensive, librarian‐designed search strategy peer‐reviewed using the PRESS checklist [40], and inclusion of a wide range of study designs consistent with JBI and PRISMA‐ScR methodology [18]. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The majority of included sources were conference abstracts, which limited data completeness and methodological detail. Most full‐text studies were retrospective or descriptive, precluding inference on treatment effectiveness. Reporting heterogeneity across EGPA diagnostic frameworks, imaging findings, and outcome measures was substantial. As specified in our protocol, no formal risk‐of‐bias assessment was performed, consistent with the objectives of scoping review methodology. Finally, restriction to English‐language publications may have excluded relevant non‐English studies, and short follow‐up durations limited conclusions about long‐term outcomes [19].

Conclusion

This scoping review demonstrates that CRS, most commonly CRSwNP, is a frequent and often early manifestation of EGPA. Greater awareness of EGPA as a potential underlying cause of refractory Type‐2‐driven CRS and adoption of systematic sinonasal assessment are critical to improving early recognition. Even if a definitive diagnosis is not possible at CRS onset, close monitoring for the emergence of red flags, such as ANCA positivity, worsening eosinophilia, or extra‐sinonasal symptoms, is essential to identify the transition to EGPA at the earliest diagnosable stage and prevent significant morbidity.

This review also maps evolving management patterns, highlighting the emergence of biologic therapies alongside traditional corticosteroid and immunosuppressive regimens. Mepolizumab and benralizumab remain the approved biologics for EGPA to help improve global outcomes. While dupilumab shows promise for refractory sinonasal disease, treatment should be individualized through multidisciplinary collaboration with careful monitoring for eosinophilia and vasculitic activity given the potential for EGPA flare with IL‐4/IL‐13 blockade. Routine use of CRS outcome measures, such as SNOT‐22, endoscopic polyp grading, and Lund–Mackay scoring, is strongly recommended to enable consistent disease characterization and outcome comparison. Future prospective studies should investigate the role of complete surgery, likely in conjunction with the use of biologics, in this challenging population. In addition, future studies should apply uniform CRS definitions and validated outcome frameworks as well as compare different biologic therapies in order to optimize diagnosis, tailor therapy, and ultimately improve outcomes for patients with EGPA‐associated CRSwNP and CRSsNP.
