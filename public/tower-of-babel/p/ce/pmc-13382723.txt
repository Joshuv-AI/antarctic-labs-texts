The Development of Green Schiff Base-Coated Papers Using Empty Palm Fruit Bunch Biomass for Enhanced Wet Strength
Authors: Indra Masmur, Elvri Melliaty Sitinjak, Hardy Shuwanto, Sari Farah Dina, Poltak Evencus Hutajulu, Gimelliya Saragih, Leo Frengki Sihaloho, Immanuel Siahaan, Fadhila Natania Damanik, Rahel Argana Doloksaribu, Septriasi Sarah Ardela, Andira Sapitri, Verianto Pakpahan, Rahel Gabriel Sionita Hutapea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, a sustainable cellulose-based Schiff base wet strength agent (WSA) was successfully synthesized from empty palm fruit bunch (EPFB) biomass. Cellulose isolated from EPFB was oxidized using sodium periodate to produce dialdehyde cellulose (DAC), which was subsequently reacted with six different amine precursors, methylamine (SB-1), ethylenediamine (SB-2), ethylamine (SB-3), diethylamine (SB-4), ethanolamine (SB-5), and diethanolamine (SB-6), to form cellulose-based Schiff base derivatives. Structural characterization by FTIR confirmed the formation of imine (CN) linkages, while XRD revealed a decrease in crystallinity after oxidation and Schiff base formation. SEM analysis demonstrated a progressive morphological transformation from fibrous cellulose to compact and aggregated Schiff base structures. BET results indicated a significant enhancement of the specific surface area, particularly for SB-2 (417.6 m2 g–1), suggesting improved interaction potential with paper fibers. Thermal analysis showed that cross-linked Schiff base structures exhibited modified thermal stability compared to DAC. Mechanical testing demonstrated substantial improvement in wet tensile strength, with SB-2 exhibiting the best performance, approaching that of commercial polyamide epichlorohydrin (PAE) agents. Biodegradability studies confirmed rapid degradation of all Schiff base samples, achieving nearly complete degradation within 20 days, outperforming conventional synthetic agents. These findings demonstrate that EPFB-derived Schiff base materials offer a promising, biodegradable, and biobased alternative to synthetic wet strength agents in paper applications.

Introduction

The global paper industry is a critical component of modern infrastructure, providing essential materials for communication, hygiene, and packaging. One of the persistent challenges in paper manufacturing, particularly for applications where moisture exposure is inevitable such as tissue paper, labels, or food packaging is ensuring adequate wet strength. Wet strength refers to the ability of papers to retain its mechanical integrity when saturated with water. Cellulosic fibers, which form the basis of paper, are inherently hydrophilic. In wet conditions, the hydrogen bonding between fibers weakens, leading to fiber swelling, network disintegration, and significant loss of strength. To overcome this issue, various multifunctional lignin-based composite and WSAs have been developed and commercialized. Currently, synthetic resins such as PAE, urea-formaldehyde (UF), and melamine-formaldehyde (MF) are the most widely used WSAs due to their strong performance and ability to form irreversible bonds with cellulose fibers. − However, these synthetic agents pose serious environmental and health concerns, since their applications are directly contacted with skins or foods. Their widespread use is associated with the release of toxic compounds such as formaldehyde, low biodegradability, and the accumulation of nonrenewable, petroleum-based residues in wastewater. Moreover, their interference with paper recyclability and rising consumer demand for safer, eco-friendly materials have increased pressure on the industry to adopt greener alternatives.

In light of these issues, there is growing interest in developing biobased, biodegradable wet strength agents derived from renewable resources. Lignocellulosic biomass is a particularly promising candidate for this application. Among various agricultural residues, EPFB stands out due to its abundance and high cellulose content. As a major byproduct of the palm oil industry in Southeast Asia, EPFB is often underutilized or discarded, contributing to waste-management challenges and environmental degradation. Extracting and valorizing cellulose from EPFB not only adds value to an otherwise low-value waste stream but also aligns with global goals for sustainable materials development. Cellulose derived from EPFB can be chemically modified to enhance its functionality. One such modification involves converting cellulose into dialdehyde cellulose (DAC) through periodate oxidation, which introduces reactive aldehyde groups into the polymer chain. These aldehyde groups offer reactive sites for further chemical reactions such as Schiff base formation through condensation with amines. Schiff bases contain imine (CN) linkages and are valued for their tunable chemical properties, stability, and potential to form covalent or hydrogen bonds with cellulose. Their application in polymer coatings and functional materials is well-documented, but their use as wet strength agents for paper remains largely unexplored.

This research investigates a novel approach to paper strengthening by synthesizing Schiff base derivatives from DAC obtained from EPFB-derived cellulose. Six different amine precursors, methylamine, ethylamine, diethylamine, ethylenediamine, ethanolamine, and diethanolamine were used to prepare structurally diverse Schiff bases. These compounds were then applied as coatings on paper sheets to assess their wet strength enhancement capabilities. Each amine introduces unique functional groups and hydrophilic/hydrophobic characteristics, potentially influencing the interaction between the coating and the paper fiber network. Despite previous research on natural polymers and green additives in paper, the systematic evaluation of Schiff base-modified DAC as a wet strength enhancer particularly derived from EPFB has not yet been reported. This study addresses that gap by combining sustainable biomass utilization, green chemistry principles, and material performance evaluation. The results will provide new insights into the structure–property relationships of Schiff bases in paper applications and offer a pathway toward replacing conventional WSAs with renewable, nontoxic alternatives. This work supports the broader agenda of developing sustainable industrial practices and circular bioeconomy solutions by introducing a biobased, functional, and environmentally benign material for enhancing wet strength in paper products.

Experimental Methods

Materials

The EPFB was kindly provided by a palm oil mill in the area of Deli Serdang, Province of North Sumatra, Indonesia. Nitric acid (HNO3, 65%), sodium hydroxide pellet (NaOH, 98%), sodium sulfite (Na2SO3, 98%), sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl, 4%), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30%), sodium periodate (NaIO4, 99%), and all amine precursors used in this study were commercially purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and directly used without any further treatment.

Isolation of Cellulose from EPFB

EPFB powder was treated with 3.5% HNO3 at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:25 (w/w). The mixture was heated at 90 °C for 2 h under continuous stirring. The resulting slurry was filtered and washed with distilled water until a neutral pH was achieved. The residue was then subjected to alkaline treatment using a solution of 2% NaOH and 2% Na2SO3 at a 1:13 (w/w) solid-to-liquid ratio and heated at 50 °C for 1 h with stirring. The mixture was again filtered and washed to neutrality. Subsequently, the residue was treated with 1.75% NaOCl solution at a 1:6 (w/w) solid-to-liquid ratio and heated at 70 °C for 30 min. The solid was filtered and washed until the filtrate reached neutral pH. A final bleaching step was carried out using 10% H2O2 at a 1:6 (w/w) ratio at 60 °C for 15 min. The bleached cellulose was filtered, washed with distilled water to neutral pH, dried in an oven at 60 °C, and stored in a desiccator.

Oxidation of Cellulose to DAC

NaIO4 was dissolved in distilled water in a 1:25 (w/w) ratio. Cellulose was added to the solution at a weight ratio of NaIO4/cellulose = 2.1:1. The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for different durations: 3, 6, 9, and 10 h, yielding samples referred to as DAC-3, DAC-6, DAC-9, and DAC-10, respectively. The reaction products were precipitated by using ethanol and subsequently dried in a desiccator.

Synthesis of Schiff Base

DAC was dissolved in ethanol at a weight ratio of 1:60 (DAC:ethanol). Subsequently, amine compounds such as methylamine, ethylamine, diethylamine, ethylenediamine, ethanolamine, and diethanolamine were added in an amount equal to three times the weight of DAC. The reaction mixture was then refluxed for 5 h. Upon completion of the reaction, the product was filtered and the precipitate was washed thoroughly with ethanol to remove unreacted reagents and impurities. The Schiff base samples synthesized using methylamine, ethylenediamine, ethylamine, diethylamine, ethanolamine, and diethanolamine were named SB-1, SB-2, SB-3, SB-4, SB-5, and SB-6, respectively.

Qualitative Characterization

Determination of Degree of Oxidation (DO%)

A total of 0.1 g of DAC was placed in a 50 mL Erlenmeyer flask and dissolved in 10 mL distilled water. Then, 10 mL of 0.1 N NaOH was added, and the mixture was heated until the sample fully dissolved. After cooling to room temperature, 10 mL of 0.15 N HCl was added dropwise until the pH was below 7. The solution was reheated for 1 min, followed by the addition of two drops of phenolphthalein indicator. The mixture was titrated with 0.1 N NaOH until a pink end point was observed. The degree of oxidation was calculated using eq : 1 %DO=(CNaOH×VNaOH)−(CHCl×VHCl)(m162)×100%

Determination of Carbonyl Content (%CHO)

1 g of the DAC sample was dispersed in 40 mL distilled water in a 250 mL beaker and heated in a water bath for 20 min. After cooling to 40 °C, the pH of the solution was adjusted to 3 using 0.1 M HCl. Then, 25 mL of hydroxylamine hydrochloride solution was added, and the mixture was heated again at 40 °C in a water bath for 4 h with constant stirring. The final solution was titrated with 0.1 M HCl until pH 3 was reached. The carbonyl content was calculated using eq : 2 %CHO=0,028×M×(Vb−Vs)beratsampel(g)×100%

Determination of Nitrogen Content by the Kjeldahl Method

Approximately 1 g of the synthesized Schiff base sample was introduced into a Kjeldahl digestion flask. Then, 15 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4) and 0.3 g of the selenium catalyst were added. The mixture was digested by heating at a temperature of 375–450 °C until a clear solution was obtained. The resulting digest was transferred into a distillation flask. Subsequently, 75 mL of distilled water was added, followed by the slow, dropwise addition of 50 mL of 30% sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution along the inner wall of the flask. A few drops of phenolphthalein (PP) indicator were added, and a piece of zinc metal was placed into the flask to prevent bumping during the distillation process. The mixture was then heated on an electromantle. In a separate Erlenmeyer flask, 15 mL of 3% boric acid (H3BO3) solution was prepared and three drops of Tashiro indicator were added. This Erlenmeyer flask served as the receiver for the ammonia distillate. The distillation was continued until the solution in the Erlenmeyer flask changed color from purple to green, indicating the presence of ammonia. The collected distillate was then titrated with standardized 0.1 N hydrochloric acid (HCl) until the green solution turned back to purple. The volume of HCl used in the titration was recorded. A blank titration was also performed under the same conditions. In the blank test, 15 mL of 3% boric acid and three drops of Tashiro indicator were added into an Erlenmeyer flask and titrated directly with 0.1 N HCl without the presence of any sample. The volume of HCl consumed in the blank titration was also recorded. The nitrogen content in the sample was calculated by correcting the titration volume using the blank and applying the appropriate formula for Kjeldahl nitrogen determination.

Samples’ Characterization

The synthesized Schiff base samples were subjected to a series of physicochemical and performance characterizations to evaluate their structural, morphological, thermal, surface, and functional properties. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, JASCO V-750) was employed to identify the functional groups and confirm the formation of Schiff base linkages through characteristic imine (CN) vibrations. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL F6500) coupled with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was used to observe surface morphology and elemental composition of both uncoated and coated paper, revealing coating uniformity and fiber interactions. X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D2 Phaser) analysis was conducted to determine the crystallinity and phase structure of the synthesized materials, offering insights into the structural modifications of cellulose and DAC during Schiff base formation.

Thermal stability and decomposition behavior of the samples were analyzed using Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA, TA Instruments) under a nitrogen atmosphere. To assess porosity and surface area, Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET, micromeritics) analysis was performed, providing the specific surface area, pore size distribution, and total pore volume, which are relevant for evaluating the potential in barrier and bonding applications of materials. Particle Size Analysis (PSA, Anton Paar PSA-1190D) was used to determine the hydrodynamic particle size distribution of Schiff base suspensions, indicating the dispersion behavior and stability of the coating formulation.

The wet tensile strength test was conducted according to ISO 12625–5:2014. To perform the wet tensile strength test, the procedure was begun by filling the lowered water reservoir to the indicated mark with distilled or deionized water maintained at (23 ± 1)°C. The dry specimen was looped horizontally under the rod and both ends were secured in the upper clamp of the tensile tester, ensuring a firm grip that prevents slippage without damaging the material. The specimen was submerged at least 20 mm by raising and locking the water container and then immediately starting a 15 s soaking period. Once timed, the reservoir was lowered and the tensile test was immediately initiated at an elongation rate of (50 ± 2) mm/min, dividing the resulting peak force by two to determine the strength of a single test piece. If the specimen breaks at the rod or the clamp, the results must be discarded and the test should be repeated with a new sample. To maintain the equipment, the rod was wiped dry and the water level was topped off after every individual test, and it was ensured that the container was thoroughly cleaned and refilled between different sample sets.

This test provided direct evidence of the effectiveness of Schiff base samples as a wet strength agent. Furthermore, the biodegradability of the coated samples was assessed through soil burial or composting tests, monitoring the weight loss and physical degradation over time. This evaluation highlighted the environmental compatibility and potential of samples as sustainable alternatives to synthetic wet strength agents.

Results and Discussion

Figure a presents the degree of oxidation (DO%) and aldehyde content (%CHO) of DAC obtained after different oxidation times (DAC-X), where X indicates the oxidation time of cellulose in hour unit. The results clearly show that both the degree of oxidation and aldehyde content increased significantly compared to pristine cellulose from EPFB, confirming the successful oxidation of cellulose using sodium periodate. The increase in DO% indicates effective cleavage of the C2–C3 vicinal diol groups of the anhydroglucose unit, forming dialdehyde functionalities. The aldehyde content follows a similar trend, demonstrating that longer oxidation times promoted greater formation of carbonyl groups. Both DO% and %CHO values are increasing with increasing oxidation times from DAC-3 to DAC-9 (45.6% to 82.1%).

However, at extended oxidation durations to 10 h (DAC-10), a slight reduction or plateau tendency (74.7%) may also suggest partial degradation of the cellulose backbone leading to brittleness, which is consistent with periodate overoxidation effects reported in the literature. , Overall, the results confirm the successful conversion of EPFB-derived cellulose into DAC, providing active carbonyl sites that are necessary for subsequent Schiff base formation.

Figure b shows the FTIR spectra of cellulose isolated from EPFB, DAC, and Schiff base samples (SB-1 to SB-6). For native cellulose, a broad absorption band at 3300–3500 cm–1 corresponds to O–H stretching vibrations, indicating extensive hydrogen bonding within the cellulose structure. The band around ∼2850 cm–1 is attributed to C–H stretching of aliphatic groups, , while characteristic peaks at ∼1100 cm–1 correspond to C–O–C and C–O stretching vibrations of the pyranose ring. After oxidation to DAC, a new absorption band appears in the region around ∼1680 cm–1, which is assigned to the CO stretching vibration of aldehyde groups. The decrease in intensity of the O–H band further supports the conversion of vicinal hydroxyl groups into dialdehyde functionalities. These spectral changes confirm the successful periodate oxidation of cellulose. For the Schiff base samples (SB-1 to SB-6), significant spectral modifications were observed. The intensity of the aldehyde CO peak decreased or shifted, indicating consumption of aldehyde groups during the condensation reaction with amines. More importantly, a new absorption band appeared in the region of ∼ 1620 cm–1, which is characteristic of the imine (CN) stretching vibration, confirming the formation of Schiff base linkages. Differences in peak intensity among SB-1 to SB-6 reflect the influence of different amine structures. Samples synthesized with primary amines (SB-1: methylamine; SB-2: ethylamine; SB-3: ethylenediamine; SB-5: ethanolamine) exhibited more pronounced CN bands compared to those prepared with secondary amines (SB-4: diethylamine; SB-6: diethanolamine). This is probably related to the higher reactivity of primary amines toward aldehyde groups in forming imine bonds. Additionally, SB-3 may show enhanced cross-linking due to its bifunctional amine structure, which can react with two aldehyde groups, potentially improving network formation. These structural changes are expected to influence wet strength performance by enhancing covalent bonding and reducing fiber swelling in aqueous conditions. Furthermore, the nitrogen content as determined by the Kjeldahl method shows that the N% values are 1.22%, 2.75%, 1.91%, 1.01%, 1.59%, and 1.75% for SB-1, SB-2, SB-3, SB-4, SB-5, and SB-6 samples, respectively.

To further elucidate the interfacial chemical environment and confirm the covalent integration of the Schiff base coating with the cellulose matrix, XPS analysis was performed on the N 1s region. While the EPFB-derived cellulose (Figure d) shows an absence of nitrogenous species, the SB-coated paper (Figure c) exhibits a robust N 1s signal that was successfully deconvoluted into three distinct chemical states. The prominent peak at 399.1 eV is attributed to the imine (CN) linkage, providing definitive evidence of the successful condensation reaction between the amine groups of the coating and the aldehyde functionalities of the dialdehyde cellulose (DAC). Additionally, the peaks at 400.5 and 398.2 eV correspond to unreacted amine (C–N) groups and nitrogen atoms involved in interfacial hydrogen bonding or localized oxidation, respectively. This spectroscopic evidence confirms that the enhanced wet strength performance is not merely a result of physical encapsulation but stems from a synergistic effect where covalent anchoring at the fiber-coating interface ensures efficient stress transfer and restricts the swelling of the cellulose network upon water exposure.

Figure shows the SEM micrographs of cellulose, DAC, and SB-x samples at two different magnifications, illustrating the morphological evolution during oxidation and Schiff base formation. The cellulose from EPFB (Figure a,b) exhibits a typical fibrous and layered morphology with relatively smooth surfaces. The fibers appear entangled and compact, indicating the preservation of the native cellulose microfibrillar structure after chemical purification. The surface is continuous with limited fragmentation, confirming that the isolation process successfully removed noncellulosic components without severe structural damage. After oxidation to DAC (Figure c,d), significant morphological changes are observed. The fibrous structure becomes rougher and partially fragmented, with visible surface cracks and irregular textures. This structural disruption is attributed to periodate oxidation, which cleaves the C2–C3 bonds of the anhydroglucose units, generating dialdehyde groups and weakening the crystalline regions. , The increased surface roughness and fragmentation suggest partial depolymerization and reduction in structural integrity, consistent with the increase in degree of oxidation reported in Figure a. Following Schiff base formation, the morphology changes further depending on the amine precursor used. SB-1 and SB-2 (Figure e,h) exhibit irregular, flake-like or granular structures with noticeable aggregation. The particles appear more compact compared to DAC, suggesting intermolecular interactions or cross-linking through imine bond formation. The surface becomes denser, indicating successful chemical modification. SB-3 (Figure i,j) shows more elongated and interconnected structures compared to SB-1 and SB-2. This is likely due to the bifunctional nature of ethylenediamine, which can react with two aldehyde groups, forming a more cross-linked network. The morphology appears more organized and rigid, suggesting enhanced structural reinforcement. In contrast, SB-4 (Figure k,l) presents less uniform and more dispersed particles. As a secondary amine, diethylamine has lower reactivity toward aldehyde groups compared to primary amines, which may result in lower cross-linking density and less structural consolidation. SB-5 (Figure m,n) exhibits a finer particle distribution with relatively smoother surfaces, possibly due to the presence of hydroxyl groups in ethanolamine that contribute to additional hydrogen bonding interactions. This may promote better compatibility with the cellulose backbone. Interestingly, SB-6 (Figure o,p) shows a more aggregated and clustered morphology, forming dense agglomerates. The bulky structure and dual hydroxyl functionalities of diethanolamine may enhance intermolecular interactions, leading to compact particle formation. Overall, the SEM observations clearly demonstrate progressive morphological transformation from fibrous cellulose to fragmented DAC and subsequently to compact or aggregated Schiff base structures. The differences among SB-1 to SB-6 reflect the influence of amine structure on cross-linking density, particle formation, and surface characteristics. These morphological modifications are expected to directly influence coating behavior and wet strength performance by altering interfiber bonding and surface coverage efficiency.

On the other hand, Figure b displays the TGA curves of cellulose, DAC, and SB-1 to SB-6. All samples exhibit three main weight-loss stages. The initial weight loss below 100 °C corresponds to moisture evaporation due to physically adsorbed water. The second and major weight-loss region (approximately 200–350 °C) is attributed to the thermal degradation of the cellulose backbone, including depolymerization and decomposition of glycosidic linkages. The final stage above 350 °C corresponds to carbonization and formation of char residue. Native cellulose demonstrates relatively higher thermal stability compared to DAC. The onset degradation temperature of DAC shifts to a lower value, indicating reduced thermal stability due to cleavage of C2–C3 bonds and decreased crystallinity. The introduction of dialdehyde groups weakens the structural integrity, making DAC more susceptible to thermal decomposition. The Schiff base samples exhibit varied thermal behavior depending on the amine precursor. In general, SB samples show slightly improved or modified thermal stability compared to DAC. The formation of imine (CN) linkages and possible cross-linked networks contributes to enhanced thermal resistance in some samples. Notably, SB-3 demonstrates comparatively higher thermal stability and char residue, which may be attributed to its bifunctional cross-linking structure. Conversely, samples synthesized from secondary amines (SB-4 and SB-6) display lower thermal stability, likely due to reduced imine formation efficiency and weaker network structure. The increased char residue observed in certain SB samples suggests improved structural rigidity and potential enhancement in flame resistance characteristics, which may contribute positively to paper coating performance under thermal stress.

The specific surface area, pore volume, and average pore diameter of cellulose, DAC, and Schiff base derivatives (SB-1 to SB-6) are summarized in Table . Significant changes in surface characteristics were observed after oxidation and Schiff base formation. Native cellulose exhibited a specific surface area of 61.5 m2 g–1, a pore volume of 10.9 cm3 g–1, and an average pore diameter of 87.33 nm, indicating a relatively porous fibrous structure consistent with its SEM morphology. After oxidation to DAC, the specific surface area increased to 75.6 m2 g–1, while the pore volume decreased to 5.0 cm3 g–1 and the pore diameter significantly reduced to 40.17 nm. The increase in the surface area may be attributed to structural disruption and partial cleavage of crystalline regions during periodate oxidation, generating additional accessible surfaces. Meanwhile, the reduction in pore diameter suggests collapse or restructuring of the internal pore network due to the oxidation-induced chain scission. A more pronounced transformation was observed after Schiff base formation. All SB-x samples demonstrated a substantial increase in the specific surface area compared with cellulose and DAC, accompanied by a marked decrease in pore volume and pore diameter. This indicates the formation of finer, more compact, and possibly micro- to mesoporous structures. Among the samples, SB-2 exhibited the highest specific surface area (417.6 m2 g–1) and the smallest pore diameter (7.81 nm), suggesting the formation of a highly developed porous network. The relatively small molecular size and high reactivity of ethylamine may facilitate efficient imine formation, leading to a more uniform and highly dispersed structure. Similarly, SB-5 also showed a high surface area (316.2 m2 g–1) with a reduced pore diameter (9.59 nm), which may be attributed to additional hydrogen-bonding interactions from hydroxyl groups. SB-3 presented a moderate surface area (185.7 m2 g–1) and pore diameter (11.37 nm), likely due to its bifunctional structure promoting cross-linking and network formation. In contrast, SB-4 and SB-6, both derived from secondary amines, exhibited comparatively lower surface areas and larger pore diameters, reflecting lower reactivity and less efficient cross-linking. SB-1 showed moderate enhancement, indicating successful modification but with less structural development compared to those of SB-2 and SB-5. The overall decrease in pore volume and pore diameter across SB samples suggests the densification and restructuring of the cellulose matrix following Schiff base formation. In addition, the significant decrease in pore volume from cellulose to SB-x samples indicates that the Schiff base polymer effectively infiltrates and plugs these micropores. Meanwhile, the SEM results show a compact macrostructure (aggregated structures), and the BET data confirm that the coating is not merely a superficial layer but fills the internal voids of the fiber. This internal filling restricts water penetration (improving wet strength) and increases the effective contact area for interfacial bonding. The significant increase in the surface area, particularly for SB-2 and SB-5, may enhance interaction with paper fibers during coating, potentially improving the mechanical interlocking and wet strength performance. The variation in porosity parameters among SB-1 to SB-6 highlights the strong influence of the amine precursor structure on network formation and surface development, which is expected to play a critical role in coating efficiency and reinforcement behavior.

Figure a,b shows the dry tensile strength and wet tensile strength of paper coated with DAC and Schiff base derivatives (SB-1 to SB-6), compared with commercial wet strength agents (PAE-SN and PAE-SL). As can be seen in Figure a, SB-2 exhibits the highest dry tensile strength among all Schiff base samples. This indicates that the ethylamine-based Schiff base provides the most effective reinforcement under dry conditions. The enhanced performance of SB-2 may be attributed to efficient imine (CN) bond formation with DAC, a relatively high specific surface area (as shown in BET results), promoting better fiber coverage, and balanced hydrophobic–hydrophilic characteristics that improve fiber-to-fiber adhesion. SB-3 shows moderate improvement, while SB-1, SB-4, SB-5, and SB-6 demonstrate similar but slightly lower dry-strength values. DAC alone exhibits lower strength compared to most SB-treated samples, confirming that Schiff base formation contributes significantly to fiber-bonding enhancement. As expected, commercial PAE-SN shows the highest dry tensile strength overall, followed by that of PAE-SL. However, SB-2 approaches the performance of PAE-SL, indicating promising potential as a biobased alternative. Notably, SB-2 exhibits the highest wet tensile strength among all of the Schiff base samples. This suggests that the ethylamine-derived Schiff base forms a more water-resistant bonding network compared with the other amine derivatives. The superior wet strength of SB-2 may be attributed to a high surface area (BET result: 417.6 m2 g–1), leading to enhanced coating uniformity and penetration. Efficient imine bond formation occurs between aldehyde groups and primary amine. Formation of a compact microstructure (as observed in SEM analysis) reduces fiber swelling and water penetration. SB-3 shows the second-highest wet tensile strength, likely due to cross-linking via its bifunctional ethylenediamine structure. However, excessive cross-linking may slightly reduce flexibility compared to that of SB-2. SB-5 demonstrates moderate wet strength enhancement, possibly due to additional hydrogen bonding from its hydroxyl functionality. In contrast, SB-4 and SB-6 (secondary amine derivatives) exhibit lower wet strength performance, consistent with reduced imine formation efficiency and lower cross-link density. DAC-treated paper shows limited wet strength improvement, confirming that aldehyde groups alone are insufficient to provide strong water-resistant bonding without amine cross-linking. Although PAE-SN remains the highest-performing commercial agent, SB-2 shows comparable performance to PAE-SL, highlighting the strong potential of the biobased Schiff base system. The revised mechanical data suggest that the surface area plays a dominant role in wet strength performance, with SB-2 (highest BET area) showing the best results. Primary amines outperform secondary amines in both dry and wet tensile strength due to higher imine formation efficiency. Excessive cross-linking (SB-3) does not necessarily lead to the highest wet strength; an optimal network density provides a better balance between rigidity and flexibility. Chemical modification reduces fiber swelling and strengthens covalent bonding, which are crucial for wet integrity.

The biodegradability of the SB-coated papers was evaluated via a soil burial test according to the standard of ASTM D 5988. Samples were cut into 15 × 15 mm squares and buried at a depth of 50 ± 2 mm in a controlled soil matrix (pH 6.8 ± 0.2). The soil composition consisted of a mixture of compost, clay, and silica sand in a 4:3:3 ratio by weight. The moisture content of the soil was maintained at 80% of its maximum water-holding capacity by periodic addition of deionized water. The test was conducted in a climate-controlled chamber at a constant temperature of 30 ± 3 °C and a relative humidity of 60%. The biodegradability behavior of DAC, Schiff base derivatives (SB-1 to SB-6), and the commercial wet strength agent (PAE-SL) was evaluated over a 20 day period, and the results are presented in Figure a. The degradation percentage increased progressively with incubation time for all samples, indicating susceptibility to microbial or environmental degradation. At the early stage (day 4), the Schiff base samples exhibited significantly higher biodegradation percentages compared to DAC and PAE-SL. SB-2 and SB-6 showed the most rapid initial degradation, reaching approximately 30–32%, while SB-1, SB-3, SB-4, and SB-5 ranged between 18–25%. In contrast, DAC and PAE-SL demonstrated minimal degradation (<10%) during this period. The higher early stage biodegradation of SB samples suggests that the introduction of imine linkages and amine functionalities enhances microbial accessibility and surface reactivity. By day 8, a substantial increase in degradation was observed for all SB samples. SB-2 and SB-3 exceeded 70% biodegradability, while SB-5 and SB-6 approached approximately 60–65%. SB-4 showed moderate degradation (∼40%), and DAC and PAE-SL remained significantly lower. The accelerated degradation of SB-2 and SB-3 may be attributed to their structural characteristics: SB-2 contains a small primary amine (ethylamine), while SB-3 contains a bifunctional amine (ethylenediamine), both of which promote efficient imine formation but do not excessively hinder enzymatic attack. At day 12, most SB samples reached above 80% degradation, with SB-2 achieving nearly complete degradation (∼100%). SB-3 and SB-5 also showed high biodegradability (>85%).

In comparison, DAC reached moderate degradation (∼55%), while PAE-SL exhibited slower degradation (∼65–70%), reflecting its more synthetic and cross-linked structure. By day 16–20, all SB samples approached nearly complete biodegradation (∼100%), demonstrating their environmentally friendly nature. Although DAC eventually reached high degradation levels, its degradation rate was slower compared to Schiff base derivatives. The commercial PAE-SL also showed significant degradation over time but generally lagged behind several SB samples in the earlier stages. The faster biodegradation of Schiff base derivatives can be attributed to (1) the presence of imine (CN) bonds, which are hydrolytically and biologically more susceptible than synthetic resin cross-links; (2) the biobased cellulose backbone, which remains enzymatically degradable; (3) increased surface area (as shown by BET analysis), enhancing microbial interaction; and (4) reduced crystallinity (confirmed by XRD), facilitating easier enzymatic penetration. Among the SB samples, those derived from primary amines (SB-1, SB-2, SB-3, SB-5) generally exhibited faster degradation than those synthesized from secondary amines (SB-4 and SB-6), likely due to differences in cross-link density and steric hindrance. SB-3 maintained a balance between mechanical strength and biodegradability, making it particularly promising for sustainable wet strength applications.

The surface wettability of the developed materials was characterized through water contact angle (WCA) measurements to evaluate the impact of different amine cross-linkers on the water resistance of papers (Figure ). The pristine EPFB-cellulose and DAC exhibited high hydrophilicity with WCA values of 28.8° and 19.5°, respectively, due to the abundance of free hydroxyl and newly formed aldehyde groups. Upon Schiff base functionalization, the WCA increased significantly for all samples (SB-1 to SB-6), ranging from 51.8° to 62.1°. Among the formulations, SB-3 and SB-5 showed the most pronounced improvements, reaching 59.7° and 62.1°, respectively. This increase in WCA is attributed to the consumption of hydrophilic polar groups (aldehydes and hydroxyls) during the Schiff base condensation and the subsequent introduction of organic alkyl or alkanol chains that reduce the surface free energy. Interestingly, the alkanolamines (ethanolamine and diethanolamine) provided slightly higher contact angles compared to the simpler alkylamines, likely due to a more compact cross-linked network formed through supplementary hydrogen bonding at the interface. While the samples remain within the hydrophilic regime (WCA < 90°), the more than 3-fold increase in the contact angle compared to DAC demonstrates that the tailored amine cross-linking significantly enhances the moisture barrier properties and water resistance of the biobased coatings.

Conclusion

A novel biobased wet strength agent was successfully developed from EPFB-derived cellulose through periodate oxidation and subsequent Schiff base formation with various amine precursors. The structural transformation from cellulose to DAC and finally to Schiff base derivatives was confirmed by FTIR, XRD, SEM, and BET analyses, revealing reduced crystallinity, an increased surface area, and modified morphology. Among the synthesized materials, the ethylenediamine-based derivative (SB-2) exhibited the most balanced performance, showing enhanced cross-linking, improved thermal stability, superior wet tensile strength, and high biodegradability. The study demonstrates that the chemical structure of the amine precursor plays a crucial role in determining the cross-link density, microstructure, and mechanical reinforcement efficiency. Importantly, several Schiff base samples approached the performance of commercial PAE wet strength agents while maintaining excellent biodegradability. This work highlights the potential of agricultural waste valorization in developing environmentally benign, high-performance materials and provides a viable pathway toward replacing conventional synthetic wet strength resins with sustainable cellulose-based alternatives.
