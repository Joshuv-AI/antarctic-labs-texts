General Approach for the Synthesis of Triazinyl Phosphoramidate Oligonucleotides via the Staudinger Reaction with the Use of 2‑Azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine
Authors: Timofey D. Zharkov, Maxim S. Kupryushkin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Developing the new types of phosphate-modified oligonucleotides and methods for their synthesis is of great interest in nucleic acid chemistry, since such derivatives could be applied as diagnostic and therapeutic tools. In the present study, our previously reported synthesis of oligonucleotide bearing two dodecyl residues on the triazine core was expanded to a general approach of synthesis of a novel class of phosphate-modified nucleic acids, triazinyl phosphoramidate oligonucleotides (TPO). The proposed approach for obtaining TPO is based on the Staudinger reaction between highly reactive 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine and the phosphite triester intermediate of oligonucleotide solid-phase synthesis, with the following β-elimination of 2-cyanoethyl protective group and substitution of chlorine atoms with various amine residues. The formation of the triazinyl phosphoramidate bond was confirmed by carrying out 31P NMR of dithymidylate TPO and its comparison with other well-known PN-modified nucleic acid classes such as phosphoryl guanidine and alkyl and sulfonyl phosphoramidates. The possibility of great diversification of substituents in the triazine core of TPO using the proposed approach with incorporation of alkylamines, arylamines, amino alcohols, and diamines with a single substitution step as well as solid-phase multistep assembly of different complex residue structures was demonstrated. The obtained modified oligonucleotides were analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and mass analysis methods. Alternative structures of azido-triazine modifiers containing methylamino, methoxy groups, and iodine atoms were also proposed and studied for the efficiency of obtaining TPO. The presented results indicate that the described triazinyl phosphoramidate oligonucleotides are a distinct class of phosphate-modified nucleic acids with a broad range of representatives and a potentially adjustable approach for their synthesis.

Introduction

Modified oligonucleotides are currently widely applied in various fields of molecular biology, biotechnology, and medicine. They are most commonly used as primers in polymerase chain reactions (PCRs), molecular beacons, and therapeutics, − with significantly improved potency compared to unmodified oligonucleotides.

The number of diverse chemical modifications of oligonucleotides is still increasing despite the limited typical positions in which they can be introduced. Such positions include 3′- and 5′-terminal chain positions, nucleobases, sugar backbone, and internucleotide phosphate group. There are various reagents that allow one to modify these positions within a standard solid-phase phosphoramidite synthesis. In this way, modified solid-phase supports are used for 3′-end variations, while for modification of the 5′-end position, sugar backbone, and nucleobases, corresponding phosphoramidites are commonly utilized. − However, synthesis of such supports and phosphoramidites is often a laborious, resource-intensive, complex synthetic task. At the same time, modification of the internucleotide phosphate group is frequently carried out just by replacement of the standard oxidizing step of the solid-phase synthesis protocol with the alternative one using different oxidizing reagents, resulting in the corresponding NA derivatives. For example, phosphoramidates and phosphorothioates could be obtained in such a manner. Each class of modified oligonucleotides, particularly phosphate-modified ones, possesses a unique set of properties with a wide range of usability. − However, note that none of the existing types of modified oligonucleotides could overcome all of the challenges in corresponding application fields. Therefore, the development of novel types of modified oligonucleotides is still of relevance.

Phosphoramidates represent a promising type of modified oligonucleotides, since there are a number of conventional methods to obtain them, and also functional residues in their composition could be flexibly varied. One of the methods of obtaining phosphoramidates is based on the Staudinger reaction, which has become a powerful tool to obtain different classes of phosphate-modified oligonucleotides. − There are also some examples of application of the 1,3,5-triazine core as a part of bifunctional reagents for oligonucleotide modification, − which allows the introduction of two functional residues within a single triazine group. We have found a way to effectively combine both of these strategies in one approach.

In our previous work, we developed the method of incorporation of two dodecyl residues into an oligonucleotide structure via a triazinyl phosphoramidate group. The method is based on the Staudinger reaction between the phosphite triester intermediate product of the oligonucleotide synthetic cycle and 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine as the oxidative and modifying reagent, with subsequent substitution using dodecylamine, resulting in the corresponding phosphoramidate. This phosphoramidate derivative containing two dodecyl residues was shown to possess useful properties such as chemical and enzymatic stability, low cytotoxicity, enhanced cellular uptake (including various NA structures with combinations of modification), formation of micelle-like structures, and duplex formation without significant destabilization. In addition, the possibility of formation of supramolecular complexes of the described derivative with human serum albumin, Ku antigen, and PARP1 was demonstrated. −

In this work, we have demonstrated that not only dodecyl residues could be introduced in the triazine core of the triazinyl phosphoramidate group but also numerous other functional residues within a single internucleotide phosphate group, thus endowing the oligonucleotide with diverse properties. The key feature of the proposed synthetic scheme that allows such diversification lies in the formation of an active intermediate following the Staudinger reaction, which serves as a common precursor for all of the triazinyl phosphoramidate derivatives.

In the current study, the main approach for obtaining triazinyl phosphoramidate oligonucleotides (TPO) was profoundly investigated by revealing some features regarding TPO synthesis, diversifying substituents within the triazine core of TPO, and utilizing various substituted azido-triazines as modifier reagents. Therefore, triazinyl phosphoramidate oligonucleotides can be distinguished as a distinct class of phosphate-modified nucleic acids with numerous representatives and potential broad applicability.

Results

General Approach for TPO Synthesis

In our previous works, we developed a particular method for the introduction of two dodecyl residues into the composition of oligonucleotide and studied some of the properties of such molecules. − In current research, we have extended the proposed method to a general approach for the synthesis of triazinyl phosphoramidate oligonucleotides (TPO).

The approach to obtain TPO is based on carrying out an alternative oxidation step of automated solid-phase phosphoramidite synthesis using specific azido-triazines as modifier reagents via the Staudinger reaction. Particular synthetic schemes for obtaining this modified oligonucleotide, where the modifier reagent is 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine, consist of several stages. Initially, the Staudinger reaction between the intermediate product of the growing oligonucleotide chain, i.e., reactive phosphite triester, and 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine takes place, resulting in the formation of triazine-phosphazene containing a P  N bond (Figure , stage A). After the Staudinger reaction, the treatment of the phosphazene compound with appropriate anhydrous amine solutions occurs for the introduction of the desired functional groups (R1–4) into the triazine backbone (Figure , stage B). This is followed by further oligonucleotide synthesis and final cleavage of the full-length product from the solid support (CPG) with desired TPO (Figure , stage c).

General scheme of obtaining TPO: (A) Staudinger reaction with triazine-phosphazene formation; (B) β-elimination of the 2-cyanoethyl group and functional residue introduction with triazinyl phosphoramidate link formation; (C) further oligonucleotide synthesis; R2-cyanoethyl group; –NR1R2 and –NR3R4 functional residues.

The only representative of TPO studied so far containing two dodecyl residues was also obtained by the given scheme with the treatment of a triazine intermediate with dodecylamine solution. It should be noted that the choice of 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine as the modifier oxidative reagent is based on its extremely high reactivity. It was previously shown that nearly quantitative incorporation of the modification core into oligonucleotide using this reagent can be achieved with 0.1 M azide concentration and 15 min of reaction at room temperature.

The considered scheme was taken into service for further investigation. In the actual study, the number of introduced functional residues was notably extended. In addition, different limitations and opportunities of this approach toward obtaining TPO were investigated.

Influence of the β-Elimination of 2-Cyanoethyl Group Synthetic Step on the Efficiency of TPO Obtaining

Previously, we have shown that nearly quantitative incorporation of the triazine core into the oligonucleotide composition could be achieved using 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine as a modifier reagent. The efficiency of modification with this reagent was assessed by obtaining one of the most compact representatives of TPO containing methylamine residues in the triazine core while optimizing the process of synthesis of a dodecyl-containing derivative. Methylamine residues were introduced into the TPO composition during the final deprotection step of the full-length oligonucleotide with aqueous methylamine treatment (Figure , –NR1R2 = –NR3R4 = –NHCH3). Total yield in this case could reach about 90%. However, even under harsh Staudinger reaction conditions with the strong excess of 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine, traces of the unmodified oligonucleotide were still found in the reaction mixture at the end of oligonucleotide synthesis. Taking this fact into consideration, we made an assumption that the origin of the byproduct in the form of an unmodified oligonucleotide occurs not from an incomplete Staudinger reaction with the azide modifier but rather from the hydrolysis of the triazine-phosphazene compound.

Phosphazenes are the common intermediates formed after the Staudinger reaction. , Phosphazenes are quite labile and can easily undergo hydrolysis with the formation of an appropriate compound of pentavalent phosphorus, typically phosphate or phosphine oxide, and amine, which represents the original application of the Staudinger reaction of mild reduction of azides to amines. ,− In the case of oligonucleotide modification by the Staudinger reaction, it is possible to carry out a distinct step of β-elimination of the protective 2-cyanoethyl group within phosphazene conversion to a stable phosphoramidate form , and prevent it from hydrolysis during the final cleavage stage in aqueous methylamine solution, which could lead to unmodified oligonucleotide formation (Figure ).

Potential routes of triazine-phosphazene intermediate transformation by β-elimination (a) and hydrolysis (b) during cleavage in aqueous methylamine solution. Vertical wavy lines represent fragments of immobilized oligonucleotide sequence, and diagonal wavy lines represent fragments of oligonucleotide sequence after final deprotection.

To check our assumption, a series of experiments on the pentathymidylate system with triazine modification positioned on the 5′-end internucleotide phosphate was conducted. In all of the syntheses, the stage of final cleavage represented treatment with aqueous methylamine for 15 min at 55 °C. The first oligonucleotide was cleaved after the Staudinger reaction and the deprotection of the DMTr-group. Herein, both processes, namely, elimination of the cyanoethyl group and phosphazene hydrolysis, may occur in the final stage (Figure , route A, oligonucleotide N-1). In the second experiment, preliminary β-elimination using treatment with anhydrous acetonitrile N,N-diisopropylethylamine solution (DIPEA) was carried out. This way, the elimination process was forced to happen prior to the deblocking stage (Figure , route C, N-2). Comparison of the RP high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) profiles of both reaction mixtures with the control unmodified oligonucleotide (Figure ) confirmed our assumption: in the course of aqueous methylamine treatment, partial phosphazene hydrolysis is observed with the formation of unmodified pentathymidylate, whereas the step of preliminary elimination minimizes this process. It was later found that internucleosidic ester bonds in the phosphazene intermediate can also undergo hydrolysis during the final deprotection step (Figure S1,A,C).

RP HPLC profiles of oligothymidylate reaction mixtures after treatment with 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine and subsequent β-elimination included (below, N-2) or excluded (above, N-1). Chromatography gradient0 to 100% content of 90% acetonitrile in 0.02 M TEA*AcOH in 15 min.

For a more detailed determination of triazine-phosphazene and triazinyl phosphoramidate bond stability, an additional series of experiments on the pentathymidylate system performing their hydrolysis in different conditions was carried out. Besides partial hydrolysis of the phosphazene link during the final cleavage stage (Figure , route A, N-1) and its prevention with the preliminary β-elimination step (Figure , route C, N-2), we decided to estimate the possibility of complete phosphazene hydrolysis. The complete conversion of the Staudinger reaction product to the unmodified pentathymidylate (Figure , route B, N-3) was shown to occur after the treatment with water under rough conditions (pH = 7, 12 h, 55 °C). At the same time, the treatment of the preliminarily obtained phosphoramidate link with water led to a distinct product (Figure , route D, N-4). Poly­(acrylamide gel) electrophoresis (PAGE) analysis and electrospray ionization (ESI) mass spectrometry data revealed that within this derivative, nucleophilic substitution of chlorine atoms to hydroxyl groups takes place (Figure S2). Notably, the derivative probably has an additional negative charge formed by the deprotonation of one of the OH group in PAGE conditions, since its relative mobility is slightly less compared with native pentathymidylate (μ = 0.73 for N-4 vs μ = 0.78 for control) and much more compared with the proper methylamine derivative (μ = 0.73 for N-4 vs μ = 0.57 for N-2, see Figure S2). Interestingly, the same result can be achieved if the standard capping stage of the phosphoramidite synthesis cycle is performed prior to the amination stage. This leads to the appearance in the reaction mixture of byproducts comprising one or two hydroxyl groups within the triazine core (Figure S1,B,C).

Routes for the conversion of the phosphazene intermediate to various derivatives under different sequences of treatment with DIPEA, water, and methylamine. R2-cyanoethyl residue. Vertical wavy lines represent fragments of immobilized oligonucleotide sequence, and diagonal wavy lines represent fragments of oligonucleotide sequence after final deprotection.

As could be seen from the conducted experiments, the phosphazene link can undergo hydrolysis upon the introduction of methylamine residues in the triazine core. At the same time, preliminary elimination greatly increases its stability by phosphoramidate formation, thus raising the TPO obtaining efficacy.

It can be concluded that the presence of water in any amine solution could lead not only to the hydrolysis of chlorine atoms in the triazine core but also to phosphazene bond degradation. As a result, in all further experiments with the introduction of various amine residues, preliminary β-elimination of the 2-cyanoethyl protective group was applied.

Diversity of Substituents in the Triazine Core of TPO

After the Staudinger reaction and sequential β-elimination stage in the considered route of modified oligonucleotide obtaining, an active phosphoramidate intermediate, being a common precursor for all of the TPO derivatives, is formed from the initial phosphazene intermediate. By changing only the solution of the appropriate amine at the stage of substitution of chlorine atoms in a common triazinyl phosphoramidate precursor, it is possible to flexibly diversify the residues introduced. The overall simplified scheme of obtaining TPO with the use of 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine containing the consecutive steps of the Staudinger reaction, β-elimination, and amine residue incorporation through the common triazinyl phosphoramidate precursor is shown in Figure .

Obtaining TPO with Simple Single-Step Substitutions

The introduced triazine core aliphatic residues varied in their length (for example, N-methyl-N-butylamine and N-methyl-N-octadecylamine) and the presence of unsaturated units (for example, oleylamine). Surprisingly enough, it was shown that the best yields of the incorporation reaction of aliphatic amines could be achieved for N-methyl-N-octadecylamine residues, while the worst yields were observed for dimethylamine residues (see Table ). However, since the conditions of the amine introduction reaction were variable, it cannot be concluded that there is some reliable correlation between the length and type of incorporated amine and the reaction efficiency. Moreover, different solutions of appropriate amines contain various amounts of water, which could also affect the overall yields by partial substitution of chlorine atoms with hydroxyl groups in the triazine core (as was mentioned earlier). The same could be concluded for other types of amines as well, i.e., containing additional cationic amino groups or hydroxyl groups.

Nonetheless, the proposed scheme based on a precursor strategy for TPO obtains moderate to high yields of corresponding derivatives, mostly due to the high reactivity of the common phosphoramidate precursor. It is worth noting that even the aromatic aniline residues could be introduced in the triazine core under harsh conditions with an acceptable yield despite the low nucleophilicity of aniline (N-22 derivative). All of the synthesized TPO were analyzed by RP HPLC, which allowed us to estimate the yields of derivatives, and ESI mass spectrometry (ESI-MS), which proved their assumed structures (Figures S3 and S4).

Obtaining TPO with Complex Multiple Step Substitutions

For the residues in the triazine core that bear terminal functional groups, e.g., primary and secondary amino groups and hydroxyl groups, the possibility of further chain assembly was also demonstrated. In general, such reactions in application toward oligonucleotide synthesis are well-known and show high efficacy, since they take place on a solid phase. − For example, primary amino groups in the composition of the triazinyl phosphoramidate derivative bearing tris­(2-aminoethyl)­amine residues (Figure , N-17) could be transformed into either amide groups (Figure , N-29) or guanidine groups (Figure , N-27 and N-28; Figure , route I). The piperazine-containing derivative (Figure , N-21) was shown to be effectively modified with another triazine core in the reaction with cyanuric chloride, with subsequent treatment with various amines (Figure , N-24 and N-25). It is worth noting that an alternate piperazine/triazine motif is a popular example of a dendrimer structure, particularly used for oligonucleotide delivery. , In this work, we have shown the possibility of step-by-step assembly of such a motif in the composition of triazinyl phosphoramidate oligonucleotides (derivative N-26, see Table S1). The hydroxyl residue within the triazine core could be modified with sulfonyl chlorides and then undergo the SN2-type nucleophilic substitution with aminesuch assembly was demonstrated on the example of introduction of piperidine moieties (Figure , N-31).

The described examples are not exhaustive; a broad set of other derivatives bearing complex assembled residues through different synthetic schemes was obtained (see Table S1 for the complete list of oligonucleotide structures with complex assembled residues and the sequence of their assembly). The proposed structures of all TPO with complex assembled residues were confirmed via ESI mass analysis (see Figure S5), which indicates the fundamental possibility of their synthesis. The final yields of some derivatives, especially with the multistep assembly, are rather low (judging by HPLC, data not shown). However, the yields could be increased, since the synthesis of TPO and complex residues assembly takes place on the solid phase, and the reaction conditions could be easily varied and hence optimized.

Taking into account a significant number of experiments using both substituted amines as the source of desired functional groups and multiple step assembly of complex linear and dendritic substituents, we can conclude that an applied strategy based on a common phosphoramidate precursor is a flexible way to obtain a wide variety of representatives of a new distinct class of nucleic acid derivatives, triazinyl phosphoramidate oligonucleotides (TPO).

NMR Study of TPO

In order to classify the TPO more profoundly, we decided to characterize the formed triazinyl phosphoramidate bond by 31P NMR analysis and compare it with the other well-known types of phosphate modifications. Thus, modified dithymidylates 5′-T*T-3′, where * - modification position, as well as native one 5′-TT-3′ (PO), were synthesized. The obtained data together with previous findings not only indicate the distinct nature of the triazinyl phosphoramidate bond but also show the correlation between the chemical shifts of various PN-containing phosphate-modified oligonucleotide derivatives and their sensitivity toward acidic treatment. Thus, alkyl phosphoramidates (Alk) are prone to hydrolysis in an acidic medium (∼11 ppm), whereas phosphoryl guanidines (PG, ∼ −1 ppm) and alkylsulfonyl phosphoramidates (Ms, ∼ −1 ppm) stay intact in similar conditions. The stability of triazinyl phosphoramidates (Tr, ∼4 ppm) was previously shown, and it is well correlated with the obtained data of 31P NMR analysis in the current study.

Considering the above-mentioned, TPO could be distinguished as a distinct class of phosphoramidate nucleic acid derivatives with the unique properties of the P–N bond, which is particularly reflected in the phosphorus chemical shift and acidic stability.

Obtaining Heteronucleotide TPO

We have previously suggested the approach of introduction of two dodecyl residues into the composition of heterooligonucleotide based on triazinyl phosphoramidate chemistry. The approach is based on obtaining the initial dithymidylate TPO link with the following introduction of dodecyl residues in the triazine core and subsequent oligonucleotide synthesis. In all of our previous works, we have only used heteronucleotide TPO with 3′-end localized modified dithymidylate. This was due to the known literature information about the potential side reactions of cyanuric chloride and its reactive derivatives with the exocyclic amino groups of nucleobases.

In the present study, two different types of heteronucleotide TPO with methylamine residues in the triazine core of corresponding sequences 5′-T*ACGT-3′ (N-35) and 5′-G*CGCCAAACAT-3′ (N-36) (* represents modification position) were synthesized via the developed scheme including a β-elimination step using 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine in the same conditions as used in the synthesis of oligothymidylates (0.1 M/RT/15 min). The resulting reaction mixtures were analyzed by HPLC and ESI-MS methods (Figures S7 and S8). According to the analysis, the absence of side products except for typical unmodified oligonucleotide and phosphite triester link degradation products can be observed regardless of oligonucleotide sequence. Only the experiment with much harsher conditions (0.1 M/55 °C/60 min) set for the Staudinger reaction with the chosen modifier led to a significant amount of byproducts (data not shown).

The demonstrated possibility of obtaining heteronucleotide TPO without any significant side products even within 5′-end localized modification greatly expands the application boundaries for the studied method of TPO synthesis. However, modification introduction should be carried out under mild conditions to avoid the side reactions of highly reactive triazine derivatives with the exocyclic amino groups of nucleobases.

Alternative Azido-Triazine Modifiers

We have shown that 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine can be efficiently used in the Staudinger reaction with a growing oligonucleotide chain. Organic azides with donor nature are known to undergo the Staudinger reaction less efficiently, ,− and hence the introduction of donor residues compared to chlorine atoms into azido-triazine composition should lower their reactivity. However, it was also interesting to explore the possibility of using other types of azido-triazines as alternative oxidation and modification agents.

In this study, several azido-triazines containing either desired functional residues or intermediate groups, which are supposed to be subsequently substituted, were obtained (Figure ).

TPO Synthesis Using the 2-Azido-4,6-dimethylamino-1,3,5-triazine Modifier

The use of azido-triazine reagent already containing the desired substituents could be a potentially advantageous approach for obtaining TPO without an additional step of amine treatment of intermediate products. Thus, it was of interest to test representatives of 2-azido-4,6-dialkylamino-1,3,5-triazine as modifiers. 2-Azido-4,6-dimethylamino-1,3,5-triazine was synthesized and examined as the simplest representative of such reagents. Synthesis was performed in two stages2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine was obtained initially and then treated with an excess of methylamine (Figure , route I, and Figure S9A). For the evaluation of the reactivity of 2-azido-4,6-dimethylamino-1,3,5-triazine, oligothymidylate (5′-T*TTTTTTT-3′) with the 5′-end localized modification was synthesized. Initially, the conditions of the Staudinger reaction were chosen as those for 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine (0.1 M, 15 min, 25 °C) in order to compare their reactivity directly. In this case, the yields of TPO obtained using 2-azido-4,6-dimethylamino-1,3,5-triazine turned out to be quite low (less than 15% according to HPLC analysis, M-1, Figure S9B). After testing various protocols, we have found that the chosen dimethylamino modifier could be introduced in the oligonucleotide structure with nearly 90% efficiency (M-2, Figure S9B), but only under significantly harsher reaction conditions (0.5 M, 60 min, 65 °C). We have also tried modifiers of a similar type, containing butylamine residues, but under the same rough conditions, the reaction efficiency did not exceed 30% (M-3, Figure S9C).

It can be concluded that changing chlorine atoms to methylamino substitutions in the initial modifier results in a drastic reduction of the reactivity of the corresponding azido-triazine. Furthermore, the introduction of a longer alkyl part in the structure of the modifier makes it even less reactive in the Staudinger reaction. Despite the apparent convenience of using such reagents that already contain the desired substituents for incorporation into the oligonucleotide structure, the corresponding azides are only suitable for single modification with moderate yields, even under harsh conditions.

TPO Synthesis Using the 2-Azido-4,6-dimethoxy-1,3,5-triazine Modifier

Another example of alternative azido-triazine structures with lower reactivity is 2-azido-4,6-dialkoxy-1,3,5-triazines. The choice of such reagents is based on the possibility of alkoxy residue substitution with amine moieties in the triazine core.

In the present work, 2-azido-4,6-dimethoxy-1,3,5-triazine was synthesized and investigated. The synthetic scheme is similar to this for methylamino-substituted reagent2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine was treated with an excess of methanol to give the final structure (Figure , route II and Figure S10A). Experiments for the evaluation of the reactivity of the obtained azide were carried out on the oligothymidylate model with a triazine core incorporated in a 5′-end localized internucleotide phosphate group. Evaluation of the obtained azide reactivity was tested during the synthesis of oligothymidylate 5′-T*TTTTTTT-3′ (M-4 derivative). It was found that the Staudinger reaction proceeds at 60% under following conditions: 0.25 M, 60 min, and 55 °C (Figure S10B). ESI-MS analysis revealed that the TPO product contains two methoxy groups in the triazine backbone (Figure S10C). Thus, in the case of utilizing 2-azido-4,6-dimethoxy-1,3,5-triazine as the modifier reagent, methoxy groups stay intact and do not undergo either hydrolysis or nucleophilic substitution even in methylamine solution treatment at the final deprotection step.

Despite the fact that the azido-triazine reagent containing methoxy groups exhibits greater reactivity than the appropriate agent with methylamino groups, it is still far from the reactivity of 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine in the Staudinger reaction. However, the probability of substitution of RO groups in TPO could be reconsidered in the case of utilizing 4,6-substituted 2-azido-1,3,5-triazines as modifier reagents with activated ester bonds, for example, pentafluorophenol or tosyl-containing ones. In this case, the activating residues are more likely to be substituted by nucleophiles, such as amines.

TPO Synthesis Using the 2-Azido-4,6-diiodo-1,3,5-triazine Modifier

Another option for alternative azido-triazine agents is based on the introduction of fewer acceptor halogen atoms in the triazine core of its structure. Thus, 2-azido-4,6-diiodo-1,3,5-triazine, which represents one such reagent, was synthesized by first converting cyanuric chloride to cyanuric iodide and then replacing one of the iodine atoms with an azido group (Figure , route III, and Figure S11, A). In contrast to cyanuric chloride, it is possible to introduce one azido group in cyanuric iodide in a reaction with sodium azide without the formation of byproducts containing two or three azido groups.

For the evaluation of the reactivity of 2-azido-4,6-diiodo-1,3,5-triazine for obtaining TPO, oligonucleotides of sequence 5′-T*TTTTTTT-3′ (M-5), where * indicates the position of modification, were synthesized using aqueous methylamine solution treatment during the final deprotection step. All reaction conditions were similar to those for obtaining oligonucleotide N-2 (Staudinger reaction: 0.1 M, 15 min, 25 °C; β-elimination step included). In this case, the yield of desired TPO could reach 90%, which means that the reactivity of the considered modifier is similar to that of 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine. Interestingly, when the β-elimination step was excluded from the synthetic scheme employing an iodine-containing modifier (M-6 derivative), the yield of the desired product decreased more significantly than that in the case of using a chlorine-containing modifier (Figure S11B). Thus, it can be concluded that the phosphazene intermediate obtained from the 2-azido-4,6-diiodo-1,3,5-triazine modifier undergoes hydrolysis much more easily than its chlorine-containing analogue.

These findings additionally demonstrate the importance of the preliminary β-elimination of the 2-cyanoethyl protective group within the phosphazene intermediate during the synthesis of different phosphate-modified oligonucleotides via the Staudinger reaction.

To summarize, we have proposed several types of substituted azido-triazine modifiers as alternatives for 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine. Dialkylamino (−NHR)-substituted modifiers demonstrated limited applicability in TPO synthesis. Modifier with methoxy groups (–OR) was not reactive enough to undergo the Staudinger reaction with acceptable yields, even in harsh conditions. At the same time, the iodine-substituted modifier (–I) can be applied in the Staudinger reaction at room temperature. Despite slightly less reactivity of 2-azido-4,6-diiodo-1,3,5-triazine compared to 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine, this reagent can be considered as a more stable and promising agent for the investigation of TPO synthesis in a fully automated manner.

Considering the fact that automated synthesis of modified oligonucleotides could greatly expand the scope of their use, the development of novel structures as well as optimization protocols of already described azido-triazines for automated TPO obtaining is of relevance.

Discussion

In the present work, the general approach for the synthesis of the novel class of NA derivatives containing a triazine group obtained via the Staudinger reaction, namely, triazinyl phosphoramidate oligonucleotides (TPO), was studied.

Different substituted azido-1,3,5-triazines were applied as modifier reagents for obtaining TPO. The structural features of the triazine core allow two functional residues to be introduced simultaneously, and these residues can be widely varied. This makes the triazine heterocycle a promising modification backbone. 2-Azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine was chosen as the main modifier reagent, since it was shown previously to be highly reactive in the Staudinger reaction, which is caused by the acceptor nature of chlorine atoms in its composition.

In the present study, several key aspects of obtaining TPO were evaluated. Thus, the stability of the phosphazene intermediate of the Staudinger reaction obtained with 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine toward hydrolysis was defined. It was demonstrated that the phosphazene intermediate undergoes hydrolysis with triazine core cleavage even with negligible presence of water. At the same time, preliminary β-elimination of the 2-cyanoethyl protective group within the phosphazene intermediate transforms it to a significantly more stable phosphoramidate form, which inhibits the hydrolysis process. The possibility of selective hydrolysis of chlorine atoms to obtain a hydroxy-substituted triazine structure was also evaluated.

Phosphoramidate, which is formed after the Staudinger reaction between 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine and the phosphite triester link with subsequent β-elimination, contains two vacant chlorine atoms and can be considered a general precursor toward all TPO. Such a strategy was productively applied to the synthesis of a great number of representatives of the TPO class bearing various residues in the triazine core, such as hydrophobic and cationic ones, which indicates notable flexibility of the developed approach. On top of that, the possibility of assembly of complex residue structures within the triazine backbone on the solid phase was demonstrated.

In the course of carrying out 31P NMR studies, chemical shifts of various phosphate-modified oligonucleotide derivatives were determined, reflecting the distinct nature of the P–N bond in TPO.

It was shown that the highly reactive 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine modifier could also be used for obtaining TPO with heteronucleotide sequence without formation of significant amounts of side products when the mild conditions of the Staudinger reaction are applied.

Several less reactive azido-triazines containing better donor groups compared to chlorine atoms were synthesized, and their applicability as alternative modifier reagents was evaluated. Further development of the proposed approach should be based on finding the right combination of substituents with appropriate donor/acceptor properties in the triazine modifier to provide sufficient reactivity and hence the possibility of automated synthesis of TPO.

Conclusions

At present, the Staudinger reaction finds broad applications toward novel phosphate-modified oligonucleotide creation. Such classes of NA derivatives as phosphoryl guanidines ,,,,, and sulfonyl phosphoramidates ,,,, are well-known and profoundly developed. Particularly, phosphoryl guanidine oligonucleotides have already been evaluated as promising drug candidates for several diseases. − However, synthetic routes for such derivatives often lack the flexibility in terms of variation of introduced functional residues, which may hinder the applicability and potency of corresponding classes of oligonucleotide analogues. Thus, the development of new types of phosphate-modified oligonucleotides with a versatile toolbox and convenient synthetic pathways with the capability of controllable substituent variation within the modification backbone is of great interest.

In our work, we have developed a general approach for obtaining a new class of triazinyl phosphoramidate NA derivatives. The approach is based on utilizing highly reactive 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine as an oxidative modifier reagent in the Staudinger reaction with subsequent introduction of functional residues in the triazine core. It was shown to be a promising tool for obtaining and expanding the class of TPO in terms of diversity of functional residues and nucleotide composition.

The gathered information about the key aspects of the Staudinger reaction and the reactivity of the obtained different azido-triazines could help with the next step of technology development: fully automated synthesis of TPO. Considering this, further investigation of azido-triazines with appropriate combinations of substituents for automated incorporation of desired modification during solid-phase synthesis as well as evaluation of the biological effect of different TPO representatives is of relevance.

Synthesis of Azido-Triazine Modifiers

2-Azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine

Sodium azide (1.06 g, 16 mmol) was added to cyanuric chloride (3.0 g, 16.3 mmol) dissolved in 40 mL of acetone. The resulting solution was vigorously stirred at room temperature for 2 h. The reaction mixture was then evaporated, dissolved in 20 mL of CH2Cl2, and washed with concentrated NaCl solution (4 mL × 20 mL). The organic layer was dried over anhydrous Na2SO4 and evaporated to a white solid. Purification was carried out by flash column chromatography in a CH2Cl2/hexane system, 1:1. Combined fractions were evaporated and dried to constant weight in vacuo over a P2O5 layer. The mass of the obtained substance after purification was 1.02 g (yield = 33%). R f = 0.33; CH2Cl2/hexane system, 1:1. IR (KBr) N3 ω (cm–1) 2190. 13C NMR (500 MHz, CDCl3, δ, ppm): 171.41, 172.61.

2-Azido-4,6-dimethoxy-1,3,5-triazine

2-Azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine (1.54 g, 8.1 mmol, 1 equiv) was added at 0 °C to sodium hydroxide (0.65 g, 16.2 mmol, 2 equiv) dissolved in 15 mL of methanol. The resulting solution was gradually heated to room temperature, vigorously stirred for 3 h, and then filtered with acetonitrile from the sodium chloride precipitate on a glass filter. The solution was evaporated, and the resulting white solid was dried to constant weight in vacuo over a P2O5 layer. The ass of the obtained substance was 0.93 g (yield = 63%). R f = 0.28 (TLC system = 10% ethyl acetate in hexane). 1H NMR (80 MHz, CDCl3, δ, ppm): 4.02 (s, 6 H, –OC H 3 ).

2-Azido-4,6-diiodo-1,3,5-triazine

Potassium iodide (2.25 g, 13.6 mmol, 5 equiv) was added in excess to cyanuric chloride (0.5 g, 2.7 mmol, 1 equiv) dissolved in 25 mL of acetone. The resulting solution was stirred overnight at room temperature. The reaction mixture was then evaporated, dissolved in 20 mL of CH2Cl2, and washed with concentrated NaCl solution (3 mL × 20 mL). The organic layer was dried over anhydrous Na2SO4 and evaporated to a light-brown solid of cyanuric iodide (m = 0.4 g, yield = 32%). Sodium azide (0.06 g, 0.96 mmol, 1.1 equiv) was then added to cyanuric iodide (0.4 g, 0.87 mmol, 1 equiv) dissolved in 20 mL of acetone. The resulting solution was stirred at room temperature for 90 min. The reaction mixture was then evaporated, dissolved in 25 mL of CH2Cl2, and washed with concentrated NaCl solution (3 mL × 15 mL). The organic layer was dried over anhydrous Na2SO4 and evaporated to a brick-colored solid of 2-azido-4,6-diiodo-1,3,5-triazine (m = 0.27 g, yield = 82%). R f = 0.71 (TLC system = 10% of ethyl acetate in hexane). IR (KBr) N3 ω (cm–1) = 2175. 13C NMR (500 MHz, CDCl3, δ, ppm): 171.26, 172.46.

2-Azido-4,6-dimethylamino-1,3,5-triazine

Water solution of methylamine (0.082 g, 1.04 mmol, 2 equiv) was added in excess to 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine (0.1 g, 0.52 mmol, 1 equiv) dissolved in 10 mL of CH2Cl2. The resulting solution was vigorously stirred for 2 h at room temperature. The reaction mixture was then washed with concentrated NaCl solution (3 mL × 20 mL). The organic layer was dried over anhydrous Na2SO4 and evaporated to a white solid of 2-azido-4,6-dimethylamino-1,3,5-triazine, and the resulting white solid was dried to constant weight in vacuo over a P2O5 layer (m = 0.035 g, yield = 37%). R f = 0.7 (TLC system = 10% ethanol in toluene). 1H NMR (80 MHz, CDCl3, δ, ppm): 3.05 (d, 6 H, –NHC H 3 ), 6.57 (br s, 2 H, –N H CH3).

2-Azido-4,6-dibutylamino-1,3,5-triazine

Butylamine (0.51 mL, 5.2 mmol, 4 equiv) was added in excess to 2-azido-4,6-dichloro-1,3,5-triazine (0.25 g, 1.3 mmol, 1 equiv) dissolved in 20 mL of tetrahydrofuran (THF). The resulting solution was vigorously stirred overnight at room temperature and then filtered from the butylammonium chloride precipitate on a glass filter. The solution was evaporated, and the resulting white solid was dried to constant weight in vacuo over a P2O5 layer. The mass of the obtained substance was 0.34 g (yield = 99%). R f = 0.58 (TLC system = 10% ethanol in toluene). 1H NMR (500 MHz, CDCl3, ppm): 0.92 (m, 6 H, –C H 3 ), 1.35 (m, 4 H, –C H 2 CH3), 1.53 (m, 4 H, –NHCH2C H 2 –), 3.38 (m, 4 H, –NHC H 2 –), 5.69 (m, 2 H, –N H –). 13C NMR (500 MHz, CDCl3, δ, ppm): 13.37, 19.73, 29.48, 39.65, 165.64, 167.89.

The standard phosphoramidite solid-phase synthesis of all modified and unmodified oligonucleotides containing phosphodiester linkages was carried out on an ASM-800 DNA/RNA synthesizer (Biosset, Novosibirsk, Russia). Oligonucleotides were synthesized at the 0.4 μmol scale using standard commercial 2-cyanoethyl deoxynucleoside phosphoramidites and CPG solid supports (Glen Research, San Diego, CA).

The phosphite triester link of the oligonucleotide resuling from monomer addition without oxidation and capping steps was treated with corresponding azido-1,3,5-triazine modifiers. Oligonucleotide Analyses

A quality assessment of oligonucleotide synthesis was performed through reverse phase HPLC analysis on a Millichrom A02 system using a ProntoSIL-120–5-C18 column 2 mm × 75 mm (Econova, Novosibirsk, Russia) in a linear gradient of acetonitrile 0–50% or 0–90% in 20 mM triethylammonium acetate, pH 7.0, at a flow rate of 200 μL/min, and with detection on 260, 280, and 300 nm wavelengths.

Molecular masses of oligonucleotides were confirmed by LC-MS/MS ESI-MS on an Agilent G6410A mass spectrometer (Santa Clara, CA) in a negative-ion detection mode or, in the case of some derivatives, in a positive-ion detection mode (oligonucleotides N-28 and N-31). The samples were prepared by dissolving oligonucleotides in 20 mM triethylammonium acetate in 60% aqueous acetonitrile at a concentration of 0.1 mM in 10 μL of the sample. Analysis was carried out using 80% aq. Acetonitrile was used as an eluent at a flow rate of 0.1 mL/min and using standard device settings. Molecular masses were calculated from the experimental m/z values obtained for each sample.

The analysis of oligonucleotides by electrophoresis in PAGE was performed using 15 or 20%, 8 M urea, gel with 0.1 M Tris-borate, pH 8.3, as running buffer. The oligonucleotide bands were visualized in a gel by staining with Stains-All (Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany).

The optical spectra of oligonucleotide solutions in mQ water was recorded on a Nanodrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA).
