Endoscopic Submucosal Dissection for Early Gastric Cancer: From Expanding Utilization to Lifelong Survivorship Care
Authors: Han Hong Lee
Published: 2026

The treatment landscape of early gastric cancer (EGC) has changed substantially over the past 2 decades. In Korea, the widespread use of endoscopic screening and advances in diagnostic endoscopy have increased the detection of early-stage disease, thereby expanding the role of organ-preserving treatment. Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) has become a key therapeutic option in this setting. Supported by technical refinement, accumulated expertise, and increasingly standardized criteria, ESD is now established as a definitive treatment for selected EGCs with negligible risk of lymph node metastasis [12]. Comparative studies have further shown that, in appropriately selected patients, ESD can provide acceptable oncologic outcomes while preserving the stomach and reducing procedural burden [23].

The nationwide Korean survey published in this issue is therefore especially meaningful [4]. It provides a real-world picture of gastric ESD practice across Korea. In 27 institutions, 5,460 ESD procedures were performed in 5,250 patients with EGC. The outcomes were excellent, with en bloc resection in 99.2%, complete resection in 91.4%, and curative resection in 80.5%. Procedure-related bleeding and perforation occurred in 3.6% and 0.9% of cases, respectively, and only a very small number of patients required surgery for adverse events. Importantly, curative resection and complication rates were broadly similar across institutions according to procedural volume, suggesting that ESD in Korea has matured into a highly standardized and reproducible treatment platform.

These findings indicate that ESD in Korea is no longer confined to a few highly specialized centers. Rather, it has become an established component of routine gastric cancer care. At the same time, once technical success and safety have reached this level, the next challenge is no longer simply procedural performance. Greater attention must now be directed toward more precise selection of candidates, interpretation of non-curative resection, and long-term management of the preserved stomach. In this sense, the maturation of ESD has shifted the field from procedural innovation to survivorship care.

This point is particularly important because ESD serves not only as treatment but also as a staging procedure. Unlike gastrectomy with lymphadenectomy, ESD preserves the stomach but cannot remove occult nodal disease. Accordingly, non-curative resection often reflects adverse pathological findings recognized only after resection rather than technical failure itself. In the Korean nationwide survey, surgical non-curative resection accounted for 16.7% of cases, and additional surgery was performed much more frequently in this group than after local non-curative resection. This distinction is clinically relevant because ESD frequently clarifies which patients truly require subsequent gastrectomy. Recent work on non-curative ESD, including eCura-based risk stratification, supports an individualized rather than uniform approach to additional surgery [56].

This issue becomes even more relevant in older patients. With population aging, ESD is increasingly used in individuals who may not be ideal candidates for surgery because of frailty, comorbidity, or patient preference. In the Korean survey, non-curative resection was more common in older patients, although age itself did not appear to significantly compromise technical safety. Japanese prospective data have likewise suggested that endoscopic treatment frameworks in elderly patients may require more nuanced interpretation [7]. These observations support a more balanced approach in which oncologic risk, physiologic reserve, life expectancy, and quality of life are all considered together.

If the first message from the nationwide survey is that ESD has become technically mature, the companion study in this issue delivers a second, equally important message [8]: technical success does not conclude patient care. Because the stomach remains in situ, metachronous gastric neoplasia remains an inherent long-term risk after curative endoscopic treatment. In the accompanying study of patients who underwent ESD followed by successful Helicobacter pylori eradication, 77 of 1,191 patients developed metachronous gastric cancer during a median follow-up of 41.5 months, corresponding to a 5-year cumulative incidence of 9.4%. Male sex, severe atrophic gastritis, multiple gastric cancers before eradication, and smoking history were identified as independent risk factors, and the proposed nomogram showed reasonable predictive performance for 3- and 5-year risk. The practical implication is clear: even after successful eradication, metachronous cancer risk persists, and continued annual surveillance remains necessary.

This conclusion is consistent with the broader literature. The landmark randomized trial by Fukase et al. [9] showed that H. pylori eradication after endoscopic resection reduced the incidence of metachronous gastric carcinoma. Subsequent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have largely confirmed a preventive benefit [10]. More recent Korean nationwide data also demonstrated that H. pylori treatment after endoscopic resection was associated with reduced risks of gastric cancer and metachronous neoplasm, including events occurring beyond 3 and 5 years [11]. However, eradication does not eliminate risk completely. Long-term follow-up studies have shown that metachronous neoplasia continues to accumulate even beyond 5 years after ESD [12].

The persistence of risk despite eradication likely reflects the long biological legacy of chronic mucosal injury. Gastric atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, and field cancerization may remain even after bacterial clearance, consistent with the long-standing concept that some patients have already crossed a carcinogenic threshold. In this regard, the companion study is important because it shows that eradication status alone is not sufficient for surveillance planning. Risk varies according to background mucosal damage and patient factors such as smoking history, sex, and multiplicity of lesions. Recent studies using OLGA and OLGIM staging have further demonstrated that background mucosal severity can stratify the long-term risk of metachronous cancer after ESD [13]. Together, these observations suggest that post-ESD surveillance may eventually evolve toward a more risk-adapted strategy.

For now, however, one principle remains unchanged: patients treated with ESD for EGC require long-term, and likely lifelong, endoscopic follow-up. In countries such as Korea, where outcomes after curative treatment are excellent and the number of long-term survivors continues to rise, surveillance should not be regarded as a minor post-treatment formality. It is an integral part of the treatment strategy itself. Accordingly, quality standards for ESD should extend beyond procedural metrics such as resection rate and adverse events to include longitudinal outcomes such as surveillance adherence, detection of metachronous lesions, and timely salvage treatment.

Taken together, the 2 studies in this issue highlight the current status and future direction of gastric ESD with notable clarity. The nationwide Korean survey demonstrates that ESD has achieved a high level of technical standardization and safety in routine practice. The companion study reminds us that organ preservation inevitably requires long-term vigilance. The future of ESD, therefore, lies not only in broader utilization but also in smarter utilization: more refined patient selection, more individualized interpretation of non-curative resection, and more structured surveillance for the preserved stomach. The success of ESD should ultimately be judged not only by how effectively an early cancer is removed, but also by how well the patient is protected from subsequent gastric neoplasia.
