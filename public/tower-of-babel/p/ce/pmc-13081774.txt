Evaluating FABP5 as a Therapeutic Target for Pain Management
Authors: William Warren, Myles Osborn, Martin Kaczocha, Andrew Yates, Saoirse O'Sullivan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and ObjectiveFatty acid‐binding proteins (FABPs) are intracellular lipid transporters. Pharmacological inhibition of FABP5 is analgesic in preclinical visceral, inflammatory, neuropathic and joint pain models. Genetic knockout or knockdown of FABP5 induces analgesia in select visceral and inflammatory pain models. This review explores the current evidence supporting FABP5 modulation in pain.Databases and Data TreatmentWe searched PubMed (January–August 2025) for studies relating to FABP5 modulation in pain. We used terms associated with inhibition, genetic ablation, analgesia, pain behaviours, pain models and associated signalling pathways.ResultsFABP5 inhibition mediates analgesia through various lipid‐signalling receptors (cannabinoid receptor type 1 [CB1], peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor alpha [PPARα] and transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily V member 1 [TRPV1]). FABP5 inhibitors upregulate N‐acylethanolamines and various other N‐acyl amino acids. FABP5 inhibitors also suppress cytokines, chemokines and other inflammatory pathways such as tumour necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin (IL)‐1β, IL‐6, prostaglandin (PG) E2 and microsomal PG E synthase‐1 (mPGES‐1). We recently found that the potent, peripherally selective, FABP5 inhibitor ART26.12 showed a no observable adverse effect level of 1000 mg/kg/day in rats and dogs. FABP5 inhibition may offer a favourable safety profile when compared to currently prescribed pain medications (e.g., opioids, corticosteroids and anticonvulsants).ConclusionsWhilst preclinical data support the potential of FABP5 inhibitors as novel analgesics, further research into sex differences and chronic dosing in visceral and inflammatory pain models is warranted. Despite this, FABP5 inhibition may hold promise for non‐opioid and non‐steroidal management of painful conditions.Significance StatementThis review highlights fatty acid–binding protein 5 (FABP5) inhibition as a novel, non‐opioid and non‐steroidal analgesic strategy with multiple mechanisms of action, broad efficacy in preclinical pain models, and a favourable safety profile. FABP5 inhibition may offer promise for the treatment of complex painful conditions.

Introduction

Fatty acid‐binding protein (FABP) 5 is one of 10 human isoforms in the FABP family (Smathers and Petersen 2011). FABP isoforms are distributed throughout the body, with some showing tissue specificity (e.g., FABP2 is found in the intestine and liver), whilst others are found ubiquitously in many tissue types (e.g., FABP5 is found in skin, tongue, adipocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, mammary gland, brain, intestine, kidney, liver, lung, heart, skeletal muscle, testis, retina, lens and spleen) (Furuhashi and Hotamisligil 2008). Comprised of a central water‐filled binding pocket surrounded by a β barrel and an α‐helical lid, FABP5 binds various lipids, including long‐chain fatty acids (FAs), retinoids, eicosanoids, bile salts and peroxisome proliferators (Zimmerman and Veerkamp 2002). By regulating the intracellular localisation of lipids, FABP5 mediates cellular energy production, lipid signalling and gene expression (Schroeder et al. 2008). FABP5 is prominently expressed in adipocytes, keratinocytes and the brain, but also in many other tissues throughout the body (Masouyé et al. 1997). This is indicative of its role in multiple metabolic processes and general lipid homeostasis. In disease pathology, dysregulation of FABP5 has been implicated in multiple cancer types, dermatological disorders, metabolic diseases, as well as inflammatory and immune signalling amongst others (Hotamisligil and Bernlohr 2015; Nowowiejska et al. 2022; Warren et al. 2023; Warren, Osborn, Duffy, et al. 2024).

FABP5 expression has been identified in pathways related to pain signalling. FABP5 is highly expressed in peptidergic calcitonin gene‐related peptide (CGRP)‐expressing dorsal root ganglia (DRG) neurons. Signalling of the peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor (PPAR)/FABP5 axis regulates the inflammatory response to pain in the DRG (Peng et al. 2017; Mat Harun 2019). Co‐expression of FABP5 with cannabinoid receptor type 1 (CB1) and tropomyosin receptor kinase A has been observed in DRG neurons (Bogdan et al. 2022). FABP5 is also colocalised with the nociceptor marker peripherin in the spinal cord (Peng et al. 2017). FABP5 colocalises with CGRP and transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily V member 1 (TRPV1; a receptor widely implicated in nociception, inflammation and analgesia) in the periaqueductal grey (PAG) (Peng et al. 2017; Maximiano et al. 2024). Patients with osteoarthritis (OA) show elevated FABP5 expression in the knee joint synovium (Gordon et al. 2024).

The endocannabinoid (eCB) system is a lipid‐signalling pathway well known to modulate pain processing. Activation of cannabinoid receptors 1 and 2 (CB2) mediates analgesia (Milligan et al. 2020; Finn et al. 2021; Xu et al. 2023). Other targets that mediate the analgesic effects of eCBs include TRPV1 and G‐protein coupled receptors (e.g., GPR18 and GPR55) (Guindon et al. 2013; Guerrero‐Alba et al. 2018). The first evidence that FABPs modulate the intracellular transport of eCBs came from the laboratory of Dale Deutsch at Stony Brook University. This work showed that overexpression of FABP5 or FABP7, but not FABP3, increased the uptake and hydrolysis of the first identified endocannabinoid, anandamide (AEA), an endogenous CB1/CB2 agonist (Kaczocha et al. 2009). This suggests that FABP5 or FABP7 deliver AEA to fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH) for degradation (increasing AEA uptake by enhancing the concentration gradient of AEA), and that FABP5 inhibition might have analgesic effects by increasing eCB tone.

Given the widespread expression of FABP5 in pain processing areas and its role in modulating the endocannabinoid system, many studies have now investigated the use of FABP5 knockout (KO), knockdown (KD) or pharmacological inhibition in preclinical pain models. The results of these studies show that FABP5 is involved in pain signalling and may be a novel target in the treatment of painful conditions. The aim of this review is to summarise the role and therapeutic potential of targeting FABP5 in pain, whilst considering the limitations of the current evidence.

Literature Search Methods

A literature search was conducted in PubMed between 6th January and 29th August 2025. The search identified studies investigating the role of fatty acid–binding protein 5 (FABP5) in pain and analgesia. Search terms included FABP5 inhibition, FABP5 ablation, FABP5 knockout, FABP5 knockdown, FABP5 silencing, FABP5 KO, FABP5 KD, FABP5 siRNA, fatty acid‐binding protein 5, epidermal FABP and EFABP in combination with pain‐related terms such as pain, analgesia, nociception, antinociception, allodynia, hyperalgesia, hypersensitivity, chronic pain, persistent pain, neuropathy, visceral pain and inflammatory pain. Search terms also included specific pain models. These included acetic acid, complete Freund's adjuvant, carrageenan, lipopolysaccharide, chemotherapy‐induced peripheral neuropathy, oxaliplatin‐induced peripheral neuropathy, paclitaxel‐induced peripheral neuropathy, diabetic neuropathy, cancer‐induced bone pain, arthritis, osteoarthritis, nerve injury, spared nerve injury, chronic constriction injury, sciatic nerve ligation, formalin and capsaicin. Additional terms included endocannabinoid, anandamide, palmitoylethanolamide, oleoylethanolamide, N‐acyl amino acids, cannabinoid receptor, peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor alpha and transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily V member 1. Articles were screened for relevance to FABP5 modulation in preclinical or clinical models of pain. Reference lists of included studies were examined to identify additional relevant publications. Articles based on our existing knowledge were also included.

Pharmacological Inhibition of FABP5 in Preclinical Pain Models

Truxillic acid‐derived FABP5 inhibitors have been in development since 2012. Truxillic acids are classified as cyclobutane‐containing dicarboxylic acids and are produced via a [2 + 2] photocycloaddition reaction involving cinnamic acid isomers. Truxillic acid itself is stereochemically diverse, with five distinct stereoisomers (Yuan et al. 2020). This makes it a promising scaffold for ligand design. Development of truxillic acid‐derived FABP5 inhibitors was predated by the discovery that FABP5 and FABP7 act as intracellular chaperones for AEA, an eCB known for its analgesic properties (Kaczocha et al. 2009). A first‐generation compound SBFI26 was shown to be anti‐nociceptive and anti‐inflammatory despite having no direct activity at CB1 or FAAH, and only weak agonism of PPARα/γ (Berger et al. 2012). Second‐generation inhibitors were introduced in 2014 (Kaczocha et al. 2014), with Artelo currently developing a third‐generation compound ART26.12 (Warren, Osborn, Tsantoulas, et al. 2024). Each successive generation of compounds aimed to improve potency, FABP5 selectivity, and pharmaceutical properties (e.g., logP).

Chemical‐Induced Pain Models

FABP5 inhibition has been assessed in the formalin (male C57Bl/6 mice), carrageenan (male C57Bl/6 mice), complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA; male C57Bl/6 mice), monoiodoacetate (MIA)‐induced model of OA (male and female Sprague–Dawley rats), and acetic acid writhing (male C57Bl/6 mice) models.

The formalin pain model involves a biphasic response that is characterised by an acute (0–5 min) and a quiescent phase (15–45 min). In this test, the first‐generation truxillic acid‐based FABP5 inhibitor SBFI26 (20 mg/kg, intraperitoneally [IP]) prophylactically reduced nocifensive behaviour (e.g., paw licking) in the primary phase, with no significant effect in the secondary phase (Berger et al. 2012). In a subsequent study, SBFI26 prophylactically reduced nocifensive behaviours (e.g., paw licking) in both phases. Analysis showed that SBFI26 was associated with increased brain levels of AEA, implicating the eCB system in analgesia. SBFI50 (20 mg/kg, IP) and SBFI60 (20 mg/kg, IP) also prophylactically reduced nocifensive behaviour (e.g., paw licking) in the first phase of the formalin test, but not the second, which may be related to their slightly lower affinity for FABP5 than SBFI26 (Kaczocha et al. 2014).

The carrageenan model is associated with an acute inflammatory response, with swelling peaking at 3–5 h (Winter et al. 1962; Otterness and Moore 1988), whilst the CFA model is associated with a more chronic response where swelling peaks after around 24 h (Iadarola et al. 1988; Stein et al. 1988). In the carrageenan model, SBFI26 (20 mg/kg, IP) prophylactically reversed thermal hyperalgesia and attenuated paw oedema (Berger et al. 2012; Kaczocha et al. 2014). These anti‐nociceptive and anti‐oedematous effects were inhibited by antagonists of CB1 or CB2 (Berger et al. 2012). The second‐generation FABP5 inhibitors SBFI50 and SBFI60 also showed preventative efficacy in the carrageenan model; however, no antagonist experiments were conducted with these compounds (Kaczocha et al. 2014). In a separate study, pretreatment with SBFI26 (20 mg/kg, IP) had a localised anti‐inflammatory effect (Bogdan et al. 2018). At the carrageenan injection site, animals treated with SBFI26 (20 mg/kg, IP) showed reduced expression of tumour necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin (IL)‐1b, IL‐6, and prostaglandin E2 (an eicosanoid that exacerbates inflammatory pain) (Harvey et al. 2004). SBFI26 also attenuated the expression of microsomal PGE synthase 1 (mPGES‐1; an enzyme implicated in the biosynthesis of PGE2) via inhibition of IL‐1β (a cytokine that induces mPGES‐1 through the nuclear factor kappa‐light‐chain‐enhancer of activated B [NF‐κB] signalling pathway) (Han et al. 2002; Catley et al. 2003; Trebino et al. 2003; Guay et al. 2004). It was also shown that FABP5 may be involved in the transportation of the prostaglandin precursor arachidonic acid to cyclooxygenase 2 (COX2; an enzyme involved in PGE2 biosynthesis). In differentiated, LPS‐stimulated, human THP‐1 monocytic cells, synthesis of PGE2 was suppressed by bath application of SBFI26. SBFI26 did not have an inhibitory effect on COX2 activity, suggesting a reduction in arachidonic acid transport to COX2 as opposed to direct inhibition of enzymatic activity (Bogdan et al. 2018). FABP5 and FABP7 are known intracellular chaperones of arachidonic acid with nM affinity (Shinoda et al. 2020).

In the CFA model, another study investigated whether curative treatment of SBFI26 mediated analgesia through peripheral or central mechanisms. Intraplantar injection of SBFI26 (50 mg) reversed mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia, with no effect seen in the contralateral control paw (Peng et al. 2017). The anti‐nociceptive effect of SBFI26 lasted for 4 h at the injection site, even after rapid drug clearance (~1 h). Intracerebroventricular (ICV) injection of SBFI26 (50 mg) also reversed thermal hyperalgesia, with no effect on mechanical allodynia, and was associated with upregulation of AEA, palmitoylethanolamide (PEA) and oleoylethanolamide (OEA) in the brain, suggesting that central modulation of the eCB system plays a role in SBFI26's analgesic effect. However, there was no effect of SBFI26 on mechanical or thermal‐induced pain when given intrathecally. SBFI26 injected into the PAG or rostral ventromedial medulla also had no effect on thermal hyperalgesia (mechanical allodynia was not measured in these assays). When co‐administered with CB1, PPARα or TRPV1 antagonists, the local effects of intraplantar SBFI26 administration were found to be mediated by CB1 and PPARα, whilst the central effects of ICV injection were mediated by PPARα and TRPV1 (Peng et al. 2017).

In a screening assay of novel truxillic acid monoesters, curative administration of compounds 3 (SBFI‐26), 3f, 3 L‐A, 4b and 4j (40 mg/kg, IP) reversed thermal hyperalgesia induced by CFA. Compounds that showed high FABP5 affinity and moderate hydrophobicity were associated with a greater analgesic effect in vivo (Yan et al. 2018). Another screening study found that compounds α‐1, α‐4, α‐16, γ‐3 and γ‐9 reversed CFA‐induced thermal hyperalgesia at 20–40 mg/kg (Wang et al. 2022). Screening truxillic monoamides (10, 20 and 40 mg/kg, IP) showed that compounds α‐1 ad, α‐3 ad and α‐6 ad reversed CFA‐induced thermal hyperalgesia, with the effects of α‐1 ad lasting up to 6 h (Zhu et al. 2023). The authors concluded that changes to binding pocket sizes could affect binding affinity. This may then alter efficacy, with high FABP5 affinity associated with greater analgesic effects.

In the acetic acid writhing test, prophylactic administration of SBFI26 (5, 20 and 40 mg/kg, IP) showed a dose‐responsive analgesic effect on writhing, with a maximal response achieved at 20 mg/kg (Kaczocha et al. 2014). When administered with antagonists of CB1, CB2, PPARα and opioid receptors (naloxone [non‐selective opioid receptor antagonist]), the analgesic effect of SBFI26 was dependent on CB1 and PPARα, but not CB2 or opioid receptors. SBFI50, SBFI60 and SBFI62 showed no effect in this model, which may be attributed to their lower FABP5 binding affinity compared with SBFI26 (Kaczocha et al. 2014).

The efficacy of SBFI26 (20 mg/kg, IP) and SBFI103 (20 mg/kg, IP) were assessed in a unilateral MIA‐induced model of OA (male and female Sprague–Dawley rats). Acute injection of both FABP5 inhibitors and chronic administration of SBFI‐103 alleviated incapacitance, resulting in more even weight distribution between the hind paws. The acute efficacy of both SBFI26 and SBFI103 was not affected by coadministration of CB1, CB2 and PPARα antagonists, unlike in inflammatory and neuropathic pain models. When compared to vehicle controls, chronic treatment with SBFI103 attenuated the expression of OA marker matrix metallopeptidase 13 and reduced upregulation of CC and CXC chemokine ligands (CCL2, CCL7, CCL9 and CXCL1), as well as markers of the NF‐κB and NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 inflammasome (NLRP3) (Gordon et al. 2024). The MIA model has limitations in that it primarily reflects chemically induced joint degeneration and may not capture the full complexity of human OA pathophysiology (e.g., mechanical or age‐related factors). Future studies may wish to include other OA models and an alternate strain or species. Further investigation into analgesic mechanisms other than CB1, CB2 and PPARα is also warranted.

In summary, FABP5 inhibition shows efficacy in the formalin (male C57Bl/6 mice), carrageenan (male C57Bl/6 mice), CFA (male C57Bl/6 mice) and MIA (male and female Sprague Dawley rats) models of inflammatory pain, as well as acetic acid writhing (male C57Bl/6 mice; see Table 1). These effects are mediated via central and peripheral mechanisms. Activation of CB1 and PPARα is implicated in the peripheral effects, whilst central mechanisms involve activation of PPARα and TRPV1, as well as upregulation of N‐acylethanolamines. In addition, FABP5 inhibition mediates a strong anti‐inflammatory effect which is underpinned by suppression of cytokine and prostaglandin signalling. Despite this, it is important to highlight some limitations of the current evidence. All experiments, except the MIA model, exclusively used male mice, which limits translation of findings to females and other species. Whilst the CFA model used a curative paradigm (i.e., dosing following CFA‐induced inflammation), most studies employed preventative dosing paradigms (i.e., dosing prior to pain induction), which may not reflect clinical scenarios where patients seek treatment after pain onset. Finally, all studies, except the MIA model, used an acute dosing scheme, with no chronic assessment of the inhibitors' long‐term efficacy. It is therefore unclear whether the compounds would show efficacy over days, weeks or months in these other models.

Neuropathic Pain Models

Neuropathic pain typically manifests as mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia, where pain is experienced in response to stimuli that would normally be below the pain threshold. Current treatment options for neuropathy include antidepressants, anticonvulsants and opioids, all of which are associated with side effects such as neuropsychological effects, weight gain, dependency, withdrawal and overdose (Bonnet and Scherbaum 2017; Wang 2019). FABP5 inhibitors have been tested in chronic constriction injury (CCI; male Fisher 344 mice), oxaliplatin‐induced peripheral neuropathy (OIPN; male Sprague–Dawley rats), and paclitaxel‐induced peripheral neuropathy (PIPN; male and female Sprague–Dawley rats), streptozocin (STZ)‐induced diabetic neuropathy (male Wistar rats) and cancer‐induced bone pain (CIBP; female Sprague–Dawley rats).

FABP5 inhibition was first assessed in neuropathy using SBFI26 (20 mg/kg, IP) in the CCI model. Curative SBFI26 administration reversed thermal hyperalgesia at 1‐ and 4‐h post‐dose, with no effect on mechanical allodynia (Kaczocha et al. 2014).

Artelo Biosciences is developing the third‐generation FABP5 inhibitor ART26.12 for the prevention and treatment of peripheral neuropathy and have carried out numerous preclinical studies. In an OIPN model, we showed that acute, curative dosing of ART26.12 (25–100 mg/kg, PO) reversed mechanical allodynia, with the effects of 100 mg/kg lasting more than 8 h (Warren, Osborn, Tsantoulas, et al. 2024). When co‐administered with antagonists of CB1, CB2, PPARα or TRPV1, the effect of ART26.12 was blocked by CB1 antagonism and reduced by CB2, PPARα and TRPV1 antagonism. In a treatment model of established OIPN, chronic oral dosing of ART26.12 (25 mg/kg BID) mechanical allodynia when tested on the first and last day of treatment. There was no effect on cold hyperalgesia. In a prevention model of OIPN, ART26.12 (25 mg/kg BID, PO) reversed cold hyperalgesia and attenuated mechanical allodynia (Warren, Osborn, Tsantoulas, et al. 2024). We also showed similar efficacy of ART26.12 in a prevention model of PIPN (i.e., concurrent dosing of paclitaxel and ART26.12). In both male and female rats, ART26.12 (25 and 50 mg/kg BID, PO) reversed mechanical allodynia and attenuated cold hyperalgesia. This was also associated with reduced weight loss in the ART26.12 (50 mg/kg, BID) group (Warren et al. 2025).

In the STZ‐induced diabetic neuropathy, male Wistar rats were injected with STZ (55 mg/kg, IP) at Day 0, which caused elevated blood glucose levels and reduced paw withdrawal thresholds. We showed that ART26.12 (25 and 100 mg/kg BID, PO) curatively reversed mechanical allodynia in comparison with the STZ‐vehicle‐treated animals. This effect was comparable to the positive control duloxetine (60 mg/kg QD, PO). There was no effect of ART26.12 or duloxetine on blood glucose levels (Warren et al. 2025).

In the CIBP treatment model, female Sprague–Dawley rats were injected with murine breast cancer cells in the left tibial bone cavity. Curative dosing of ART26.12 (100 mg/kg BID, PO) showed acute efficacy on Days 15 and 17, whilst the lower dose (25 mg/kg BID, PO) showed latent efficacy with chronic dosing on Days 19 and 21. This is contrasted against tramadol (30 mg/kg QD, PO), which showed significant analgesia compared to CIBP vehicle‐treated animals at all time points. Only CIBP vehicle‐treated animals showed a significant reduction in bone mineral density when compared with the sham‐treated cohort (Warren et al. 2025).

In summary, FABP5 inhibition is efficacious in CCI, oxaliplatin‐induced peripheral neuropathy, paclitaxel‐induced peripheral neuropathy, cancer‐induced bone pain, and STZ‐induced diabetic neuropathy (see Table 2). The effects (at least in OIPN) appear to be mediated through CB1, CB2, PPARα and TRPV1. Overall, the current evidence suggests that FABP5 inhibition may be a therapeutic tool for the treatment of several neuropathic conditions. Despite this, it is important to note that most of the studies described above used a small range of species and strains (Sprague–Dawley or Wistar rats and Fisher 344 mice). This may limit translation of findings between different species. Only the PIPN prevention study included males and females in the same paradigm, with other studies only focusing on one sex. However, the use of female rats in the CIBP study broadens the scope of ART26.12's potential effects across sexes. Acute and chronic dosing regimens were used across the various neuropathy studies, suggesting that FABP5 inhibition may be efficacious over extended periods; however, the maximum dosing period testing in these studies was limited to 1 week. Further efficacy studies should aim to extend the dosing period to better model the requirements of patients with chronic neuropathic pain. Most of the current evidence assessed the effects of FABP5 inhibition 15 days following induction of neuropathy. However, dosing in the CCI model was after 10 days of surgery, which may bring into question the specificity of the neuropathy model as opposed to post‐surgical effects. Finally, although both preventative and curative paradigms were explored (especially with ART26.12), the preventative models (e.g., concurrent dosing with chemotherapy in PIPN/OIPN) are less reflective of typical clinical practice, where patients usually receive treatment after symptom onset. Greater emphasis on curative models with longer delays between injury and treatment initiation may enhance translational relevance.

Potential Mechanisms of Action of FABP5 Inhibition in Pain

Our OIPN study clearly showed that ART26.12‐mediated analgesia was dependent on CB1 and also involved CB2, TRPV1 and PPARα (Warren, Osborn, Tsantoulas, et al. 2024). Other studies have also investigated the CB‐dependent effects of other FABP5 inhibitors as well as the modulation of endogenous lipids. In HeLa cells, SBFI‐26 caused upregulation of AEA (Berger et al. 2012), whilst in FABP5/7‐KO mice, AEA was increased in the brain (Kaczocha et al. 2015). AEA has long been known as a mediator of analgesia. In mice, peripherally restricted upregulation of AEA levels via the peripherally selective FAAH inhibitor URB937 (IP) attenuated pain‐associated behaviours in the acetic acid, carrageenan and nerve ligation assays. In all models, these effects were blocked by a CB1 antagonist showing AEA's CB1‐dependent mechanism of action (Clapper et al. 2010). In the carrageenan model, AEA's anti‐oedema effect was ameliorated by a CB2 antagonist (Clapper et al. 2010). AEA delivered intrathecally ameliorated carrageenan‐induced thermal hyperalgesia; this effect was attenuated by a TRPV1 antagonist (Horvath et al. 2008). In xenopus oocytes, AEA has been shown to inhibit Nav1.2, Nav1.6, Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 with a half‐maximal inhibitory concentration of 17, 12, 27 and 40 μmol/L, respectively (Okura et al. 2014). These receptors have been greatly implicated in pain transmission (St John Smith 2018).

PEA and OEA are upregulated by FABP5/7‐KO (Kaczocha et al. 2015). In the carrageenan (subcutaneous) and phorbol ester‐induced ear (topical) oedema models, PEA and OEA ameliorated inflammation in wild‐type mice, yet had no effect in PPARα‐KO mice (Lo Verme et al. 2005). Additionally, in the CFA model, PEA (subcutaneous) mediated a PPARα‐dependent anti‐nociceptive effect. This was caused by downstream modulation of TRPV1 channels following PEA‐induced PPARα activation (Aldossary et al. 2019).

In our OIPN study, we carried out targeted lipidomic analysis on pain processing tissues. This aimed to establish the role of lipid modulation in analgesia caused by ART26.12. This analysis showed upregulated N‐acyl amino acids in the spinal cord, a group of lipids known to regulate pain (Warren, Osborn, Tsantoulas, et al. 2024). N‐arachidonoyl glycine (NAGly) has shown analgesic effects in both the formalin (subcutaneous) and sciatic nerve ligation (intrathecal) models (Huang et al. 2001; Vuong et al. 2008). In the sciatic nerve ligation model, NAGly's anti‐nociceptive effect was not affected by CB1 or CB2 antagonists (Vuong et al. 2008). However, subsequent analysis from whole cell patch clamp recordings in lamina II neurons from rat spinal cord slices showed that NAGly blocks glycine uptake via GLYT2, causing inhibition of glycinergic synaptic transmission (Jeong et al. 2010). This points to a potential mechanism of action for NAGly in the spinal cord, which supports the finding that intrathecal administration of NAGly in the sciatic nerve ligation model caused analgesia (Vuong et al. 2008). In the CCI model of neuropathic pain, injection of NAGly into the PAG produced an anti‐allodynic effect, which was attenuated by a GPR18 antagonist (Narasimhan et al. 2020). In vitro research in HEK‐293 cells also shows that NAGly has an anti‐inflammatory effect via GPR18 (Burstein et al. 2011). Other mechanisms of action have been suggested for NAGly's anti‐nociceptive effect. For example, NAGly is a FAAH inhibitor that significantly increased plasma AEA levels when given orally (Burstein et al. 2002; Grazia Cascio et al. 2004). Finally, in mouse sensory neurons, NAGly inhibited native T‐type I(Ca) channels, which are an emerging analgesic target (Shin et al. 2008; Ross et al. 2009; Harding and Zamponi 2022). This mechanism was supported by a murine model of thermal hyperalgesia where NAGly (subcutaneous) mediated analgesia in wild‐type animals but not Cav3.2‐KO mice (Barbara et al. 2009).

Other N‐acyl amino acids modulated by ART26.12 were N‐palmitoyl glycine (PalGly), N‐docosahexaenoyl proline, N‐palmitoyl tyrosine, N‐arachidonoyl tyrosine, N‐linoleoyl tyrosine and N‐palmitoyl alanine. Of these, PalGly has been studied the most, with a paucity of research regarding the others. In the carrageenan and acetic acid models, orally delivered PalGly showed dose‐dependent oedema inhibition and attenuated writhing behaviour (Usifoh et al. 2014). PalGly is expressed at high levels in the rat skin and spinal cord. Research showed that PalGly effectively attenuated heat‐evoked firing of nociceptive neurons in the rat dorsal horn, which was mediated by Ca2+ influx in the DRG. The same study showed that pharmacological inhibition of FAAH caused upregulation of PalGly (Rimmerman et al. 2008). In response to the carrageenan model, certain N‐acyl amino acids were upregulated in different regions of the brain. For example, PalGly was upregulated in the hippocampus, N‐palmitoyl alanine was downregulated in the midbrain, and N‐arachidonoyl tyrosine was upregulated in the thalamus (Raboune et al. 2014). These are all brain regions known to be involved in pain processing (Loyd and Murphy 2009; Yen and Lu 2013; Vasic and Schmidt 2017). N‐docosahexaenoyl proline was shown to be a TRPV1 antagonist, whilst N‐palmitoyl tyrosine, N‐arachidonoyl tyrosine and N‐linoleoyl tyrosine were TRPV4 agonists. N‐palmitoyl tyrosine was also a TRPV2 agonist (Raboune et al. 2014). Whilst direct binding of N‐acyl amino acids to FABP5 has not been studied, structural similarities to known ligands (alongside modelling work) suggest that it is highly likely that they are chaperoned by FABP5 (Warren, Osborn, Tsantoulas, et al. 2024).

Taken together, this evidence suggests that FABP5 inhibition modulates multiple lipids, which have shown analgesic activity at known pain targets (see Figure 2). Lipid modulation is thought to result from decreased delivery of lipids to intracellular catabolic enzymes such as FAAH (N‐acylethanolamines) and ACY1 (N‐acyl amino acids), or anabolic enzymes such as COX2 (arachidonic acid for PG synthesis).

FABP5 Knockout or Knockdown in Preclinical Pain Models

The genetic KO or KD of FABP5 has been assessed in several preclinical pain models. The first of these studies used dual FABP5/FABP7‐KO mice. Both isoforms were ablated together given their involvement in eCB transportation, a key mechanism of FABP5‐mediated analgesia (Kaczocha et al. 2015). In the carrageenan, formalin and acetic acid pain models (male C57Bl/6 mice), dual FABP5/7‐KO attenuated thermal hyperalgesia, nociception and writhing, respectively. Dual FABP5/7‐KO was also associated with a reduction in paw oedema following administration of carrageenan. Further characterisation of these animals showed that dual FABP5/7‐KO increased AEA, PEA and OEA levels in the brain. These changes were not caused by alterations to FAAH (the primary degrading enzyme of AEA) activity, nor were there perturbations to CB1 expression, N‐acyl phosphatidylethanolamine phospholipase D (an enzyme involved in the biosynthesis of AEA), monoacylglycerol lipase (the primary degrading enzyme of 2‐AG) or COX2. Analgesia in the acetic acid test was dependent on CB1 and PPARα, whilst the effect of dual KO in the carrageenan test was PPARα and TRPV1‐dependent (Kaczocha et al. 2015). The role of FABP7 in this model was thought to be limited. Comparing the relative fold‐change, FABP5/7‐KO and FABP5‐KO similarly increase AEA levels in the brain and adult mice show relatively low brain expression of FABP7 (Owada et al. 1996; Yu et al. 2014). It is therefore likely that FABP5‐KO was the main driver behind the effects seen in this study.

Due to FABP5's expression in nociceptors and its role in the innate immune system, research began to explore how FABP5 regulates pain via inflammatory mechanisms. FABP5‐KO attenuated thermal hyperalgesia in the carrageenan model (male C57Bl/6 mice), which was associated with reduced oedema at the injection site (Bogdan et al. 2018; Yan et al. 2018). This effect was associated with attenuated release of CGRP (a molecule associated with nociceptor sensitisation) in ipsilateral spinal sections and reduced release of TNF, IL‐1β, IL‐6, PGE2 and mPGES‐1 at the carrageenan injection site. However, there was no effect of FABP5‐KO on COX2, despite carrageenan‐induced upregulation of both mPGES‐1 and COX2 at the inflammation site. Investigation with CB1, CB2, PPARα and PPARγ antagonists showed that FABP5‐KO‐mediated suppression of mPGES‐1 was not dependent on any of these receptors. Instead, it was shown that FABP5 induces mPGES‐1 through NF‐κB activation and nuclear translocation and that FABP5‐KO‐mediated suppression of IL‐1β attenuated mPGES‐1 expression (Bogdan et al. 2018).

Under conditions of inflammatory pain, sensitisation of TRPV1 receptors is known to sustain hyperalgesia (Yu et al. 2008; Xiao et al. 2015; Lis et al. 2017). Using the carrageenan (male and female C57Bl/6 mice) and capsaicin/nerve growth factor pain models, the effect of conditional FABP5‐KO (FABP5‐cKO) in TRPV1‐positive nociceptors was assessed to see if this could disrupt hyperalgesia. The authors found that both global FABP5‐KO and cKO in TRPV1‐positive nociceptors produced anti‐nociceptive effects by preventing the sensitisation of TRPV1 (Bogdan et al. 2022). This was associated with reduced CGRP expression and increased levels of AEA, PEA and OEA in the DRG, which was caused by reduced N‐acylethanolamine transportation and subsequent metabolism. CB1 and PPARα antagonists restored thermal pain hypersensitivity in these models. Calcineurin (also known as protein phosphatase 2B) is a protein known to desensitise TRPV1 activity (Mohapatra and Nau 2005). CB1 agonists have been shown to desensitise TRPV1 via calcineurin (Patwardhan et al. 2006). In the FABP5‐KO model, the calcineurin inhibitor cyclosporin A restored TRPV1 sensitisation in the DRG. Taken together, this evidence suggests that FABP5‐KO‐mediated analgesia is dependent on CB1, PPARα and calcineurin. These effects were not seen with FAAH inhibition. This may be explained by the fact that FAAH inhibition did not block TRPV1 sensitisation as FABP5 inhibition did. Additionally, FAAH inhibition may induce TRPV1 activation and increase nociception (Carey et al. 2016). It is possible that FAAH inhibition‐induced upregulation of N‐acyl taurines (Saghatelian et al. 2006), which are known TRPV1 agonists, may oppose N‐acylethanolamine‐ and CB1‐induced antinociception (Bogdan et al. 2022).

FABP5‐KD in male C57Bl/6 mice has been assessed using adeno‐associated viruses (AAVs) bearing shRNA targeting FABP5 (Peng et al. 2017). In the dorsolateral PAG, a brain region involved in the analgesic effects of cannabinoids and eCBs (Lichtman et al. 1996), AAVs attenuated FABP5 expression by around 55%. Using the CFA pain model, FABP5‐KD in the PAG caused an increase in AEA tone but had no effect on thermal hyperalgesia following injection of CFA (Peng et al. 2017).

Overall, these studies show that the anti‐nociceptive effects of FABP5‐KO and KD are dependent on a plethora of receptors, including CB1, CB2, PPARα and TRPV1. Additionally, analgesia is associated with a reduction in inflammatory mediators, such as TNF, IL‐1β, IL‐6, PGE2 and mPGES‐1. The mechanism of action between FABP5 and FAAH inhibition is distinct, given that FABP5 inhibition mediates suppression of TRPV1, whilst FAAH inhibition does not. The current evidence is limited by the predominant use of male mice, reducing translational relevance and generalisability. The use of dual FABP5/7 knockouts and global FABP5‐KO models also complicates the interpretation of isoform‐specific or cell‐type‐specific effects; however, the use of cKO in TRPV1‐positive nociceptors allows for greater specificity.

In Vitro Evidence of FABP5 Involvement in Clinical Pain Pathology

Only one study to date has clinically assessed the involvement of FABP5 in pain (Gordon et al. 2024). Tissue samples from the synovium of male and female patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty were analysed for FABP5 expression. Expression profiles showed no sex differences and that FABP5 was localised in the intima and tissue interior. Assessing the different cell types within the synovium, FABP5 was expressed in fibroblasts, endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, as well as antigen presenting cells, which are implicated in OA pain (Sakurai et al. 2019). After incubation with SBFI103, human synovial tissue showed attenuated expression of CCL2, IL‐6, IL‐8 and CXCL1 (Gordon et al. 2024), which have previously been associated with clinical and preclinical OA pain (Orita et al. 2011; Raoof et al. 2018; García‐Manrique et al. 2021; Nanus et al. 2021). This suggests that FABP5 inhibition has a relevant target profile for attenuating OA pain.

Safety Implication of FABP5 Inhibition

Currently prescribed pain medications are not without their limitations. For example, non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs cause gastrointestinal issues (Tai and McAlindon 2021), patients using opioids or anticonvulsants are liable to addiction and overdose (Bonnet and Scherbaum 2017; Wang 2019), whilst corticosteroids are unsuitable for long‐term treatment due to immunosuppression, osteoporosis and other complications (Rice et al. 2017). We showed that ART26.12 has a no‐observable‐adverse‐effect‐level of 1000 mg/kg in both rats and dogs, suggesting a favourable safety profile (Warren, Osborn, Tsantoulas, et al. 2024). Given ART26.12 has very low brain penetrance, the potential for ART26.12‐induced CNS effects is negligible. The first‐generation compound SFBI‐26 was also shown to have no risk of dependence or impairment of motor/cognitive processes (Thanos et al. 2016). Our recent review assessing the safety of FABP inhibition found that there was a low risk of toxicity associated with pharmacological inhibition of FABP5 (Warren, Osborn, Duffy, et al. 2024). Additionally, our recently completed single ascending dose study assessing ART26.12 in human healthy volunteers demonstrated there were no drug‐related adverse events, and no tolerability issues or safety signals were detected across multiple assessments (vital signs, electrocardiograms, clinical laboratory tests, physical examinations and visual analogue mood scales) (Yates 2025).

Conclusion

Suppression of FABP5 is efficacious in preclinical models of visceral, inflammatory, neuropathic and joint pain. FABP5 inhibition mediates analgesia through several mechanisms, including CB1, CB2, PPARα and TRPV1, but also through non‐endocannabinoid mechanisms yet to be fully elucidated. FABP5 inhibition is also anti‐inflammatory, with suppression of cytokines, chemokines, PGE2 and the NF‐κB pathway. Many of the effects of FABP5 inhibitors are through modulation of lipid signalling; for example, through upregulation of N‐acylethanolamines and N‐acyl amino acids. Future preclinical research may look to evaluate the efficacy of FABP5 inhibition in both sexes and more species, with longer dosing periods and alternative pain models. The clinical prospects of FABP5 inhibition seem positive, with ART26.12 having successfully been dosed in a Single Ascending Dose study. ART26.12 is currently being developed for the treatment of chemotherapy‐induced peripheral neuropathy. Overall, the current evidence suggests that FABP5 inhibitors may hold promise as a non‐steroidal, non‐opioid alternative to pain management.
