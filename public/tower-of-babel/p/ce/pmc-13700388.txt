Use of multi-modal artificial intelligence model for prediction of atrial fibrillation in hospitalized patients
Authors: Unmesh Khanolkar, Ashish Yadav, Avdhesh Mann, Laith Alomari, Emmanuel Otabor, Rukmini Pisipati, Abhishek Deshmukh, Scott Pappada, Abhishek Maan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) tends to be a common cardiac arrhythmia in hospitalized patients with acute illness. Although some general predictors are known, a systematic and integrated prediction schema that incorporates clinical predictors along with machine learning (ML) is lacking. In our study, we aimed to develop a multimodal artificial intelligence–based approach as a potential predictive tool for AF in hospitalized patients.ObjectiveIn our study, we sought to develop 3 different ML models for AF prediction in hospitalized patients.MethodsWe used the Symile-MIMIC dataset to develop 3 ML models: (1) clinical only, (2) electrocardiogram (ECG)-based, and (3) a multimodal fusion model.ResultsWe used a total of 13,817 ECGs from the Symile-MIMIC-IV dataset. These ECGs were then split into a training set (8923 ECGs, 65%), a validation set (675 ECGs, 5%), and an independent testing set that included a total of 4219 ECGs (30%). Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is commonly encountered in patients hospitalized with acute illness.1,2 Overall, there is some consensus on the general clinical factors, electrocardiogram (ECG), and metabolic imbalance-based factors that increase the risk of AF occurrence in hospitalized patients.2,3 Recent studies have investigated the role of machine learning (ML) algorithms based on sinus rhythm ECGs to predict the risk of developing subsequent AF. However, such an approach is limited as it does not incorporate the antecedent electrical changes and the predisposing clinical factors for AF.4,5 The clinical relevance of AF occurrence in hospitalized patients is multifold, which ranges from its role as an important factor that affects thromboembolic risk, mortality, and duration of hospitalization. It is also notable that, through these implications, the onset of AF during hospitalization has also been associated with a subsequent increase in healthcare expenses.6,7 Furthermore, there are also challenges with the use of oral anticoagulation in AF patients, as it is likely that there are competing factors (such as concomitant drug intake and increased risk of bleeding) in patients who are hospitalized with acute, critical illnesses.8 Considering the role of clinical and electrical risk factors which are relevant for AF occurrence and its prognostic relevance, it is intuitive to consider the role of a multimodal approach that incorporates these factors in an integrated ML model.

Therefore, in our study, we sought to develop a multimodal ML–based algorithm that incorporates data from clinical factors, electronic health records (EHRs), and 12-lead ECGs for prediction of AF in patients hospitalized with acute illness.

Methods

Dataset

We used the Symile-MIMIC dataset, which is a publicly available, open-source dataset that represents a subset of the larger MIMIC-IV clinical database.9,10 The research reported in our study adhered to the Helsinki Declaration guidelines. The requirement for individual patient consent was waived because, before data collection, all the protected health information had been removed and deidentified from the free-text notes and structured data in accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act guidelines. Access to the deidentified data was granted to the lead author (A.Y.), who had completed the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative Data or Specimens Only Research Training (completion ID: 70158336). This dataset has time-aligned clinical data, laboratory results, imaging (chest X-ray), and data on 12-lead ECGs, which were obtained within 24–72 hours of a patient’s hospitalization to the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. Considering that the patient-level information was anonymous, de-identified in this dataset, and the dataset is available in the public domain, approval from the institutional review board was not required for our study. The research reported in our study has adhered to the ethical guidelines as outlined in the Helsinki Declaration.

Study objective and patient population

The primary objective of this study was to predict the occurrence of AF within a 48-hour prediction window after a patient’s hospitalization. To address this, we excluded patients with a history of AF. This was done to ascertain that we only capture the events of AF that occurred during the 48-hour time-window of a patient’s admission.

Outcome definition

For the purpose of our study, we only selected the initial event of hospitalization and intensive care unit admission, and the subsequent hospitalizations were excluded. The initial ECG at the time of admission was carefully assessed for rhythm documentation, and if it indicated AF, then that patient was excluded from the cohort. Considering that the index ECG in the MIMIC dataset was obtained at varied time intervals, for the purpose of our analyses, we only used the ECG that had the earliest time stamp from a given hospitalization. The positive class (label = 1) was then established for patients who developed AF during the initial 48 hours of hospital admission. These outcome events were based on confirmation by serial documentation of cardiac rhythm in medical records. The remaining patients from the cohort of patients who presented in sinus rhythm at the time of admission and did not develop AF during the initial 48 hours of hospital admission were assigned to the negative class (label = 0). Duration of monitoring

For relevance of our ML model, and particularly considering the role of ECG obtained at the time of admission (which can be considered an ECG snapshot), the time window of 48 hours seemed to be most suitable. We also suspected that selection of a longer time window would diminish the relevance of an admission ECG to the subtle electrical changes preceding the occurrence of AF. Additionally, a longer window of hospitalization would have introduced the competing role of medications and procedures, which were challenging to account for the purpose of developing our ML algorithms. Furthermore, from a clinical standpoint, it is also likely that the majority of triaging and management decisions (such as rate/rhythm control and initiation of oral anticoagulation) tend to be made in the earlier phase of a patient’s admission. After considering these aspects, we selected a 48-hour time window for the purpose of developing our ML algorithm.11

Description of variables collected

The dataset had information on patient demographics, clinical information, laboratory variables (such as information from basic metabolic panel, cell count, arterial blood gas), and markers of acute changes (such as occurrence of sepsis). Data on coexisting medical diagnoses were collected using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes.

Data splitting

The study follows the predefined patient splits from the Symile-MIMIC dataset. This ensures strict separation of patients between training, testing, and validation sets, prevents any kind of leakage of data, and ensures reproducibility for future comparative studies. The cohort definition and labelling logic were applied independently to each data split. The entire dataset for our analyses comprised a total of 13,817 ECGs, which were then split into a training set (8923 ECGs, 65%), a validation set (675 ECGs, 5%), and an independent testing set that included a total of 4219 ECGs (30%).

Signal pre-processing

We processed 2 modalities of data: (1) pre-processed clinical data and (2) raw 12-lead ECG signals.

ECG signal pre-processing

We used the raw 12-lead ECG signals from the NumPy array output in Symile-MIMIC. For developing our ECG-based model, we only selected the sinus rhythm ECGs from the time of admission to the hospital in each patient. Each ECG recording consisted of a 10-second sample obtained at 500 Hz, which yielded a total of 5000 data points for every ECG recording. ECGs with missing values or poor signals were excluded, and each ECG signal was normalized to lie within the range of −1 to 1. The original shape of this data was then reshaped to a 12,500 tensor to satisfy the channel-first input requirement of PyTorch’s 1D convolutional layers. Considering that the baseline signals were of satisfactory quality for feature extraction for the deep-learning system, the use of filtering of signals (such as a notch or band-pass filter) was not deemed to be necessary.

ECG adjudication

From the entire sample, we randomly selected a total of 250 ECGs for manual adjudication of rhythm (with particular focus on differentiating sinus rhythm or AF) and verification of rhythm labels in the dataset. This verification was conducted independently by 2 co-authors (A.Y and A.M.; an internal medicine physician and a board-certified cardiac electrophysiologist). In case of a disagreement or discrepancy between the original labels and the manually adjudicated rhythm diagnosis, we also sought resolution from another independent board-certified cardiac electrophysiologist. Although we can only attest to this for the randomly selected ECGs, to maintain quality control in the process of the model’s development, we only included ECGs that clearly showed sinus rhythm.

Clinical feature engineering

We derived a full set of engineered structured clinical features from MIMIC-IV. In addition to the data on patient demographics and vital signs, we also engineered a few highly impactful composite features. These consisted of binary indicators from ICD-9/10 codes to indicate sepsis history, congestive heart failure, myocardial infarction (MI), and a cardiac risk score, which was derived as a weighted sum of cardiac comorbidities. To develop a well-tuned and complete feature set, we adopted a feature selection method. All engineered clinical features in the training data were then modeled via a Random Forest model and ranked by Gini importance, and we then selected the top 25 ranked features. The last step was to address variability in the amplitude and magnitude of these features. In order to do so, we then used sample-wise Z-score normalization (Standard Scaler), where each feature was normalized to have a mean of 0 and unit standard deviation. The scaler was fitted solely to the training set and applied without re-fitting to the validation and test sets to maintain data integrity. Missing data

We handled the missing values for our continuous clinical variables using the process of mean imputation (which corresponds to a value of 0.0 after Z-score standardization). Before the mean imputation, we calculated the exact percentages of missing values for each of the 25 clinical parameters. We observed that the highest missingness of any single clinical/laboratory parameter was 15.8% (for the neutrophil count). In contrast, the missingness of clinical and demographic features, such as age, gender, and cardiac risk score, was near 0%. For the categorical variables, the missing ICD codes were treated the same as absence of a given medical condition (value of 0). The summary of missing variables is presented in Supplemental Table 1.

Model architecture

We devised 3 distinct ML models: 2 unimodal (ECG-Only and Clinical-Only) and our final multimodal fusion model.

The ECG-only model and the ECG-processing component of the fusion model used a 1-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D-CNN) architecture. The network consisted of 3 successive convolution blocks that serve as feature extractors. Each block contains a 1D convolutional layer, followed by Batch Normalization, a ReLU activation function, and a Max-Pooling layer to reduce the temporal resolution. More filters are present with the depth of the network (32, 64, 128) in order to enable the model to learn more complex temporal features. The clinical-only model was employed as a random forest classifier, which is a robust tree-based ensemble method that has been considered appropriate for structured clinical records (tabular data).12 The model had 250 estimators and balanced class weights. This model was constructed using an ensemble of 250 decision trees to ensure robust and stable predictions. We also limited the complexity of these decision trees by restricting their depth, ensuring that the model learned broad, generalizable clinical patterns rather than simply assessing features based on memory. The clinical features were entered into the ML model in the form of a standard, structured numerical vector of 25 continuous and categorical variables (for example, age was entered as 65.0 instead of “age of sixty-five”). Before being entered into this ML model, these 25 numerical values underwent Z-score normalization (scaling to a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1) to ensure the model’s stability during neural network training. The Multimodal fusion model integrated and synthesized data from both the ECG encoder (the 1D-CNN model as described in the previous section) and the clinical encoder (a multi-layer perceptron). This model consists of 2 parallel, independent branches: the ECG encoder (the 1D-CNN, as described above) and a clinical encoder (a neural network that is designed for tabular data). Feature vectors from both encoders are concatenated and passed into a final dense classification fusion head. Training and evaluation

We trained the deep-learning models in our study using a Binary Cross-Entropy with Logits loss function. Similar to previously reported literature, we also encountered the issue of “class-imbalance,” considering that the occurrence of AF as compared to sinus rhythm would be relatively uncommon occurrence in a given arrhythmia dataset.13,14 To address the specific issue of “class-imbalance,” we used the following 2 complementary approaches. (1) Weighted random sampler: this approach relies upon having a sample of balanced representation of both classes (in our case, AF events and sinus rhythm) in every batch.14 This technique adjusts the frequency with which patient records are drawn during training, which allows it to encounter an equal number of records of AF and Sinus rhythm from every learning batch. This ensures that the algorithm receives sufficient training in identifying occurrences of relatively uncommon events (such as AF in our study dataset). (2) Loss-weighting approach: This is a complementary approach to the weighted sampling strategy, which is based on assigning a greater weight to the minority set of events.15,16 In our model’s case, the diagnosis of AF was assigned a greater weight than the events of sinus rhythm. Furthermore, this is a cost-sensitive learning mechanism that allows the model to evaluate its own set of errors. By assigning a significantly higher penalty for missing an AF case than for a false-positive detection of AF, our model’s optimization process prioritized detection of the minority set of events (AF) as its main objective.

The Fusion model was trained using a 2-stage transfer learning technique. For this purpose, the ECG encoder was pre-trained with weights of the pre-trained ECG-only model, and the training layers were frozen. The first phase of the model training involved only the new components (clinical encoder and fusion head). The clinical “encoder” extracted modality-specific features (in our case, only the clinical feature representation) in our ML model. In the second phase, the entire model was unfrozen, and end-to-end fine-tuning was performed with a decreased rate of learning for the ECG encoder to preserve the learned features but apply them in the multimodal environment. The model weights were then optimized further using the Adam optimizer at a learning rate of 1 × 10-4. Final testing was done using the model checkpoint that demonstrated the highest area under the receiver operating characteristiccurve (AUROC) in the validation set. This final model was then run on the held-out test set.

Model calibration

Considering that calibration is an important aspect of the development and training of any clinical ML model, particularly from the standpoint of its clinical utility. Therefore, we used the Brier score to evaluate the predictive accuracy. The Brier score represents the mean squared differences between the predicted probability by ML and the observed outcomes. Lower values would indicate better model performance.17,18

Performance metrics

We evaluated the performance of our ML model on the test set using standard classification metrics. The model’s ability to discriminate between the positive and negative classes (in our case, AF vs sinus rhythm) was assessed using the area under the curve (AUC). To assess performance at specific decision points and given the clinical context, we also calculated sensitivity (recall), precision (positive predictive value), and the F1-score, which is the harmonic mean of precision and sensitivity. The summary of these calculations is as follows.

Sensitivity: True positive/True positive + False negative

Precision = True Positive/ True Positive + False positive

F1-Score = 2 × (Precision × Sensitivity)/(Precision + Sensitivity)

The confusion matrices were analyzed at both a standard threshold of 0.5 and also at a high-confidence threshold of 0.98 to balance the trade-offs between recall and precision.

Model-explainability

To further understand the explainability of our s1D-CNN models (clinical and ECG), we used Grad-CAM and saliency mapping techniques. The Grad-CAM method generates class-specific maps of relatively important features by computing the gradient of the predicted class score with respect to the final CNN feature maps. This method also aids in identifying segments that might be relevant from a physiological standpoint for the prediction of AF.19 On saliency mapping, we assessed the relative sensitivity of the model’s output to each segment of the 12-lead ECG. For instance, it would help assess the magnitude of prediction change if the signal at a given time point is slightly lower or higher. This tool also helps to localize the influential ECG features and waveform patterns which might be relevant for AF.20 Although both methods aid in visualizing how the ML model uses the ECG signals, they address different aspects and therefore should be interpreted differently.

Results

Of the entire cohort of 13,817 patients in the MIMIC dataset, a total of 7.5% (1031) had the occurrence of AF during a given 48-hour window of their hospitalization event. In our study based on a publicly available dataset of hospitalized patients, the 1-dimensional, deep-learning-based fusion model demonstrated an effective level of discrimination for the primary goal of AF prediction. Overall, the fusion model achieved an AUROC of 0.81 for the prediction of AF within a timeframe of 48 hours. From a clinical perspective, we would consider this to be reasonably effective for the task of AF prediction.

In our clinical-only ML model, the patient’s age was the strongest predictive factor for the occurrence of AF based on the Shapley Additive exPlanations analysis (Supplemental Figure 1). The AUC of the clinical-only model was 0.71, and the ECG-based ML model achieved an AUC of 0.78, and the Fusion model achieved an AUC of 0.81. We then conducted a statistical comparison of the AUROCs on the independent test set using 1000-iteration bootstrap hypothesis testing. The multimodal fusion model was statistically superior to both the clinical-only model (AUC of 0.81 vs 0.71, P < .001) and the ECG-only model (AUC of 0.81 vs 0.78, P = .005). Similar results supporting the superior performance of the multimodal fusion model vs the other 2 models were again evident on comparison with the DeLong test. These findings indicate that a deep-learning fusion model, which combines data from clinical factors (records) and 12-lead ECGs, seems to outperform ML models based on individual factors alone. This is likely plausible because in a fusion-based model, the deep-learning framework can learn subtle features from waveform and imaging data, which enhances the model’s ability of AF prediction. The relative comparison of these AUCs of these 3 deep-learning models is summarized in Figure 5A. In addition to assessing the performance of deep-learning models with AUC, we also evaluated the performance of these 3 models with several threshold-dependent metrics. This was done to assess further relevance from a clinical standpoint. Similar to the findings of assessment with AUC, the fusion model outperformed the clinical-only and the ECG-only models based on the thresholds that we used. At the default threshold of 0.50 (chosen for optimal recall), the fusion model achieved a relatively high degree of sensitivity of 83.5%. In a clinical context, it would entail detection of over 80% of the episodes of AF that occur during a patient’s hospitalization. However, the trade-off with such an approach was its low degree of precision of 10.3% and a specificity of 54.7%. A lower degree of precision would increase the rate of false-positive detection of AF events as the outcome being investigated.

Model calibration findings

In regard to the model calibration of the 3 ML models, we acknowledge that in our study, the calibration process for unidimensional deep-learning models (such as our 1-D CNN ECG model) was relatively challenging. Based on our assessment with the Brier score, the ECG model scored 0.29. On the contrary, the deep-learning models that were tree-based tend to be better calibrated, and this was evident with the random forest–based clinical-only ML model (Brier score of 0.087). Therefore, our multimodal fusion model was able to balance the ECG-only model with the integration of the clinical-only ML model. The calibration plot of our 3 ML models along with their respective Brier scores is summarized in the Supplemental Figure 2.

Model explainability findings

We used Grad-CAM and saliency mapping to understand the “black-box” features of our ML model. Based on the Grad-CAM figure (Figure 7A), the darker red areas represent the segments of the 12-lead ECG that had a stronger influence on the estimated risk of occurrence of AF, and conversely, the lighter red areas had less influence on risk prediction. On the saliency map (Figure 7B), the points that are highlighted in dark seem to coincide with the sharp changes in the signals of the 12-lead ECG. Discussion

The salient findings of our study are summarized as follows. (1) We developed deep-learning models for prediction of AF in 48 hours of a patient’s hospitalization. (2) Our deep-learning models based on clinical factors only and 12-lead ECGs only achieved a reasonable degree of accuracy and precision for the prediction of AF. (3) In comparison, our multimodal fusion model, which was based on a combination of clinical factors as well as 12-lead ECGs, achieved a higher degree of precision for the prediction of new-onset AF. (4) In our clinical-only model, the patient’s age was the strongest predictive factor for the occurrence of AF based on Shapley Additive exPlanations analysis.

The coexisting diagnosis of AF continues to be an important clinical issue in patients who are hospitalized with acute illnesses. Based on previously published data, it has been observed that the occurrence of AF in hospitalized patients is associated with an increased length of stay, resource utilization, and heightened risk of adverse outcomes such as mortality.6,7 From a practical perspective, scores such as the Hypertension, Age (greater than 75 years), Transient ischemic attack or stroke, Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and Heart failure (HATCH) (elevated score > 3) have been described for AF prediction in patients admitted to the hospital with ST-segment elevation MI. However, despite the relative ease of application of clinical scores such as Hypertension, Age (greater than 75 years), Transient ischemic attack or stroke, Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and Heart failure, its predictive accuracy, as assessed with ROC, remains approximately 0.73.21 Similarly, Wang et al22 had investigated the role of platelet-to-albumin ratio in combination with C2HEST score for the purpose of prediction of AF in older patients admitted with ST-elevation MI. Although the patient cohort in this study had included relatively high-risk patients for developing AF (such as older, > 60 years of age), this score only achieved an AUC of 0.84. Both these studies reflect important limitations of such approaches based on clinical data alone. In addition, at least some of the clinical factors (such as electrolyte imbalance, renal injury and heart failure exacerbation) tend to be variable rather than static. Therefore, predictive schemas (both clinical as well as ML-based) which are primarily based on clinical factors might have important limitations.

Another limitation of these studies is that clinical risk scores tend to rely upon manual calculation, availability of data points, and physician adjudication. Each of these aspects pose their own challenges, such as the process of manual calculation, which might be labor-intensive, and some of these variables might not be readily available in a given patient dataset. Additionally, adjudication by physician/healthcare professionals is also subject to inter-observer variability. It is also challenging to embed these risk-prediction schemes in an automated manner in EHRs. All these limitations make it challenging to accomplish the goal of making an upfront prediction of subsequent AF at the time of acute patient hospitalization. From a biological perspective, these risk-prediction factors might not completely capture the potential pathways implicated in the pathophysiology of AF in patients admitted with acute illnesses.

In our study, a deep-learning model that learns from structured clinical data was able to achieve a reasonable predictive ability for the diagnosis of AF. In a prospective study by Weng et al,22 the investigators used routine clinical data from over 350,000 patients from family practice encounters in the United Kingdom to predict their first cardiovascular event (including the diagnosis of AF) over a follow-up period of 10-years. In comparison to the established risk prediction algorithm (such as the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association algorithm), various ML algorithms derived from structured clinical data performed better at predicting the risk of AF (reported AUC values of 0.73–0.76).23 Similarly, Kao et al24 had also developed a similar deep-learning model based on the random forest algorithm using a combination of medical records, medications, and laboratory data for predicting AF diagnosis within 1 year. The investigators had reported a high degree of specificity (98.7%) and a reasonably good AUROC of 0.74.24

Previously published studies have also widely investigated the role of 12-lead ECGs of sinus rhythm for the purpose of AF prediction. Jabbour et al25 had developed an ECG-artificial intelligence model from over 650,000 ECGs and reported an AUC of 0.78 for prediction of AF, and in a separate subgroup analysis, these investigators also reported that the ECG-artificial intelligence model outperformed both the Cohorts for Heart and Aging Research in Genomic Epidemiology - Atrial Fibrillation score and a polygenic score (AUC values of 0.62 and 0.59, respectively). In another study, Raghunath et al26 trained a deep-learning model on 1.6 million sinus rhythm ECGs from over 430,000 patients with no history of AF to predict AF occurrence over the course of 1 year. Regarding the performance metrics, the investigators reported an AUROC of 0.85 and an area under the precision-recall curve of 0.22.26 The investigators also performed a simulation of their deep learning model’s deployment and then reported a sensitivity of 69% and a specificity of 81% for AF prediction at a 1-year follow-up period.

Notably, the maladaptive physiological changes that occur in hospitalized patients with acute illness cannot be reliably captured by clinical factors alone. For instance, clinical factors, such as hypertension and diabetes, reflect chronic conditions, rather than acute processes in a hospitalized setting. Additionally, these factors also do not account for the electrical changes that can be assessed on 12-lead ECGs. Another study by Raghunath et al27 supports this assertion; in this study, an ECG-based ML model demonstrated a higher efficiency (as assessed by the number needed to screen) when compared to the clinical features-based model for screening patients at high risk of AF and AF-related stroke. Therefore, it is intuitive to incorporate clinical factors as well as ECGs in a fusion ML model. Considering these factors, we believe that the superior performance of our fusion model for AF prediction is not entirely surprising.

From the comparative performance of our unidimensional and fusion models, it is also reasonable to suggest that a multimodal approach that encompasses both clinical and electrical factors might be more appropriate for AF prediction. Methodologically, the multimodal approach also balances the calibration between the clinical-only model and that of the ECG-only model. In addition, given the proximity of timeline for the occurrence of AF (such as within 48 hours in our study), it is important to incorporate data from ECG to account for the electrical changes that might precede the occurrence of AF. We believe that an ML-based risk-prediction approach could be used to complement standard risk-prediction schemas. In addition, an ML approach could also help assess the role of latent variables which might not be otherwise included in the existing prediction tools.28

Study limitations

Our study is based on a publicly available dataset rather than prospective data such as in a randomized controlled trial. Therefore, we acknowledge that our study also has the inherent limitations of a retrospective study. Our clinical model is also a relatively static model, which does not account for the dynamic biological changes that would be relevant to consider, particularly in the context of acute illness. As part of the ECG assessment of labels from the dataset, we randomly selected a sample of 250 ECGs to verify the original labels with our assessment of rhythm from these ECGs. Although we sought consultation from another independent cardiac electrophysiologist in case of discrepancy in labeling and our assessment, we acknowledge that this is an important limitation of our study. This could have introduced errors as well as inter-observer variability. We would emphasize that the presence of sinus rhythm on admission ECG does not rule out a history of AF, and the use of serial ECGs is not sufficient to classify detection of AF as a new-onset AF; also, these are not adequate to assess whether AF did or did not occur during a given hospitalization period. Therefore, we acknowledge that at best our ML algorithm should be interpreted for AF prediction. Additionally, we would also add that a 48-hour time window might be insufficient to assess the occurrence of AF in hospitalized patients, and ideally a longer time window, while balancing the confounding effect of acute changes that occur during subsequent hospitalization, would have been ideal. We suspect that the missed cases of AF likely reflect the limitations of our deep-learning model, which might not capture some of the subtle physiological patterns from the data. It is also plausible that such patterns might not even be evident on the 10-second ECG tracings. We would also add that the findings of Grad-CAM and saliency mapping should be interpreted as explainability aids rather than definitive causal links.

Conclusion

Our clinical, ECG, and multimodal fusion deep-learning models developed using a publicly available dataset of patients hospitalized with acute illnesses demonstrated a reasonable predictive ability for AF occurrence. Expectedly, a fusion/multimodal ML approach combining data from clinical information and 12-lead ECGs performed better than individual ML models. A prospective validation study aimed at assessing our ML models in real-world settings would be helpful to further strengthen our findings.
