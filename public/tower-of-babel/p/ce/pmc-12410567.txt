Near Field Communication (NFC) -assisted prehospital extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation activation: a novel tool to reduce cognitive load on emergency medical technicians
Authors: Yan-Bo Huang, Sun-Hsin Liao, Ming-Ta Hsieh, Shang-Chiao Yang, Chien-Hsiung Huang
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (E-CPR) has emerged as a promising intervention for out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), with high-quality studies demonstrating improved neurologic outcomes and survival in select patient populations.1, 2 In Taiwan, some fire departments have collaborated with medical centers on prehospital E-CPR activation protocols. However, E-CPR is a resource-intensive therapy, and each hospital’s unique inclusion criteria and dedicated hotlines create a significant cognitive load for frontline emergency medical technicians (EMTs). They report difficulty memorizing complex protocols and dialing the correct number in time-critical situations, leading to suboptimal activation rates and delays that miss the crucial therapeutic window.

To address these barriers, paramedic Sun-Hsin Liao and his colleague Ming-Ta Hsieh from the Taoyuan City Fire Department developed a novel cognitive aid using Near Field Communication (NFC) technology. Following initial resuscitation efforts, the EMT can tap a small NFC tag on the emergency equipment with a smartphone to instantly launch a web interface (Fig. 1). This interface serves as a dynamic decision support tool, presenting a checklist that allows the EMT to rapidly verify key inclusion criteria (e.g., witnessed arrest, initial shockable rhythm, age) and confirm the absence of absolute contraindications for various receiving hospitals. Once the criteria for a specific hospital are met and confirmed on the checklist, a single button press immediately dials that facility's dedicated E-CPR hotline, eliminating lookup or dialing errors. The system’s back-end automatically logs these interactions, providing granular data for quality assurance.

NFC-assisted prehospital E-CPR activation.

The choice of NFC is deliberate. Compared to QR codes, which require unlocking the phone, opening a camera app, and focusing, NFC’s “tap-and-go” functionality involves fewer steps and has a lower failure rate in chaotic prehospital environments. Furthermore, this solution leverages existing EMT smartphones and inexpensive NFC tags, obviating the need for a costly dedicated application and its associated maintenance.

The feasibility of this system was recently demonstrated in an OHCA case managed by two EMTs without on-site paramedic support. After identifying the patient’s potential eligibility, one EMT used a smartphone to activate the tag; the entire process from tapping the tag to placing the call took only 11 s. This experience shows that this tool helps providers execute rapid and accurate activation protocols under high stress. Beyond E-CPR, this NFC-assisted model is highly scalable. Its technical architecture can be easily replicated for other time-sensitive prehospital pathways, such as those for acute stroke or major trauma, creating a versatile prehospital activation platform. By lowering cognitive load, this low-cost solution has the potential to improve patient access to a range of critical, time-sensitive interventions.
