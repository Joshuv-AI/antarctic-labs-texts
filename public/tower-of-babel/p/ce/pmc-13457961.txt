Propagating slow-mode shocks discovered in dynamical solar flare loops
Authors: Jing Ye, Chengcai Shen, John C Raymond, Qiangwei Cai, Zhixing Mei, Jialiang Hu, Yuhao Chen, Yan Li, Jun Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSolar flares are the largest energy releasing events in the solar system, where the open magnetic field lines reconnect and form the closed flare loops. During this process, rapid magnetic reconnection, the associated shock waves, and chromospheric evaporation are expected but not yet well understood. These processes are crucial for understanding similar features in stellar flares and other astrophysical jets. Here, we report the characteristics of propagating slow-mode shocks in the flare loop system, by combining a 3D high-resolution magnetohydrodynamics modeling and spectral analysis of Extreme Ultra-Violet observations. It is found that normal slow shocks are recurrently formed after the collision between the post-reconnection downflows and evaporation flows in the flare loops, which subsequently propagate toward the chromosphere at speeds comparable to the evaporation flows. In particular, the Doppler analysis of the Fe XXI 1354 Å line normally shows a sharp change in blueshifted velocity and an asymmetrical line broadening once the line-of-sight passes through the shock front. This study highlights that propagation of slow shocks can facilitate energy release in flares and affect energy transport, suggesting an advancement in the standard flare model framework.Subject terms: Solar physics, Astrophysical plasmas, Computational astrophysics

Introduction

The most recognized characteristic of an erupted solar flare is the impressive bright flare loop system. These loops can be observed as the hot soft X-ray loops that rise above the relatively cooler loops viewed in other wavebands, presenting the most dynamic phenomenon in the solar atmosphere1,2. It is now commonly believed that flare loops are formed due to magnetic reconnection in the solar corona, where the anti-parallel magnetic field lines can be thought to close to each other, and then break and reconnect, forming the new cusp-shaped loops rooted on the solar surface, dramatically releasing the energy stored in the magnetic field. As the newly reconnected magnetic flux accumulates and the reconnection sites gradually rise, the previous cusp loops tend to relax toward a potential state, representing a more rounded shape. This process is commonly referred to as field line shrinkage. In the standard flare model3–6, the energy releasing during the magnetic reconnection is transported in the flare loop system by a series of dynamic physics processes, including thermal conduction, energetic particles, plasma flows, and radiative cooling7, resulting in the multi-waveband flare emission features, including hard X-ray, soft X-ray, and extreme ultraviolet (EUV) on observable images and spectrum. Thus, these dynamic flare loops are the most important observational indicator of magnetic energy release. In fact, the dynamic behavior and plasma properties inside flare loops have been a central research area in solar physics for a number of years, which is the key to understanding the energy transport process affecting the solar atmosphere and corona during the solar eruption.

In theory, the shrinkage of one individual magnetic loop has been well analyzed in early analytical models8. Researchers explained that the degree of shrinkage significantly depends on the geometry of the magnetic configuration, such as X-type or Y-type reconnection sites, above the cusp loops9. In observations, flare loop shrinkage is indirectly demonstrated by the X-ray observations10–12, suggesting that shrinkage provides substantial additional energy release for heating the loops of plasma13,14. However, various magnetic reconnection mechanisms, including turbulence, plasmoid instabilities, and Petschek-type shocks, may coexist in large-scale reconnecting current sheets in three-dimensional (3D) environments. These highly dynamic processes significantly impact the accumulation of the reconnected magnetic flux above the flare loops, prompting a reevaluation of our understanding of how shrinking flare loops evolve. In previous studies, one-dimensional radiative hydrodynamic (RHD) models15–17 suggest that nonthermal electron beams can contribute to evaporation flows in flare loops. And the thermal evolution and shock structures in flare loops have been explored via the magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) simulations, including thermal conduction18–22. Slow-mode shocks are found in Petschek-type reconnection simulations, and chromospheric evaporation due to thermal conduction is examined23. The evaporation features predicted in both MHD and RHD models are consistent with the observed emission lines. In particular, the collision between evaporation flow and post-reconnection flow was previously reported in 2D MHD simulations18,24, which leads to the high density hump formed on the legs of the flare loops19 has revealed a pair of slow shocks associated with the high density hump using higher spatial resolution simulations. Similar normal slow shocks have been reported in the earlier MHD simulations25–28, that the shocks are formed by the plasmoids traveling at Alfvén or sub-Alfvén speed in a low-beta reconnection current sheet. And hybrid particle-in-cell simulations29 further explored the slow shock’s formation in the upstream regions of the reconnecting current sheet and measured their contribution to electron heating. However, the 3D modeling of these shock structures in flare simulations remains lacking. Several attempts30–32 have been made to model solar flares in 3D based on the standard flare model. These simulations mainly focus on the instabilities occurring above the post-flare loops during the later phases in solar flares when the chromospheric evaporation declines greatly. In this work, we present the formation and propagation of slow-mode shocks due to the downflow-evaporation collision in a more comprehensive solar flare model, and successfully identify the existence of these normal slow shocks in spectrum observations.

Results

Compared to previous simulations30–32, our model starts with the global coronal mass ejection (CME) geometry in a gravitationally stratified atmosphere (“Methods”). This model offers a comprehensive approach by integrating the classic flare model10 with the commonly accepted CME eruption theory33, which therefore provides a complete description of CME/flare eruptions. During the eruption of the CME, which is represented by a preexisting magnetic flux rope, magnetic reconnection takes place within a self-consistently formed current sheet, as shown in Fig. 1a and Supplementary Movie 1. First, our model successfully exhibits a series of fundamental components of CMEs: (1) twisted flux rope and post-arcade formation; (2) larger-scale magnetic reconnection regions between two legs of the CME; (3) bidirectional reconnection outflows. The eruption scenario revealed in our model agrees with the classic analytical model33 and numerical simulations and observational analysis34,35 for typical solar eruptions. More importantly, our model resolved the details of the magnetic reconnection current sheet and the associated flare loop system in a global CME simulation with high resolution up to 156 km: (1) turbulent reconnection current sheet in which the fast reconnection process occurs, driving and heating reconnection outflows (also see the Kolmogorov-like power law spectrum of these turbulent structures in the Supplementary Fig. 1 and Movie 2); (2) supermagnetosonic reconnection downflows and associated termination shock (TS) above the flare loop-top, which has been suggested to be the dominant area of energetic particle acceleration21,36,37; (3) turbulent interface region below the TS, where the finger-like structures, known as supra-arcade downflows (SADs), are clearly visible (Fig. 2a), plays a crucial role in flare energy release and particle acceleration30,38. Therefore, by incorporating the crucial local features in the flare loop system into the disrupting magnetic configuration that creates CME and flare, we constructed a model that is able to account for both the global evolution in the magnetic structure of CME and the local sophisticated dynamic features of plasma inside flare loops. With a close look at the arcade in Fig. 1b, c, one can clearly see the distinct hot and dense loop-like structures, mapping to the shrinking field lines and associated hot plasmas.

The flare loop shrinkage can be clearly demonstrated by tracing magnetic field lines anchored to the solar surface originating from a selected point (Fig. 2a). As this marked magnetic field line broke and reconnected with another one due to the magnetic reconnection, a closed loop formed and gradually moved downward in a manner that is consistent with the field line shrinkage. A 2D projection of this special line on the central xz-plane is shown in Fig. 3 to better visualize the loop dynamics before and after reconnection. Along the field line, the time-distance maps are produced to follow the loop evolution in Fig. 2b–e. Remarkably, we have identified two new features: (1) quasi-periodic manifestation of evaporation flows while the loop shrinks, and (2) propagating slow shocks formed associated with these evaporation flows.

The former, for a single loop, shows the recurrent evaporation flows with a period of 0.3 t0 (about 116.7 s), leading to the subsequent increase in plasma density and temperature. This period is consistent with the quasi-periodic pulsations (QPPs), generally detected from the hot solar flare loops, such as EUV and soft X-ray passbands39. One common explanation is the kink-mode for the shrinking loop. Taking this period and the loop length in our case, the interpretation of QPPs suggests a loop Aflvén speed of about 305 km s−1, which is outside the reasonable range for coronal loops40. It implies that the energy release due to the periodic evaporation during episodic magnetic reconnection could be one promising source of QPPs, rather than the kink mode.

The latter, propagating slow shocks, realizes the strong interaction among various plasma flows in the flare loop system. The supermagnetosonic reconnection flows form Petschek slow shocks in the reconnection outflow regions, which serve as efficient sites for plasma heating. The thermal energy is then transported into the chromosphere and heats the chromosphere. The heated plasma expands upward rapidly at speeds up to 200 km s−1, invoking the explosive evaporation of the chromosphere15,41, displayed as the blue region in Fig. 2d. On the other hand, the behavior of plasma flows at the flare loop system is manifestly dominated by magnetic reconnection and the associated reconnected magnetic field, which can be illustrated by examining the magnetic field line shrinkage process. The shrinkage of the reconnected field takes place at a supermagnetosonic speed described by the change in the apex height versus time and experiences significant deceleration at time = 0.94 t0 (Supplementary Fig. 2). The density and temperature are greatly enhanced due to the compression (with negative ∇⋅V) above the loop-top region (Fig. 2b, c, e), indicating that the kinetic energy is converted into thermal energy. Then, the hot and dense plasma in the loop top region expands along the magnetic field to form the post-reconnection downflows, indicated by the red region in Fig. 2d. As the evaporation flows collide with the downflows, a hump structure forms, as indicated by an enhancement of density shown in the legs of the flare loop system (Figs. 2b and 3). The fronts of downflows become steep and eventually form the normal shocks, indicated by the strong negative ∇⋅V as shown in Fig. 2e. By examining the shock jump condition of primary physical variables across these shock fronts, we can identify these shocks as slow shocks (“Methods,” Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). In particular, the slow shocks are found to propagate toward the footpoints at a high speed of approximately 100–300 km s−1, which is comparable to the typical evaporation flow speed. While propagating, the shocks are damped by conduction and show no reflection by the chromosphere42. Associated with the shrinkage of every single reconnected loop, plasma flows together with the slow shocks in the flare loop system manifest apparent 3D behaviors. Previous studies have indicated interactions between reconnection downflows and evaporation, as well as the associated slow-mode shocks31. In this work, a 3D CME/flare model resolves the formation of the normal slow shocks within flare loops in a self-consistent fashion and further reveals the downward motion of the slow shock in flare loops.

If a strong slow shock presents in the evaporation flows, there will be a noticeable change in the density and velocity of the medium upstream and downstream of the shock, potentially affecting the emission characteristics of the high-temperature spectral lines, such as Fe XXI observed by the Interface Region Imaging Spectrograph (IRIS)43–46. Benefitting from the thermal dynamics treatment employed in our simulation, we are able to reconstruct precisely the emission profiles of the Fe XXI 1354.08 Å line and EUV images (“Methods”), allowing for a direct comparison with observations. Here, the simulation data cube intended for synthetic images is extracted from the domain, spanning the ranges (x, y, z) ∈ [−0.2, 0.2] × [−0.4, 0.4] × [0.4, 1.2]. We generate the synthetic emissions from the ideal top-down view (Fig. 4a–d), as well as from the oblique viewing angle for comparison with an observational event (Fig. 4e–i). In Fig. 4, the peak intensity of the Fe XXI 1354.08 Å line is obtained from the single-Gaussian fit to each line profile, and individual loops can be clearly seen in this Fe XXI map. Using the synthetic Fe XXI emission, we obtained the Doppler velocity map of the flare loops (Fig. 4c) and found that strong blueshift features appear near the two edges of the flare ribbons due to chromospheric evaporation. In contrast, slight redshift signals are observed around the loop-top region, indicating a decrease of loop heights as a result of the loop shrinkage. For the top-down viewing case, we measured the Fe XXI 1354.08 Å line broadening width using the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the line profiles (“Methods”), and found that the large FWHM value appears mainly at the flanks of the loops. We further confirm that the large FWHM values found at the flanks of the loops are associated with the normal slow shocks. The synthetic IRIS slits, marked in white in Fig. 4a, are selected to cross two regions: one with slow shocks (left dashed line at y = 0) and one without (right dashed line at y = 0.14). Figure 5 shows the cross-section snapshots for the above two IRIS slits. In the case with slow shocks (Fig. 5a), the high-density hump and the discontinuity are present in the leg region of the flare loop, resulting in a shock front that is associated with the large FWHM shown at sampling points P2 and P3 in Fig. 4d. In contrast, for the case in Fig. 5b, there is no normal slow shock formed where the density and velocity are smoothly distributed, and the FWHMs are relatively smaller at the corresponding points P5 and P6 in Fig. 4d. Thus, the significant line broadening on the leg is likely attributed to slow shocks formed from the downflow-evaporation collision.

Specifically, we investigate the Fe XXI 1354.08 Å spectral line profiles at the selected sampling points marked in Fig. 4. Two points, P2 and P3, are chosen according to the slow shock’s appearance position (Fig. 5a), ensuring both P2 and P3 are across the shock front along the line of sight. In Fig. 7, the corresponding Fe XXI 1354 Å line profiles of P2 and P3 show a clear asymmetric feature with significant line broadening, where two well-separated blueshifted components are found for each point. For P3, the first component results from the bulk evaporation flow of v1 = − 116.1 km s−1, while the combination of the post-shocked flows (density hump) and background emissions accounts for a slight blueshift of v2 = − 15.8 km s−1 as the second component. Indeed, the ratio of the peak of the second component to that of the first component is measured to be about 1.5. And the compression ratio of the density across the shock is 1.23 at this time. Since the slow shock is nearly isothermal, the ratio of downstream to upstream intensity is calculated to be approximately 1.51, according to Eq. 9. This confirms that the double-Gaussian line profile at P3 is primarily due to the change in plasma conditions ahead of and behind the shock. For the inner sample P2, the dominant velocity drops toward zero without a change of sign. The transition of the line profiles from P3 to P2 indicates that the dominant component cannot be considered stationary. While tending to the loop-top region (P1), the emission without the contribution of the above-mentioned slow shocks exhibits a single-peak feature with a slight redshift due to the shrinkage of the loops. On the other hand, the points P4, P5, and P6 are sampled on the slit where no slow shocks are present (Fig. 5b). The spectral line at the central sample (P4) fits well the single-Gaussian distribution as expected, while the spectral lines on the legs (P5 and P6) also show broad single Gaussian-type profiles, indicating that superposition of evaporation flows alone can result in somewhat line broadening without change of the profile symmetry.

The above asymmetric spectral profiles associated with the slow shocks can still be observed when considering projection effects, by viewing the flare loop system from different directions. We therefore examine the spectral lines at a set of sampled points located on the legs of flare loops, for both synthetic (Q1, Q2, and Q3) and observational images (S1, S2, and S3), also marked in Fig. 4. It is noted that Q2, Q3 are associated with the normal shocks, similar to the previous samples P2, P3. The observed emission features of sampled lines agree well with the synthetic ones in both Doppler velocity and width. Indeed, for points Q1, S1 close to the loop-top, the Fe XXI lines reveal the single-Gaussian feature with a slight redshift. And for points Q3, S3, the line profiles exhibit a clearly asymmetric feature with two-peak features with distinguished blueshifted components. And the ratio of the two components v1/v2 becomes larger when approaching the adjacent points Q2, S2. Although turbulence and superposition of evaporation flows may lead to broader spectral lines52, they are unlikely to account for the asymmetry in spectral line profiles and the sharp transitional features, as described by samples Q3(S3) to Q2(S2). The opacity and pressure broadening or the Stark effect are ruled out as negligible for the high-temperature Fe XXI line broadening as well53. Owing to the aligned appearing position and similar spectral profile features, we conclude that the normal slow shocks can be detected in evaporation flows through the Fe XXI line profiles in this flare event.

Our results enrich the understanding of the flare loop formation and evolution by incorporating the effects of evaporation18,54,55. Specifically, the evolution of the flare loop system is a natural result of the global CME and flare eruption with the self-consistent magnetic reconnection. Figure 8 illustrates the general organization of magnetic fields after the eruption, including the reconnection region, the flux rope and the flare arcade. The Alfvénic outflow forms the TS on the top of the flare loops, and the finger-like structures underneath the TS are found as the indirect products of the reconnection outflows due to various MHD instabilities (e.g., Richtmyer–Meshkov instability/Rayleigh–Taylor instability). Both downward and upward flows occur in the outer loops simultaneously, but the downward flow is more tenuous than the evaporation flow (Fig. 3). Most notably, propagating slow shocks can recurrently form after the downward post-reconnection flow collides with the evaporation flow, which has not been predicted in the standard flare model. As shown in Fig. 8, the left half of the flare loops depicts plasma motion for the situation without the slow shock, while the right half indicates more complicated dynamics in the presence of the propagating slow shock, as discussed in Fig. 5.

In conclusion, the plasma dynamics, including the evaporation and the downward post-reconnection flows on individual loops, have been well resolved in a completed CME/flare model. In the standard flare model, only faint, field-aligned slow shocks are present below the TS, resulting in a relatively small energy release underneath. But our simulation and observation together strongly support the existence of the propagating slow shocks due to the downflow-evaporation collision, which contributes to a more efficient energy dissipation. Also, our simulation indicates that explosive evaporation flow plays a critical role in the formation of slow shocks, which are coupled to the naturally developed magnetic reconnection in the current sheet regions below the CME. Notably, the normal slow shocks can significantly impact energy transport in the flare loop system by modulating the motion of heated plasma and potentially changing the spatial distribution of energetic particles56. Thus, these motions might contribute to other observable signals in the leg region of flare loops, such as microwave sources57, which require further examination by combining fine modeling and observational analysis.

Our model reproduces the intricate dynamics of magnetic loops with different shrinkage velocities, along with the quasi-periodic manifestation of evaporation flows and the resulting slow shocks. The plasmoid instability in the reconnection outflow region may complicate the loop dynamics22, but the slow shock formation scenario remains the same. Additionally, we proposed an effective method to identify the slow shocks in the flare loops from the spectrum data. Despite the project effects, these normal, slow shocks can be more easily detected by the Fe XXI 1354.08 Å line in the early phases of a flare. Finally, our findings hold insights into flare dynamics, particularly concerning the shrinkage of loops, which has the potential to improve the standard flare model.

Beyond solar flare research, our model provides a valuable approach to emission diagnostics for stellar flares. The geometry of stellar flares still cannot be spatially resolved due to the current observational instrument limitations. Thus, understanding the observed light intensity and spectral features of superflares on stars58 mainly relies on loop modeling derived from solar flare theories. In those studies, simplified loop models—such as single semicircular loop or multi-thread systems-have been widely employed for analyzing stellar flares59, considering the simplified energy release due to magnetic reconnection. Upward and downward motions of hot plasmas in stellar flare loops have been observed in time-resolved X-ray spectroscopic observations60,61. However, our results demonstrate that the shrinking loops and plasma motion within them are strongly affected by the magnetic reconnection occurring above the loop system. The slow shocks revealed in the flare loop system can reasonably be expected to appear in superflares on stars, where the magnetic energy released is several orders higher than in the solar atmosphere. Therefore, the interpretation of emission features (e.g., X-ray and (E)UV) of superflares might be significantly different from these based on the simple loop hypothesis and requires more careful reevaluation. Furthermore, the periodic behavior of plasma flows revealed in our model offers potential value for interpreting the nature of QPP observations in various superflares, ranging from a few seconds to hundreds of minutes.

Methods

Observational data

The solar flare event studied in this work is an M-class flare occurring on June 22, 2015, which has been captured by SDO/AIA and IRIS. To diagnose the plasma from the chromosphere to the corona covering temperatures of 6 × 104–2 × 107 K, SDO/AIA observes the sun in 2 ultraviolet (UV) (1600 and 1700 Å) and 7 EUV (94, 131, 171, 193, 211, 304, and 335 Å) bands. The spatial resolution is 0.6″ pixel−1, with time cadences of 24 s for UV and 12 s for EUV. IRIS is sensitive to emission from plasma at temperatures between 5000 K and 10 MK, focusing on phenomena occurring in the chromosphere and transition region, including some coverage in the corona. During the evolution of the flare, the IRIS/SJI observed the corresponding region with a spatial pixel of 0.33″ and a cadence of 17 s. The IRIS spectrometer slit scanned the region with a step cadence of 2.1 s, the step size of 1″, the exposure time of 1 s, and the spectral resolution of 26 mA in the far ultraviolet bandpass in a 16-step scan mode.

In this work, we focus on the SDO/AIA 131 Å band that is mainly sensitive to high-temperature plasma emission, such as Fe XX and Fe XXIII, which are commonly observed in reconnection current sheet regions and flare loops. Specifically, the hot post-flare loops and dynamic plasma flows, such as SADs in the above the loop-top regions can be well identified from the AIA 131 Å band images. Meanwhile, the IRIS/SJI 1400 Å channel probes plasma at a temperature of about 65,000 K, and its observational data can reveal flare ribbon structures distributed across the upper chromosphere and lower transition region. As for the Fe XXI spectral line at 1354.08 Å from IRIS, its formation temperature reaches approximately 11 MK. Supplementary Fig. 5 presents a full-disk snapshot from the SDO/AIA 131 Å, where the target flare event originates from Active Region 12371. All AIA image datasets were processed and calibrated following the standard pipelines provided by Solar Software62 (SSW). For IRIS observations, we utilized level 2 data products, which have undergone pre-corrections for dark current noise, flat-field bias, and geometric distortion artifacts in advance. Prior to carrying out detailed quantitative analysis, all image frames acquired by different space telescopes must be spatially registered. We adopted the cross-correlation algorithm to achieve co-alignment between SDO and IRIS datasets, taking AIA 1600 Å and SJI 1400 Å images as matching references. Fiducial markers embedded within SJI imaging and spectral data were further utilized to fine-tune the alignment of IRIS measurements. Additionally, absolute wavelength calibration is a prerequisite for deriving precise Doppler velocity maps of the Fe XXI line. Here, the S I 1401.5136 Å line serves as the calibration reference, given that it can be distinctly resolved from adjacent spectral features without overlap interference 63,64.

During solar flares, the Fe XXI line can be blended with numerous cooler, photospheric, or chromospheric lines (e.g., C I, Si II, and Fe II), complicating its detection and analysis. If the Fe XXI line is severely blended with stronger emissions, a preprocessing procedure must be applied to subtract the associated contaminating features. In the circumstances, we give an example to show how to clean the Fe XXI lines in Supplementary Fig. 7. The original line profiles are selected for points S2 and S3 from Fig. 7. The spectrum at S3 includes three strong emission lines at the characteristic wavelengths of C I, Si II, and Fe II. We first employed multi-Gaussian fitting to identify these blended components. The de-blended Fe XXI profile was then obtained by subtracting the fitted contributions of these components from the original spectrum. For S2, only the C I emission was subtracted. This approach significantly improved the accuracy of our spectral analysis.

The initial magnetic field consists of three components. The first one comes from a magnetic flux rope (MFR) with a minor radius a, a major radius R, carrying a uniformly distributed ring current I. And the flux rope is full of cool and dense plasma from the chromosphere within the radius a for an initial temperature T = 0.02. The second one is the background field created by a pair of magnetic sources ±q on the axis of symmetry of MFR, separated by the distance L at depth −d under the photosphere (z = 0). The third one represents a dipole source q0 buried at depth −d0 below the origin. The external equilibrium is achieved through the interaction between the MFR and the background field caused by magnetic resources. The internal equilibrium corresponds to the equality between the gas pressure and the magnetic pressure due to the toroidal field inside the MFR. The critical parameters in our model are set to be L = 0.4, R = 1.2, a = 0.2, d = 0.1, d0 = 1.2, q = 40.75, q0 = 2.92, and I = 7.37 (for more details, see ref. 22). We start the simulation from the non-equilibrium state by reducing the charge of magnetic resources by q1 = 0.7 q to have a successful eruption.

In order to capture highly dynamic flows and shock structures, we employed high-accuracy and low-diffusion numerical schemes in MHD simulations. The above governing MHD equations are solved numerically by utilizing an open-source framework, “MPI-AMRVAC.”68,69 Here, the Harten-Lax-van Leer approximate Riemann solver70, in combination with a three-step Runge–Kutta solver, has been selected to improve the order of convergence accuracy in space and time. Moreover, the SuperTimeStepping method71 is used to efficiently solve the anisotropic thermal conduction problem. To better capture the detailed dynamics of the CS and in the flare loops, the use of the adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) of 5 levels is limited in the region of [−0.3, 0.3] × [−0.5, 0.5] × [0.4, 4]. The effective resolution of the simulation thus attains 2560 × 2560 × 3840 with a very high spatial resolution of about 0.2″ (equivalent to 156 km/cell), which is finer than the current resolution of instruments on SDO and IRIS. In addition, we activate the AMR in the region of [−0.8, 0.8] × [−0.5, 0.5] × [0.4, 0.6] for time t > 0.8 t0 to reduce the numerical error near the magnetic sources. Otherwise, a couple of very low-pressure regions form at the magnetic source locations, allowing the dense plasma in the chromosphere to unexpectedly flow into these areas. The boundary conditions are the line-tied condition at the bottom and open conditions for the other boundaries.

3D turbulent reconnection and magnetic spectra

Supplementary Movie 2 shows clearly the generation and dynamics of numerous 3D magnetic flux tubes (plasmoids) within the reconnecting current sheet. The first plasmoid appears at time = 0.3 t0. At this time, the width of the current sheet is measured as about 482.5 km, and its length grows to 1.48 × 104 km, resulting in an aspect ratio of 30.7. Thus, the sheet would become unstable to the tearing mode since its aspect ratio already apparently exceeds 2π, which is the threshold for the occurrence of the tearing mode instability75. Usually, the instability that occurs at such a large aspect ratio is also known as the plasmoid instability76,77. Here, we select the box defined by [−0.2, 0.2] × [−0.2, 0.2] × [0.5, 3.4] to estimate the turbulent energy. Since the system in the box has approximately translational symmetry in the y-direction, we calculate the ensemble average for any physical quantity f over y via f¯=⟨f⟩y. Then the fluctuation can be determined by the remaining part f~=f−f¯. In this work, we only focus on the turbulent magnetic energy δEm=∣B~∣2/2. Supplementary Fig. 1a presents the spatial distribution of the turbulent energy averaged over the y-direction, which grows and spreads mainly in the current sheet, indicating the occurrence of the Petschek reconnection78. To evaluate the turbulent spectra, we have performed the 3D Fourier transform on δEm to calculate the power spectral density δEm^(kx,ky,kz). Then, one-dimensional spectra are obtained by integrating over the azimuthal direction in (kx, ky, kz) space similar to ref. 79. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1b, the spectra exhibit the power-law features, indicating that the magnetic energy cascades efficiently from large-scale to small-scale structures. At time = 1.42 t0, the reconnection enters into a well-developed turbulent stage, showing a stable spectral index of −5/3 expected for Kolmogorov turbulence32,79,80. In short, plasmoid instability was found to occur in our simulation, and invokes the 3D turbulence that is self-consistently generated in the growing current sheet during the eruption.

Formation and propagation of slow shocks after the downflow-evaporation collision

To identify the shock properties in this process, we examine the shock’s positions on the velocity divergence maps in the centre plane (y = 0) at time = 1.06 t0 as shown in Supplementary Fig. 3b. In general, the shock front can be easily identified by examining the location with negative ∇⋅V, as strong compression is expected in the immediate vicinity of the shock. Then we examine the distribution of physical quantities crossing it along the shock normal (represented by a black solid line), and compare them with the jump conditions of MHD shocks81. In Supplementary Fig. 4, the primary variables, including plasma density, gas pressure, temperature, velocity, and magnetic field components, are displayed with the black box indicating the shock interface. One can see that the density and pressure are compressed by a factor of about 1.35, while the normal velocity of the shock front drops by a factor of 2.3. The temperature increase is only around 2%, potentially suggesting that thermal conduction is greatly efficient and the shock is almost isothermal. Based on our calculation, we obtain the propagation speed of the shock of about 258 km s−1 at time = 1.06 t0. Using the moving coordinates of the shock, the ratio of the normal velocities ∣Vn∣ across the shock is found to be about 1.33, which conserves roughly the mass. As the initial magnetic field is set to be nonuniform, the normal magnetic component, ∣Bn∣, cannot remain constant. But the slope of the tangent component, ∣Bτ∣, becomes apparently greater than the background at the shock interface, suggesting a decrease in magnetic strength across the discontinuity. So these quantities upstream and downstream are roughly consistent with the Rankine-Hugoniot relations, and we finally attribute this shock to a slow-mode MHD shock.

The fixed field line (in red) in Fig. 2a has reconnected to form a cusp-shaped loop at time = 0.9 t0, which is obviously not the lowest energy state. It evolves toward the potential rounded form after leaving the reconnection site. We plot the apex height of this loop and the associated speed of shrinkage against time in Supplementary Fig. 2. The loop shows the apparent decrease in height immediately after it is newly produced. And we notice that the shrinkage of the loop mainly occurs before time = 1.2 t0, and then the loop tends to be round, consistent with the previous theory9. The initial shrinkage motion can reach a high speed of about 1580 km s−1, accompanied by fast reconnection flows into the flare loops. And it is much easier to generate the slow shocks owing to the collision between the fast downward flows and evaporation flows.

In Supplementary Fig. 3, we follow the evolution of a pair of slow shocks from their emergence to their extinction, in terms of density and velocity divergence in the central xz-plane (y = 0). At time = 0.98 t0, the post-reconnection downflow starts to collide with the evaporation flow, where the front of the denser downflow can be seen. After the collision, the slow shocks gradually form and propagate along the magnetic field toward the footpoints. One can easily identify the location of the propagating slow shocks within the evaporation flows by the large negative values of the velocity divergence. Different from 2D simulations reported in ref. 19, no distinct shock front of evaporation flows could be recognized after the collision, probably due to the relatively weak evaporation in the 3D configuration, because the compressed plasma has one more direction to expand. It is worth considering a more comprehensive model that combines energetic particles and a more delicate thermal conduction module to investigate the properties of evaporation flows in future work.

However, the continuous propagation of normal slow shocks can hardly be followed in spectral observations because of the limited time resolution (i.e., about 33 s for each raster step of the Fe XXI line observations by IRIS). Even so, we have found some points sharing similar characteristics of spectral lines on two steps earlier than slit 9 from Fig. 4j. The local significant broadening, together with the continuous variation of the two-component Fe XXI lines at adjacent points suggests that the slow shocks are likely present nearby (Supplementary Fig. 8). Assuming that all these emission features come from the same slow shock, one can “image” the shock propagation in a short time while scanning from raster step 7 to 9. For the next scanning period, the Doppler maps are provided in Supplementary Fig. 9. The line widths at previous sampled points (indicated by the magenta box) for slit 7–9 show clearly a decrease, probably suggesting that the slow shocks propagate somewhere else or disappear at the footpoint in a longer time (about 33 s).
