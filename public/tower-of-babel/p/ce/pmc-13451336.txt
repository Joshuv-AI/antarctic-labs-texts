Template guided versus freehand rod bending in spinal deformity surgery: a feasibility study
Authors: L Bättig, A R Dixon, E Molero Hidalgo, N Hejrati, F C Stengel, F Corr, T Schöfl, L Feuerstein, N A Pogonitz, S Singh, B Martens, M N Stienen, Stefan Motov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinal deformity correction relies on osteotomies and precisely contoured rods, which are critical for restoring sagittal alignment. Inaccurate rod bending may compromise alignment and increase the risk of junctional failure. This feasibility study compares the accuracy of template-guided versus freehand rod contouring in a simulated environment. Using SurgiMap® software, 12 double-curved rods were designed from full-spine radiographs. Six surgeons from a tertiary spine centre bent rods freehand or using 2D paper templates. Two rod specifications were tested: ⌀4.0 mm stainless steel rods for cervicothoracic and ⌀6.0 mm cobalt-chromium rods for thoracolumbar models, representing the mechanical extremes of commonly used spinal implant configurations. Digitized rod contour angles (RCAs) were compared with planned contours using tangent-vector analysis. Template-guided bending significantly improved accuracy (mean RCA difference: 2.6° ± 1.3°, p < 0.01) and required fewer bends (4.1 ± 1.3, p < 0.01), without a significant time increase (p = 0.11). Subgroup analysis showed significant improvement only in less experienced surgeons. In conclusion, template-based rod bending improved sagittal contouring accuracy and efficiency in a simulated environment, with the greatest benefit for less experienced surgeons. Introduction

Corrective surgery for adult spinal deformity (ASD) aims to restore sagittal alignment, given its strong association with patient-reported outcomes and quality of life1,2. Osteotomies and manually bent rods remain standard corrective tools3–5, though surgical complications—including proximal junction kyphosis, hardware failure, or revision procedures—significantly affect outcomes3,6–9. Loss of sagittal balance, often caused by degenerative lumbar scoliosis and coronal malalignment, occurs in 19–35% of cases7,9. Accurate rod contouring is essential for achieving both sagittal and coronal plane correction9,10.

Although several rod-bending techniques guided by patient anatomy and planned correction exist, manual execution may introduce inaccuracies in rod angulation and inflection points between curves. Previous studies comparing template-based and freehand rod bending8,11 focused on simplified, single-curved rods or in situ fusions, limiting generalizability to complex, long-segment deformities. Recent studies showed that computer-assisted6 and augmented-reality (AR) assisted rods8 demonstrate higher accuracy than manually bent rods.

This study assesses the sagittal plane accuracy of rod bending using 2D templates based on planned correction versus freehand bending guided by preoperative lateral radiographs, specifically for long-construct (double-curve) cervicothoracic and thoracolumbar deformity corrections. An AP view analysis was excluded in this study to generate a homogeneous simulation of the bending process and as a simplification.

Methods

Study design

This prospective, observational simulation study investigates template-based versus freehand rod bending feasibility.

A 2D templating approach was chosen to isolate rod contouring accuracy as a single variable in a controlled, reproducible manner. While 3D templating better reflects clinical reality, establishing reliability in this simplified framework is necessary before advancing to 3D models.

This feasibility study, conducted with the support of Brainlab SE (Germany), assessed rod bending variability using a simplified methodology independent of proprietary software. Though dedicated software like NEO Medical may enhance clinical translation, this design ensured generalizability and avoided vendor bias.

Rod planning

Twelve rods were planned from whole spine anterior/posterior and lateral radiographs using SurgiMap software (Nemaris Inc, USA) by a single surgeon (S.M.) with over 10 years of spine surgery experience. The same surgeon was also involved in the bending simulation. Planning was based on clinical cases from real patients who consented to anonymized data use for research through Brainlab SE. Six cervicothoracic (stainless steel) and six thoracolumbar (cobalt chrome) rods were designed with double curves, partially replicating osteotomy-related angulations while conforming to spinal alignment. Due to increased difficulty with double-curved rods, we focused entirely on sagittal angles for simplicity, acknowledging this does not perfectly reflect in vivo settings. The length of the planned rods ranged from 101.0 to 371.4 mm.

Planned rods were imported into CAD software (CREO, PTC Inc., USA) to extract centerlines and generate 2D rod templates showing lateral views of 3D rod models, including upper and lower instrumented vertebrae and rod length in millimeters. The 2D approach was chosen to analyze sagittal bending angles while minimizing costs. Brainlab SE supplied the rods.

For controls, printed whole-spine radiographs displayed screw positions and alignment changes without rod overlay, printed to scale on A3 paper without millimeter markings. Template group prints included a 100 mm reference scale.

Rod preparation

Material selection balanced Young’s modulus and yield strength using common spinal implant alloys (Co28Cr6Mo, Ti6Al4V, stainless steel). Co28Cr6Mo alloys have the highest stiffness and yield stress. Though Ti6Al4V has a lower Young’s modulus than stainless steel (~ 100 vs. ~190 GPa), its higher yield stress (~ 550 vs. ~200 MPa) requires greater force for permanent deformation. Therefore, stainless steel was preferred, and Ti6Al4V was excluded. Rod materials and diameters represented mechanical extremes: ⌀4 mm stainless steel for cervicothoracic rods (easiest to bend) and ⌀6 mm Co28Cr6Mo for thoracolumbar rods (most difficult). All rods were pre-cut to the planned length.

Bending

Six spine surgeons from a Swiss tertiary centre volunteered. Surgeons were stratified by clinical rank: surgeons holding attending-level positions (≥ 8 years of experience) were classified as experienced, whereas residents and deputy senior physicians (< 8 years) were classified as less experienced. This categorization was based on institutional hierarchy rather than an arbitrary years-of-experience threshold. Each surgeon received either a wall-mounted rod template or a radiograph image (Fig. 1) and bent rods using their preferred pliers from a standard set until satisfied. Bending duration was recorded. A balanced Latin square design randomized rod order to prevent fatigue and learning effects. Two observers documented bends (defined as closing the French bender). Each surgeon bent 24 rods—12 cervicothoracic and 12 thoracolumbar—with six using templates and six without for each region. The 24 rods were the same for all 6 surgeons and were handed to them in a random manner by the observers. Two materials and anatomical regions enhanced generalizability.

Exemplary 2-D radiograph and exemplary rod template, which aided surgeons in bending rods.

Rod processing and data analysis

Bent rods were digitized with a handheld 3D scanner (HandySCAN 300, CREAFORM, Canada) and exported as triangulated surface meshes in STL format. Centerlines were extracted using a custom Python workflow that incorporated the Vascular Modeling Toolkit (VMTK). The surface meshes underwent preprocessing to ensure mesh quality and reduce noise from scanning. The two endpoints of each rod were initially identified as the pair of surface points with the greatest Euclidean distance, and a centerline was computed between these points, smoothed, and uniformly resampled. The resulting centerlines were subjected to a length consistency check and were visually inspected to ensure correct endpoint selection and centerline geometry. Each centerline was sampled at 1000 equally spaced points.

Each bent rod was then aligned to its corresponding template rod. Procrustes analysis was applied first to remove global translation, rotation, and scale, followed by classic point‑to‑point Iterative Closest Point (ICP), which used the mean Euclidean distance between corresponding points as the error metric.

Rod contour angles (RCAs) were defined as the angles between tangent vectors at successive inflection points along the rod centerline (Fig. S2, see Supplementary Files). Inflection points—locations where curvature changes sign—were first detected automatically. Because noise in the scanned geometries occasionally produced erroneous points, all inflection points were reviewed using an interactive interface and adjusted when necessary. This verification was performed three times by one author (ARD) to remove intra-rater variability. For each repetition, RCAs were computed separately, and the mean of the three RCA values was used as the final RCA measurement for that rod. Each rod contained two sagittal curves; the absolute RCA differences for these curves were then calculated and averaged to yield one overall accuracy measure per rod for analysis.

Because the 12 rod designs varied in planned length, bending time and number of bending manoeuvres were additionally normalised to rod length.

Statistical analysis

Analyses used Python (version 3.11, 2023). Descriptive statistics covered surgeon characteristics. RCAs were assessed via tangent vector analysis (Fig. 2). Group comparisons examined absolute RCA differences, number of bends, duration, and ICP alignment error for entire datasets and stratified by region (cervicothoracic vs. thoracolumbar).

Example of preplanned and bent lumbar rod contour angles with and without template.

Normality was assessed using Shapiro–Wilk tests. Within-subject comparisons used paired t-tests (normal data) or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (non-normal). Surgeons were divided by experience: two with ≥ 8 years (experienced, mean 9 ± 1.0 years) and four with < 8 years (less experienced, mean 5 ± 1.09 years). Subgroup comparisons used unpaired t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests based on normality.

To account for potential non-independence of observations (multiple rods per surgeon), we additionally ran a mixed-effects model (Table S5, see Supplementary Files). The mean absolute RCA difference was entered as the dependent variable, with template use and rod shape as fixed effects, and the surgeon included as a random effect.

Descriptive data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Mixed-effects model results are reported as effect estimates with p-values (and 95% confidence intervals where applicable). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The local ethics committee confirmed that no formal approval was required, as all imaging data were used internally and anonymized. No patient-identifiable data were published. Protocol No. BASEC Req-2025-00333.

Impact of template utilization

Template-guided bending resulted in significantly greater accuracy, as reflected by a lower RCA difference (8.3 ± 4.5° vs. 10.7 ± 6.1°; mean difference 2.6°; p < 0.01). Additionally, fewer bending manoeuvres were required when using a template (19 vs. 23 bends; mean difference 4.1 bends; p < 0.01). After normalising the number of bending manoeuvres to rod length to account for differences in planned rod size, this difference was attenuated but remained statistically significant (0.09 ± 0.04 bends/mm vs. 0.1 ± 0.05 bends/mm; mean difference 0.04 bends/mm; p < 0.01) (Fig. 3; Table 2).

Subgroup analysis revealed a significant reduction in ICP deviation for both cervical (1.3 ± 0.77 mm vs. 2.0 ± 1.56 mm; p < 0.01) and lumbar rods (1.5 ± 0.69 mm vs. 2.5 ± 1.73 mm; p = 0.01) with template assistance. Moreover, cervical rods bent using templates demonstrated a significantly lower mean absolute rod contour angle difference compared to those bent freehand (7.4 ± 3.8° vs. 10.6 ± 5.5°; p < 0.01).

After adjustment for surgeon level clustering, template use remained a significant independent predictor of improved bending accuracy ( CI−3.90° to − 0.81°, p = 0.003), corresponding to an approximate 2.35° reduction in mean absolute RCA error.

Rod geometry also influenced accuracy. To interpret shape-related effects, the curvature of each geometry was quantified using the sum of its template angles. Rod shapes exceeding the median value were categorized as higher-curvature geometries, while those below were considered lower-curvature geometries. In the mixed effects model, all rod shapes with statistically significant increases in bending error (e.g., + 6.50° relative to the reference; p = 0.001) belonged to the higher-curvature group. No geometries below the curvature median showed significant differences (all p > 0.05). Random surgeon variance was low (0.41), indicating minimal between-surgeon variability. Level of surgical experience

Among experienced surgeons, the use of a template did not yield statistically significant differences in bending accuracy (absolute RCA difference), time to bend, or number of bending manoeuvres. The same findings were observed after normalization of both bending time and bending manoeuvres to rod length, with no significant differences between groups.

In contrast, less experienced surgeons achieved significantly improved accuracy with the template (mean RCA difference: 2.8°; p < 0.01) and required significantly fewer bends (mean difference: 4.6 ± 2.3 bends; p < 0.01). This difference was attenuated after normalisation to rod length, but remained statistically significant (mean difference: 0.03 ± 0.02 bends/mm; p = 0.02). Bending time did not differ significantly (mean difference: 15.2 ± 55.0 s; p = 0.06), and this remained unchanged after normalisation to rod length (mean difference: 0.19 ± 0.16 s/mm; p = 0.15).

No significant differences were observed between the cervical and lumbar rods for any measured parameter across all surgeons. When using the template, the absolute RCA difference was nearly identical for experienced and less experienced surgeons (8.3 ± 4.5° and 8.4 ± 4.5°, respectively). Similarly, the number of bends was nearly identical (19.5 ± 9.4 bends vs. 18.6 ± 6.8 bends), and remained comparable after normalisation to rod length (0.09 ± 0.03 bends/mm vs. 0.09 ± 0.04 bends/mm). No significant differences between groups were observed for either RCA deviation or bending manoeuvres.

Although not statistically significant, experienced surgeons required slightly less time for template-guided rod bending compared with less experienced surgeons (106 ± 78 s vs. 127 ± 50 s; p = 0.10). When adjusted for rod length, the time-to-length ratio was significantly lower in the experienced group (0.44 ± 0.24 s/mm vs. 0.59 ± 0.23 s/mm; p = 0.02), suggesting that experienced surgeons bent rods more efficiently per unit length when using templates (Fig. 4; Tables 3 and 4).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that template-based rod bending improves accuracy and may reduce bending manoeuvres compared with the freehand technique, without increasing bending time.

Importance of rod bending in adult spinal deformity correction

Optimal rod contouring is critical, as rod curvature influences final postoperative spinal alignment, as shown in the adolescent idiopathic scoliosis literature11. Sagittal alignment parameters, including sagittal vertical axis (SVA) and pelvic incidence minus lumbar lordosis (PI–LL), strongly correlate with patient-reported outcomes5,12,13. Combined with multilevel spinal release and disc replacement, accurately contoured rods offer high correction potential while minimizing complications2. Mild to moderate deformities can often be addressed via posterior-only approaches, emphasizing the importance of precise rod bending5,11. In this context, even seemingly modest differences in RCA merit careful consideration.

A mean RCA difference of 2–3° may appear quantitatively small. Whether improvements of this magnitude translate into clinically meaningful changes in postoperative sagittal alignment, implant longevity, or complication rates remains to be established through prospective in vivo studies. Based on existing literature14–16 suggesting that rod contour inaccuracies may contribute to alignment deviations and junctional complications, we hypothesize that the observed improvements could be clinically relevant, particularly in long-construct deformity corrections with narrow alignment targets. However, this hypothesis requires validation in clinical cohorts.

The observed improvements with template-guided rod contouring, particularly for double-curved rods, may have several implications that are supported by existing literature. Double-curved rods are used to achieve simultaneous sagittal and coronal corrections in complex deformities. Small angular deviations in multi-planar contouring can accumulate across levels and result in global alignment errors. Sardi et al. demonstrated that inaccuracies in rod contouring can result in unintended deviations from preoperative alignment goals even in short constructs with a perceivably greater clinical impact in multi-planar constructs7. Thus, the modest relative reduction of 2–3° per rod bend may aggregate over multiple levels, particularly in long constructs requiring double curvatures and precise inflection point distribution.

Notably, template-guided bending significantly reduced the ICP distance error for both cervical and lumbar rods, whereas the absolute RCA difference was significant only for cervical rods (p < 0.01) but not for lumbar rods (p = 0.46). This discrepancy may be explained by the different aspects each metric captures: the ICP distance error reflects the overall spatial deviation between the bent and planned rod contours along the entire centerline, whereas RCA specifically quantifies angular differences at discrete inflection points. In the lumbar rod cohort, surgeons may have achieved a globally closer contour match (lower ICP error), potentially attributable to greater familiarity with bending thoracolumbar rods in routine clinical practice, while still exhibiting angular variability at individual inflection points. Additionally, the larger diameter and stiffer material of the lumbar rods (⌀6 mm CoCr) may have constrained the range of achievable angular deviations, compressing the RCA distribution and reducing the statistical power to detect differences.

Yang et al. proposed patient-specific, pre-contoured rods using lateral radiographs in ankylosing spondylitis patients17. While these serve as three-dimensional templates, they incur higher costs and may require adjustments. In this context, template-based bending might not only be more cost-efficient but also particularly valuable during percutaneous instrumentation, where bony structures, spine curvature, and screw tulips are not visible, making rod fitting challenging without excessive pedicle screw stress.

Mechanical impact of rod bending

Studies examining cobalt-chromium and titanium rods found that French bender contouring reduces yield strength and stiffness regardless of material or diameter3, intensifying with increasing bends or angles3,6,7. From a mechanical perspective, the repeated rod contouring is a source of residual stress, work-hardening, and stress concentration that can adversely affect construct durability18,19. Experimental studies clearly demonstrate that contouring alters rod fatigue behaviour: for example, contoured titanium posterior rods have significantly lower fatigue life than straight rods when subjected to cyclic loading, with failure frequently initiated at the bend/notch location itself–a direct consequence of plastic deformation and stress concentration at the contour points. Slivka et al.20 similarly demonstrated that fatigue endurance limits of common spinal rod materials (titanium, stainless steel) are reduced after bending, with changes explained by residual stresses induced during contouring. These findings underscore that each additional manipulation (bend, re-bend) incrementally introduces microstructural alterations and stress risers that cumulatively might reduce fatigue resistance under physiological loads. Although there is no data on the exact number of bendings that might impact rod fatigue, our results demonstrated a significant reduction of the required bendings with a potentially beneficial effect on the rod characteristics. Further rod failure typically occurs at the curve apex, where bending stress is greatest, causing material fatigue and increased mechanical failure risk7. Improper contouring increases mechanical load on pedicle screws due to screw tulip-rod position mismatch, causing screw pullout or loosening—contributors to revision surgery and poor outcomes6,8. Biomechanical models indicate that interface stress heterogeneity increases when rods are not well matched to the preplanned curvature, leading to altered load distribution across pedicle screws and potential micro-motion21. Although direct quantification of interface stresses as a function of bending manoeuvres is not yet reported, the above-mentioned studies support the general principle that improved contour congruency enhances the construct durability.

Ohba et al. demonstrated that computer-assisted rod bending results in better clinical outcomes, including reduced back pain, radiculopathy, and screw pullout due to improved precision6. Von Atzigen et al. reported reduced bending time and fewer manoeuvres using AR-based navigation versus freehand techniques22.

Our study supports these findings: 2D templates enabled more accurate contouring, reduced bends, improved angle precision in double-curved rods, and provided better (though not statistically significant) inflection point reproduction. These factors may preserve rod strength and stiffness, potentially reducing hardware failure and surgeon fatigue in long-construct surgery.

Computer-assisted and template-assisted rod bending

With growing awareness of the importance of sagittal alignment in ASD, surgical planning software adoption has increased7. These tools tailor correction to individual anatomical parameters (PT, PI, SVA). However, rod bending remains largely manual, introducing error risk. Sardi et al. found surgeons frequently overbend rods, potentially increasing proximal junctional failure (PJF) risk7. In our study, especially with pliable materials (cervical rods), template assistance significantly reduced planned-actual rod angle differences.

Although not tested in vivo, the templates in our study may serve as practical intraoperative guides, improving preoperative planning-actual contouring congruence and potentially reducing PJF risk. Wanivenhaus et al. demonstrated AR-assisted bending reduced time by 20% and improved accuracy versus freehand bending—aligning with our accuracy findings, though we observed no bending time improvement8.

Rod bending in education

Template-based bending may serve as both a surgical aid and an educational tool for less experienced surgeons. Levy et al. introduced a rod-bending simulation model for practice outside ORs, demonstrating that structured training improved speed and confidence4.

Farshad et al. showed AR navigation systems allowed precise screw placement and rod bending independent of experience9,10. The observation that less experienced surgeons appeared to benefit more from template-guided bending is indeed intuitive and clinically plausible. Rod contouring is a skill that improves with repetition and tacit three-dimensional spatial understanding, and less experienced surgeons may rely more heavily on visual estimation and iterative adjustments. A standardized sagittal template may therefore reduce variability and compensate for limited procedural experience by providing an objective geometric reference. Still, we acknowledge that our subgroup analysis was based on a very small sample size and was not powered to definitively assess interaction effects between experience level and bending accuracy. The observed differences should therefore be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory. More importantly, even experienced surgeons demonstrated some improvements in contour precision through template application, suggesting that the potential utility of templates may not be restricted to junior surgeons. We therefore believe it is more appropriate to frame rod templates as a standardization instrument that may reduce inter-operator variability across experience levels. However, larger, prospective studies with stratified analyses are required to validate whether experience modifies the effect of template-guided bending.

While pre-contoured rods from manufacturers improve alignment, reduce operative time, and mitigate hardware failure, these custom solutions require longer manufacturing time, are cost-intensive, often need intraoperative modification, and may face regulatory constraints. In contrast, 2D templates are simple, cost-effective, and rapidly deployable.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first feasibility study comparing template-based rod bending using double-curved rods for sagittal realignment against freehand techniques, providing a controlled baseline for subsequent 3D and software-integrated studies. We included surgeons from diverse backgrounds following standardized protocols. This approach may support junior spine surgeons intraoperatively.

However, our controlled laboratory design may overestimate template benefits. Surgeons bent rods under ideal conditions: ample light, no tissue resistance, blood loss, or fatigue. In reality, rod bending occurs late in complex cases under challenging circumstances; the absence of these stressors may bias results toward 2D templates.

Although we controlled for fatigue using a Latin square design, continuously bending 24 rods is atypical and may introduce cumulative fatigue or learning effects. Two participants (F.S., L.B.) interrupted tasks and resumed later, introducing unaccounted variability. We randomized the order of the lumbar and cervical rods before bending for all participants to minimize learning and fatigue effects, but we cannot entirely exclude them. We asked the surgeons who performed the bending to be as precise and quick as possible. However, intra-surgeon correlation was not investigated, as the surgeons only bent the same rod twice (once with and once without the template), precluding repeated-measures comparison. Future studies should consider this.

We attempted to minimize coronal deviation during scanning and angle calculation, though some inaccuracies may exist. Our decision to assess rod contouring accuracy using a two-dimensional sagittal template was deliberate and methodologically motivated, but it represents a simplification that warrants explicit clarification.

The primary objective of this feasibility study was to isolate the sagittal rod contouring as a single, measurable variable under controlled conditions. Sagittal alignment, particularly in lumbar lordosis restoration, remains a predominant determinant of postoperative mechanical behaviour and clinical outcome in adult deformity surgery. By restricting the analysis to the sagittal plane, we aimed to reduce confounding influences and improve internal validity when assessing differences in contour precision and bending manoeuvres. This approach allowed us to quantify the RCA, bending frequency, and contour deviation reproducibly.

However, we fully acknowledge that this model does not capture the complexity of in vivo rod shaping during deformity surgery. In practice, rod contouring frequently involves combined sagittal and coronal curvature, axial rotation/derotation manoeuvres, asymmetric segmental corrections, and coupled plane interactions during screw reduction. A 2D sagittal template might not account for coronal alignment targets, vertebral rotation, or torsional stresses introduced during multi-planar correction. The observed improvements with template guidance should be interpreted as limited to sagittal contouring accuracy and might not be extrapolated to imply three-dimensional rod fit nor reflect the rod congruency within a fully corrected 3D spinal construct. Also, we restricted the analysis to 2D templates without incorporating commercial rod-bending software. While reducing immediate clinical applicability, this was deliberate to establish reproducibility and baseline feasibility, providing a foundation for future 3D planning and software studies.

Rod geometry also influenced bending accuracy. Rod geometries with greater overall curvature tended to show larger contouring errors, whereas lower curvature shapes showed no significant differences. This may represent the difficulty in bending highly curved rods. However, only two of five highly curved rods showed statistical significance, highlighting the limited sample size in this study and possible effects from other unidentified factors. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Larger studies with balanced representation of geometries are needed to clarify whether these shape-related differences are genuine or simply reflect the small samples used here.

Only six surgeons from a single centre participated, potentially introducing bias from similar training. Low participant numbers in both groups (experienced/less experienced) reduced statistical power. We mitigated this by selecting participants from different subspecialties (neurosurgery and orthopedics) and experience levels. Observed experience-level differences suggest trends favouring template assistance in less experienced surgeons, requiring validation in larger, balanced cohorts.

The conclusions in this study are based on a feasibility model and therefore remain inferential. The present data do not allow definitive statements regarding the impact on global sagittal parameters or junctional complication rates in vivo, and prospective clinical studies with a rod template utilization during surgical cases would be required to substantiate these assumptions in real-world deformity surgeries.

Conclusion

This feasibility study provides foundational data for future translation into 3D templating and software-assisted rod bending systems. Template-guided bending may also serve as a valuable adjunct in the training of less experienced surgeons, contributing to more precise spinal deformity correction.
