Expanding the palette of cis‐elements to tune the expression of chloroplast transgenes in tobacco
Authors: Rosalind Williams‐Carrier, Margarita Rojas, Alice Barkan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYThe capacity of the chloroplast genetic system to express proteins from polycistronic mRNAs offers advantages for applications that require the expression of multiple transgenes. Optimal outcomes in such applications often require the production of transgene products in particular stoichiometries. A current limitation in this regard is the paucity of characterized cis‐elements that program transgene expression in increments over a wide dynamic range. We leveraged advances in understanding chloroplast gene expression to design cis‐elements that modulate the expression of a transgene within a polycistronic transcript. In one approach, we tuned down the activating effects of cis‐elements that bind the pentatricopeptide repeat proteins PPR10 and HCF152 by introducing mutations that modestly decrease binding affinity in vitro. In a second approach, we used translational efficiencies determined by ribosome profiling to select 5′UTRs anticipated to program varying translational activities. Elements were evaluated in dicistronic reporters in transplastomic tobacco. The results showed that mutations that decrease PPR10 binding affinity caused a corresponding decrease in GFP abundance and that the suite of native 5′UTRs programmed a wide range of GFP abundance in incremental steps and with various developmental dynamics. Our results demonstrate that binding affinities of PPR proteins for modified cis‐elements in vitro can be used as predictive tools to design cis‐elements for transgene tuning and that translational efficiencies programmed by endogenous 5′UTRs approximate relative transgene output in synthetic chloroplast operons. These findings add to the options for cis‐elements to optimize protein balance while minimizing effects on host plant vigor.

INTRODUCTION

The genetic modification of plants holds great promise for enhancing crop resilience and nutritional value, and for developing sustainable systems for the production of pharmaceuticals and industrial biomaterials (Boehm et al., 2017; Ronald, 2023; Wurtzel et al., 2019). Many such applications require the expression of multiple transgenes, with optimal outcomes depending on suitably balanced expression levels. Modification of the chloroplast genome is particularly well suited for this because chloroplast genes are naturally expressed in polycistronic transcription units, transgenes can be integrated at specified sites via homologous recombination, they do not experience epigenetic effects, and they can achieve very high expression levels (Bock, 2022; Maliga & Bock, 2011). Furthermore, chloroplasts are maternally inherited in most crop species, mitigating concerns of unintended pollen transmission. These features have been exploited to synthesize valuable metabolites and show promise for the production of complex multiprotein structures (Chen et al., 2023; Fuentes et al., 2018; Jensen & Scharff, 2019; Jin & Daniell, 2015; Long et al., 2018; Lopes et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2013; Orr et al., 2020). However, achieving the optimal balance of protein output among multiple transgenes remains a key challenge.

Nicotiana tabacum (tobacco) is the primary testing ground for applications involving the modification of the chloroplast genome in vascular plants due to its amenability to chloroplast transformation (reviewed in Maliga et al., 2021). Efforts to design cis‐elements have generally focused on maximizing transgene expression (reviewed in Bock, 2021). Toward that end, a strong chloroplast promoter, typically P rrn or P psbA , is typically combined with a 5′ untranslated region (5′ UTR) from a highly expressed chloroplast gene (e.g., rbcL or psbA) and a 3′ UTR encoding a stable RNA hairpin to stabilize the upstream RNA. An important advance toward multigene applications came with the discovery of an ‘intercistronic expression element’ (IEE), which, when placed upstream of an internal open reading frame (ORF) in a polycistronic mRNA, boosts ORF expression (Zhou et al., 2007). The IEE was derived from the chloroplast psbT‐psbH intergenic region, and was proposed to exert its effect by promoting RNA cleavage, thereby increasing the translational efficiency of the downstream ORF (Zhou et al., 2007). However, subsequent advances in understanding mechanisms underlying the processing of polycistronic chloroplast mRNAs (reviewed in Barkan, 2011), together with the finding that the IEE exerts its effects by binding the nucleus‐encoded protein HCF107 (Hammani et al., 2012), suggest an alternative view—that the binding of HCF107 to the IEE simultaneously stabilizes the downstream RNA by blocking a 5′ → 3′ exonuclease and promotes its translation by maintaining a structure‐free ribosome binding site (RBS). The IEE is often inserted upstream of each internal ORF in synthetic chloroplast operons. However, this practice has deleterious effects on host plant vigor due to the titration of HCF107 (Legen et al., 2018), which is required for the expression of the PsbH subunit of photosystem II (Felder et al., 2001).

HCF107 is a helical repeat RNA‐binding protein (Hammani et al., 2012; Sane et al., 2005), and is but one of many helical repeat proteins that exert similar effects on chloroplast RNAs (Hammani et al., 2014). Most such proteins belong to the pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) protein family, whose members are characterized by degenerate repeating units of 35 amino acids that adopt a helical hairpin fold and stack to form an elongated surface that binds RNA (Small & Peeters, 2000; Yin et al., 2013). Hundreds of PPR proteins localize to chloroplasts, some of which bind specific UTRs; these serve to stabilize the adjacent RNA by blocking exonucleases, and/or activate translation of the downstream ORF (reviewed in Barkan & Small, 2014). The ‘footprints’ of RNA‐stabilizing PPRs can be detected as abundant small RNAs in RNA‐seq datasets (Pfalz et al., 2009; Ruwe & Schmitz‐Linneweber, 2012; Zhelyazkova et al., 2012). Sequences corresponding to several such PPR footprints were shown to substitute for the binding site of the PPR protein PPR10 within the atpH 5′UTR in a synthetic operon, stabilizing the downstream RNA and increasing the output of adjacent transgenes to varying extents (Legen et al., 2018, 2023). That work served as a proof‐of‐concept for the use of alternative ‘IEEs’ to mitigate the titration of trans‐factors by repeated use of the same element. A similar approach gave analogous results in the non‐vascular plant Marchantia polymorpha (Frangedakis et al., 2021). The elucidation of an amino acid code for RNA recognition by PPR motifs (Barkan et al., 2012; Takenaka et al., 2013; Yagi et al., 2013) allowed the engineering of modified PPR activators that bind cis‐elements not present in the wild‐type chloroplast genome; expression of such proteins from a nuclear transgene activates a chloroplast transgene harboring the cognate cis‐element in its 5′UTR, providing a means for regulating chloroplast transgenes according to the activity of any nuclear promoter (Mirzaee et al., 2024; Rojas et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2019).

Despite these advances, the current toolkit offers little capacity for predictable, graded control of protein output from chloroplast transgenes. Recent reports used cell free chloroplast extracts as a means to rapidly assay the expression‐enhancing effects of various cis‐elements (Böhm et al., 2024; Clark et al., 2024). However, the RNA‐stabilizing and translation‐enhancing functions of many (if not most) chloroplast 5′UTRs requires the binding of gene‐specific nucleus‐encoded RNA binding proteins (PPR proteins and others) (Barkan, 2011; Small et al., 2023; Zhelyazkova et al., 2012), and it is unlikely that the activities of such proteins are accurately recapitulated in in vitro systems. Thus, it is essential to assess the activities of candidate cis‐elements in transplastomic chloroplasts.

In this study, we leverage advances in the quantitative analysis of chloroplast gene expression and in the biochemistry of PPR‐RNA interactions to design cis‐elements predicted to achieve graded expression of a transgene in the context of a synthetic operon. In one approach, we tested the hypothesis that mutations in the binding sites of characterized PPR activator proteins will decrease transgene output in proportion to the decrease in protein‐RNA binding affinity measured in vitro. In a second approach, we tested the hypothesis that native 5′UTRs function as transportable modules to program translational efficiencies inferred from plastome‐wide ribosome profiling datasets. The results largely support these hypotheses. The UTRs described herein provide new options for transgene tuning in synthetic chloroplast operons.

RESULTS

Identification of mutations in the PPR10 and HCF152 binding sites that moderately decrease binding affinity in vitro

We selected the UTRs bound by the PPR proteins PPR10 and HCF152 to evaluate the in vivo effects of binding site mutations that modestly decrease binding affinities in vitro. PPR10 binds the atpH 5′UTR, serving to stabilize the downstream mRNA and stimulate its translation (Pfalz et al., 2009; Prikryl et al., 2011; Zoschke et al., 2013). The maize and tobacco atpH 5′UTRs strongly stimulate expression of a reporter gene in transplastomic tobacco, and this activating effect relies on PPR10 (Rojas et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2024). We previously analyzed the effects of mutations at each position in the PPR10 binding site on PPR10's binding affinity in vitro (Miranda et al., 2017). We selected two mutant variants from that experiment that reduce PPR10's affinity several fold to test for effects on transgene output in vivo (see below).

We then extended this approach to HCF152 and its RNA binding site in the psbH‐petB intergenic region. HCF152 activates petB expression by blocking 5′ → 3′ RNA degradation and by promoting splicing of a group II intron near the start of the petB ORF (Meierhoff et al., 2003; Zhelyazkova et al., 2012). The position of its binding site suggests that HCF152, like PPR10, also stimulates petB translation by reducing the potential for inhibitory RNA structures that sequester the petB RBS (Zhelyazkova et al., 2012). We showed previously that recombinant HCF152 binds with high affinity and specificity to an RNA sequence corresponding to HCF152's in vivo footprint (Zhelyazkova et al., 2012) (Figure 1a). Here, we used gel mobility shift assays to examine effects of transversion mutations at different positions in the maize HCF152 (Zm‐HCF152) binding site on the binding affinity of recombinant Zm‐HCF152 (Figure 1b). With this assay, RNA binding can be discerned by both the decrease in unbound RNA and the appearance of bound RNA. For many of the mutant RNAs we tested, a discrete ‘bound’ band did not increase in intensity in proportion with decreased intensity of the unbound RNA band, presumably due to dissociation of the RNA‐protein complex during electrophoresis. This prevented the calculation of precise binding affinities. Nonetheless, the data show that the majority of the mutations reduced binding affinity, and that the G9C mutation was the only one that eliminated binding. We selected A4U and G15C for in vivo analysis in tobacco because both caused a moderate decrease in binding affinity. The maize and tobacco HCF152 binding sites differ in sequence only at position 11, where there is a pyrimidine transition (Figure 1a), a change that generally has minimal effect on PPR binding affinities (Barkan et al., 2012; Miranda et al., 2018). We anticipated, therefore, that the A4U and G15C mutations selected for in vivo analysis in tobacco would have similar effects on the affinity for tobacco HCF152.

Point mutations in the atpH and petB 5′UTRs that program incremental decreases in reporter expression

We created five dicistronic reporter constructs to examine the consequences of the mutations in the PPR10 and HCF152 binding sites (Figure 2a; Figure S1). Each construct was integrated at the same site between trnV and rps12 in the large inverted repeat of the chloroplast genome (Figures S1 and S2). The constructs used the P rrn promoter, the selectable marker aadA as the first gene, and gfp as the second gene. The intercistronic regions were derived from the atpI‐atpH and psbH‐petB intergenic regions, which bind in vivo to PPR10 and HCF152, respectively. The processed 5′RNA terminus mapping to each region corresponds to the 5′ boundary of the cognate protein's footprint on the RNA. We included an additional 40–50 nucleotides upstream to maintain the normal sequence context of the binding sites, which might influence protein binding via effects on RNA structure.

The constructs harboring the PPR10 binding site and its variants leveraged an existing construct containing the 97 nucleotides upstream of the maize atpH start codon (Figure 2a) (Yu et al., 2024). In these constructs, aadA is flanked on its 3′ end by the 3′UTR of rbcL to stabilize the upstream RNA. The variant atpH UTRs contained U16A or C5G transversion mutations in the PPR10 binding site, which decrease PPR10's binding affinity in vitro roughly twofold and fourfold, respectively (Miranda et al., 2017). The constructs harboring variant HCF152 binding sites contained the 80 nucleotides upstream of the tobacco petB start codon as well as the first two petB codons and did not include an RNA‐stabilizing hairpin between aadA and gfp (Figure 2a). We were unable to recover a line harboring the wild‐type petB 5′UTR for unknown reasons. Homoplasmy of lines used for subsequent experiments was confirmed by Southern blot hybridization (Figure S2).

The five reporter constructs had varying effects on plant growth and development (Figure 2b; Figure S3A). The Zm‐atpH construct caused severe stunting of mature plants. Stunting was less severe but still substantial in plants harboring the variant Zm‐atpH UTRs, whereas plants harboring the two petB UTR variants were phenotypically normal. The severity of these growth defects was inversely correlated with the degree of GFP fluorescence at the seedling stage (Figure 2b). We quantified the relative abundance of GFP in the different lines by immunoblot analysis. The stained immunoblots showed that GFP abundance in Zm‐atpH transformants was similar to that of the large subunit of Rubisco (RbcL) (Figure 3a), consistent with a prior analysis of the same construct in independent transformants (Yu et al., 2024). GFP comprised ~16% of total soluble protein (TSP) (Figure S4), somewhat less than the ~20% TSP reported previously for the same construct (Yu et al., 2024). This variation could reflect different growth conditions, developmental stage, or quantification methods. The U16A and C5G mutations decreased GFP abundance to roughly 2/3 and 1/4 of that in Zm‐atpH plants, respectively (Figure 3a; Figure S4), similar to the decrease in PPR10 binding affinities observed in vitro (Miranda et al., 2017). GFP abundance in plants harboring the two Nt‐petB variants was roughly eightfold lower than that in the Zm‐atpH plants.

In their native contexts, PPR10 and HCF152 stabilize processed RNAs whose 5′‐boundary maps to the 5′‐end of their binding sites (Pfalz et al., 2009; Zhelyazkova et al., 2012). We used RNA gel blot hybridizations to address whether this function is maintained in the context of these synthetic operons (Figure 3b). Dicistronic RNA (aadA‐gfp) was abundant in all lines, albeit with some variability that may reflect differences in nuclease sensitivity among the sequences. However, processed monocistronic gfp RNA was barely detected even in the Zm‐atpH line in which GFP accumulated to very high levels. The point mutations in the PPR10 binding site decreased the abundance of monocistronic gfp mRNA still further. GFP was highly abundant in the Zm‐atpH:U16A line (Figure 3a) despite the near absence of processed monocistronic gfp mRNA (Figure 3b). Processed monocistronic gfp mRNA was barely detectable in the two Nt‐petB lines. As expected, aadA was represented in both monocistronic and dicistronic RNAs in the Zm‐atpH constructs, which include an RNA‐stabilizing hairpin in the aadA 3′ UTR, but was found only as a dicistronic mRNA in the Nt‐petB constructs, which lack this hairpin.

In summary, the 5′UTRs analyzed in this set of experiments program incremental differences in transgene output, ranging from very high (similar in abundance to RbcL) to 1.5‐fold, 4‐fold, and 8‐fold reductions thereof. Furthermore, the decreases in GFP output programmed by point mutations in the PPR10 binding site were roughly proportional to the decreases in PPR10‐RNA binding affinity observed in vitro.

Testing whether chloroplast 5′UTRs are sufficient to program translational efficiencies of the corresponding endogenous genes

Ribo‐seq, the deep sequencing of mRNA fragments that are protected by ribosomes from nuclease attack, provides a transcriptome‐wide readout of the number of ribosomes bound to mRNAs (Ingolia, 2014). In conjunction with RNA‐seq, ribo‐seq data can be used to infer translational efficiencies: the average number of ribosomes bound to each ORF. This metric is generally a reliable indicator of the relative rate of protein synthesis per mRNA molecule under a given condition. Ribo‐seq coupled with RNA‐seq has been used to catalog translational efficiencies of all chloroplast ORFs in maize (Zea mays), Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana), tobacco (N. tabacum), M. polymorpha, and Chlamydomonas reinhardtii (Chotewutmontri & Barkan, 2016, 2018; Ghandour et al., 2025; Gotsmann et al., 2024). The translational efficiencies of chloroplast ORFs vary over an approximately 20‐fold range in each organism. These datasets provide a potential resource for the selection of endogenous cis‐elements to tune rates of protein synthesis to desired levels in synthetic chloroplast operons. The goal of the next set of experiments was to test this concept.

Current data suggest that sequences within and directly adjacent to 5′UTRs are typically sufficient to dictate translational efficiency in bacteria and chloroplasts (reviewed in Zoschke & Bock, 2018; Webster, 2025). Initiating chloroplast ribosomes bind single‐stranded mRNA proximal to start codons, and this interaction is inhibited by RNA structure involving the RBS. The translation of many chloroplast ORFs requires gene‐specific translational activators, the best characterized of which bind 5′UTRs a short distance upstream from the RBS they activate (Hammani et al., 2012; Higashi et al., 2021; McDermott et al., 2019; Prikryl et al., 2011). Most such activators are helical repeat proteins that recognize their RNA binding sites in a single‐stranded context, so their binding to RNA is inhibited by RNA secondary structure (Kindgren et al., 2015; Marzano et al., 2024; McDermott et al., 2018; Zoschke et al., 2016). As such, the binding of chloroplast translational activators in vivo is likely to be influenced by sequences flanking the binding site due to effects on the RNA structure landscape. The first few codons of an ORF can also impact translational efficiency in chloroplasts (Gray et al., 2011; Kuroda & Maliga, 2001).

With these features in mind, we designed six dicistronic reporter constructs to test whether chloroplast 5′UTRs together with the first few codons recapitulate the translational efficiencies inferred from chloroplast ribo‐seq/RNA‐seq data. We used two datasets to guide our choices of UTRs to test: a published ribo‐seq/RNA‐seq analysis of Arabidopsis seedling leaf tissue (Chotewutmontri & Barkan, 2018) and a single ribo‐seq/RNA‐seq replicate of tobacco leaf tissue reported here (Dataset S1). The translational efficiencies measured in these two experiments were well correlated despite the use of different organisms and developmental stages (Figure 4a). We selected 5′UTRs from genes internal to polycistronic transcription units whose translational efficiencies ranged from very low to very high consistently in the two experiments: rps14, rpl14, petA, atpA, psbH, atpH (Figure 4b), using the tobacco sequences in the constructs. These choices sampled genes whose maize orthologs exhibit different developmental dynamics (Chotewutmontri & Barkan, 2016): maize rps14 has higher translational efficiency in proplastids and immature chloroplasts near the leaf base than in mature chloroplasts toward the leaf tip, psbH peaks in translational efficiency in the leaf tip harboring mature chloroplasts, whereas the translational efficiencies of petA, rpl14, atpH, and atpA in maize peak at intermediate developmental stages. Each construct included between 64 and 105 nucleotides of 5′ UTR sequence and the first five codons of the corresponding ORF fused in‐frame to the gfp ORF. These UTR lengths were selected to include all conserved sequence blocks and to extend at least 30 nucleotides upstream of binding sites of known translational activators (see Figure 4b). The atpA 5′UTR is a special case in that the distance between atpA and the upstream gene (atpF) is unusually short (54 nucleotides). We therefore extended the region in this construct to include 10 nucleotides of the atpF ORF. Homoplasmy of each line was confirmed by Southern blot hybridization (Figure S2).

Plants harboring the rps14, rpl14, petA, and atpA 5′ UTR reporters were phenotypically normal, whereas those with the psbH and atpH constructs were mildly and severely stunted, respectively (Figure 5a; Figure S3B–D). GFP fluorescence was very strong in 4‐week‐old plants harboring the atpH and psbH constructs, very low in plants with the rps14 construct, and intermediate in plants containing the other three constructs (Figure 5a right; Figure S3B). GFP fluorescence also varied among the lines in its developmental profile. The rps14 construct was expressed at the highest level in the youngest leaves (leaf 1 and the newly emerging leaf in Figure 5a). Expression of GFP in the rpl14 line was also biased toward younger developmental stages, with strong GFP fluorescence in the three expanded young leaves (leaves 1, 2, 3). By contrast, GFP fluorescence in the atpA line was strongest in the two oldest leaves (leaves 3 and 4). GFP fluorescence in the other three lines did not show obvious differences among the stages examined in 4‐week‐old plants. In 50 day old plants, GFP fluorescence in the petA line was stronger than that in the rpl14 line (Figure 5b), whereas the opposite was true at the earlier developmental stages (Figure 5a).

To further examine differences in developmental dynamics of gfp expression suggested by GFP imaging in planta, we used immunoblots to compare GFP abundance at three stages of leaf development: a young leaf from 5‐week‐old plants, an older leaf from 5‐week‐old plants, and a mature leaf from 7‐week‐old plants (Figure 6b). Due to its severe stunting, we excluded the atpH line from the full analysis because it was impossible to harvest comparable developmental stages. Blots were also probed to detect AadA, which is encoded upstream of gfp in the same dicistronic transcription unit (Figure 4). AadA accumulated to similar levels and in a similar developmental pattern in all of the lines, with lower levels in the young leaf samples and higher levels in the older and mature samples. GFP abundance was similar in the young leaves of the rps14, atpA, and petA lines. By contrast, in older leaves, GFP accumulated to considerably higher levels in the atpA and petA lines than in the rps14 line (Figure 6b, left). Whereas GFP abundance was similar at all three stages in the rps14 line, GFP abundance peaked in the ‘older’ leaf in the rpl14 line, declined slightly in the mature leaf in the atpH and psbH lines, and plateaued at the two older stages in the petA line. We generated a semi‐quantitative view of these data by digital quantification of the ratio of AadA to GFP signal in comparison with that in the Zm‐atpH samples on the same blots (Figure 6b). This quantification is not precise due to non‐linearities inherent in immunoblot assays. However, the consistent ratio in all dilutions of the Zm‐atpH sample suggests that these values approximate the true relative ratios. The results confirm that the rps14 UTR is unique among the UTRs assayed in that it prioritizes expression at early developmental stages. When expressed as a ratio to AadA, GFP abundance in older leaves is highest for the psbH and atpH lines, roughly threefold lower in the rpl14 line, roughly fivefold lower in the atpA and petA lines, and roughly 15‐fold lower in the rps14 line. These values are similar to those based on the dilution series in Figure 6a, but there is some variation as is to be expected from imprecisions in the methods employed.

RNA gel blot hybridizations (Figure 6c) showed variable gfp mRNA levels among the lines. The dicistronic aadA‐gfp mRNA was most abundant in the atpH and psbH lines, suggesting that these UTRs are less susceptible to endonucleolytic cleavage. Monocistronic gfp mRNA was also the most abundant in those lines. The monocistronic RNAs presumably result from the action of HCF107 and PPR10, which bind the psbH and atpH UTRs, respectively, and act as blockades to exonucleases (Hammani et al., 2012; Pfalz et al., 2009; Sane et al., 2005). Monocistronic gfp mRNAs were not detected in the other lines, consistent with the absence of processed RNA termini mapping to those UTRs in other species (Barkan et al., 1994; Hammani et al., 2016; Jiang et al., 2019; Malik Ghulam et al., 2013; Pfalz et al., 2009).

The Zm‐atpH line discussed earlier has abundant monocistronic aadA mRNA due to the RNA‐stabilizing hairpin downstream of aadA (Figure 6c). The other UTRs did not include an RNA‐stabilizing hairpin, so monocistronic aadA mRNA in those lines presumably results from protein‐mediated protection. The atpH line accumulated monocistronic aadA (Figure 6c), as expected from the fact that PPR10 stabilizes a processed 3′end mapping to the atpH 5′UTR (Pfalz et al., 2009). No monocistronic aadA mRNA was detected in the psbH line, consistent with the fact that HCF107 binding to the psbH RNA does not promote the accumulation of mRNA with a processed 3′ end at the boundary of its binding site (Felder et al., 2001). The fact that monocistronic aadA accumulated in the rpl14 construct was a surprise, as a 3′ RNA terminus has not, to our knowledge, been mapped to that UTR.

The goal of this set of experiments was to assess whether translational efficiencies inferred by ribo‐seq/RNA‐seq data are recapitulated when UTRs are used in ectopic contexts. To evaluate this, relative translational efficiencies can be approximated by the ratio of GFP protein to gfp mRNA. Keeping in mind that immunoblot and RNA gel blot data are only semi‐quantitative, our results suggest that in mature leaf tissue, the translational efficiencies conferred by the atpH and psbH UTRs were the highest, followed by rpl14, petA, and atpA which were roughly equal, and with rps14 the lowest. This corresponds roughly to the relative translational efficiencies inferred by ribo‐seq/RNA‐seq (Figure 4a). In the youngest leaf tissue, the translational efficiency conferred by the rps14 UTR was increased relative to the other UTRs tested, consistent with prioritized rps14 translation very early in chloroplast development in maize (Chotewutmontri & Barkan, 2016). Translational efficiencies of all chloroplast ORFs vary with developmental stage (Chotewutmontri & Barkan, 2016), so the general approach used here would likely be more predictive of outcomes with excised UTRs if guided by replicated ribosome profiling datasets from multiple developmental stages of wild‐type tobacco.

Evidence that the atpH 5′UTR stunts growth by titrating PPR10

Titration of HCF107 in transplastomic tobacco harboring the classic IEE decreases plant vigor, especially when the IEE is used in multiple copies (Legen et al., 2018). Transplastomic plants harboring the maize or Arabidopsis atpH 5′UTR are even more stunted than those with the psbH 5′ UTR (which contains the IEE) (Figure 5a; Figure S3B–D). The stunting of the atpH lines could be due to the metabolic burden of massive GFP production, the titration of PPR10 by transgene mRNA, or both. The fact that GFP is found at similarly high levels in the psbH and atpH lines argues against metabolic burden as being the sole contributor. To investigate the basis for stunting in the atpH lines, we used immunoblots to quantify a core subunit of the ATP synthase (AtpB), Photosystem II (D1), Photosytem I (PsaD), and the cytochrome b 6 f complex (PetD) (Figure 7), each of which accumulates to reduced levels when the synthesis of any closely associated subunit is reduced. The rps14, atpA, petA, and rpl14 lines had normal levels of these proteins (Figure 7a), consistent with their normal growth. The psbH line had a strong reduction in D1, an expected consequence of HCF107 titration which is essential for PSII biogenesis; the fact that the plastid‐encoded proteins RbcL, AtpB, and PetD accumulated to near normal levels in the psbH line argues against metabolic burden as a significant issue, because this would likely cause a global reduction in chloroplast protein synthesis. Because PPR10's primary function is to promote synthesis of the ATP synthase subunit AtpH (Pfalz et al., 2009), PPR10 titration is expected to have a particularly strong effect on ATP synthase levels. However, subunits of other complexes are also reduced in ppr10 mutants, albeit less severely (Pfalz et al., 2009). The Zm‐atpH and tobacco atpH lines showed some decrease in all of the assayed proteins, but the magnitude of the decrease for AtpB (~16‐fold) was greater than that for the others. This pattern resembles the protein losses in maize ppr10 mutants (Pfalz et al., 2009), strongly implicating PPR10 titration in the stunted phenotype. That said, a global decrease in plastid translation is likely in the atpH lines, as reflected by their severe loss of RbcL. We suspect that this results primarily as a secondary effect of their ATP synthase deficiency, because these protein deficiencies were milder in the psbH line, whose GFP levels are similar to those of the atpH lines. A similar analysis of the Zm‐atpH lines with point mutations in the PPR10 binding site (Figure 7b) showed less severe protein deficiencies, correlating with their more vigorous growth (Figure 2b). Mature plants harboring the Zm‐atpH U16A and C5G UTRs were stunted to a similar extent as the psbH line (Figure 2b; Figure S3). These UTRs may provide a good alternative to multiple IEEs when high output of multiple transgenes in a polycistronic transcript is desired. However, it is also possible that the combined titration of PPR10 and HCF107 would be similarly deleterious to the titration of HCF107 from two IEEs.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we used features of chloroplast gene expression established in recent years to guide the design of cis‐elements that program transgene expression in a graded fashion over a broad dynamic range. The designs were grounded in the hypotheses that 5′UTRs will be sufficient to program translational efficiencies of the genes from which they are derived and that mutations in the binding sites of characterized translational activators will decrease transgene output in proportion to the decrease in protein‐RNA binding affinity measured in vitro. The results generally support those hypotheses, albeit with caveats discussed below. Together, the set of UTRs we analyzed resulted in a roughly 30‐fold range of GFP abundance in graded increments. In the context of our reporter design—the P rrn promoter and placement as the second gene in the operon—the psbH, atpH, and Zm‐atpH UTRs yielded GFP at levels approaching that of RbcL (~15% TSP), similar to prior results with atpH‐derived 5′UTRs (Rojas et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2024). The point mutations in the PPR10 and HCF152 binding sites programmed GFP levels roughly 1.5‐, 4‐, and 8‐fold lower than that maximal amount, and the native UTRs reduced maximal GFP levels roughly 4‐fold (rpl14), 5‐fold (atpA and petA), and 30‐fold (rps14), although these levels varied with developmental stage.

Quantifying GFP as %TSP can be useful as a point of comparison with other studies, but these values should be interpreted with caution because they imply a level of precision and reproducibility that can be misleading. For example, for constructs that reduce the level of Rubisco (e.g., the atpH and Zm‐atpH UTRs), normalizing to TSP artificially inflates apparent GFP abundance. In addition, the abundance of the transgene product can change during plant development (Ruhlman et al., 2010) (Figure 6). Furthermore, protein abundance is influenced by protein half‐life, which varies in an unpredictable fashion based on the amino acid sequence. In our UTR constructs, the gfp ORF was preceded by the first five codons of the gene with the corresponding UTR (Figure 4b), so protein stabilities might differ (Bouchnak & van Wijk, 2019). Furthermore, from the practical standpoint, estimates of relative transgene output are sufficient to guide cis‐element choice because variation in the stabilities of transgene mRNAs and their protein products will prevent precise recapitulation of findings made with different transgenes.

A potential consequence of the use of native UTRs to promote transgene expression is the titration of trans‐factors that bind those UTRs. This issue was originally reported for constructs employing multiple IEEs (Legen et al., 2018). We found that this problem is still more severe for the atpH UTRs from both maize and tobacco. In fact, the stunting caused by those UTRs makes them unsuitable for practical applications. Mutations that mildly decrease the binding affinity for the activating protein (PPR10 in the case of atpH UTRs) can mitigate this problem to some extent (Figure 2b). However, in the context of metabolic engineering, suitably balanced expression of transgene products is likely to be more important than very high expression. As such, the modest expression levels programmed by the rps14, atpA, petA, and rpl14 UTRs may be quite useful, as these program a roughly 10‐fold dynamic range among themselves with no impact on host plant vigor. These UTRs are roughly 100 bp, well below the length that promote homologous recombination with endogenous sequences elsewhere in the engineered genome {Mudd, 2021 #114}. Still lower expression can easily be accomplished with many approaches, including eliminating the binding site for the activator protein in an otherwise ‘strong’ UTR (Rojas et al., 2019) or inserting a tRNA to promote intercistronic RNA cleavage (Yu et al., 2024).

Our results have several implications with regard to the control of chloroplast gene expression. First, we show that UTRs vary in the developmental dynamics they program. The rps14 and rpl14 5′ UTRs differed from the others we tested in that GFP expression peaked in young leaf tissue (Figure 5a); this was most dramatic for the rps14 line, which exhibits very bright GFP fluorescence in newly emerging leaf tissue and very little in the older leaves (Figure 5a). Ribosome profiling analysis of chloroplast gene expression along the developmental gradient of the maize seedling leaf blade showed a strikingly similar pattern: translational efficiency of rps14 peaked at the very base of the leaf, rpl14 translational efficiency peaked at a subsequent but still early stage in chloroplast development, whereas genes corresponding to the other UTRs we tested peaked in translational efficiency in mature chloroplasts (Chotewutmontri & Barkan, 2016). It seems likely that this feature reflects the developmental timing of proteins that interact with each UTR. The prioritized early developmental timing of expression programmed by the rps14 and rpl14 UTRs may be advantageous for some applications.

Our results also have implications with regard to the mechanism by which the classic IEE element boosts expression of internal genes in polycistronic transcription units. It has often been assumed that it does so by triggering RNA cleavage which, in turn, is proposed to increase translational efficiency (Bock, 2021; Zhou et al., 2007). The fact that the psbH 5′UTR (within which the IEE maps) boosts levels of both monocistronic gfp mRNA and its aadA‐gfp precursor (Figure 6c) in comparison with several of the other UTRs we analyzed argues against that mechanism, as RNA cleavage in the IEE would reduce the abundance of the dicistronic transcript. Our data are better aligned with the alternative view that the binding of HCF107 to the IEE simultaneously blocks 5′ → 3′ RNA degradation and increases translational efficiency (Hammani et al., 2012). This mechanism also best accounts for the strong activating effect of the Zm‐atpH UTR: In the tobacco reporter context, a minimal amount of monocistronic gfp mRNA (Figures 3 and 6) corresponds with very high GFP output, strongly arguing that PPR10 binding rather than accumulation of monocistronic gfp mRNA underlies the activating effect of this UTR.

Our findings add to the evidence that the sequence context of binding sites for activating proteins can have a large impact on the activity of the corresponding UTR. This was apparent in a study in M. polymorpha (Marchantia), where the processed petB 5′UTR programmed much lower transgene expression than the full psbH‐petB intergenic region (Frangedakis et al., 2021). The processed petB 5′terminus in Marchantia is presumably protected by the HCF152 ortholog, so the poor performance of the processed UTR implies poor access of HCF152 to its binding site. Similarly, the tobacco atpH 5′UTR did not promote high transgene expression when the sequence upstream of the PPR10 binding site was not maintained (Legen et al., 2018), whereas constructs that extend the atpH UTR sequence for an additional ~40 nucleotides upstream promote very high transgene expression (Figures 3, 5, and 6) (Yu et al., 2024). These observations imply that the activating effects of the classic IEE as well as the many IEE‐like elements defined by footprints of PPR proteins (Ruwe & Schmitz‐Linneweber, 2012; Zhelyazkova et al., 2012) will be inconsistent if inserted as isolated elements into differing UTR sequences. Our psbH 5′UTR line seems to be more stunted than what has typically been reported for transplastomic lines harboring the IEE in ectopic sequence contexts, presumably because the intact UTR more effectively bound HCF107. This highlights that improved binding of a UTR to a trans‐factor does not necessarily result in an improved outcome for applications.

In closing, this work expands the synthetic biology toolbox for chloroplast engineering by suggesting a theoretical framework for prioritizing cis‐elements for in vivo testing and by providing new options for elements that program a range of transgene outputs. Mixing and matching elements such as the petA, atpA, rpl14, and rps14 5′UTRs, and mutant atpH and petB UTRs in synthetic chloroplast operons will likely enable transgene products to be produced in a range of stoichiometries while minimizing deleterious effects on the host plant. Grounding cis‐element designs in chloroplast gene expression profiling data and known activating mechanisms may be a more efficient path toward achieving desired outcomes than the use of in vitro or heterologous expression systems that poorly mimic findings in vivo.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant material

Nicotiana tabacum Petit Havana seeds were germinated on agar plates containing MS medium (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA; M5519), 0.64% Micro Agar (Gold Bio, St. Louis MO, USA; M1002.1000) and 3% (w/v) sucrose. When working with transplastomic lines, the medium included 500 mg L−1 spectinomycin HCl to select for aadA expression. Plants were grown in a tissue culture incubator for ~3 weeks at 25°C at a light intensity of 100 μmol m−2 sec−1 with 16‐h light/8‐h dark cycles. Seedlings were then transplanted to soil and grown under the same conditions prior to tissue sampling for protein and RNA analysis. Older plants were transferred to a greenhouse for propagation.

Chloroplast reporter constructs and transformation

Sequences of the constructs are provided in Figure S1. Transplastomic plants were generated using the method described in Maliga et al. (2021). The Zm‐atpH construct was reported previously (Yu et al., 2020, 2024) where it was named pAI3, and was generously provided by Qiguo Yu and Pal Maliga (Rutgers University, New Brunswick, NJ, USA). All other constructs were generated by modification of plasmid pAI3.

SDS‐PAGE, immunoblot analysis, and GFP quantification

Protein was extracted from leaf tissue, separated by SDS‐PAGE and analyzed by immunoblotting as described previously (Barkan, 1998) except that protein concentrations were determined with a Bradford protein assay (Bio‐Rad Protein Assay, Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and precast Tris‐glycine 4–20% polyacrylamide gels (Novex, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) were used for electrophoresis. The GFP antibody was purchased from Clontech (Mountain View, CA, USA; clone JL‐8, 632380) and the AadA antibody was purchased from Agrisera (Vannas, Sweden, AS09 580); both were used at 1:2000 dilution. The D1, PsaD, PetD, and AtpB antibodies were described in McCormac and Barkan (1999). To quantify GFP as %TSP, thylakoid membranes were pelleted by centrifugation at 13 000× g for 5 min. Protein concentration in the supernatants was determined with a Bradford assay using BSA for the standard curve. Soluble fractions were resolved by SDS‐PAGE and gels were stained with InstantBlue Coomassie Protein Stain (Abcam, Waltham, MA, USA). Known amounts of carbonic anhydrase (Sigma) served as quantification standards in each gel. Dilution series of samples with abundant GFP were included to ensure the assay was in the linear range. The GFP bands and carbonic anhydrase standards were scanned with an Epson Perfection V600 Photo flatbed scanner and bands were quantified with Quantity One Version 4.6.9 (Bio‐Rad). The amount of GFP in each sample was determined by comparison to the carbonic anhydrase standard curve; this was divided by the mass of protein loaded to calculate %TSP. As a reality check, we compared GFP band intensity to intensity of the carbonic anhydrase standards by eye. This comparison validated the %TSP values for the high‐expressing lines (atpH, Zm‐atpH, and psbH) but demonstrated that the values obtained for the other lines were artifactually high, presumably due to the lower signal‐to‐background ratio. Therefore, %TSP for all other lines was inferred based on dilutions of the atpH, Zm‐atpH, and/or psbH samples included in immunoblots in Figures 3 and 6.

Gel mobility shift analysis

The expression and purification of recombinant maize HCF152 (Zm‐HCF152) were described previously (Zhelyazkova et al., 2012). In brief, the sequence encoding mature Zm‐HCF152 was cloned in‐frame downstream of sequences encoding maltose binding protein (MBP) in the expression vector pMAL‐TEV. The MBP‐Zm‐HCF152 fusion protein was expressed and purified as described previously (Zhelyazkova et al., 2012) except that CHAPS was not added to the lysis buffer. Gel mobility shift assays were performed with synthetic oligonucleotides radiolabeled with 32P at their 5′‐end as previously described (Barkan et al., 2012), except that binding reactions were incubated at 25°C for 30 min and the MBP tag was not removed. Protein concentrations are indicated in the legend to Figure 1. Synthetic RNA oligonucleotides (IDT) were used at 15 pM in binding reactions. Results were imaged with a STORM phosphorimager.

RNA and DNA gel blot hybridizations

RNA was extracted from leaf tissue with TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) and DNA was extracted with DNAzol reagent (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA gel blot hybridizations were performed as described previously (Barkan, 1998) except that blots were hybridized with IRDYE800 fluorescently labeled DNA oligonucleotides (IDT, Coralville, IA, USA) complementary to gfp (/5IRD800/GATAATGGTCTGCTAGTTGAACGCTTCCATCTTCAATGTTGTGTCTAATT) or aadA (/5IRD800/CTCCATTGCCCAGTCGGCAGCGACATCCTTCGGCGCGATTTTGCCGGTTA). RNA gel blots were hybridized overnight in Church hybridization buffer (7% [w/v] SDS, 0.5 m NaPhosphate pH 7) at 48°C and were washed three times for 5 min each with 5XSSC (0.75 m NaCl, 0.075 m NaCitrate) supplemented with 0.2% SDS at 48°C. Results were imaged on a Typhoon Imager (Amersham, Piscataway, NJ, USA). DNA gel blot hybridization to assess homoplasmy used 3 μg of leaf DNA digested with BamHI and probed with an IRDYE800 fluorescently labeled DNA oligonucleotide (IDT) complementary to plastid 16S rRNA (/5IRD800/ATGGCTGACCGGCGATTACTAGCGATTCCGGCTTCATGCAGGCGAGTTGC). Hybridizations and imaging were performed as for RNA gel blot hybridizations.

Ribo‐seq and RNA‐seq

Ribosome footprints and total RNA were prepared from the sixth leaf to emerge on a 37‐day‐old tobacco plant. The leaf was roughly 5 cm long at the time of harvest. The leaf was cut down the mid‐rib, and half of the leaf was used to make ribosome footprints and the other half of the leaf was used to make total RNA. Ribosome protected fragments from ~20 to ~40 nt were selected for ribo‐seq library preparation, and libraries were prepared with the NEXTflex Small RNA Sequencing Kit v3 (Bioo Scientific, Austin, TX, USA) in combination with rRNA depletion with customized biotinylated antisense oligonucleotides designed for Arabidopsis (Chotewutmontri & Barkan, 2018). RNA‐seq libraries were prepared from total RNA after rRNA depletion with the Ribo‐Zero rRNA Removal Kit (Plant leaf; Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using the NEXTflex Rapid Directional qRNA‐Seq Kit (Bioo Scientific, Austin, TX, USA). The libraries were sequenced using a HiSeq 4000 (Illumina) in single‐read mode with read lengths of 100 nt. Ribo‐seq and RNA‐seq data processing was done as described previously (Chotewutmontri & Barkan, 2021). Reads were aligned to the tobacco chloroplast genome (GenBank accession Z00044). Read counts were normalized based on the total number of reads mapping to chloroplast ORFs, using the TPM normalization method (Zhao et al., 2020). The normalized values for chloroplast genes are provided in Dataset S1.
