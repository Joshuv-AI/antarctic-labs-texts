Investigating the relationships between social support and hospital anxiety- depression with quality of life in geriatric cancer outpatients under oncology treatments
Authors: Nahid Jamshidi, Fatemeh Mehravar, Mahtab Alizadeh-Khoei, Farnaz Etesam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncreasing life expectancy leads to more cancer incidence in older people, consider that preservation and improvement of Quality of Life (QoL) is a major goal of geriatric oncology and examining the factors affecting QoL is very important, this study aimed to explore the relationship between social support and hospital anxiety-depression with QoL in the geriatric cancer outpatients under oncology treatments.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted from June to September 2023, on 300 elderly aged 60 ≥ and over, who received at least two sessions of chemotherapy or radiotherapy and or both, selected from a cancer institute of a general hospital in Tehran, Iran. Data sampling was collected by demographic/clinical questionnaires (need help in doing ADLs (Activities of Daily Living), number of comorbidities, cancer types, and stage), and tools (MSPSS (Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support ), HADS (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale), and EORTC QLQ-C30 (EORTC QLQ C-30, European Organization of Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire)) to analyze.ResultsSignificant positive relationships between social support with global health status/QoL scale (P < 0.0001) and functional scales (P < 0.001) were observed, but a negative relationship between social support and symptom scales (P < 0.05) was noted. Also, significant negative relationships between hospital anxiety (P < 0.001) and depression (P < 001) with global health status/QoL scale (P < 0.001) and functional scales (P < 0.001) were observed, but there was a significant positive relationship between hospital anxiety (P < 0.05) and depression (P < 0.05) with symptom scales. So, more social support and less anxiety and depression were related to better global health status/QoL scale, better functional scales, and lower symptoms/problems.ConclusionConsidering the positive role of social support and mental health in improving QoL, carrying out interventions to increase better social support sources (family, friends, and other important people) and preventing or reducing anxiety and depression could be effective in improving QoL in cancer geriatric outpatients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06497-1.Keywords: Geriatric cancer outpatients, Quality of life, Social support, Oncology treatments, Hospital anxiety-depression

Introduction

It is estimated that by 2030, 1 in 6 people in the world will be aged 60 years or over. At this time, the share of the population aged 60 years and over will increase from 1 billion in 2020 to 1.4 billion. By 2050, the world’s population of people aged 60 years and older will double (2.1 billion). The number of people aged 80 years and older is expected to triple between 2020 and 2050 to reach 426 million. Moreover, it’s estimated that by 2050, 24% of Iran’s population will be old people [1, 2].

Cancer is a disease associated with ageing, so more than 60% of all cancers are reported in people 65+ years old, and the risk of cancer in people over 65 years is 10 times higher [3, 4]. Also, according to the report of the WHO5, cancer is the second and third leading cause of death in the world and Iran, respectively [5].

Chemotherapy and radiotherapy as effective treatment methods, besides therapeutic benefits, have complications that harm the mental health of cancer patients, so anxiety and depression are the most common psychological disorders among cancer patients that lead to a decrease in their Quality of Life (QoL), it makes a disturbance in the patient’s social interactions that as a result, the probability of the patients’ isolation increased and the need of these patients for social support is greater than before [6–11]. Therefore, social support becomes especially important as a factor in reducing anxiety and depression and improving the QoL in older cancer patients [9, 12–14]. Social support refers to different types of support that are received from other people in the community, composed of two areas, including perceived social support, that reflects people’s opinion about the support provided, and the size of the social network included all known people by persons or the patients [12, 15]. Social support is known as the strongest facilitating factor when people are dealing with cancer and stressful situations, which makes it easier for patients to endure problems and reduces the experienced tension, improves the effectiveness of treatment, and ultimately improves the QoL in cancer patients [9].

The WHO defines the QoL as the understanding of each person, from a self-life situation according to the culture and value systems in which they live [16]. Today, the QoL is evaluated as one of the indicators that determine the effect of treatment in patients, so examining the factors affecting the QoL is a basic approach for maintaining and improving the QoL in people with chronic diseases, cancers, and especially in vulnerable older patients [16–18].

However, in previous studies [13, 19–22], the importance of the impact of anxiety, depression, and social support on the QoL in different age groups has been emphasized. But, only one study [23], has been conducted on the elderly with cancer that it has not investigated the impact of anxiety and depression on the QoL; also, it was conducted just on two types of cancers (lung and gastrointestinal cancers); therefore, this study is the first to examine this in geriatric Iranian cancer outpatients undergoing oncology treatments (chemotherapy or radiotherapy, and or both). Since cultural context influences social support and social support also influences QoL, the findings of this study can help plan or create interventions tailored to Iranian culture in elderly people with cancer.

Materials and methods

Sampling and study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted from June to September 2023 on 300 people aged 60≤ (based on Kugbey et al.‘s study and considering a 30% probability of missing) who were selected out of cancer outpatients referred to the oncology department in a general training hospital of Tehran Medical University, as a using available sampling [22].

In this study, ethics approval was granted by Tehran University of Medical Sciences, the faculty ethical committee under ethical number IR.TUMS.IKHC.REC.1402.045. Also, this research adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. After explaining the objectives of this study to participants, the data were collected by face-to-face interviews. Then, researchers asked the elderly participants to confirm the consent Form with their signature or fingerprint if they wished to participate in the study, and they were assured that their personal information would remain confidential and that their withdrawal from the study was optional.

The inclusion criteria included the elderly with cancer who had received at least two sessions of chemotherapy or radiotherapy, and or both who did not have obvious physical defects, and who had consented to enter the study. Before starting the interviews, to ensure that they had normal memory, the AMT6 test was performed, and the elderly who scored 7 or more on this test were considered cognitively healthy to participate in this study. The exclusion criteria comprised the elderly who did not have the satisfaction and ability to communicate effectively with the researchers to complete the study process.

Then, demographic data such as age, gender, supplemental insurance status, living status (with companions), need help in doing the ADLs, number of comorbidities by Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), cancer types and stage, and the number of chemotherapy and or radiotherapy sessions were asked or extracted from the medical records of patients who were present in the oncology outpatient department.

Measures

Multidimensional scale of perceived social support (MSPSS)

A 7-point (1–7) Likert-type scale consisting of 12 items scored between “Definitely no” and “Definitely yes”. The scale contains three subscales of four items each intended to determine support from family, friends, and a significant other. ‘Significant others’ refers to the support received from other important people in life and measures combined perceptions of support from friends and family (Relatives, caregivers, neighbors, colleagues, teacher, counselor, and others). The lowest possible score from the subscales is 4, and the highest is 28. The lowest possible total score for the entire scale is 12, and the highest is 84. Higher scores indicate higher perceived social support. In a study, Cronbach’s alpha reported 0.94 [24]. This tool has previously been translated and validated in the Persian language version, and an Iranian study convergent validity and internal consistency coefficients of this scale had been confirmed [25].

Hospital anxiety and depression scale (HADS)

This scale is a self-administered questionnaire including 14 items. Each domain has seven items measured on a 4-point Likert scale scored from 0 (Not at all) to 3 (Very often indeed) that apply to the previous week. The total score for both subscales can range between 0 and 21, with higher scores indicating higher levels of depression and anxiety. Patients with a total score of 11 or above for both subscales were diagnosed as suffering from clinical anxiety or depression [13]. This instrument has previously been translated and validated in the Persian language version and approved to be valid and reliable in the Iranian samples [26].

European organization of research and treatment of cancer quality of life questionnaire (EORTC QLQ-C30)

It’s a 30-item cancer-specific questionnaire that assesses the health-related QoL of cancer patients and incorporates five functional scales (physical, role, emotional, cognitive, and social), three symptom scales (fatigue, nausea and vomiting, and pain), a global health status/QoL scale, and single items to assess additional symptoms commonly reported by cancer patients (e.g., dyspnea, sleep disturbance, appetite loss, constipation, and diarrhoea), and the perceived financial impact on the disease and treatment. All items are scored on four-point Likert scales, ranging from 1 (Not at all) to 4 (Very much), except for two items in the global health status/QoL scale that apply modified seven-point linear analogue scales [19]. According to the user manual, all of the scale measures range in score from 0 (Worst) to 100 (Best) based on global health status/QoL scale and functioning scales, and from 0 (Best) to 100 (Worst) on symptom scales/items [8]. This tool has previously been translated and validated in the Persian language version, and the validity and reliability of this instrument were confirmed in the research of Sadoughi et al., with Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89 in Iran [27].

Statistical analysis

In this study, descriptive statistics were used to analyze demographic information. Measures of central tendency (mean) and dispersion (standard deviation) were analyzed to describe the scores of social support, hospital anxiety depression, and QoL. Also, Pearson’s correlation was used to evaluate the relationships between social support scores, hospital anxiety depression, and QoL. Also, the independent t-test and One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to evaluate the relationship between those variables and demographic variables. Version 24.0 of SPSS7 software, with P < 0.05 being statistically significant used to analyze. Multiple linear regression models were constructed to examine the simultaneous effects of social support and anxiety-depression scores on QoL outcomes while controlling for significant demographic variables identified in univariate analyses. Variables were entered simultaneously using the enter method, and model assumptions (normality of residuals, homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity) were verified through diagnostic testing. Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) remained < 5 for all predictors, indicating acceptable multicollinearity.

Predictor variables

The multivariate multiple linear regression models included Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) subscales (Anxiety and Depression) and Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) subscales (Family, Friends, and Significant others) as primary predictors.

Covariates

Significant demographic variables identified in univariate analyses (e.g., age, gender, need help in doing ADLs8, number of comorbidities, cancer types, and cancer stage with p < 0.20) were included as covariates to adjust for confounding effects.

Model specification

All predictors and covariates were entered simultaneously using the enter method. Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) confirmed no critical multicollinearity (VIF < 5 for all variables).

Outcome variables

The 15 functional and symptomatic subscales of the EORTC QLQ-C-30 served as dependent variables (e.g., Global Health Status/QoL, Physical functioning, Fatigue).

Results

Characteristics of participants

The participants (n = 300) were in the age group range of 60–87, and the Mean (SD) age of them was 66.75 (± 6.14) years old. The Mean (SD) number of comorbidities was 3.31 (± 2.21) with a range of 1–13 diseases at the same time in geriatric outpatients so hypertension (35%), diabetes (27.3%), cholesterol (24%), and sleep disorder (17.7%) reported as four common diseases. Relationships between characteristics of geriatric outpatients with EORTC QLQ C-30, MSPSS, and HADs

There were significant relationships between better QoL (higher global health status/QoL scale, higher functioning (in most scales)), higher total social support and less anxiety and depression with being male, being married, living with a spouse, being employed, being independent in ADLs9, having supplemental insurance, having fewer stage of cancer, fewer comorbidities and fewer chemotherapy or radiotherapy sessions (P < 0.05). Also, the worst QoL (lower global health status/QoL scale, poorer functioning (in most scales)), the weakest social support, and the highest level of anxiety and depression reported in older women, single, housewives, dependent in ADLs, without supplemental insurance, with high stage of cancer, with a greater number of comorbidities, a greater number of oncology sections, and living alone (P < 0.05). In general, though correlations were observed between the demographic and clinical characteristics and the scales of the study, no significant relationships were found between the QoL, social support, and hospital anxiety and depression with the age groups of the elderly and types of cancer. Being literate was only associated with better QoL (Tables 1 and 2).

Multivariate analysis

As shown in Table 4, the multivariate analysis revealed distinct patterns in quality of life (QoL) determinants among geriatric cancer outpatients (n = 300). Anxiety showed significant negative associations with emotional functioning (β=−2.15, p < 0.001), while depression adversely impacted both emotional (β=−1.47, p = 0.003) and social functioning (β=−1.31, p = 0.027), and was unexpectedly associated with increased pain (β = 1.70, p = 0.012). Social support demonstrated differential effects, with family support emerging as particularly salient, showing positive associations with global health status/QoL (β = 1.53, p < 0.001) and social functioning (β = 1.75, p = 0.005), while paradoxically correlating with reduced financial difficulties (β=−1.89, p = 0.038). The multivariate multiple linear regression (MMR) model demonstrated significant overall effects, as indicated by Wilks’ Lambda = [0.80, p < 0.001] and Pillai’s Trace = [0.18, p = 0.004]. These results confirm that the combined predictors (HADS and MSPSS subscales) significantly explain variance across the multivariate outcomes of the EORTC QLQ-C-30 scales.

Discussion

The results of this study indicated that scores from scales/items of QoL were relatively satisfactory, according to high scores in the global health status/QoL scale and the functioning scales, and low in the scales/items related to symptoms. Cognitive function was reported as the highest function scale, which seems logical because having cognitive health of the elderly based on the AMT test was one of the inclusion criteria for the study, which were similar to the results of several studies on cancer patients [19, 28]. However, there was no study on elderly cancer patients; meanwhile, emotional function was obtained as the weakest function in the cancer geriatric outpatients, which was similar to the results of other studies on cancer patients [28–30]. In terms of symptoms, fatigue was the most severe [19, 30], and dyspnea was the least severe in geriatric cancer outpatients, which was similar to the result of Gonzalez et al. [8].

This study showed that social support scores were satisfactory, because high scores in all three dimensions (family/friends/significant others) were similar to other studies [13, 31]. One possible reason for this favorable result was the ability to communicate effectively with others as an entry criterion for the study.

The prevalence of anxiety and depression had a high clinical significance; in this research found at 12.3% and 13.7%, respectively, which does not seem to be very high. Given that our study was conducted on outpatient geriatric patients usually have better physical and mental conditions than hospitalized patients, the results are justifiable. A study in the elderly with cancer reported higher anxiety and almost similar depression compared to our results [32].

In this study, there were no statistically significant differences in the QLQ-C30, HADS, and MSPSS scores in age groups of elderly outpatients, which this result is consistent with some previous studies on cancer patients [32–35], and contrasts with others [36–39]; although none of the mentioned studies conducted on the elderly age group and a study specifically on older adult aged groups is recommended.

However, in this study, more social support and better QoL (higher global health status/QoL scale and better functioning) were reported in male elderly, while anxiety and depression were more common in women, that was similar to the results of some studies on cancer patients [40–44], and contrasted with others [20, 34]. However, none of the mentioned studies had been conducted on the elderly age group. This gender difference can be explained by possible reasons such as women’s greater vulnerability, due to hormonal changes or more widowhood, and a greater need for support in older women.

The highest level of social support, the lowest level of anxiety-depression, and the best QoL in this research were reported in elderly cancer outpatients who were married (living only with their spouses), while single and alone elderly cancer outpatients were the worst in terms of these scales. One possible reason is that the elderly who live alone, due to the loss of their spouses, are more isolated and vulnerable; previous studies on cancer patients [45, 46] confirm our results; however, those studies were not on elderly age groups.

Beyond the primary multivariable findings, a previous research had emphasized the multidimensional nature of QoL among elderly cancer patients. Several studies have shown that both emotional and instrumental support are critical for better psychological adjustment and social integration [45–49]. Moreover, the Iranian validation studies and regional investigations highlight the role of cultural norms and health system factors in shaping patients’ perceived QoL [47, 50, 51]. Evidence also suggests that comorbidities, illness perception, and socioeconomic factors (e.g., insurance satisfaction, support networks) significantly influence functional independency and emotional resilience among older adults with cancer [50–59]. These findings are great consistent with our results, which also identified social support, anxiety and depression, and certain clinical and demographic variables as important correlates of QoL.

In our multivariable regression analyses, anxiety and depression emerged as consistent and independent predictors of lower QoL across most domains. This is in line with previous studies showing that emotional distress substantially reduces subjective well-being and physical functioning in cancer patients [14, 21, 33, 60]. Interestingly, depression showed stronger and broader associations with QoL subscales than anxiety, which may reflect its deeper and more prolonged impact on social withdrawal, fatigue, and loss of interest [61].

Social support was another key predictor of QoL. The MSPSS on each subscales (family, friends, significant others) showed specific associations with different QoL outcomes. For instance, support from significant others was more strongly related to emotional functioning and pain reduction, while family support had a notable influence on physical and role functioning [48, 62]. These patterns are consistent with prior study suggesting that the source and perceived quality of social support play a critical role in patients’ coping strategies. In the Iranian cultural context, family ties are often central to emotional and instrumental caregiving, which may explain these findings [13, 51].

Notably, some psychosocial predictors had domain-specific or nonsignificant associations. For example, anxiety did not significantly predict physical functioning, and social support was not associated with cognitive functioning. These patterns may be attributed to methodological limitations (e.g., self-report bias, cross-sectional design) or cultural nuances affecting symptom reporting and perceived relevance of support. Prior literature had similarly noted that the cognitive domain is less consistently associated with social variables, particularly in older populations [46, 54, 63].

The MMR’s multivariate lens uncovered nuanced mechanisms: while depression impaired both emotional (β=−1.47) and social functioning (β=−1.31), its association with heightened pain (β = 1.70) suggests that untreated depression may exacerbate somatic symptoms. Conversely, family support improved global QoL (β = 1.53) and reduced financial strain (β=−1.89), underscoring its role as a multidimensional resource in collectivist societies. These findings align with our aim to disentangle how psychosocial factors distinctly shape QoL in Iranian elders—a population where familial bonds often mediate health outcomes [64, 65].

Overall, the regression models explained a considerable proportion of variance in multiple QoL subscales. This confirms the importance of targeting psychosocial variables, particularly emotional well-being and social support, in efforts to enhance QoL among older adults undergoing cancer treatments. Interventions incorporating psycho-oncological support and culturally tailored family involvement may offer the most promising results [33, 53, 66].

Limitations

This study have several limitations that should be considered. First, due to its cross-sectional design, causal relationships between the studied variables cannot be established. Second, the sample was limited to geriatric outpatients undergoing oncology treatments; these individuals likely had better physical and mental conditions than inpatient geriatric hospitalized, which may limit the generalizability of these findings. Future research should include geriatric inpatients in oncology wards to enhance representativeness. Furthermore, although demographic and clinical variables were statistically adjusted for and included in the multivariable model, future studies could explore in more depth how these variables interact with psychosocial factors to influence quality of life over time.

Conclusions

In general, the results confirm the positive effect of perceived social support and the negative outcome of anxiety and depression on the quality of life of elderly people with various types of cancer undergoing oncology treatments. Meanwhile, this study revealed that demographic and clinical characteristics of geriatric cancer outpatients such as being male, being literate, being married, living with a spouse, being independent in Activities of Daily Living (ADL), having supplemental insurance, having lower cancer stage, less number of comorbidities, and fewer chemotherapy or radiation sessions could be considered as predictors of better QoL, higher social support, and less anxiety and depression.
