Combined maternal separation and limited bedding-nesting increase goal tracking, delay discounting, and impulsive action, and moderate sign-tracking-perseverance associations in male rats
Authors: Julian Cifuentes, Mónica Arias-Higuera, Cesar Acevedo-Triana, Michael J Hylin, Lucas Pozzo-Miller, Camilo Hurtado-Parrado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtensive research with rodent models has shown detrimental effects of early-life adversity (ELA) on behavioral (e.g., impulsive behavior, anxiety, and depression) and neurobiological processes (e.g., alterations of neuroendocrine processes and maturation of brain areas). However, heterogeneous methodologies, including types and variations of ELA manipulations could have contributed to inconsistent findings across studies. Recent research indicates that the combination of the two most widely implemented rodent ELA protocols, Maternal Separation (MS) and Limited Bedding/Nesting (LBN), produces consistent and robust behavioral effects. We assessed the effects of combined MS-LBN on four processes linked to both ELA and behavioral disorders in later stages of life: incentive salience of reward cues, and impulsive choice, action, and persistence/perseverance.MethodsSixteen male Sprague Dawley rats were divided in groups of combined MS-LBN during postnatal days 2–21 and without ELA. They were exposed to an Autoshaping Pavlovian conditioning task (AUT), a delay-discounting task (DDT), and acquisition and extinction of a multiple schedule of reinforcement with long and short Variable Intervals (VI) across a span of 18 weeks.ResultsCompared to the No-ELA group, ELA rats displayed higher goal-tracking during the AUT (higher nose-poking in the food-delivery location), higher impulsive choice during test and 7-week re-test of the DDT (preference for the smaller-sooner reinforcer over the larger-later), and less efficient responding during the long-interval schedule of reinforcement (more unnecessary responses per pellet). Associations between autoshaping and reinforcement-schedule performance were identified, with ELA moderating the relationship between sign tracking and efficiency during training and persistence/perseverance during extinction.ConclusionsCombined MS-LBN was associated with task-specific changes in reward-related and impulsive behavior in male rats. These complex patterns of disruptions across behavioral processes adds further support to the notion that combined ELA protocols are promising robust models of adverse rearing in humans, which often entails multiple stressors.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12868-026-01018-1.Keywords: Early-life adversity, Maternal separation, Limited bedding and nesting, Autoshaping, Goal-tracking, Sign-tracking, Delay discounting, Impulsive behavior, Impulsive action, Behavioral persistence/perseverance

Introduction

Early-life adverse experiences, including neglect, abuse, and household dysfunction, are associated with greater susceptibility to developing a variety of health disorders (e.g., depression, anxiety, metabolic abnormalities), which often emerge during adolescence and persist into adulthood [1–4]. The high prevalence of childhood adverse events (57% of U.S. adults report experiencing at least one of them [5]) underscores the need to identify the behavioral and neurobiological mechanisms through which early adversity exerts its long-term impact on health and disease vulnerability [6]. This knowledge is crucial for the development of targeted and effective preventive and therapeutic interventions.

Rodent models of early-life adversity: Limited bedding/nesting and maternal separation

Ethical constraints on conducting studies on early-life adversity (ELA) with humans have highlighted the value of reliable and robust animal models that researchers can utilize to identify relevant behavioral and neurobiological processes [7, 8]. Limited bedding/nesting (LBN) and maternal separation (MS) are two widely implemented rodent models of early-life adversity. The LBN protocol entails housing dams in a wire mesh floored cage with no bedding and a scarcity of material necessary to make a nest [9–12]. This manipulation increases maternal anxiety and disrupt caregiving, and it is associated with elevated anxiety- and depressive-like behavior during adulthood [13]. LBN produces lasting transcriptional reprogramming/alterations within reward-related regions such as the ventral tegmental area (VTA) [14]. LBN also causes alteration of reward-circuit function through CRH-related signaling within the basolateral amygdala (BLA) [15], and through stress-induced plasticity in CRH/GABA projections linking the BLA with the nucleus accumbens, a pathway implicated in reward dysregulation under stress [16]. Together, these adaptations in the stress–reward circuitry may increase vulnerability to social stress and depressive-like states [15]. In addition, LBN produces morphological changes in the dorsal hippocampus during adolescence, including reduced volume and disrupted dendritic structure, consistent with altered local connectivity [8].

In the MS paradigm, pups are removed from their mother starting on postnatal day 2 for 3-hr daily sessions. This daily manipulation continues until animals are weaned, typically in the second-to-third postnatal week [17–19]. Similar to the LBN procedure, MS increases anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors in adult rodents [13, 20]. MS is also associated with long-term dysregulation of hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis function, including alterations in CRH, ACTH, and corticosterone levels, as well as sustained decreases in hippocampal glucocorticoid receptor expression into adulthood [15]. MS has additionally been linked to reduced adult neurogenesis and altered dendritic morphology of granule cells of the dentate gyrus [21].

Despite wide implementation and sizeable evidence of translational validity, studies with rats and mice have shown considerable variability in the behavioral results produced by LBN and MS protocols (e.g., unreliable reproduction of increments in anxiety- or depressive-related responses across studies [22–25]). Heterogenous methodology seems a major contributing factor to the inconsistent outcomes of LBN and MS across laboratories, including inconsistencies in sex, genetic background, timing and duration of the ELA events, housing and testing conditions, and dam experience prior to breeding [13, 22, 23]. Concern for these inconsistencies, together with the notion that adverse rearing in humans often entails multiple stressors (e.g., abuse, neglect, household chaos, and lack of resources), has resulted in interest in implementing combinations of ELA protocols. Combining MS and LBN shows promise, as it seems to produce consistent and strong anxiety- and depression-related behavioral and physiological effects [14, 15, 25–27]. Importantly, combined ELA also produces reliable neurobiological effects, including increased microglial size/density and reduced oligodendrocyte density, consistent with enduring neuroimmune and myelination-related alterations [22].

These detrimental effects of combined MS and LBN have been interpreted as the result of complementary roles of the two manipulations, which entail a jointly increase of the challenging conditions imposed to both the dam and the offspring. For instance, the pattern of maternal care post-MS reunion could be fragmented by the necessity of the dam to locate resources during LBN conditions, which increases the frequency of exits from the nest [25].

ELA and incentive salience of reward cues

Mounting evidence points to high incentive salience of reward cues as a strong correlate of substance use disorders [28]. Incentive salience has been defined as the “process by which the perception of stimuli [associated with rewards] is transformed, giving the stimuli meaning and salience, and therefore making them more attractive” [29]. Pavlovian conditioning is a robust model for the understanding of the incentive function of reward-related cues and their role in behavioral disorders, including substance abuse [30, 31]. Reward cues typically elicit approach, are rewarding per se as conditioned reinforcers, and increase reward-related responses, including reward-seeking and consumption.

The Autoshaping (AUT) paradigm is a widely employed Pavlovian conditioning procedure to assess the incentive value attributed to reward-predictive cues. In this task, rats undergo repeated pairings of a conditioned stimulus (CS), typically the extension of a lever in an operant chamber, with an unconditioned stimulus (US), such as food delivery. Over successive trials, distinct conditioned response (CR) patterns emerge, i.e., some animals preferentially interact with the lever (sign-tracking; ST), whereas others focus on the site of reward delivery (goal-tracking; GT) [32]. A smaller subset of animals often exhibits no clear bias and alternates between both responses [31].

High incentive salience of reward cues is a behavioral phenotype particularly relevant for modeling substance abuse. Sign-trackers exhibit stronger cue reactivity to stimuli associated with nicotine, cocaine, and opioids compared with goal-trackers [33–37]. Early studies demonstrated that removal of a cocaine-paired CS reduced self-administration in sign-trackers and that they showed cue-induced reinstatement of drug seeking (“relapse”) [38–41]. However, more recent work has yielded mixed findings, reporting both sign- and goal-tracking patterns during abstinence or reinstatement phases [42, 43].

Studies in humans show a link between ELA, heightened incentive salience of reward cues, and negative health outcomes. DeMartini et al. [29] reported that childhood emotional abuse or neglect, together with a family history of alcohol dependence, correlated with both increased incentive salience and alcohol use disorders. Similarly, rodent studies support this association. Lomanowska et al. [44] found that social isolation and artificial rearing (pup separation from dams on postnatal day five and isolation until adulthood) produced higher incentive salience to reward cues than normal maternal rearing. Beckmann and Bardo [45] applied an ELA protocol after weaning (postnatal day 21–51) combining social isolation and environmental impoverishment (small hanging cages, no novel objects). ELA rats mainly showed ST responses, whereas rats exposed to enrichment showed GT responses. Hynes et al. [46] used a complex ELA protocol (single housing, food restriction, forced-swim, predator odor; postnatal day 21–35) and observed more ST and fewer GT responses in adults. In contrast, Fuentes et al. [47] only used a LBN protocol (postnatal day 2–9) and found no effect on ST or GT responses of male and female rats.

Overall, the reviewed evidence indicates that ELA increases incentive salience of reward cues, particularly when multiple stressors are combined. However, methodological heterogeneity in ELA protocols, such as timing, duration, and type, may explain the null effects in Fuentes et al. [47], and limit conclusions about which specific conditions drive this outcome.

ELA and impulsive choice, action, and persistent/perseverative behavior

Impulsive behavior has been identified as a key mechanism linking adverse rearing environments and increased vulnerability to behavioral disorders such as substance abuse and pathological gambling [48, 49]. Impulsivity is a multifaceted construct encompassing impulsive choice (preference for smaller, immediate rewards over larger, delayed ones), impulsive action (premature or inefficient responding), and perseveration (repetitive, inappropriate responding when reinforcement is no longer available) [50, 51]. Distinct forms of impulsivity are differentially associated with psychopathology, for example, pathological gambling is primarily related to impulsive choice [52], while its severity may be influenced by impulsive action [53].

Delay discounting (DD) provides a robust conceptual and methodological framework for understanding impulsive choice and guiding intervention development [54–56]. DD refers to the devaluation of behavioral outcomes as they become temporally distant [57]; thus, steeper discounting entails higher impulsive behavior, characterized by a preference for smaller-sooner (SS) over larger-later (LL) rewards. Because of its consistent association with maladaptive behaviors and psychiatric disorders, DD has been described as a trans-disease process, predicting poor health outcomes such as smoking, risky sexual behavior, gambling, sedentarism, and limited treatment success for substance use [58, 59]. In rodent DD tasks, individuals choose between two alternatives across multiple trial blocks and sessions [60]. One option delivers a SS reward (e.g., a single food pellet immediately after lever pressing), while the other provides a LL reward (e.g., three pellets after a delay). Preference for SS versus LL options is analyzed across progressively increasing delays imposed on LL delivery (e.g., 0, 4, 8, 12, and 16 s), with SS choices reflecting impulsive responding and LL choices indicating self-control [61].

Despite decades of research in the link between DD and several phenomena [62], interest in the relationship between ELA and DD is recent [51]. The few studies conducted with rats indicate an association, but the findings are inconsistent across types of ELA and sex. MS and social isolation [63] and LBN alone [64] reduced DD (i.e., fewer impulsive responses) in male but not female rats. In another study, female, but not male rats, exposed to a surrogate mother and a variation of a LBN protocol showed lower DD [65]. In contrast, one study found that MS increased DD of both male and female rats, but there was no direct comparison by sex [66]. These inconsistent findings seem related to highly heterogeneous methodology, including major variations in ELA manipulations and DD protocols (e.g., different delays implemented, timing in postnatal days and duration of the training, etc.).

Excessive, inefficient, ineffective, mistimed, and/or premature responding, or difficulties inhibiting responding are the defining aspects of impulsive action [51, 67]. It has been investigated in animal models using various tasks, including the five-choice serial reaction time [68], reinforcement schedules that target rates or responding (e.g., differential reinforcement of low rates, DRL [69]) or time discrimination (e.g., interval schedules [70]), and the elevated plus maze [70, 71]. There is limited evidence that ELA affects impulsive action. Rats exposed to complex early stressors (e.g., restraint, forced swim, food deprivation, reversed light-dark cycle) displayed more premature responses in the five-choice serial reaction time task than controls in males and females [72], and similar effects were found in males after MS in the same task (no females were tested) [73]. Combined ELA protocols involving MS, artificial rearing, and low-simulated grooming likewise increased premature responding in both sexes under schedules of differential reinforcement of low rates (DRL) schedules [63].

Interest in the impact of ELA on perseverative, persistent, and compulsive-like behaviors associated with adverse health outcomes in adulthood has been also limited, but some recent studies suggest there is a link [50, 74]. Levis et al. [75] showed that rats exposed to LBN displayed greater resistance to extinction and enhanced cue-induced reinstatement of heroin seeking during lever-pressing procedures, indicating heightened behavioral persistence relative to controls. Similarly, Brydges et al. [50] reported increased perseverative nose-poking in rats subjected to a “brief, variable prepubertal stress” (p. 301), protocol involving swim, restraint, and footshocks, with effects observed primarily in females during a choice task in which responding no longer produced reinforcement. Notably, this same ELA manipulation did not alter impulsive choice in a delay-discounting task for either sex [50], further highlighting task-specific and protocol-dependent ELA effects. Extending these findings, Sneddon et al. [76] demonstrated that an acute ELA manipulation, i.e., 15 footshocks administered to mice on postnatal day 17, produced increased resistance to extinction of conditioned fear responses in adulthood, although it did not influence behavioral persistence during changes to reinforcement contingencies. The authors proposed that discrepancies across studies may arise because acute and chronic ELA protocols, such as footshock versus MS or LBN, “alter neural circuits in unique ways” (p. 15).

Evidence from research with humans shows promise for the translational validity of these findings on ELA and perseverative/persistent behavior. Franco and Knowlton [77] observed that childhood emotional neglect and sexual abuse predicted greater behavioral persistence in a probabilistic learning task, whereas emotional abuse reduced perseverance. Moreover, physical neglect correlated with response persistence and alcohol use, suggesting that perseverative tendencies constitute another behavioral mechanism linking ELA to mental health disorders in adulthood.

The present study

Extensive research with rodent models has provided evidence of the detrimental effects of ELA on behavioral and neurobiological processes. However, heterogeneous methodologies, including types and variations of ELA manipulations and behavioral tasks and measures could be responsible for inconsistent findings across studies. Recent evidence shows promise that a combination of two widely implemented ELA protocols (MS and LBN) produce consistent and robust effects due to the complementary roles of the two manipulations, which jointly increase the challenging conditions imposed on both the dam and the offspring. This approach also better resembles the fact that humans often experience more than one form early childhood adversity [5].

The present study aimed to evaluate the effects of a combined MS–LBN protocol on four processes associated with ELA and behavioral disorders later in life: incentive salience of reward cues, and three facets of impulsive behavior, i.e., choice, action, and persistence/perseverance. These processes were selected due to differential and inconsistent evidence of their susceptibility to specific ELA manipulations (e.g., conflicting effects on DD) and the absence of systematic testing of effects produced by combined ELA on all these outcomes.

Incentive salience was assessed with an Autoshaping Pavlovian conditioning task (AUT). Impulsive choice was assessed using a delay-discounting task (DDT). Impulsive action was assessed with schedules of reinforcement with long and short variable intervals (VI15s-VI60s), and persistence/perseverance was assessed with the introduction of extinction (EXT) after steady performance in the variable-interval schedules was reached. The choice of these tasks was strategic, as they allowed assessing all behaviors within the same cohort of animals using related operant protocols well established in our lab [70, 78, 79]. Lever-pressing acquisition through AUT could be easily transferred to the delay-discounting task and variable-interval schedules using the same operant chambers and stimuli. Variable-Interval schedule performance was assumed as a measure of impulsive-action-related response dysregulation not equivalent to premature responding or DRL-based response withholding. Unlike DRL schedules, which require withholding responses for a specified duration, VI schedules assess how efficiently animals regulate response output when reinforcement becomes available after variable intervals and only one response is required to produce the reinforcer. Thus, our responses-per-pellet measure is best interpreted as an index of response inefficiency or excessive responding under time-based reinforcement conditions. This approach had the advantage of preventing adding other potentially confounding contingencies, including possible aversive factors entailed in DRL schedules. Lastly, the use of a single cohort of animals allowed us to examine interrelations among behavioral measures and their modulation by ELA.

Based on previous research in humans and rodent models, the combination of MS-LBN was expected to result in consistent detrimental effects across the different measures; namely, higher incentive salience of reward cues (i.e., higher sign-tracking), impulsive choice (i.e., higher DD), impulsive action (i.e., higher inefficient responding during time-based schedules of reinforcement), and perseverance/persistence (i.e., higher resistance to extinction). Moreover, the expected relationships between these measures (e.g., incentive salience and perseverance, impulsive choice and action) were predicted to have been also impacted by the combined ELA manipulation.

Materials and methods

Subjects

Four pregnant Sprague Dawley rat dams were obtained from a commercial vendor (Inotiv, Indianapolis, US). Rats were kept on a 12 h light/dark cycle during the entirety of the experiment (lights on 7:00 A.M. to 7:00 P.M.). Upon giving birth, two of those four dams and corresponding pup litters were randomly selected to receive the combined maternal separation (MS) and limited bedding/nesting (LBN) early-life adversity (ELA) protocol during the first 21 postnatal days (pre-weaning). More details on those ELA protocols are provided in Procedure section below.

Litters were weaned at postnatal day 21. Sixteen males were randomly chosen and housed individually starting on postnatal day 45, eight across the non-ELA litters and eight across the ELA litters. Animals were singled housed for weight monitoring and control. All experimental protocols described below were implemented on those 16 males, with ELA group N = 8 and No-ELA group N = 8. Average effect size (ηp2) and statistical power for this sample were 0.34 and 0.50, respectively. During behavioral testing, animals were maintained at 85% of their free-feeding weight and had free access to water. Supplementary Fig. 5 shows details on weights and amount of food consumed throughout the experiment. At the end of the study, rats were euthanized via carbon dioxide (CO2) inhalation.

All experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the US National Institute of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by IACUC at Southern Illinois University.

Behavioral tests

Eight operant conditioning chambers (MED Associates® Model ENV 508) each measured 25 cm long by 32 cm wide by 25 cm high, were located within sound-attenuating enclosures. The floor was a stainless-steel grid comprised of thirty-six 2 mm diameter rods. Each chamber had two retractable response levers located 5.8 cm. above the floor, on the front wall. Each lever was 4.5 cm wide and required an inverted force of approximately 0.2 N to operate. Two yellow LED lights were installed 11 cm above the levers. A 28-V DC house light provided ambient illumination centered on the back wall at 3.0 cm below the ceiling. A pellet receptacle (feeder) was in the center of the front wall, 2.5 cm above the floor, and received, according to the scheduled delivery (see details in experimental conditions below), 45 mg food pellets (Bio-Serv, F0021) from a modular pellet dispenser.

Dams and litters randomly selected to receive ELA were exposed to a protocol of Limited Bedding and Nesting (LBN) during postnatal days 2–9 and Maternal Separation (MS) during postnatal days 2–21.

Limited bedding/nesting protocol (LBN)

The LBN protocol replicated Orso et al.’s [25] and Strzelewicz et al.’s [12]. Dams and their pups were housed in cages with a wired mesh floor with no bedding and scarcity of material necessary to build a nest (a single paper towel sheet). The mesh size was 0.65 cm by 0.65 cm squares, which prevented the animals from getting stuck. Dams and pups remained in these conditions for eight days, starting on postnatal day two.

Maternal separation protocol (MS)

The MS protocol replicated Biagini et al.’s [17] and Muhammad and Kolb’s [18]. Pups were removed from their mother starting on postnatal day 2 for daily sessions of 3 h., which were scheduled at the same time of the day. Each pup was individually placed in a standard mouse cage (29.21 cm x 15.75 cm x 12.7 cm) for the scheduled 3-hr period. It was warmed with a heating pad set at a constant temperature of 32 °C, and no bedding was provided in it. All the cages with pups were placed in the same room, while the dam was kept in a separate room.

We used a combination of maternal separation (MS) and limited bedding-and-nesting (LBN) protocols as our model of early-life adversity (ELA). This multi-domain adversity has been reported to produce robust dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, epigenetic modifications [80, 81] in plasticity-related genes such as Bdnf, and neuroinflammatory and myelination changes in key neural circuits [82]. These physiological alterations are accompanied by consistent increases in anxiety-like behavior and by changes in impulsive choice and reward-cue processing from adolescence into adulthood. Despite this evidence, here it was not assumed that the combination of MS + LBN would necessarily produce additive or more severe effects than either manipulation alone, e.g., maternal care following MS reunions may mitigate some stress effects, whereas LBN may alter the quality or patterning of maternal care [11, 25]. Investigating these mechanisms was beyond the scope of the present study and should be addressed in future research.

The 16 animals of both groups (ELA and No-ELA) were simultaneously exposed to the same sequence of behavioral tasks, namely Autoshaping of Lever Pressing (AUT) -> Delay Discounting Task -> Multiple Schedule of Reinforcement VI15s – VI60s -> Extinction of VI15s-VI60s -> Re-Test Delay Discounting Task. This sequence was implemented to account for possible confounding effects of other behavioral training on development of Sign-tracking or Goal-tracking and possible effect of Multiple Schedule training on Delay Discounting (Fig. 1).

Sequence of the behavioral tasks to which all rats were exposed and corresponding postnatal weeks and number of daily sessions. (1) During postnatal week 0–3, combined ELA (MS-LBN) was implemented on the group of rats assigned to this condition (N = 8). No-ELA rats had normal rearing. (2) During postnatal week 11, 6 sessions of Autoshaping Pavlovian task were implemented in all rats. (3) During posnatal week 14–21, all rats received 32 sessions of the delay discounting task (DDT). (4) During postnatal week 21–24, all rats were exposed to sessions of a multiple schedule of reinforcement VI15s-VI60s until reaching steady performance. (5) During postnatal week 24–25, all rats received 10 sessions of extinction of the multiple schedule VI15s-VI60s. (6) During postanatal week 27–29, all rats were exposed to a re-test of the DDT, which lasted 14 sessions

Autoshaping of lever pressing (AUT)

To assess the attribution of incentive salience of reward cues we used an AUT procedure based on Myer and Hull’s [83] report. Each trial started with the extension of a lever (Conditioned Stimulus – CS+). After 10 s, the lever was retracted, the feeder light was turned on, and 4 pellets were delivered across 1 s (Unconditioned Stimulus – US). Each trial was separated from the next with a blackout intertrial interval (ITI) averaging 35–45 s (see Supplementary Fig. 1 for a schematic representation and details). A total of 20 trials were scheduled in each daily session. Animals were exposed to 6 daily sessions (postnatal day 74–79). Interaction with the lever within the 10 s CS+ trial was classified as sign-tracking (ST) whereas interaction with the food receptacle was classified as goal-tracking (GT). Number of ST responses was used as an indicator of increased incentive salience of the reward cue.

Delay discounting task (DDT)

We used a DDT procedure that replicated Liley et al.’s [61] to assess impulsive choice. As illustrated in Fig. 2, one lever delivered 1 pellet immediately (Smaller-Sooner option - SS) and the other lever delivered 3 pellets after different delays (Larger-Later option - LL). Each daily session consisted of 6 blocks of two forced trials and 10 free-choice trials with different delays to the LL option (i.e., 4, 8, 12, 16 s, 0-s delay to LL reward). The sixth and last block, hereon labeled as 0 C, served as control for rats’ discrimination and preference for the larger reward [61].

Diagram of the delay discounting task (DDT) adapted from Liley et al. [61]. One lever delivered 1 pellet immediately (Smaller-Sooner option - SS) and the other lever delivered 3 pellets after different delays (Larger-Later option - LL). Daily sessions consisted of 6 blocks of 12 trials each. The first block had 0-s delay to the LL reward. The following four blocks increased the LL delay by 4 s each (i.e., 4, 8, 12, 16-s delay to LL reward). The sixth and last block (labeled 0 C s) had no delay (i.e., 0s to LL reward) and served as control for rats’ discrimination and preference for the larger reward [61]. Each trial started with illumination of the house-light and food dispenser (food through). If the rat entered (nose poke) the dispenser within 10-s of the start of the trial, both stimuli were turned off and one lever (forced trial) or two levers (free-choice trial) were extended. If the rat failed to nose-poke within 10-s, the trial was counted as an omission. After the pellet delivery, the house-light was turned off and an intertrial interval (ITI) of 10 s (± 2-s range) separated the trials

The position of SS and LL was kept constant for each animal across all the sessions but was counterbalanced across subjects. Subjects were trained until achieving a steady choice defined as consistent percentage of responses on LL across 5 sessions. Stability was confirmed with a repeated-measures day-by-block ANOVA over the last 5 days of behavior, replicating Liley et al.’s [61] procedure (more details in Data Analysis section below). The percentage of choices on the LL alternative was used as the main measure of impulsive behavior, with lower preference (lower percentages) for the delayed reward (LL) as indicative of elevated impulsive behavior.

Multiple schedule of reinforcement and response perseverance/persistence under extinction

Impulsive action and perseverance/persistence were assessed using a Multiple Schedule and extinction procedure based on Nevin’s [84]. All rats were trained to respond to two different variable-interval (VI) schedules of reinforcement (i.e., components), namely VI15s and VI60s. The subject could obtain one food pellet by pressing a lever on average after 15 s (VI15s) or 60 s (in the VI60s) elapsed since the last reinforcer was delivered. Each component lasted 60 s and was associated with either a flashing or a constant houselight. Each cycle of the multiple schedule consisted of the alternation of the two components plus 30 s intercomponent interval (ICI), for a total of 150 s. The duration of each daily session was limited to a maximum number of 24 cycles or 60 min. The rate of responses per pellet was used as a measure of impulsive action, since only one response was necessary to produce each programmed pellet. In other words, more unnecessary responses = more impulsive action. Responses per pellet were used as an index of interval-schedule response efficiency. Because only one response was required to produce each programmed reinforcer once the interval had elapsed, higher responses-per-pellet values indicated excessive or inefficient responding. We interpreted this outcome as reflecting response-rate dysregulation under interval schedules, an impulsive-action-related measure, rather than as a direct measure of temporal withholding such as that assessed by DRL schedules. Each rat was assessed separately for steady lever-pressing on both component schedules. The average number of lever presses per component across the last five sessions was calculated daily for each animal. If the number of lever presses of each of the five days remained within ± 5% of the average responding was considered steady [85].

Once rats achieved steady responding on both schedule components, 10 extinction (EXT) sessions were conducted. During EXT all aspects of the task remained the same, except reinforcement was interrupted on both schedules. This manipulation allowed us to test rats’ response persistence/perseverance during extinction of the reinforcement. See schematic representation and details of the multiple-schedule and extinction conditions in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were implemented using IBM SPSS 24® and GraphPad Prism 8.01® and employed an alpha level of .05. Effect-size indices were calculated and reported when applicable (Cohen’s d and ηp2).

Autoshaping pavlovian conditioning (AUT)

Average number of Sign-Tracking (ST) and Goal-Tracking (GT) responses per trial across the last three sessions of the AUT were calculated for each subject. One-Way ANOVA tests were conducted on these measures comparing ELA and No-ELA groups of rats. Levene’s test for equality of variances showed that measurements fulfilled this assumption (ps > .64).

Delay discounting task (DDT) test and re-test

Data analyses replicated those reported by Liley et al. [61]. The percentage of trials in which each rat chose the large-later option (LL) was calculated for each of the 6 blocks of trials (0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 0 C s delays) and for each daily session by dividing the total number of choices of the LL lever by the total number of choice trials completed (excluding omissions). Steady choice of the LL option was tested using a Day (1–5 days) X Delay Block (0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 0 C s) ANOVA across the final 5 days of testing and was defined as follows: (1) lack of main effect of day, and (2) lack of significant Day x Delay Block interaction. The average percentage of LL choice for each block of trials across these five days of stability was calculated. Omissions were excluded from the calculation, as they remained below 1 per session. ELA and No-ELA groups were compared across the DDT test and re-test using a Group (ELA vs. No-ELA) X Phase (Test vs. Retest) X Delay Block (0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 0 C s delays for the DDT test and retest) mixed ANOVA. Any violations of Mauchly’s test of sphericity were taken into account and statistical reporting was based on Greenhouse–Geisser values, with degrees of freedom adjusted accordingly. When applicable, post hoc analyses were performed using paired multiple comparisons with the Bonferroni adjustment.

Multiple schedule VI15s-VI60s

Efficiency of each animal during the multiple schedule of reinforcement was calculated for each component schedule (VI15s and VI60s) per daily session. It was calculated by dividing the total number of responses displayed per component in each session over the total number of pellets obtained per component in that same session, i.e., ratio of the number of responses per reinforcer per component. The assumption of this calculation is that VI schedules entail that only one response is required to produce the reinforcer on each trial [70]; e.g., in a VI15s, a reinforcer is delivered in a given trial if the rat presses the lever once after an average of 15 s have elapsed since the last reinforcer was delivered. Accordingly, higher number of responses per pellet were indicative of more unnecessary responses, i.e., higher impulsive action. For instance, an efficiency score of 20 indicated that the rat on average lever pressed 20 times more than necessary to obtain each reinforcer. A mixed ANOVA was conducted on mean responses per pellet (efficiency), with Group as a between-subjects factor and Session (last 10 sessions) and reinforcement schedule value (VI 15 s vs. VI 60 s) as within-subjects factors. Levene’s tests indicated that the assumption of homogeneity of variance was met for all between-subject comparisons (ps > .05). Mauchly’s test indicated that the assumption of sphericity was fulfilled for effects involving Session; therefore, Greenhouse–Geisser–corrected degrees of freedom are reported for those effects.

Extinction of VI15s-VI60s

The average number of lever presses per each of the two components of the multiple schedule (VI15s and VI60s) during the last five sessions of training (when steady responding was achieved) was used as baseline for comparison with performance during extinction. The average number of lever presses during each schedule and each of the 10 sessions of extinction was also calculated. A proportion-to-baseline calculation per session of extinction and per component schedule (VI15s and VI60s) was obtained by dividing the average number of lever presses per each component over the corresponding baseline, e.g., if a rat displayed during the VI15s baseline sessions an average of 20 responses per trial and during the first session of VI15s extinction displayed 10 responses per trial, the proportion to baseline for that first session of extinction would have been equal to 10/20 = 0.20.

ELA and No-ELA groups were compared in their proportion to baseline only during the first five sessions of extinction to maximize statistical power and based on visual analyses of graphed data, which indicated no changes were evident after session five. A repeated-measures Mixed ANOVA was conducted on VI15s and VI60s proportion-to-baseline data with Group (ELA vs. No ELA) as the between-subjects factor and Session (1–5) as the within-subjects factor. Any violations of Mauchly’s test of sphericity were taken into account and statistical reporting was adjusted by using Greenhouse–Geisser values, with degrees of freedom adjusted accordingly. Post hoc analyses were performed using paired multiple comparisons with the Bonferroni adjustment.

Associations between measures of the behavioral tasks and moderation effect of ELA

Pearson correlations were calculated across measures of the different tasks, i.e., number of sign-tracking and goal-tracking responses for AUT, percentage of LL responding on each delay of test and re-test for DDT, and efficiency and extinction of VI15s and VI60s schedules of reinforcement. Analysis of resulting data indicated a pattern of associations only between performance during AUT and multiple-schedule efficiency and extinction. See correlation matrix in Supplementary Tables 1 and heatmap in Supplementary Fig. 4. Accordingly, further analyses only focused on those related measures via regression-based moderations [86] fitted on AUT variables (number of sign-tracking and goal-tracking responses per trial), and efficiency and extinction of VI15s and VI60s schedules of reinforcement comparing ELA and No-ELA groups of rats. These methods have well-documented suitability to test hypotheses about mechanisms and contingencies of effects when direct experimentation is not available, including testing under what circumstances a given association exists and/or in what magnitude [87]. Our primary assumption was that a history of ELA could modulate a known link between motivational reward-related variables (e.g., sign-tracking and goal-tracking) and operant interval-schedule performance (e.g., efficiency and extinction) [88, 89].

Resulting data (graphical and statistical) were assessed via visual inspection of scatterplots, R2 calculations, and 95% confidence limits [90]. A moderation effect of ELA on the relation between sign-tracking and goal tracking and efficiency and extinction of VI15s and VI60s was tested using Hayes’ [87] PROCESS procedure for SPSS with Model 1, 5000 bootstrap resamples, and 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (CI). Confidence intervals that were entirely above or below zero (i.e., did not include zero) were considered evidence of significant regressions and moderations [86]. Outliers were identified with GraphPad Prism’s built-in Rout method set at Q = 1% [90]. Additionally, Mahalanobis distance values were examined to detect multivariate outliers among the four variables entered in the moderation models. Using a chi-square critical value of χ²(4) = 18.47, no cases exceeded the cutoff, indicating the absence of multivariate outliers in the dataset. The highest Mahalanobis value observed was 13.34.

Discussion

Extensive research with rodent models has shown detrimental effects of ELA on behavioral and neurobiological processes. However, inconsistent use of types and variations of ELA manipulations seem to have contributed to unreliable findings across studies. Recent research indicates that a combination of MS and LBN produces consistent and robust neurobehavioral effects. The present study tested the effects of such combined protocol on four processes linked to both ELA and behavioral disorders in later stages of life, namely incentive salience of reward cues, impulsive choice, impulsive action, and persistence/perseverance.

As compared to the No ELA group, rats with a history of combined ELA displayed higher number of goal-tracking responses during the Autoshaping Pavlovian task, higher impulsive choice during test and retest of the delay discounting task, and less efficient responding during the long-interval schedule of reinforcement (VI60s). No direct effects of ELA on resistance to extinction of lever pressing (i.e., response persistence/perseverance) were found. Associations between performance during the autoshaping task and the training and extinction of the reinforcement schedules were found (ST and GT with VI15s efficiency and ST with extinction of VI15s and VI60s). No other associations between measures were identified. Further analysis indicated that the relation between ST and efficiency and resistance to extinction of lever pressing was moderated by ELA. Overall, our data suggest that ELA attenuated the association between ST and efficiency observed in No-ELA rats, inverted the relationship between ST and extinction of lever pressing observed in No-ELA rats, and induced a positive relationship between GT and extinction of lever pressing, which was also not observed in No-ELA rats.

Research with rodents indicates that a variety of social factors combined with other forms of adverse events increase ST but not GT [44–46]. Our finding does not align with this previous literature but still adds evidence to the overall conclusion that ELA affects behavioral processes involved in an Autoshaping Pavlovian task. Compared to ST, GT behavior has received less attention. For both sign trackers and goal trackers, reward cues (e.g., lever extension signaling food) acquire predictive value and elicit a conditioned response, but only for sign trackers do they also acquire incentive value (i.e., the reward cue becomes an incentive/motivational stimulus). This seems to explain why, relative to rats with GT preference, sign-tracking rats also show greater motivation to work for cocaine and show greater drug-seeking behavior during tests for cue- and cocaine-induced reinstatement [30].

Methodological heterogeneity provides an alternative and perhaps supplementary interpretation of the effect of ELA on GT and not ST, as would have been expected from previous studies with positive [44–46] or negative [47] findings. Comparison of the protocols reported in those studies and the present shows variations in some aspects, including breed (Long Evans and Sprague Dawley), postnatal day in which training begun [91], pre-exposure to the rewards and food hopper/feeder (e.g., pellets given in home cages days before training started, different number of sessions and procedures for magazine/food hopper training, etc.), previous experience with other behavioral protocols (e.g., Hynes et al., [46] randomly scheduled the sequence of five behavioral tasks, including autoshaping) and specifics of the apparatus and stimuli (e.g., configuration of the conditioned stimulus, e.g., a light turned on additional to extension of the lever [47].

Altogether, the ELA-related increase in goal-tracking should be interpreted cautiously. In Autoshaping procedures, sign-tracking and goal-tracking represent different conditioned response patterns, but sign-tracking is typically the phenotype most directly linked to attribution of incentive salience to reward-predictive cues [32, 35–39]. Therefore, the present goal-tracking effect could not be interpreted as definitive evidence that combined MS-LBN increased incentive salience attribution to the lever or other cues. Rather, it may reflect greater reward-location-directed conditioned responding under the specific conditions of the present autoshaping task. This cautious interpretation is consistent with the heterogeneous findings reported in prior ELA studies, such as, early social deprivation, environmental impoverishment, or complex ELA protocols have been associated with changes in sign-tracking or incentive-salience-related responding, whereas LBN alone did not reliably alter sign- or goal-tracking responses [44–47]. Given the sample size, male-only cohort, and fixed task sequence, this result could have been the result of task, cohort, or context-specific features.

Interest in the relationship between ELA and DD is recent [51]. The few studies conducted with rats to date indicate an association, but the findings are inconsistent across types of ELA and sex. Our finding that combined ELA increased DD adds support to the overall conclusion that ELA indeed affects impulsive choice, in this case, increasing it, but future replications comparing males and females are needed. Moreover, the present was the first attempt to test the consistency and long-term effect of ELA. The observed maintenance of the detrimental effect after seven weeks suggests it is robust and consistent, which shows promise for future developmental studies on lasting neurobiological and behavioral effects of ELA.

The quality of the affectation identified in the present study, i.e., impairment in self-control choice, seems more aligned with the findings of Gondre-Lewis et al. [66] than those from the other studies that reported improvements in self-control with ELA manipulations [63–65]. More importantly, our result reproduces the overall finding reported in mounting research with humans; namely, that childhood adversity is strongly and consistently associated with higher impulsive choice in later stages of development [3, 48, 92–97]. Also, it provides support to Ordones and Bangasser’s [51] interpretation of the opposite effect of ELA on impulsive choice when comparing humans and rodents exposed to DD tasks. In their review of the relevant literature, the authors argue that research with rodent models of ELA have utilized only one specific adverse event manipulation (e.g., MS or LBN), but most human individuals with a history of ELA have been exposed to multiple adverse events during childhood and adolescence. This interpretation adds to Orso et al.’s [25] conclusion that combined MS and LBN manipulations produce more consistent and robust effects. Also, it seems cohesive with the notion that some forms of trauma often experienced by children may not be fully captured by current rodent models of ELA (e.g., sexual and emotional trauma; [98, 99].

The finding that a combined ELA protocol produced less efficient responding during the long-interval schedule of reinforcement (VI60s) adds evidence of the relationship between ELA and impulsive action. It is also aligned with Lovic et al. [63] findings that female and male rats with a history of MS displayed more premature/ineffective responses than control rats under schedules of differential reinforcement of low rates (DRL). In DRL schedules, reinforcement is delivered only if a given time has passed since the last operant response was emitted. The fact that in the present study no effect was found in the short-interval schedule of reinforcement (VI15s) suggests that temporal parameters are relevant to this behavioral trait. Furthermore, it is aligned with the findings of other studies in which similar long-interval schedule performance (e.g., efficiency) consistently predicts impulsive traits in other behavioral tasks (e.g., elevated plus maze measures [70]). The use of two schedules of different intervals (i.e., multiple schedule), as in the present study, appears to be an effective approach to obtaining within-subject comparisons. Similarly, the use of schedules of reinforcement that are not fixed in the intervals at which rewards become available may be more sensitive in dissociating ELA effects on these behavioral traits as they introduce a degree of unpredictability across trials. Worth noting is that our procedure did not assess response withholding in the same way as DRL schedules. Instead, the VI60s effect is better interpreted as ELA rats emitted more responses per reinforcer under a longer interval schedule, suggesting less efficient response-rate regulation since additional premature responses did not increase programmed reinforcement. Thus, this outcome is best described as interval-schedule response inefficiency or impulsive-action-related response dysregulation.

In the present study, ELA did not show a main effect on resistance to extinction of lever pressing. Prior work has reported more persistent responding after specific ELA manipulations, for example, Boutros et al. [74] found increased perseverative nose poking after maternal separation when rewards were no longer available in the 5-choice serial reaction time task, and Levis et al. [75] reported greater resistance to extinction after LBN, when heroin-seeking lever presses no longer produced infusions (reinforcer withdrawal). However, procedural differences between those studies and the present one (e.g., reward type and behavioral assay) limit strong conclusions about whether combined ELA reliably increases perseverative/persistent behavior. Systematic comparisons across tasks and measures will be necessary to rule out task-specific confounds. More broadly, the presence of ELA effects on impulsive choice and action, but not on perseveration/persistence, supports the idea that these traits can be dissociable and differentially sensitive to ELA [50]. In addition, we interpret the ELA-related increase in goal-tracking as reflecting greater reliance on the reward outcome/location as a predictor, rather than increased incentive salience attribution to the cue itself. Consistent with the ST/GT literature, we avoid framing goal-tracking as inherently “adaptive” or “maladaptive,” and instead describe both phenotypes as strategies with potential tradeoffs: sign-tracking can be effective in some contexts but may become problematic when it is perseverative and extinction-resistant, whereas goal-tracking is typically more tightly coupled to the predictive CS–US contingency and reward delivery site.

Importantly, we were able to test relationships among measures and whether those relationships were moderated by ELA because the same animals completed all behavioral tasks. ELA attenuated the association between sign-tracking and efficiency observed in No-ELA rats; within the ELA group, higher sign-tracking tended to be associated with less perseverative/persistent responding, whereas higher goal-tracking tended to be associated with greater resistance to extinction. Taken together, these findings suggest that ELA-related differences in persistence may be expressed depending on conditioned approach style even in the absence of a uniform ELA effect on extinction. While prior work often links sign-tracking with greater extinction resistance than goal-tracking, our results suggest that ELA may reshape extinction dynamics by modulating phenotype-linked cue-control processes. This highlights the moderation effects as a novel and promising aspect of the present study.

Finally, a history of combined ELA may have affected interrelated learning and neurobiological processes involved in both the Autoshaping Pavlovian task and the multiple schedule of reinforcement acquisition and extinction. Autoshaping is a Pavlovian conditioning protocol in which individuals develop conditioned responses (CRs). Acquisition and extinction of lever-pressing under schedules of reinforcement involves both Pavlovian and Operant phenomena, the latter related to the regulation of behavior by rewarding and punishing consequences. Research on persistence of operant behavior has shown that resistance to disruption (e.g., extinction or punishment) depends on reward-related value of the context in which behavior occurs, and such value is supported by Pavlovian processes [100, 101]. Specifically, a Pavlovian relation between the stimuli in the context in which an operant behavior occurs and the rewards obtained in that context determines the resistance of that behavior to disruptors. Altogether, this account entails that the stimulus context–reward relation is strengthened through Pavlovian learning processes, which afford incentive salience of stimuli related to rewards (motivational value) to stimuli in the environment (i.e. contexts), and in turn determines the degree of operant behavior resistance to disruptors [102]. The associations in performance during the autoshaping and multiple schedule tasks observed in the present study are thus predicted from this approach considering the commonalities of Pavlovian and Operant learning processes involved.

It seems plausible that the observed moderation of associations between ST and GT and schedule performance, including efficiency and extinction, are part of the complex behavioral and neurobiological effects of the combined ELA manipulation. Dopaminergic transmission is highly involved in both Pavlovian-related changes in incentive salience of stimuli associated with rewards and persistence of operant behavior. For instance, increments in dopamine levels enhance stimulus–reward associations, which in turn predict perseverative cue-induced food-seeking behavior and relapse of extinguished behavior in rats [103]. Also, persistence of behavior is enhanced in a context associated with dopamine-receptor agonism [102].

Study Limitations and Future Directions

Exposing all animals to all the experimental tasks allowed to control within subjects the effects of ELA and identify associations between behavioral measures and moderation effects of ELA. However, it also introduced limitations in terms of possible carry-over effects. Autoshaping protocols, or variations of them, are often used to train lever pressing in preparation for other operant tasks, including delay-discounting tests (e.g [104–106]). However, it is unclear if aspects of such training, especially those temporal related (e.g., intertrial interval duration and randomization, duration of the CS, duration of the US, etc.) could affect the subjects’ choice when later exposed to different delays to the reward during a DDT. For instance, effective interventions to reduce impulsive choice and delay discounting based on timing and exposure to delay to rewards appear to entail Pavlovian processes [54, 55].

In turn, a history with the DDT could have affected the performance during the multiple schedule training and extinction, which ultimately could have confounded the effects of ELA (e.g., no direct effect of ELA on extinction was identified). Although an analogous concern could be raised regarding a history with the multiple schedule of reinforcement affecting the subsequent re-test of the DDT, our data suggests the detrimental effect of ELA was not disrupted by having those behavioral tasks introduced in between. Evidently, future replications that implement other designs to control for possible sequence effects are needed (e.g., different groups of animals exposed to each behavioral task and waitlisted animals exposed to only one task across different postnatal weeks to control for maturation effects). Hynes et al.’s [46] approach seems appropriate to this end since they exposed all their animals to five different behavioral tasks. They arranged for same-litter siblings to complete the behavioral assessments in the same order, and pseudo-randomized and counterbalanced order across litters to mitigate experiential carryover effects. Worth noting is the fact that Hynes et al. [46] did not report sequence effects, which shows promise for the findings reported in the present study to hold after controlling for potential interactions across tasks.

Related to the decision of exposing all animals to all behavioral tasks is the fact that the age at which animals were tested on each measure was dependent on the completion of previous tasks. This entails the effects of ELA reported here are limited to the specific ages and thus specific maturation stages. For instance, the effect of ELA on GT was detected on postnatal week 11, which corresponds to adolescence [107], and it is uncertain if the lack of effects on ST could be related to the timing of the testing. Delay-discounting testing took place on weeks 14 and 27, which entails two different stages (late adolescence and early adulthood; [107]). However, future research could systematically test for consistency in performance across other maturational stages, which has only been partially explored to date (e.g [78, 104, 105]), while testing for differential effects of sex, MS, LBN or their interaction. Ongoing efforts in our laboratory have started in this direction focusing on effects of separate and combined MS and LBN on delay discounting and comparing males and females. Preliminary data overall show reproduction of the effects on DD reported here only in males, which adds to the previous reports of differential effects of ELA on impulsive choice by sex [51]. More importantly, it appears that the detrimental effect of ELA on impulsive choice extends to the delay discounting of aversive stimuli (punishers), which are tasks that emulate human impulsive choice in risky-impulsive contexts, i.e., choice of a larger reward over a smaller reward is followed with different delays or probabilities of a punishing electric shock; [61].

Regarding the choice of a combination of MS and LBN as a model of complex ELA, it is important to acknowledge that there is evidence that MS increases post-MS maternal care, which produces long-term mitigating effects of MS on behavior and biological variables [25]. Our findings add to previous that suggest that the specific combination of MS and LBN produces detrimental effects due to the introduction of challenging conditions to both the mother and offspring [25]. There is evidence that LBN decreases the frequency of maternal care, producing fragmented and even erratic maternal behavior due to the mother’s need to seek resources for nest maintenance (e.g., increased frequency of nest departures) [11]. There is thus need for future research that use additional groups in which the behavioral and procedural tasks explored in this study are evaluated using groups with MS without LBN and LBN without MS. This could isolate the effects of each of the adverse factors and compare them with the findings of the combined protocol implemented here. In addition, future studies should aim to characterize the buffering effects that animals may experience after weaning via observations of the dam and pup behavior.

Statistical power estimations indicated that group size used in the present study, n = 8 per group, was appropriated for detecting large effects, but underpowered for small to moderate effects. Although this is a limitation, the study was designed as an initial test of whether a multidimensional ELA manipulation could affect several behavioral processes within the same cohort of animals. Accordingly, future studies should increase sample size to confirm the present findings, estimate moderate and small effects more precisely, and determine whether the observed outcomes are robust across independent cohorts.

Another limitation of the study was the inclusion of only male rats. This decision was guided by prior evidence of sex-specific ELA effects on delay discounting and related phenotypes (e.g., LBN-associated changes reported in males but not females, and early-stress effects in females that are more consistent with compulsive-like responding than impulsive choice) [50, 63]. Nonetheless, sex is an important moderator of the processes studied here. Impulsivity is commonly parsed into dissociable components, such as impulsive action (inhibitory control) and impulsive choice (preference for smaller-immediate over larger-delayed rewards), and these aspects show different sex and hormonal sensitivity [108]. In laboratory conditions, sex differences in impulsive behavior become more consistent when hormonal status is considered. Thus, some studies have shown that circulating gonadal hormones (including testosterone) are related with premature responding/inhibitory control [108]. In addition, robust sex differences have been demonstrated for impulsive choice, for example, females show greater preference for immediate rewards than males, an effect not explained by motivational or reward-sensitivity differences and not modulated by estrous cycle [109]. Moreover, orchiectomy shifts males toward a more female-typical impulsive-choice pattern, suggesting a role for testicular hormones in maintaining male-typical lower delay-discounting [109]. Taken together, these findings underscore that the magnitude and even direction of MS and LBN effects on impulsive behavior could differ by sex, potentially via sex-hormone–sensitive developmental and/or activation mechanisms.

Therefore, the present findings should not be generalized beyond male rats tested under the current task sequence. This limitation is particularly relevant because prior work suggests that ELA effects on delay discounting, compulsive-like responding, and impulsive-action-related measures can differ by sex and by behavioral domain [50, 51, 63]. In addition, impulsive action and impulsive choice show sex- and hormone-sensitive patterns, including evidence that gonadal hormones contribute to sex differences in impulsive choice in rats [108, 109]. Future studies including females and larger samples are necessary to establish the sex-specificity and generality of these effects.

Finally, considering the evidence that LBN can produce sex-differential stress physiology (e.g., anxiety-like behavior and corticosterone responses) [8], testing in females will be critical for clarifying generalizability of findings and mechanisms involved. Related to these future efforts required to isolate the specific factors responsible for the ELA effects here reported, it is the fact that rats in the present study were housed individually starting on postnatal day 45 to control food consumption and weight. Reports of the negative impact of single housing in rodents are inconsistent and, among others, seem to depend on variables such as sex, age, and duration of isolation [110–113]. Future studies could test interactions between types of housing and the ELA protocol here reported.

Conclusion

Our study shows preliminary evidence that a history of combined early-life adversity (MS and LBN) is associated to a complex pattern of behavioral disruptions in male adolescence rats and adulthood across reward-related domains. Specifically, higher goal-tracking in Pavlovian autoshaping, higher impulsive choice in delay discounting, and less efficient and premature responding under a long-interval reinforcement schedule (VI60s), without affecting persistence during extinction. Combined ELA seemed to also moderate an impact on the relationship between some of the behavioral processes we assessed. The positive association between sign-tracking and efficiency in lever pressing during the interval-schedule performance was attenuated, the pattern of sign-tracking and extinction was reversed to (fewer responses during extinction as sign tracking increased) and induced a positive relationship between GT and extinction of lever pressing. These moderation effects suggest that ELA may reshape extinction dynamics and operant control primarily by shifting the functional coupling between Pavlovian approach tendencies and operant responding, rather than producing global increases or decreases in persistence per se. Besides, our delay-discounting findings align with the consistent association between childhood adversity and steeper discounting in humans and support the rationale that combined adversity manipulations may capture clinically relevant exposure patterns better than single-hit models. Our findings overall show promise for future work that test sex-dependent effects, establish task-general versus task-specific mechanisms, and identify neurobiological pathways through which ELA recalibrates cue- versus outcome-focused behavioral control. However, our conclusions should be interpreted conservatively because the study included only male rats, used a modest sample size, and exposed all animals to a fixed sequence of behavioral tasks. Also, despite evidence of physiological and behavioral alterations produced by MS and LBN, our results do not allow us to conclude that the combination of MS and LBN necessarily will produce additive or uniformly more severe effects than either manipulation alone. Combined MS-LBN is a complex rearing manipulation whose behavioral consequences may vary by task, cohort, sex, developmental timing, and outcome measure. Investigating these mechanisms was beyond the scope of the present study and thus should be addressed in future research.
