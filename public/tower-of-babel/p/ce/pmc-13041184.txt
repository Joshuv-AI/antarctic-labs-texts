Obturation quality analysis of furcation perforations repaired with different magnifications and biomaterials
Authors: Mustafa Mert Tulgar, Yağmur Kılıç, Merve Işık Aydın, Ali Keleş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aimed to evaluate the obturation quality of furcation perforations repaired using two different calcium silicate-based materials under two different magnification levels, using micro-computed tomography analysis.MethodsForty-eight standardized dentin disks with artificially created perforations were randomly assigned into four groups: White MTA (Angelus, Londrina, Brazil) under 4×, Bio-C Repair (Angelus, Londrina, Brazil) under 4×, White MTA under 10×, and Bio-C Repair under 10× magnification (n = 12). The perforated dentin disks were bonded to a resin crown using composite resin and subsequently mounted onto a phantom head model. The repairs were performed using a dental operating microscope (DOM), and a single experienced endodontist completed all procedures. After setting, the samples underwent micro-CT scanning. The volumes of materials, gaps, and voids were measured and differences between groups were analyzed using Kruskal Wallis test, t-test and Mann Whitney U test.ResultsAll samples exhibited some degree of voids and gaps. Magnification level (4× vs. 10×) did not significantly affect obturation quality (p > 0.05). However, the type of repair material had a significant impact on the outcome. White MTA exhibited significantly fewer voids, gaps, and total unfilled volume compared to Bio-C Repair (p < 0.05).ConclusionIncreasing the magnification level from 4× to 10× did not improve the sealing quality in furcation perforation repair. However, White MTA demonstrated superior adaptation and condensation characteristics to Bio-C Repair, regardless of magnification. While handling the properties of powder-liquid MTA may pose clinical challenges, it remains a more effective option for achieving superior sealing in furcation perforation repairs than premixed putty formulations.Keywords: Root perforation, Dental operating microscope, Calcium silicate cements

Introduction

Furcation perforations (FP) are among the critical clinical complications that can impair the prognosis of root canal treatment [1]. They may occur due to deep caries, internal resorption, or iatrogenic causes during dental procedures, creating a communication between pulpal and periodontal tissues [2]. The primary objective of perforation repair is to achieve a hermetic seal to prevent bacterial contamination [3, 4]. Failure to achieve an adequate seal may facilitate bacterial penetration, which has been associated with persistent inflammation, periodontal destruction, and ultimately, treatment failure [5]. Thus, proper material selection and sealing ability are critical for long-term success. Several factors influence the clinical success of FP repair, including the size and visibility of the operative field, the time before repair, the material used, and the operator’s experience [6, 7].

The use of magnification tools has been recommended to improve the efficacy of both nonsurgical and surgical endodontic procedures. Among these, the dental operating microscope (DOM) provides superior magnification and illumination of the operative field [5]. Its clinical benefits include improved visualization in procedures such as retrieval of broken instruments, overcoming ledges, managing obstructed canals, and repairing perforations [8, 9]. Low magnification (×3–×8) is generally recommended for identifying anatomical structures, while medium magnification (×8–×16) is preferred for more complex procedures, such as perforation repair and broken instrument removal [8, 10, 11].

Tricalcium silicate-based biomaterials, such as MTA, have improved treatment outcomes by reducing bone destruction, promoting epithelial cell growth, and inducing hard tissue barrier formation [12, 13]. A meta-analysis of clinical trials reported an overall success rate of 72.5% for FP repair, with MTA showing 80.9% success [14]. White MTA (Angelus, Londrina, Brazil) is a powder–liquid material with low solubility. It contains tricalcium silicate, dicalcium silicate, tricalcium aluminate, calcium oxide, and bismuth oxide in the powder phase, with distilled water as the liquid [15]. However, traces of heavy metal ions have been reported [16, 17]. MTA is widely used due to its calcium ion release, antibacterial effect, high pH, and ability to induce hard tissue formation [16]. Bio-C Repair (Angelus, Londrina, Brazil) is a ready-to-use bioceramic putty with a composition that includes tricalcium silicate, calcium oxide, calcium aluminate, zirconium oxide, iron oxide, silicon dioxide, and a dispersing agent [15]. It shares many favorable properties with MTA, including calcium ion release, high pH, low solubility, and biocompatibility, but does not contain heavy metals [18, 19]. However, differences in particle size and composition may influence their behavior under condensation.

In recent years, micro-CT has been widely used to assess obturation quality [1, 20]. Due to its superior resolution, Micro-CT allows accurate visualization of internal structures and quantitative assessment of minor defects without damaging specimens. For these reasons, it is a more useful method than CBCT for detecting voids and gaps in in vitro studies. The present study aimed to evaluate the quality of obturation in simulated perforations repaired with two MTA-based materials at different magnification levels. Two null hypotheses were formulated:

Primary null hypothesis (H10) suggested that there is no significant difference in the obturation quality of biomaterials used for repairing furcal perforations between the two magnification settings (4× and 10×).

Secondary null hypothesis (H20) suggested that there is no significant difference in the obturation quality between White MTA and Bio-C Repair when used for repairing furcal perforations.

Methods

The experimental procedure was reviewed and approved by the local Health Research Ethics Committee (approval number 2024/652) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was obtained from all participants whose extracted teeth were used in the study. After extraction, soft tissue remnants were carefully removed, and the teeth were stored in a 1% thymol solution at room temperature. The storage duration did not exceed 25 days before specimen preparation. The study workflow, including sample selection, group allocation, interventions, and outcome assessment, is illustrated in Fig. 1. Sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.2, Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) based on data from a similar study [21]. Assuming an effect size of f = 0.40 (large effect), an α level of 0.05, and a desired power of 90% (1 − β = 0.90), the required sample size was calculated to be 10 specimens per group. To allow for potential sample loss, 12 specimens were recruited per group. During the experiment, one specimen was lost, and the final analysis included 47 specimens. A post-hoc power assessment using the planned parameters indicates that the achieved power with n = 47 remains at or above 90% (i.e., despite the single loss, the study retained adequate power). Therefore, the final sample size is sufficient for the planned comparisons. The inclusion criteria were intact mandibular molar teeth without cracks or caries. Teeth with structural defects or resorption were excluded. Dentin disks with a thickness of 2.5 mm were cut from the crowns of the teeth in a direction parallel to the occlusal surface using a low-speed saw (IsoMet; Buehler, Lake Bluff, IL, USA). Both surfaces of the dentin disks were examined under x16 magnification to confirm dentin continuity. Subsequently, 15° vertically angled holes were drilled on the disks using a 1.6 mm diameter fissure carbide bur (Densply, Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) under water cooling. To maintain a constant angle in each specimen, the high-speed rotary tool was attached to a workstation (Dremel Workstation Wisconsin, United States) that permitted only linear movement.

To standardize the coronal part of the dentin disks, a mandibular tooth access cavity was opened and the coronal tooth was scanned using an intraoral scanner (i500, Medit Corp., Seoul, South Korea). Files were exported in the STL format and imported into a 3D mesh software program (Autodesk Inc., CA, USA). Analog crowns were fabricated using a Phrozen Sonic Mini 4 K 3D printer (Phrozen Technology, Hsinchu, Taiwan) with a temporary resin, Senertek Temp V4 (Senertek, İzmir, Türkiye).

The dentin samples were randomly allocated to two main experimental groups based on magnification. Subsequently, the main group was further subdivided into two subgroups based on the type of repair material used.

Printed resin crowns and dentin disks were bonded with a flowable composite, as illustrated in Fig. 2a. As in an analogous study [22], a piece of gelatin sponge (AbGel, Sri Gopal Krishna Labs. LTD, Mumbai, India) was moistened with distilled water and placed below the perforation area to compensate for the humidity of the periodontal tissues. The specimens were embedded in the phantom jaw model using C-type silicone. These models were then connected to the phantom head model to create a simulation of the intraoral vision. (Fig. 2c)

Perforation repairs were carried out under ×4 magnification in Group 1 and ×10 magnification in Group 2, using a dental operating microscope (OPMI Pico, Zeiss, Germany). MTA was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions, delivered with the MAP-One system (Produits Dentaires SA, Vevey, Switzerland), and compacted using pluggers and blunt paper points. Bio-C Repair was delivered with a plugger. In both groups, a moist cotton pellet was placed in the cavity to facilitate setting. The specimens were stored in an incubator at 37 °C under 100% humidity. After 72 h, the cotton was removed and the resin crowns were separated from the dentin disks with a chisel; one specimen was lost due to dentin disk fracture at this stage. All procedures were performed by a single endodontist with seven years of clinical experience. Blinding of the operator to the magnification level and biomaterial was not feasible.

Each sample was placed on the rotating platform of a micro-CT device (Skyscan 1172; Bruker microCT, Kontich, Belgium) with 500-µm-thick aluminium and 38-µm-thick copper filters at 85 kV, 118 µA, and a 9.99 μm pixel size. The scanning procedure was performed with a 180° rotation around the vertical axis, a 1500 ms camera exposure time, a 0.5° rotation step, two-frame averaging, and 20 random movements. The raw data were reconstructed using NRecon software (NRecon v.1.7.1.1, Bruker micro-CT). Both the operators performing the micro-CT scanning and the evaluators analyzing the images were blinded to the group allocation of the specimens. CTAn (version 1.18.8, Bruker-micro-CT) was used to analyze the data. The volume of interest (VOI) was defined as the portion extending from the bottom surface of the material to the top of the material within the cavity. 3D analysis was used to determine the material volume, total space volume, void and gap volume in VOI. To evaluate obturation quality, the parameters “void” and “gap” are used, as in a previous microCT study [23]. The gaps represent the space between the material and the dentin wall, while the voids represent the space between the biomaterial (Fig. 2b). The percentages of these volumes were then calculated. CTVol (Bruker micro-CT, v. 2.3.2) was used for three-dimensional visualization and qualitative evaluation of the samples. Micro-CT scanning and image analysis were performed independently by two experienced operators. Both operators were blinded to the group allocation of the specimens throughout the evaluation process.

Statistical analysis

Data were evaluated using IBM SPSS Statistics Standard Concurrent User Version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). Before analyzing gaps and voids, the initial volumes of perforation area were compared between groups. The ratio of the volume of the voids and gaps to the total volume of the repair material presented the percentage of voids and gaps. The Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests were used to assess the normality of distribution and equality of variances, respectively. Comparisons between groups in terms of the analyzed values were evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis test with the Bonferroni correction or one-way analysis of variance. The significance level was set at 5%.

Discussion

Perforation management can be achieved through a non-surgical orthograde approach to prevent microleakage and bacterial infection [24]. Achieving adequate obturation is crucial for this purpose. In this context, the current study investigated the quality of repair materials with different physical forms at multiple magnifications by quantifying the percentage volume of voids and gaps using micro-CT analysis.

There is evidence in the literature showing the effect of magnification on clinical practice. Several studies have demonstrated the superiority of DOMs over loupes and unaided vision in detecting missed canals [25–27] and in improving root canal cleanliness during retreatment [28]. Perrin et al. reported that using ×6 DOM at the maxillary molar orifice level is significantly superior to ×2.5 loupes and unaided vision [29], and Bowers et al. [30] showed that operators using ×8 magnification with DOM performed better in manual dexterity tests compared to ×2.5 loupes and unaided vision. Based on these findings, magnification is recommended for challenging procedures, such as perforation repair [3]. The results of this study demonstrated that magnification level (x4 vs. x10) with a dental operating microscope did not significantly affect the quality of repair material obturation; therefore, the primary null hypothesis (H10) was failed to be rejected. This may be explained by the relatively wide and accessible nature of the furcation perforation area, in which both magnification settings provide sufficient visualization. Moreover, optical factors such as illumination, field of view that affect performance may not differ between ×4 and ×10 under a DOM to translate into measurable differences in material adaptation in this standardized model.

In the present study, ×4 and ×10 magnifications were selected because they fall within the clinically relevant range of prismatic dental loupes and DOM. Although medium magnifications (×8–×16) are usually recommended for complex procedures such as perforation repair [8, 10, 11], a higher range was not included because the FP area is relatively wide and easily accessible compared to perforations within the root canal, thereby reducing the need for extreme magnification. A no-magnification control group was not included because previous reports [7, 10] did not recommend performing FP repair without magnification.

The secondary null hypothesis (H20) was rejected, as White MTA exhibited fewer voids and gaps compared to Bio-C Repair. This finding may be attributed to factors such as handling properties, particle size, and material composition [31]. Recent evidence highlights that the chemical and structural condition of dentin plays a critical role in material adaptation and sealing performance, which may further influence how repair materials interact with dentinal walls under condensation forces [32]. The shorter setting time of White MTA (≈ 15 min) enables earlier hardening and gives the operator more control during handling and condensation, which may facilitate deeper infiltration and closer adaptation of finer particles to the dentinal walls under applied condensation pressure, supports better adaptation and fewer voids [9, 33–35]. Regarding our study’s results, it should be noted that the use of the MAP system with White MTA may have improved the quality of MTA obturation. Although Bio-C Repair demonstrates excellent biocompatibility and bioactivity as a ready-to-use material, its lower viscosity and different particle size distribution may weaken its adaptation to cavity walls and increase the risk of void formation [18, 36]. Its longer setting time (≈ 120 min) also reduces the possibility of adequate condensation during placement and makes the material more dependent on moisture for setting, further predisposing it to gaps [37]. Overall, these results highlight that both the intrinsic properties of the materials and the handling techniques employed are critical to the quality of the repair. Previous studies have reported conflicting results. For instance, Nair et al. [38] and Abdelmotelb et al. [39] found no significant difference in sealing ability between hand-mixed and ready-to-use materials using microbial leakage models, whereas Toia et al. [20] reported superior performance of ready-to-use materials in terms of gaps and voids. Conversely, another study reported that MTA demonstrated higher bond strength compared to Bio-C Repair [36]. In a study comparing White MTA and Biodentine as root end materials, it was also found that White MTA resulted in lower rates of voids and gaps [23].

The studies in most of the current literature have assessed obturation quality by creating artificial perforations on extracted teeth [1, 3, 20, 40–42]. The perforation area shape, depth and variations of bifurcation anatomy are considered to influence the outcomes of such interventions. Therefore, it is chosen to have dentin disks as the pulp chamber floor merged with a pre-accessed resin crown, which will provide standardized access.

One of the limitations of this study may be the sample model. The use of standardized dentin disks with artificially created perforations, along with 3D-printed coronal structures, may not perfectly simulate the complexity and variability of natural perforations such as narrow or irregular perforations with active bleeding, in which the benefits of higher magnification may be more pronounced. In addition, mechanical loading was not applied; thus, its effect on the presence of gaps and voids is not explained. Another limitation is the absence of correlation between the unfilled area and tooth survival. While unfilled areas within the repair material have been associated with microbial leakage, the potential impact of these defects on tooth survival remain speculative and should be considered cautiously when extrapolating the results to clinical practice. To address this limitation, future studies incorporating both advanced imaging techniques and leakage assessment methods, as well as long-term in vivo clinical investigations, are recommended.

Conclusion

The increase in magnification from x4 to x10 on the DOM did not enhance the obturation quality in perforation repair applications. All materials in this study showed some voids and gaps formation. Powder-liquid White MTA showed fewer voids and gap formation on furcal perforation repair compared to Bio-C Repair, a putty form. Although its handling properties are considered a clinical challenge, White MTA stands as a reliable option, and bioceramic putties are a strong alternative as a repair material. From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that using white MTA and lower magnification levels such as ×4 may be relatively sufficient to achieve acceptable obturation quality in furcation perforation repair.
