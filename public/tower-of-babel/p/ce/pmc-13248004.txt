Looking Under the Hood of Elite Rowers: Skeletal Muscle Determinants of Rowing Performance
Authors: Phillip Bellinger, Taylor Dick, Llion Roberts, Clare Minahan, Katsuki Takahashi, James P. Charles, Kirstin Hannah, Thomas Wackwitz, Eline Lievens, Freek Van De Casteele, Ben Kennedy, Steven Duhig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe aimed to identify the physiological and muscle characteristics associated with mean power output in the final stage of a rowing incremental test in elite rowers. Twenty‐two national level rowers (n = 9 female) completed the following assessments (i) a 7‐stage incremental rowing ergometer test to determine V̇O2peak, maximal muscle deoxygenation and final incremental stage mean power output, (ii) strength and power, (iii) lean body mass (LBM) by dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry, (iv) lower extremity muscle volumes using MR imaging, (v) muscle architecture using diffusion tensor imaging; and (vi) estimated muscle typology via proton MR spectroscopy. Hierarchical multiple linear regression was used to identify the characteristics associated with final incremental stage mean power output with sex and LBM as covariates. Sex explained a substantial proportion of the variance in final incremental stage mean power output (R 2 = 0.74; adjusted ΔR 2 = 0.72; p < 0.001), while inclusion of LBM further improved model fit (R 2 = 0.87; ΔR 2 = 0.13; adjusted ΔR 2 = 0.13; p = 0.006). After accounting for sex and LBM, V̇O2peak demonstrated the largest independent association with final incremental stage mean power output (ΔR 2 = 0.047; adjusted ΔR 2 = 0.047; partial r = 0.60; p = 0.0048), followed by the estimated proportion of type II fibers (ΔR 2 = 0.035; adjusted ΔR 2 = 0.033; partial r = 0.52; p = 0.0188). Most other muscle characteristics explained minimal additional variance. Sex and muscle characteristic interaction terms indicated no detectable sex‐specific differences in this small cohort. These findings highlight the diversity in the underpinning muscle characteristics and may inform personalized interventions targeted at improving rowing performance.

Introduction

Performance in middle‐distance endurance events (i.e., 2–7 min) provides a unique complexity where different mechanical and physiological characteristics (i.e., characteristics across the endurance/aerobic and speed/anaerobic continuum) may contribute meaningfully to performance [1, 2]. One sport that may possess athletes with diverse physiological profiles is rowing [3]. Rowing challenges athletes to optimize both endurance/aerobic and speed/anaerobic characteristics given the requirements for a high maximal oxygen uptake (i.e., V̇O2max) and power at V̇O2max [4, 5], as well as the substantial contribution from anaerobic metabolism during 2000‐m races [6]. As such, both endurance performance and short‐duration sprint performance are complementary determinants of 2000‐m rowing performance in competitive female [7, 8] and male rowers [4, 5, 8]. Although endurance and sprint capabilities are known elements of rowing performance, it remains unknown how elite rowers can maximize both of these characteristics, given that the underpinning determinants are often conflicting [9].

Previous studies [4, 5, 7] investigating the determinants of 2000 m rowing performance have largely focused on whole‐body measurements (i.e., V̇O2max, rowing economy and the lactate threshold), which are considered to be important. Furthermore, these characteristics have also been associated with final rowing incremental stage mean power output, indicating that the underlying determinants of performance in both tests are similar. Nonetheless, gaining a better understanding of the skeletal muscle characteristics that underpin these whole‐body measurements is required to improve our ability to prescribe individualized training advice for elite rowers. Several key muscle characteristics that have either been associated with or are theoretically linked to high‐intensity endurance performance relevant to rowing include oxidative capacity (i.e., muscle fiber oxidative capacity and oxygen supply) [10], architecture (i.e., the length, orientation, and physiological cross‐sectional area (PCSA) of muscle fibers) [8, 11], morphology (i.e., muscle volume) [8] and muscle typology (i.e., the distribution of type I and II fibers) [2, 12, 13]. Indeed, establishing the individual and interactive contribution of these muscle characteristics is important to understand how to maximize sprint and endurance performance for elite rowers.

Muscle morphology and architecture may be important considerations for the endurance and sprint performance trade‐off, given that muscle fiber size (i.e., increasing maximal force capabilities for sprint capacity) and muscle fiber oxidative capacity (i.e., endurance capacity) are inversely related in well‐trained athletes [9, 14]. For instance, maximal muscle force directly underpins sprint capacity and is determined by the muscle CSA [15], but if this is constructed of many large‐diameter muscle fibers, there would be a larger oxygen diffusion distance into the muscle fiber, effectively impairing the rate of aerobic metabolism [14]. Longer muscle fascicles (i.e., greater number of sarcomeres in series) are associated with greater lower‐body power production and sprint performance in endurance and power trained men, but may also increase the energetic cost of exercise, limiting the endurance component of high‐intensity endurance performance [16]. Determining whether these characteristics relate to rowing performance is critical given the possible trade‐offs between them whereby enhancements in one may constrain another. Muscle typology is a prime determinant of maximal muscle fiber shortening velocity, whereby type II fibers have a much higher (5‐ to 10‐fold) maximal shortening velocity, and consequently peak power as compared to type I fibers [17]. While type II fibers also have the potential to achieve a similar oxidative profile to type I fibers in response to training, type I fibers have the advantage of superior fatigue resistance [17]. As such, it is difficult to hypothesize the “optimal” combination of muscle characteristics for a rowing athlete who is required to excel in both endurance capability and sprint capacity.

In the present study, we aimed to identify the deterministic potential of noninvasive measurements of these muscular characteristics on final stage rowing incremental test mean power output. We hypothesized that muscle volume, architecture, and oxidative capacity would be positively associated with final stage rowing incremental test mean power output. Given the diverse characteristics of the different muscle fiber types, we did not formulate a hypothesis on whether an “optimal” muscle typology exists for a rowing athlete who is required to excel in both endurance and sprint capacity. A secondary aim was to explore whether these deterministic characteristics were different between male and female rowers.

Methods

Participants

Twenty‐two national‐level rowers (13 males; age 22.6 ± 3.17 years, height 1.87 ± 0.07 m, body mass 88.0 ± 7.9 kg; nine females; age 21.9 ± 3.14 years, height 1.77 ± 0.05 m, body mass 73.3 ± 7.73 kg) volunteered to participate in this study. Five rowers would classify as tier 4, while 17 would be classified as tier 3 [18]. There were two lightweight male rowers and one lightweight female rower. All rowers had a consistent training history of at least 6 years and were without major injury interruption for the previous 6 months leading into the study testing. None of the subjects had consumed beta‐alanine in the 6 months prior to participation in the study. All rowers provided written informed consent prior to participating in this study, which was approved by the Griffith University Human Research Ethics Committee (Ethics protocol GU ref. no: 2022/273).

Study Design

Participants completed a 7‐stage incremental rowing ergometer test to determine rowing efficiency, lactate threshold and lactate turn point, peak oxygen uptake (V̇O2peak), the magnitude of maximal muscle deoxygenation via Near‐Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS) and final incremental stage mean power output. Participants also completed a series of strength and power assessments, including a countermovement jump, an isometric mid‐thigh pull and 3‐repetition maximum (RM) leg press. Participants underwent an assessment of body composition by dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry (DXA), magnetic resonance (MR) imaging of their thigh to determine volumes of the major hip‐ and knee‐spanning muscles, diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) to estimate muscle fiber lengths, 3D pennation angles and physiological CSA of the vastus lateralis, and proton MR spectroscopy (1H‐MRS) of the soleus to estimate muscle typology [19, 20, 21]. The rowing ergometer trials were performed on an air‐braked rowing ergometer (Concept 2 Model C, Concept 2 Ltd., Wilford, Notts, UK). Before each testing session, the ergometer drag factor was adjusted to correspond with the rower's weight and sex category whereby the drag factors were 95 for lightweight women, 105 for lightweight men and heavyweight women, and 115 for heavyweight men. For each trial, participants were asked to arrive at the laboratory in a hydrated state, having abstained from exercise on the day of testing.

7‐Stage Incremental Rowing Ergometer Test

Participants were instructed to consume their usual pre‐race meal and beverages before attending the laboratory but to avoid caffeine within 8 h of testing. Participants completed a 7 × 4‐min incremental step test, interspersed with 1‐min recovery periods, whereby the initial workload and workload increments were individualized based on each participant's best 2000 m ergometer time in the previous 12 months [22]. The first six workloads were submaximal and prescribed whereby stroke rate was self‐selected to allow each athlete to maintain their prescribed power output. For example, an athlete who achieved a recent 2000 m rowing ergometer time of < 5:50.0 would be prescribed an initial workload of 200 W and increments of 45 W, while a rower who achieved a recent 2000 m rowing ergometer time of 6:10.0–6:20:0 would be prescribed an initial workload of 160 W and increments of 35 W [22]. The seventh and final stage was a maximal effort, representing a 4‐min time trial where each participant was instructed to cover as much distance as possible. The mean power output from the seventh stage was determined as the primary outcome variable and described as final incremental stage mean power output. During the 1‐min recovery periods, an earlobe blood sample was taken to determine blood lactate concentrations with a Lactate Pro 2 device (Arkray inc., Japan). Respiratory variables were measured using open‐circuit spirometry with 30‐s values (TrueOne 2400, Parvomedics, Sandy, UT, USA). A portable NIRS monitor (PortaMon, Artinis Medical Systems BV) was placed on the right vastus lateralis of each subject. Mean power output and distance covered were recorded during each of the 4‐min workload periods. Mean power output during the final 4‐min stage was recorded as the criterion performance variable, while gross rowing efficiency, lactate threshold and lactate turn point and V̇O2peak were physiological characteristics. The 7 × 4‐min incremental step test was selected as a rowing ergometer trial because this protocol allows for the determination of physiological characteristics associated with endurance performance, and the duration of the final stage time trial may closely reflect the race time of the 1500 m event at the Los Angeles Olympics in 2028 consistent with a more even pacing strategy [6].

Calculation of the Lactate Threshold and Turn Point, Gross Rowing Efficiency and V̇O2peak

The lactate threshold and turn point were calculated from the blood lactate‐power output relationship using third‐order polynomial regression. The lactate threshold was defined as the power output at which blood lactate increased > 0.4 mmol·L−1 above baseline concentration. Automated software (ADAPT; AIS, Canberra, Australia) identified the lactate turn point as the power output on the polynomial regression curve that yielded the maximum perpendicular distance to the straight line formed between the lactate threshold and the peak blood lactate concentration (i.e., modified Dmax method). We calculated gross rowing efficiency during the final minute of the submaximal stage that preceded the lactate threshold by first quantifying the metabolic power (i.e., V̇O2 × [(4940 × respiratory exchange ratio + 16 040)/60]) according to Garby and Astrup [23]. The measured rowing power output was then divided by the calculated metabolic power to quantify rowing gross efficiency. V̇O2peak was taken as the highest 30‐s averaged value obtained from the mixing‐chamber metabolic system during the final stage of the test.

Muscle Oxygenation

With subjects in a seated position, the site for the NIRS monitor was marked 3 cm laterally and one third of the distance proximally from the most lateral and superior aspect of the patella, and the anterior superior iliac spine and secured with bi‐adhesive tape and covered with nonstick opaque crepe bandages. NIRS signals reflected the combined concentrations of deoxy‐hemoglobin and deoxy‐myoglobin and oxy‐hemoglobin and oxy‐myoglobin, respectively. Within 5 min following the 7‐stage incremental rowing ergometer test an ischemia/hyperemia calibration was used to normalize the NIRS signals, accounting for inter‐individual differences in skin and adipose tissue thickness. To do this, a blood pressure cuff (Hokanson SC12D, Bellevue, WA) attached to a rapid‐inflation system (Hokanson E20, Bellevue, WA) was placed proximal to the NIRS optode high on the proximal thigh. The blood pressure cuff was inflated to 300 mmHg for approximately 5 min, until the NIRS signals plateaued. Upon release of the cuff, a 1–3‐min period of hyperaemia occurred. This calibration was used to scale the NIRS signals to be a per cent of this range (i.e., the absolute deoxy‐hemoglobin/deoxy‐myoglobin difference between the maximum value of the ischaemic period and the minimum value of the hyperaemia = 100%). NIRS data were acquired at 10 Hz and exported as a csv file. To reduce the noise created by movement, we applied a moving mean with a window of 5‐s and treated the deoxygenation signal with a smooth spline filter. Subsequently, we determined the magnitude of maximal muscle deoxygenation during the final 4‐min stage relative to the minimum and maximum values obtained from the calibration period.

Strength and Power Assessments

Each participant completed a 20‐min general warm‐up consisting of joint mobility exercises, which was followed by a specific warm‐up comprising several trials of both unloaded and loaded exercises relevant to the strength and power assessments. Participants performed a countermovement jump (CMJ) and an isometric mid‐thigh pull using the ForceDecks (Vald Performance Pty Ltd., Brisbane, Australia). These strength and power tests are regularly performed with these athletes as part of their program. Peak power was derived from the CMJ, while absolute and relative peak force were derived from the isometric mid‐thigh pull. Participants also performed an assessment of a 3‐RM leg press. Participants performed 8–10 repetitions of a light load (~50% of predicted one repetition maximum [1‐RM]) and after a short rest, a load of ~80% of estimated 1‐RM through the full range of motion. After each successful performance, the load increased until a failed attempt occurred at the 3‐RM leg press. For the leg press, each participant took the pressing position with their knees flexed at 90°. A trained strength and conditioning coach (Level 2; Australian Strength and Conditioning Association) monitored each lift.

Muscle Typology

Muscle carnosine content was measured by 1H‐MRS in the soleus muscle of each participant's right limb to estimate muscle typology [21]. The 1H‐MRS measurements were performed on a 3‐T whole body MRI scanner (Philips Medical Systems Best, The Netherlands) as previously described [19]. The carnosine concentration of each muscle was converted to a sex‐specific Z‐score relative to an age‐matched control population of active, healthy nonathletes, consisting of 40 men and 33 women. We chose to determine muscle typology in the soleus by measuring muscle carnosine because (1) it is one of the muscles in which carnosine can be reliably measured (CV for test–retest is 3.5%–4.3%) [19], (2) strong positive correlations have been reported between muscle carnosine derived from 1H‐MRS and the proportional area of fast muscle fibers derived from biopsies [21], (3) the strong intermuscular associations for carnosine content [24] indicate that the soleus muscle typology reflects the muscle typology of other major muscles and (4) this allows us to compare the carnosine concentration to a large reference database.

Muscle Volume

Axial T1‐weighted images were acquired using a 3‐T MRI scanner (Philips Medical Systems, The Netherlands) from the level of the iliac crest to below the patella, spanning both lower limbs while the participant lay supine in the scanner. The images were acquired in the axial plane using multiple stations of 3D mDIXON gradient echo sequences with 0 mm overlap between stations (TR/TE1/TE2 – 5/1.94/4.4 ms, FOV 448 × 380, acquired voxel size 1 × 1 × 2 mm), 10 mm overlap between stations with compressed SENSE acceleration 8 and SMART Speed (AI Deep learning) reconstruction to 0.4 × 0.4 × 2 mm at a time of 44 s per station. The cross‐sectional area (CSA) of lower‐limb muscles was determined by manually tracing the margin of the respective muscle in each axial slice of the right limb using image analysis software (Mimics, version 17; Materialise). The analyzed muscles were the gluteus maximus, medius and minimus, tensor fascia latae, sartorius, iliopsoas, pectineus, gracilis, adductor magnus, adductor longus and adductor brevis, rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, vastus intermedius, vastus medialis, semimembranosus, semitendinosus, biceps femoris long head and short head. We defined the proximal start and distal end points of each muscle based on where the muscle of interest first appears and last appears on successive slices. All segmentations were performed by one member of the research team. Muscle volume was calculated as the sum of the cross‐sectional areas of all traced slices multiplied by the slice thickness. The volume of four functional muscle groups was calculated as the sum of the following muscles: hip extensors (gluteus maximus, adductor magnus, biceps femoris long head, semimembranosus and semitendinosus); hip flexors (iliopsoas, rectus femoris, sartorius, tensor fascia latae); knee extensors (rectus femoris, vastus intermedius, medialis and lateralis); and knee flexors (gracilis, biceps femoris long and biceps femoris short head, semimembranosus, semitendinosus and sartorius). We mainly focused on the volume of the knee extensors given the relevance of this functional muscle group to rowing performance. In some analyses, we also normalized muscle volume by the product of height and body mass to reduce the effects of body size on muscle size differences [25]. The intra‐rater reliability of the muscle volume of the vastus lateralis was excellent (mean ICC = 0.963, CV = 2.1%; n = 8), although the small reliability subsample should be considered when interpreting the precision of this estimate.

Muscle Architecture

MR images were acquired from the iliac crest to below the patella using a 3T scanner (Philips Medical Systems), with each subject in a supine position and with the lower limbs in the anatomical position. Imaging consisted of a single set of sagittal slices, phased anterior–posterior, using diffusion‐weighted single‐shot, spin‐echo, echo planar (voxel size 4.5 × 4.5 × 4.5 mm3 gradients), b value 0 and 800 s/mm2, TR/TI/TE 11031/230/50 ms, 20 direction diffusion, short tau inversion recovery (STIR) fat suppression utilizing additional gradient reversal fat suppression, number of slices—30, NSA—2 utilizing opposed echo planar readouts per NSA, acceleration factor—2, bandwidth—97.5 Hz, 4.45 pixel water fat shift. Advanced B0 shimming was done to reduce spatial distortion and minimize the residual fat chemical shift in the diffusion‐weighted images. B1 mapping was performed in the utilization of Mult‐transmit B1radiofrequency technology for efficiency and even B1 RF distribution throughout the large field of view for even excitation/signal distribution. In‐line geometric correction using opposed phase B0 maps and background coil information was implemented using the EPIC functionality in the brain product using clinical science agreement with Philips Healthcare. Total image acquisition time was ~16 min for the DTI sequence per subject.

The muscle architecture analysis using DTI and Dixon images was similar to a previous study [26]; however, we focused primarily on the vastus lateralis muscle with few modifications. The scanned DTI and Dixon images were imported into 3D slicer software. Since DTI images were distorted around the proximal and distal ends of the vastus lateralis because of a possible magnetic field inhomogeneity, we used 20 sequential slices around the mid‐belly of this muscle for muscle architecture analyses. In these slices, the voxels, including the deep fascia (fascia lata) and superficial aponeurosis, or deep aponeurosis around the vastus lateralis, were manually segmented by referring to the corresponding Dixon In‐phase and water images. It should be noted that we cannot separately segment the deep fascia and superficial aponeurosis because of the image resolution. The vastus lateralis muscle belly in the most proximal/distal visible slices was manually segmented and used for defining the muscle line of action and length in the subsequent muscle architecture analyses.

The DTI images were then imported into a DSI studio software and the deterministic fiber tracking was conducted with the following fiber tracking parameters: fractional anisotropy ≥ 0.1, turning angle ≤ 30 degrees, step size = 1.0 mm, 20 mm ≤ tract length ≤ 200 mm, number of tracts = 2000. The deep fascia and superficial/deep aponeuroses were defined as “End” regions where fiber tracking stopped. The 3D position coordinates of the tracked fibers and all tissues were imported into MATLAB version R2023a (MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA), and they were reconstructed in 3D space (Figure 1). For each tracked fiber, a sum of distances between 3D position coordinates of consecutive points along the fiber was calculated, and the median value across all fibers was reported as “fascicle length”. The distance between the geometric centres of the 3D position coordinates of the vastus lateralis in the most proximal and distal visible slices was assumed as “muscle length”, and a vector passing through the geometric centres was defined as the line of action of the whole muscle. The 3D angle between the line of action of the whole muscle and the line of action of the tracked fiber, defined as a vector passing through its origin and insertion, was calculated for each fiber, and the median value across all the fibers was reported as “3D pennation angle”. It should be noted that this is the angle between the fascicle and the whole muscle direction in 3D space, which accounts for fascicle orientation both in the sagittal and coronal planes. PCSA was estimated by dividing the total VL muscle volume by the median fascicle length.

Dual‐Energy X‐Ray Absorptiometry (DXA)

DXA was used to determine whole body bone mineral content, fat, and lean (fat‐free) mass (Medix DR, Medilink, France). The DXA was calibrated in accordance with manufacturer guidelines each day prior to measurement. All DXA scans were performed and analyzed by one trained technician, with an emphasis on the consistent positioning of subjects on the scanning bed. All subjects arrived in an overnight fasted state. Scans were analyzed automatically by the software, but regions of interest were subsequently confirmed by the technician. Short‐term DXA measurement precision in our lab is 0.9%, 2.3%, and 0.8% for whole‐body bone mineral content, fat, and lean mass, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted in R (v4.4.1; R Core Team, 2024). Ordinary least‐squares linear regression models were fitted using the lm() function from the stats package and nested models were compared using the change in explained variance (ΔR 2) and associated F‐tests from nested‐model comparisons. Final incremental stage mean power output was treated as the primary outcome variable. Sex was coded as a binary variable and lean body mass was included as a continuous covariate to account for differences in body size.

Unadjusted associations between final incremental stage mean power output and each variable were first examined using simple linear regression. Associations between final incremental stage mean power output and each variable were then examined using hierarchical multiple linear regression with predictors entered sequentially in pre‐specified blocks. Model 1 included sex to quantify variance attributable to sex differences. Model 2 added lean body mass to account for body size. Model 3 evaluated individual physiological and muscle characteristics by entering each characteristic separately into the sex and lean body mass model to determine its independent association with performance while minimizing model overfitting. Model 4 included an exploratory sex and characteristic interaction term to examine potential sex‐specific associations with final incremental stage mean power output. Continuous predictors were z‐standardized prior to analysis to facilitate comparison of standardized regression coefficients (β). Model fit was assessed using the coefficient of determination (r 2), and the explanatory value of each predictor was quantified using the change in explained variance (ΔR 2) between nested models. Partial correlations (r) were derived from hierarchical regression models and represent associations between each characteristic and final incremental stage mean power output after adjustment for sex and lean body mass. The ΔR 2 values represent the additional proportion of total variance accounted for when a given characteristic is added to the preceding model.

Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05, although interpretation emphasized effect sizes and explained variance given the sample size. The magnitude of correlation coefficients was considered trivial for values less than 0.10, small for 0.10 to less than 0.30, moderate for 0.30 to less than 0.50, large for 0.50 to less than 0.70, very large for 0.70 to less than 0.90, nearly perfect for 0.90 to less than 1.00, and perfect at exactly 1.00. Coefficients of variation (CVs) were calculated as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean. Differences in CVs between absolute and lean body mass normalized final incremental stage mean power output were quantified using standardized effect sizes calculated as the mean difference divided by the pooled standard error of the CV estimates. Differences between male and female athletes were examined using independent‐samples t‐tests.

Results

Tables 1 and 2 summarize performance/physiology from the incremental rowing test, strength/power assessments, and muscle characteristics; key variables are illustrated in Figures 2 and 3. When expressed relative to DXA‐derived lean body mass, sex differences remained (Figure 2). Male rowers had a higher lean‐mass–normalized V̇O2peak (75.5 ± 5.46 vs. 69.1 ± 3.62 mL·kg LBM−1·min−1; p = 0.006; Figure 2B) and final‐stage mean power output (5.78 ± 0.51 vs. 5.19 ± 0.27 W·kg LBM−1; p = 0.005; Figure 2D). In female rowers, expressing final incremental stage mean power output to lean body mass reduced between‐subject variability from ~9.0% to ~5.3% with a large effect size (d = −1.65). In male rowers, the reduction was smaller (~9.9% to ~8.8%) with a moderate effect size (d ≈ −0.52). Male rowers also had greater vastus lateralis 3D pennation angle (44.3° ± 3.84° vs. 38.0° ± 6.21°; p = 0.008; Figure 3C), PCSA (199.6 ± 13.0 vs. 129.2 ± 30.7 cm2; p < 0.001; Figure 3D), absolute volume (1228.5 ± 110.9 vs. 861.5 ± 81.5 cm3; p < 0.001; Figure 3E), and relative volume (7.51 ± 0.78 vs. 6.69 ± 0.68 cm3·kg−1·m−1; p = 0.019; Figure 3F).

Discussion

Key Findings

In the present study, we aimed to identify the physiological and muscle characteristics associated with final incremental stage mean power output in well‐trained rowers. Final incremental stage mean power output was largely explained by sex and lean body mass, which together accounted for the majority of variance in performance. After adjustment for these factors, V̇O2peak had the strongest positive correlation with final incremental stage mean power output, as well as a greater estimated proportion of soleus type II fibers. Vastus lateralis fascicle length exhibited a small, directionally positive association with final incremental stage mean power output although the confidence interval spanned trivial and meaningful effects. Our estimates of strength and power, muscle oxygenation and rowing efficiency were not significantly associated with final incremental stage mean power output when controlling for sex and differences in body size. Exploratory sex and muscle characteristic interaction terms contributed little additional explanatory value across variables, indicating no detectable sex‐specific differences in the relationships between muscle characteristics and final incremental stage mean power output within this small cohort.

Importance of Controlling for Lean Body Mass and Sex With V̇O2peak as a Key Determinant

Most of the previous research assessing the determinants of rowing performance have not controlled for variation in lean body mass and sex. Failure to control for these factors can obscure the true association between a given characteristic and rowing performance. For example, previous research [8] conducted in a cohort of 13 Olympic rowers (7 male, 6 female) highlighted that muscle volume explained a substantial magnitude of variation in 2000 m rowing performance (r 2 = 0.85), but after controlling for sex (r 2 = 0.52) and lean body mass (r 2 = 0.05), the strength of the association was reduced. Therefore, where appropriate, we determined whether sex and/or lean body mass moderated the association between each characteristic and rowing performance. These factors are particularly important to consider given that differences in body size and composition between rowers would largely inflate the strength of association between measurements of rowing performance and given physiological and muscle characteristics. For example, total lower limb muscle volume is almost perfectly associated (r = 0.96) with the product of height‐mass [27]. Therefore, studies that include male and female rowers, with a wide range of body sizes (i.e., lightweight and heavyweight rowing classifications) are more likely to have a large variation in performance and physiological characteristics, thereby leading to inflation of associations between dependent and independent variables. Indeed, in the present study, when expressed relative to lean body mass (i.e., W·kg−1 lean body mass), there is a moderate to large reduction in the between subject CV in final incremental stage mean power output in male (8.67%) and female rowers (4.98%) when compared to expressing this variation in absolute terms (i.e., W; CV: female 9.03%, male 9.89%). Despite constraining our models by sex and lean body mass, we were still able to identify some meaningful associations which highlights that some physiological and muscle characteristics were associated with final incremental stage mean power output. Above all characteristics, the strongest determinant of final incremental stage mean power output was V̇O2peak. The association between V̇O2peak and final stage mean power output should be interpreted cautiously because both variables were derived from the same incremental test. This finding therefore reflects their expected coupling within the protocol, rather than independent predictive evidence. Nonetheless, this finding is supported by Bourdin et al. [28] who reported that final stage mean power output at the end of a step wise incremental rowing protocol (3 min stages) was strongly associated with 2000 m rowing ergometer mean power output (r ≈ 0.92) and differed by only ~13 W. In addition, V̇O2peak from the step test had an r value of 0.55 with 2000 m rowing ergometer mean power output and 0.63 with mean power output at the end of the step test. These findings highlight the relevance and comparability of final stage mean power output and V̇O2peak from an incremental rowing test and 2000 m rowing ergometer mean power output.

Muscle Typology

Most of the rowers in the present study had negative Z‐scores (17/22; mean Z‐score = −0.44 ± 0.85) compared to a sex‐matched healthy, nonathlete control group, indicative of a greater estimated proportion of type I fibers. Unfortunately, there is limited historic data, and a complete absence of contemporary reports characterizing the muscle typology of elite rowers. Steinacker [13] reported that more successful rowers had a higher percentage of type I fibers in the vastus lateralis (76%) compared to less successful rowers (66%). Given the relatively slow cadence of rowing competition (~32–38 strokes·min−1), it could be hypothesized that possessing a higher proportion of type I fibers would be advantageous given that there is a very strong association between variation in muscle typology and movement frequency across different sport disciplines [29]. Bex et al. [29] demonstrated that athletes with a greater estimated proportion of type I fibers specialized in event disciplines that featured slow movement frequencies which would substantiate the hypothesis that rowers would ideally possess a slow muscle typology. In opposition to this hypothesis, we identified that a greater estimated proportion of type II fibers in the soleus was positively associated with peak incremental test power output. These findings are supported by Baguet et al. [30] who reported a positive association between soleus and gastrocnemius muscle carnosine content and 100‐, 500‐, 2000‐, and 6000‐m rowing ergometer time trial performance. A rationale does exist that may support these findings, whereby a greater proportion of type IIa fibers might be preferable for athletes that compete in events which require both endurance and sprint capabilities, such as rowing. Indeed, type IIa fibers in both the deltoid and vastus lateralis of highly trained skiers possess an equally high oxidative capacity as type I fibers [31], which demonstrates that these fibers have incredible flexibility when exposed to chronic training. Given that type IIa fibers also typically have a larger cross‐sectional area, contraction velocity and sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ handling capacity than type I fibers [17], possessing a greater proportion of these fibers may be beneficial for rowing performance. Furthermore, Holt et al. [32] suggested that when controlling for power output, higher stroke rates were associated with enhanced rowing performance, which would also align with the benefits of possessing a fast muscle typology, in particular, more type IIa fibers. As such, there is a rationale that supports rowers who sit across the entire muscle typology spectrum to be successful; however, it is likely that different training approaches would be required to maximize performance between rowers who have polarized muscle typology.

Muscle Volume

Muscle volume is thought to be an important determinant of rowing performance [8, 33]. Van der Zwaard et al. [8] reported that vastus lateralis volume was the primary morphological determinant explaining 85% of the variance in 2000‐m rowing ergometer performance (r 2 = 0.85, n = 13) in male and female Olympic rowers. However, it should be noted that this analysis did not account for differences in body sizes, whereby taller rowers are likely to have a greater muscle volume due to anthropometric differences, which may obfuscate the association between muscle volume and absolute 2000‐m rowing ergometer power output. In order to reduce the effect of body size on muscle volume differences, it is recommended to normalize muscle volume by the product of height and mass [25] or control for total lean body mass by adding this characteristic as a covariate within the analysis. Van der Zwaard et al. [8] did attempt to control for total lean body mass by performing partial correlations controlling for sex and body size, which demonstrated that vastus lateralis volume and 2000‐m rowing ergometer performance were still strongly associated after controlling for sex differences (r = 0.72) but were weakly related after controlling for lean body mass (r = 0.23). In the present study, absolute knee extensor muscle volume showed a negative association with final incremental stage mean power output after adjustment for sex and lean body mass (r = −0.21, p = 0.36), although this relationship was imprecise and contributed little explanatory value. While it is clear that muscle volume is a well‐recognized determinant of its force‐generating capacity [11], there is a known trade‐off between increasing the CSA of individual muscle fibers (and overall muscle volume), given the inverse relationship between muscle fiber CSA and oxidative capacity [9]. As such, we could also postulate that increased muscle fiber CSA may be beneficial for rowing performance if accompanied by increases in muscle oxidative capacity via enhanced capillarization and/or mitochondrial capacity [9].

Muscle Architecture

We assessed muscle architecture including the 3D pennation angle, fascicle length, and PCSA of the vastus lateralis using a DTI‐based approach. Indeed, this is the first study to report the 3D fascicle architecture in any skeletal muscle of elite rowers. The observed vastus lateralis PCSA of the female (129.2 cm2) and male rowers (199.6 cm2) in the current study was larger compared to Van der Zwaard et al. [8] who calculated those with the 3D ultrasound imaging (females: 69.9 cm2; males: 87.4 cm2). Apart from differences in the training status of the rowers from either study (National level here vs. Olympic rowers), the differences in PCSA may be largely explained by differences in muscle volume and fascicle length between 3D ultrasound and DTI methods. For example, ultrasound imaging is limited by depth and field of view, which makes the boundaries of muscle, particularly in proximal and distal locations, difficult to discern. Indeed, previous research [34] characterizing the CSA of the vastus lateralis reported that 3D ultrasound methods systematically underestimate muscle CSA by ~10% when compared with the MRI methods. Also, the previous study with 3D ultrasonography [8] estimated the fascicle length from within the mid‐longitudinal fascicle plane at a position 2/3rd along the muscle belly, whereby the limited field of view typically only permits a small number of fascicles to be viewed end to end. Here, we conducted fiber tracking on 2000 tracts, which is important given the nonnormal distributions of fascicle lengths within a skeletal muscle. For instance, Takahashi et al. [26] reported that DTI‐based fascicle architecture (length and pennation angle) substantially varied within the medial gastrocnemius, and the magnitude of the regional variation was dependent on muscle size. We also report substantially larger 3D pennation angles (females, 38.0°; males, 44.3°) than a previous study using 3D ultrasonography (females, 19.4°; males, 18.6°, [8]), which is likely related to the difference in measurement technique as well as the difference in the definition of the pennation angle. The 3D ultrasound imaging [8] estimated the pennation angle from a single projection within the vastus lateralis, which essentially provides an estimation of a 2D pennation angle. This method may provide an underestimation, given that fascicles within the vastus lateralis seem to be angulated in the coronal plane as well as the sagittal plane, similar to the medial gastrocnemius [26]. Thus, we were able to derive 3D pennation angles as the angle between the fascicle and the whole muscle line of action, which results in a pennation angle much larger than a 2D pennation angle.

Pennation angle directly influences the effective physiological cross‐sectional area (i.e., effective PCSA = PCSA × cos[pennation angle]), which governs the proportion of fiber force transmitted along the muscle's line of action [35]. Increases in 3D pennation angle, well above 40° in our rowers, reflect a force‐oriented adaptation, where fiber packing enhances total PCSA despite a directional loss in force transmission (cos 45° ≈ 0.71; effective PCSA = 71%). This architectural shift aligns with the concept of “pennation mechanical advantage,” whereby greater obliquity enables more contractile material within a given volume [35]. Furthermore, other research [36] has demonstrated that large pennation angles may also enhance the rate of force development through its effect on increasing gearing (i.e., the ratio of whole‐muscle shortening to fiber shortening), which may be more readily reflected in dynamic as opposed to isometric test conditions. However, if a greater pennation angle is compensated by shorter fascicles and reduced fascicle excursion this may be associated with trade‐offs [37], which could limit maximum shortening velocity and rate of force development, especially during rapid or cyclic tasks [36]. In rowing, this may impair acceleration at the beginning of the catch phase or reduce efficiency at high stroke rates. Thus, while the large 3D pennation angles observed here likely optimize force production during the drive phase, they may also constrain “explosive” transitions such as the catch. Whether targeted training interventions (e.g., supra‐maximal eccentric loading) can induce preferential architectural adaptations that concomitantly enhance maximal force and rate of force development that transfer to improvements in rowing performance requires further evaluation.

One previous study [38] has reported the DTI‐derived vastus lateralis muscle architecture in healthy nonathlete subjects. In comparison to our rowing data, the untrained subjects in Charles et al. [38] reported smaller vastus lateralis pennation angles (male: 25.2° ± 3.24°; female: 22.7° ± 3.31°) and longer fascicle lengths (male: 83.3 ± 27.2 mm; female: 80.7 ± 15.7 mm) than observed in our rowers (pennation angle: male 44.3° ± 3.84°, female 38.0° ± 6.21°). Our rowers also exhibited larger vastus lateralis volumes (male: 1229 ± 111 cm3; female: 862 ± 82 cm3), whereas the untrained cohort in Charles et al. reported lower vastus lateralis PCSA (male: 91.6 ± 19.3 cm2; female: 62.9 ± 13.9 cm2), which is directionally consistent with their smaller pennation angles. These between‐cohort differences should be interpreted cautiously, as they may partly reflect differences in overall body size/lean mass as well as training status. Furthermore, it is conceivable that the shorter vastus lateralis muscle fascicles of the rowers (male: 61.7 ± 5.54 mm; female: 68.4 ± 10.5 mm) in the present study may relate to the repetitive, concentric dominant nature of rowing, whereby previous work has demonstrated that concentric training of the knee extensors results in reductions in vastus lateralis fascicle length [39]. In support, Van Der Zwaard et al. [40] reported a significant ~5% reduction in vastus lateralis fascicle length in elite female rowers following 8 weeks of concurrent endurance and resistance training.

In the present study, vastus lateralis fascicle length demonstrated a small, directionally positive association with final incremental stage mean power output after adjustment for sex and lean body mass (r = 0.43, p = 0.059), although the estimate lacked precision and explained little additional variance. Theoretically, muscles with longer fascicles, and presumably, more sarcomeres in series, may exhibit higher shortening velocities (when completely unloaded) than muscles with shorter fascicles [11]. As such, there is evidence that has reported longer muscle fascicles in sprinters and reported a negative association between muscle fascicle length and personal best 100‐m times (r = −0.40 to −0.54, p < 0.05) [41]. Nonetheless, how longer fascicles contribute to high‐intensity endurance exercise (i.e., repeated submaximal contractions) warrants more consideration. In Olympic rowers, Van Der Zwaard et al. [8] reported a significant positive correlation (r = 0.66) between 2000 m rowing ergometer performance and vastus lateralis fascicle length. Furthermore, Van Der Zwaard [9] documented that the combined sprint and endurance performance of cyclists was associated with vastus lateralis fascicle length. Indeed, increasing muscle volume by increasing muscle fascicle length, rather than by increasing muscle fiber CSA, may maximize improvements in muscle force production, while preventing any loss in oxidative capacity [9]. In relation to rowing, increasing muscle fascicle length may increase maximal shortening velocity [11], which would allow for greater force output at an identical shortening velocity, and this would also hold true for submaximal shortening velocities, thereby potentially increasing the force output during the high effort, but submaximal strokes throughout a rowing race. Nonetheless, increasing muscle fascicle length may not be without its own trade‐off for endurance athletes [37]. Theoretically, longer muscle fascicles may reduce exercise economy because more energy‐consuming contractile units are activated for a given force production under the assumption of the same change in fiber length. In the present study, we did not observe a meaningful association between vastus lateralis fascicle length and rowing efficiency (r = 0.21).

Muscle Strength and Power

In the present study, we did not find that the measurements of strength and power implemented explained a substantial degree of final incremental stage mean power output when controlling for sex and lean body mass. Previous research has indicated a positive influence of strength on 2000 m rowing performance. For instance, Akça [42] reported a significant relationships between lower limb maximal strength (1‐RM leg press r = −0.536) and 2000 m rowing performance time. It should be noted that this study did not control for differences in body size (i.e., lean body mass) and sex, which may obfuscate the true association between muscle strength and power and rowing performance. Indeed, we found significant positive associations (r = 0.636–0.891) between our measures of strength and power and final incremental stage mean power output without controlling for sex and lean body mass. As such, the findings of the present study and those of others may suggest that maximizing strength is important for rowers, but body size may mediate this relationship.

Limitations

The findings from this study should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating given the modest sample size (n = 22; 9 female, 13 male) and absence of formal correction for multiple comparisons. In addition, inferences regarding muscle typology were based on the noninvasive estimation of muscle carnosine and were not validated against muscle biopsy data in this cohort. Segmentation‐derived measures were completed by a single rater, which likely minimized between‐rater variability; however, replication with independent raters would strengthen evidence of inter‐rater reproducibility. Scan–rescan reliability was not assessed for the DTI‐derived architecture variables in the present cohort. However, prior studies using DTI‐based muscle tractography have reported acceptable‐to‐high repeatability for fascicle length and pennation angle estimates, including regional medial gastrocnemius fascicle length CVs of 3.0%–5.3% and ICCs of 0.526–0.925 [26], and medial gastrocnemius fascicle length and pennation angle ICCs of 0.92 and 0.95, respectively, using anatomically constrained tractography [43]. Future research should replicate these analyses using a criterion 2000 m rowing ergometer time trial alongside the incremental step test to confirm whether the same muscle characteristics explain variance in 2000 m performance.

Conclusion

The findings from the present study highlight the diversity in the physiological characteristics of rowers and the complexity of controlling for differences in body size and sex when establishing the determinants of performance. When controlling for sex and lean body mass, V̇O2peak and a greater estimated proportion of type II fibers were associated with greater final incremental stage mean power output. In the confines of the cohort of rowers in the present study, we found no detectable sex‐specific differences in the relationships between muscle characteristics and final incremental stage mean power output in this small cohort. These findings may inform the individualization of performance‐enhancing interventions targeted at improving rowing performance.

Perspective

The findings from the present study extend previous work showing that rowing performance is strongly influenced by endurance‐related characteristics [4] by indicating that, after accounting for sex and lean body mass, final incremental stage mean power output appears to reflect both whole‐body physiological characteristics and peripheral muscle characteristics. These findings have practical implications for training prescription, whereby coaches may achieve greater performance gains by identifying each rower's primary limiting factor and prescribing more targeted interventions rather than applying uniform training programs. Given that cyclic movement frequency appears linked to muscle typology [29], the present data further suggest that crew‐boat selection may benefit from considering whether rowers possess compatible pacing and physiological profiles, or potentially complementary profiles, to optimize crew formation and performance.
