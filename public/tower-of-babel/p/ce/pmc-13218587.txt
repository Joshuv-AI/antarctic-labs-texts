Spatial Heterogeneity in Synergistic Effects of Extreme Heat and NO2 Exposures on Cardiorespiratory Hospitalizations in California
Authors: Yiqun Ma, Chen Chen, Rosana Aguilera, Alexander Gershunov, Michael Jerrett, Rachel Connolly, Miriam E. Marlier, Tarik Benmarhnia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Extreme heat and high air pollution levels may co-occur and act synergistically. The synergistic health effects of extreme heat and nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and the fine-scale spatial heterogeneity in such joint effects, remain unclear. We aimed to investigate the synergistic effect of extreme heat and NO2 exposures on cardiorespiratory hospitalizations in California and explore its spatial heterogeneity.Methods:Daily ZIP Code Tabulation Area (ZCTA)-level hospitalization data, 2000–2019, were obtained from the California Department of Health Care Access and Information. Extreme heat and NO2 exposures were defined as days when the heat index and NO2 concentration exceeded a ZCTA-specific threshold, respectively. We first investigated the state-level synergistic effects with a case-crossover analysis and then estimated the ZCTA-specific synergistic effects using a within-community matched design combined with a spatial Bayesian hierarchical model. Finally, we explored how community characteristics modify these effects using meta-regressions.Results:We found mild synergistic effects of extreme heat and NO2 exposures on cardiorespiratory hospitalizations at the state level, with a relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) of 0.005 (95% confidence interval = −0.002, 0.011). Yet, great spatial heterogeneity was observed, with ZCTA-specific RERIs ranging from −1.08 to 2.22. Higher RERIs were observed in communities with lower socioeconomic status, higher population density, reduced green space, greater proportions of racial and ethnic minority residents, and higher historical temperatures.Conclusions:Our findings underscore the need for adaptation policies that integrate compound exposures to heat and air pollution and inform the development of targeted intervention strategies to protect vulnerable communities.

Over recent decades, extreme heat events have become increasingly frequent due to climate change, posing substantial public health threats. The heat-related disease risk and mortality burdens are well-documented globally.1–6 Extreme heat may also interact with air pollution, exacerbating heat-related adverse health outcomes.7–10 Numerous studies have identified a synergistic effect between heat and air pollution, notably with fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and ozone, on cardiorespiratory morbidity and mortality in Europe,11 the United States,12–14 and many other regions worldwide.15,16

Synergistic health effects of extreme heat and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) remain relatively underexplored. Among the limited previous studies on this topic, most of them focused on mortality outcomes, with relatively little attention to morbidity.16,17 In addition, most studies examined the interactions of heat and NO2 on a multiplicative scale, whereas assessing interactions on an additive scale may more accurately reflect the underlying causal mechanisms and could thus be more relevant to public health decision-making.18,19

NO2 is one of the criteria air pollutants regulated by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). Numerous studies have documented the effects of short-term NO2 exposure on human health, particularly regarding respiratory and cardiovascular outcomes.20–22 During extreme heat events, physiological responses such as increased ventilation rates can increase individuals’ exposure to NO2; the body’s capacity to detoxify chemicals may also be compromised as a result of increased thermoregulatory responses to heat stress.23 In addition, the health impacts of NO2 and heat may share similar physiological pathways, involving oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular damage.8,24,25 Therefore, extreme heat and NO2 may act synergistically and further aggravate the health effects of heat or NO2 alone. Furthermore, although NO2 concentrations have generally decreased nationwide in recent years, a regional study reported that rural areas in California experienced stable or increasing NO2 levels, partially attributed to increased wildfire frequency and soil emissions in response to climate change.26 These findings indicate potentially growing coexposure to extreme heat and NO2 in certain regions.

In addition, the potential synergistic effects of NO2 and extreme heat may substantially vary across communities. First, the combined exposures are particularly problematic in urban areas with large populations,27 where both air temperature and NO2 levels tend to be higher than in surrounding regions due to the urban heat island effect and higher traffic volumes.28,29 Second, previous studies consistently found that communities with higher proportions of racial and ethnic minority residents are more vulnerable to both extreme heat,10 air pollution,30–32 and their joint effects.12,13 Related characteristics, such as neighborhood-level socioeconomic status and greenness, may also act as potential effect modifiers due to variations in psychological stress, adaptive capacity, and healthcare access across these communities.10,33–35 To date, the role of these community characteristics has not been explored in association with the synergistic effects of extreme heat and NO2 at a fine spatial scale.

California, with its increasing extreme heat events,36 some of the highest levels of NO2 pollution in the United States, and diverse subpopulations, is a good setting for studying the synergistic effects of extreme heat and NO2. In this study, utilizing statewide daily unscheduled cardiorespiratory hospitalization data in California from 2000 to 2019, we aim to (1) investigate the state-level additive synergistic effect of extreme heat and extreme NO2 exposures on cardiorespiratory hospitalizations; (2) estimate the ZIP Code Tabulation Area (ZCTA)-specific synergistic effects of these two exposures; and (3) explore how community characteristics modify these synergistic effects.

METHODS

This study was approved by the California Health and Human Services Agency’s Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects (project number: 2021-116).

Data Sources

Extreme Heat Exposure

Daily mean, minimum, and maximum air temperature, and mean dew point temperature data from 1981 to 2019 at a 4 × 4 km resolution were obtained from the Parameter-elevation Regressions on Independent Slopes Model Climate Group.37 We extracted the daily ZCTA-level values using the population-weighted centroid of each ZCTA. The daily heat index was then calculated using the daily mean air temperature and dew point temperature values.38,39

In the main analysis, we defined extreme heat exposure as days when the daily heat index exceeded the 85th percentile of historical summertime (July and August, 1981 to 1999) heat index values for each ZCTA. This definition, adapted from the U.S. EPA’s definition of heatwaves and consistent with previous studies,12,40 captures unusually hot days relative to local climate norms and accounts for local climate adaptation. We also considered alternative heat metrics (minimum and maximum air temperatures) and thresholds (90th and 95th percentiles) in secondary analyses. The 85th percentile was chosen for the main analysis because it identified the greatest number of ZCTA-days with extreme heat exposure, which improves statistical precision for ZCTA-specific estimations, while the heat index was chosen as the primary metric because it takes humidity into account and better reflects what the temperature feels like to the human body.38,39

Extreme Nitrogen Dioxide Exposure

Daily ZCTA-level maximum 1-hour NO2 concentrations in California from 2000 to 2019 were estimated using an ensemble-based statistical approach (eAppendix 1; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C318).41 To capture acute exposure to extremely high levels of NO2, we defined extreme NO2 exposure as days when the maximum 1-hour NO2 concentration exceeded the ZCTA-specific 85th percentile of concentrations during the study period in the main analysis. We used data during the study period to calculate the NO2 thresholds, rather than a historical baseline, given unclear evidence for long-term acclimatization to air pollution. We considered alternative thresholds (90th and 95th percentiles) in secondary analyses. Similar to the definition of extreme heat, we selected the 85th percentile as the NO2 threshold for the main analysis because it identified the greatest number of days with extreme NO2 exposure across ZCTAs and maximizes the sample size for statistical analysis.

Cardiorespiratory Hospitalization

Data on daily unscheduled hospital visits and emergency department visits (hereafter referred to as “hospitalizations”) in California from 2000 to 2019 were obtained from the California Department of Health Care Access and Information.42 The dataset included the patient’s residential ZIP code, date of hospitalization, and primary International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9 or ICD-10 codes.

The outcome of interest in this study is cardiorespiratory hospitalizations (circulatory or respiratory hospitalizations), identified using the relevant ICD-9 or ICD-10 codes (eAppendix 2; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C318). We matched each ZIP code to its corresponding ZCTA (eAppendix 3; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C318) and aggregated the individual-level data into daily counts of cardiorespiratory hospitalizations for each ZCTA.

Community Characteristics

A set of ZCTA-level community characteristics, including racial and ethnic composition, socioeconomic factors, tree canopy, and historical mean summer and winter temperatures, was collected to explore potential modifiers of the synergistic effects of extreme heat and NO2. The specific variables and data sources we used for each category of variable are listed in eAppendix 4; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C318. We included summer and winter temperatures to represent long-run local thermal climate conditions and account for California’s diverse climate types and their distinct seasonal temperature variability.

Statistical Analyses

Inclusion Criteria

We included all California ZCTAs that met the following criteria: (1) ZCTAs with a population of at least 1,000 to improve statistical precision; and (2) ZCTAs that experienced at least 1 day of exposure to extreme heat alone, extreme NO2 alone, and both extreme heat and extreme NO2 (i.e., experienced all three exposure types), which was necessary for estimating ZCTA-specific synergistic effects. Of 1,412 ZCTAs meeting the first criterion, 1,398 (roughly 99%) also met the second criterion under the main exposure definitions.

State-level Case-crossover Analysis

We investigated the state-level synergistic effects of extreme heat and extreme NO2 exposures on daily cardiorespiratory hospitalizations using a time-stratified case-crossover design with conditional quasi-Poisson regression.43,44 In this design, a case day was defined as the date of hospitalization. Matched control days were other days occurring on the same day of the week (but not the same week) within the same calendar month, year, and ZCTA.45 Only binary indicators of extreme heat and extreme NO2 exposures and an interaction term between them were included in the model. We did not adjust for PM2.5 in the model because, since NO2 is a precursor to PM2.5, PM2.5 is on the causal pathway between NO2 exposure and hospitalizations. The main analysis focused on lag 0 – that is, hospitalizations occurring on the same day as the exposure to extreme heat and/or NO2. We reported rate ratios (RRs) for the individual and joint effects. We also conducted a stratified analysis by urbanicity by classifying all ZCTAs in California into metropolitan and nonmetropolitan areas, based on the Rural-Urban Commuting Area codes (1–3 for metropolitan and 4–10 for nonmetropolitan).46

Relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) was calculated to quantify the difference between the joint effect of two exposures and the sum of their individual effects. The 95% confidence interval (CIs) of the joint effect and RERI were calculated using the delta method.19 The R package gnm was used for the conditional quasi-Poisson regression, and the mcm package was used for the delta method.47,48

ZIP Code Tabulation Area-level Within-community Matched Design With Spatial Bayesian Hierarchical Model

We applied a within-community matched design combined with a spatial Bayesian hierarchical model to estimate the synergistic effect for each ZCTA, as developed and applied in previous studies.12,13 First, in the within-community matched design, for each ZCTA and exposure type, matched unexposed days were identified based on four criteria: (1) in the same ZCTA as the exposed day, (2) in the same year, (3) without any type of exposure and not within 3 days of any exposed days, and (4) within a 30-day window before or after the exposed day. For each exposed day, we calculated the ratio of hospitalization counts on that day to the weighted average of hospitalization counts on all matched unexposed days, using an inverse time weighting scheme (i.e., weights were assigned as 1 divided by the number of days from the exposed day to give more weights to the closest days). ZCTA-specific RRs for extreme heat alone, extreme NO2 alone, and joint exposure to both were computed as the average RRs from all corresponding pairs of matched exposed days and unexposed days in each ZCTA. These three ZCTA-specific RRs were then used to calculate the ZCTA-specific RERI values.12,13 Although this within-community matched design did not explicitly control for the day-of-the-week effect, it controlled for measured and unmeasured confounders that remain relatively stable over 2 months and minimized the potential influence of seasonality by giving more weight to control days closer to the exposed day. We chose this within-community design for ZCTA-specific estimates because days with compound exposures were sparse in many ZCTAs. By pairing each exposed day with unexposed, temporally proximate controls, the within-community design uses limited exposed days more efficiently than a ZCTA-level time-stratified case-crossover analysis, yielding more stable estimates and fewer convergence problems. Previous studies have shown that alternative matching strategies, such as selecting controls from different years or including day-of-the-week fixed effects, do not have a meaningful influence on the results.12,49,50

Then, we utilized a spatial Bayesian hierarchical model to enhance the precision of the ZCTA-specific RERI estimates by leveraging the information from surrounding ZCTAs. A detailed theoretical explanation of this model is provided in a previous study.13 ZCTAs with implausibly high raw RERI values (>10) were excluded in this step. Based on the semivariogram (eFigure 1; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C318), we selected a spherical covariance structure and set starting values of 0.4 for the sill, 0.17 for the nugget, and 2.5 for the range. More details of the model specification can be found in eAppendix 5; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C318.

To assess the statistical precision of the pooled RERI estimates from the spatial Bayesian hierarchical model, we calculated the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) as the ratio of the mean to the standard deviation of the recovered samples. We applied the traditional cutoff of |SNR| > 2 to identify areas where the RERI estimates from the Bayesian hierarchical model are considered precise. The R package spBayes was used for the spatial Bayesian hierarchical model.51

Effect Modification by Community Characteristics

After estimating the pooled ZCTA-specific RERIs, we used meta-regressions to explore potential effect modification by community characteristics on the synergistic effects of extreme heat and NO2 exposures.12,13 Each community characteristic was analyzed separately using a univariate linear model, with the pooled ZCTA-specific RERIs from the spatial Bayesian hierarchical modelas the dependent variable. Results were reported as the increase in RERI per interquartile range (IQR) increase in each community characteristic. The R package meta was used for this step.52

Sensitivity Analyses

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of our results: (1) in the definition of extreme heat, we used the historical period of May to September instead of July and August to calculate the local threshold heat index value; (2) for the definition of extreme heat, we replaced ZCTA-specific heat index threshold values below 80 ℉ or above 105 ℉ with 80 ℉ and 105 ℉, respectively, to avoid implausibly high or low threshold values;53 (3) we calculated the heat index threshold values using data during the study period instead of the historical reference period; (4) we defined extreme NO2 exposure using an absolute threshold of 60 ppb for all ZCTAs; (5) instead of using the ZCTA-specific 85th percentile, we applied the 85th percentile of NO2 concentrations across all ZCTA-days as the threshold; and (6) we recalculated the ZCTA-specific NO2 threshold using a 3-year rolling basis instead of the full study period.

We explored the cumulative effects of the joint exposures over a lag period with a distributed lag linear model using the R package dlnm.54 The lag-response curve was specified as a natural cubic spline with 3 degrees of freedom over a lag period of 5 days. All analyses were performed in R Studio with R version 4.3.1.

State-level Synergistic Effects

The estimated individual, joint, and synergistic effects are shown in Figure 2 and eTable 1; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C318. Using the main exposure definitions (i.e., the 85th percentile), we observed RRs of 1.005 (95% CI = 1.003, 1.008) and 1.021 (95% CI = 1.019, 1.022) for the individual effects of extreme heat and extreme NO2, respectively, and an RR of 1.03 (95% CI = 1.02, 1.04) for their joint effect. The individual effect of extreme heat was larger when using maximum air temperature as the metric and smaller when using minimum air temperature (Figure 2A).

State-level estimated individual, joint, and synergistic effects of extreme heat and extreme NO2 exposures on daily cardiorespiratory hospitalizations by varying definitions of exposures. Estimated individual effects of exposure to extreme heat alone, extreme NO2 alone, and their joint effects (A), and their additive synergistic effect (RERI) (B), across different temperature metrics and exposure thresholds used in the exposure definitions. RERI indicates relative excess risk due to interaction; RR, rate ratio.

Under the main heat index exposure definition, the estimated RERI was 0.005 (95% CI = −0.002, 0.011), corresponding to approximately 13 additional cardiorespiratory hospitalizations per coexposed day in California during our study period (see details in eAppendix 6; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C318). With more stringent exposure thresholds, RERI estimates increased, though CIs widened. For example, using the 95th percentile as the cutoff threshold for heat index and NO2 concentration resulted in an estimated RERI of 0.018 (95% CI = 0.001, 0.035) (Figure 2B).

In a stratified analysis by urbanicity, although we did find a higher individual effect of extreme heat and extreme NO2 in metropolitan areas, no statistically meaningful differences were detected in the estimated joint effect or the RERI between these regions (eTable 2; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C318).

Effect Modification by Community Characteristics

Figure 4 shows the change in pooled ZCTA-specific RERIs per IQR increase in each community characteristic. We found that communities with higher synergistic effects tended to have greater population density, higher proportions of non-Hispanic Black, Pacific Islander, Asian, and Hispanic residents, and higher historical summer and winter temperatures. Conversely, lower synergistic effects were observed in communities with higher tree canopy coverage, per capita income, proportions of residents with a bachelor’s degree or higher, automobile ownership, health insurance coverage, and proportions of non-Hispanic White residents.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses indicated that the results remained generally robust under different exposure definitions. In the statewide case-crossover design, when defining extreme NO2 using an absolute threshold of 60 ppb (the Canadian Ambient Air Quality Standard for 1-hour NO2), the estimated individual effect of extreme NO2 and the synergistic effect of compound exposure were higher, with much wider CIs (eFigure 5; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C318). This result is expected, as with this high threshold, only 387 ZCTAs with particularly high NO2 levels were captured in the analysis (eTable 3; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C318). Despite the different sample sizes captured by the sensitivity analyses, the overall spatial pattern of the estimated ZCTA-specific raw RERIs remained consistent with the main analysis (eFigure 6; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C318). In terms of the lagged effect, the cumulative effect of the joint exposure reached its maximum 3 days after the exposure (cumulative RR: 1.04; 95% CI = 1.03, 1.05) (eFigure 7; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C318).

DISCUSSION

This study suggested an overall synergistic effect of extreme heat and NO2 exposures on cardiorespiratory hospitalizations in California (RERI: 0.005; 95% CI = −0.002, 0.011). Our findings revealed substantial spatial heterogeneity in this effect on a local scale. We also identified a set of community characteristics that may explain this spatial variability in vulnerability to the joint effects of heat and NO2. Our findings highlight the importance of integrating considerations of compound hazards and environmental justice into health impact assessments and climate change adaptation strategies.

The synergistic health effect of heat and NO2 exposures is relatively understudied in existing literature; however, our results generally align with those of a few previous studies on mortality. For example, using data from 482 cities globally, Rai et al.15 observed an increase in the effect of heat on cardiovascular and respiratory mortality with elevated NO2 levels (categorized as low, medium, and high). Another study in Northeast Asia also reported a higher NO2-mortality relationship at the 99th percentile of temperature compared with the 90th percentile, with a 2.4% increase in relative risk55 Other studies, however, did not detect any effect modification by NO2 levels on the health impacts of heat, or vice versa.16,17 This variability in previous findings could potentially be explained by the important spatial heterogeneity of the synergistic effects, which we demonstrated in this study.

In the United States, warning systems are established for both extreme heat and high air pollution, but through independent systems. For example, local National Weather Service offices typically issue heat advisories when excessive heat is expected in the next 24 to 36 hours and provide recommendations to inform the public to take precautions.56 In parallel, local air pollution control agencies call Air Quality Action Day when the Air Quality Index is forecast to reach the Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups range in the next day, providing activity guidance for the general public and particularly for sensitive groups.57,58 However, given the observed synergistic health effects between extreme heat and air pollution, a joint warning system that captures the co-occurrence of these two exposures and their synergistic impacts across specific communities could more effectively inform and prepare the public.59 However, despite the observed synergistic effects between extreme heat and extreme NO2 events, their joint occurrence remains relatively rare compared with single events (Table).

We found stronger synergistic effects in communities characterized by lower socioeconomic status, higher population density, reduced green space, greater proportions of racial and ethnic minority residents, and historically higher temperatures. In general, these findings align with those of previous studies, focusing on single exposures, identifying vulnerable populations affected by heat or NO2, and by the synergistic effects between heat and other air pollutants.12,13,33,60–62 Populations of racial and ethnic minorities may be particularly vulnerable to such environmental exposures due to factors including limited access to equitable healthcare, constrained financial resources to mitigate environmental hazards, a lack of awareness about the health impacts of these exposures, a lack of air conditioning, and the cumulative effects of chronic racial stressors.63–65 Contextual deprivation at the community level, such as household income, educational level, and health insurance prevalence, has been shown to be a robust socioeconomic characteristic that modifies the association between cardiovascular health and PM2.5.66 The modifying effects of greenness may potentially be explained by several factors, including mitigated air pollution and heat exposures, increased physical activity, reduced stress, enhanced social interaction, and other variables that influence susceptibility to environmental stressors.67–69 The observed higher synergistic effects of extreme heat and NO2 exposures in communities with higher historical temperatures are consistent with the results of our state-level case-crossover analysis (Figure 2), where the RERI estimates increased as more stringent thresholds were applied, indicating a stronger severity of the extreme heat events. Intervention strategies for such synergistic exposures might involve, for example, enhancing public health education about the combined effects of heat and air pollution or increasing access to air conditioners and air filters in affected areas.

In our study, the ZCTA-level RERI map identifies positive synergistic effects in the Central Valley; these results are similar to the results of prior California studies on the synergistic effects of heat with ozone and wildfire smoke.12,13 This suggests that the Central Valley may be particularly vulnerable as climate change increases the frequency of co-occurring hazards. Future studies may model major climate and air-pollution stressors jointly and estimate their overall health burden.

While NO2 concentrations have dramatically decreased in California over the past few decades, our findings indicate that the NO2-related hospitalizations may not be decreasing as drastically. This may be due to the increasing frequency of extreme heat events over the same time period, and the synergistic health effects of exposure to extreme heat and NO2.

Some limitations of our study should be noted. First, the NO2 concentrations were modeled and subject to uncertainty. However, given that the thresholds used to define extreme NO2 exposure (11.3 ppb to 52.3 ppb; eFigure 2B; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C318) were in the concentration range with good model performance (eAppendix 1; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C318), this limitation is unlikely to contribute to exposure misclassification in our analysis. Second, in the main analysis, extreme NO2 exposure was defined using ZCTA-specific thresholds to capture days with relatively high concentrations within each ZCTA and to ensure a sufficient sample size for estimating the ZCTA-specific RERI. This approach could potentially underestimate the effects of truly extreme NO2 conditions (eFigure 5; https://links.lww.com/EDE/C318), as it mixed days with varying absolute severities of NO2 exposure. Despite this conservative approach, our findings demonstrate adverse health effects from NO2 exposure even at concentrations below the EPA’s 1-hour NO2 standard. Third, this study only considered single-day exposures due to considerations of sample size, but exposure to such environmental hazards over multiple consecutive days (e.g., heatwaves) may have stronger health effects. Future studies are warranted to further explore the synergistic effects of such cumulative exposures. Fourth, our study excluded ZCTAs with less than 1,000 residents; therefore, our findings, including the urbanicity-stratified results, may not apply to very rural, sparsely populated regions.

In conclusion, this study found adverse health effects from compound exposure to extreme heat and NO2 in California, particularly in communities characterized by lower affluence, higher population density, larger minority populations, historically higher temperatures, and less green space. This synergistic effect suggests that the health benefits achieved through improvements in air quality may be compromised due to the increasing frequency of extreme heat events under climate change. Our findings underscore the urgent need for climate change adaptation policies that account for compound exposures to heat and air pollution. The findings ultimately could inform the development of targeted intervention strategies to protect vulnerable populations.
