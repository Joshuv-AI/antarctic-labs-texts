Reversible layered/non-layered phase transition in a topological semimetal
Authors: Qingrong Liang, Jiali Chen, Zhaoyang Xie, Xun Shi, Liu Yang, Wei Jiang, Jiadong Zhou, Shoujun Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe essence of phase transition is symmetry breaking according to Landau’s theory, which serves as a fundamental spatial metric to shape materials’ structures and facilitate the discovery of emergent quantum phases. However, isocompositional phase transitions between layered and non-layered structures are typically restricted to the same bonding network due to the significant differences in surface energies and out-of-plane chemical bonds. Here, we demonstrate a reversible phase transition between layered and non-layered phases in the semimetal PtBi2, where two phases exhibit diverse structural and physical properties in terms of atomic bonding types and topological properties. Reversible resistance hysteresis loops are revealed in the electrical measurements, which originate from the layered/non-layered phase transition based on the mechanism of intralayer splitting and interlayer reconstructions. Theoretical calculations further support that the interfacial phase transition not only produces a layered/non-layered structural change, but also structure-dependent topological invariants, establishing layered/non-layered phase engineering as a platform for exploring topological and functional quantum states.Subject terms: Phase transitions and critical phenomena, Electronic properties and materials, Topological insulators

Introduction

In solid-state physics, the essence of phase transitions is typically the breaking and restoration of symmetry. Based on the phase transition theories of Landau and Lifshitz, group theory has evolved from a static language purely describing crystal morphology into a physical criterion language that directly constrains free energy, order parameters, and predicts phase transition pathways. Phase transitions involve changes of order parameters or topological invariants, which can be used to manipulate structural, electrical, and magnetic properties of solid materials1. Phase transitions can be classified into three categories based on the variation trend of Gibbs free energy (G) governed by various order parameters: first-order phase transitions (e.g., structural phase transitions), second-order phase transitions (e.g., ferromagnetic and ferroelectric transitions, superconducting transitions), and higher-order phase transitions (e.g., Bose-Einstein condensate transitions)2–6. The first-order phase transitions refer to the discontinuity of first-order partial derivative of G, dG=SdT+∑ixidXi+∑jEjdDj, which can be triggered by various order parameters, such as temperature T, stress Xi, electrical field Dj, and so on. However, most of the phase transitions are studied within systems maintaining the same bonding networks, and theories typically focus on spontaneous symmetry breaking during cooling (the evolution from a high-symmetry phase to a subgroup with lower symmetry)7–10. The critical role of interlayer bonding changes driven by external fields is less investigated. The bonding type is highly coupled with space group symmetry, profoundly influencing surface energies and atomic bonding types, thereby modulating physical property behaviors. For example, a band gap is opened in ferroelectric SnTe as the thickness is thinned down from bulk to the atomically thin limit, its topological properties remain unchanged even though inversion symmetry is broken in the few-layer case. Switching between bonding networks (e.g., between layered and non-layered structures) can also induce transformations in structural symmetry, vibration modes, and topological phases. Therefore, interlayer bonding evolution could be an additional order parameter to modulate phases of matter.

Van der Waals (vdW) materials exhibit layer-dependent anisotropic properties due to the strong in-plane (IP) covalent interaction and relatively weak out-of-plane (OOP) vdW interaction. Phase transitions in layered materials differ significantly from those in non-layered bulk materials due to the anisotropic bonding types as well as kinetic mechanisms11,12. Typically, isocompositional phase transitions in layered materials involve the IP layer and do not change interlayer vdW interactions, like the phase transition of MoTe2 from 2H to 1 T’ phases13–19, which is essentially a symmetry change caused by intralayer lattice distortion. A pulse-triggered phase transition of magnetic FeSe2 from 1 T phase to 1 T’ phase produces a magnetic transition from antiferromagnetic to a ferromagnetic configuration20. Phase transition with an interlayer reconstruction was reported in In2Se321–24, but it still remains in a layered network. Even though a layered/non-layered phase transition was reported in TiSe2-Ti2Se925, this type of phase transition experiences atomic precipitation and composition variation, which is typically irreversible. The interlayer bonding type endows such systems with unique characteristics via short-range and long-range interactions: short-range interactions predominantly manifest in coordination numbers and chemical bond symmetry; in contrast, long-range interactions like electrostatic and magnetostatic forces may operate at scales far exceeding atomic size. When a material structure changes from a layered material to a non-layered material, its electronic properties also switch their topological nature due to layered structural confinement. However, systematic investigation of isocompositional transitions between layered and non-layered structures and structure-dependent topological phases remains rare26–29.

In this work, we report a reversible phase transition of PtBi2 between the layered ferroelectric (L-FE) phase and the non-layered T’ (NL-T’) phases driven by an external field stimulus. Under both voltage and current sweeps, the PtBi2 device exhibits a reversible resistance hysteresis loop, which originates from the interfacial Joule-heating effect and phase transition-induced contact resistance jump. The formation of an unexpected NL-T’ phase in the phase transition is confirmed by high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM). Half of the Pt atoms in the L-FE phase shift into the vdW gap to form covalent bonds with Bi atoms in the neighboring layer, thereby unveiling a unique mechanism of intralayer splitting and interlayer structural reconstruction. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations attest to the modulation of topological boundary states of the L-FE and NL-T’ phases, resulting in a layered/non-layered topological phase transition and providing an innovative pathway for predicting and designing incommensurate structures and distinctive quantum phases in topological materials.

Results

Temperature-induced phase transition in PtBi2

According to crystal structure analysis based on theoretical calculations30, PtBi2 exhibits rich polymorphic states with a series of stable structural phases, including L-FE phase, layered T (L-T) phase, and the proposed NL-T’ phase in this work. The L-FE phase of PtBi2 is the layered trigonal structure with the lowest energy according to both experimental and theoretical results. The crystal structure of pristine PtBi2 belongs to the non-centrosymmetric space group P31m (157), where three mirror planes are along the a, b axes with lattice constants of a = b = 6.606 Å and c = 6.111 Å. Each layer of the L-FE PtBi2 consists of two Bi layers and one Pt layer, as shown in Fig. 1a. Bi atoms in the top layer are located nearly in a plane with the shortest Bi-Bi connections forming a twisted Kagome lattice. Bi atoms in the bottom layer are highly corrugated and can be regarded as a decorated honeycomb lattice. The splitting of Bi atoms leads to a 60° rotation of the mirror plane and generates polarity along the [001] stacking direction and an OOP ferroelectricity. The L-T phase of PtBi2 is also predicted to be a layered structure with the centrosymmetric space group P−3m1 (164) with lattice constants of a = b = 4.039 Å and c = 5.608 Å, as shown in Fig. 1b. The interlayer spacing difference between the L-FE phase and the L-T phase brings an inevitable volume variation and the introduction of interlayer strains at the domain boundary if a phase transition occurs between the two phases. Therefore, we proposed a NL-T’ phase with the same composition as the L-T phase by shifting half of the Pt atoms to the vdW gap based on theoretical calculations. After sufficient relaxation, the NL-T’ phase belongs to the space group of the centrosymmetric Pnnm (58) with lattice constants of a = 6.807 Å, b = 5.604 Å, and c = 4.192 Å, respectively (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 1). Phonon spectra NL-T’ phase by first-principles calculations show that there is no imaginary frequency, attesting its stability as shown in Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 2.

We conducted second-harmonic generation (SHG) and Raman spectroscopy measurements to study the temperature-dependent phase transitions. Figure 1e is the polarization-resolved SHG plot of one L-FE PtBi2 flake on a SiO2/Si substrate at room temperature, exhibiting its intrinsic sixfold rotational symmetry. Next, we tested the temperature-dependent in-situ SHG spectra of the L-FE PtBi2 flake. We found that the SHG intensity suddenly drops as the temperature increases to around 650 K (as shown in Fig. 1f, g), indicating that the structure of PtBi2 changes from the original non-centrosymmetric state to a centrosymmetric state, implying the occurrence of a phase transition. Subsequently, we performed temperature-dependent in-situ Raman measurements of the L-FE PtBi2 sample as shown in Fig. 1h. The Raman spectra show that there are four Raman-active phonon modes at 57.4 cm⁻¹, 66.9 cm⁻¹, 91.1 cm⁻¹ (E), and 107.5 cm⁻¹ (A1), respectively. All Raman peaks show clear redshifts as temperature increases, and the E Raman peak of 91.1 cm⁻¹ gradually disappears around 675 K. Theoretical analysis demonstrates the visualization of two Raman-active phonon modes shown in Fig. 1i and Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4, indicating that the absence of the E mode is related to the reconstruction of the bottom Bi layer. A Raman mapping of the derivative Raman spectra as a function of temperature clearly shows the trends of the temperature-dependent Raman modes. Temperature-dependent SHG spectra are consistent with Raman spectra, indicating that the original L-FE phase undergoes a phase transition near 650 K.

Current-induced resistance hysteresis loops

To explore the electrical properties of PtBi2, we prepared a Hall-bar device with electrodes of Cr/Au (5/50 nm) and conducted both two-probe and four-probe measurements of this device (detailed information on device fabrication and electrical measurements, and resistance calculations can be found in Method and Supplementary Note S2). The device was tested according to the schematic diagram shown in Fig. 2a, and the inset is the corresponding optical image. The four-probe resistance of the PtBi2 device (green points in Fig. 2b) rises with increasing temperature, indicating its metallic nature. However, the total contact resistance of the two-probe test has a complicated dependence on temperature. The contact resistance gradually decreases with increasing temperature in the range of 10 K to 200 K, but turns to rise in the range of 200 K to 300 K. Moreover, the contact resistance is approximately two orders of magnitude higher than four-probe resistance, indicating a serious Joule heating effect at the PtBi2/electrode interfaces.

Interestingly, a reversible hysteresis loop is observed in the two-probe I-V curve in Fig. 2c at 10 K. The device experiences an abrupt switch from a high-resistance state (HRS) to a low-resistance state (LRS) when the bias (Vb) rises above 0.8 V. The LRS maintains when Vb sweeps from 2 V to −2 V, indicating that the LRS is stable. Importantly, the LRS abruptly switches back to HRS until Vb sweeps back close to −0.8 V from −2 V. To clarify the abnormal resistance state switching, we plotted the R-V loops of two-probe and four-probe resistance tests at 300 K in Fig. 2d. The absence of resistance state switching in the four-probe R-V curve indicates that the resistance state switching occurs at the electrode interfacial region rather than the middle region of PtBi2. Compared with nearly constant four-probe resistance, the two-probe resistance first gradually drops with increasing voltage due to the thermal effect at contact resistance regions, then suddenly transitions from HRS to LRS at Vb = 1.5 V. The LRS remains stable after a backward sweep to −2 V. Finally, the LRS transits back to HRS at Vb = −1.5 V in a forward sweep, making a hat-like R-V curve in Fig. 2d. We confirmed the stability of resistance state switching between HRS and LRS by multiple tests and the current sweeping mode in Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7.

To further study hat-like R-V curves, we measured temperature-dependent R-V curves, as shown in Fig. 2e. We found that the critical voltage for triggering the resistance-state switching depends on temperature. It increases from 0.8 V to 1.6 V from 10 K to 240 K, but turns to fall from 240 K to 300 K. This temperature-dependent trend is consistent with the trend of temperature-dependent contact resistance in Fig. 2b, implying the same origin of the two trends. The unique hat-like R-V curves indicate that the mechanism of this resistance state switching is related to a first-order phase transition rather than a memristive or ferroelectric switching. The large contact resistance generates a strong thermal effect and the rise of the device temperature at the interfaces of device/electrode, leading to a Joule-heating-induced structural phase transition from the high-resistance L-FE phase to the proposed NL-T’ phase. The NL-T’ phase has a similar energy with the L-FE phase based on theoretical calculations and can transit back to the L-FE phase under the fast-cooling process. Noting that the reversible phase transition can not be triggered just by the unipolar voltage sweeping, as shown in supplementary Fig. 8. The phenomenon that phase transition from NL-T’ phase to the L-FE phase mainly occurs at the negative bias might be attributed to the Peltier effect-induced cooling power24. The Peltier coefficient difference between NL-T’ PtBi2 and Cr/Au metal generates an apparent energy absorption rate and quickly decreases the interface temperature. Therefore, back-sweeping at negative voltage can enhance the cooling effect at the NL-T’ PtBi2/Cr/Au interface and trigger the transition from the NL-T’ phase back to the L-FE phase.

Next, we simulated the phase transition process with electrothermal simulations using finite element modeling (simulation details and parameters are described in the methods). Figure 2f shows the temperature distribution of a Hall-bar device with the same conductivity as the experimental results, showing that the temperature rises only at the two electrodes where current flows. Spatially dependent temperature plots with different applied biases are shown in Fig. 2g, showing the voltage-induced inhomogeneous temperature distribution. Note that the temperature distribution at two electrodes is nonuniform, revealing spatially and current direction-dependent heating effects. We conducted R-V curves simulation based on experimental conductance and Joule-heating effect, and the simulated results are consistent with the experimental results in Fig. 2h.

Atomic structures of the L-FE and NL-T’ phases

Precise control of phase transition by the applied bias enables us to unveil the atomic structure of L-FE and NL-T’ phase by HAADF-STEM. Figure 3a is the atomic cross-section images of a PtBi2 device at the view plane of (010) before testing. The distorted Pt-Bi bonds produce an OOP L-FE polarization, consistent with the previous report31. The atomic structures can also be seen from another view plane of (210) in Fig. 3b, which matches well with the calculated STEM images. The c lattice constant of the L-FE phase is 0.611 nm, consistent with theoretical values. After applying a positive bias, the L-FE phase below the electrode starts to change to the proposed NL-T’ phase in Fig. 1c. As the NL-T’ phase is stable at room temperature, it allows us to see the lattice structures by HAADF-STEM, as shown in Fig. 3c, d with the same dual-view directions. The NL-T’ phase has b lattice constant of 0.566 nm (the vertical direction) and does not have a vdW gap. Two phases can be reflected by Fast Fourier transforms (FFT) shown in Supplementary Fig. 18, where FFT patterns of both the L-FE and NL-T’ phases are consistent with the corresponding structures.

To clarify the detailed phase transition process, we analyzed the transition pathway between L-FE and NL-T’ phases. Figure 3e shows that both the L-FE (left part) and NL-T’ (right part) phases are present at the domain boundary region. A boundary between two phases reveals that the NL-T’ phase is transformed from the L-FE phase by reconfiguring the Bi-Pt-Bi trilayer. First, the six-membered Bi-Bi-Pt-Pt-Bi-Bi rings in the L-FE phase are deformed, and Bi-Pt-Bi atoms are perpendicularly aligned. Second, the Pt atomic layer in the middle of the Bi-Pt-Bi layer is split into two Pt layers, and one Pt layer shifts down to the vdW gap by crossing the bottom Bi layer (see white dashed lines in Fig. 3e). Finally, Pt atoms in this specific Pt layer form covalent bonds with top Bi atoms in the adjacent PtBi2 layer. The intralayer splitting and interlayer structural reconstructions mechanism is also confirmed by STEM in another view direction in Fig. 3f, highlighting the specific phase transition pathway related to IP deformation and OOP interlayer atomic migration. We also confirmed the specific atomic migration mechanism by STEM along non-high-symmetry directions (see more images in Supplementary Figs. 21). The key difference between two phases is the interlayer atomic bonding types: in the L-FE phase atoms are chemically bonded in a single layer and interacted with other atoms in the adjacent layer via vdW forces, while in the NL-T’ phase atoms are bonded at non-layered network. Consequently, a Bi-Pt-Bi trilayer in a single layer of the L-FE phase gradually transforms to a Bi-Pt-Bi-Pt quadralayer in NL-T’ phase, leading to a reversible layered/non-layered phase transition between the P31m group and the higher-symmetry Pnnm (No. 58) group (see supplementary Movies 1 and 2).

PtBi2 exhibits rich phases according to theoretical calculations and the L-FE phase PtBi2 is the common one investigated in theoretical and experimental reports30,32. Our NL-T’ phase is similar to the structure of the L-T phase but lacks a vdW gap. Figure 3g, h are the atomic intensity profiles following the white dashed lines in Fig. 3a and c, showing the contrast of interatomic distances between L-FE and NL-T’ phases. Figure 3i shows the energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) results of two phases, confirming that the NL-T’ phase has the same composition as the L-FE phase (see Supplementary Fig. 23 and X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy in Supplementary Fig. 24). The isocompositional phase transition ensures the stability and reversibility of the resistance hysteresis loop in the electrical measurements.

Mechanism of the layered/non-layered phase transition

Understanding the interlayer bonding reconstruction involving IP and OOP atomic reconfiguration is key to designing and exploring distinctive quantum phases via structural connectivity modulation. We performed DFT calculations to evaluate the energy barriers of different transition steps during intralayer splitting and interlayer reconstruction, as shown in Fig. 4a. The energy difference between NL-T’ phase and L-FE phase is around 3.12 meV/f.u., suggesting that the two phases are energetically competitive and may coexist at room temperature. Also, the T phase present in the NEB reaction pathway is proved to be a metastable state, which is consistent with phonon vibration spectra calculations. All three phases have been confirmed in the inset STEM images in Fig. 4a. Notably, the interlayer spacing of the L-T phase measured in our experiment is approximately 5.76 Å, larger than the theoretical value, indicating that it exists inevitable interlayer strains. The lattice mismatch-induced strain can also be seen in the strain analysis of mixed phases in Supplementary Fig. 26, which might promote the layered/non-layered phase transition. Based on the experimental and calculated results, we can construct the detailed phase transition processes. Firstly, an IP atomic reconstruction is required to transit from the L-FE phase to the L-T phase by relaxing the six-membered ring to the octahedral coordination. In this step, there are no covalent bonds breaking, and vdW gaps still exist. However, the vdW gap of the L-T phase is larger than the theoretical value, indicating that the L-T phase is unstable with strong lattice strains. Secondly, half of the Pt atoms in the L-T phase split and shift down to the vdW gap to relax the intrinsic strains. In this step, the splitting Pt atoms experience covalent bond breaking with top Bi atoms in its Bi-Pt-Bi layer and bond forming with top Bi atoms in adjacent Bi-Pt-Bi layer. Finally, the reconstructed structure transforms to the NL-T’ phase after the lattice softening. Figure 4b shows the atomic reconstruction processes related to the intralayer splitting and interlayer reconstruction. A schematic in Fig. 4c demonstrates the interlayer reconstruction processes from a trilayer to a quadrilayer in the layered/non-layered phase transition. Therefore, we can conclude that the L-FE phase finally transforms to the NL-T’ phase at the contact interface via an L-T phase, producing a mixture of the NL-T’ phase and the L-FE phase in a domain form.

It requires multiple steps to finish the layered/non-layered atomic reconstruction due to the high energy barrier. Multiple current jumps were observed in the I-V curve shown in Fig. 4d when the bias rises. The multi-conductance states indicate that it exists multiple NL-T’ phase states. STEM images in Fig. 4e reveals that the interlayer reconstruction of Pt atoms is partially finished in the part of NL-T’ phase, attesting the existence of multiple NL-T’ phases. These phases possess different chemical potentials, which can be used to tune the device contact resistances and generate resistance jumps in the electrical measurements. Therefore, the rich phase states of PtBi2 allow us to explore distinctive quantum states and electronic devices.

Topological evolution by layered/non-layered phase transition

Traditional topological phase transitions typically achieve the transition of topological invariants by adjusting a certain physical parameter in the system (such as spin-orbit coupling (SOC) strength, lattice distortion, or magnetic field)33. However, we found that the interlayer crystalline symmetry can also act as a key parameter to drive topological phase transitions. For layered/non-layered materials, there is the following symmetry-dependent topological invariants: Z2 (layered)=vkz=0, Z2 (non-layered)=(v0;vkx=0.5,vky=0.5,vkz=0.5). In the specific material PtBi2, both the L-FE and NL-T’ phases exhibit topological semimetal behavior in the absence of spin-orbit coupling (SOC), as indicated in Fig. 5a and b (red solid lines). When SOC is included, distinct topological phases emerge. For the L-FE PtBi2, a band gap opens along the K-Γ path (highlighted by a green circle in Fig. 5a) and topological invariant is calculated to (0;001) by Wannier charge center evolution (see Supplementary Fig. 30). Thus, topological edge states of multiple-layer PtBi2 are similar with that of monolayer PtBi2. The topological invariant across the kz = 0 plane is determined to be Z2 = 1, indicating that the L-FE phase is a spin Hall insulator. For the NL-T’ phase, band gaps open along the Γ-X and Γ-Z directions as highlighted by green circles in Fig. 5b. The Z2 topological invariant is determined to be (1;000) through examination of Wannier charge center evolution across six time-reversal invariant planes in the Brillouin zone, confirming it as a strong topological insulator phase.

The non-trivial topological invariants imply the existence of protected surface states. We therefore calculated surface states for different crystal planes of both phases (Fig. 5c–f). For the L-FE phase, topologically protected edge states appear clearly along the Γ’-M’ path on the (100) plane (Fig. 5c), while no edge states are observed on the (001) plane (Fig. 5d). The crystal plane-dependent edge states indicate that the L-FE phase is a topological semimetal with only IP edge states. ARPES results show the surface Brillouin zones and topological Fermi arcs in L-FE phase PtBi2 in Supplementary Fig. 31. In the NL-T’ phase, distinct topological surface states are observed: Dirac cones projected onto the (100) surface along the Γ’-Y’ path and onto the (010) surface along the Γ’-X’ path (Fig. 5e, f), further verifying its layered topological nature. Both topological invariant calculations and edge/surface state analysis confirm that in the layered/non-layered phase transition, a spin Hall insulator (the L-FE phase) transforms to a strong topological insulator (the NL-T’ phase).

The layered/non-layered phase transition can be understood according to the symmetry-dependent topological invariants. Under specific coupling modes and symmetry protection, the topological invariant (vkz=0 = 1) in the L-FE phase system can naturally extend to (1;000) in the NL-T’ phase system, realizing the topological states mapping from a layered spin Hall insulator to a strong non-layered topological insulator. The boundary states between layered and non-layered topological systems are schematically shown in Fig. 5g, where a topological state switch is proposed based on a layered/non-layered topological heterostructure. This type of symmetry-governed topological phase transition provides an innovative means for the design of topological materials and reveals the mechanism of geometric-topological evolution. The structural phase transition in PtBi2 not only allows us to explore unexpected topological phases but also provides a route to design multifunctional heterostructures. For example, a layered/non-layered topological interface can be generated by applying an external electrical field based on the structure-dependent topological natures in PtBi2. Interfaces between different topological phases are natural places to look for enhanced electron-electron interactions, potentially leading to distinctive magnetic ordering, charge density waves, or exotic superconductivity. Therefore, our phase transition technique introduces layered/non-layered phase engineering into material design and device fabrication, opening up an unexpected avenue for exploring condensed-matter physics and practical applications.

In summary, a reversible layered/non-layered phase transition of PtBi2 between the L-FE phase and the NL-T’ topological phase is achieved by applying an external field. A Joule-heating-induced reversible resistance hysteresis loop is related to the layered/non-layered phase transition. The atomic structures of NL-T’ phase and formation processes are confirmed by HAADF-STEM results, indicating that this phase transition has a specific formation mechanism of the intralayer splitting and interlayer reconstruction. By DFT calculations, we attest to the stability, transition energy barrier of the NL-T’ phase, as well as its topological nature. Our work demonstrates the pivotal role of interlayer bonding types and symmetry in governing the kinetics of phase transitions, offers innovative insights into the kinetics of symmetry-dependent phase transitions and paves a path for discovering topological quantum phases and developing multifunctional quantum devices.

Methods

Device fabrication and measurement

In a vacuum glove box, PtBi2 crystals were repeatedly exfoliated and stacked on SiO2 using the mechanical exfoliation method. Then, metal electrodes (5/50 nm chromium/gold, Cr/Au) were formed through electron-beam lithography and evaporation. All electrical measurements were carried out on a low-temperature probe station (Lakeshore) in a vacuum and dark environment using a semiconductor parameter analyzer (Keysight, B1500). Micro-Raman spectroscopy (WITec alpha 200 R) was used to verify the phases of PtBi2 at different temperatures. KPFM and AFM measurements were performed using a multimode atomic force microscope (Bruker) with a sample probe having a Co/Cr conductive coating and a spring constant of 5 N/m to examine the thickness and surface morphology of PtBi2 flakes. The SHG signal was measured using a horizontal-polarization reflection-geometry vertical microscope system equipped with a X100 objective (NA = 0.5) and a spectrometer. A fiber-pulsed laser with a central wavelength of 1550 nm, a repetition frequency of 18.5 MHz, and a pulse width of 8.8 ps was used as the fundamental pump radiation. Raman spectroscopy analysis was conducted using a LabRAM HR Evolution spectrometer with a 532 nm laser. The PL spectrum was collected using a 532 nm laser for excitation.

HAADF-STEM measurement

The specimens for STEM studies were prepared by a focused ion beam in a Helios G4 UC dual-beam scanning electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Few-layer PtBi2 flakes were mechanically exfoliated from bulk crystals and then transferred to TEM grids by FIB. For HAADF imaging, the beam current was set to ~80 pA. Atomic-scale HAADF-STEM images of PtBi2 before and after phase transition were obtained using a Titan Themis G3 60-300 microscope from FEI.

DFT calculations

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio Simulation Package (VASP 5.4). Electron-ion interactions were treated with projector-augmented wave (PAW) potentials within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) form. Van der Waals interactions were accounted for via the DFT-D3 functional of Grimme34–36. The first Brillouin zone (BZ) was sampled using Monkhorst-Pack grids with resolutions of 9 × 9 × 5, 12 × 12 × 6, and 5 × 6 × 8 k-points for the L-FE, L-T, and NL-T’ phases, respectively. A Gaussian smearing width of 0.03 eV was applied, with electronic convergence criteria set to 10−7 eV and force tolerance of 10−3 eV/Å for geometry optimization. Phase transition pathways were determined through the solid-state nudged elastic band (SS-NEB) method37. The energy obtained in the NEB reaction pathway is the value per formula unit relative to the elemental solids. Phonon spectra were computed using the finite displacement method as implemented in PHONOPY. In order to calculate the surface states, we use the tight-binding method by combining wannier9038 and wanniertools39.

Temperature simulations

This project uses the COMSOL software’s solid flow heat transfer module and electrical flow module for comprehensive calculations. Electrical flow module is ∇⋅J=Qf,v, J=δE+Je, E=−∇V, where J is the electrical current density, Qj,v is the electric charge per unit volume, E is the electric field, Je is the current introduced by external power sources, V is the electric potential, δ is the material conductivity. Heat Transfer Module is ρCpu⋅∇T+∇⋅q=Q, q=−k∇T, where ρ is the material density, Cp is the material’s specific heat, u is the thermal conductivity field,T is the temperature, q is the heat flux,Q is the heat source term, k is the material’s thermal conductivity. To simulate the temperature distribution of the device, the COMSOL software package (COMSOL Multiphysics, version 5.6) was used to construct a specific model. The length of the electrode was divided into 20 μm, 4 μm, 50 nm, and the width of the device was set as 30 μm, 30 μm, 150 nm, while the height of SiO2/Si was 100 μm, 100 μm, 80 nm/500 μm. The heat conduction rates of L-FE PtBi2 and NL-T’ PtBi2 were calculated using DFT, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 11.
