A commentary on ‘Clinical outcomes and quality of life after contemporary isolated coronary bypass grafting: a prospective cohort study’
Authors: Tiangang Zhou, Yidan Wang, Guoying Wang, Qingwei Liu, Zhiming Jiang
Published: 2023

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the most common type of heart disease and the third leading cause of death in both women and men worldwide1,2. Coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) is the most performed procedure in patients with multivessel CAD. Despite the increasing number of CABG surgical procedures, there is a lack of data reporting on the perioperative outcomes of patients with CAD undergoing CABG surgery. Sandner et al.3 performed a prospective cohort study to report clinical outcomes and quality of life in European patients undergoing isolated CABG. The results showed that contemporary European patients undergoing isolated CABG have a low 1-year clinical event rate and an improved quality of life.

The etiology of CAD is multifactorial, stemming from both modifiable and nonmodifiable risk factors: age, smoking status, diabetes, obesity, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia4,5. All but age are modifiable factors. Given the high prevalence of CAD globally, CABG is performed on patients across all age ranges. Age plays a role as both a cause of CAD and as an effect modifier of CAD treatment outcomes6. It is important to characterize the effects of age on operative outcomes to inform patient and provider decisions. Several studies have been published that report on the effects of age on the outcomes of CABG, allowing for comparisons of the efficacy of different operative strategies within different populations. However, heterogeneity between study populations and methods creates difficulty in formulating a comprehensive algorithm for treating CAD within different age ranges.

In our institution’s experience, we suggested that (1) intervention with arterial grafts at younger ages produces the best balance of high survival with low adverse events, (2) that arterial grafting (especially multiarterial grafting) is superior to the use of vein grafting until the age of 80 years given currently available data, and (3) that advanced age alone should not be a factor in deterring surgeons from intervening with CABG on an individual. In addition, low left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) is an important predictor of mortality and morbidity after CABG; however, CABG is the treatment of choice in patients with impaired LVEF and is associated with better survival compared to medical therapy alone. CABG in such patients is associated with higher postoperative morbidity and mortality compared to those with normal left ventricular function. However, patients with impaired LVEF have higher preoperative comorbid conditions. Patients with different ejection fraction levels may have different comorbidities, which will affect the postoperative outcomes.

There are several grafting strategies that can be employed during CABG, but there exists no comprehensive guideline on which strategies would confer the greatest benefit and least risk to patients based on their age. Both interinstitutional cooperation and cooperation on an international scale are essential for future studies to generate data with sufficient power to resolve the facets of each group in the endeavor to craft robust CABG guidelines.
