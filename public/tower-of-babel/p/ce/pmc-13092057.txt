Response on the commentary on “Relevance of cerebral microbleeds with leaky blood brain barrier in traumatic brain injury and protective role of interferon-gamma: a cohort study”
Authors: Xuan Vinh To, Fatima Nasrallah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Partial-volume effects impact DCE-MRI and other modalities.•Tractography and structural connectivity (SC) are layers of modelling assumptions.•SC is model-dependent and requires validation against clinical outcomes.•Peripheral sampling is imperfect but practical.•Additional MRI sequences and serial samplings are done in our more recent studies.

We write this in response to the commentaries by Manikya et al. on our study [1]. The commentaries raised interesting points, which warrant careful consideration.

While the commentary authors used Yi et al. 2018 [2] as the citation for the “in small foci such as microbleeds, voxel contamination from adjacent normal appearing brain may influence permeability estimation” claim, a close reading of the referenced study indicated that the study did not present results that directly support this specific assertion. Nevertheless, the broader concept that Patlak-derived volume transfer constant maps are susceptible to partial volume effects is reasonable, though this limitation is also true for nearly all Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) modalities which often have even lower spatial resolution than the dynamic contrast enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI) acquisition used in our study. The commentary of the usefulness of absolute T1 mapping and other MRI modalities is indeed valid; our study reported on data acquired using relatively older sequences and study designs, and we acknowledged their limitations. In our more recent MRI studies of traumatic brain injury, we have incorporated T1 mapping and multi-shell diffusion MRI in addition to DCE-MRI [3].

The suggestion of using diffusion MRI and network-based analyses is common, in our experience—we received similar peer reviewers’ requests in other studies for tractography analysis (e.g. [4]). Nevertheless, we wish to clarify that using diffusion MRI as quantitative measure of structural “connectivity strength” (as we interpreted the commentary to be) is non-trivial. Network-based analyses of diffusion MRI data typically rely on tractography, as has been cautioned in the literature, streamline counts should not be interpreted as “fibre counts” or “connectivity strength” [5]. The tractography-reconstructed streamlines are probabilistic (where the fibres might be): when cross-validated with tracer injections (where the fibres actually are), variations in the reconstruction specifications (e.g. step, curvature, cut-off, number of streamlines) lead to monotonic trade-offs between true positive (TPR) and false negative rates (FPR) [6]. Gains in TPR were concurrent in gains in FPR and there was not an obvious combination of settings where, for example, disproportionate gains in TPR were only accompanied by minimal increases in FPR. Thus, tractography-based network metrics layer additional modelling assumptions on top of those used to fit the diffusion signal.

Furthermore, it should be noted that structural connectivity (SC) derived from diffusion-MRI tractography provides a map of potential anatomical pathways. SC constrains and influences neural dynamics but does not uniquely determine it; there are numerous network communication models that can be used to estimate neural function from SC based on specific neural communication strategies (e.g., shortest path routing, diffusion, path transitivity, search information, or navigation) [7]. These network communication models predict different functional connectivity (FC) patterns based on the same SC data. These FC predictions can be compared to the observed FC (e.g. from resting-state functional MRI) to derive SC-FC coupling measures [7]. Comparison of these coupling measures across groups can reveal the differences in FC related to the SC architecture as well as the putative communication strategies. In short, conclusions about SC and how that relates to FC or functional outcomes involve several layers of modelling assumptions of indirect measurements of structural and functional features. Consequently, integrating putative SC with microbleeds location and permeability, and linking these to potential functional differences may not be straightforward. Regardless of the layers of modelling assumptions, modelling results are only validated through correlations with functional outcomes or group differences. In our study, since data on the actual functional outcomes were limited, patient stratification was done using Glasgow Coma Scale-based Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI) severity stratification, which in turn was originally validated by correlations with long-term TBI functional outcomes [8]. Accordingly, the utility of our cerebral microbleeds’ permeability stratification in our study was still compared against a proxy of long-term TBI outcomes.

With regards to the commentary authors’ concern about how peripheral cytokine concentrations do not necessarily reflect intracerebral signalling, we concurred and made the same points in an earlier report of the same dataset [9]. Nevertheless, we note that blood, plasma, or serum levels of analytes are still popular biomarkers for the practical matter of accessibility. Blood sampling is minimally invasive; this is an important consideration given that MRI is non-invasive. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) sampling is invasive and difficult to justify ethically, unless CSF samples are accessible as part of medically indicated care, e.g. ventricular-peritoneal shunt in severe TBIs. We also agree with the commentary on the need for serial cytokine sampling and this has been implemented in our subsequent TBI studies [3].
