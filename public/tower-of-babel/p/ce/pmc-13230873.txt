Dietary Bacillus subtilis modulates rumen epithelial barrier function and immune responses in weaned Hu sheep
Authors: Haibi Zhao, Lishan Liu, Zhengwen Wang, Yuzhu Sha, Shuzhen Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated 30- and 60-day dietary Bacillus subtilis supplementation on rumen development and immune indices in weaned male Hu sheep. Forty-five lambs (60 ± 10 d) were randomized to a basal diet (CON) or the basal diet plus B. subtilis at 100 mg/kg (BSN) or 200 mg/kg (BSH) (n = 15). After 30 d, rumen papilla height was significantly higher in the BSN group than in the CON and BSH groups (P = 0.026); Total volatile fatty acids (TVFA) and propionate were significantly higher in the BSN and BSH groups than in the CON group (P < 0.001); Acetate/propionate was significantly lower in the BSN and BSH groups than in the CON group (P = 0.010). BSH increased the relative abundance of beneficial bacteria (including cellulolytic taxa and Lactobacillus), and serum IL-1β decreased; rumen epithelial IL-1β, CCL2, and NF-κB p65 were downregulated in BSN and BSH (P < 0.05). After 60 d, papilla height and total VFA remained higher in BSN/BSH and the acetate to propionate ratio remained lower in BSH, with sustained increases in beneficial taxa (P < 0.05). Serum IgA and IFN-γ increased and IL-1β decreased in BSN/BSH, and epithelial NF-κB p65 decreased in BSN (P < 0.05). Therefore, this study demonstrates that a 30-day supplementation of B. subtilis is effective, while for longer-term (60-day) use, a dosage of 100 mg/kg is optimal for enhancing rumen health and immune function in sheep.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47065-w.Keywords: Bacillus subtilis, Immunity, Rumen, Serum, Gene expressionSubject terms: Biochemistry, Immunology, Microbiology, Physiology, Zoology

Introduction

In animal husbandry, the use of antibiotics enhances growth performance but also disrupts the microbial balance of the host. Consequently, this issue in livestock production has urged the development of probiotics. Probiotics offer numerous benefits to the host1. They not only improve growth performance but also promote a favorable microbial community by enhancing gut microbiota balance, thereby positively influencing metabolism and immune performance2,3. Bacillus subtilis, as a probiotic, can synthesize various enzymes that support feed digestion and absorption. Moreover, B. subtilis produces spores that are highly resilient, stable, and resistant to oxidation, extrusion, heat, and varying pH levels. These spores stay active in acidic gastric environments, thus playing a crucial role in supporting the health of both humans and animals4,5. Research has shown that numerous metabolites of B. subtilis exhibit antiviral effects, making it an important source of natural antiviral drugs6. This bacterium has been utilized as a feed additive to modulate immune function in animals, counteract pathogenic bacteria, and promote growth7,8. Our previous study indicated that feeding B. subtilis had no significant effect on the growth performance of weaned male sheep, but did enhance rumen fermentation, improved the rumen fermentation environment, increased the activity of rumen digestive enzymes and helped maintain rumen microbial homeostasis in sheep. In addition, supplementation increased the relative abundance of fiber-degrading bacteria. However, prolonged supplementation at a higher dose (200 mg/kg) appeared to weaken this regulatory effect and was associated with an imbalance in the rumen microbiota9,10. Other studies have demonstrated that feeding B. subtilis to chickens enhances the serum bactericidal activity in hens4. A single oral administration of B. subtilis was shown to protect mice from diseases induced by the intestinal pathogen Citrobacter rodentium, without signs of diarrhea, maintaining normal levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and normal intestinal structure11,12. Feeding B. subtilis to calves improves their intestinal microbiota and promotes rumen development13. Currently, B. subtilis is primarily used and studied as a feed additive for monogastric animals, with limited research on its application in ruminants. To our knowledge, few studies have investigated its effects on immune function in ruminants. Therefore, based on past research, we aimed to explore the effects of feeding B. subtilis to sheep on rumen tissue development, rumen microflora structure, rumen epithelium, and serum immune factors.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All experimental procedures involving animals were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Animal Husbandry, Pasture and Green Agriculture, Gansu Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Approval No. 2021.010). All animal procedures and experimental methods were performed in accordance with the relevant institutional guidelines and regulations. Throughout the experimental period, all animals were managed and cared for in accordance with the NIH guidelines, and the study is reported in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Management of animal husbandry and sample collection

Forty-five weaned male Hu sheep of sheep of similar age [(60 ± 10) d] and body weights [(19.51 ± 2.07) kg] were assigned to three treatments (n = 15): (CON) control, BSN (100 mg/kg Bacillus subtilis), and BSH (200 mg/kg Bacillus subtilis).The basal diet was formulated according to the Nutritional Requirements of Meat Sheep (NY/T 816–2021), and its ingredient composition and nutrient levels are shown in Table 1. The B. subtilis preparation used in the study was purchased from a bioengineering company in Shandong Province, having a viable bacteria count of ≥ 1 × 1011 CFU/g. The experimental period lasted 70 d (10 d adaptation + 60 d trial). Pens (2.5 m2 per sheep) were cleaned and disinfected before the trial; sheep had ad libitum access to feed and water and were fed twice daily at 08:00 and 17:00. Bacillus subtilis was thoroughly mixed into the morning feed. Venous blood was collected before morning feeding on days 30 and 60; serum was obtained by centrifugation, aliquoted into 2-mL tubes, and stored at − 80 °C. At each sampling time (days 30 and 60), six sheep per group were randomly selected for slaughter after 12 h fasting (water withheld for the final 4 h) to collect rumen tissue and rumen contents. At each sampling time point (days 30 and 60), six Hu sheep per group were randomly selected for terminal sampling using a computer-generated random number list (Microsoft Excel RAND function) from the animals available at that time point and were slaughtered after 12 h of fasting (water withheld for the final 4 h) to collect rumen tissue and rumen contents. After six sheep per group were removed for terminal sampling at day 30, the remaining sheep continued on their assigned diets until the next sampling time point (day 60). Animals not selected for terminal sampling after completion of the experiment were returned to the farm for routine production management. Rumen contents were collected immediately after slaughter; pH was measured promptly, and aliquots were prepared for NH₃-N, volatile fatty acid analysis, and microbial DNA extraction (stored at − 80 °C until analysis). All experimental sheep were raised and slaughtered at the off-site fattening facility in Pingshanhu Mongolian Township, Ganzhou District, Zhangye City, Gansu Province, China. Slaughter was performed using a two-step protocol: electrical stunning to induce immediate unconsciousness followed by cervical exsanguination. Ruminal abdominal sac tissue (1 cm × 1 cm) and rumen epithelial tissue (5 mm × 5 mm × 5 mm) were rinsed with saline and fixed in 10% neutral formaldehyde or 3% glutaraldehyde for histology and electron microscopy, respectively. Additional rumen epithelium samples were collected into 2-mL tubes and stored at − 80 °C for gene expression analysis and transcriptome sequencing.

Rumen tissue section preparation and observation

Rumen tissues were fixed and processed by dehydration, clearing, embedding, sectioning, dewaxing, staining, further dehydration, and sealing14. The sections were photographed using an orthogonal optical microscope (Nikon Ti2-E, Japan) and subsequently analyzed with NIS-Elements Viewer 4.2 (Nikon Instruments Inc., Melville, NY, USA).

Rumen tissue electron microscopy

After fixation, the rumen epithelial tissue was rinsed three times with double-distilled water for 30 min each time. The tissue was subsequently fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide at room temperature and in the dark for 1.5 h. Next, the tissue was rinsed again three times with double-distilled water for 30 min each. Dehydration was then performed sequentially using ethanol gradients of 50%, 70% and 100%, each applied twice for 10 min. The tissue was subsequently dried using a tert-butanol vacuum drying method. Initially, it was treated with 75% tert-butanol for 30 min, followed by two treatments with 100% tert-butanol, each lasting 10 min. Subsequently, the tissue was infiltrated sequentially with acetone: epoxy resin (2:1), acetone: epoxy resin (1:1), and pure epoxy resin at a constant temperature of 37 °C. Each infiltration step lasted for 10 h. Double staining with uranium and lead was carried out using a saturated aqueous solution of 2% uranyl acetate and lead citrate. After vacuum drying and ion sputter coating, the scanning electron microscopy samples were completed. Finally, observations and imaging were performed using a scanning electron microscope (Quanta 200, Netherlands) at an accelerating voltage of 3.0 kV. Images were collected using Quartz PCI 10.0 (Quartz Imaging Corp., Vancouver, Canada) to allow comparison and analysis of structural changes in the rumen epithelial tissue resulting from different treatments.

Quantitative expression analysis of rumen epithelial genes

Frozen rumen epithelium samples were retrieved from the − 80 °C refrigerator and thoroughly ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen using a mortar and pestle. Total RNA was extracted from these samples using AG RNAex Pro RNA Reagent (obtained from Accurate Biology Co., Ltd). A Nano Drop-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to measure RNA concentration, with an OD260/280 ratio of 1.8 to 2.2 indicating good RNA purity. The Evo-M-MLV RT Mix Kit (Accurate Biology Co., Ltd) was used to remove gDNA before reverse transcription. Reaction conditions involved incubation at 37 °C for 15 min for reverse transcription, followed by a 5-second heat inactivation at 85 °C to deactivate the reverse transcriptase, yielding cDNA. Finally, a quantitative PCR reaction was prepared according to the instructions of the Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (Accurate Biology Co., Ltd). Reverse-transcribed cDNA was used as a template for RT-PCR amplification of target gene sequences. The reaction program included: 95 °C for 30 s; 95 °C for 5 s, and 60 °C for 30 s, repeated for 40 cycles. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with β-actin as the reference gene. The used primer sequences are presented in Supplementary material Table S1.

Determination of serum immune and biochemical indices

Jugular venous blood was collected at 08:00 before morning feeding on days 30 and 60 under fasting conditions using 10-mL vacuum tubes without anticoagulant. Samples were centrifuged (2312 × g, 10 min) to obtain serum, which was then aliquoted into 2-mL cryovials, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, transported to the laboratory, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Serum samples were thawed on ice prior to subsequent measurements. Serum immunological indicators were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), using ELISA kits obtained from Hunan Fengrui Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The analysis was performed using a microplate reader (RT-6100, Mindray). Serum biochemical indicators, including alanine Aminotransferase (ALT, ultraviolet-lactate dehydrogenase method), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, ultraviolet-malate dehydrogenase method), total protein (TP, biuret colorimetric method), albumin (ALB, bromocresol green method), alkaline phosphatase (ALP, AMP buffer method), urea nitrogen (BUN, ultraviolet-glutamate dehydrogenase method), glucose (GLU, oxidase method), triglyceride (TG, glycerol phosphate oxidase method), and cholesterol (CHO, oxidase method), were determined using a fully automated serum biochemical analyzer (Mindray BS-350 S, 2019).

Determination of rumen fermentation parameters

The NH₃-N content in rumen fluid was measured using a colorimetric method. A calibration curve was first established by dissolving 0.382 g of ammonium chloride in 0.2 mol/L hydrochloric acid solution, and then diluting this solution to a final volume of 100 mL. Standard solutions containing nitrogen concentrations of 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8, and 1.2 mg per 100 mL were prepared. Exactly 0.4 mL aliquots drawn from each standard solution were placed into five 10 mL test tubes. Then, 2 mL of solution A (prepared by dissolving 0.08 g of nitroprusside in 100 mL of 14% sodium salicylate solution) and 2 mL of solution B (prepared by mixing 2 mL of sodium hypochlorite solution containing 5.2% active chlorine in 100 mL of 0.3 mol sodium hydroxide solution) were sequentially added to each tube. After mixing, these mixtures will be allowed to rest for 10 min. The nitrogen-free tube served as a blank control. The optical density (OD) values were measured using a 721 spectrophotometer at 700 nm to construct the calibration curve. Subsequently, an appropriate volume of pre-treated rumen fluid was processed following the same steps to measure its OD values. Then, the concentration of NH₃-N in the rumen fluid was calculated based on the calibration curve. The pH of the rumen contents was measured using a portable pH meter (Leici PHS-3 C, Shanghai). Volatile fatty acids (VFAs) were determined using a Shimadzu gas chromatograph (GC-2010 Plus, Japan). Instrument parameters were set following the method of Liu et al.15.

Quantification of rumen flora

After thawing the rumen contents, total DNA was extracted using the CTAB method, performed according to past literature14. The relative quantification methods and calculations for rumen bacteria were consistent with the aforementioned methods for quantifying rumen epithelial genes. Bacteria served as the internal reference. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary material Table S2.

Data analysis

SPSS software (Version 25.0; SPSS, Inc.) was used for statistical analyses. Data were checked for normality and homogeneity of variance before analysis. For variables measured at a single time point (e.g., average daily gain, rumen morphology, fermentation indices, microbial abundance and gene expression), treatment effects were tested using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test when appropriate. Tukey’s multiple-comparison can be found Supplementary material Table S3. The sample size for terminal measurements was n = 6 sheep per group at each sampling time point. Results are presented as means ± SEM. Statistical significance was declared at P < 0.05.

Results

Effects of B. subtilis feeding duration on average daily weight gain (ADG) in sheep

As shown in Fig. 1 and Table S3, 30-day feeding of B. subtilis resulted in significantly higher ADG in the BSN and BSH groups compared to CON group (P = 0.009). At 45 days, the ADG in the BSN group remained significantly higher compared to CON group (P = 0.040). However, after 60-day feeding of B. subtilis, the ADG of BSN and BSH groups did not significantly differ from CON group (P = 0.215). This result indicated that 30-day feeding of B. subtilis enhanced the ADG of sheep, but 60-day feeding did not produce significant effects.

Effect of B. subtilis feeding on rumen fermentation parameters and rumen microorganisms of weaned sheep

As shown in Table 3 and Table S3, 30-day feeding with B. subtilis significantly increased the total VFAs (TVFAs) (P < 0.001), acetate (P < 0.001), and propionate (P < 0.001) in the BSN and BSH groups compared to CON group. The ratio of acetate to propionate in the BSH and BSN groups significantly decreased compared to CON group (P = 0.010). No significant differences occurred in pH (P = 0.796) and NH₃-N (P = 0.417) among the three groups. The day-60 rumen fermentation parameters were previously reported by our group in a Chinese-language publication derived from the same experimental trial and animal cohort described in this manuscript16. We include these values to provide a complete duration-dependent evaluation (day 30 vs. day 60), which is essential for interpreting whether the effects of B. subtilis supplementation are sustained or attenuated over time and for linking fermentation changes to the newly presented epithelial barrier and immune responses. 60-day feeding with B. subtilis significantly increased TVFAs in the BSN and BSH groups compared to CON group (P = 0.001). Propionate content in the BSH group also significantly increased compared to CON group (P = 0.001). The acetate/propionate significantly decreased in the BSH group compared to CON group (P = 0.018). No significant differences appeared in propionate content and acetate/propionate between the BSN and BSH groups (P = 0.196, P = 0.607).

Effects of B. subtilis on the expression of rumen epithelial barrier and immune function-related genes in sheep

As shown in Fig. 4 and Table S3, the effects of B. subtilis on rumen epithelial gene expression differed between the 30-day and 60-day feeding trials. 30-day feeding trial. B. subtilis supplementation did not affect Claudin 1 mRNA expression (P = 0.128). In contrast, ZO-1 mRNA expression was significantly higher in the BSH group than in CON group (P = 0.007), while no difference was detected between the BSN and BSH groups (P = 0.078). Regarding transport- and immune-related genes, MCT1 expression was significantly increased in both the BSN and BSH groups compared with CON group (P = 0.007). Moreover, the expression of IL-1β (P = 0.001) and NF-κB p65 (P = 0.045) was significantly decreased in the BSN and BSH groups relative to CON group. No significant differences were observed in IL-6 (P = 0.883), TNF-α (P = 0.706), CCL2 (P = 0.289), IFN-γ (P = 0.387), or SBD-2 (P = 0.178) among the three groups. 60-day feeding trial. Both BSN and BSH showed numerically higher Claudin 1 expression than CON group, but the differences were not significant (P = 0.133). ZO-1 expression was significantly higher in the BSN group than in both the BSH and CON groups (P = 0.030), whereas BSH did not differ from CON group (P = 0.839). For MCT1, expression was significantly lower in CON and BSH groups than in the BSN group (P = 0.048). In addition, IL-1β and IFN-γ expression levels were significantly reduced in the BSN and BSH groups compared with CON group (P = 0.001, P < 0.001). No significant differences were detected in IL-6, TNF-α, CCL2, NF-κB p65, or SBD-2 among the three groups (P = 0.728, P = 0.810, P = 0.157, P = 564, P = 0.354).

Effect of B. subtilis on serum immune indicators of sheep

As shown in Fig. 5 and Table S3, 30-day feeding of B. subtilis led to significantly higher immunoglobulin A (IgA) levels in the BSH group compared with those in CON group (P = 0.026). Immunoglobulin M (IgM) levels in the BSH group were also significantly higher than those in CON and BSN groups (P = 0.001). Levels of IL-1β in the BSN and BSH groups were significantly lower than those in CON group (P < 0.001). 60-day feeding of B. subtilis resulted in significantly higher IgA levels in the BSN and BSH groups compared with CON group (P = 0.011). IgM in the BSH group was significantly higher than those in the BSN group (P = 0.043). Levels of IL-1β in the BSN and BSH groups were significantly lower compared with CON group (P < 0.001). Discussion

Effects of feeding B. subtilis on growth performance, rumen fermentation parameters, and microflora structure

Direct-fed microbials based on B. subtilis have been evaluated in ruminant nutrition as alternatives to antibiotic growth promoters. In this study, supplementation with B. subtilis improved growth performance, with the BSN treatment increasing average daily gain after 30 days of feeding. Rumen pH, NH3-N, and VFAs serve as vital indicators of rumen function and environmental stability17. In our experiment, feeding 200 mg/kg B. subtilis for 60 days resulted in a lower rumen pH and higher concentrations of total VFA and propionate, while NH₃-N was not affected. Collectively, these changes indicate enhanced fermentative activity and a shift in end-product patterns toward propionate. Consistent with our observations, B. subtilis (including B. subtilis natto) has been reported to increase total VFA and propionate and to improve production performance in dairy cows, accompanied by shifts in the rumen microbiota18. Moreover, previous studies suggest that B. subtilis supplementation can reduce the acetate-to-propionate ratio in the rumen18, which aligns with the decreased ratio observed here. B. subtilis may also facilitate beneficial interactions among rumen microorganisms, thereby promoting a more efficient and stable fermentation environment19.

At the microbial level, we observed increased relative abundances of fibrolytic bacteria (Ruminococcus flavefaciens and Ruminococcus albus) and Lactobacillus following B. subtilis supplementation. This pattern suggests improved fiber degradation capacity and microbial ecosystem stability. The increased Lactobacillus may, in part, be supported by B. subtilis metabolites (e.g., enzymes and bacteriocins such as subtilin), which can enhance the growth and activity of lactic acid bacteria20,21. Ruminalococcus albus is a key cellulolytic species that produces multiple cellulases to degrade plant fiber and generate VFAs, thereby contributing to fermentation efficiency and community stability22. R. flavefaciens is another efficient fiber-degrading bacterium that can adapt to acidic conditions and cooperate with other microbes to maintain normal rumen function23. In addition, Lactobacillus can modulate gastrointestinal pH, compete with potentially harmful bacteria, and produce bacteriocins that inhibit certain pathogens; it may also promote other beneficial taxa (e.g., Bifidobacterium) and contribute to microbial balance and gut function24,25. Furthermore, 60-day B. subtilis feeding increased the relative abundance of B. amyloliquefaciens, which enhances VFA production from starch and carbohydrate decomposition, improving feed utilization and releasing amino acids, vitamins, and other nutrients for ruminant absorption26–28. Overall, these results support that B. subtilis supplementation improves rumen fermentation characteristics and optimizes rumen microbial structure, with more consistent effects after 60 days of feeding. In ruminants, B. subtilis has also been reported to influence fermentation end-products in vitro and may affect methane production29, further highlighting its potential to modulate rumen fermentation pathways. Importantly, the weaker growth response at 60 d and the lack of additional epithelial transporter (MCT1) upregulation with 60-d supplementation at 200 mg/kg suggest diminishing returns at prolonged high dosage. One plausible explanation is that sustained acidification and altered fermentation profiles can favor acid-tolerant taxa, reduce community diversity, and increase the risk of ruminal dysbiosis typical of subacute ruminal acidosis, which is associated with epithelial stress and inflammatory signaling30. In addition, excessive probiotic pressure can perturb microbial succession; in other host systems, probiotic administration after microbiome disturbance delayed mucosal microbiome reconstitution31. Together, these considerations support the use of 100 mg/kg for longer-term (60-d) supplementation in Hu sheep.

Effect of feeding B. subtilis on rumen tissue morphology

The structural integrity of the rumen epithelium is critical for nutrient absorption, metabolism, pH regulation, the immune barrier, and microbial homeostasis32. This study revealed that feeding B. subtilis significantly increased the height of rumen papillae. This effect is attributed to enhancements in the microbial community structure. For instance, Lactobacillus produces acidic substances such as lactic acid. This acid lowers the rumen’s pH, inhibits harmful bacteria like Escherichia coli and Salmonella, and facilitates the growth of beneficial microorganisms, including fiber-degrading bacteria33. These bacteria efficiently break down complex carbohydrates like cellulose and hemicellulose in feed. As a result, a stable microbial balance ensures a consistent fermentation environment, crucial for rumen papillae development. Furthermore, VFAs, metabolic byproducts of these microorganisms, act as chemical stimulants that enhance the growth, morphology, and functionality of rumen papillae. They support cellular proliferation and growth34,35. Additionally, probiotics stimulate the mucosal immune system, boost immune cell activity, and increase resistance to pathogens36. A robust mucosal immune system preserves rumen integrity, minimizing inflammatory responses that might impair papillae, thereby supporting normal growth and development. In this study, both 60-day and 30-day feeding of B. subtilis at 100 mg/kg led to favorable papilla development. 30-day feeding at 200 mg/kg also yielded positive results.

Effect of feeding B. subtilis on gene expression in rumen epithelium and blood

The rumen epithelium is the primary interface between luminal microbes and the host, and its barrier function is essential for limiting the translocation of endotoxin while allowing absorption of short-chain fatty acids. Tight junction proteins, including claudin-1 and zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), are key components that maintain epithelial integrity and reduce tissue injury caused by bacteria and toxins37. Tight junction permeability is influenced by multiple factors; energy status can affect tight junction protein expression in animal tissues38, and short-chain fatty acids have also been reported to regulate barrier permeability and influence tight junction protein expression39,40. In the present study, B. subtilis supplementation modulated barrier-related genes in the rumen epithelium. Both BSN and BSH increased ZO-1 expression after 30 days, with a stronger effect in BSH. After 60 days, BSN produced a more sustained increase in ZO-1 expression. This time-dependent response may be linked to the fermentation shift induced by B. subtilis, although the precise mechanism warrants further investigation. MCT1, prevalent in animal tissues, facilitates the transport of monocarboxylic acids and ketone bodies, thus participating in VFAs transport41. B. subtilis feeding at 100 and 200 mg/kg increases VFA concentrations in the short term, and at 100 mg/kg over the long term, influencing MCT1 expression42. The findings reveal consistency in the effect of 30-day and 60-day feeding of B. subtilis at 100 mg/kg on MCT1 expression. However, 60-day feeding at 200 mg/kg did not augment MCT1 expression, suggesting a duration effect in epithelial transport adaptation. Beyond barrier and transport functions, B. subtilis also influenced inflammatory signaling and systemic immune indices. Probiotics can interact with the host and enhance immune regulatory capacity43. In the present study, B. subtilis supplementation generally decreased IL-1β expression in rumen epithelium and blood, suggesting attenuation of local and systemic inflammatory tone44. This effect is biologically plausible because inflammatory responses in rumen epithelium can be initiated by luminal endotoxin; exposure of primary bovine ruminal epithelial cells to lipopolysaccharide induces pro-inflammatory gene transcription through the TLR4/NF-κB axis45. NF-κB p65 activates gene transcription of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α, playing a crucial role in initiating and maintaining the inflammatory response46.Therefore, the improved epithelial barrier (higher ZO-1/claudin-1) observed here may reduce trans-epithelial endotoxin translocation, thereby limiting TLR4 activation and downstream NF-κB signaling. In addition to indirect barrier-mediated effects, B. subtilis can release immunomodulatory metabolites (e.g., lipopeptides such as surfactin) that suppress LPS-induced NF-κB activation and cytokine production in immune cells47, and purified exopolysaccharides from B. subtilis have been shown to engage pattern-recognition receptors (including TLR4) while biasing toward anti-inflammatory programs48. Finally, the shifts in ruminal fermentation end-products induced by B. subtilis (notably increases in propionate/total VFA) may contribute to immune modulation because short-chain fatty acids can regulate epithelial signaling and inflammatory gene expression via G-protein-coupled receptors and histone deacetylase inhibition49. Collectively, these pathways provide mechanistic support for the observed dose- and time-dependent changes in NF-κB p65 and IL-1β expression. Notably, inflammatory markers measured in serum reflect systemic immune status and may not directly mirror rumen epithelial mRNA patterns because of tissue specificity, differences between transcriptional and circulating protein responses, and potential time lags between local and systemic regulation. In addition, immunoglobulins IgA, IgG, and IgM play crucial roles in animal immune function50. Previous studies showed that B. subtilis feeding increases serum immunoglobulins in broiler chickens, enhancing immunity51,52. our results further demonstrate that B. subtilis supplementation also increased serum immunoglobulins (IgG, IgA and IgM) in sheep, indicating improved humoral immunity in ruminant animals. Overall, these findings suggest that B. subtilis supplementation supports rumen epithelial barrier function (upregulated tight junction-related genes), promotes epithelial adaptation for VFA transport (MCT1), and attenuates inflammatory signaling (IL-1β/NF-κB), which together may contribute to enhanced systemic immune status in weaned Hu sheep.

Effect of feeding B. subtilis on blood biochemical indicators

Serum biochemical indicators reveal the extent of lipid mobilization, energy balance, and pathophysiological processes in the host. Serum TP, which comprises ALB and globulin GLB, serves as a protein synthesis indicator53. In this study, both TP and GLB levels were higher in the BSH group following the administration of B. subtilis. Globulin is intricately linked to the immune function of the host. It appears that prolonged exposure to B. subtilis may modulate the gastrointestinal flora of sheep, thereby enhancing the host’s immune function more effectively. This enhancement potentially stimulates the synthesis and secretion of globulin. Cholesterol metabolism relates to lipid metabolism and gut microbiota factors54,55. This study observed a rise in CHO levels following 30-day B. subtilis feeding, likely attributable to temporary shifts in the gut microbiota that influence cholesterol metabolism. These shifts affect microbial cholesterol absorption and metabolic pathways. Conversely, after extended B. subtilis feeding, the rumen microbial community structure stabilizes, leading to normalized CHO absorption and metabolism, ultimately exerting no significant effect on serum CHO levels. The specific mechanisms of these influences warrant further investigation.

A main limitation of this study is that it involved a single sheep breed under specific rearing conditions, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. In the absence of on-farm validation, a targeted supplementation program at a moderate dose for approximately 30 days is recommended rather than prolonged high-dose supplementation. Future studies should validate the optimal supplementation strategy under commercial production conditions and evaluate longer-term outcomes using broader immune profiling and functional immune assays. Mechanistic work integrating high-resolution microbiome sequencing, metabolomics, and epithelial signaling analyses will help clarify how B. subtilis-derived metabolites and host-microbe interactions contribute to barrier reinforcement and immune modulation in ruminants.

Conclusion

Overall, dietary supplementation with Bacillus subtilis improved rumen development and fermentation in weaned Hu sheep, as evidenced by greater rumen papilla height, higher total volatile fatty acids and propionate, and a lower acetate-to-propionate ratio. B. subtilis also increased the relative abundance of beneficial fibrolytic bacteria (e.g., Ruminococcus flavefaciens and Ruminococcus albus) and Lactobacillus, promoted epithelial barrier- and transport-related gene expression (e.g., ZO-1 and MCT1), and attenuated inflammatory signaling (e.g., IL-1β and NF-κB p65) in the rumen epithelium, accompanied by improved systemic immune indices (higher IgA/IFN-γ and lower IL-1β). Collectively, these results suggest that a 30-day supplementation period is sufficient to achieve clear benefits, whereas for longer-term (60-day) supplementation, 100 mg/kg appears preferable to avoid diminishing returns at a higher dose.
