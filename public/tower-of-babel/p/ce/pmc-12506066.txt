Beyond diabetes: harnessing the power of metformin in burn care
Authors: Fadi Khalaf, Daniella Touma, Sean Saldanha, Georges Khalaf, Dalia Barayan, Marc G Jeschke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBurn injuries are complex and devastating traumas that trigger a profound systemic metabolic response, characterized by hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and a hypermetabolic state. Notably, hyperglycemia is a critical determinant of worse prognoses in burn patients. While insulin has long been the gold standard for managing post-burn hyperglycemia, its therapy is associated with a risk of hypoglycemic events, which can exacerbate morbidity and compromise patient outcomes. As such, investigation of alternative therapeutics is warranted to improve glycemic control while mitigating associated risks. Recently, metformin, a first-line therapy for the treatment of type II diabetes, has emerged as a potential therapeutic agent for the management of post-burn hyperglycemia as well as other burn injury sequelae. This review examines the mechanistic underpinnings of metformin, its potential application in managing post-burn hyperglycemia, and its comparative advantages over other hypoglycemic agents. Additionally, we examine the broad spectrum of metformin’s pleiotropic effects in the context of burn injury–extending beyond glycemic control to include attenuation of muscle catabolism, suppression of lipolysis, regulation of non-shivering thermogenesis, support of mitochondrial and immune function, enhanced wound healing, and its potential role in addressing burn-induced acceleration of biological aging. Taken together, we discuss how metformin represents a paradigm shift in burn care, with the potential to substantially improve patient outcomes.Keywords: Thermal injury, Burns, Metformin, Hypermetabolism, Hyperglycemia, Diabetes, Trauma, Hypoglycemia

Background

Burn injuries remain a significant but often overlooked cause of global morbidity and mortality, affecting an estimated 9 million individuals and leading to approximately 115,000 deaths each year [1, 2]. Unlike other forms of trauma, burn injuries provoke a severe hypermetabolic response that can persist for up to 3 years after the initial injury [3]. This metabolic disturbance is also linked to whole-body catabolism, inflammation, hyperlipidemia, insulin resistance, and hyperglycemia, with the last being a key determinant of poorer outcomes for critically ill patients, including those with burns (Fig. 1) [4–6]. Currently, insulin is the gold standard for managing post-burn hyperglycemia. However, insulin therapy is associated with a risk of hypoglycemic events, which can exacerbate morbidity and compromise patient outcomes [7]. Consequently, alternative therapeutic approaches are needed to optimize glycemic control while minimizing risks. One promising candidate is metformin, a well-established hypoglycemic agent recognized for its safety and efficacy in treating hyperglycemia.

The Hallmarks of a Burn Injury. Burn injuries trigger a range of systemic disruptions, including impaired wound healing, fibrosis, a severe catabolic hypermetabolic response, systemic inflammation, immune dysfunction, fluid loss, and organ dysfunction

Metformin, a biguanide originally extracted from Galega officinalis (French lilac), a traditional herbal remedy used since the 17th century to manage symptoms now associated with diabetes. The plant’s hypoglycemic effects are attributed to guanidine, a naturally occurring compound enriched in G. officinalis [8]. Biguanides, synthetic compounds characterized by two N-linked guanidine groups, were first synthesized in 1922. However, their clinical application was initially overshadowed by the advent of insulin [9]. Metformin’s clinical utility gained traction in the 1950 s after pivotal trials demonstrated its efficacy as an anti-diabetic agent [10]. While other biguanides, such as phenformin and buformin, were initially preferred due to their greater potency, their association with lactic acidosis led to their eventual withdrawal from clinical use [9, 10]. Metformin, however, emerged as a safer and cost-effective alternative, achieving regulatory approval in the United States in 1994. Notably, it is now one of the most widely prescribed medications, with nearly 90 million prescriptions filled annually in the United States alone [9, 11].

Despite metformin’s extensive safety profile and demonstrated advantages over insulin in other contexts, it has yet to be investigated for routine use in burn patients. This review aims to explore the mechanistic underpinnings of metformin, its potential utility in the management of post-burn hyperglycemia, and its comparative advantages over other hypoglycemic agents. Furthermore, we will examine other potential therapeutic benefits of metformin beyond glycemic control and discuss why metformin represents a paradigm shift in improving outcomes for burn patients.

Stress hyperglycemia

Severe trauma, infections, and surgical interventions trigger a hypermetabolic state characterized by hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and glucose intolerance—collectively referred to as ‘stress hyperglycemia’ [12]. Stress hyperglycemia is primarily driven by the activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, which induces the release of cortisol, catecholamines, and glucagon—hormones essential for the stress response and metabolic regulation [12, 13]. One of the primary drivers of stress hyperglycemia is increased hepatic glucose production, largely mediated by the elevated levels of glucagon and cortisol, which enhance gluconeogenesis by upregulating gluconeogenic enzymes [14]. Moreover, these hormones impair the peripheral tissue’s ability to utilize glucose, exacerbating the hyperglycemic state. The severity of stress hyperglycemia often correlates with clinical scoring systems, such as the acute physiology and chronic health evaluation (APACHE) score and Glasgow Coma Scale, reflecting the magnitude of underlying physiological stress [15]. Notably, it serves as a critical marker for assessing injury or disease severity [13, 16]. Indeed, recent studies indicate that stress hyperglycemia correlates strongly with adverse clinical outcomes, including increased rates of infection, prolonged mechanical ventilation, organ dysfunction, and higher mortality [17, 18]. Consequently, understanding the mechanisms underlying this condition is essential.

Effective regulation of stress-related hyperglycemia is crucial for the management of critically ill patients. Indeed, evidence suggests that maintaining blood glucose levels below 110 mg/dL yields significant clinical improvements, including reduced mortality, lower infection rates, and diminished occurrences of sepsis-associated multiorgan failure [5, 19]. This pivotal research conducted by Van den Bergh et al. led to a reevaluation of hyperglycemia management strategies and spurred widespread interest in intensive glycemic control protocols. However, the relationship between hyperglycemia and clinical outcomes is nuanced. In critically ill septic patients, only severe hyperglycemia (> 200 mg/dL) on admission was associated with increased 30-day mortality, whereas mild hyperglycemia (141–199 mg/dL) did not show the same detrimental impact [20]. This suggests that mild stress hyperglycemia may not be harmful and could even confer advantages in specific populations, such as those with sepsis [21].

Thermal trauma represents one of the most severe stressors, often leading to pronounced hyperglycemia and insulin resistance. Stress-induced diabetes is prevalent among severely burned patients and significantly increases their mortality risk. Elevated glucose levels are common during the initial resuscitation phase, with non-survivors often exhibiting higher concentrations [22, 23]. Poor glycemic control in this population correlates with increased infection rates, impaired wound healing, and enhanced catabolism, emphasizing the importance of vigilant glucose regulation [24]. Recent studies have highlighted the efficacy of tight glycemic control in burn patients. Indeed, randomized controlled trials consistently demonstrate that maintaining blood glucose levels between 90 and 130 mg/dL not only enhances organ function, but also significantly reduces the risk of infections and sepsis [25–27]. Moreover, achieving normoglycemia (80–100 mg/dL) has also been associated with decreased systemic inflammation, lower bacteremia rates, and improved overall survival outcomes in children with severe burn injuries [25]. Notably, early glycemic control minimizes the duration of elevated blood glucose and reduces glycemic variability, further improving patient outcomes [28]. Tailoring glycemic control strategies to the type and severity of trauma, along with individual patient characteristics, can optimize outcomes in critically ill patients.

Metformin as a hypoglycemic agent

Decades after its introduction, metformin remains the cornerstone of type II diabetes mellitus (T2D) management, widely recognized as the first-line oral glucose-lowering treatment [29]. Its value stems from its ability to effectively lower blood glucose, its affordability, its neutral impact on weight, and its relative safety, particularly, the minimal risk of hypoglycemia and a decreased risk of cardiovascular events [29, 30]. In the management of T2D, metformin is effective both as a standalone treatment and when used in combination with other glucose-lowering agents. Interestingly, when used as a monotherapy, metformin can reduce fasting plasma glucose by 19%, endogenous glucose production by 18%, and is capable of inducing weight loss in individuals with higher body mass index (BMI) [31, 32]. Additionally, metformin therapy has been shown to result in a 42% reduction in diabetes-related death [30]. Beyond its immediate benefits, metformin has further been shown to provide lasting reductions in microvascular risk, as well as decreased risk of myocardial infarction and all-cause mortality. These continued benefits have been observed in overweight patients even after long-term follow-up [30, 33].

It’s important to note that the efficacy of metformin is dose dependent. In a 14-week, multicenter, double-blind study of 451 individuals with T2D, Garber and colleagues investigated the pharmacodynamic effects of different metformin doses, compared to a placebo, on changes in fasting plasma glucose and glycated hemoglobin levels [34]. Interestingly, researchers found the minimal effective dose to be 500 mg, while maximal efficacy was achieved at a dose of 2000 mg [34]. At daily doses between 500 and 2000 mg, metformin reduced the adjusted mean fasting plasma glucose from baseline by 19 to 84 mg/dL and decreased the adjusted mean glycated hemoglobin levels by 0.6% to 2.0% more than the placebo [34]. Regarding safety, treatment with metformin was well tolerated by the study participants, with no significant adverse events reported compared to the placebo group, except for mild gastrointestinal disturbances [34].

Metformin’s mechanism of action

Following oral administration, approximately 70% of an immediate-release dose is absorbed within 6–10 h through the organic cation transporter (OCT) 3 and plasma membrane monoamine transporter (PMAT) on the apical membrane of enterocytes [35, 36]. Metformin exits enterocytes via OCT1 on the basolateral membrane, remaining unbound in circulation with a plasma half-life of approximately 5 h [36]. The drug is subsequently transported to the liver via the portal vein and absorbed by hepatocytes through OCT1/3, with hepatic excretion facilitated by multidrug and toxin extrusion (MATE) 1 transporters [37]. Metformin exhibits a characteristic distribution to the intestines, liver, kidneys, and bladder, with elimination occurring predominantly through active renal secretion via OCT2 and MATE1/2k transporters [8, 38]. Notably, metformin undergoes no metabolic transformation and is excreted unchanged in urine.

AMPK-dependent mechanisms

The primary glucose-lowering effect of metformin arises from the suppression of hepatic gluconeogenesis, enhancement of insulin sensitivity, and modulation of cellular energy status [8, 39]. Although the mechanistic pathways of metformin are highly complex and multifaceted, the widely accepted central mechanism involves the accumulation of metformin within hepatocyte mitochondria, where it partially inhibits complex I of the electron transport chain (ETC), reducing oxidative phosphorylation and thereby depleting intracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels. This leads to an elevated adenosine monophosphate (AMP): ATP ratio, which triggers activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a key regulator of cellular energy homeostasis [47–49]. Once activated, AMPK promotes a metabolic shift toward catabolism by stimulating fatty acid oxidation and inhibiting lipogenesis. These effects are mediated through the suppression of sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1 (SREBP-1) and inhibition of lipogenic enzymes, including HMG-CoA reductase [40, 41, 43–45]. In hepatocytes, AMPK also downregulates the transcription of critical gluconeogenic enzymes, such as phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) and glucose-6-phosphatase (G6Pase), leading to decreased hepatic glucose output [46, 47]. In peripheral tissues, including adipose and skeletal muscle, metformin enhances insulin-stimulated glucose uptake, in part, by promoting AMPK-dependent translocation of the glucose transporter GLUT4 to the plasma membrane [48, 49].

The mitochondrial mechanism described above is most evident at supraphysiological concentrations of metformin (~ 500 µM) [50]. At therapeutic plasma concentrations (~ 70 µM), AMPK activation occurs through an alternative mechanism that does not rely on changes in AMP [42]. Instead, metformin promotes the formation of a protein complex involving Axin and liver kinase B1 (LKB1) at the lysosomal membrane, where the adaptor protein late endosomal/lysosomal adaptor, MAPK and mTOR activator 1 (LAMTOR1) anchors the complex, enabling spatially regulated AMPK activation [51–53]. This lysosomal pathway leads to downstream inhibition of the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), contributing to the restoration of metabolic balance under nutrient stress. Metformin also inhibits mTORC1 through AMPK-independent mechanisms, including the induction of regulated in development and DNA damage responses 1 (REDD1), a known negative regulator of mTOR signaling [53–55].

AMPK-independent mechanisms

The glucose-lowering effects of metformin are not entirely dependent on AMPK activation. Studies in liver-specific AMPK knockout models demonstrate that metformin retains its ability to suppress gluconeogenesis, indicating the involvement of alternative pathways. One such mechanism involves the inhibition of mitochondrial glycerophosphate dehydrogenase (mGPD), a key enzyme in the glycerophosphate shuttle [56, 57]. Metformin-mediated inhibition of mGPD perturbs the cytosolic redox balance by elevating the NADH: NAD⁺ ratio, which impairs the conversion of lactate and glycerol into glucose, thereby limiting hepatic gluconeogenic flux [58, 59]. In addition to its effect on mGPD, the energetic consequences of complex I inhibition including ATP depletion and AMP accumulation, directly inhibit fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, a critical rate-limiting gluconeogenic enzyme [60]. Elevated AMP levels further suppress hepatic glucose output by inhibiting adenylate cyclase activity, leading to reduced cyclic AMP (cAMP) and protein kinase A (PKA) signaling, which in turn dampens the transcriptional activity of cAMP response element binding protein (CREB) and downstream expression of gluconeogenic genes [53, 61, 62].

Beyond its hepatic actions, emerging evidence suggests that the gastrointestinal tract is an important site of metformin action, particularly at therapeutic doses. High intraluminal concentrations of metformin alter the composition of the gut microbiota, promoting expansion of taxa such as Akkermansia muciniphila and other short-chain fatty acid-producing organisms, which may contribute to improved glucose tolerance and reduced systemic inflammation [63–66]. Metformin also stimulates glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) secretion, delays glucose absorption, and may inhibit sodium-glucose cotransporters like SGLT1 at the intestinal epithelium [67]. These effects act synergistically to reduce postprandial glucose levels and improve insulin sensitivity, independent of AMPK activity or mitochondrial inhibition [68].

Together, these mechanisms suggest that metformin functions not through a single linear pathway but via an integrated network of AMPK-dependent and independent processes, spanning mitochondrial bioenergetics, cytosolic redox regulation, nutrient-sensing pathways, and gut-derived signaling. This multifactorial mode of action likely underpins both the robustness and the tissue specificity of metformin’s metabolic effects.

Metformin therapy to treat post-burn hyperglycemia

Recently, metformin has gained recognition as a promising alternative to insulin in the management of post-burn hyperglycemia. As previously outlined, metformin exhibits a dual mechanism of action: inhibiting hepatic gluconeogenesis and enhancing peripheral insulin sensitivity. This allows it to counter the two primary drivers of burn-induced hyperglycemia. Though clinical research remains limited, several key studies have begun to shed light on the potential role of metformin in burn care (Table 1). A phase II randomized controlled trial conducted by Jeschke et al. compared metformin to insulin therapy in 44 severely burned adults with > 20% total body surface area (TBSA) burns [24]. While both groups achieved comparable glycemic control, the metformin group experienced a significantly lower incidence of hypoglycemia (6% vs. 15%) [24]. Furthermore, in a double-blind, placebo-controlled study of 10 patients with burns covering > 60% TBSA, 8 days of metformin treatment reduced fasting glucose production and oxidation, improved glucose clearance and uptake, and increased plasma insulin compared with placebo [69]. More recently, a large retrospective cohort study by Hallman et al. analyzed outcomes in 10,749 burn patients treated with insulin, metformin, or a combination of both [70]. Metformin use was associated with a markedly reduced risk of hyperglycemia, hypoglycemia, sepsis, lactic acidosis, and mortality within three months post-burn, compared to insulin alone [70]. Metformin has even demonstrated benefits in older adult burn patients. Specifically, we observed improved survival, a lower incidence of lactic acidosis, and reduced circulating levels of organ damage markers, underscoring its safety and efficacy in this population [71]. These findings highlight the potential efficacy and safety of metformin in mitigating post-burn metabolic dysregulation.

Despite these encouraging results, clinical research on the use of metformin in burn patients remains limited. To date, only a small number of studies have evaluated its effects in this population, and existing trials are constrained by small sample sizes and single-center designs [24, 70]. Moreover, metformin has not yet been incorporated into standard burn care guidelines, and questions regarding optimal dosing, timing, and patient selection remain unresolved. Larger, well-powered randomized controlled trials are needed to validate its efficacy and safety, particularly across diverse patient subgroups and varying degrees of burn severity. Nonetheless, the available data suggest that metformin holds significant potential as an adjunct therapy in the comprehensive management of burn patients, offering not only glycemic control but also potential benefits in modulating the broader hypermetabolic response. However, as with any pharmacologic intervention, it is essential to consider potential complications, patient selection, and safety considerations—particularly in the context of burn injury.

Complications of metformin use in burn patients

Metformin is considered the preferred first-line medication for T2D largely due to its established safety profile and low risk of complications [29]. Adverse effects are commonly gastrointestinal in nature, with the most prevalent being diarrhea (6.9%), abdominal bloating (6.2%), and abdominal pain (5.3%) [72]. Vitamin B12 deficiency is also associated with the use of metformin but is typically limited to cases of long-term use (exceeding four years) and reversible with supplementation [73]. Most notably, metformin monotherapy does not induce hypoglycemia, a benefit not shared by insulin therapy [74]. Importantly, this benefit remains in cases of severe burn injuries [24], where hypoglycemia is associated with longer hospitalization, infection, sepsis, multiorgan failure, and increased mortality [75].

Instead, the most concerning complication of metformin is metformin-associated lactic acidosis (MALA). MALA is a state of altered lactate and hydrogen metabolism, defined by plasma lactate > 5.0 mmol/L and arterial pH < 7.35 in the context of metformin use. Typically, lactic acidosis arises in conditions of reduced oxygen delivery to tissues, such as shock, sepsis, severe hypoperfusion, or mitochondrial dysfunction [76]. In MALA, metformin contributes through dose-dependent inhibition of mitochondrial respiratory complex I, particularly in the liver where it is taken up [77]. The effects of this inhibition are twofold: first, it shifts the cellular redox state to promote anaerobic glycolysis and favor the conversion of pyruvate to lactate, increasing lactate production [78]. Second, it activates AMPK, which inhibits gluconeogenesis and impairs hepatic lactate clearance [77]. Together, the combination of enhanced lactate production and reduced lactate clearance leads to lactate accumulation.

Although the inhibition of complex I occurs at all doses, it is only seen as significant to lactate accumulation when metformin’s plasma concentrations exceed 5 µg/mL (therapeutic range < 2 µg/mL) [77]. Such elevations are rare, as metformin is primarily eliminated by renal excretion; thus, impaired renal function is the principal factor behind its accumulation in plasma [29]. Accordingly, the association of metformin to lactic acidosis was only observed with eGFR levels less than 30 mL/min/1.73 m² [79]. To mitigate risk, metformin is contraindicated in patients with renal failure (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²) and dose-adjusted in those with moderate renal impairment (eGFR 30–45 mL/min/1.73 m²) in current guidelines [80]. These guidelines prove useful in making MALA exceedingly rare, reporting only 4.3 cases per 100,000 patient-years when followed appropriately [81].

Despite its rarity, MALA remains an important consideration in burn patients due to its high mortality rates, typically ranging from 30 to 50% [30, 82, 83]. Additionally, burn patients are at an increased risk of acute kidney injury, with incidence rates ranging from 15 to 30%, depending on the severity of the burn [84, 85]. Given that renal impairment is the primary risk factor for metformin accumulation, this population is theoretically more susceptible to developing MALA. Yet, even with the elevated risk of renal dysfunction, several studies reported no cases of MALA with metformin in burn patients [24, 71, 86], while one study not only showed a minimal incidence of lactic acidosis with metformin but also showed a statistically significant increased risk of lactic acidosis in burn patients treated with insulin [70]. Altogether, these findings suggest that with appropriate patient selection and renal monitoring, metformin may offer a safe and effective alternative for glycemic control in burn patients. Given its well-established safety profile, the following section will evaluate metformin in comparison to other hypoglycemic agents, with a focus on its potential advantages in the management of burn patients.

Alternative hypoglycemic agents and how they compare to metformin

In this section, we will review the various classes of hypoglycemic agents—insulin, sulfonylureas, thiazolidinediones, incretin analogs, and GLP-1 and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) agonists—and examine their roles in burn patients. While each has its place in managing hyperglycemia, we will focus on how they compare to metformin, ultimately suggesting why metformin stands out as the most effective and practical therapeutic for burn patients.

Insulin

Since its discovery over a century ago, insulin has remained central to the management of diabetes and other hyperglycemic conditions. It serves as a required treatment of type 1 diabetes, the first-line adjunct therapy to metformin in severe cases of T2D, and the primary choice for controlling hyperglycemia in acute hospital settings [87–89]. As a result of insulin’s widespread application, various formulations with differing onsets of action, times to peak effect, and durations of action have been developed, allowing clinicians to tailor therapy to individual needs [90]. Regardless of formulation, insulin acts by binding to insulin receptors on cell membranes, thereby increasing glucose uptake and inhibiting gluconeogenesis, lipolysis, and proteolysis, ultimately lowering blood glucose levels [91]. Despite its well-established benefits, insulin administration is associated with weight gain and increased risk of hypoglycemic episodes [92]. Indeed, studies such as VISEP, GLUCONTROL, and NICE-SUGAR have documented a troubling association between intensive insulin therapy and a heightened incidence of hypoglycemic events. Retrospective analyses reveal that mortality rates for patients experiencing mild to severe hypoglycemia can reach 36.6%, compared to 19.7% in patients without hypoglycemic episodes [27, 28, 93–98]. Thus, hypoglycemia warrants careful consideration when administering antihyperglycemic therapy in hospital settings.

In fact, post-burn insulin administration is associated with a 4−5-fold increase in hypoglycemia, which in turn is linked to a 9-fold increase in mortality [70, 75]. Additionally, in burn patients, maintaining tight glycemic control is challenging due to frequent interruption of enteral feeding for daily dressing changes and weekly operations, potentially disrupting gastrointestinal motility and complicating adjustments [2, 27, 99]. Despite these concerns, insulin remains the gold standard for managing hyperglycemia in burn patients and other critically ill individuals due to its multifaceted effects on metabolic regulation and its ability to counteract trauma-induced catabolic processes [70]. Administered during hospitalization, insulin exerts anabolic properties by preserving lean body mass, promoting muscle protein synthesis, and reducing proteolysis [7]. Moreover, insulin modulates the inflammatory response, decreasing the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators while enhancing anti-inflammatory pathways [100].

However, when directly compared, burn patients receiving insulin displayed a significantly increased risk for hypoglycemia, sepsis, lactic acidosis, and death compared to those receiving metformin. In fact, even the metformin/insulin combination cohort was at an increased risk of all these outcomes except death when compared to metformin alone [70]. Additionally, another study demonstrated that metformin regulated glucose levels equally to insulin, but with a lower incidence of hypoglycemia, significantly improved insulin sensitivity at discharge, and a comparatively strong antilipolytic effect, which was associated with significantly reduced inflammation [24]. Altogether, these findings suggest that current practices in glycemic management for burn patients may need reconsideration and support further research into metformin and other alternatives in this patient population.

Sulfonylureas

Sulfonylureas were the first oral medications introduced for the management of diabetes [101]. This class of hypoglycemic agents is comprised of two subclasses. First-generation sulfonylureas include tolbutamide, chlorpropamide, acetohexamide, and tolazamide [102], while second-generation sulfonylureas, which are preferred over their first-generation counterparts, include glibenclamide, glipizide, glimepiride, and gliclazide [103]. Though both first- and second-generation sulfonylureas work by stimulating insulin release from pancreatic ß-cells through the same mechanism, they differ in their intrinsic antidiabetic activity, receptor binding affinity, and pharmacokinetics [101]. They also exert various effects outside the pancreas, such as reducing hepatic insulin clearance and decreasing glucagon secretion for individuals with T2D [101, 103]. Notably, however, efficacy of these drugs necessitates the presence of residual pancreatic ß-cells, and a significant degree of insulin resistance can lead to drug failure.

While metformin is the preferred first-line treatment for individuals with T2D, progression of the condition often necessitates combination therapy with other agents such as sulfonylureas [32]. Sulfonylureas are typically used in diabetic patients who are not overweight, are unable to achieve sufficient glycemic regulation, or those who have contraindications to metformin therapy. Sulfonylureas are typically well tolerated, but a common side effect is hypoglycemia, which depending on severity, can have various adverse effects [101]. Interestingly, in a retrospective study investigating the impact of sulfonylureas and metformin on outcomes of cardiovascular disease in individuals with T2D, researchers found the use of sulfonylureas for initial treatment of the condition to raise both the incidence and risk of cardiovascular events and mortality compared to metformin [104]. This treatment approach was associated with significantly increased incidence of ischemic stroke, cardiovascular mortality, and all-cause mortality as compared to prescription of metformin [105]. Moreover, when used as second-line agents– either as a switch from or addition to metformin treatment– sulfonylureas were found to be associated with a higher risk of myocardial infarction, all-cause mortality, and severe hypoglycemia, as compared to the continuation of metformin treatment alone [106]. Additionally, results of a meta-analysis revealed that treatment with sulfonylureas may be associated with an elevated risk of all-cancer, while metformin appears to exert a protective effect, and was associated with a reduction in all-cancer risk in individuals with T2D [107]. Similarly, in a meta-analysis of 15 studies involving over 840, 000 diabetic individuals, Singh et al. found sulfonylurea, thiazolidinediones, and insulin to be associated with neutral or slightly increased colorectal cancer risks, while metformin use showed an 11% reduction in risk [108].

Given the lack of comparative research between metformin and sulfonylureas in burn patients, further studies are needed to explore the potential of sulfonylureas in this context. However, based on the current available data, metformin appears to be a superior alternative to sulfonylureas, owing to its favorable safety profile, enhanced protective effects against cardiovascular complications, and greater efficacy in improving insulin sensitivity without the associated risk of hypoglycemia.

PPAR agonists (including Thiazolidinediones)

Thiazolidinediones (TZDs), such as pioglitazone and rosiglitazone, represent a class of hypoglycemic agents that serve as insulin sensitizers, reduce insulin resistance, particularly in liver and adipose tissues, while also enhancing the glucose-responsiveness of ß-cells [109]. They function by binding to the transcription factor PPARγ, which promotes the synthesis of glucose transporters and alters adipocyte signals, thereby targeting insulin resistance [110].

However, despite the well-established benefits of TZDs in glycemic control, their use in recent years has decreased due to their side effect profile and risk for adverse outcomes. A notable side effect of TZD monotherapy, particularly in comparison to metformin, is weight gain accompanied by increased subcutaneous fat deposition [111, 112]. Moreover, a side effect of both monotherapy with either pioglitazone or rosiglitazone, as well as their combined treatment with other glycemic agents, is edema [111, 113, 114]. TZDs are further associated with heart failure, and interestingly, Hernandez et al. found TZD use for less than 12 months to be linked to a greater risk of developing heart failure, although confirmation through additional studies is required due to limitations that introduce uncertainty to this finding [113–116]. Additionally, TZD use has also been associated with an increased risk of myocardial infarction [115–118], bone fractures [113, 119–121], and bladder cancer [122–124]. Nevertheless, despite these side effects, TZDs are more effective in terms of long-term glycemic control and whole-body insulin sensitivity [112]. Therefore, careful weighing of the risk-benefit profile of TZDs is necessary prior to prescription.

Fenofibrate, a PPAR-(alpha) agonist that has been used in the treatment of dyslipidemia for decades, has shown promise in this burn patients, offering the advantage of decreasing hypoglycemic events when given with insulin [125]. A randomized controlled trial involving 18 severely burned pediatric patients also found that short-term fenofibrate administration improved insulin receptor signaling and sensitivity without adverse effects [126]. However, more research is needed to validate its safety and efficacy, with an emphasis on long-term studies to explore their suitability for extended use and their impact on clinical outcomes.

While there is a lack of research comparing PPAR agonists to metformin in burn patients, existing data suggest that PPAR agonists, though effective in improving insulin sensitivity, come with significant side effects that may limit their use in this population. In contrast, metformin offers a more favorable safety profile and superior efficacy, making it the preferred choice for managing hyperglycemia in burn patients.

Incretin analogs (GLP-1 Agonists)

The final class of glycemic agents covered in this review includes incretin analogs, such as GLP-1 agonists and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors. Notably, GLP-1 is the most potent insulin-stimulating hormone, produced through the proteolytic cleavage of proglucagon and inhibited by DPP-4 [127]. GLP-1 agonists, including exenatide and liraglutide, effectively manage hyperglycemia without inducing hypoglycemia, As they cease stimulating insulin secretion when plasma glucose levels reach 70 mg/dL [128]. Beyond their role in glycemic regulation, they may also preserve β-cell function, reduce glucagon secretion and gluconeogenesis, promote satiety and weight loss, and offer additional benefits such as cardiovascular protection [127, 129–134].

Indeed, exenatide, an incretin mimetic, has demonstrated efficacy in improving glycemic control for patients with T2D who do not achieve adequate results with metformin alone. While it can be used as monotherapy, it is more commonly administered subcutaneously alongside metformin, offering superior benefits with a low risk of hypoglycemia. Studies indicate that exenatide reduces glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels by 0.6–0.8% over 30 weeks and promotes weight loss of 1.6–2.8 kg, in contrast to insulin therapy, which is often associated with weight gain [135–137]. Additionally, exenatide has been shown to improve β-cell function, C-peptide levels, and adiponectin levels. It is generally well tolerated, with the most common adverse effects being mild to moderate gastrointestinal symptoms, particularly nausea, which typically diminishes over time [137–140]. Other reported side effects include injection site reactions, headaches, and nasopharyngitis [138]. In critically ill patients without a prior history of diabetes, GLP-1 infusion has been shown to attenuate the glycemic response to enteral feeding [141]. Furthermore, a study in severely burned pediatric patients demonstrated that exenatide reduces the insulin requirements needed for glycemic control [142]. While promising, further research is required to validate it safety and efficacy in burn patients.

Similar to GLP-1 agonists, DPP-4 inhibitors are effective in patients who fail to achieve glycemic control with metformin monotherapy [143]. These inhibitors enhance GLP-1 activity by preventing its degradation through DPP-4 inhibition; however, their glycemic regulation is generally less potent than that of GLP-1 agonists. Due to their similar mechanisms of action, GLP-1 agonists and DPP-4 inhibitors share a comparable side effect profile [144]. Additionally, these medications are proscribed in patients with renal impairment, which may limit their use in older adults and burn patients [145]. Similarly, metformin was traditionally contraindicated in older adult burn patients due to concerns over renal impairments. However, a recent study has demonstrated that this is not the case, showing that metformin is both safe and efficacious in this patient cohort, offering a reliable option for managing hyperglycemia in burn patients of all ages [71].

Beyond glycemic control: the multifaceted benefits of metformin in burn recovery

While metformin is primarily recognized for regulating blood glucose, its relevance in burn care extends well beyond glycemic control (Fig. 2). Indeed, growing evidence underscores metformin’s broader therapeutic potential, highlighting its ability to modulate post-burn hypermetabolism and inflammation, support wound healing, and potentially play a role in burn-induced biological aging. Briefly, in the aftermath of a burn injury, the body initiates a complex systemic response, characterized by extensive inflammation and hypermetabolism, which unfolds in a biphasic manner. The early (72–96 h post-injury) ebb phase is marked by decreased metabolism, cardiac output, and oxygen consumption [2]. This is followed by a hypermetabolic state known as the flow phase, in part characterized by heightened resting energy expenditure, markedly increased nutritional demands, browning of subcutaneous white adipose tissue (WAT), systemic catabolism of adipose and skeletal muscle tissues, and widespread inflammation [2]. While initially essential to patient recovery–supporting energy demands, tissue repair, wound healing, and infection control–persistence of this response can be detrimental. Conversely, attenuation of the post-burn response, as seen in older adults, is also associated with unfavourable outcomes. Indeed, older adult burn patients exhibit markedly decreased inflammatory and hypermetabolic responses compared to their adult counterparts, which is associated with increased organ dysfunction and mortality [146]. Therefore, targeting metabolic and inflammatory dysregulation is pivotal for improving patient outcomes, with metformin emerging as a promising therapeutic in this context.

The Multifaceted Benefits of Metformin for Burn Patients. Following a burn injury, metformin administration improves mitochondrial dysfunction, regulates glucose levels, attenuates lipid and muscle catabolism, enhances wound healing, and has the potential to slow down post-burn aging acceleration

Mitochondrial dysfunction and endoplasmic reticulum stress

Interestingly, a recent study examining a murine model of thermal injury revealed that metformin mitigates mitochondrial dysfunction based on the bioenergetic state of the subject during treatment [147]. Specifically, the biguanide was shown to reduce hypermetabolic mitochondrial activity in adult mice, while markedly enhancing mitochondrial bioenergetics of older mice, which tend to have dysfunctional mitochondria [147]. Although further studies are necessary to validate and expand upon these findings, this study provides exciting new insights into targeting age-dependent mitochondrial dysfunction, particularly given the detrimental consequences of both prolonged and delayed or blunted hypermetabolic responses following burn injury.

In addition to its effects on mitochondrial bioenergetics, the role of metformin in modulating endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress following burn injury warrants attention. Burn trauma induces marked ER stress, activating unfolded protein response (UPR) pathways in multiple organs, including the liver and adipose tissue, which have been closely linked to post-burn hyperglycemia and insulin resistance [148, 149]. In fact, an in vivo study demonstrated that augmenting ER stress with Tunicamycin not only increases mortality in mice after thermal injury but also resulted in increased hepatic damage and hepatic steatosis [150]. Importantly, post-burn treatment with chaperone ER stress inhibitors attenuated hepatic ER stress and improved organ function following injury [150]. Interestingly, studies in models of obesity and T2D have demonstrated that metformin alleviates ER stress by activating AMPK, enhancing autophagy, and reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation [151, 152]. These effects ultimately preserve insulin sensitivity and restore metabolic homeostasis. While direct studies in burn models remain limited, a previous study by Hiyama et al. has shown that metformin treatment reduces hyperglycemia and improves insulin sensitivity in both the acute and chronic phases following burn injury, yet these benefits occur without detectable changes in hepatic ER stress markers [153]. These findings suggest that metformin’s beneficial effects in burn care may be independent of ER stress regulation. Nevertheless, further studies are needed to clarify whether ER stress modulation may occur in other tissues, such as the adipose and skeletal muscle, or under different contexts after metformin treatment in the burn setting.

Lipid metabolism

Further, at the tissue level, metformin has also been shown to modulate outcomes of the post-burn hypermetabolic response. As previously discussed, a hallmark of the post-burn response is WAT browning, which is associated with increased lipolytic activity and elevated whole-body energy expenditure [154]. Indeed, lipid metabolism is significantly altered following a burn injury, with increased lipolysis and free fatty acids mediating insulin resistance and ectopic fat deposition, thereby contributing to adverse clinical outcomes. As such, effective management of lipid metabolism is crucial in burn care to mitigate these negative effects and improve overall patient recovery. Notably, a recent study conducted by Auger and colleagues provides evidence that metformin may alleviate the post-burn hypermetabolic response by driving the conversion of beige adipose tissue into energy-storing WAT, thereby mitigating the lipotoxic consequences of hypermetabolic states and WAT browning [155]. Moreover, a clinical trial examining the safety and efficacy of metformin in severely burned patients found that the biguanide lowers levels of saturated fatty acids, a change that may help mitigate inflammation [24]. This was supported by a marked decline in circulating levels of interleukin (IL)−1β and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), pointing to an anti-inflammatory effect and attenuation of immune system reactivity [24].

Conversely, older adult burn patients are unable to activate the post-burn WAT browning response, which is thought to play a critical role in the reduced hypermetabolic and hypoinflammatory phenotype observed in these individuals following burn injury [156]. This inability to initiate the browning response has previously been linked to a lack of IL-6 and M2 macrophages, key drivers of the process [156]. However, a recent study by Barayan et al. utilizing single-cell RNA sequencing has shown that metformin treatment increases M2-like macrophage polarization and IL-6 expression in the subcutaneous adipose tissue of older adult burn patients [71]. These findings suggest that metformin may restore immune and inflammatory responses in adipose tissue, thereby re-establishing the tissue’s metabolic function and enhancing its stress responses, such as WAT browning. However, more research is required to understand the effects of metformin on WAT browning in older adult burn patients. The multifaceted nature of metformin in regulating various molecular pathways involved in lipid metabolism, inflammation, and immune responses may offer a novel approach to restoring the metabolic disturbances seen in burn patients. Given that different age groups exhibit distinct responses to burn injury, metformin’s ability to tailor metabolic and inflammatory pathways could position it as a critical tool in precision medicine. Its effects could be harnessed to optimize treatment strategies for various patient cohorts, addressing the unique metabolic challenges faced by both younger and older adults, and ultimately enhancing recovery and outcomes in burn care.

Muscle catabolism

Beyond adipose tissue, metformin also exerts beneficial effects on muscle tissue. In fact, in a study conducted by Gore et al. examining the peripheral metabolic effects of metformin after severe burn injury, treatment with metformin was found to not only attenuate hyperglycemia but also revealed an improvement in net muscle protein balance after the injury. Notably, this improvement has been attributed to an increase in fractional synthetic rate of muscle protein rather than to changes in muscle protein breakdown [86, 157]. Importantly, however, the anabolic effect of metformin is further potentiated by the co-administration of insulin, resulting in a greater enhancement in the rate of muscle protein synthesis [157]. Lastly, a recent study examining a murine model of thermal injury revealed that metformin mitigates muscle wasting and supports the maintenance of myofiber cross-sectional area following a burn injury by promoting paired box 7 (Pax7) expression and enhancing the proliferative capacity of muscle progenitor cells [158]. Overall, these findings position metformin as a promising adjunct therapy for mitigating the catabolic muscle wasting seen in burn patients.

Immune and inflammatory modulation

Burn injury disrupts the balance between innate and adaptive immunity, initially causing a systemic inflammatory response characterized by elevated innate immune cells and pro-inflammatory cytokines. This is often followed by profound immunosuppression, particularly affecting adaptive immunity, leading to reduced T-cell function, decreased B-cell counts, and suppressed type 1 T helper cell (Th1) responses [159]. Indeed, patients often present with elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, reduced neutrophil and macrophage function, and T-cell exhaustion, which together contribute to increased infection risk, delayed wound healing, and multi-organ dysfunction [160]. Interestingly, metformin has been shown to exert immunomodulatory effects that may counterbalance this dysregulation [161].

Several studies demonstrate that metformin promotes anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization (M2), reduces tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), IL-1β, and IL-6 secretion, and enhances T-cell survival and metabolic fitness, improving pathogen clearance and tissue repair in sepsis and chronic inflammation models [162–165]. Metformin’s effects are context-dependent: it enhances immune activity in tumors [166–168] but is predominantly anti-inflammatory in tuberculosis and multiple sclerosis [169–175], reflecting its capacity to fine-tune immune responses according to environmental cues. This duality aligns with the biphasic immune response in burns—attenuating acute hyperinflammation while supporting adaptive immunity during subsequent immunosuppression in older adults. Such context-dependent modulation likely involves shifts in immune cell metabolism, macrophage polarization, and cytokine signaling, highlighting metformin’s potential to restore immune balance in settings of both excessive inflammation and immunosenescence.

Emerging burn-specific data supports this paradigm. Jeschke et al. reported decreased IL-1β and MCP-1 expression in adipose tissue without changes in TNF-α, consistent with acute-phase anti-inflammatory effects [24]. Complementing these findings, the previously mentioned study by Barayan et al. using single-nucleus RNA sequencing of subcutaneous WAT (sWAT) from older burn patients demonstrated increased proportions of T cells and macrophages in metformin-treated patients [71]. Gene expression analysis revealed upregulation of M2-associated genes (RPBJ, CD163, MRC1, CD86, IL-10) and, to a lesser extent, M1-associated genes (NFKB1, IL1B, IL-6). Pathway enrichment highlighted leukocyte migration, chemotaxis, phagocytosis, differentiation, and IL-6 signaling. Notably, these tissue-level changes occurred despite unchanged circulating IL-6, underscoring the importance of local immune modulation. Collectively, this data suggests that metformin can reverse post-burn immune senescence in older adults, enhancing inflammatory responses at the tissue level that are otherwise diminished with age. Critical gaps remain, including small sample sizes, the systemic relevance of tissue-level changes, and the need to determine how metformin-mediated modulation of ER stress and other metabolic pathways may shape macrophage polarization, T-cell metabolic fitness, and overall post-burn immune recovery.

Wound healing

Unsurprisingly, wound healing is an important facet of the post-burn recovery process. Wound healing is a complex, multiphase process consisting of a coordinated series of events including hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling [2]. The regeneration of extensive wounds, such as those resulting from burn injuries, often presents a significant clinical challenge due to the limited regenerative capacity of the skin [176]. As such, surgical intervention or the application of bioactive dressings are often required to facilitate healing [176].

A prior study demonstrated that metformin promotes accelerated wound healing by inhibiting NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation and enhancing M2 macrophage polarization, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic agent for wound repair [177]. Intriguingly, a recently developed sprayable thermosensitive gel containing zinc-modified metformin (ZnMet-PF127), has demonstrated remarkable potential in promoting wound healing. Indeed, the study, which examined a murine model, demonstrated that ZnMet-PF127 accelerates the healing of burn-induced skin wounds by promoting key regenerative processes, including angiogenesis, cell proliferation, granulation tissue formation, and collagen deposition. Additionally, ZnMet prevents the accumulation of reactive oxygen species, thereby mitigating oxidative stress-induced cellular damage, which facilitates more effective wound healing [176]. ZnMet also displays potent antibacterial properties against pathogens like Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli, potentially reducing the risk of secondary wound infections [176]. Importantly, studies conducted by Ozyilmaz et al. and Chen et al. also demonstrated marked reductions in burn wound size when metformin was applied topically, delivered either through a hydrogel or metformin-releasing nanofiber dressing, respectively [178, 179]. Despite its beneficial effects on wound healing, metformin has also been reported to impair human keratinocyte proliferation, highlighting potential limitations [180]. Nonetheless, these findings point to a promising clinical application of metformin in burn injury management, though further research is warranted.

Accelerated biological aging

Lastly, we explore the potential use of metformin in aging, particularly in the context of burn injuries. Biological aging is widely recognized as a major contributor to frailty, morbidity, and mortality, but emerging research reveals that burn injuries may mimic and even accelerate this process. Indeed, two independent research groups recently discovered that burn injuries accelerate biological aging and the pace of aging [181, 182]. Alarmingly, our group recently reported burn injuries to be the most powerful and enduring accelerator of epigenetic aging documented to date [182]. Consistent with accelerated aging, burn injuries also increase the epigenetically inferred risk of frailty and mortality [182]. Thus, aging may represent a key target for improving the long-term outcomes of burn patients. Importantly, a growing body of evidence suggests that metformin may also serve to mitigate biological aging. In fact, a forty-month study of adult male cynomolgus monkeys highlighted the geroprotective effects of metformin, revealing its ability to decelerate the aging process across various tissues. The biguanide was found to rescue the majority of aged tissues, with the most notable effects observed in the frontal lobe, lungs, kidneys, liver, quadriceps, and skin. Metformin therapy was linked to the suppression of aging-associated pathways such as the inflammatory response, apoptosis, fibrosis, and ROS. In parallel, it was found to restore pathways typically downregulated during aging, such as those involved in development processes, lipid metabolism, and DNA repair. Notably, the study also observed a remarkable neuroprotective effect of metformin treatment, maintaining the structural integrity of the brain and promoting improvements in cognitive performance [183]. In humans, an association between metformin use and slower epigenetic aging has also been identified in individuals with diabetes mellitus [184]. Additionally, preliminary findings from the Metformin in Longevity Study (MILES) suggest that metformin influences gene expression and pathways linked to both healthspan and lifespan. Moreover, given the mounting evidence supporting metformin’s pleiotropic effects, the ongoing Targeting Aging with Metformin (TAME) clinical trial is investigating whether metformin can delay the onset of a range of age-related conditions by addressing the underlying biological mechanisms of aging [185]. However, whether metformin addresses burn-induced aging is yet to be explored.

Underexplored benefits of metformin relevant to burn care

Drug interactions and combined action with current standard interventions

When considering metformin as a post-burn hypoglycemic agent, it is important to understand how it may interact with or influence other standard treatments and management strategies in burn care. Unfortunately, research in this domain is extremely limited, as metformin is a relatively new consideration in burn injury. As previously mentioned, insulin remains the current gold-standard in managing hyperglycemia in burn patients, despite its risk of eliciting hypoglycemic episodes [92]. When combined with metformin in burn patients, outcomes improved compared to insulin monotherapy but were still worse than those of burn patients who received metformin alone [70]. However, research on combination insulin-metformin therapy in burn patients is limited and would benefit from further studies. Additionally, both propranolol, a non-selective beta blocker, and anabolic agents (typically oxandrolone) are often used as an effective strategy to mitigate post-burn hypermetabolism, a state that causes severe negative nitrogen balance, glucose intolerance, and insulin resistance [186, 187]. Although no study directly measures metformin’s interaction with these drugs, we suspect that metformin may indirectly support anabolism by stabilizing glucose and lipid metabolism and by alleviating post-thermal injury muscle wasting, as previously demonstrated [158]. Nevertheless, the use of metformin as a combination therapy with these medications has yet to be investigated. Finally, silver sulfadiazine and metformin have not been found to interact. However, certain cationic drugs such as morphine (an analgesic) and vancomycin (a broad-spectrum antibiotic), which are often used in severe burn injuries, theoretically have the potential of interacting with metformin by competing for common renal tubular transport systems, increasing its concentration [188, 189]. However, these interactions remain theoretical, prompting more research, careful patient monitoring, and dose adjustments.

Nutritional support and macronutrient administration

Beyond standard treatments, metformin could also affect macronutrient intake in burn patients. Due to their hypermetabolic state, burn patients are suggested to receive high-carbohydrate, high-protein, and low-fat diets to meet their increased demands [190, 191]. Although there is a gap in the literature surrounding this topic, we believe that based on metformin’s noted effects, it is likely to have positive effects on the administration of these macronutrients to burn patients. Specifically, metformin’s reduction of hepatic gluconeogenesis and enhancement of peripheral glucose uptake can help stabilize glycemia during aggressive feeding and help cells meet their energy demands [8, 39]. In addition, metformin’s promotion of fatty acid oxidation and reduction of excess lipolysis aligns with the lower-fat guidelines [40, 43–45]. Finally, metformin’s alleviation of post-burn muscle wasting potentially facilitates meeting protein demands [158]. Though there is no direct evidence that metformin changes prescribed macronutrient ratios, these effects altogether may improve tolerance and utilization of nutritional support in burn patients.

Conclusion

Metformin offers a unique therapeutic profile that aligns well with the complex needs of burn patients. Beyond its well-established ability to regulate glycemia, metformin directly targets several key metabolic and physiologic disturbances induced by severe burn injury. It not only reduces hepatic glucose production and enhances peripheral insulin sensitivity but also attenuates muscle catabolism, suppresses lipolysis, regulates WAT browning, supports mitochondrial function, enhances wound healing, and may even counteract the burn-induced acceleration of biological aging.

Clinically, metformin demonstrates non-inferiority to insulin for glycemic control, with superior safety and tolerability—markedly lowering the risk of hypoglycemia and lactic acidosis. Its oral administration and reduced need for intensive glucose monitoring further enhance its practicality in burn care settings. Moreover, metformin’s low cost and broad availability make it an accessible option globally. Importantly, as discussed, metformin’s multifaceted actions may also lend themselves to a more precision medicine-based approach to burn care. Its ability to modulate immune and metabolic responses appears to vary across different age groups—offering the potential to tailor therapy based on the unique needs of adult and older adult burn patients, who often exhibit divergent metabolic and inflammatory trajectories post-injury.

Given this breadth of benefit, metformin should not be viewed solely as an antihyperglycemic agent, but rather as a potential cornerstone of post-burn metabolic therapy. Incorporating metformin into burn care offers an opportunity to address not only hyperglycemia but the wider metabolic dysfunction that drives poor outcomes in this population. More importantly, given that post-burn pathological metabolic alterations are not homogeneous, treatment of these patients must be individualized and should not rely exclusively on the use of one agent. Rather, a more holistic approach to attenuate the catastrophic metabolic response in severely injured burn patients should ideally be adopted by combining various effective therapeutic strategies, such as the use of hypoglycemic agents, anabolic pharmacological agents, in conjunction with nutritional support. Thus, we encourage clinicians and burn specialists to consider integrating metformin into their treatment protocols and call for larger, prospective studies to further refine its role in this setting. The time is ripe to move beyond traditional approaches to burn metabolic care—and metformin stands as a safe, effective, and mechanistically sound candidate to help drive this shift.
