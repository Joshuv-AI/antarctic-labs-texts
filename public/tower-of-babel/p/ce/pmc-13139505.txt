Integrated proteomic and single-cell transcriptomic profiling elucidates immunomodulatory effects of L-serine in autism spectrum disorder
Authors: Jaemyung Jang, Seungeun Yeo, Jong Pil Kim, Soonbong Baek, Hyun Jin Jung, Su-Kyeong Hwang, Youngshik Choe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) is classified as early-emerging developmental divergences distinguished by aberrant interpersonal interactions as well as inflexible, stereotyped activities with no definitive treatments targeting the core symptoms. Accumulating evidence suggests that immune dysregulation critically contributes to ASD pathophysiology. We aimed to elucidate the potential immunomodulatory effects of L-serine in patients with ASD by investigating proteomic alterations in immune cell communication. Across 12 weeks of daily oral L-serine, we observed improvements in behavioral measures alongside more favorable Clinical Global Impression scores. Plasma extracellular vesicle proteomic profiling showed unique protein changes in immune-related pathways and T cell functions. From single-cell transcriptome sequencing profiling of peripheral blood mononuclear cells, the expression signatures of naive CD4 T cells shifted toward a more typical transcriptional state after L-serine treatment. Overall, integrating proteome-scale measurements with single-cell transcriptomes provided new mechanistic insight into ASD intervention effects and suggests that L-serine can reshape adaptive immune cell states.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43467-y.Keywords: Autism spectrum disorder, L-serine, Peripheral blood mononuclear cells, IL7Rlow naive CD4 T cells, ImmunometabolismSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Immunology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Exhibiting significant phenotypic heterogeneity, autism spectrum disorder (ASD) represents a developmental disorder defined by social interaction differences alongside restricted interests as well as stereotyped behaviors, with onset typically occurring in early childhood1. Although ASD has long been considered a purely neurological disorder, mounting evidence implicates the immune system in its pathogenesis. Patients with ASD exhibit higher rates of atopic conditions and inflammatory bowel disease2,3. More importantly, maternal infections affecting the immune axis increase offspring risk, and molecular links have been vigorously studied in animal models of maternal immune activation that recapitulate neurodevelopmental defects4,5. Immune stress accompanied by metabolic remodeling tracks with ASD severity and aligns with evidence of disrupted synaptic function under maladaptive brain–immune coupling6–8.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) provide a minimally invasive window into immune dysregulation in ASD. Investigations into peripheral immunity in ASD generally reveal an inflammatory profile, with IL-17A and IL-16 (and, in some cases, IL-9) representing key mediators reported to differ from controls2,3,9–11. T cells are crucial components of immune dysregulation in patients with ASD12. Imbalances in T cell subpopulations, including reductions in regulatory T cells, have been identified as sources of inflammatory cytokines and responses, alongside abnormal cytokine levels13,14. Furthermore, analyses of T-cell populations have identified alterations in specific subsets in patients with ASD, accompanied by significant changes in cytokine production and gene expression profiles in PBMCs following stimulation15. These molecular-level studies of ASD etiology have concentrated on factors associated with inflammation, but they have not clearly identified the specific cell types responsible for the immune abnormalities associated in individuals with ASD.

The intricate link between metabolic dysregulation and immune dysfunction in ASD suggests that targeting cellular metabolism could offer novel therapeutic avenues. Among potential metabolic modulators, certain amino acids have garnered attention for their roles in neurodevelopment and immune cell function. L-serine, which contributes serine-derived lipid synthesis and redox-buffering pathways, promoting membrane biogenesis and cellular stress tolerance16,17, has shown promise in preclinical models of autism-like behaviors in early clinical studies18–22. In pediatric ASD, an oral formulation with L-serine as the active ingredient has advanced to Phase III evaluation to examine efficacy on clinically relevant endpoints23. Therefore, we hypothesized that oral L-serine treatment could reprogram the transcriptomic and proteomic landscape of systemic immune cells, thereby modulating neuroimmune communication.

Because brain tissue is rarely obtainable in living autistic cohorts, we leveraged blood-based immune phenotyping as a practical window into immune perturbations relevant to central nervous system (CNS) disease processes. We therefore analyzed the proteomic composition of plasma extracellular vesicles (EVs) to identify systemic changes in immune-related pathways and investigated cell-type-specific transcriptomic alterations within PBMCs. First, we characterized the plasma EV proteomes in a subset of patients for whom sufficient plasma was available at each visit. Next, we acquired single-cell transcriptome profiles of PBMCs from two patients diagnosed with ASD (paired before/after L-serine treatment groups; four single-cell RNA sequencing libraries) to reconstruct CD4 T cell subtype dynamics, pseudotime trajectories, and ligand–receptor (LR) interaction networks. By integrating shared molecular signatures from plasma EV proteomic and single-cell transcriptomic analyses, we aimed to cross-validate our findings and ensure robust interpretation despite the limitations of the sample size for the sequencing. This integrative approach allows us to characterize immune-metabolic signatures associated with L-serine treatment in ASD, establishing a basis for identifying novel peripheral therapeutic targets.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

This study was conducted to examine these molecular foundations following the initial clinical trial (2021–2022), preceding broader validation. The primary endpoints were changes in the EV proteome as well as the single-cell RNA sequencing profiles of PBMCs following L-serine oral administration. Our analytical cohort comprised eleven individuals presenting with 2 patients for single-cell PBMC RNA sequencing and 9 patients for plasma EV proteomics. For plasma EV proteomic analysis, plasma samples were obtained from 9 patients; 5 patients provided blood at both untreated and treated status, 2 patients provided blood only in the untreated state, and 2 patients provided blood only in the treated state (Supplementary Table 1).

A proteomic analysis was conducted on blood plasma from nine participants before and/or after L-serine oral administration (400 mg/kg/day). While the study employed a longitudinal pre- and post-treatment design, strict adherence to paired sampling was constrained by logistical factors inherent to the pediatric population. These factors, including restrictions on the volume of blood collected from pediatric participants and inconsistencies in visit scheduling due to their pediatric status, meant that not all samples were paired for downstream omics analysis. L-serine was provided as a powder to be dissolved in water or juice. This dosage fell within the effective and safe dose range (308–571 mg/kg/day) determined by the weight-tiered dosing simulations of the phase 2 trial. In our previous study, pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic analyses (n = 145) demonstrated that L-serine exhibited greater efficacy at higher doses, with regimens equivalent to 400 mg/kg/day leading to notable increases in K-VABS-II-ABC scores (1.7–3.7 points vs. 0.9–1.2 with placebo)22. Based on this optimized L-serine dosing, we subsequently investigated its effects in patients with ASD. To minimize potential confounding effects, the use of medications, dietary supplements, or herbal products intended to improve ASD–related symptoms, including omega-3 fatty acids, L-carnitine, methyl B12, probiotics, and related supplements, was not permitted within two weeks prior to screening. Participants maintained their usual diet throughout the study period. Because apparent clearance was 34–41% lower in the youngest/lightest children, a weight-based (mg/kg) regimen was selected to achieve comparable systemic exposure across all ages and weights and for getting the target (about 40–73% across bands) compared to placebo. Longitudinal PBMC sampling from two donors generated four single-cell RNA sequencing libraries in total. The study subset was determined by the availability of explicit informed consent for high-resolution genomic profiling. PBMCs from the two participants who consented to this comprehensive analysis were processed. Study participants were assessed by clinical experts for no other illnesses or comorbidities other than the participants’ ASD symptoms for this study. No substantial protocol amendments affecting eligibility criteria, outcomes, or analyses were made after trial initiation. No participants used psychotropic medications or amino-acid–containing supplements during the study. Caregivers were instructed to report any new medications or supplements initiated during participation. Prohibited medications included antipsychotics, psychostimulants, antidepressants, anxiolytics, mood stabilizers, neuroleptics, investigational products in the month leading up to screening, and supplements taken for ASD symptom improvement in the two weeks leading up to screening. No formal interim analyses were planned or conducted. Participants could be discontinued for serious adverse events, withdrawal of consent, or clinician judgment of unacceptable risk.

Patient and public involvement

This experiment was designed, carried out, analyzed, and reported without any official involvement from patients or the general public. Caregivers contributed to study procedures by administering the investigational product, completing dosing diaries, reporting adverse events, and completing caregiver-reported instruments; investigators conducted clinician-rated assessments. After study completion, participating families will receive a plain-language summary of results, and broader dissemination will be pursued through local ASD advocacy and caregiver groups.

Site and personnel eligibility

The Department of Pediatrics at Kyungpook National University Hospital (KNUH) served as the single study site for this investigation, a tertiary care center with clinical laboratory accreditation and capabilities for pediatric neurodevelopmental assessments, phlebotomy, and secure biobanking. The site held IRB approval for Protocol Version 2.3 and maintained secure storage for study medication and records, with on-site emergency pediatric support. On-site activities were performed by board-certified pediatric clinicians and trained research coordinators; research nurses from the KNUH Clinical Trial Center were trained in Good Clinical Practice (GCP) and study-specific procedures. Clinical assessors had relevant clinical backgrounds and certification in the instruments used; the same assessor evaluated each participant when feasible and was not involved in molecular analyses. All blood processing was performed by staff trained per standard operating procedures for PBMC isolation and EV handling.

Participant eligibility and recruitment

Inclusion criteria. Participants were 2–11 years of age at consent; had a clinician-confirmed ASD diagnosis according to DSM-5, requiring evidence of social-communication impairment across contexts together with at least two restricted/repetitive behavior features; and had ASD confirmed by Autism Diagnostic Interview–Revised at baseline (Visit 2). Consent to participate was documented in writing from the participant and/or legal guardian(s) prior to the first study visit/procedure. The same legal guardian(s) were required to remain available throughout the 24-week study to provide reliable information, attend visits, supervise study product administration, and communicate fluently in Korean.

Recruitment. Consecutive eligible participants were recruited at KNUH between 3 February 2021 and 12 May 2022 through the hospital’s neurodevelopmental clinic; all patients who met the eligibility criteria and provided consent were enrolled. Recruitment and follow-up proceeded at a single site within the IRB-approved study window.

Safety monitoring and harms assessment

Adverse events were collected from consent through the end of the study at each scheduled contact (Visit 2, Visit 3 [telephone], and Visit 4-8) and during unscheduled contacts. At visits, caregivers participated in a structured interview (open-ended queries followed by targeted questions on gastrointestinal, urinary, respiratory, dermatologic, and behavioral symptoms, including sleep and appetite). Clinicians performed focused exams as indicated and assessed vital signs (BP after 5-minute rest, pulse, temperature when febrile symptoms were reported, weight, and height at Visit 1, Visit 2, Visit 6, and Visit 8).

Plasma EV proteomic analysis

Human plasma sample

Plasma samples were obtained from the study participants at KNUH. Blood was drawn into BD Vacutainer EDTA tubes (BD Biosciences), and plasma (0.25 ml) was recovered from each sample and stored in a low-protein-binding tube prior to proteomic research.

EVs preparation

To eliminate cellular remnants and large-scale vesicles, plasma samples were thawed on ice, followed by centrifugation at 1,500 × g at 4 °C for 10 min. Preparation of EVs was carried out using a polymer-based precipitation and cleanup workflow (ExoQuick ULTRA isolation kit; SBI System Biosciences). The EV fractions were resuspended in Hanks’ balanced salt solution (Welgene) for nanoparticle tracking analysis or treated with RIPA lysis solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with Halt™ Protease inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for proteomic analysis.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

NTA evaluated EV size profiles using a NanoSight LM10 (Malvern Panalytical). Samples were recorded at 25 °C (three 30-second recordings per specimen) and processed using NTA v3.2. The acquisition/analysis parameters (camera level 10; threshold 5) were chosen to optimize particle detection while minimizing pixel saturation.

Western blotting analysis

EV protein was quantified by a BCA protein assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For immunoblotting, 20 µg per specimen was separated on 4–20% gradient precast gels (Mini-PROTEAN® TGX™, Bio-Rad) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Immobilon®-P, Merck Millipore). After being blocked with 5% milk, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing, horseradish peroxidase-linked secondary antibodies were applied for 30 min at room temperature, and chemiluminescence was developed using an ECL substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Antibodies (listed below) were prepared in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) with 0.1% Tween 20 and 3% bovine serum albumin (primary 1:1000; secondary 1:5000), consistent with prior validation.

Liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis

Label-free proteomics was performed as described previously with minor adaptations24. Proteins were reduced (10 mM DL-dithiothreitol; 55 °C; 30 min) and cysteines were alkylated (20 mM iodoacetamide; 20 min; protected from light), followed by overnight trypsin digestion at 37 °C. After being desalted on a C18 spin column (Pierce™, Thermo Fisher Scientific), the peptides were reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid (20 µL). LC–MS/MS was carried out on an UltiMate 3000 nanoLC coupled to a Q Exactive Plus Orbitrap (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using aqueous and acetonitrile mobile phases, each supplemented with 0.1% formic acid. After loading on a PepMap 100 C18 trap, peptides were resolved on a PepMap RSLC C18 analytical column (0.3 µL/min; 135 min gradient) and acquired in DDA mode (MS1: 350–2000 m/z, followed by MS/MS). Database searching was performed in Proteome Discoverer (PD, Thermo Fisher Scientific) v3.0 against the UniProt human reference proteome, allowing tryptic peptides (7–30 aa), up to two missed cleavages, and up to three variable modifications per peptide; fragment tolerance was set to 20 ppm. The normalized abundance of quantified proteins with a protein accession number, a fold change (FC), and a P value were imported into the R environment. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs, |Log2FC| ≥ 2 and a P-value < 0.01) identified by the PD software were graphically represented via volcano plots utilizing the EnhancedVolcano tool in R 25.

To identify the source of the proteome from ASD plasma EVs, we employed the QUANTISEQ immune-deconvolution approach26 to assess the probability of aligning our proteomic findings with certain immune cell types. The DEP R package27 converted the findings exported by the PD software into a SummarizedExperiment structure. The abundance value of proteins with missing values (those displayed as ‘NaN’) was imputed based on the function “MinProb”. A matrix containing normalized abundance values with unique protein names as rows and experimental groups as columns was applied to the immunedeconv R package28.

We used the EnrichR software29 to do functional enrichment analysis on the proteins that were differentially expressed (|Log2FC| ≥ 2 and a P-value < 0.01). The databases employed for this analysis were WikiPathways, GO Biological Pathway 2023, and Reactome.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

PBMCs collection

Fresh whole blood samples were obtained from participants using anticoagulant-containing tubes, such as BD Vacutainer® EDTA tubes (BD Biosciences). PBMCs were isolated using SepMate-50 (StemCell Technologies). Whole blood (5 mL) was mixed with PBS (5 mL) and density gradient medium (15 mL). Following centrifugation (800 × g, 30 min), PBMCs were collected from the upper layer, rinsed twice in PBS supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (1,200 × g, 10 min per wash), and finally resuspended in 400 µL Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium containing 0.04% bovine serum albumin.

Single-cell encapsulation and library preparation

Encapsulation/barcoding of single cells and subsequent library construction were carried out following the protocols described in previously published protocols30. Single-cell suspensions (100–200 cells/µL) were introduced into a custom droplet-microfluidic device and packaged together with barcoded capture beads and reverse-transcription reagents to generate nanoliter reaction droplets. cDNA synthesis proceeded within droplets for 2 hr, after which sequencing libraries were constructed using the established workflow reported previously30.

Single-cell RNA sequencing data processing and analysis

The libraries were quantified and then loaded onto a NovaSeq 6000 system (PE 50 S2). Raw sequencing data were demultiplexed into FASTQ files and mapped to a reference human transcriptome (Ensembl GRCh38 release 100 version) using a previously published pipeline31 in Python that is available at https://github.com/indrops/indrops. Count matrices (genes × cells) and associated quantification metrics were exported as separate files for each experiment or condition, and read through the R package indRop (https://github.com/caleblareau/indRop). We used the scDblFinder algorithm to inspect putative doublet cells, and they were excluded from analysis32. To reduce ambient RNA and damaged cells, we calculated Seurat QC covariates (mitochondrial, ribosomal, and hemoglobin fractions, plus log10GenesPerUMI) and used robust sample-wise filtering. Barcodes were kept only if they passed the minimum complexity criteria (nFeature_RNA ≥ 500; log10GenesPerUMI > 0.80) and the mitochondrial signal remained within a robust upper bound (median + 2× median absolute deviation). To reduce multiplet contamination, we removed barcodes of excessive depth and complexity using upper limits determined by median + 5× median absolute deviation for nCount_RNA and nFeature_RNA. Genes found in fewer than 10 barcodes were not included in the following study.

Initial single-cell preprocessing, including dataset integration, graph-based clustering, and low-dimensional embedding, was carried out in R with Seurat (version 4)33. Following quality filtering, expression values were normalized with SCTransform, and we included covariates capturing cell-cycle state and mitochondrial signal to reduce unwanted technical/biological variation during normalization. To integrate samples and mitigate batch-associated structure, we applied Harmony on the reduced-dimensional representation prior to downstream clustering and visualization34. Cell identities were inferred using the FindAllMarkers function to calculate gene signatures as genes overexpressed in the given cell type by comparing them to all other cells. Clustering in the integrated PBMC datasets was annotated according to canonical marker expression35: CD4 T cells (IL7R, CCR7), CD8 T cells (CD8A, CD8B), monocytes (CD14, MS4A7), natural killer (NK) cells (GNLY, NKG7), B cells (CD79A, MS4A1), and platelets (PPBP, PF4). Furthermore, metadata from integrated reference datasets, including adult samples (GSE150728) and healthy pediatric samples (GSE135779), were used to further refine cluster annotations.

Gene score calculation of risk genes

Using UCell36 via AddModuleScore_UCell, we obtained cell-level scores for autism-specific gene lists (inflammation-related gene sets) and quantified the pre/post L-serine Δscore for each signature.

Cell population analysis

Cellular composition was summarized as stacked bar charts in R using dittoSeq’s dittoBarPlot()37. To assess whether the cluster proportions were significantly different between cell populations, we used the R package scProportionTest38 for statistical testing and visualization. To test for shifts in cluster composition between two conditions, we used the scProportionTest, which generates a P value per cluster by permuting L-serine-treated groups and quantifies uncertainty in the predicted proportion difference.

Differential expression and functional enrichment analysis

Cluster-enriched markers were obtained with Seurat’s FindAllMarkers, while within-cell-type contrasts were evaluated using FindMarkers with the MAST test39. We retained DE candidates only when they satisfied both a P-value threshold (< 0.01) and an effect-size filter (|Log2FC| > 1). For genes shared across groups, we summarized group-level mean expression and visualized the resulting matrix as a heatmap.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were looked into using clusterProfiler40 in order to identify enriched Gene Ontology-Biological Process (GO-BP) categories and pathway signals from Reactome and WikiPathways. To unravel the biological processes relevant to L-serine, we searched for the pathways including the DEGs of interest (P-value < 0.05).

Single-cell trajectory inference analysis

Using Slingshot in R, we derived lineages via curve fitting and summarized developmental progression with pseudotime computed along the resulting trajectories41. The slingshot function has the same dimensionality reduction and the same clusters as the Seurat objects. The main trajectories were inferred by setting the specific subset as the principal node, and the identified paths were projected onto the UMAP embedding for visualization42. To identify the genes affected during the course of the trajectory in the early stage, the cells were divided along these trajectories. To investigate gene expression changes over pseudotime, we applied the fitGAM function and the associationTest function of the tradeSeq software43. To understand comprehensive pathways for the genes associated with pseudotime in the two groups, we performed GO-BP and WikiPathways enrichment analyses using the ClueGO plugin44 in Cytoscape, with an adjusted P-value cutoff of 0.05.

Cell-to-cell interaction analysis

Significant LR pairs were inferred using CellPhoneDB’s statistical analysis framework45 with its accompanying database (version 5.0.0) in a Python (version 3.9.19) environment, separately for ASD samples before and after L-serine oral administration. For each condition, CellPhoneDB’s statistical analysis was conducted, and LR pairs (“sender” or “receiver”) were included only if their components met a 10% cluster-level detection cutoff in the interacting cell populations. Significance of interactions was assessed using 1,000 random permutations (P-value < 0.05), and all potential interactions were scored. To focus on CD4 T cell communications, significant LR pairs involving CD4 T cells were extracted by filtering the CellPhoneDB output tables for entries where CD4 T cells were listed as one of the interacting cell types. In Python, ktplotspy was used to generate figures from CellPhoneDB output tables summarizing LR interactions.

Weighted correlation network analysis for single-cell RNA sequencing data

High-dimensional gene co-expression correlation structure was modeled in R with hdWGCNA (v0.4.06) as part of our workflow46. All calculations were performed on a Seurat object, and hdWGCNA outputs were stored within the object’s @misc slot for downstream analysis. The Seurat object was first prepared for analysis using the SetupForWGCNA function. To ensure consistency with upstream processing, only genes included in the SCTrans form normalization were selected for network analysis. To reduce noise and computational burden, we aggregated similar cells into metacells using ConstructMetacells, leveraging the SCTransform-corrected data (slot = “scale.data”, assay = “SCT”). We then assessed soft-thresholding power with TestSoftPowers and selected the smallest power (model fit ≥ 0.8). Using the chosen soft-power, we built a topological overlap matrix via BuildNetwork, which partitioned genes into modules. For each module, we computed a single representative expression profile and stored these module-level summaries in the Seurat object for future comparisons.

To visualize the co-expression network structure, we applied RunModuleUMAP, restricting it to the top 16 hub genes per module. To detect modules whose overall expression differed between biological groups, we implemented FindDMEs. Module-level differential activity was evaluated by DME analysis (FindDMEs, Wilcoxon/Mann–Whitney) and visualized with volcano plots. We plotted the resulting UMAP embedding with ggplot2 to display module-specific gene neighborhoods. To assign biological meaning to modules, we performed over-representation analysis using Enrichr via RunEnrichr, querying the “GO_Biological_Process_2023” database. Enrichment results were visualized with ggplot2.

Statistical analysis

The statistical tests were predefined as part of the experimental design. Clinical and behavioral data were analyzed using paired t-tests, as all participants completed all assessments at both time points. Conversely, the Western blot dataset included unbalanced measurements due to missing data at certain time points. To appropriately handle this, Western blots were examined employing a linear mixed-effects model that included L-serine treatment as the fixed term and patient ID as a random-intercept grouping factor. This approach accommodates the partially paired structure of the data. Models were fitted in R using lme4, and P-values were obtained using lmerTest with the Satterthwaite approximation. Proteomic statistical analysis was performed using the built-in functions of PD software, following a standardized pipeline provided by the mass spectrometry manufacturer to ensure reliable and reproducible results. For proteomic data, missing protein abundance values were imputed using the PD’s own functions. Statistical analyses of single-cell transcriptome datasets were performed in R environment. Unless otherwise noted, differences between two groups were analyzed using the Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test. All graphs show mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). We did not apply a single global multiple-comparisons correction across all hypotheses. Because our exploratory analyses comprise multiple, heterogeneous test families with strong dependence structures (e.g., differential expression, pathway enrichment, pseudotime-based trajectory testing, LR interaction inference, and network/module-level analyses). Instead, multiple-testing control was applied separately within each statistical module. For selected exploratory follow-ups, we used an unadjusted cutoff (P-value < 0.01) to retain enough genes for rank-based enrichment, because adjusted thresholds often left too few features to interrogate pathways. Accordingly, these outputs are treated as provisional signals and weighed alongside effect magnitudes and cross-omic agreement.

Results

Improvement of the behavioral core symptoms in patients with ASD by L-serine

L-serine enhances cognitive function and behaviors in animal models exhibiting autism-like characteristics23,47, as well as in patients with GRIN2B-related neurodevelopmental disorders18–22. L-serine appears able to reshape brain wiring patterns linked with ASD pathogenesis. To evaluate its efficacy in a clinical context, we studied how patients with ASD responded to L-serine administration. All enrolled participants (100%) received L-serine (400 mg/kg/day) and were followed through the week-12 assessment, with zero early exits from the trial. All participants were included in the clinical outcome analyses. None had been previously exposed to L-serine. All participants were medication-naive at baseline (no psychotropic medications or amino acid supplements). Following a 12-week regimen of oral L-serine at 400 mg/kg/day, multiple clinical endpoints were improved relative to baseline. Distribution-based minimal clinically important difference (MCID) on Vineland-II scores was calculated to evaluate the improvement of participants after L-serine treatment. The participants showed improved clinical global impression (CGI) scores following treatment; specifically, L-serine decreased CGI-severity and CGI-improvement (Table 1).

Proteomic characterization of plasma EV derived from patients with ASD after L-serine treatment

To better understand the mechanisms underlying these clinical improvements, we employed an integrative approach, analyzing proteomic and transcriptomic alterations in peripheral blood components. Although analysis of human brain tissue would provide the most direct insights, ethical and practical constraints, particularly in pediatric patients with ASD, required the utilization of alternative accessible tissues. We therefore selected PBMCs and plasma EVs due to easy availability through minimally invasive procedures. Furthermore, as these components circulate throughout the body, we could take indirect information about the physiological changes as a result of L-serine treatment.

EVs released by many cell types disseminate bioactive cargo through body fluids, enabling communication between distant cell populations, such as circulating immune cells48–51. In this study, to investigate proteomic changes in pediatric patients with ASD following L-serine treatment, we conducted EV proteomic profiling to capture circulating EVs that likely reflect systemic (including immune-related) changes. To investigate proteomic changes in EVs from patients with ASD after oral L-serine administration, we extracted EVs from plasma (Fig. 1A). EVs were characterized using NTA, which showed the majority exhibited a size distribution within 100 nm, consistent with the typical size range of 30–150 nm for normal EVs (Fig. 1B). EVs were validated by immunoblot detection of TSG101, ALIX, CD63, and GAPDH. Among the markers, CD63 and ALIX exhibited a significant increase after 12 weeks of L-serine administration (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. 1). This result indicates that L-serine may influence vesicle biogenesis or sorting, compatible with altered incorporation of CD63 and ALIX into EVs.

To further explore the heterogeneity of plasma EVs, we investigated proteomic alteration in EVs before and after oral administration with L-serine (Fig. 1D). We found a total of 944 proteins, and 130 proteins were increased (Log2FC > 2, P-value < 0.01) and 82 proteins were reduced (Log2FC < −2, P-value < 0.01) by L-serine treatment (Fig. 1E, F). To understand the observed shifts, we applied DEPs to enriched biological pathways with EnrichR, drawing on several annotation libraries. We found 51 pathways from GO-BP, 296 pathways from Reactome, and 36 pathways from WikiPathways associated with L-serine treatment. These pathways were significantly enriched among the positively DEPs (adjusted P-value < 0.1, Supplementary Data 1). While the majority of enriched terms pointed to systemic alterations in cellular metabolism and signal transduction, the interesting terms covered immune-related categories, including the Regulation of T cell activation pathway (GO:0050863) related to LGALS3, DUSP3, HLA-DRA, and CTSG, and the T Cell Receptor and Co-Stimulatory Signaling pathway (WP2583) related to RASA1 and FYN. These findings imply that oral L-serine administration may alter T cell-related immune systems in the patients. As EVs can originate from various cell types, it was necessary to determine the specific cell types responsible for the EV secretion. To identify the potential sources of EVs, we performed a deconvolution analysis26. The inferred composition indicated that the EV proteomic signal could be mapped to a multi-lineage immune origin, most prominently aligning with CD4 T cells and regulatory T cells (Fig. 1G). However, this deconvolution offers only a coarse, indirect approximation of potential cellular origin and does not specify which cell types are responsible for the L-serine response or ASD pathogenesis.

Single-cell transcriptomic changes in PBMCs from patients with ASD after L-serine administration

Using single-cell transcriptomics, we expanded our analysis to predict the cellular source of EVs, informed by the deconvolution analysis. Single-cell transcriptomes from PBMCs were profiled using a laboratory-tailored inDrop™ droplet workflow developed in-house30,31,52 (Fig. 2A). Our transcriptome datasets of 14,603 total cells passing multiple quality control and filtering steps were created across the conditions. To analyze the transcriptome, we first integrated the single-cell transcriptome dataset with two datasets: 16,627 PBMCs derived from healthy adults (GSE150728)53 as a reference dataset and 85,807 PBMCs from healthy children as a healthy control (GSE135779)54. To ensure age-appropriate comparisons, subsequent analyses following cell type assignment were restricted to four pediatric healthy control samples aged 7–8 years (GSM4029907, GSM4029914, GSM4029931, GSM4029938) comprising 36,919 PBMCs, matching our transcriptome datasets. Visualization of datasets from patients with ASD and healthy controls in two UMAP dimensional spaces showed no batch effects driven by sequencing and processing sites (Fig. 2B). Using the Louvain clustering algorithm with multilevel refinement and canonical cell-type-specific marker genes35, we manually confirmed 11 cellular identities, including CD4 T cells (IL7R, CCR7), CD8 T cells (CD8A, CD8B), NK cells (GNLY, NKG7), B cells (CD79A, MS4A1), monocytes (CD14, MS4A7), and platelets (PPBP, PF4) (Fig. 2C).

We profiled gene-expression changes across 11 PBMC cell populations after L-serine treatment and contrasted these signatures with healthy controls. Cell-type composition analysis in ASD indicated enrichment of CD4 T cells and monocytes alongside a relative depletion of NK cells when compared with the control group (Fig. 2D). ASD samples showed a compositional shift characterized by increased CD4 T cells and monocytes together with fewer NK cells. Next, we summarized ASD-gene expression using two independently computed gene-set scores: one from the SFARI Category 1 list55 and another from the TADA-supported list56. Interestingly, the gene score analysis revealed that L-serine significantly reduced both the SFARI gene score and the ASD risk gene score derived from TADA in CD4 T cells, monocytes, and NK cells (P-value < 0.01, Fig. 2E and Supplementary Table 2).

Functional implications of transcriptional changes in IL7Rlow naive CD4 T cells

To identify CD4 T cell-specific alterations with L-serine treatment, we extracted 16,809 CD4 T cells (Fig. 3A), of which 2,511 cells were collected before treatment and 2,145 cells were after the administration of L-serine, respectively (Fig. 3B). Five CD4 T cell subtypes were mapped based on previously established CD4 T cell subtype-specific gene sets33: An IL7R low or high naive CD4 T cell-, a dividing T cell-, a central memory T cell-, and a regulatory T cell-cluster (Supplementary Fig. 2A). To further define the two naive subpopulations, we first confirmed the expression of canonical naive T cell markers within these clusters and examined the expression of representative genes known to distinguish naive CD4 T cells57, separating IL7Rlow and IL7Rhigh naive CD4 T cells for downstream analyses (Supplementary Fig. 2B). At baseline, PBMCs from the ASD group were biased toward IL7Rlow naive CD4 T cells relative to healthy controls (Fig. 3C); this bias was markedly attenuated following L-serine treatment (Fig. 3D).

Furthermore, we identified subtype-specific DEGs (P-value < 0.05) across five CD4 T cell subtypes and interpreted these gene lists using GO-BP along with pathway sets drawn from Reactome and WikiPathways (Supplementary Data 2). It revealed that the IL7Rlow naive CD4 T cell subtype had the most genes with significant expression changes among the cell types (adjusted P-value < 0.01, average |Log2FC| > 1, Fig. 3E). Within the IL7Rlow naive CD4 T cell subtype, differential expression analysis identified 699 genes with lower expression and 7,081 genes with higher expression in ASD versus healthy controls. Treatment of L-serine also significantly downregulated 195 genes and upregulated 136 genes in the IL7Rlow naive CD4 T cell subtype of the patients (Supplementary Data 2).

The analysis revealed 90 genes shared between the transcriptomic signatures of the L-serine treatment response and the baseline differences observed between ASD cohorts and healthy controls in the IL-7Rlow naive CD4 T cell subtype. Among these common genes, 20 genes, with altered expression in the patients following L-serine treatment, were subsequently restored to the levels in healthy controls (Fig. 3F). S100A4 and FOS were repressed in the patients but were restored after L-serine oral administration. The expression of the remaining 18 genes, including ORC4, BCL11B, and PAG1, which were upregulated in the patients not treated with L-serine, was reduced following L-serine treatment. To characterize immune-related transcriptional programs in the IL7Rlow naive CD4 T cells, we executed pathway enrichment studies using all DEGs and chose several pathways incorporating the 20 genes that shifted toward healthy-control levels. Immune-related pathways, including T-cell selection/differentiation and Toll-like receptor signaling, were negatively enriched in ASD cohorts relative to age-matched pediatric healthy groups (Supplementary Fig. 3A). In contrast, after L-serine administration, multiple immune-related categories (including adaptive immune system terms) showed positive enrichment compared with the before-L-serine-treatment group (Fig. 3G). Interestingly, the inflammation score remained unchanged in this transcriptomic analysis regardless of L-serine treatment (Supplementary Fig. 3B). The findings suggest that L-serine could impact the maturation and differentiation of IL7Rlow naive CD4 T cells, potentially guiding them toward a typical developmental pathway.

Transcriptomic modulation of CD4 T cells in patients with ASD

L-serine is known to protect cells from oxidative stress and apoptosis, possibly via one-carbon metabolism and biosynthesis16,17. We hypothesized that L-serine reshapes CD4 T cell developmental trajectories by metabolically balancing maturation and survival of IL7Rlow naive CD4 T cells. To identify L-serine-induced transcriptional changes along their development, we employed trajectory inference analysis to order CD4 T cells along a differentiation axis (Fig. 4A). Given that early differentiation proceeds from naive CD4 T cells58, we set the IL7Rlow naive CD4 T subset as the starting cluster. This analysis demonstrated a bifurcating pseudotime trajectory, including a maturation process (trajectory A) and a proliferation process (trajectory B). Trajectory A indicated maturation, ending in CD4 T central memory/regulatory cells, whereas Trajectory B indicated proliferation, terminating in CD4 T-dividing cells. We fitted a generalized additive model using the tradeSeq approach to find significant gene expression patterns along these trajectories (adjusted P-value < 0.01). For trajectory A, 1,083 and 832 genes were associated with pseudotime before and after L-serine administration, respectively (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Data 3). Similarly, 858 and 701 genes related to pseudotime progression were detected along trajectory B in the before- and the after-L-serine administration groups, respectively. Our previous deconvolution analysis of EV proteomics suggested that CD4 T cells could be a key cellular source. To integrate these results, we identified genes that exhibited pseudotime-dependent changes during CD4 T cell development and were also differentially expressed in the EV proteomic dataset. We identified 13 and 8 unique genes in Trajectory A and 8 and 3 unique genes in Trajectory B before and after L-serine administration, respectively (Fig. 4B). Gene expression along pseudotime revealed distinct phases in both Trajectories A and B (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Data 4). Among the genes, ANXA1 and RAN were increased, and MGST3 was reduced along trajectory A by L-serine treatment (Fig. 4D). Along trajectory B, which reflects the conversion from IL7Rlow naive CD4 T cells to CD4 T-dividing cells, primary metabolic process (GO:0044238)-related genes, such as RAN, SEC11A, and B2M, were highly expressed following L-serine administration (Fig. 4D). These results indicate that metabolic alterations by L-serine treatment-induced genes are involved in the maturation of naive CD4 T cells. Collectively, these trajectory analyses revealed that L‑serine fundamentally reprogrammed the metabolic state of IL7Rlow naive CD4 T cells, and CD4 T cells were moving from a developmental bottleneck toward a functionally competent state, with potential implications for overall immune balance in patients with ASD.

L-serine treatment is associated with changes in inferred immune communication networks

To evaluate the functional changes in CD4 T cells by L-serine treatment, we analyzed single-cell–based cell–cell interactions. CD4 T cells exhibited potent interactions with monocytes, NK cells, and γδ T cells, and these interactions were diminished by L-serine (Fig. 5A). We further analyzed representative LR pairs among the cell-cell interactions (Fig. 5B). CD8 T cells and monocytes interacted with CD4 T cells via an SEMA4D-PTPRC LR pair. KLRB1 expressed by NK cells was paired with CLEC2D expressed by CD4 T cells. L-serine treatment reduced these interactions and promoted CD44 from CD4 T cells to interact with TYROBP in monocytes, NK cells, and pDCs (Fig. 5B).

To understand the molecular alterations underlying these inferred interactions, we ran cell-type–specific differential expression studies for CD8 T cells and NK cells (Fig. 5C). Representative ligand-related genes, including CD8A, CD8B, PAG1, and KLRB1, were downregulated by L-serine treatment. However, SPARC and MTPN, also identified in the EV proteomics analysis, were upregulated. (Fig. 5C). The EV-sourced ligands may be hidden in the cell-cell interaction analysis. To further identify co-regulated genes with EV-cargo proteins and representative ligands from the cell-cell interaction prediction, we built a weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) framework on CD8 T- and NK-cell single-cell RNA sequencing profiles. WGCNA identified 19 modules in CD8 T cells. The brown modules, downregulated following L-serine administration, contained CD8A, CD8B, and PAG1, which are associated with the complex of LCK–CD8 receptor and are implicated in T cell activation (Fig. 5D)59. As expected, the turquoise module showed upregulated MTPN and SPARC, encoding EV-cargo proteins. In NK cells, we found that 8 out of 13 co-expression modules had significantly changed module eigengene expression (Fig. 5E). The turquoise module, including FYN and PIK3R1, was decreased by L-serine treatment. The blue module contained RPL23, which implicates ribosomal macromolecular biosynthesis in CD4–NK cross-talk. These findings showed that the EV proteins were highly related to genes differentially regulated by L-serine in immune cell interactions. In essence, the accumulated evidence highlights that L-serine reconfigures the intercellular immune communication landscape through a dual mechanism. It selectively reduces abnormal direct cell-cell interactions and changes the cargo of CD4 T cell-derived EVs. This L-serine-mediated extensive immune rewiring may restore immune balance in patients with ASD.

Sensitivity analysis to mitigate bias concerning ethnic and geographical origin

To assess whether cross-study population background would influence our external-control comparisons, we performed a sensitivity analysis using three independent PBMC single-cell RNA sequencing datasets generated from China/Asia-based cohorts (GSE27945260, GSE20629561, and GSE16873262). After integrating these datasets, the overall PBMC landscape remained stable: 11 major immune cell groups were identified using similar clustering structure and canonical marker gene expression (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). Under these alternative healthy control datasets, the direction of the major compositional differences was qualitatively consistent with the primary analysis. Specifically, children with ASD before L-serine treatment exhibited higher proportions of CD4 T cells. However, these proportions shifted towards the control distribution following L-serine administration (Supplementary Fig. 4C). Consistently, SFARI and TADA gene-set scoring across cell types demonstrated that the post-L-serine-treatment decrease in ASD risk-gene scores in CD4 T cells was maintained when utilizing the China/Asia-based healthy controls (Supplementary Fig. 4D).

We noticed minor quantitative variations compared with the primary analysis: a rise of 47 CD4 T cells (1.87%) in the before-L-serine-treatment group and a drop of 193 CD4 T cells (9.00%) in the after-L-serine-treatment group (Supplementary Fig. 5A). The larger number of CD4 T cells contributed by the China/Asia-based healthy controls (25,044 cells) increased the number of CD4 T cell subtypes in this sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5B). Despite these differences in cell number and clustering resolution, the central findings regarding CD4 T cells were broadly reproduced. The IL7low naive CD4 T cells persisted as the most predominant population in ASD prior to L-serine treatment and exhibited a notable reduction subsequent to L-serine administration (Supplementary Fig. 5C, D). Differential expression analysis consistently showed the most significant transcriptional alterations in IL7low naive CD4 T cells (Supplementary Fig. 5E). The number of genes exhibiting different expression before versus after L-serine administration decreased from 20 to 16 under this alternative framework, but the majority of the molecular signature remained (Supplementary Fig. 5F). Furthermore, GSEA of all DEGs confirmed enrichment of pathways involving these 16 genes, including adaptive immune system terms, with directionality consistent with the primary pre/post-treatment comparison (Supplementary Fig. 5G).

We also performed the trajectory analysis under the China/Asia-based control framework to evaluate the robustness of the inferred developmental changes observed in the primary analysis. The general lineage structure and the direction of L-serine-associated transcriptional alterations over pseudotime were mostly consistent, despite minor topological deviations compared with the original analysis (Supplementary Fig. 6A). Integration with EV proteomics continued to reveal overlaps between pseudotime-associated genes and EV signals, with 24 and 10 overlapping genes in Trajectory A and 18 and 8 for Trajectory B for the before-L-serine- and the after-L-serine-treatment groups, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 6B). Gene expression dynamics along pseudotime revealed distinct phases that were consistent with the primary findings (Supplementary Fig. 6C). In particular, several previously highlighted genes, including ANXA1 and RAN, showed directionally consistent L-serine–associated trends along the trajectories in this sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Fig. 6D).

Finally, we repeated the cell–cell interaction and co-expression network analyses under the China/Asia-based control framework. Global interaction heatmaps indicated that CD4 T-cell–centered predicted communications were more extensive prior to treatment and became less intricate following L-serine administration (Supplementary Fig. 7A). Consistent with the primary analysis, several CD4 T cell-associated interactions remained detectable after treatment, including CD44–TYROBP (Supplementary Fig. 7B). Within the CD8 T-cell, NK-cell, and monocyte compartments, the differential-expression test indicated that a subset of genes encoding representative LR components and related immune signaling molecules exhibited changed expression between the before- and the after-L-serine-treatment groups (Supplementary Fig. 7C). We next performed hdWGCNA separately within CD8 T cells, NK cells, and monocytes (Supplementary Fig. 7D, E, F). Modules exhibiting condition-associated shifts in module eigengene expression captured co-regulated genes, whose expression changes paralleled those observed for selected LR components. Several proteins identified in the EV proteomic analysis overlapped with these modules. Functional enrichment analysis of the impacted modules underscored immune signaling pathways and related cellular processes. Taken together, the sensitivity analysis using Asian pediatric PBMC datasets as alternative healthy controls showed that the significant immunological and transcriptomic alterations of CD4 T cells following oral L-serine administration were reliably replicated. While expected quantitative differences due to dataset composition and integration were present, these results were consistent with the main findings.

Discussion

L-serine represents a multifaceted amino acid with therapeutic potential for immune-related neurological disorders. High-dose oral L-serine administration has been reported to improve neurodevelopment in inborn errors of serine biosynthesis, such as phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH)-related microcephalic dwarfism63. In reports of GRIN loss-of-function cases, L-serine administration has been associated with amelioration across outcome domains spanning neurobehavioral assessment, EEG-based measures, and seizure endpoints20,64. In a clinical study, L-serine was associated with a slower rate of clinical worsening in hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathy type 165. Impaired CD4 T cell development and reduced progenitor pools may compromise immune responsiveness in the nervous system. L-serine–mediated modulation of the neuroimmune axis could attenuate clinical symptoms in ASD by improving peripheral T-cell development. This interpretation is consistent with emerging evidence suggesting a developmental contribution of immune cells to CNS homeostasis. Brain-resident CD4 T cells, entering the CNS during birth, affect CNS development, synaptic pruning, anxiety behaviors, and the transcriptional maturation in microglia and innate immune compartments66. Immature CD4 T cell–microglia crosstalk could plausibly contribute to neurodevelopmental impairment and the emergence of atypical behavioral phenotypes. Such effects may result in impaired synaptic pruning and phagocytic clearance of excess synapses by immature microglia during postnatal brain development. Whether amino acid stimulation of the developing immune cells could modulate the CNS innate immune system via immune-microglia interactions should be further studied.

L-serine is required for the de novo purine biosynthesis that fuels T-cell proliferation and differentiation. Glutathione and serine metabolism mediate T-cell function and are related to inflammation via mTOR activation67,68. Effector T cells rely on extracellular serine as a key immune-metabolite for de novo nucleotide biosynthesis and cellular expansion69. Several amino acids not only serve as intermediates for the tricarboxylic acid cycle in mitochondria but also function as instructive signals that shape immune cell function70. During naive T-cell activation, exit from quiescence is supported by the uptake of specific extracellular amino acids. L-alanine is primarily used as a substrate for nascent protein production, whereas L-arginine availability shapes cellular fitness and viability71,72. In Alzheimer’s disease, PHGDH upregulation, an upstream control point in the serine synthesis pathway, supports the idea that disrupted serine metabolism is coupled to persistent neuroinflammatory signaling73. Future systemic and temporal analyses of the immune-metabolic dynamics of ASD may provide insights into metabolic-immune crosstalk.

This work is exploratory and includes design constraints that warrant a cautious reading of the findings. Because this was a pre-post study without randomization or placebo control, the observed molecular shifts cannot be attributed unambiguously to L-serine. For the single-cell RNA sequencing analyses, healthy controls were derived from publicly available external datasets rather than controls processed in parallel with our patient samples, which may introduce batch effects. To mitigate these concerns, we prioritized age-appropriate pediatric controls, used the adult dataset only for cell-type annotation, and performed sensitivity analyses using Asia-based external cohorts, which supported the directionality of key CD4 T cell–related findings. Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths. By integrating plasma EV proteomics with single-cell PBMC transcriptomics, we obtained convergent, orthogonal evidence for L-serine associated remodeling of immune developmental programs and intercellular communication. Finally, the molecular signatures identified here provide a mechanistic framework that is consistent with previously reported clinical observations at comparable dosing in a prior study22.

Conclusion

Cytokine profiling and genome-wide expression studies have indicated a tight association between the immune system and ASD74–76. We tested whether L-serine could rewire adaptive immune cells by normalizing the dysregulated immune system in ASD. Our integrative multi-omics approach demonstrated that L-serine administration in children with ASD led to the normalization of an atypically expanded population of IL7Rlow naive CD4 T cells. Consistent with this, T-cell–associated proteins showed corresponding shifts in the plasma EV proteome. Specifically, both the proportion of the aberrant IL7Rlow naive CD4 T cells and the associated core 20 gene signatures, including S100A4, FOS, ORC4, BCL11B, and PAG1, were restored to levels observed in healthy controls. Additionally, pseudotime analysis verified that these changes influenced CD4 T cell maturation (Trajectory A) and proliferation (Trajectory B) without elevating inflammatory responses. Furthermore, oral L-serine treatment reconstructed CD4-centered LR networks and EV-linked co-expression modules, including CD8A/CD8B/PAG1 modules, especially in cytotoxic lymphocytes. These molecular findings were well-aligned with the behavioral and clinical scores (CGI-S, CGI-I) as secondary endpoints. Together, these results support the possibility that peripheral immune-metabolic alterations are linked to neurodevelopmental pathways implicated in ASD, consistent with emerging evidence that immune signaling can influence brain development.
