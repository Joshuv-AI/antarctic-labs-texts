The Effect of Progressive Relaxation Exercises Applied to Individuals With Type 2 Diabetes on FATIQUE Level and Sleep Quality: A Randomised Controlled Study
Authors: Bahar Vardar inkaya, Ayşegül Koç, Birgül Genç, Ceren Mumcu, Canan Güngör, Reyhan Ersoy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundProgressive relaxation exercises involve systematically tensing and relaxing all muscle groups in the body to achieve relaxation. Progressive relaxation exercises may be beneficial for individuals with various chronic conditions, including diabetes, by effectively reducing fatigue levels.MethodThis study was conducted to examine the effects of progressive relaxation exercises on fatigue levels and sleep problems in individuals with type 2 diabetes. It is an experimental study conducted in a randomized controlled pretest/posttest design. The method of the study included the creation of an experimental group (n = 33) and a control group (n = 30) using simple randomization. Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, Fatigue Severity Scale, and progressive relaxation exercise videos were used for data collection. The study was a total of 63 individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes who applied to outpatient treatment and met the research criteria. In the evaluation of the data, the ‘Independent Sample‐t’ test (with Z‐table value) was used for comparing measurement values between two independent groups, and the “ANOVA” test (with F‐table value) was used for comparing three or more independent groups. The ‘Mann–Whitney U’ test (with Z‐table value) was used for comparing measurement values between two independent groups, and the ‘Kruskal‐Wallis H’ test (with χ2‐table value) was used for comparing three or more independent groups. To examine the relationship between two categorical variables, ‘Pearson‐χ2’ cross‐tables and Fisher‐Exact test. After eliminating the effect of the pretest on the posttest during group comparison, ANCOVA analysis was applied to examine the differences between the groups.ResultsAccording to the research findings, a significant difference was observed in fasting and postprandial blood glucose levels between the pretest and posttest in the experimental group (p < 0.05). A decrease was observed in the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index posttest scores in the experimental group, but it was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). On the other hand, a statistically significant difference was found in the fatigue severity scale pretest‐posttest scores of diabetic individuals in the experimental group (Z = −4.718; p = 0.000).ConclusionIt can be concluded that progressive relaxation exercises can be utilized in the elimination of fatigue and sleep problems in diabetic individuals.Trail RegistrationNCT05893784Keywords: diabetes mellitus, fatigue, progressive muscle relaxation, sleep quality

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a metabolic disease characterized primarily by hyperglycemia, resulting from a deficiency or defect in insulin secretion, insulin activity, or both, which leads to impaired utilization of glucose, fats, and proteins in the body [1]. Diabetes prevalence is increasing day by day. According to the IDF 2021 data, 537 million adults (aged 20–79) are living with diabetes. This number is estimated to reach 643 million by 2030 and 783 million by 2045. Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) constitutes approximately 90% of all diabetes cases worldwide [2].

Type 2 diabetes, being a chronic disease, can lead to various symptoms, especially fatigue [3]. It is a condition that contributes to low levels of physical activity and affects diabetes self‐management. Fatigue in diabetes is a distressing problem that can result from physiological abnormalities like hypoglycemia, hyperglycemia, or fluctuations between the two. The occurrence of fatigue in people with T2DM is related to insulin deficiency. When glycogen stores are depleted, the rate of adenosine diphosphate phosphorylation decreases, thereby slowing the rate of synthesis of adenosine triphosphate, which serves as a short‐term energy [4]. The association between fatigue and factors such as inflammation, body mass index, insulin therapy, and depression is frequently observed in individuals with T2DM and may intensify fatigue symptoms. In addition, side effects of medications, anxiety about disease management, and various short‐ and long‐term complications can cause fatigue; diabetes, sleep problems, and fatigue are interrelated symptoms. Sleep disturbances, which are prevalent among individuals with type 2 diabetes, can further aggravate fatigue and disrupt overall metabolic control. Diabetic individuals who cannot get enough restful sleep often wake up feeling tired, creating a vicious cycle [5].

In addition to conventional medicine, complementary and alternative therapies are used to prevent these symptoms. Among the widely used complementary therapies are aromatherapy, relaxation exercises, music therapy, reflexology, yoga, and therapeutic touch. One of these techniques is progressive relaxation exercises (PMR), which was defined by American physician Edmund Jacobson. PMR exercises involve systematically tensing and relaxing all muscle groups in the body to achieve relaxation. It leads to a decrease in sympathetic nervous system activity and an increase in parasympathetic nervous system activity, resulting in positive effects on blood pressure, heart rate, and pulse. During the exercise, it is essential to explain to the individual the importance of using the entire lung capacity and inhaling the necessary oxygen [6, 7]. Proper and deep breathing will strengthen the respiratory muscles, facilitate circulation, and promote a calm mind [8].

Although there are studies in the literature [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14], in a study on the effect of PMR exercises on fatigue symptoms in diabetes, it was determined that PMR exercises provided a decrease in fatigue levels [15]. Based on this information, this research was conducted to examine the effects of PMR exercises on fatigue levels and sleep problems in individuals with type 2 diabetes. It is expected that the results of this study can contribute to independent nursing interventions for diabetic individuals and provide a different perspective on fatigue and sleep problems in diabetic patients.

Methods

Aim

This research examines the effects of progressive relaxation exercises on the fatigue level and sleep problems of individuals with type 2 diabetes.

Research Hypotheses

The progressive relaxation exercises applied to individuals with type 2 diabetes improve sleep quality.

The progressive relaxation exercises applied to individuals with type 2 diabetes reduce the level of fatigue.

Research Type

It is an experimental study conducted in a randomized controlled pretest/posttest design.

Participants

The research was conducted with a total of 63 individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes who applied to the Endocrine Clinic of Ankara City Hospital for outpatient treatment between April 20th and July 20th, 2022, and met the research criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: being 18 years of age or older, using insulin, not having any medical conditions that could hinder exercise, being able to communicate effectively, and voluntarily agreeing to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria were as follow: those with visual, speech and hearing related diseases that may prevent understanding the information given and the application of PMR. Those with different chronic diseases that may affect sleep quality (such as cancer, COPD, asthma), neuro‐psychiatric disorders (such as dementia, psychotic disorder, bipolar disorder). Individuals whose condition is unstable and who develop a serious complication (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy, etc.)Those with disabilities that cannot perform Progressive Relaxation Exercise. Exclusion Criteria from the study were as follow: Those who experience any health problems during the application process, those who cannot continue the study for any reason and wish to leave the study with their own consent. Those who do not exercise more than twice a week.

To determine the differences between the groups, an ANOVA analysis will be conducted using the G*Power program (version 3.1.9.2). The participants were randomly assigned to either the experimental group (n = 33) or the control group (n = 30) using a simple randomization method. Individuals with diabetes who did not perform progressive relaxation exercises regularly were excluded from the study (n = 3). As a result of the post hoc power analysis performed using the G*Power 3.0.10 program; the power of the study was found to be sufficient as 83.1% with a 5% margin of error and the determined effect size in a 2‐group, 2‐repetition study design.

Data Collection Tools

Tools

Patient Identification Form

The researcher created a questionnaire with a total of 10 questions based on a literature review [8, 16]. The questionnaire includes socio‐demographic characteristics such as the coding purpose of the first letter of the first name and surname, age, marital status, employment status, educational status, smoking habits, alcohol consumption, and questions related to the study, including previous participation in an exercise program, current exercise habits, and questions evaluating sleep patterns.

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was adapted into Turkish by Agargün and colleagues in 1996 [17]. The scale consists of 24 questions that assess sleep quality and disturbances over the past month. Among these questions, 19 are self‐report items and are answered by the patient. The last self‐report question (Question 19) pertains to the presence or absence of a roommate or partner and is not used for scoring. Five questions are answered by the partner or roommate and are not included in the scoring; they are used for clinical information only. The 18 scored questions of the scale constitute seven components: Subjective Sleep Quality, Sleep Latency, Sleep Duration, Habitual Sleep Efficiency, Sleep Disturbances, Use of Sleep Medication, and Daytime Dysfunction. Each component is evaluated on a scale of 0 to 3 points. The total score of the seven components gives the scale total score, ranging from 0 to 21. A total score greater than 5 indicates ‘poor sleep quality’. The Cronbach's Alpha internal consistency coefficient of the scale is found to be 0.80 [13]. In this study, Cronbach's Alpha was found to be 0.85.

The Fatigue Severity Scale

This scale was adapted into Turkish by Armutlu and colleagues in 2007 [18]. The Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) is considered one of the best examples among single‐dimensional scales. The individual indicates their level of agreement with each item by selecting a number from 1 to 7. A score of 1 indicates ‘strongly disagree’, and a score of 7 indicates ‘strongly agree’. The scale consists of a total of 9 questions, and the score range is 9–63. A score of 36 or higher indicates severe fatigue. The total score is calculated by taking the average of the 9 items. The lower the total score, the lower the level of fatigue. The Cronbach's Alpha reliability coefficient of the FSS is 0.96 [14]. In this study, Cronbach's Alpha was found to be 0.92.

Data Collection and Analysis

Experimental group

In the experimental group, before the PMR exercises training, the researcher conducted face‐to‐face interviews with diabetic individuals in the endocrine outpatient clinic using the diabetic patient introduction form PSQI and FSS. Each interview lasted approximately 30 min. Written consent was obtained from the participants before the research.

After explaining the benefits, application steps, frequency, and duration of PMR exercises, the researcher provided practical exercise training to the participants. Following the training, an 8‐min video demonstrating the PMR exercises was sent to the participants' mobile phones. They were asked to practice these exercises regularly every night, about an hour before bedtime, for a duration of 4 weeks. Patients were asked to send messages regarding whether they do/do not exercise. The video included soothing meditative background music to relax the patients and covered breathing exercises and progressive muscle stretching exercises. Weekly reminder messages were sent to the diabetic individuals to encourage them to perform the exercises shown in the video.

After 4 weeks, when the participants in the experimental group came for their follow‐up appointment at the endocrine clinic, the PSQI and FSS forms were re‐administered by the researcher as post‐tests. Routine tests were used for HbA1c measurements. Patients were informed and their HbA1c level was requested by the physician in the research team. Since the HbA1c value is affected by many factors (such as nutrition, exercise, insulin treatment) and the main purpose of the study was not the change in this value, it was checked after 1 month. The fact that this value was not checked 3 months later for the change in HbA1c was added as a weakness of the study.

Control group

In the control group, the researcher conducted face‐to‐face interviews with diabetic individuals in the endocrine outpatient clinic using the diabetic patient introduction form PSQI and FSS. Each interview lasted approximately 30 min. No specific intervention was applied to this group. At the end of the fourth week, the PSQI and FSS were administered again as post‐tests. Routine tests were used for HbA1c measurements. Patients were informed and their HbA1c level was requested by the physician in the research team.

After completing the research, the patients and/or their relatives who volunteered in this group received voice recordings containing PMR exercises instructions on their phones, along with information on how to perform the exercises.

Practicing Progressive Relaxation Exercises

After providing information about the exercises, the patient's room was ensured to be quiet and well‐ventilated to enhance concentration. Depending on the patient's condition, the bed was adjusted to provide either a sitting, semi‐fowler, or supine position. Following the instructions in the video recording, the patient was shown practically how to stretch and relax specific muscles in the body, and the steps to exit the relaxation session were demonstrated.

Subsequently, the video recording, lasting for 25–30 min, was shown to the patient, encouraging them to perform the exercises. The basic steps of progressive relaxation exercises were explained as follows:

Each muscle group from head to toe is stretched, counted up to 5, and then relaxed.

Each muscle group from head to toe is stretched, counted up to 5, and then relaxed.

If necessary, the stretching and releasing phase is repeated, focusing on the tense muscle groups.

If necessary, the stretching and releasing phase is repeated, focusing on the tense muscle groups.

After relaxing each muscle group, take a deep breath and slowly release it.

After relaxing each muscle group, take a deep breath and slowly release it.

The research was conducted with diabetic individuals receiving services from the Endocrinology and Metabolism Clinic at Ankara City Hospital within the boundaries of Ankara province, between April 20, 2022, and July 20, 2022.

Ankara City Hospital's Endocrinology and Metabolism Clinic consists of four internal medicine outpatient clinics. The hospital has one diabetes nurse who works from Monday to Friday between 08:00 and 17:00. Every patient newly diagnosed with diabetes goes to the diabetes nurse for diabetes education after their doctor's examination.

PMR is a skill that nurses can teach and is learned in inpatient and outpatient settings that include the roles of nurses as educators, caregivers, and patient advocates, and is included in the Nursing Interventions Classification (NIC). PMR has both physiological and psychological benefits. PMR reduces the activity of the sympathetic nervous system and regulates the functioning of the peripheral and central nervous systems. It is known that progressive relaxation exercises, when done regularly, have many positive effects such as regulating shortness of breath, pain, and sleep [19, 20, 21], reducing restless leg syndrome symptoms, reducing fatigue symptoms, improving quality of life, and regulating stress levels and blood pressure [22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. In addition to these benefits, it is important to be careful about PMR as it can also cause unwanted side effects such as hypoglycemia and dawn phenomenon.

Randomization

The participants for the study were assigned to two groups to prevent bias in the allocation process. This was achieved by generating random numbers using the website https://randomizer.org/. The incoming individuals were then assigned to the relevant groups based on these random numbers.

Blinding was implemented during data collection, statistics, and reporting of the study. Data collection and data entry (transferring pre‐test and post‐test data to the computer) were carried out by an expert nurse who was independent of the researcher. Data entries were labelled with codes ‘A’ and ‘B’ without specifying the experimental and control groups. This ensured that the data analysis and interpretation were conducted without any knowledge of the group assignments.

Analysis of Data

The study data was transferred to IBM SPSS Statistics 26 program for analysis. During data evaluation, frequency distributions were used for categorical variables, and descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation) were provided for numerical variables. The reliability of the scales used in the research was examined using Cronbach's Alpha internal consistency coefficient. To decide on the appropriate analyses, the Shapiro–Wilk Test (for sample size > 30) was applied to test the assumption of normal distribution for all scores. For data with a normal distribution, the ‘Independent Sample‐t’ test (with Z‐table value) was used for comparing measurement values between two independent groups, and the ‘ANOVA’ test (with F‐table value) was used for comparing three or more independent groups. For data without a normal distribution, the ‘Mann–Whitney U’ test (with Z‐table value) was used for comparing measurement values between two independent groups, and the ‘Kruskal‐Wallis H’ test (with χ2‐table value) was used for comparing three or more independent groups. The Chi‐Square Test was used to examine whether there was a relationship between two independent categorical variables. In cases where the Chi‐Square Test assumption was not met, Fisher's Exact Test was used in 2 × 2 tables, and Freeman Halton Fisher's Exact Test was used in nxm tables.

After eliminating the effect of the pretest on the posttest during group comparison, ANCOVA analysis was applied to examine the differences between the groups. Analysis of covariance is used to examine differences between groups by neutralizing the effect of a third variable, other than the dependent and independent variables, on the dependent variable. This third variable is called the ‘covariate’ in the ANCOVA model. After eliminating the effect of the pretest on the posttest during group comparison, ANCOVA analysis was applied to examine differences between the groups.

Ethics

This study was approved by The City Hospital (ref. number E2‐21–1108). All participants were asked to provide a written informed consent. The participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without giving any reason; their identities would be kept confidential, and they would not pay any fees for this study.

Discussion

In this study, the effects of progressive relaxation exercises applied to individuals with type 2 diabetes on fatigue levels and sleep quality were investigated. The research included 63 participants, with 33 in the experimental group and 30 in the control group. The results of the study confirmed the H2 hypothesis, indicating that progressive relaxation exercises have a positive effect on fatigue levels in individuals with type 2 diabetes. However, the H1 hypothesis, which suggested a positive effect of progressive relaxation exercises on sleep quality in individuals with type 2 diabetes, was rejected.

When comparing the experimental and control groups in terms of age, gender, marital status, educational level, alcohol use, exercise habits, sleep patterns, fasting blood glucose, postprandial blood glucose and HbA1c levels, no statistically significant differences were found between the groups (Table 1). Based on this result, it can be stated that the experimental and control groups were similar in terms of their baseline characteristics, indicating comparability between the groups.

PMR is a method that stimulates muscle‐nerve relaxation. It first causes all muscle groups in the body (hand, arm, nose, jaw, neck, chest, shoulder, waist, abdomen, stomach, hip, leg, foot) to contract and then relax. Relaxation occurs when the first muscle group relaxes, then the other muscle group contracts and relaxes. These processes are continued until all body muscles relax [27].

Comparison of Blood Glucose Levels According to Groups

According to the research results, there was no significant difference between the pre‐test and post‐test fasting and postprandial blood glucose levels in both the experimental and control groups (p > 0.05). However, in the experimental group, a significant difference was found between the pre‐test and post‐test fasting and postprandial blood glucose levels (p < 0.05).

When examining HbA1c levels, a decrease was observed in both groups in the post‐test. It is believed that the decrease in the experimental group is due to the progressive relaxation exercises, while the decrease in HbA1c levels in the control group may be attributed to the participants being conscious of their health and paying attention to blood glucose control.

Similar results have been reported in the literature, where progressive relaxation exercises have been found to lead to a decrease in blood glucose levels [28, 29, 30, 31]. The study's findings are consistent with the existing literature and are promising for individuals with diabetes.

Comparison of PSQI Scores According to Groups

When comparing PSQI scores according to groups, it was determined that both the experimental and control groups showed a significant decrease in post‐test scores compared to their pre‐test scores, indicating an improvement in sleep quality. Looking at the pre‐test scores, it is observed that the PSQI scores of the experimental group were higher than those of the control group.

In the post‐test, both the experimental and control groups showed improvements in sleep scores. However, according to the scale evaluation, the improvement in sleep quality did not reach the desired level. This result is thought to be related to the irregular practice of PMR exercises.

In DM patients, high blood glucose levels and high Glycosylated Haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels also adversely affect sleep duration and increased glycemic levels cause deterioration of sleep quality [31, 32]. In this context, the reason for poor sleep quality in patients with chronic diseases is thought to be the development of a specific disease symptom during sleep: peripheral neuropathy, nocturia due to poor glycemic control, or night waking due to pain due to widespread complications [33, 34].

In a study by Burke et al. [35], it was found that progressive relaxation exercises improved sleep quality in elderly individuals [35]. Many studies in the literature have also shown that progressive relaxation exercises improve sleep quality and lead to a significant improvement in sleep duration in various chronic diseases [9, 10, 12, 14].

Similarly, it has been determined that progressive relaxation exercises improve sleep quality in individuals with COVID‐19 [8], fractures, and COPD [6, 36]. Although not statistically significant in our study, an improvement in sleep scores was observed in the experimental group, which is consistent with the literature.

Therefore, considering all these reasons, it can be concluded that progressive relaxation exercises improve sleep quality, making it an important intervention for chronic conditions such as diabetes. The decrease in PSQI scores in the control group, despite no intervention, may be attributed to the lower pre‐test scores and fewer individuals rating their sleep quality as poor.

Since the PSQI scores decreased in both the experimental and control groups, further analysis showed that the risk of poor sleep quality in the experimental group was 4.197 times higher than that in the control group, supporting our results (OR = 4.197). Additionally, the Backward LR test results for the posttest indicated that progressive relaxation exercises had an effect on the fatigue level variable. This result supports our research findings.

Comparison of Fatigue Severity Scale Scores According to Groups

According to the research results, it was found that the post‐test Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) scores were significantly lower than the pre‐test scores in both groups. Similarly, in a study conducted by Izgu et al. (2020) with individuals with type 2 diabetes, progressive relaxation exercises were found to reduce fatigue levels, while mindfulness meditation was not effective. Different studies with patients who have chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, undergo dialysis, or have undergone liver transplantation, as well as diabetic patients, have also shown that progressive relaxation exercises reduce fatigue levels. Our study results are consistent with the existing literature [10, 16, 37, 38, 39].

The reason behind the control group's post‐test results decreasing in a manner similar to the experimental group can be attributed to the lower pre‐test scores in the control group and the fact that fatigue perception has multiple different indicators and factors.

Overall, the study's findings align with previous research, suggesting that progressive relaxation exercises can effectively reduce fatigue levels, making them beneficial for individuals with various health conditions, including diabetes.

This concept is called the Howthorne effect in the literature, known as the observer effect, is an important source of error related to modifications in behaviour while observed [40]. At this point, it was concluded that although there was no interaction between the control and experimental groups, the decreases seen in the control group values may be due to the fact that individuals pay attention to disease management by feeling that they are being observed due to their participation in a study and the results should be evaluated in this respect.

Strengths and Limitations of the Research

This is a pioneering study evaluating the effect of progressive relaxation exercises on fatigue and sleep levels in individuals with diabetes. The fact that it is a randomized controlled study is an important contribution to the literature. The strengths of the study are that the exercises were explained face‐to‐face and the data were collected face‐to‐face.

The weakness of the study is that the change in HbA1c level after 3 months was not analysed.

Conclusion

As a result of this study, the hypothesis ‘Progressive relaxation exercises applied to individuals with type 2 diabetes improve sleep quality’ was rejected, while the hypothesis ‘Progressive relaxation exercises applied to individuals with type 2 diabetes reduce fatigue levels’ was accepted. At this point, the incorporation of progressive relaxation exercises into the diabetic patients' care process is a new element in the holistic care. Therefore, we think that it is important for nursing care to implement these practices by including the family with social, psychological, and spiritual support.

Based on these findings, the following recommendations can be made:

Implementation of progressive relaxation exercises in clinics to improve sleep quality and reduce fatigue levels in individuals with T2DM.

Implementation of progressive relaxation exercises in clinics to improve sleep quality and reduce fatigue levels in individuals with T2DM.

Providing education and resources such as videos or CDs about progressive relaxation exercises to individuals with diabetes before discharge from the hospital.

Further investigation of the effectiveness of progressive relaxation exercises in managing different symptoms associated with diabetes.

By implementing these recommendations, healthcare providers can potentially improve the overall well‐being and management of type 2 diabetes in patients, addressing both sleep quality and fatigue levels through progressive relaxation exercises.
