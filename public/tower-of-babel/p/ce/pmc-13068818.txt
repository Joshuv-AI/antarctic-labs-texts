OGG1 increases exercise endurance via elevated skeletal muscle FGF21
Authors: Bhavya Blaze, Priyanka Sharma, Bhavya Prakash Gupta, Souvik Mandal, Sai Santosh Babu Komakula, Tracy Anthony, Emmanuel Marfo, Harini Sampath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe base excision repair pathway maintains genomic integrity in the face of oxidative insult. It is initiated by DNA glycosylases such as 8-oxoguanine DNA glycosylase (OGG1) and is implicated in various pathologies, such as cancers and neurodegenerative diseases. Base excision repair proteins also modulate body weight and metabolic health. Mice lacking OGG1 are susceptible to obesity and its sequelae, whereas overexpression of human OGG1 (in OGG1-transgenic mice; Ogg1Tg mice) reverses these metabolic defects. We report here that OGG1 overexpression induces a remarkable over threefold increase in muscle endurance. This is accompanied by significant increases in muscle mitochondrial content and size and a selective increase in expression of the myokine, fibroblast growth factor 21 (Fgf21), in skeletal muscle of Ogg1Tg mice. Together with elevated circulating FGF21 levels and peripheral markers of FGF21 action, these data demonstrate a novel role for skeletal muscle OGG1 in modulating mitochondrial health and muscle endurance via FGF21 secretion and signaling.Keywords: myokine, exercise tolerance, mitochondria, base excision repair, skeletal muscle, fibroblast growth factor

Preserving nuclear and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) integrity in the face of continuous damage is crucial for maintenance of genomic stability (1, 2). Oxidative damage is one of the most common forms of DNA damage, arising from both endogenous reactive oxygen species generated as a part of normal cellular metabolism and exogenous insults, including UV light, toxins, and pollutants (3, 4). Due to the low redox potential of guanine bases, they can undergo preferential oxidation, resulting in oxidized products including 7,8-dihydro-8-oxo-2′-guanine (8-oxoG) and 2,6-diamino-4-hydroxy-5-formamidopyrimidine. Oxidized guanines have been shown to be most abundant in guanine-rich regions of the genome that are frequently associated with promoters and transcription factor binding sites (4). This nonrandom distribution of oxidized guanines, coupled with their potential to give rise to transversion mutations, generates great potential for alteration of cellular function in the absence of efficient repair mechanisms (5, 6).

Repair of nonbulky oxidative lesions, such as oxidized guanines, is primarily accomplished via the evolutionarily conserved base excision repair (BER) pathway. BER is initiated by DNA glycosylases that have varying and sometimes overlapping substrate specificities (4, 5). The BER glycosylase, 8-oxoguanine DNA glycosylase (OGG1), recognizes oxidized guanine products and cleaves them from the DNA backbone, generating a single-stranded break that is subject to downstream repair in the nucleus and mitochondria (4, 7, 8, 9). Optimal OGG1 activity prevents the accumulation of mutations and protects animals from tumorigenesis, progressive diseases such as Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s disease, and metabolic syndrome (10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15). Polymorphisms in the OGG1 gene have also been shown to increase (16) susceptibility to cancer in multiple organs and to contribute to insulin resistance (10, 14, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25).

We have previously shown that mice with a whole-body deletion of OGG1 (Ogg1−/−) have marked impairments in skeletal muscle function and health. Skeletal muscle plays a significant role in determining body composition and metabolic health. Impairments in skeletal muscle metabolic function and strength are associated with chronic conditions, such as diabetes and obesity, as well as with the aging process. Consistent with a role for OGG1 in regulating muscle function, Ogg1−/− mice display increased lipid accumulation in skeletal muscle and significant reductions in both exercise endurance and grip strength, indicating loss of muscle health upon deletion of OGG1 (26). Ogg1−/− mice are also prone to diet- and age-induced obesity and insulin resistance (11, 12). Interestingly, overexpression of a mitochondrially targeted human OGG1 (in OGG1-transgenic [Ogg1Tg] mice) protects mice from age-, diet-, and genetically induced obesity through significant increases in energy expenditure (11, 14). Previous studies have reported that overexpression of a similar mitochondrially targeted OGG1 construct in muscle cells in vitro protects them from palmitate-induced damage (27). However, the in vivo role of mitochondrially targeted OGG1 in skeletal muscle and its role in maintaining muscle health outside the context of lipid overload have not been previously investigated.

Skeletal muscle is also recognized as a source of circulating factors or myokines that mediate both its own function and communication with distal organs, such as the liver. One such myokine, fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), is expressed in and secreted by muscle and other tissues in response to various physiological and pathological conditions that signal an energy deficit, including exercise, starvation, and muscle mitochondrial dysfunction (28, 29, 30, 31, 32). FGF21 has been shown to be transcriptionally regulated by numerous factors, including PI3K–Akt signaling, the unfolded protein response, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress (29). Muscle-directed overexpression of FGF21 has been recently shown to improve metabolic health and prevent hepatic steatosis (33, 34); however, an association between muscle FGF21 and exercise capacity has not been explored. In this study, we investigated muscle health and function in Ogg1Tg mice and found a remarkable increase in both running capacity and mitochondrial content in the muscle of these animals. Intriguingly, these enhancements in muscle function were accompanied by large increases in muscle-derived FGF21, without underlying deficits in mitochondrial content or ER stress, uncovering a potentially novel mode of regulation of both FGF21 expression itself as well as its impact on muscle endurance.

Results

OGG1 overexpression increases exercise endurance in young and aged mice

The generation of Ogg1Tg mice has been previously described (30, 35). Briefly, Ogg1Tg mice constitutively overexpress human OGG1- type 1a in the mitochondria via the use of a mitochondrial targeting sequence derived from the MnSOD gene. This results in constitutive overexpression of hOgg1 in all tissues (Fig. 1A). Our laboratory has backcrossed these mice into a C57BL6J background for over 12 generations and reported on the metabolic phenotypes and adipogenic changes in this model (11, 14, 24). However, in vivo changes in skeletal muscle of these mice have been relatively undercharacterized. Here, we report that OGG1 activity was significantly induced in tissue whole cell homogenates from skeletal muscle of Ogg1Tg mice relative to WT controls (Fig. 1, B and C). This was also reflected in a large increase in mitochondrial OGG1 activity (Fig. 1, D–F) in Ogg1Tg mice. There were no differences in body weights between genotypes, although Ogg1Tg mice tended to weigh less (p = 0.07) (Fig. 1G). The nonsignificant reductions in body weights were apparent in significant reductions in adipose mass, without alterations in lean mass (Fig. 1G), consistent with our prior reports (11, 14). Muscle masses of quadriceps, soleus, and gastrocnemius were also not significantly different between genotypes (Fig. 1H). We previously reported that constitutive deletion of OGG1 resulted in reduced muscle function in Ogg1−/− mice (26). To investigate whether OGG1 overexpression would impact muscle endurance, 15-week-old WT and Ogg1Tg mice were subjected to an acute treadmill test (Fig. 1I), and time to exhaustion was recorded (Fig. 1J). Interestingly, Ogg1Tg mice ran over two times longer than control mice (Fig. 1J). The same mice were subjected to repeated runs with a day of rest between each bout of exercise. This repeated running protocol resulted in a 38% increase in exercise endurance in WT mice following the fifth bout of exercise, which was not statistically significant. However, in the same period, Ogg1Tg mice displayed a 128% increase in running times. At the fifth run, Ogg1Tg mice ran for an average of 144 min compared with control mice that ran for 42 min (Fig. 1J), representing a 3.4-fold increase in exercise endurance because of OGG1 overexpression. These data demonstrate a novel role for OGG1 in both exercise endurance and possibly in adaptation to repeated exercise. These studies were carried out in young mice, but both DNA repair capacity and exercise endurance are known to be impacted by aging (36, 37, 38, 39). We therefore investigated whether OGG1 overexpression would confer similar endurance benefits in older animals. 40-week-old mice were run on a treadmill (Fig. 1I), and the time to exhaustion was recorded. In this older cohort, Ogg1Tg mice ran twice as long as control animals (Fig. 1K) on the first run, indicating that the endurance benefits conferred by OGG1 overexpression are apparent even in older animals. Interestingly, older WT mice did not show any improvements in running time upon repeated bouts of running, unlike younger mice (Fig. 1, J and K). Ogg1Tg mice showed a slight but significant increase in running time at the second running trial, without additional gains throughout the exercise regimen. These data indicate that OGG1 overexpression does confer advantages to muscle endurance, even in the context of aging, but that some age-related decline cannot be counteracted by OGG1 overexpression alone. To determine if exercise and OGG1 genotype impact levels of 8-oxoG, a key substrate of OGG1, we measured 8-oxoG levels in age-matched WT and Ogg1Tg mice, either before any exercise or after running to exhaustion. Exercise increased 8-oxoG levels in WT mice but not in Ogg1Tg animals (Fig. 1L).

Muscle glycogen and triglycerides are increased in Ogg1Tg mice

Given the important role of skeletal muscle in modulating exercise endurance, we measured skeletal muscle content of fuel sources capable of supporting the increased endurance of Ogg1Tg mice. During running to exhaustion, the majority of muscle ATP is generated via oxidative phosphorylation, and intramuscular glycogen is a predominant early fuel source. Ogg1Tg mice had 39% higher glycogen stores than WT animals, prior to the onset of exercise, corresponding with their ability to run for significantly longer durations than WT controls. Following running to exhaustion, glycogen content in the WT muscle was decreased by 35%, whereas the Ogg1Tg muscle was decreased by 76% (Fig. 2A), indicating that these animals deploy their glycogen stores more completely than control mice. We also subjected animals to just 20 min of running to normalize running time and measured muscle glycogen content following this protocol. Despite starting with higher glycogen stores, Ogg1Tg mice had an 86% reduction in glycogen, whereas a 49% reduction was observed in WT mice following 20 min of exercise, indicating that they are better able to liberate stored muscle glycogen to fuel their running capacity. Together, these data indicate that Ogg1Tg mice are able to better utilize stored muscle glycogen to support their running capacity. In addition to gastrocnemius, which is a mixed muscle type and expresses both white glycolytic and mitochondria-rich red oxidative fibers, we also measured glycogen stores in quadriceps, which primarily consists of white fibers. Glycogen content was largely similar between WT and Ogg1Tg mice in quadriceps (Fig. 2B). While Ogg1Tg mice retained about 8% to 16% higher quadricep glycogen content, these differences were not significant at any time point. About 20 min of exercise depleted quadricep glycogen by ∼50% in both genotypes, while running to exhaustion, which was a significantly longer duration in Ogg1Tg mice, resulted in an over 80% reduction in quadricep glycogen content in both genotypes. Hepatic glycogen stores were not altered by genotype before exercise and were similarly reduced in WT and Ogg1Tg mice following exercise (Fig. 2C).

In addition to glycogen, fatty acids derived from both intramuscular triglycerides (TG) as well as adipose tissue lipolysis can serve as important fuel sources during exercise. Before exercise, Ogg1Tg mice had slightly lower total intramuscular lipids relative to WT mice (Fig. 2, D and E), including 17% lower free fatty acids (FFAs) and 7% lower stored TG (Fig. 2, D and F); these changes were not statistically significant (Fig. 2, E and F). Total muscle lipids (Fig. 2, D and E) were not changed by exercise to exhaustion in WT mice, despite a nonsignificant increase in muscle TG. However, exercise induced a significant increase in muscle TG in Ogg1Tg mice, relative to sedentary controls (Fig. 2, D and F). This significant increase in muscle TG in Ogg1Tg mice suggests possibly greater lipid uptake and thus availability of lipids as a fuel source during exercise in Ogg1Tg mice.

Plasma TG was similar across both genotypes, both before and after exercise (Fig. 2G). Plasma FFAs were measured before and after exercise as a measure of lipid mobilization from adipose tissue (Fig. 2H). There were no significant differences in baseline plasma FFA levels between genotypes. After 20 min of exercise, WT mice had a nonsignificant rise in plasma FFA, which returned to baseline levels by the time of exhaustion. In contrast, Ogg1Tg mice had a slower increase in plasma FFA at 20 min, which was sustained until the time of exhaustion (Fig. 2H). Although these changes were not statistically significant, we noted that the pattern of change in plasma FFA was markedly different between genotypes. The slower early rise in plasma FFA in Ogg1Tg mice could be a result of better glycogen utilization in these animals at earlier time points. We interpret the more sustained increase in plasma FFA levels in Ogg1Tg animals to indicate better utilization of mobilized lipids, which is supportive of a higher reliance on fat oxidation for fuel during running.

To directly test potential alterations in substrate utilization during exercise, respiratory exchange ratios (RERs) were measured in WT and Ogg1Tg mice during exercise (Fig. 2I). RERs are calculated as VCO2/VO2 and serve as an indicator of substrate utilization, with RERs close to 1.0 denoting carbohydrate utilization primarily, and RERs closer to 0.7 indicating a reliance on fat oxidation. Both genotypes had an elevation in RERs during the initial 2 min of running, corresponding with glycogen usage for ATP synthesis. After the first 2 min, all mice had a sharp drop in RER, indicating a switch toward fat catabolism and oxidative metabolism for energy needs. Ogg1Tg mice had significantly lower RERs than WT controls both at baseline and during exercise, indicating a greater reliance on fat oxidation in these animals. These data are consistent with both the altered pattern of plasma FFA and higher exercise endurance in these mice. Taken together, we interpret these data to indicate more efficient fat uptake and oxidation in Ogg1Tg mice that fuels their markedly greater exercise endurance.

Mitochondrial content, ultrastructure, and function are altered in the muscle of Ogg1Tg mice

Given these observations of improved running capacity and improved fuel usage in Ogg1Tg mice, we investigated mitochondrial content in the muscle of Ogg1Tg mice. mtDNA content was significantly increased in the muscle of Ogg1Tg mice (Fig. 3A). Muscle content of mitochondrial proteins, including heat shock protein 60, voltage-dependent anion channel, and cytochrome oxidase IV, was also significantly increased in the muscle of Ogg1Tg mice (Fig. 3, B and C). Concomitantly, expression of the transcriptional coactivator, peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor gamma coactivator-1a (PGC-1α), a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, was significantly upregulated in the gastrocnemius muscle of Ogg1Tg mice (Fig. 3, B and C).

We further assessed mitochondrial count and ultrastructure by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). These analyses confirmed a significantly higher mitochondrial count in Ogg1Tg muscle in both the subsarcolemmal (SS) and intermyofibrillar (IF) regions (Fig. 3, D and E). The SS mitochondrial number was increased by threefold, and the IF mitochondria were elevated by almost twofold in Ogg1Tg muscle relative to WT mice. In addition to the total count, SS mitochondria were significantly longer and wider, and IF mitochondria were longer in Ogg1Tg muscle relative to WT controls (Fig. 3, D and F). Given these alterations in mitochondrial size, we measured expression of genes regulating mitochondrial dynamics and fiber type. However, no significant differences in these factors were noted in WT versus Ogg1Tg mice (Data S1).

Improved mitochondrial function and a higher mitochondrial content may be associated with reduced levels of plasma lactate because of increased capacity for oxidative metabolism. Conversely, reduced mitochondrial respiration is associated with elevated lactate levels (40). Blood lactate levels were measured before and after exhaustion running in WT and Ogg1Tg mice. Prior to exercise, blood lactate was 12% lower (p > 0.05) in Ogg1Tg mice (2.42 mM), relative to WT controls (2.76 mM) (Fig. 3H). Running to exhaustion raised blood lactate in WT mice by 1 mM to 3.76 mM (p < 0.05), but blood lactate remained unchanged at 2.76 mM in Ogg1Tg mice after exercise. The lower lactate after exercise in Ogg1Tg mice was observed despite this cohort running almost twice as long as WT mice (46.7 min versus 23.9 min). The lower blood lactate levels in Ogg1Tg mice are consistent with increased aerobic metabolism and improved mitochondrial function in these mice.

Elevated muscle-derived plasma FGF21 in Ogg1Tg mice

To gain mechanistic insights into changes in muscle function in an unbiased manner, we carried out RNA-Seq analyses of gastrocnemius from WT and Ogg1Tg mice, prior to exercise. Interestingly, after correction for false discoveries, Ogg1Tg mice muscle had over 438 genes that were significantly differentially regulated by at least twofold, including over 334 upregulated genes and over 104 downregulated genes (Fig. 4A). Among the most differentially regulated genes, expression of Fgf21, a known metabolic modulator, was increased by almost 1000-fold (log2 fold change [FC] = 10.91) in the muscle of Ogg1Tg mice (Fig. 4A). This large increase in gastrocnemius Fgf21 gene expression was confirmed by quantitative PCR (qPCR) across multiple skeletal muscles, including gastrocnemius, quadriceps, and soleus (Fig. 4B). Concomitantly, plasma FGF21 levels were elevated by over 11-fold in Ogg1Tg mice (Fig. 4C). Expression of Fgf21 in the liver, the predominant source of circulating FGF21, as well as cardiac and adipose expression, was unaltered in Ogg1Tg mice (Fig. 4B), indicating skeletal muscle to be the source of the significantly elevated circulating FGF21 in Ogg1Tg animals. FGF21 has been shown to impact metabolism through its activation of the cellular energy sensor, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) (41). Consistent with elevated FGF21, AMPK phosphorylation was elevated by almost twofold in the muscle of Ogg1Tg mice (Fig. 4, D and E), along with phosphorylation of its target, acetyl-CoA carboxylase, which was 1.4-fold higher in Ogg1Tg mice (Fig. 4, D and E). This elevation of AMPK activation and consequent inactivation of acetyl-CoA carboxylase via its phosphorylation (Fig. 4, D and E) is also consistent with elevated fatty acid oxidation as indicated by the reduced RERs in Ogg1Tg mice (Fig. 2G).

Previous models of muscle FGF21 overexpression have been associated with mitochondrial myopathies, ER stress, and induction of autophagy (42, 43, 44). However, TEM analyses did not reveal any abnormalities in mitochondrial ultrastructure in the muscle of Ogg1Tg mice (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, the improved running endurance (Fig. 1, B and D), elevated mitochondrial count and size (Fig. 3, D–G), and increased markers of mitochondrial biogenesis (Fig. 3C) are all supportive of enhanced, rather than diminished, mitochondrial function in these mice. We assessed ER stress markers in the muscle of Ogg1Tg mice and found no differences in multiple metrics, including expression of Hspa5, Atf4, Chop, Atf6, or spliced X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1) (Fig. 5, A and B). Thus, the elevation in muscle FGF21 in this model is not associated with mitochondrial dysfunction or induction of ER stress. Akt overexpression has been shown to induce muscle FGF21 (45). Since we previously reported enhanced insulin sensitivity in Ogg1Tg mice (14), we investigated whether this may be associated with alterations in Akt signaling in the muscle of these animals that may correlate with FGF21 expression. Indeed, Akt phosphorylation was increased by twofold in skeletal muscle of Ogg1Tg mice (Fig. 5, C and D), suggesting that this may be a possible mechanism contributing to elevated FGF21 in Ogg1Tg mice.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes analyses reveal transcriptional changes associated with muscle function and nutrient status in Ogg1Tg muscle

In addition to examining changes in FGF21, we carried out Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analyses of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified from the muscle of Ogg1Tg mice (Table S1). These analyses revealed diverse pathways to be significantly altered in Ogg1Tg mice. Of these, a pathway entitled “Pathways in cancer” had the most number of genes represented in the DEGs, including Fgf21 itself (Supplementary Table S1). Further network analysis of these genes revealed five distinct clusters that were differentially regulated in Ogg1Tg mice (Fig. 6). Intriguingly, the largest of these clusters was dominated by several genes regulating immune function, including numerous members of the Cd family as well as genes, such as integrin subunit alpha E (Itgae), Fc receptor-like A (Fcrla), protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type C (Ptprc), and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (Cxcl2), to highlight a few (46, 47, 48, 49). The majority of these genes are linked with immune cell activation and recruitment. It is notable that exercise is associated with immune cell infiltration into muscle as a mechanism to reduce inflammation and increase repair (50). Among these immune-associated genes, one that was particularly noteworthy was an observed increase in Cxcl2. CXCL2 has been shown to both coordinate immune responses as well as regulate tissue remodeling as a part of the tissue repair process (51), suggesting that its upregulation in Ogg1Tg muscle may be associated with a more robust tissue repair process in these mice. Another cluster that was noted in the “Pathways in Cancer” pathway included genes, such as pyrroline-5-carboxylate reductase 1 (Pycr1), asparagine synthetase (Asns), and tribbles pseudokinase 3 (Trib3), which are all activated in response to conditions of nutrient deprivation or protection from oxidative stress (52, 53, 54, 55), respectively. Notably, Trib3 has been shown to be upregulated by PGC-1α (56), thus, its upregulation is consistent with our observed increases in PGC-1α in Ogg1Tg muscle. A third cluster in this same pathway was also associated with genes, such as spermine oxidase (Smox) (57), which have been shown to be regulated under conditions of muscular stress, including nutrient deprivation. Interestingly, this was balanced by the appearance of a gene network that included upregulated genes, such as cysteine and glycine-rich protein 3 (Csrp3). In mouse models, deletion of Csrp3 is associated with loss of muscle mitochondrial function (58). Taken together, these network analyses revealed a complex array of changes related to muscle function and nutrient status in the context of OGG1 overexpression.

Discussion

We show here for the first time that transgenic overexpression of a BER protein is associated with significant increases in skeletal muscle endurance. Remarkably, mice overexpressing human OGG1 (Ogg1Tg) showed an >2-fold increase in running endurance. This exercise capacity was further increased upon repeated exposure to exercise, a trend that was not observed in WT mice. Both the tremendous increase in initial exercise endurance and the continued improvements in endurance upon sequential exercise bouts in Ogg1Tg mice were highly intriguing. Acute exercise is associated with increased mitochondrial respiration to meet energetic demands. It is thus plausible that this higher metabolic rate may be accompanied by greater generation of ROS capable of damaging DNA. Indeed, exercise is associated with greater import of OGG1 into the mitochondria, presumably to meet the increased need for repair (59). Also supporting a role for OGG1 in modulating muscle function, we previously reported that mice lacking OGG1, and thus the capacity to efficiently repair oxidized guanines, have impaired exercise tolerance and muscle strength (26). Conversely, the improved endurance in Ogg1Tg mice is supportive of enhanced DNA repair, aiding overall muscle endurance and recovery. These measures are supportive of improved muscle mitochondrial function in Ogg1Tg mice; however, future studies directly investigating mitochondrial respiration using muscle myotubes or isolated mitochondria are necessary and will further clarify the direct impact of OGG1 overexpression on mitochondrial function in skeletal muscle.

Exercise endurance is known to be reduced in aging skeletal muscle (60, 61, 62). The progressive loss of muscle mass and function, also termed sarcopenia, is one of the most debilitating conditions associated with aging (63). Concomitantly, aging is also associated with elevated oxidative stress and damage to both the nuclear and mitochondrial genome (64, 65, 66, 67), and skeletal muscle may be particularly sensitive to age-induced reductions in OGG1 activity (37, 68, 69).

We observed that while aging resulted in a reduction in running capacity in all animals, Ogg1Tg mice continued to maintain higher endurance relative to age-matched controls. While sarcopenia is associated with loss of mitochondrial mass and function, we found that OGG1 overexpression induced a remarkable increase in mitochondrial number and area, both in the SS and IF regions. These two populations of mitochondria differ not only in their localization but also in their response to contractile activity, exercise, and substrate utilization (70, 71). To our knowledge, no studies have examined potential differences in BER capacity between the two mitochondrial subpopulations, particularly in the context of aging or exercise. Our results in young as well as middle-aged mice are consistent with the idea that enhancement of OGG1 activity is associated with improved muscle function and may thus hold translational promise in the treatment of sarcopenia.

Levels of PGC-1α, a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and mitochondrial dynamics, were elevated in the muscle of Ogg1Tg mice (Fig. 3, B and C). Consistent with this increase, we observed elevations in mitochondrial content, evidenced by increased mtDNA content, increased mitochondrial protein content, and increased mitochondrial count by TEM (Fig. 3, A–E). PGC-1α is a known regulator of mitochondrial dynamics, including via its regulation of Tfam, Drp1, and Mfn2 (72, 73). Interestingly, we did not observe changes in the expression of these genes at the transcriptional level (Fig. S1B). However, functional markers of mitochondrial dynamics, including the observed increases in mitochondrial length and width, indicated potentially decreased mitochondrial fragmentation in the muscle of Ogg1Tg mice, consistent with a functional role for elevated PGC-1α. Similarly, genetic knockout of PGC-1α has been associated with a shift of oxidative type I and IIa toward type IIx and IIb muscle fibers (74). This was in turn associated with reduced exercise endurance in muscle PGC-1α knockout mice (74). Our observations of elevated PGC-1α prompted us to examine muscle fiber type in Ogg1Tg mice. However, we did not note any changes in fiber type distribution at the transcriptional level in these animals (Fig. S1A). We also did not observe any changes in the overall mass of predominantly oxidative (soleus), predominantly glycolytic (quadriceps), or mixed fiber type (gastrocnemius) muscles between genotypes (Fig. 1H). These data suggest that OGG1 overexpression does not result in overt changes in fiber type distribution, but additional studies examining mitochondrial dynamics and muscle fiber switching in greater detail may be warranted.

Enabled by unbiased assessment of the muscle transcriptome in Ogg1Tg mice, we discovered a marked elevation of muscle Fgf21 expression and a commensurate increase in circulating FGF21, without alterations in hepatic expression. Originally identified as a member of the FGF family with hepatic enrichment (75), FGF21 has also been shown to be secreted from skeletal muscle under conditions generally associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and myopathies (43, 45, 76, 77, 78, 79, 80). In contrast to these previously reported models, however, the OGG1 overexpression model is associated with elevated FGF21 without any overt signs of respiratory chain dysfunction, as evidenced by the dramatically enhanced endurance and increased mitochondrial content in these mice. This raises the intriguing possibility that some of the mitochondrial myopathies associated with FGF21 increases may also be associated with oxidative damage to mtDNA, which may negate the endurance improvements observed in our model of OGG1 overexpression. Assessment of OGG1 activity and testing any potential benefit of pharmacological activation of OGG1, such as using dietary zinc (37) or polyphenols such as the flavonoid dihydroxyflavone (34), may be of therapeutic interest in the treatment of these myopathies or age-related sarcopenia. Similarly, while many of these other models of FGF21 overexpression are associated with metabolic dysfunction, Ogg1Tg mice are protected from metabolic derangements, such as high-fat diet–induced obesity and insulin resistance (11), making these mice a distinct paradigm of muscle FGF21 induction with improved muscle endurance and metabolic protection. Indeed, studies by Bosch et al. utilizing muscle-directed FGF21 overexpression have similarly demonstrated significant protection from obesity, insulin resistance, and hepatic steatosis in mice with elevated muscle-derived FGF21 (28, 31). Thus, our previously reported metabolic phenotypes in Ogg1Tg mice, including protection from diet-, age-, and genetically induced obesity, adiposity, and steatosis (11, 14), are likely to be causally linked with our current observations of elevated muscle-derived circulating FGF21 in this model. The mechanisms by which OGG1 overexpression leads to elevation of muscle FGF21 are still under investigation. Furthermore, we acknowledge that studies that target FGF21 expression in Ogg1Tg muscle are required to unequivocally link FGF21 to the observed phenotype of elevated exercise endurance in these mice; these studies are warranted but were outside the scope of the current investigation.

It is intriguing that this effect is only apparent in skeletal muscle, without alterations in hepatic or cardiac FGF21 expression. This argues for an indirect role for OGG1 in regulating FGF21, despite the documented impact of OGG1 in transcriptional regulation (4). While our studies rule out elevated ER stress as a linking mechanism, it is possible that improved insulin signaling and Akt phosphorylation may, at least partially, mediate the observed increases in FGF21 in Ogg1Tg mice (45). Apart from these changes in FGF21 levels, PGC-1α signaling, and Akt phosphorylation, other mechanisms may link the changes in OGG1 expression to muscle function. Germane to this, two recent studies have suggested that OGG1 expression modulates the secretion of mtDNA to the cytosol (39, 81). Upon induction of oxidative damage, mtDNA not repaired by OGG1 can be cleaved by the endonuclease FEN1, resulting in small mtDNA fragments that exit mitochondria via mPTP- and voltage-dependent anion channel–dependent channels and trigger cellular inflammation via the NLRP3 inflammasome and via activation of cGAS-pSTING signaling (39, 81). Whether similar OGG1-mediated changes in cytosolic mtDNA fragments play a role in tissues, such as skeletal muscle, particularly in response to exercise-induced stress, warrants further investigation.

In summary, we report here a novel role for enhanced BER in enhancing skeletal muscle endurance through significant increases in muscle mitochondrial content and functions. These functional changes are accompanied by and consistent with remarkable increases in muscle-derived FGF21 levels. Given the decline of both muscle function and muscle DNA repair during aging, these results may be particularly significant to developing strategies to combat age-related declines in muscle mitochondrial function and endurance.

Experimental procedures

Animals

The generation of Ogg1Tg mice on the C57BL/6J background has been previously described (14). The breeding and care of animals were per the protocols approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Rutgers University, New Brunswick, NJ, USA (protocol ID: 201900077). The sample size for the experiments was 3 to 12 male mice, as indicated in each figure legend. Animals were 12 to 16 weeks of age, except for the middle-aged cohort, which was studied at 40 weeks of age. All mice were fed a chow diet with ad libitum access to food and water and were housed with 12-h light and dark cycles. Body composition was determined by NMR (echo MRI), as previously described (11), and muscle weights were recorded. All exercised mice were fasted for 4 h prior to exercise. Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane prior to euthanasia by cardiac exsanguination, and the collected tissues were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Given the documented differences in exercise endurance between male and female mice (33), all studies in the current investigation were carried out in male animals.

OGG1 activity assay

Substrate for the OGG1 activity assay was prepared by heating oligomers at 65 °C for 15 min, and annealing was completed by incubating the oligomers at room temperature for 3 h. Oligomers contained a 40-mer nucleotide with 8-oxoG(G∗) at the 19th position and with an indodicarbocyanine label at the 3′ end (5′AGAGAAGAAGAAGAA(G∗) AGATGGGTTATTCGAACTAGCCy5Sp/3′) hybridized to its complementary sequence with cytosine opposite of G∗ (82). Tissue homogenates were incubated with the substrate for 3 h at 37 °C for the enzyme activity, as described previously (37). The reaction was stopped by adding an equal volume of gel loading buffer II (AM8547, Thermo Fisher) containing formamide and heated at 95 °C for 5 min. The excised substrate and product were separated by loading 10 μl in 20% polyacrylamide gel containing 8 M urea in Tris–borate–EDTA buffer and ran at 90 V. Fluorescence in the separated DNA bands was visualized using an Azure c600 imager (Azure Biosystems), and product fluorescence was quantified and normalized for protein concentration and the assay reaction time, represented as nmol/g/h. Controls run on every gel included substrate only and Ogg1−/− liver whole cell lysate as negative controls, and Ogg1Tg liver whole cell lysate as a positive control.

Skeletal muscle mitochondrial fractionation

OGG1 activity was assessed in skeletal mitochondrial fractions, as previously described (37). Briefly, gastrocnemius muscles were rinsed, trimmed to remove visible fat, and minced in ice-cold PBS solution containing 10 mM EDTA. The minced tissues were centrifuged at 200g for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was homogenized in mitochondrial isolation buffer (MIB—250 mM mannitol, 5 mM Hepes; 0.5 mM EGTA; 0.1% bovine serum albumin) and centrifuged at 700g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was centrifuged at 10,400g for 10 min, and the pellet containing mitochondria was washed in MIB twice at 10,400g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting mitochondrial pellet was resuspended in MIB and used for OGG1 activity assays, as above.

8-oxoG measurement

DNA was isolated from gastrocnemius using the Qiagen DNA isolation kit (n = 4–6 each group). 8-oxoG was measured by ELISA (Trevigen), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Treadmill studies

Mice were run on an animal treadmill (Columbus animal treadmill: Exer 3/6) as previously published (26) for a timed duration or until exhaustion, as indicated. Briefly, mice were placed on a belt of a six-lane motorized treadmill (Exer 3/6 Treadmill; Columbus Instruments) supplied with shocker plates. The treadmill was run at an incline of 0° at 5 m/min for 5 min, after which the speed was increased by 1 m/min every minute up to 15 m/min until 20 min or until exhaustion. For exhaustion studies, the test was stopped when the mouse remained on the shocker plate for 10 s without attempting to reengage the treadmill, and the time to exhaustion was noted; mice were euthanized immediately following exercise. RERs were determined by running 15-week-old male mice on a Columbus Instruments single-lane isolated treadmill (Metabolic Treadmill) within respiratory chambers (Oxymax, Columbus Instruments), using the same 20-min or exhaustion protocols described above. All mice were acclimatized for 3 days at 5 m/min before being subjected to exercise protocols.

Protein analyses

Whole cell lysates were prepared, and protein concentrations were determined by Bradford assay, as previously described (34). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Total protein staining was performed using Ponceau stain to confirm uniform protein loading and transfer; all Ponceau blots are shown in Fig. S2. Membranes were blocked in 5% nonfat dried milk in Tris-buffered saline (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4) with 0.1% Tween-20 for an hour and were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody (Table 1). Membranes were further incubated with horseradish peroxidase- or Alexa-Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies, and signal was detected using enhanced chemiluminescence or fluorescence imaging, respectively, on an Azure c600 imaging system (Azure Biosystems). Images were quantified using the Java-based image processing software ImageJ 1.53a.

RNA-Seq analyses

Total RNA was isolated from 20-week-old male WT and Ogg1Tg mice using the RNeasy mini kit (QIAGEN). Complementary DNA library preparation and RNA-Seq were performed at RUCDR Infinite Biologics, Rutgers University. Library preparation for whole transcriptome sequencing, with rRNA depletion, was performed using the NuGen Ovation Universal RNA-Seq system according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The libraries were analyzed on the Agilent 4200 TapeStation System using High Sensitivity D1000 ScreenTape Assay and quantified using KAPA qPCR. Libraries were then normalized to 10 nM, and nine libraries were combined per pool to get the specified number of reads per sample. Each pooled library was then clustered and sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq instrument to obtain 2 × 75 bp paired-end reads. Sequencing data from the NextSeq instrument were demultiplexed and adapter-trimmed to generate sample-specific fastq files. Fastq files were used for quality check and adaptor removal using “Trimmomatic.” Adaptor removed files were used to map the reads with the referenced mouse genome GRCm38 (Synonym: mm10; GenBank assembly accession: GCA_000001635.2) using TopHat v2.0. Mapped reads were analyzed for DEGs using “edgeR” (empirical analysis of DGE in R) (84). Data are represented as volcano plots. The RNA-Seq data generated for this study are available at Gene Expression Omnibus with accession number GSE267211.

Pathway enrichment and network analysis

Pathway enrichment and protein–protein interaction networks were generated using the STRING enrichment plugin in Cytoscape (version 3.10.4). The analysis was performed on DEGs identified through bulk RNA-Seq of Ogg1Tg skeletal muscle (FC >±0.5, p < 0.05). The network was constructed with a confidence (score) cutoff of 0.8, and up to 10 additional interactors were included to capture closely connected nodes. Functional clusters within the pathways were identified using the Molecular Complex Detection algorithm with a node score cutoff of 0.2 and a degree cutoff of 1. Network visualization was done using the yFiles circular layout. The node color indicates the FC gradient (ranging from −1 to 1), and the node size is proportional to the degree of interaction. Only the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathways with a false discovery rate <0.01 and p value <0.01 were considered as significantly enriched.

Transmission electron microscopy

A fresh gastrocnemius slice was minced in a reagent containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer. The minced tissue was fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide. After dehydrating in a graded acetone series, samples were embedded in Embed812 resin. The Leica UC6 ultramicrotome was used to make 90 nm sections, stained with a saturated solution of uranyl acetate and lead citrate. A Philips CM12 transmission electron microscope with an Advanced Microscopy Techniques XR111 digital camera at 80 kV was used to capture images. Ten images were used to estimate mitochondrial count, and an average was taken.

Plasma FGF21

A sandwich-type ELISA kit from BioVendor R&D (catalog no.: RD291108200R) was used according to the protocol to assess the plasma FGF21.

Metabolite analysis

Glycogen was analyzed using the amyloglucosidase–glucose oxidase method (85, 86, 87). Homogenized tissue was treated with amyloglucosidase to hydrolyze glycogen into glucose. Glucose was measured using glucose oxidase and peroxidase enzymes in the colorimetric absorption assay. Glycogen concentration was calculated using a standard curve generated using a known amount of glycogen. Lactate was assessed from tail blood using the Lactate Plus Meter from Nova Biomedical.

Statistical analyses

A Student’s t test for two-group comparisons or one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests for multiple groups was used to analyze biological replicates. Data are expressed as mean ± SD. p < 0.05 were considered significant.
