Fibrosing Mediastinitis With Complex Airway Obstruction: A Combination of Fixed Obstruction, Dynamic Collapse, and Vascular–Esophageal Involvement
Authors: Stephany Ivonne Briones Alvarado, Elena Ojeda Castilleja, Francisco José Caballero Segura, Alicia Oliva Ramos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFibrosing mediastinitis is a rare fibroinflammatory condition characterized by progressive fibrous infiltration of the mediastinum that can compromise bronchovascular, esophageal, and vascular structures. Its clinical presentation is heterogeneous and can mimic more prevalent respiratory diseases, such as bronchial asthma, leading to significant diagnostic delays. This case reports a patient with fibrosing mediastinitis whose presentation was suggestive of bronchial asthma. This patient developed a set of findings that revealed a triple mechanism of airway obstruction: fixed obstruction, dynamic collapse, and vascular–esophageal involvement. Treatment with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) produced significant and sustained clinical improvement, even after bronchodilators were withdrawn. This case highlights the importance of considering fibrosing mediastinitis in patients with refractory respiratory symptoms and underscores the role of CPAP as a symptomatic therapeutic option in selected cases.Keywords: Mediastinitis, Fibrosing, Obstruction, Bronchomalacia, Asthma, CPAP

Fibrosing mediastinitis (FM) is an uncommon fibroinflammatory disorder for which no established or clearly effective medical therapies currently exist. It is considered the result of an idiosyncratic immune response to various stimuli, leading to progressive fibrotic infiltration of the mediastinum, with subsequent narrowing and involvement of bronchovascular, esophageal, and vascular structures.1, 2 We report the case of a patient with FM whose clinical presentation mimicked bronchial asthma and progressively evolved into bronchial and mediastinal vascular encasement.

The patient was an 82-year-old woman, a never-smoker, with a history of prolonged exposure to biomass fuels (coal and wood), gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), and hiatal hernia, who had experienced orthopnea and predominantly nocturnal wheezing for approximately seven years. During this period, she had multiple hospital admissions for episodes labeled as bronchospasm. A presumptive diagnosis of bronchial asthma was made, and she was empirically treated with bronchodilators, eventually escalating to triple inhaled therapy, without clinical improvement. Pulmonary function tests were not evaluable due to the patient's inability to perform reproducible maneuvers. Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis was investigated as a potential comorbidity contributing to poor symptom control and was ruled out. The patient subsequently experienced progressive clinical deterioration, evolving to dyspnea at rest and requiring long-term oxygen therapy. During a subsequent hospital admission, the appearance of cervical and central chest tightness was noted, along with worsening dyspnea and wheezing in the supine position.

On physical examination, inspiratory stridor at the cervical level was auscultated, which did not change with the Valsalva maneuver, together with rhonchi and wheezing in the middle and lower lung fields. No other relevant findings were observed. Computed tomography (CT) of the neck and chest performed during forced expiration revealed esophageal dilatation, soft-tissue density encasing the bronchovascular structures, bibasal pulmonary opacities, findings compatible with malacia of both main bronchi, and bilateral pleural effusion (Fig. 1). Thoracentesis revealed a transudative pleural effusion, with negative cytology and NTproBNP. Given the suspicion of pulmonary aspiration, a videofluoroscopic swallow study was performed, confirming aspiration. Due to clinical worsening despite bronchodilators and systemic corticosteroids, treatment with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) at 9 cmH2O was initiated. With this, saturations remained above 94% and remarkable clinical improvement was achieved. This improvement persisted even after bronchodilators were withdrawn and corticosteroids were gradually reduced. Bronchoscopy was not performed because, despite clinical improvement, the patient had poor baseline functional status and the procedure was considered to be poorly tolerated.

The trachea shows mild luminal narrowing without significant collapse at this level. The pulmonary arteries show reduced caliber and irregular contours, without intraluminal filling defects, consistent with extrinsic vascular compression. A small bilateral pleural effusion is also present, slightly more prominent on the left side (diagnostic thoracentesis revealed a transudative effusion). (E) Axial CT in lung window during forced expiration demonstrating severe dynamic collapse of both main bronchi, with a luminal reduction greater than 80%, a finding diagnostic of bronchomalacia.

An extended etiological work-up was carried out, including serological testing, autoimmune studies, sputum cultures, sputum eosinophil count, and interferon-gamma release assay (QuantiFERON), all of which were negative. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a tricuspid regurgitation velocity of 2.9 m/s and right ventricular dilation, indirect signs of pulmonary hypertension (PH). Based on these findings and the favorable response to CPAP, a diagnosis of idiopathic fibrosing mediastinitis complicated by pulmonary hypertension was established. FM is considered a clinicopathological syndrome rather than a single disease entity, and multiple triggers have been described, including granulomatous infections, autoimmune diseases, radiotherapy, Hodgkin lymphoma, trauma, medications, and IgG4-related disease. Histoplasmosis is the most common cause of FM in North America, accounting for up to 78% of cases 1–3. In regions where histoplasmosis is not endemic, idiopathic FM may represent up to 58% of cases. Due to its nonspecific clinical presentation, which overlaps with more common conditions such as asthma, diagnosis is often delayed. Exposure to tobacco smoke or other inhaled toxins has been associated with more severe disease forms.3, 4

Symptoms result from obstruction of mediastinal structures, including the esophagus, airways, pulmonary vasculature, or central systemic veins. The most frequent symptoms are cough, dyspnea, hemoptysis, and pleuritic chest pain. The main mechanisms of airway obstruction include bronchial invasion by fibrotic tissue and pulmonary vascular compression secondary to PH 3. In our patient, bilateral main bronchial malacia developed. One possible explanation is that chronic reduction in pulmonary vascular volume secondary to arterial obstruction may allow reciprocal airway enlargement. Another hypothesis suggests that the development of collateral bronchial circulation in the setting of PH may produce a steal phenomenon, resulting in secondary weakening of the bronchial wall.4, 5 This may explain the coexistence of fixed airway obstruction (due to fibrotic tracheobronchial involvement) and dynamic airway collapse (secondary to malacia), a combination that has been rarely described in FM.4 This also supports the favorable response to CPAP and the lack of response to bronchodilators, making a diagnosis of asthma unlikely. Contrast-enhanced CT is the imaging modality of choice for diagnosing FM, as it allows accurate delineation of disease extent and severity, including vascular and airway stenosis.3

Typical findings include calcifications, heterogeneous enhancement of infiltrative tissue, occlusion or subocclusion of pulmonary vasculature, and pleural effusion.6, 7 Pulmonary vascular obstruction may lead to PH and manifest on CT as focal or diffuse areas of increased lung attenuation, ground-glass opacities, and interlobular septal thickening.4 These findings may explain the indirect echocardiographic signs of PH, the requirement for oxygen therapy, and the ground-glass pattern observed in our patient. The predominance of lesions in the lower lobes may additionally be attributed to chronic aspiration, facilitated by underlying GERD and hiatal hernia. In this case, the severity of GERD may be explained by functional esophageal obstruction secondary to fibrotic compression, leading to increased intraluminal pressure, esophageal dilatation, and promotion of reflux and pulmonary aspiration.8 This mechanism may account for the esophageal dilatation observed on CT, poor GERD control, and recurrent aspiration episodes. Systemic corticosteroids remain the most commonly used treatment, although their efficacy is variable. Other potential therapies include tamoxifen, methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab. Surgical resection may be curative in localized disease but often requires complex vascular and airway reconstruction and is associated with high morbidity and mortality.

Patients with bilateral mediastinal involvement, extensive fibrosis, calcifications, and collateral circulation are generally not candidates for surgery. In such cases, endovascular or endobronchial interventions aimed at restoring patency of the affected structures may be considered.2, 3 CPAP has been described as a therapeutic option in patients with tracheobronchomalacia; however, its role in FM is not well established.9 This case highlights both typical and atypical features of a rare disease and underscores the diagnostic challenge it poses. The diagnosis of idiopathic FM requires a combination of suggestive radiological findings and, when feasible, histological confirmation, along with systematic exclusion of alternative diagnoses. The coexistence of tracheal involvement, dynamic airway collapse, pulmonary hypertension, esophageal dilatation, and pulmonary aspiration illustrates the complex and multifactorial nature of FM. In this context, CPAP proved to be an effective symptomatic treatment by addressing both fixed and dynamic components of airway obstruction, even in the absence of invasive therapeutic options.
