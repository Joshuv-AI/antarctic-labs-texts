“It’s very difficult to navigate the health care system”: structural barriers and the need for affirming care for LGBTQ+ young adults
Authors: Ria Umesh Maindan, Moushume Chowdhury, Gauri Birmole, Ja’nya Hill, Anthony Santella, Alvin Tran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLGBTQ+ young adults face significant barriers to healthcare access, including stigma, misgendering, and a lack of provider competence. These experiences contribute to heightened psychological distress and delays in seeking care. Minority Stress Theory offers a framework for understanding how structural and interpersonal discrimination impacts health outcomes in this population.PurposeThis study qualitatively explores the healthcare experiences of a sample of LGBTQ+ young adults in the United States to identify barriers to affirming care and generate recommendations for inclusive, equitable healthcare delivery.MethodsIn February 2025, four virtual focus group discussions were conducted with 35 LGBTQ+ individuals between the ages of 18 and 26 years living in the United States. Participants were diverse in terms of sexual orientation, gender identity, and race/ethnicity. A semi-structured focus group guide informed by prior research guided the discussions. All focus group transcripts were analyzed via thematic analysis. Coding was conducted collaboratively using MAXQDA, and intercoder reliability was established. Minority Stress Theory guided the interpretation of themes.ResultsFour key themes emerged: (1) Building Trust Through Affirming and Competent Care Practices; (2) Expanding Pathways to LGBTQ+ Healthcare; (3) Provider Education, Training, and Accountability; and (4) Cultivating Safe, Inclusive, and Visibly Affirming Healthcare Spaces. Participants identified multiple interpersonal and structural barriers, while also offering actionable strategies for system improvement.ConclusionsLGBTQ+ young adults require more than inclusive language; they need healthcare systems committed to structural change, accountability, and visibility. Findings underscore the importance of embedding LGBTQ+ competence and affirmation across all levels of care to promote health equity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14432-2.

Background

Lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, or questioning (LGBTQ+) young people, between 18 and 30 years old, routinely face identity-based adversity that manifests in both interpersonal and institutional settings. In particular, they often encounter challenges related to bullying, victimization, and negative attitudes from both peers and their families due to their identity [1–3]. For LGBTQ+ young adults, bullying can occur either at an individual level, including verbal or physical harassment, social isolation, intimidation, and pressure to conform, or at an institutional level, where they may be deemed as “unnatural”, their reports of being bullied are dismissed, and their needs are disregarded [4]. LGBTQ+ young adults also experience significantly higher rates of bullying and cyberbullying and engage in more risk-taking behaviors, such as substance use and drunk driving, compared to their heterosexual counterparts; they also have worse overall health consequences [5]. Such patterns of exclusion and marginalization toward LGBTQ+ young adults extend beyond social and educational contexts and are also reflected in healthcare access and utilization [1].

LGBTQ+ young people frequently report challenges accessing affirming healthcare [6]. Studies have suggested that barriers such as fear of stigma, risk of rejection, concerns about disclosing one’s sexual orientation or gender identity, and negative experiences with healthcare providers contribute to avoidance of care, unmet health needs, and unique identity-based stressors [7–9]. Access to healthcare is also stratified within the LGBTQ+ community; for instance, transgender and non-binary individuals often face greater challenges in obtaining gender affirming care, compared to their lesbian, bisexual, and gay peers [6]. While seeking gender-affirming care, transgender and non-binary individuals often encounter challenges such as feeling misunderstood, frustrated, or disrespected by providers who are unable to see beyond the binary, lack cultural competence, and have inadequate training and education [10]. For instance, studies have documented how LGBTQ+ individuals, particularly those who are also Black and Indigenous, respond to bias and discrimination in healthcare by delaying care, mistrusting providers, or developing protective strategies such as self-advocacy or changing providers [11].

Beyond physical health, LGBTQ+ young adults experience disproportionately high levels of psychological distress due to unique identity-based stressors [12]. Owing to their gender or sexual identities, LGBTQ+ young adults may experience unique identity-based stressors such as rejection from family or peers, heterosexism, internalized homophobia, and identity concealment – thus triggering mental health issues [13]. In turn, exposure to negative reactions and stigma from close friends and relatives puts LGBTQ+ young adults at an increased risk for psychological distress. Depression, anxiety, substance use, and suicidal behavior and ideation in LGBTQ+ young adults are elevated in comparison to cisgender and straight/heterosexual populations, and this is further exacerbated in transgender young adults [5, 14, 15]. Experiences with stigma, discrimination, and marginalization not only compromise one’s mental health but may also diminish life satisfaction and sense of community belonging [16]. For example, studies of transgender and non-binary young adults of color found that experiences of racial and gender discrimination in healthcare settings were common, and participants described adopting protective behaviors such as selectively choosing providers or seeking information independently to preserve their overall well-being [17, 18]. Additionally, studies show that transgender and nonbinary individuals practice strategies of self-advocacy and empowerment in peer-supported spaces to resist and reduce healthcare inequity [19]. Overall, transgender and non-binary health can greatly benefit from accessing empowering healthcare and social support systems [20].

While healthcare providers have the potential to support LGBTQ+ young adults in overcoming disparities related to their physical health, mental health, as well as other disparities tied to their identities, gaps in provider training remain significant. While an attempt to increase LGBTQ+-centered care persists, about 70% residency programs do not have LGBTQ+-related training, and approximately 79% physicians report no prior transgender or bisexual health training [21]. Based on the curricula, about 56% medical students felt underprepared to treat and care for LGBTQ+ individuals [22]. Discomfort while accessing healthcare, withholding personal information, changing healthcare providers frequently, or avoiding seeking healthcare altogether are some of the ramifications of the limited medical training and knowledge of LGBTQ+ life experiences and health needs [23]. These problems are often intensified in geographically underserved areas [24].

The Minority Stress Theory provides a useful framework for understanding these disparities, which burden LGBTQ+ young people in healthcare settings. It posits that distal stressors such as stigma, discrimination, rejection, and proximal stressors such as identity concealment and internalized homophobia are key drivers of negative health outcomes among LGBTQ+ individuals. These stressors, which are central to their social lives, are the leading causes of mental health conditions among LGBTQ+ young adults [25, 26]. Minority stress can be heightened during the formative development life stage, where young adults are learning and expressing their sexual and gender identity [15]. Radicalized and minoritized populations who are also LGBTQ+ face additional stressors due to the intersection of multiple identities, causing a higher risk for poor mental health outcomes compared to LGBTQ+ young adults who do not have overlapping marginalized identities [27, 28]. Although Minority Stress Theory was originally developed to explain mental health disparities among sexual minority populations, it has increasingly been used to understand broader health experiences among LGBTQ+ individuals, including how anticipated stigma shapes healthcare interactions, why some individuals delay or avoid care, and how affirming healthcare environments can mitigate minority stress [26, 29].

As studies have shown, young adulthood is a critical developmental period for key health outcomes and behaviors, including mental health symptomology and substance use [16]. Most studies either group LGBTQ+ experiences across broad age ranges or zero in on specific groups—like transgender people or rural residents—leaving young adults largely overlooked [30]. Thus, it is imperative that more studies focus on the healthcare experiences of LGBTQ+ people across the lifespan. This study addresses that gap by qualitatively exploring the diverse healthcare experiences of a sample of LGBTQ+ young adults in the U.S. Additionally, although existing research has shown that adequate support can substantially improve the mental, physical, and social well-being of LGBTQ+ young adults, systemic barriers to affirming and equitable care remain entrenched. Informed by the Minority Stress Theory, this study seeks to address the following research questions: (1) How do LGBTQ+ young adults describe their lived experiences navigating healthcare systems in select urban areas in the United States? and (2) What recommendations do LGBTQ+ young adults offer for creating affirming and inclusive healthcare for their communities?

Methods

Study design and participants

This qualitative study, informed by a phenomenological approach, explored the lived healthcare experiences of LGBTQ+ young adults in the U.S. In February 2025, a team of five researchers from the University of New Haven, West Haven, Connecticut, conducted four virtual focus groups with 35 participants recruited from the U.S. These focus groups aimed to capture a diverse range of perspectives and in-depth experiences from LGBTQ+ young adults. Participants were eligible if they: (1) self-identified as a member of the LGBTQ+ community; (2) were between 18 and 30 years old; (3) spoke English; and (4) lived in one of the recruitment areas (Orange County, California, Chicago, Illinois, and Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania). There was no prior relationship between the participants and any member of the research team. Informed consent was obtained electronically prior to the start of each focus group through an online form that also collected demographic information.

Data collection

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of New Haven (#2024 − 109). Recruitment was carried out in partnership with CenterLink, a national organization that connects more than 375 LGBTQ+ community centers across the U.S [31]. CenterLink distributed a digital flier through its member network, and interested individuals contacted a representative to enroll in a virtual focus group. Participants were recruited from three regions in the U.S.: Orange County, California; Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania; and Naperville, Illinois. Additionally, focus groups were not organized by sexual orientation or gender identity; instead, participants elected to join sessions based on availability, resulting in groups that included individuals with diverse sexual orientation and gender identities.

All focus groups were conducted virtually via Zoom. To prioritize participant comfort, camera use was optional. At the beginning of each session, the corresponding author described the study’s purpose, reviewed the informed consent process, and addressed any questions from participants. Participants were presented with an electronic consent form in Qualtrics; after providing consent, they were directed to the demographic questionnaire within the same platform. The focus group interview guide was informed by preliminary findings from a prior national study conducted by Born This Way Foundation and Hopelab [32]. This foundational study, which included more than 1,200 LGBTQ+ young adults across the U.S., explored online experiences and the role of community support in fostering well-being. Findings from that study informed the development of questions used in the present focus group study [32]. The research team, comprising individuals of diverse sexual orientation and gender identities, collaboratively developed and refined the focus group guide. The guide was informed by the Minority Stress Model–questions on outness and safety reflected proximal and distal stress processes, while questions on kindness, support, and social media platform safety addressed resilience and protective factors. The number of focus groups ceased once the research team agreed they had reached data saturation. The team conducted preliminary analyses, which consisted of independently highlighting themes and subthemes from the transcripts after each focus group, and by the fourth discussion, no new themes were emerging. Data saturation was defined as the point at which no new themes emerge, and responses become repetitive [33].

The corresponding author facilitated all focus groups to maintain consistency and minimize interviewer bias, while a second team member served as a notetaker. All sessions were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim using Otter.ai, a widely used real-time transcription software [34]. Transcripts were de-identified to ensure participant confidentiality, reviewed for accuracy, and errors were corrected by the researchers within 24 hours of each session.

Data analysis

A phenomenological approach guided this study, as we sought to understand the lived experiences of LGBTQ+ young adults [35]. MAXQDA, a software used to organize and analyze qualitative data, was used to code the transcript and form preliminary themes. Data analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework for reflexive thematic analysis, a theoretically flexible method that allows both inductive and theoretical approaches [36]. We employed a hybrid strategy where initial codes were generated inductively from the transcripts and refined collaboratively through iterative review, while the Minority Stress Model served as a sensitizing framework to shape the codebook and map themes onto distal stressors, proximal stressors, and protective factors. This approach ensured that findings were grounded in participants’ narratives while also being interpreted through a well-established theoretical lens.

Following the six-phrase framework, four members of the research team familiarized themselves with the transcripts by thoroughly reading and re-reading them to gain a comprehensive understanding of the content. Each researcher prepared a preliminary set of codes and created notes for comparison based on the responses of the participants of each focus group. To finalize the codebook, the team used MAXQDA’s intercoder agreement function to assess coding consistency across researchers. This tool allowed for comparison of code application across all four coders, helping to identify discrepancies, refine code definitions, and enhance overall reliability. Codebook adjustments were made collaboratively [37]. Four members of the research team then independently coded all focus group transcripts using the finalized codebook. Discrepancies in coding and theme development were discussed during regularly scheduled meetings. Team members reviewed each disagreement, shared their perspectives, and resolved differences through discussion until all members agreed on the codes and themes, and a final consensus was reached. Additionally, during the final stages of theme refinement, the research team considered how the emergent themes reflected or extended the core constructs of Minority Stress Theory. This theoretical lens helped to contextualize participants’ narratives within broader structural and psychosocial stress processes experienced by LGBTQ+ youth. Final themes were reviewed and refined to ensure clarity, coherence, and consistency across the dataset.

Participants were 18–26 years old (mean = 21.9), with slightly more aged 22–24 (54.3%) than 18–21 (45.7%). The sample was racially and ethnically diverse: 46.7% White, 31.4% Black or African American, 8.6% Asian, and 11.4% Other; most were non-Hispanic (80.0%). Qualitative results

We identified four overarching themes that illustrate how both proximal and distal stressors shape LGBTQ+ young adults’ healthcare experiences. Participant narratives underscored the ways in which healthcare systems and providers can either alleviate or intensify minority stress. Together, these themes provide actionable insights at the organizational and policy levels for advancing affirming and equitable healthcare. Direct quotes are included under each theme, with minimal editing, to preserve participants’ voices and highlight key findings:

Theme 1: Building trust through affirming and competent care practices

This theme reflects distal stressors, including structural stigma, identity invalidation, and discriminatory treatment. Participants emphasized the urgent need for respectful, identity-affirming protocols as expanded below, to counter anticipated stigma and promote psychological safety during care encounters.

Inclusive language and pronoun use

This subtheme highlights the importance of providers consistently using affirming language, including chosen names and pronouns, to promote respect, recognition, and trust during healthcare encounters. Most participants frequently emphasized that healthcare providers’ use of accurate, affirming, and non-stigmatizing language, including consistently asking for and using chosen names and pronouns, significantly enhanced their comfort and trust during care encounters. These practices, when implemented both verbally and in written documentation, helped participants feel seen and respected while ensuring continuity across providers. A Black, Hispanic gay man from Pittsburgh shared:

Healthcare providers can improve LGBTQ+ care by offering inclusive training, using gender-affirming language, ensuring confidentiality, addressing specific health needs, creating welcoming environments, providing mental health support, and advocating for equitable healthcare policies to enhance patient trust and well-being.

Healthcare providers can improve LGBTQ+ care by offering inclusive training, using gender-affirming language, ensuring confidentiality, addressing specific health needs, creating welcoming environments, providing mental health support, and advocating for equitable healthcare policies to enhance patient trust and well-being.

A Black, non-Hispanic lesbian woman from Pittsburgh added:

… I feel like that’ll be so much easier, and physicians overall, even if you’re not in the community, just have more knowledge, regardless of people in the community, so you can know how to go about who you’re seeing because you never know who’s walking into your office anymore, who’s seeing you virtually online anymore. You know, the first thing that comes out of your mouth should be, ‘What are your pronouns?’

Visible signals of affirming care

This subtheme reflects participants’ emphasis on visible indicators of LGBTQ+ inclusion, such as affirming messaging, online provider profiles, and signals of competence that help patients identify safe healthcare spaces. To avoid stigma, one participant shared he would actively screen providers online for evidence of LGBTQ+ competence. A White, bisexual, transman from Chicago explained:When I look for [a healthcare provider], I look for pronouns in the bio and LGBTQ+ mentioned on their website so that I know it’s a safe place.

I found my top surgeon through Tiktok, and a huge reason why I picked that surgeon was not only because he was a good plastic surgeon, but also because he was so LGBTQ+ forward as a cis, straight man, he focused so much on making sure that my experience as a trans person is very queer-focused, and that it wasn’t I was going to a plastic surgeon to get top surgery. Establishing confidentiality and trust

This subtheme captures participants’ concerns about privacy and disclosure, particularly for those not out to family members, and underscores the importance of confidentiality practices that foster psychological safety. In particular, there were concerns about privacy for participants not out to their families. In such cases, confidentiality care practices should be established beforehand to decrease anxiety surrounding their identities and enhance their overall healthcare experience. As a trans man from Chicago shared:Also, when it comes to privacy, not outing them to their parents and stuff like that is a big thing because you’re not allowed to share medical information if you choose not to disclose that with your parents. So it should be the same for gender identity, if you’re not comfortable with your healthcare provider saying those things to your parents or guardians.

Also, when it comes to privacy, not outing them to their parents and stuff like that is a big thing because you’re not allowed to share medical information if you choose not to disclose that with your parents. So it should be the same for gender identity, if you’re not comfortable with your healthcare provider saying those things to your parents or guardians.

Other participants emphasized the importance of providers asking family members to leave the room to screen for trauma or gender dysphoria, practices that build trust and encourage honest disclosure. A White, non-Hispanic, bisexual, queer, transman from Orange County explains:

In terms of healthcare providers, I think that they can play a really important role. The questionnaires that they give you, if you’re experiencing anxiety or depression, I think that they could also ask your parents or someone to leave so they can ask about childhood abuse, financial situation, stuff like that. I think that in those moments, they can ask about transgender [healthcare related] things like, ‘Are you experiencing gender dysphoria?’

Theme 2: Expanding pathways to LGBTQ+ healthcare

This theme illustrates structural barriers that limit LGBTQ+ young adults’ access to affirming, identity-conscious healthcare. These structural barriers include the lack of LGBTQ+-competent providers, logistical obstacles to gender-affirming care, and insufficient visibility of inclusive services. Most participants described how systems that prioritize transparency, accessibility, and autonomy, both in-person and virtually, can reduce structural stressors and support engagement in care.

Promoting access to LGBTQ+ health services

This subtheme reflects participants’ experiences navigating fragmented healthcare systems and highlights the need for more accessible resources and guidance for LGBTQ+ individuals seeking care. Many participants emphasized the complexity and emotional burden of navigating fragmented healthcare systems without adequate support or visibility. Many described the healthcare navigation process as isolating and overwhelming. As an Asian, White, non-Hispanic, lesbian, transwoman from Orange County shared:

I just feel like it’s very difficult to navigate and it’d be very nice if there were more resources to help people navigate them, and also, in general, have more resources online to get these things set up, because for me I don’t have a primary care doctor or physician, so I kind of had to do things on my own a lot at the time, and that’s scary.

I just feel like it’s very difficult to navigate and it’d be very nice if there were more resources to help people navigate them, and also, in general, have more resources online to get these things set up, because for me I don’t have a primary care doctor or physician, so I kind of had to do things on my own a lot at the time, and that’s scary.

Streamlining gender-affirming care access

This subtheme emphasizes participants’ calls for reducing administrative barriers and gatekeeping that limit access to gender-affirming treatments and services. Several participants discussed the need for simplified access to gender-affirming healthcare. Some shared how innovative services such as mail-order hormone therapy reduced gatekeeping and logistical hurdles. Others noted the benefit of providers using social media platforms to share accessible, evidence-based information—allowing patients to feel more prepared and empowered [38]. A White, Hispanic, pansexual, queer, transman from Orange County shares:

He would provide studies [on TikTok]. It was a lot of stuff that I then took and could go to my provider and be like, ‘Hey, this is what I want to start doing.’ And it was just an easy, digestible way to understand medical information.

Use of online resources, telehealth, and directories

This subtheme illustrates how digital platforms and telehealth services can increase accessibility, safety, and autonomy for LGBTQ+ patients seeking care. The widespread shift toward telehealth, accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic, was described by many participants as a meaningful improvement in care accessibility and safety. Telehealth platforms allowed some participants to avoid discriminatory in-person experiences and communicate more freely and comfortably, as highlighted by a Black, non-Hispanic, bisexual man from Pittsburgh:

So, by going to telehealth, I think it could be a way of helping us to express ourselves. Some people like myself, I express myself online more than in any other place. So, going through the processes of a business to assess healthcare services would be a long process for us. Maybe you could be looked down upon, discriminated against, and all of that. So I prefer telehealth, being consulted online or being treated online, and it goes a long way to promote our integrity and image.

Referral systems

Finally, participants emphasized the importance of reliable referral systems that connect LGBTQ+ individuals to trusted, identity-affirming providers. This subtheme highlights the importance of trusted referral networks that connect LGBTQ+ individuals to affirming and competent providers. Without such systems, care experiences can feel fragmented and unsafe. A Hispanic, gay, genderqueer participant from Orange County noted:

Healthcare professionals should direct LGBTQ+ patients to the right people, to those who are not biased and will not discriminate.

Even though he was a plastic surgeon, he’s like, ‘Here are other good, LGBTQ+ friendly surgeons.’ I can now look for other reliable, trans-friendly medical professionals.

Theme 3: Provider education, training, and accountability

Most participants in this study consistently identified gaps in provider knowledge and LGBTQ+ cultural competence as sources of distress that reinforced their vulnerability and mistrust in clinical settings. They called for widespread, mandatory education, both at the professional and school levels, as a critical step toward dismantling systemic barriers and improving care outcomes. In particular, many emphasized that providers play a vital role in bridging the gap between marginalized patients and affirming healthcare systems. When adequately trained and resourced, providers not only deliver more competent care but also help foster inclusive environments that validate LGBTQ+ identities. A White, non-Hispanic, bisexual, queer, transman from Orange County explained how simple actions, such as sharing information and connecting patients to resources, can significantly improve care experiences:

They can give them a pamphlet or say, ‘Here’s the LGBTQ+ center’s number. If you want to call something like that.’ I was really grateful I had family that connected me to the LGBTQ+ Center and showed me that they have resources for therapy. I think that some kind of way of making this more accessible would be really important. Kind of like an incubating effect for people in more in-person spaces.

They can give them a pamphlet or say, ‘Here’s the LGBTQ+ center’s number. If you want to call something like that.’ I was really grateful I had family that connected me to the LGBTQ+ Center and showed me that they have resources for therapy. I think that some kind of way of making this more accessible would be really important. Kind of like an incubating effect for people in more in-person spaces.

Participants also emphasized the importance of integrating LGBTQ+ health content not only into medical education but also into broader K-12 school-based health curricula to normalize queer and trans health needs early in life. A Black, non-Hispanic, pansexual genderqueer participant from Pittsburgh stated:

Hold medical schools accountable for training and classes about LGBTQ+ history and how to handle people within the LGBTQ+ community. Make classes that focus on things like LGBTQ+ health and anything related to the LGBTQ+ community mandatory.

In school, I remember health class in fifth grade, where they separated us by sex, and then showed us health videos about puberty and things. And I think that that would also be an opportunity to talk more about how trans people also exist in a sort of healthcare-based way.

Some participants shared that they felt burdened by the need to educate providers themselves regarding their complex and specific health issues, especially when their health needs are tied to their gender or sexual identity. A White, Hispanic, pansexual, queer, transman from Orange County explains how challenging it is to educate providers on gender-affirming care, in addition to finding a trustworthy healthcare provider:

It’s extremely overwhelming to try to look for healthcare, whether you’re doing it online or whether you’re trying to go in person through insurance. There were so many barriers just trying to get started and then educating the providers and making sure that every experience is as positive as it can be. Again, I think there’s only so much information we can provide to every single doctor out there.

There were so many barriers just trying to get started and then educating the providers and making sure that every experience is as positive as it can be. The psychological impact of provider bias was also described as a deterrent to future help-seeking, especially among those who had already experienced rejection. As a Black, non-Hispanic, gay man from Pittsburgh explained:

I think healthcare professionals should learn to build the trust of patients who come in to discuss their identity. I’ve come across cases where healthcare professionals perpetuate discrimination toward a young person coming in to get advice. That should not happen. If it does, other people will be very afraid to have such open-ended discussions with professionals.

Theme 4: Cultivating safe, inclusive, and visibly affirming healthcare spaces

Participants described how visibly inclusive, identity-affirming spaces can foster a sense of belonging, reduce internalized stigma, and encourage care-seeking. These environmental cues, when paired with respectful interpersonal conduct and supportive policies, communicate safety and legitimacy, particularly for those navigating systems where their identities are often erased or marginalized. Many participants emphasized that small, visible signs of LGBTQ+ inclusion (i.e., pride flags, safe space signage, and the display of pronouns) helped establish a baseline of comfort and respect. These visible affirmations were described not only as symbolic but as active signals that a provider or clinic was prepared to welcome and affirm LGBTQ+ patients. As a Black, non-Hispanic, bisexual transman from Pittsburgh explained:

A greeting and welcoming environment by displaying inclusive materials like LGBTQ+ materials, such as rainbow flags and safe space stickers, or LGBTQ+ friendly vouchers, will also help to improve interaction with LGBTQ+ members.

A greeting and welcoming environment by displaying inclusive materials like LGBTQ+ materials, such as rainbow flags and safe space stickers, or LGBTQ+ friendly vouchers, will also help to improve interaction with LGBTQ+ members.

In clinical settings, participants highlighted the importance of tools that allow for easier communication and self-advocacy. An example of advocacy tools is Q-cards, which are small cards the size of cue cards, used to facilitate comfortable dialogue between the healthcare provider and patients, and can be used to help patients communicate gender identity, pronouns, and preferred terms to their providers [39]. They are viewed as a promising strategy for reducing anxiety and improving care interactions, according to a White, non-Hispanic, pansexual, transman from Chicago:

Q-cards should be at every doctor’s office. Basically, you can fill it out. It says, ‘Please call me’, and you can put your preferred name, your pronouns, and your sexual orientation, because a lot of doctors will assume you’re straight. And depending on what you’re going to the doctor for, sometimes it is important that they know your sexual orientation. And then you can say what you’d like to talk about at your visit today. You fill it out, and you can just hand it to your doctor, so you don’t have to come out and say, ‘Hey, like, I’m trans, I’m whatever.’ Also, just having the cards there, even if you’re not filling them out, will probably make you feel safer. It also makes the doctors more used to having queer and trans patients, and how to deal with that and identify them correctly.

Many participants also spoke to the harm that occurs when bias and stigma go unaddressed by providers or clinic staff. They called for proactive accountability and anti-discrimination policies that extend beyond intention to measurable action. A Black, non-Hispanic, bisexual woman from Pittsburgh shared:

The healthcare provider should train their staff about the best way to treat and respect the LGBTQ+ identity, rather than misjudging them or approaching and treating them based on their assumptions.

In contrast, the presence of supportive family members during clinical encounters was described as a meaningful buffer against discomfort or misrecognition. For example, a Hispanic, bisexual transman from Orange County reflected on how his grandmother’s presence helped him navigate an otherwise uncomfortable appointment:

I just had an experience yesterday where I had to get an ultrasound for some health issues I’ve been having lately. And I guess the doctor who was helping me was confused because she didn’t think it was for me. She thought it was for my grandma, because she came with me, and I specifically asked my grandma to come with me because I knew it was going to be a very uncomfortable situation for me.

Collectively, these accounts illustrate that safety in healthcare is not only about interpersonal respect but also about creating and sustaining inclusive environments through intentional design, policy change, and visibility. Such spaces can serve as powerful counterweights to minority stress, enabling LGBTQ+ young adults to access care with dignity and trust.

Discussion

Our study explored the healthcare experiences of a sample of LGBTQ+ young adults living in the U.S., revealing both persistent challenges and community-informed solutions for improving affirming care. Informed by Minority Stress Theory, our analysis demonstrated how distal and proximal stressors, such as stigma, anticipated discrimination, and internalized marginalization, may shape LGBTQ+ young adults’ interactions with healthcare systems. While participants demonstrated resilience in navigating hostile or uninformed environments, they also highlighted critical gaps in provider competence, systemic inclusion, and visibility of support.

Participants emphasized that affirming and inclusive care begins with fundamental practices, including the use of chosen names and pronouns, confidentiality protections, and inclusive language. Many reported that assumptions of heterosexuality or cisgender identity led to discomfort, distrust, and disengagement from care. These findings reinforce the moral and professional imperative for healthcare providers to deliver culturally competent, identity-affirming care. Basic practices, such as asking for pronouns, avoiding stigmatized language, and offering inclusive intake forms, were repeatedly described as small yet transformative acts that can foster trust and encourage care-seeking. These findings align with prior research demonstrating that trans-inclusive language and gender-affirming workplace practices are significantly associated with improved mental health and well-being, thus underscoring the broader public health impact of affirming language and practices across institutional settings [40–42].

The study also highlighted that the diversity within the LGBTQ+ community is not always recognized and must be studied to ensure diversity-affirming care as well. Stigma and discrimination were encountered rampantly by our study population, and this was particularly amplified by those who were transgender or nonbinary. Our transgender and nonbinary participants characterized their healthcare experiences as negative when they felt unheard and disrespected. Additionally, LGBTQ+-related topics in healthcare are rapidly evolving, and providers are unfamiliar with the appropriate protocols for delivering competent care [1]. While this could be an underlying reason for the gap between the healthcare system and LGBTQ+ communities, the onus for initial education falls on health professional schools, and continued medical training falls on health professionals themselves, as well as professional societies.

LGBTQ+ young adults face high rates of harassment, stigma, discrimination, and violence, which aligns with the concepts of distal stressors (institutional bias, stigma, etc.) and proximal stressors (internalized homophobia, fear of disclosure, etc.). Our findings posit that when confronted with discomfort or discrimination from healthcare providers, these young adults often turn to online resources as an alternative pathway to seek help [43]. This exhibits both a coping strategy in response to minority stress and a structural gap in healthcare, pertaining to accessible and affirming services. Despite modest efforts to improve provider training, significant gaps remain in both clinical education and organizational leadership. Studies have found that nearly one-third of healthcare staff and nearly a quarter of healthcare leaders are unaware of policies that support gender and sexual minorities within their organizations [16]. These knowledge gaps echo participants’ experiences and highlight how organizational-level oversights or failures can perpetuate minority stress.

Consistent with our theme of ‘Provider Education, Training, and Accountability’, our findings call for accountability and structural change, not only at the provider level but across healthcare systems, to implement, monitor, and evaluate inclusive practices and training. Recent evidence underscores the importance and effectiveness of mandatory, theory-driven, and longitudinal LGBTQ+ cultural competency training that incorporates interactive and multimodal learning, integrates LGBTQ+ individuals into training design, and embeds inclusive practices into institutional infrastructure such as electronic health records and non-discrimination policies [44]. Similarly, our theme of ‘Cultivating Safe, Inclusive, and Visibly Affirming Healthcare Spaces’, organizational leadership plays a critical role in fostering inclusive care environments by aligning policies, workflows, and accountability measures to provide affirming and equitable care [45].

Telehealth has been established as an effective method to increase healthcare access in marginalized populations [46]. While telehealth offers the ability to connect with healthcare providers remotely, unreliable internet connection, or high costs of stable internet could act as a possible deterrent to seeking competent care [47]. Investing in digital literacy programs, expanding internet access, and overall digital infrastructure can optimize remote digital healthcare access, reduce costs, and enhance health outcomes [48].

While our sample was limited to 35 participants, the findings suggest that LGBTQ+ young adults hold diverse experiences and needs, particularly around seeking mental and behavioral health support related to their sexual or gender identity. These accounts underscore the importance of affirming care that takes a holistic, person-centered approach, one that respects both commonalities across LGBTQ+ populations and the unique aspects of each individual’s identity [49]. Our participants further emphasized that healthcare must not only affirm gender identity and sexual orientation but also recognize diversity within the community, including intersections of race, class, and ability. Transgender and nonbinary participants reported especially negative experiences, suggesting the need for targeted interventions that address their specific needs and reduce systemic erasure. This corroborates prior findings from a study, which reported that transgender and nonbinary patients mistrust the healthcare system due to microaggressions, discrimination, and gender invalidating experiences [50, 51]. Furthermore, due to the structural and systemic inequities, patients with intersectional identities forgo accessing necessary care altogether because of the anticipated stigma and invalidation, consequently broadening the healthcare gap [51].

Finally, the findings should be interpreted within the context of the evolving U.S. health policy landscape, which directly affects LGBTQ+ populations. Recent federal policy changes have repealed several executive orders that previously supported LGBTQ+ health equity and non-discrimination protections. Newly introduced policies may influence the recognition of diverse gender identities, inclusive practices in health programming, and the collection and reporting of sexual orientation and gender identity data [52]. These changes may further exacerbate existing barriers to healthcare for LGBTQ+ individuals, particularly for transgender and nonbinary populations, and may shape the broader structural context in which individuals access care. As such, our findings underscore the continued importance of policies and public health initiatives that promote equitable and affirming healthcare for LGBTQ+ communities.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, although participants were recruited from three urban regions across the U.S., the sample is not nationally representative, and the findings are not intended to be generalizable. Transferability to other populations, particularly those in rural areas, in different sociopolitical contexts, or those younger than 18 years old, may therefore be limited. Additionally, because recruitment relied on voluntary participation through LGBTQ+ community networks, representation across sexual and gender identity subgroups may not be evenly distributed, which may influence the range of experiences captured in the study. Future research should explore these perspectives across more diverse geographic and demographic populations to better reflect the broader spectrum of LGBTQ+ experiences.

Second, recruitment was conducted online through LGBTQ+-serving organizations in the U.S. and social media platforms, which may have introduced selection bias. Participants who are more digitally connected, community-engaged, or already seeking affirming resources may have been overrepresented. As a result, the perspectives of more isolated, disconnected, or marginalized LGBTQ+ youth may not be fully captured. Nonetheless, a key strength of this study is its ability to provide in-depth, contextual insights into the lived healthcare experiences of LGBTQ+ young adults.

Third, although the virtual focus group format enabled geographic diversity and facilitated participation during a post-pandemic landscape, it may have constrained the depth of nonverbal communication. Body language, facial expressions, and group dynamics, often rich sources of meaning in in-person settings, were more difficult to observe and interpret in the virtual environment. This limitation may have influenced the moderator’s ability to probe effectively or recognize emotional nuance during the discussion [53].

Fourth, while the researchers worked collaboratively to minimize interviewer bias, it is important to acknowledge how our positionality, including our identities, experiences, and social locations, may have shaped interactions with participants and the interpretation of findings. Our team included both cisgender and nonbinary individuals, as well as people representing diverse sexual orientation identities, which may have fostered rapport with participants and informed a more nuanced interpretation of LGBTQ+ experiences in healthcare. At the same time, these shared or differing identities could have influenced how participants disclosed information, either by increasing comfort or, conversely, by introducing perceived power dynamics, social desirability bias, or hesitation in discussing sensitive topics—even in a virtual setting. Ongoing reflexivity, member checking, or participatory approaches may strengthen future studies aiming to elevate LGBTQ+ young adults’ perspectives.

Future studies should strive to include a larger and diverse LGBTQ+ sample and employ long-term or intersectional approaches to examine how affirming and non-affirming healthcare experiences impact health outcomes across extended periods. Research assessing healthcare provider education programs, institutional guidelines, and digital health solutions, including telehealth, would also help develop approaches to minimize discrimination-related stress and enhance the availability of inclusive healthcare services in the U.S.

Conclusion

Overall, this study offers critical insights into the structural, interpersonal, and environmental factors that shape healthcare experiences among LGBTQ+ young adults in the U.S. Through the lens of Minority Stress Theory, we identified the pressing need for U.S. healthcare systems to actively dismantle discriminatory practices, invest in comprehensive provider training, and foster visibly inclusive and affirming environments. When LGBTQ+ young adults encounter care that recognizes and respects their identities, they are more likely to engage in preventive services, disclose relevant health concerns, and build long-term relationships with providers. To move toward health equity, affirming care must become the standard, not the exception, within U.S. healthcare settings. By centering the voices of LGBTQ+ young adults in the U.S., we can work toward building systems of care that are not only inclusive but also transformative.
