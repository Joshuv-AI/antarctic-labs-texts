Formula feeding knowledge, practice, and associated factors among mothers visiting public hospitals with infants under six months in Southwest, Ethiopia, 2022
Authors: Nigatu Dessalegn, Tamiru Alene, Tamene Fetene Terefe, Melaku Bimerew, Haile Workye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdequate nutrition during infancy and early childhood is crucial for developing a child’s full potential. After birth, the capacity to achieve standard growth and development will be determined by selecting an appropriate diet and adequate feeding frequency. Formula feeding is feeding an infant manufactured from cow’s and other animals’ milk, which has been processed to make it suitable and copy or mimic the nutrient composition of breast milk. Maternal feeding practices are increasingly being replaced by formula feeding due to the global promotion of formula milk products, which is closely tied to the globalization of milk production. Feeding formula to an infant will predispose to infection and other health risks. This study aimed to assess the knowledge and practice of formula feeding and its associated factors among mothers of infants aged 0–6 months in public hospitals in Southwest Ethiopia, 2022.MethodAn institutional-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 559 mothers of infants 0–6 months of age from May 13 to May 28, 2022. Data were collected using a structured, interviewer-administered, and pre-tested questionnaire. The data were entered into EpiData version 4.2.0 and analyzed using SPSS version 25. Bivariable and multivariable analysis was done by using binary logistic regression.ResultFormula feeding knowledge and practice were found to be 61.6% and 36.8% respectively. Being a government employee [AOR 2.37; 95% CI, 1.23–4.58], giving birth via a cesarean section [AOR 3.03, 95% CI, 1.66–5.52], and pre-lacteal feeding [AOR 4.88, 95% CI, 3.09–7.69] were significantly associated with formula feeding practice.ConclusionIn this study, nearly greater than one third of the study participant were practice formula feeding. Being a government employee, cesarean mode of delivery, and pre-lacteal feeding were significantly associated with formula-feeding practices. Therefore, educating mothers about the importance of breastfeeding and providing support for mothers giving birth through a cesarean mode of delivery to promote exclusive breastfeeding should be prioritized at both community and institutional levels.

Introduction

Adequate nutrition during infancy and early childhood is crucial for developing a child’s full potential [1]. After birth, the capacity to achieve standard growth and development will be determined by the selection of an appropriate diet and adequate feeding frequency [2]. According to World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations, infants should be breastfed within one hour of birth and exclusively breastfed for the first 6 months of life, then continue to be breastfed up to 2 years of age and beyond [3–5]. Proper feeding of infants and young children can increase their chances of survival and promote optimal growth and development [5]. Despite this recommendation, there is an increasing rate of formula feeding practice in developing and developed countries [6–8].

Formula feeding is defined as feeding an infant manufactured from cow’s and other animals’ milk, which has been processed to make it suitable and copy or mimic the nutrient composition of breast milk as closely as possible instead of or in addition to breastfeeding [1, 2]. When formula milk is introduced at an early age, it causes an increased risk of asthma, allergy, acute respiratory disease, and infection from the contaminated formula, nutrient deficiencies, childhood cancers, chronic diseases, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, obesity, increased infectious disease occurrence, and reduced cognitive development [9–11]. In other way, children who are breastfed for longer periods have lower infectious morbidity and mortality, and higher intelligence than those who are breastfed for shorter periods, or not breastfed [11, 12]. Moreover, breastfeeding supports infants’ immune systems and may protect them later in life from chronic conditions such as obesity and diabetes [2, 12].

Globally, it is estimated that sub-optimal breastfeeding, especially non-exclusive breastfeeding in the first 6 months of life, results in 1.4 million deaths and 10% of the disease burden in children younger than 5 years [13]. This is because an infant, not being breastfed is associated with an increased incidence of infectious morbidity, as well as elevated risks of chronic disease occurrence [14]. Literature documents that formula feeding is a maladaptive practice in the face of contemporary global environmental and population health challenges [15].

According to the mini Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS) report of 2019, nearly 41% of infants did not get exclusive breastfeeding, from this approximately 16.5% and 10% of children were received pre-lacteal and mixed feed (formula and/or animal milk in addition to breast milk), respectively [16]. In addition to the above data, a recent single-center study conducted in Addis Ababa city shows that 83.4% of mothers with infants younger than six months get information about infant formula feeding, and 46.2% practice formula feeding [17].

Limited studies have been conducted in Ethiopia to assess maternal knowledge of formula feeding [18, 19]. There is a knowledge gap among mothers in choosing an infant feeding for their baby since many types and brands of formula are available in the market. Because mothers don’t know the nutritional content of formula milk, they practice formula feeding without considering its health consequences. Different studies revealed that some socio-demographic and maternal factors increase the choice of maternal formula feeding, these factors include younger age, low education status, low income (socioeconomic status), mode of delivery, and occupational status have impacts on mothers’ formula feeding [17, 20–23].

The government of Ethiopia enacted infant formula feeding and advertisement directives in 2014 to promote infant exclusive breastfeeding and lessen breast milk replacement like formula feeding [24]. Regardless of the guidelines and efforts to protect, promote, and support breastfeeding, formula feeding is still common in both developed and developing countries [25, 26]. Different studies has shown that there is a policy limitation to protecting, promoting, and supporting breastfeeding but the promotion and market of formula-feeding products globally has increased [24, 27]. These gaps and what infants consume at an earlier age have both short- and long-term impacts on health, so evaluating the maternal knowledge and practice within the first six months who are feeding formula is very important.

Methods and materials

Study area and period

This study was conducted in selected public hospitals of Gurage Zone, Sothern Nation Nationality People Region (SNNPR) from May 13 to May 28, 2022. Gurage Zone, with its capital city Wolkite, is located 153 km Southwest of Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia, and 178 km northwest of Hawassa, the capital of the SNNPR region. Gurage Zone is bordered to the Southeast by Hadiya and Yem special woreda, to the West by North, and East Oromia Region, and to the Southeast by Silt’e Zone. According to the 2007 Ethiopian census, the total population of Gurage Zone was 622, 254 men and 658, 229(51.4%) women, with 90.66% of the population being rural inhabitants. Guragie zone has 5 public hospitals, one specialized hospital, one general hospital, and 3 primary hospitals.

Study design and populations

An institutional-based cross-sectional study design was employed. The source population comprised all mothers with infants aged 0–6 months who were receiving immunization and postnatal care services in public hospitals in the Gurage Zone. The study population consisted of selected mothers with infants age 0–6 months during the study period.

Eligibility criteria

Mothers of infants from 0 to 6 months of age who were receiving immunization and postnatal care services at the time of data collection were included. Mothers who were unable to give information due to severe medical illness, due to psychiatric problems, and mothers who fulfilled the AFASS criteria for formula feeding were excluded.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

The required sample size for this study was determined by using a single population proportion formula. Based on the following assumptions, a proportion of 34.4% was taken from a previous study [17], a 4% margin of error, and a 95% confidence interval. According to the above assumptions, the required sample size was 542. After adding a non-response rate, the final sample size was 559. Before the actual data collection, the number of mothers giving birth in the last six months was taken from the labor and delivery registry book of each hospital and a sampling frame was prepared. The sample size for each hospital was determined by proportional allocation. Finally, study participants were selected using a simple random sampling technique.

Sampling procedure

There are five public Hospitals in Guragie zone. These are Wolkitie University Comprehensive specialized hospital, Butagira General Hospital, Gunchere Primary hospital, Agena Primary hospital, and Buee Primary Hospital. All five hospitals were included in this study. The sample size for each hospital was determined by proportional allocation. Study variables

Socio-demographic characteristics (Education, occupation, income, and marital status), Obstetric and maternal-health-service utilization variables, and formula-feeding knowledge were the independent variables, and formula-feeding Practice was the dependent variable.

Operational definitions

Bottle feeding

Is defined as a method of feeding an infant with expressed breast milk or manufactured milk-based food designed and marketed for feeding to babies and infants less than one year [28].

Pre-lacteal feeding

Feeding an infant with any fluid or semisolid food before the mother has begun to breastfeed [28].

Knowledge of formula feeding

The knowledge part was assessed using yes/no dichotomous questions, which consisted of ten items. The response to each question was analyzed as known and not known for each item asked and coded as 1 for a correct response and 0 for no/incorrect response. The maximum score attained in the knowledge question was 10 and the minimum score was 0. Mothers who responded above the mean or six and above questions from the total ten questions were considered as knowledgeable, and mothers who responded six and below the mean from the total ten questions were considered to have poor knowledge about formula feeding [19].

Formula feeding practice

Formula feeding practice was defined according to WHO as feeding an infant less than six months old with commercially prepared infant formula, either as a supplement or a complete substitute for breast milk during the last 24 h [29]. It was measured as yes/no and coded 1 (yes) and zero (no) for analysis. The mothers who answered “Yes” to the question were considered mothers who fed their infants with formula milk [30].

Data collection tools and techniques

A pretested interviewer-administered structured questionnaire adapted from a previous similar study was used to collect the data. The tool was developed after exhaustively reviewing relevant literature [17, 19, 31, 32]. A minor modification was made to suit the local context. The tool includes the socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants (age, marital status, occupational status, and educational status), obstetric-related factors (ANC visit, colostrum feeding, initiation of breastfeeding, place of delivery), and maternal knowledge on formula feeding. The data collectors were nine diploma nurses and two BSc Nurses from all health facilities. During data collection, supervisors checked each filled questionnaire for completeness.

Data quality control

Before the actual data collection time, the questionnaire was pre-tested on 5% (28 respondents) of the sample size at a nearby hospital to check the clarity, consistency, acceptability, and applicability of the tool. Two-day training was given to nine data collectors and two supervisors to create a common understanding of the approaches to be used during data collection. Data were checked for completeness throughout the data collection time by supervisors and data collectors, and the data completeness was also checked during entry. Besides these, the investigators carefully entered and thoroughly cleaned the data before the commencement of the analysis.

Data analysis

The collected data were entered into Epi-Data version 4.2.0.0 and exported to Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) version 25 for coding and analysis. The findings were presented using text and tables. Bivariate logistic regression analysis was carried out to assess the association between independent variables and the outcome variable, as well as to identify candidate variables for multivariable logistic regression analysis. The model’s fitness was checked using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and the model was fit (p-value > 0.05). Variables with p < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were included in the final model of multivariable analysis to control all possible confounders. The direction and strength of statistical association were measured using an odds ratio with 95% CI. Adjusted odds ratio (AOR) along with 95% CI was estimated to identify factors significantly associated with formula feeding. A P-value < 0.05 was considered to declare a statistical significance.

Ethical considerations

An ethical letter was obtained from Wolkite University College of Health Sciences ethical review committee and written informed consent was obtained from each respondent, and they were told to have the right to give up the interview at any time. The study was approved by Wolkitie University’s ethical review committee with reference number WKU/CMHS/RCS 0023/22, and it was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants

A total of 559 participants were selected, and 544 participants gave consent and participated in this study, with a response rate of 97.3%. The mean age of the study participants was 27 years (SD ± 4.9). Nearly two-thirds, 354(63.4%) of the respondents were Orthodox religion followers. Almost one-fourth, 247 (25.4%) of the respondents had attended primary education. Nearly half, 261 (48.0%) of the study participants were merchants by their occupation. Two-fifths, 209(38.4%) of the respondents had 2000–3499 Ethiopian birr average monthly income (Table 1).

Knowledge of mothers who have an infant less than six months towards formula feeding

In this study, 335(61.6%) of the participants had good knowledge about formula feeding. Furthermore, 436 (80.1%) of the participants had been aware that the practice of formula feeding is not ensuring optimal health for the baby. More than two-thirds, 376(69.1%) of the respondents had been aware that the practice of formula feeding can cause excessive weight gain for babies. Nearly four-fifths, 432(79.4%) of the study participants had responded that exclusively breast-fed children are more benefited than formula-fed children. The majority, 495(91.0%) of the participants had responded that the immune systems of breast-fed infants are more developed than formula-fed infants. Four hundred eight seven, (89.5%) of the participants had responded that formula feeding predisposes the baby to infections. Almost four-fifths, 462(84.9%) of the participants had responded that breast milk is easily digested as compared to formula milk. Four hundred seventy, 80.4% of the participants responded that breastfeeding is more convenient than formula feeding. Nearly one-fifth, 97(17.8%) of the study participants had responded that formula-feeding babies tend to be fed less frequently.

Of mothers who formula-feed their babies, just over half 106(53%) got information from the media. The remaining mothers received information from relatives 46(23.0%) and the workplace 48(24.0%). (Table 3).

Formula feeding practice of mothers with an infant under six months

In this study, 200 (36.8%) of participants reported that they undertook formula-feeding practices for their infants.

Factors associated with formula feeding practice among mothers with infants under six months

Multivariable analysis revealed that the odds of formula feeding practices were almost two times higher in women who were government-employed than those who were self-employed [AOR 2.37, 95%CI, 1.23–4.58]. Regarding the mode of delivery, participants who had given birth via the cesarean section were almost three times more likely to execute formula feeding practices [AOR 3.03, 95%CI, 1.66–5.52]. Mothers who were provided pre-lacteal feeding after childbirth were nearly fifth times more likely to practice formula-feeding than those who did not give pre-lacteal feeding [AOR 4.88, 95%CI, 3.09–7.69] (Table 4).

Discussion

It has been demonstrated that healthy and optimal nutrition at earlier stages of infants’ lives may have a critical role in promoting cognitive and physical growth, boosting immunity, reducing the risk of childhood obesity, and preventing non-communicable disease occurrence (NCDs) [33]. In this study, 61.6% of the study participants had good knowledge about formula feeding which is in line with a study conducted in Addis Ababa [34], but lower than a study conducted in Mettu town(81.4%) and Bahir Dar (79.8%.) [19, 31]. The discrepancy between these studies might be mothers with poor knowledge of formula feeding would have a probability of inadequate breastfeeding knowledge that leads them to initiate formula milk for their babies. Mothers who had inadequate knowledge of infant formula were more likely to be shaped by the effects of infant formula promotion that derives them to formula feeding practices. However, education by members of the healthcare system on aspects of formula feeding, appropriate complementary practices, and more favorable living conditions could contribute to better practices of breastfeeding.

Accordingly, this study found that 36.8% of the study participant were fed formula for their infant. The finding of this study was in line with a study conducted in Addis Ababa (39%) [34]; however, the finding of this study is higher than a study reported in Mettu town (28.4%), Dire-Dawa (21.4%), and Bahir Dar (25%) [18, 19, 31]. The discrepancy in these findings might be attributed to the difference in method used and study settings, socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants, and the availability and accessibility of the health services infrastructures. On the other hand, the result of this study was lower than the study conducted in Addis Ababa(46.2%), Jimma (47.2%), Sorro district, southern Ethiopia(49.4%), Woldia (42.7%), Mekele (68.8%), Bishoftu (65%), and Egypt (76.2%) [17, 30, 35–37]. The difference can be explained by the discrepancy in the background of the study participants and method used and study settings, the time gap, and the availability and accessibility of the infrastructures. Another possible reason might be because of the study design; some of the previous studies were community-based which may access the mothers easily and cultural variation between these studies was also another possible reason for the discrepancy. In addition to this, the health system-related factors might be contributing to this difference due to the extensive work of health extension workers and various health care institutions in awareness creation about the drawbacks of formula feeding practices in the study area. The finding implies that there is created a platform for maternity care, providers could help them to be aware of local values, beliefs, and traditions to anticipate and meet the needs of women, gain their trust, and work with them.

In the present study, women who had government employees were almost 2 times more likely to practice formula feeding than women who had self-employees. A similar finding was reported in studies conducted in Bahir Dar, Addis Ababa, and Egypt [6, 17, 31]. The reason why government employees prefer formula feeding is that they didn’t get enough time to practice exclusive breastfeeding rather they choose to feed their babies via formula feeding. In our context employed mothers after completing their four-month maternity leave returned to their work which caused an interruption of exclusive breastfeeding. This led them to practice formula feeding, and the absence of designated maternity facilities, such as lactation rooms in workplaces, and inadequate support for breastfeeding mothers may be accountable for the inadequate status of the aforementioned breastfeeding parameters [33]. The other possible reason that employed mothers practice formula feeding might be they have more income to buy infant formula than housewives.

In this study, women who had delivered via the cesarean mode of delivery were almost 3 times more likely to practice formal feeding than women who had given birth via spontaneous vaginal delivery. The findings of this study were comparable with studies conducted in Addis Ababa, Dire Dawa, Eastern Sudan, and Egypt [17, 18, 38, 39]. This may be due to post-operative conditions, as mothers with cesarean sections were less likely to have had skin-to-skin contact with their infants and felt fatigued and less relaxed after birth in the delivery room, and mothers take a long time to recover from anesthesia and additional discomfort. This leads to delayed initiation of breastfeeding, which will cause improper breast stimulation and emptying, which in turn reduces maternal milk secretion and causes the introduction of formula feeding.

There are also differences in the pre-lacteal feeding of mothers. Mothers who gave pre-lacteal feeding were five times more likely to practice formula feeding as compared to mothers who didn’t give pre-lacteal feeds for their infants. The findings of this study were consistent with the studies done in Raya Kobo district, Motta district, Debre Markos, and Addis Ababa [17, 23, 40, 41]. The possible reason is that pre-lacteal feeding delays immediate breastfeeding and decreases breast stimulation and infant suckling activity, which in turn leads to inadequate milk production. When milk production decreases the mother introduces supplementary formula for their babies which compromises milk production.

Conclusion

In this study, more than one-third of the participants practiced formula feeding and nearly two-thirds of the respondents were knowledgeable about formula feeding. In this study, being a government employee, cesarean mode of delivery, and pre-lacteal feeding history were factors significantly associated with formula feeding practices. Therefore, educating mothers about the importance of exclusive breast feeding, and supporting mothers who gave birth by cesarean section for exclusive breastfeeding should be encouraged at the community and institutional levels. The government should come up with policies that are helping women who are government employees during childbirth to promote exclusive breastfeeding. Future researchers recommended conducting qualitative research design to identify the perception and reason why mothers prefer formula feeding.

Limitations of the study

The limitation of this study is that the study might be subjected to recall bias because the mothers failed to remember what they did during the early neonatal period. This was minimized by probing the respondents about the event. Due to the nature of the cross-sectional study cause and effect relationship cannot be ascertained.
