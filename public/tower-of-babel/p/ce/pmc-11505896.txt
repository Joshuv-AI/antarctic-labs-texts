Small Molecule Assembly Agonist Alters the Dynamics of Hepatitis B Virus Core Protein Dimer and Capsid
Authors: Ravi Kant, Lye-Siang Lee, Angela Patterson, Nora Gibes, Balasubramanian Venkatakrishnan, Adam Zlotnick, Brian Bothner
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) poses a significant public health burden worldwide, encouraging the search for curative antivirals. One approach is capsid assembly modulators (CAMs), which are assembly agonists. CAMs lead to empty and defective capsids, inhibiting the formation of new viruses, and can also lead to defects in the release of the viral genome, inhibiting new infections. In this study, we employed hydrogen–deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HDX-MS) to assess the impact of one such CAM, HAP18, on HBV dimers, capsids composed of 120 (or 90) capsid protein dimers, and cross-linked capsids (xl-capsids). HDX analysis revealed hydrogen bonding networks within and between the dimers. HAP18 disrupted the hydrogen bonding network of dimers, demonstrating a previously unappreciated impact on the dimer structure. Conversely, HAP18 stabilized both unmodified and cross-linked capsids. Intriguingly, cross-linking the capsid, which was accomplished by forming disulfides between an engineered C-terminal cysteine, increased the overall rate of HDX. Moreover, HAP18 binding induced conformational changes beyond the binding sites. Our findings provide evidence for allosteric communication within and between capsid protein dimers. These results show that CAMs are capable of harnessing this allosteric network to modulate the dimer and capsid dynamics.

Introduction

The hepatitis B virus (HBV) is an icosahedral enveloped hepadnavirus. Chronic HBV infection can lead to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. The World Health Organization estimates that, as of 2022, 254 million people worldwide are living with chronic HBV, leading to 2.2 million new infections and 1.1 million deaths per year.1 In an HBV infection, an episomal form of the viral genome, located in the nucleus, is the template for viral mRNA transcribed by the host machinery. In the cytoplasm, a copy of full-length pregenomic RNA and a copy of the viral reverse transcriptase are packaged by the dimeric viral core (or capsid) protein (Cp) to form an RNA-filled core. Within the core, the RNA is reverse transcribed and digested to yield a mature core filled with circular, partially double-stranded DNA. Mature cores can either acquire an envelope for secretion or be transported to the nucleus to maintain infection. Because of this peculiar life cycle, chronic HBV can be treated with nucleos(t)ide analogues. However, such treatment may be lifelong, and almost all patients are still infected after years of treatment.2−4 In the quest for a curative treatment, one of the focuses has been the core protein and its roles in assembly and genome release.

Capsid assembly modulators (CAMs) are assembly agonists.5 These small molecules bind in a pocket at the interdimer interface of Cp dimers, where they increase the amount of the buried nonpolar surface, which can strengthen association energy and increase the rate of assembly kinetics6−10 (Figure 1). From a therapeutic perspective, CAMs can interfere with the assembly of new particles. CAM-A molecules (CAM-aberrant) distort Cp–Cp interactions, preventing the formation of intact capsids; CAM-E class (CAM-empty) accelerates assembly without distortion, resulting in intact but genomeless capsids.11 Some CAMs can lead to capsid dissociation, preventing new infection.12,13 It has been suggested that CAMs accomplish this effect by stabilizing local interdimer conformation with a geometry incompatible with an icosahedral structure.14 Different CAM molecules can have profoundly different effects on a preassembled capsid.14−17

Heteroaryldihydropyrimidines (HAPs) are a group of CAM-A molecules.18 HAPs lead to complexes enriched in hexamers that can result in the formation of tubes and sheets.5,15,19,20 Structures reveal that HAP binding causes capsid expansion and flattening of hexamers at the capsid quasi-6-fold axes, but there is variation in the tertiary and quaternary effects observed.10,15,19,21 The different CAM-bound capsid structures paint the picture of a dynamic capsid that fluctuates in response to the filling of the CAM pocket.

In a key study for understanding HBV capsid flexibility, it was observed that capsid subunits asymmetrically sample different tertiary and quaternary structures.22 This is consistent with the hypothesis that CAMs binding to preassembled capsids can lead to asymmetric capsid distortion.14 Notably, a solid-state NMR study of CAM-A molecules binding to non-cross-linked Cp found that the distinctions between the different Cp conformers were actually lost with drug binding; it was suggested that many subunits rearranged into a hexagonal lattice.23 Through a comparison of NMR relaxation rate constants, the same study observed that binding significantly increased the flexibility in the capsid spike and moderately increased the flexibility in some places in the spike base.

Hydrogen–deuterium exchange-mass spectrometry (HDX-MS) is a technique that can be used to study capsid dynamics upon CAM binding by measuring the exchange rate of protons involved in H-bonds, particularly those of the peptide backbone.24,25 HDX-MS is particularly well-suited to the investigation of large complexes and the end-state of dynamic regions affected by binding of an allosteric ligand.26 In prior work, we used HDX-MS to investigate allosteric effects during HBV capsid assembly.27 Researchers have also used HDX-MS to study antibody binding to virus components, including the HBV capsid, mapping the binding epitopes, and, in some cases, identifying antibody-induced conformational changes.28−30

In this investigation, we used HDX-MS to investigate the dynamics of a Cp dimer, a non-cross-linked capsid, and a capsid with engineered cross-links. To our surprise, we found that cross-linking broadly increased HDX, suggesting a tensegrity effect (that is, interconnections between subunits modulate reaction to perturbation). We examined Cp interaction with a CAM-A, HAP18, and found that it modulated the behavior of the dimer, a first demonstration of CAM interaction with the dimer. We were initially interested in HAP18 because it induced assembly of large tubular structures when incubated in molar excess with a free Cp dimer, suggesting a highly regular effect on Cp behavior.19 We found that this CAM modulated HDX of both the dimer and capsid.31

Results

Hydrogen Bonding Network of the Cp150 Dimer Is Dynamic

The initial building block to capsid assembly is a dimer of the core protein. An HBV capsid is constructed of 120 such dimers arranged with T = 4 icosahedral symmetry (Figure 1A).32,33 In a capsid, half of the dimers extend from 5-fold to quasi-6-fold vertices (the AB dimers), and half of them bridge quasi-6-fold vertices (the CD dimers). The individual capsid proteins have a 149 residue assembly domain (Cp149) and a C-terminal arginine-rich domain that are required for packaging RNA but not for assembly. The assembly domain is composed of five alpha helices. In a dimer, two pairs of helices (α3 and α4) from each subunit form the intradimer interface, a four-helix bundle (Figure 1B). In the context of a capsid, helix 5 from one subunit fits into a groove formed by helices 2, 3, and 5 of the neighboring subunit; this is the interdimer interface. A pocket in the groove capped by helix 5 of the neighboring subunit is the CAM-binding pocket.34 A central hydrophobic core (gray in Figure 1C), connected to peripheral regions of the dimer by glycine or proline hinges, has been described as a chassis; conformation changes of helix 5 associated with assembly appears to be coordinated by a “fulcrum” (residues 10–25) that bridges helix 4 and helix 5.35

To understand the behavior of the starting material, we began by investigating the unassembled Cp150 dimer by HDX-MS. Cp150 is a construct consisting of the Cp149 assembly domain with three cysteine (C) to alanine (A) mutations (residues 4,761,107) and a cysteine residue appended to the C-terminus.36 In capsid form, the C-terminal cysteine of the Cp150 construct is localized to 5-fold and quasi-6-fold vertices and will spontaneously oxidize to form disulfide cross-links. Cross-linked Cp150 capsids are structurally nearly identical to capsids of the native assembly domain except that they are resistant to distortions and disruptions induced by CAMs that can interfere with structural studies.15 To initiate H-D exchange, the dimer was diluted 10-fold with a deuterated buffer. Deuterium uptake was measured at four times: 0.25, 3, 30, and 360 min. The protein was maintained at low ionic strength, 50 mM NaCl, to prevent dimer–dimer interaction during the HDX reaction. After quenching with acid to stop exchange, the protein was digested with porcine pepsin and analyzed by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS).

Twenty-nine unique peptides that covered ∼95% of the Cp150 sequence were selected to follow exchange across dimer samples (Figure 2A). HDX-MS reports on the exchange of the amide hydrogen on the protein backbone with solvent. To provide a global view of the deuterium uptake of the dimer, the kinetics of exchange were plotted as a heat map that shows the time-dependent deuterium uptake across Cp150 (Figure 2A). Exchange in regions with overlapping peptides was resolved using HDExaminer, Sierra Analytics. We observed an increase in the level of deuterium uptake with time throughout the protein. Some regions showed rapid, near-complete exchange at early time points, while others showed slow exchange throughout the time course. Three regions, the middle of α3 (residues 60–65) and near the hinges preceding helix 3 and helix 5, showed little to no exchange up to 360 min. The increase in deuterium uptake over time revealed the presence of labile amide hydrogens and is indicative of conformational dynamics on the seconds to minutes time scale.

To put the exchange data into a structural context, time-dependent deuterium uptake was mapped onto a model of the dimer (Figure 2B). Peptides from the N terminus, spike tip, interdimer interface, and C terminus showed rapid deuterium exchange (≥45% at 0.25 min), suggesting a less stable structure and/or exposure to the solvent. Conversely, peptides representing the C terminus of helices α2, α3, middle of α4, and the C terminus of α5 all had low deuterium uptake (≤25% at 0.25 min), indicating a more stable hydrogen bonding network. Our HDX data is similar to the previously reported HDX of the wild-type Cp149 dimer, with minimal differences that can be attributed to the three C to A mutations within the Cp150 construct.37

HAP18 Disrupts the H-Bonding Network in Free Dimers

The capacity of HAP18 to redirect HBV dimer assembly toward tube-like structures19 prompts the question of how HAP18 influences dimer conformations. At low ionic strength, where assembly is not a significant reaction,38 we have the opportunity to examine the effect of HAP18 on free dimer or on highly labile intermediate complexes that do not progress to capsid or large aberrant structures. HDX investigations were conducted on two ratios of dimer to HAP18 (1:2 and 1:4) at low ionic strength (50 mM NaCl). Because HAP18 is a racemic mixture of R and S conformers with only the R form being active, the actual molar ratios in solution were 1:1 and 1:2. Dimers were incubated with HAP18 for 15 min at 25 °C before initiating HDX measurements. If HAP18 indeed forms a stable complex with the dimer, we would anticipate observing sustained HDX protection compared with the dimer in isolation. As before, HDX analysis for the HAP18-bound dimer was performed at time intervals of 0.25, 3, 30, and 360 min. Differences in uptake comparing Cp150 to Cp150 + HAP18 were calculated by subtracting the deuterium uptake (%). A negative difference in uptake indicates protection with HAP18 and a positive change in deprotection.

HAP18-bound dimer, at a 1:1 ratio, increased deuterium exchange within the initial 0.25 min (Figure 3). The affected regions include the N-terminus, fulcrum, C-terminus of α4 (part of the chassis), spike tip, and interdimeric interface. As time progressed, the exchange differences in the fulcrum, spike tip, and interdimeric segments decreased. The N-terminus, C-terminus of α4, and very C-terminus consistently exhibited increased deuterium uptake across all time points. At a 1:2 ratio, increased deuterium uptake was observed in the N-terminus, interdimeric interface, C-terminus of α4, and very C-terminus, although the magnitude was somewhat less than for the 1:1 ratio (Figures S1 and S2). At 360 min, there was a reversal in the HDX trend. Specifically, α1, the loop connecting α1 and α2, the N-terminus of α3, the spike tip, the N-terminus of α4, and the interdimer interface all displayed increased protection (Figure S3A). The HDX trend at 360 min was similar for both the 1:2 and 1:1 ratios (Figure S3B). This experiment shows that HAP 18 binding increases the dynamics of the dimer on a fast time scale while suppressing slow conformational changes.

Assembly Stabilizes the Hydrogen Bond Network of the Dimer

After assessing HDX of the Cp150 dimer with and without CAM, we turned our attention to the Cp150 capsid to examine how it responds to HAP18. Our analysis focused on 23 peptides from the pepsin digestion that covered ∼90% of the amino acid sequence (Figure S4); this includes peptides spanning α3 and α5 helices that were missing in previous studies.37 Capsid HDX experiments used the same four time points, 0.25, 3, 30, and 360 min, as with the dimer. Differences in uptake were calculated by subtracting the deuterium uptake (%) of the dimer from the capsid (Figure 4).

These differences are computed by subtracting the deuterium uptake % of the dimer from that of the capsid across three time points. The color scale highlights regions in red where the capsid exhibits higher deuterium uptake than the dimer and in blue, where the capsid displays lower deuterium uptake than the dimer. Regions lacking coverage are shown in gray.

Compared to the dimer, capsids show a decrease in deuterium uptake in peptides from the interdimeric interface across all time points. This indicates persistent stabilization of the H-bonding network in this region after assembly. The observed stabilization aligns with the structural model of the HBV capsid, where amino acid residues 120 to 143 within the interdimeric interface play a crucial role in stabilizing the capsid structure.32

Other regions in the capsid also had reduced deuterium exchange compared to that of the dimer. These regions include the fulcrum, the C terminus of the α4 helix (part of the chassis with protection at 0.25 and 3 min), and the spike tip (protection at 3 min) (Figure 4). However, in these regions, the total deuterium exchange at 360 min between the dimer and capsid remains similar (Figure S5). These findings suggest that in the assembled state, fast-scale motion in these regions is suppressed. Within the capsid, the N terminus showed reduced deuterium exchange at 3 and 30 min. Notably, in α2 and α5, the initial uptake at 0.25 min was similar, but reduced deuterium uptake was observed at longer time points (3, 30, and 360 min), suggesting a suppression of slower motions within the capsid (Figures 4 and S5). The deuterium exchange in the far C-terminus (143–149) shows similar deuterium uptake between the dimer and capsid structures. However, there is an increase in deuterium uptake observed at intermediate time points. This indicates that the hydrogen bonding in the far C-terminus is less stable, suggesting greater flexibility in this segment within the capsid compared to the dimer. Overall, the HDX study demonstrates that the Cp150 capsid has a more stable hydrogen bond network, leading to reduced dynamics compared to the free dimer.

Cross-Linking of the C-Termini Increases the Conformational Flexibility of the Capsid

The Cp150 mutant is an important tool in the study of molecules that modulate assembly because the C-terminal cysteines can form a disulfide cross-link that maintains capsid integrity in the presence of CAMs.8,15 This raises the obvious question of how the overall stability of the cross-linked capsid (xl-capsid) compares with the non-cross-linked form. Pepsin digestion of xl-capsid produced 19 peptides that covered ∼80% of the sequence (Figure S4B). Differences in uptake were calculated by subtracting the deuterium uptake (%) of the capsid from the xl-capsid.

Surprisingly, the spike tip exhibited more exchange in xl-capsid compared to non-cross-linked capsid. Similarly, α1, mid-α3, and the interdimer interface showed greater exchange at 0.25 min, with no difference observed at longer time points (Figures 5 and S6). The fulcrum and the connection between α4 and α5 exhibited a distinct trend of increased exchange at later time points. Overall, the data show that the H-bonding network becomes less stable, indicating a more dynamic capsid upon cross-linking. Therefore, tethering of the subunit C-termini induces a small but significant destabilization of the global H-bonding network in the capsid, though residues 144–149 always appear to be disordered in crystal and cryo-EM structures.33 This unexpected result hints at long-range communication between subunits that is more effectively transduced by a covalent disulfide bond.

HAP18 Stabilizes Hydrogen Bonding within the Capsid

We investigated the impact of HAP18 on capsid and xl-capsid. The capsid was analyzed using the same ratios of HAP18 as those for dimers. Owing to the slow uptake rate of HAP18 by the capsid, they were incubated together for three h before deuterium exchange was initiated for 0.25, 3, and 30 min. The HDX differences were determined by subtracting the deuterium uptake (%) of the capsid from that of the HAP18-bound capsid. At a 1:1 ratio, HAP18 binding resulted in a reduction of deuterium uptake in the capsid’s interdimeric interface, four-helix bundle base, fulcrum, and spike tip over a period of 0.25–30 min (Figure 6A). This finding is consistent with a crystallographic study of the Cp150 capsid, which demonstrated that HAP18 fills a gap between hydrophobic surfaces in interdimeric regions, effectively clamping them down with the assistance of α5.10 HDX protection at the binding interdimeric interface could potentially be attributed to the involvement of amide hydrogen bonds with HAP18 and/or the contribution of hydrophobic stacking between the interdimeric interface and HAP18, which may shield the exchange of hydrogen bonds with deuterium. At the 30 min time point, a decrease in deuterium uptake was observed in α2 and the N-terminus of α4. The higher concentration of HAP18 (1:2) showed similar effects, with an additional reduction in deuterium uptake observed at the α1–α2 loop, spike tip, and C-terminus regions (Figures S7 and S8). Overall, the binding of HAP18 serves to stabilize the hydrogen bonding network within the capsid, resulting in decreased dynamics.

To rule out the possibility that changes in deuterium exchange observed in HAP18-bound capsids were not due to capsid disassembly or rearrangement, we investigated the impact of HAP18 on the xl-Cp150 capsid. Using the same HAP18 ratios as in the regular Cp150 capsid, we monitored deuterium exchange at three time points: 0.25, 3, and 30 min. At a 1:1 ratio, the HAP18-bound xl-capsid showed a decrease in deuterium uptake at the interdimeric interface, fulcrum, and spike tip at all three time points (Figure 6B). Compared to the Cp150 capsid, HAP18 caused a more significant reduction in deuterium exchange in the xl-Cp150 capsid, indicating a more profound stabilization (Figure S8). The higher HAP18 ratio (1:2) did not affect the exchange rate (Figures S9 and S10). Overall, the HDX data suggests that HAP18 binds to similar sites in both the regular capsid and the xl-capsid. However, it provides enhanced conformational stabilization to the xl-capsid compared to the regular capsid.

Discussion

HBV is an endemic pathogen and an important model system for the study of capsid assembly and biophysics. These studies have made a direct contribution to the understanding of a class of antivirals directed at structural proteins, CAMs.7,8,11,14,17,27,40−53 To elucidate the mechanism of CAM-induced assembly misdirection, we conducted HDX analyses of Cp150 and xl-Cp150 in the presence and absence of HAP18.

HDX of the Dimer with HAP18

The observed HDX in Cp150 confirmed that the stability and dynamics were similar to those of Cp14929,37 with loops distal to the central chassis having the greatest exchange. CAMs affect both the rate and the product of capsid assembly reactions. They misdirect assembly to nonproductive products such as tubes and sheets by making nonicosahedral geometries kinetically accessible. HAP18 leads to increased Cp150 HDX in the C-terminus, base of the chassis, and spike tip regions, particularly at early time points. Ordinarily, binding of a ligand to a protein leads to a decrease in deuterium exchange in the region of binding. In some cases, ligand binding characterized by increased HDX has been observed and suggests that globally increased protein motion stabilizes the local interaction by balancing entropic and enthalpic contributions.49

The observed change in exchange rate indicates that CAMs can affect the behavior of Cp dimers, even in the absence of assembly. These effects were most pronounced at early time points (0.25 and 3 min), suggesting an increase in small scale, relatively rapid motion. Several structural studies have shown that HAPs bind at the interdimeric interface.10,15,50,51 MD simulations indicate that C-terminal residues in Cp149 are highly mobile.52 Further, the two peptides reporting on the region closest to the CAM binding site (123–140 and 126–140) include a significant stretch of amino acids beyond the binding site and into regions of substantial flexibility. While protection may take place at the binding site, destabilization in the more distal C-terminus could allow the overall exchange for the peptides to increase. Overall, the absence of a clearly defined binding footprint for HAP18 is likely due to an increase in the level of HDX in the C-terminal region of the peptide reporter. These allosteric changes are not altogether unexpected, as a previous HDX study on the dimer provided evidence of an allosteric network that connects the interdimeric interface to the spike tip.27

HDX of Capsids with HAP18

Our HDX-MS data indicate that the HBV capsid is less conformationally dynamic than the Cp150 dimer, which is not a surprising observation. This effect is seen in residues in the fulcrum, spike tip, part of the chassis, and the interdimeric interface. The observed protection from exchange in the chassis and the intradimer interface is similar to the pattern of protection observed by Bereszczak et al. when comparing the Cp149 dimer to capsid29,37 Helix 4, located in the intradimer interface, which has previously been shown to play a pivotal role in capsid assembly, though it is distal from the interdimer interface. Modification of the intradimer interface via a disulfide bridge or a D78S mutation causes reduced rates of assembly.53,54

The covalent cross-linking of C150 in the xl-capsids globally tethers the capsid together, preventing disassembly or rearrangement into noncapsid geometries.15 In the HDX data, we observed that cross-linking had no observable effect on the C-terminal peptide’s rate of exchange but does show an increase in exchange in the spike tip and chassis. This increase in exchange upon the addition of a molecular tether supports the presence of tensegrity within the structure.

Tensegrity, short for tensional integrity, describes a structure stabilized by having its members under tension or compression in a single network.55 In macroscopic tensegrity structures (e.g., the sculptures of Kenneth Snelson), tension is typically accomplished by a prestressed cable. Because the members of a tensegrity structure are part of a network, the structure is able to absorb perturbations by distributing the energetic cost over all members. At a molecular level, tensegrity has been used to describe the network of hydrogen bonds in small proteins as observed by molecular dynamics56 and elements of a DNA origami structure.57 In the HBV capsid, interfaces between protein subunits appear to function as tensional elements that respond to the perturbation of CAM binding, as evidenced by changes in HDX. This approach to understanding the action of CAMs on HDX is consistent with the observations that CAMs at sufficient dosage can induce capsid failure.

In contrast to the dimer, HAP18 binding to capsids leads to a reduction in H/D exchange within the interdimeric interface, spike tip, fulcrum, and four-helix bundle base. The absence of a bimodal pattern in peptides representing the HAP18 binding interdimeric interface (123–140 and 126–140) indicates that all capsids are occupied with HAP18 and the unbound state is absent (Figures S11–S14).58 This reduction suggests an increased stability of the hydrogen bonding network that supports the secondary and tertiary structures of the capsid. Furthermore, similar observations were made in the epitope mapping studies of HBV capsids. These studies demonstrated that an antibody targeting the spike tip can stabilize distal segments within the capsid. This finding further supports the presence of an allosteric network connecting the spike tip to other regions in both the xl-capsid and free capsid forms.29 There were distinct differences observed between the xl-capsid and uncross-linked capsid (Figures 7 and S9). A notable observation is the difference in HDX protection between the spike tip and the interdimeric interface in the xl-capsid compared to the capsid. Specifically, there was approximately 20% less HDX protection at the spike tip of the xl-capsid compared with the interdimeric interface (Figure 7). This indicates a stronger influence of HAP18 on the spike tip in the xl-capsid, potentially due to its allosteric network within and between dimers.27 Even within a dimer, the distance between a bound HAP18 and the spike tip (Cα of D78) is on the order of 37 Å. However, the effect of cross-linking enhances HDX, which suggests that cross-linking supports communication between subunits. Though not to the extent observed in cross-linked capsid, this concept is most likely present in non-cross-linked capsid as well. Tensegrity may explain such allostery within the capsid.15

Illustration of the H-Bonding Network

Our data indicate that HAP18, an assembly agonist, alters the dynamics of the HBV core protein dimer and capsid through allostery. The allosteric effect of HAP18 on dimers and capsids is mediated through the hydrogen bonding network, which is best visualized by examining the H-bonds in the structural models (Figure 8). The magenta lines represent all hydrogen bonds present in dimers extracted from the capsid structure. When HAP18 binds to the free dimer, it destabilizes the hydrogen bonding network (dimer + HAP18). Conversely, binding of HAP18 to the capsid stabilizes this network (capsid + HAP18). Hydrogen bonds in the blue regions are longer-lived, whereas those in the red segments are destabilized. Collectively, these changes impact the overall hydrogen bonding network, linking the HAP18 binding site to distal structural components of the capsid, such as the chassis, spike, and fulcrum. The distance between these regions and the resulting effects on the assembled capsid provide a mechanical basis for allostery.

The red regions have increased exchange, while those in blue color showed a decrease upon HAP18 binding. The HBV dimer model is from the capsid structure (PDB ID: 2g33).

Summary

This study of HBV Cp150 with HAP18 emphasizes the importance of augmenting structural studies with solution-based biophysical approaches to understand the mechanism of CAMs. Here, HDX-MS has shown how the dynamics of HBV dimers and capsids respond to HAP18. Our results reinforce the existence of an allosteric network within both the HBV dimer and capsid. Future HDX studies may further investigate the complex interactions among CAMs, capsids, and host factors to enhance our understanding of their collective impact on capsid stabilization and destabilization. In a different vein, the responsiveness of the intradimer interface to CAMs and cross-linking could be exploited in the development of HBV capsid nanocontainers with enhanced functionalities. Overall, these findings lay the groundwork for the further development of therapeutics and nanotechnology using HBV capsids.

Experimental Methods

Cp Preparation

HBV Cp149 and Cp150 were prepared as previously described.59 In brief, Escherichia coli harboring a pET11 expression plasmid that supported leaky expression were grown overnight in Terrific Broth at 37 °C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5000g for 30 min and frozen at −80 °C for later use. Frozen cells were thawed and then lysed by sonication, followed by clarification of the lysate by centrifugation at 150,000g for 1 h. Cp precipitated from the supernatant by the addition of Ammonium Sulfate to 20% w/v, mixing for 1 h, and then centrifugation at 25,000g for 1 h. The pellet was resuspended in buffer A (100 mM Tris pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT), and capsid was purified from host proteins by size exclusion chromatography (SEC) through a Sepharose CL-4B (6 cm × 70 cm) equilibrated in buffer A. The capsid peak, confirmed by SDS-PAGE, was pooled and dialyzed into buffer N (50 mM sodium bicarbonate, pH 9.6, 2 mM DTT) to prepare for dissociation. Capsids were dissociated by addition of urea to 3 M. The resulting dimer was separated from contaminants and capsid by SEC with a Sephacryl S-300 column. For Cp149, dimers were concentrated to about 2 mg/mL, dialyzed into 50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, and reassembled by addition of NaCl to 500 mM before isolation of purified capsids by SEC on an S-300 column equilibrated in buffer N. The isolated capsids were again dissociated by addition of urea to 3 M, followed by SEC to desalt and isolate dimers. Purified dimers were concentrated to about 2 mg/mL, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C.

After thawing and prior to HDX experiments, the C-terminal cysteines of Cp150 dimers were blocked with N-ethyl maleimide (NEM) to prevent cross-linking. Dimers were transferred to 50 mM HEPES at pH 7.5 by chromatography through a G25 PD10 desalting column, and NEM was added at a 10× concentration and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Excess NEM was removed with a PD10 column. Capsids were assembled by dialysis into 20 mM Tris (pH 7.0) and 300 mM NaCl. For the cross-linked capsid (xl-capsid), an unblocked dimer was used for assembly.

Hydrogen–Deuterium Exchange Mass Spectrometry

For nondeuterated experiments, 33 μM dimer was diluted 10 times in H2O-based buffer (10 mM sodium acetate, 50 mM NaCl, pH 7.4). For the capsid, 33 μM of dimer was diluted 1:10 in the same buffer but with a higher salt concentration (10 mM sodium acetate, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4). For exchange experiments, deuterium exchange was initiated by diluting 33 μM protein 1:10 into a D2O-based sodium acetate buffer. The samples were removed at 0.25, 3, 30, and 360 min. After dilution, samples were quenched by a 1:5 dilution into a 0.1% formic acid (FA) solution (to attain a final pH of ∼2.5) and porcine pepsin (2 mg/mL, Sigma). HDX quenching and digestion were conducted simultaneously over two min with vortexing every 30s. Post digestion, vials were flash frozen in liquid N2 and stored at −80 °C until LC–MS.

For HAP18 experiments, dimers and capsids were mixed with HAP18 (a racemic mixture) at molar ratios of 1:2 and 1:4. Dimer was incubated with HAP18 for 15 min in 10 mM sodium acetate, 50 mM NaCl, pH 7.4, while capsid was incubated for 3 h with HAP18 in 10 mM sodium acetate, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4.

The LC–MS experiments were performed with an Agilent 1290 ultrahigh pressure (UPLC) series chromatography system connected to an Agilent 6538 UHD-QTOF mass spectrometer. Peptides were separated on an Onyx Monolithic 100 mm × 2 mm C18 column (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA) using the following gradient; 5% B; 0–0.7 min, 5–45% B; 0.7–9.00 min, 95% B; 9–9.1 min, 98% B; 9.1–9.8 min, 5% B, 9.8–0.7 min. Solvent A: 0.1% FA (Sigma-Aldrich) in water (Honeywell/Burdick and Jackson, Morris Plains, NJ) and solvent B: 0.1% FA in acetonitrile (Burdick and Jackson). During chromatography, a low temperature was maintained by placing solvents and containers on ice. The column temperature was set at 1 °C. For the MS, the following electrospray conditions were set: drying gas flow, 8.0 L/min at 350 °C, nebulizer 55 pounds per square inch, capillary 3500 V, fragmentor 100 V using a scan range of 50–1700 m/z. Each experiment was replicated a minimum of three times.

Data Processing and Analysis

Agilent Mass Hunter B.04.00 and Bio-Confirm were used to identify unique peptides. The peptides were assigned to the protein sequence based on mass accuracy within 10 ppm error and MS/MS sequencing within 100 ppm for the fragmentation pattern. Different collision energies (25, 30, and 40 V) and a linear gradient of voltages were used to confirm the peptide identity. MS/MS data were processed and analyzed using Bruker Daltonics Bio Tools 3.2 (Bruker Daltonics Inc., Billerica, MA). Peptide shaker (CompOmics, Gent, Belgium) was also used to map the MS/MS fragmentation data.60 The peptide sequence, m/z, charge, and retention time from above were used as an input for HDExaminer Version 1.3.0 beta6 (Sierra Analytics, Modesto, CA). This software was used to determine the centroid of the peptide isotopic distribution by comparing the experimental distribution to the theoretical one. Peptides were not corrected for back exchange during the chromatography. During analysis, the first two residues of a peptide were assumed to not contribute to the number of deuterium. The statistical deuterium difference between the two states was calculated using an error propagation-based approach.61 The statistical difference between the two states was also validated by the global significance limit, which is calculated using a two-tailed t-test value (p ≤ 0.05), and the standard error of the mean was calculated from the pooled standard deviation of each state.62 The peptide-level cumulative differences were also compared with those from the propagated error-based approach. The cumulative differences greater than three times the propagated error were considered statistically significant.28,63 The HDX data were mapped on the structure using UCSF Chimera.64
