Epidemiological patterns of cutaneous leishmaniasis in north-west Pakistan (2022–2023)
Authors: Shahab Uddin, Izharullah Khan, Atta Ur Rehman, Hamid Ur Rahman, Hizbullah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCutaneous Leishmaniasis (CL), the most prevalent type of Leishmaniasis, is a significant public health problem in various regions of the world including Pakistan, resulting in huge morbidity and social stigmatization.MethodsThis retrospective study was carried out mainly in three districts: Bajaur, Malakand and Dir Lower of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KP), Pakistan. Additionally, some cases from other districts were also recorded. Patients of different localities and age groups visiting healthcare centers including District Headquarters Hospitals in Dargai, Malakand and Bajaur were included in the study, with the approval from District Health Officer (DHO) of concerned districts.ResultsIn total, 2035 cases of CL were recorded during the study period from January 2022 to February 2023. Statistical analysis revealed that the disease predominantly affected patients under 20 years of age (n = 1259, 61.9%), particularly males (n = 1067, 52.5%). The proportion of facial lesions was predominant (n = 1145, 56.3%), followed by lesions on the upper extremities (n = 317, 15.6%) and lower extremities (n = 338, 16.6%). Our findings indicate a high prevalence of CL in district Bajaur (n = 1222, 60.04%), followed by Malakand (n = 587, 28.84%) and Dir Lower (n = 153, 7.52%). Peak cases were recorded during summer and spring whereas autumn and winter showed a relatively lower incidence of CL, likely due to harsh environmental conditions that reduce the survival of sandflies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-23175-4.

Introduction

Leishmaniasis, a widely distributed disease of the tropical and subtropical regions, is caused by protozoan parasites of the genus Leishmania [1]. According to an estimate, leishmaniasis ranks as the second highest in mortality and fourth highest in morbidity among all the parasitic diseases in the tropical and sub-tropical regions [2]. This vector-borne disease is transmitted through the bite of infected female sandflies, which belong to the genus Phlebotomus in old World and the genus Lutzomyia in the New World [3]. Cutaneous Leishmaniasis (CL) is the most prevalent form of leishmaniasis, accounting for more than 50–75% of cases and is characterized by well-defined ulcerated skin lesions or nodules at the site of bite [4]. This disease is mostly associated with the poorest populations having low socioeconomic status and is often considered a neglected tropical disease (NTD) [5]. In Pakistan, Leishmania tropica and Leishmania major are the two most prevalent species contributing to the high burden of CL [6].

The history of leishmaniasis dates back to ancient times, with documentation from 1500 to 2500 BCE in Latin America and Arabic societies [7]. CL has been given various names in different civilizations such as “Baghdad boil” in Iraq, “Delhi boil” in India, and “Kal dana” in Afghanistan [8]. Currently, more than half of the world’s population is at risk of leishmaniasis. Leishmaniasis has been reported in 98 countries around the world, with an overall prevalence of 12 million cases and 70,000 deaths per year. The annual global incidence of both cutaneous and visceral leishmaniasis is about 0.7–1.2 million and 0.2–0.4 million, respectively, with 350 million people at risk worldwide [9, 10]. Literature suggest that Leishmania parasites are found worldwide, except in Antarctica and Australia [11]. It is estimated that more than 85% cases of CL have been reported in 10 countries, namely, Algeria, Brazil, Bolivia, Colombia, Tunisia, Syria, Iran, Iraq, Afghanistan and Pakistan [12].

CL is an emerging vector-borne disease and has posed a significant public health problem in Pakistan. CL is endemic throughout Pakistan, and cases are reported from every province, including Sindh, Punjab, Baluchistan, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Gilgit-Baltistan and Kashmir [13]. CL is the second most prevalent infectious disease in Pakistani population following malaria. In 2016, the burden of CL in Pakistan exceeded 400,000 cases which corresponds to almost 10% of the global burden of CL [14]. The rapid increase in prevalence of CL in Pakistan correlates with the migration of many Afghan refugees in 1988 to the north-western parts of Pakistan. However, the migration of people from these endemic border regions to cities in search of better economic opportunities further spread the disease to other parts of the country [14].

Although CL has been reported throughout Pakistan, the province of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KP) is considered to be the most afflicted by the disease. CL is endemic to all the districts of KP especially those bordering or near Afghanistan, such as Malakand division and the newly merged Federally Administered Tribal Areas (FATA) with more than 42,000 cases reported from district Khyber alone in the past decade [15]. As per WHO 2023 report, an estimated 59,255 cases of CL have been reported in Pakistan. This high burden of CL in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa is due to a combination of various factors including environmental, biological and socio-economic, for example, climate conductive to sandfly breeding, over-crowded and poor housing conditions, the presence of many Afghan refugee camps, internally displaced people due to military operations, and a lack of awareness and resources [16]. The ecology of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KP) provides highly favorable conditions for the survival and spread of sandfly vectors, responsible for leishmaniasis transmission. GIS-based habitat characterization has revealed a strong correlation between sandfly distribution and elevation, with Phlebotomus sergenti (P. sergenti) more commonly found at higher altitudes and P. papatasi in lower regions of the province [17].

There is a lack of community knowledge about the severity of the disease; as a result, CL in rural areas of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa is often referred to as “kal dana” meaning one-year lesion. Patients, instead of seeking proper medical care, often wait from 6 months to a year until the scar worsens and causes disfigurement [14]. Despite the high prevalence of CL, the disease is still underreported in various regions of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, including district Bajaur, Malakand and Dir Lower. This scarcity of valuable data regarding the prevalence and risk factors of CL further complicates the severity of the disease. Therefore, the study aims to address this gap and advise public health authorities to develop control strategies for CL in this endemic region of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa.

Methodology

Study area and ethical approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Hazara University Mansehra prior to sampling. After ethical approval, the study was mainly carried out in three selected north-western districts of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan namely Malakand, Bajaur and Dir Lower. However, patients from neighboring districts, such as Mohmand, Khyber, Mardan, and Dir Upper who visited hospitals in the area during the study period were also included. For a better understanding of disease’s geography, the study area was mapped with the help of QGIS (Quantum Geographic Information System) software (version 3.36.3), as shown in Fig. 1.

Study design and setting

This retrospective study was carried out from January 2022 to February 2023. Data for the study were collected from various hospitals and healthcare centers, including District Headquarter Hospitals in Dargai, Malakand and Bajaur, with approval from District Health Officer (DHO) of Malakand. In rural areas of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa public hospitals and DHQs are the primary healthcare access points, and most community cases report here. Our inclusion criterion consisted of patients/cases who were suspected of having CL based on clinical signs and symptoms. On the contrary, patients/cases with other skin diseases, incomplete records and those not examined by any dermatologist were excluded from the study. Google Earth Engine (GEE (https://earthengine.google.com/)) (accessed 12 Dec 2024) was used to collect and retrieve environmental data to analyze the correlation between CL and various environmental factors such as temperature, humidity, and rainfall.

Clinical examination

The patients with CL were clinically examined by specialized dermatologists and other medical officers in hospitals and healthcare centers. Clinical examination was based on the affected tissues and characteristics of the lesions, including site, appearance, and number of lesions (13). Patients were subsequently diagnosed with CL by trained dermatologists using microscopy.

Data analysis

The association of CL with its potential risk factors was observed using R Software (version 4.4.1) and Microsoft Excel 2021. To visualize the relationship between CL and various climatic factors such as humidity, temperature, and rainfall, circular heatmaps were created using the website ChiPlot (https://www.chiplot.online/ (accessed 14 Dec 2024)).

Results

During the study period from January 2022 to February 2023, a total number of 2035 cases of CL were recorded in Malakand, Bajaur, Dir Lower and other districts of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province. A detailed statistical analysis of all the variables in relation to prevalence of CL in north-west Pakistan has been mentioned in Table S1. The seasonal trends in CL in these districts were almost the same due to similar climatic conditions; however, the differences observed were due to the living and hygienic conditions of the people.

District-wise prevalence of CL

Analysis of the collected data revealed a very high prevalence of CL in district Bajaur (n = 1222, 60.05%), followed by Malakand (n = 587, 28.84%) and Dir Lower (n = 153, 7.52%), while the rest of the data (n = 73, 3.59%) were reported from other districts, including Mohmand, Khyber, Charsadda, Mardan and Dir Upper. Our statistical analysis confirmed that significant variation in CL existed across districts (p < 0.001). A detailed district wise prevalence of CL in all the districts is mentioned in Fig. 1.

Gender-wise prevalence of CL

The study observed a slight difference in case number between males and females; the ratio of males affected was generally higher than females. Of all collected cases (n = 2035), males accounted for (52.5%), while the remaining (47.5%) patients were females (Fig. 2). In Bajaur district, CL was almost equally distributed among males and females (51% vs. 49%), while in Malakand district, it was (53% vs. 47%) respectively. In district Dir Lower, males were more affected with CL as compared to females (56.8% vs. 43.2%), while same trend was also observed in other districts (61.7% vs. 38.3%). Collectively, the data showed marginally significant difference across genders (p = 0.028).

Age-wise prevalence of CL

CL was predominant (61.9%) in children aged ≤ 20 years, while less prevalent in people aged > 50 years (9.4%). Age-wise data analysis of the ≤ 20 year’s group showed a high prevalence of CL in Bajaur (62.5%), followed by Malakand (27.8%), Dir Lower (5.8%), and other districts (3.9%). The prevalence of CL in the age group 21–50 years was 53.6% in Bajaur, 32.2% in Malakand, 11.1% in Dir Lower, and 3.1% in other districts. The prevalence of CL in the age group ≥ 51 years was 63.6% in Bajaur, 25.5% in Malakand, 7.8% in Dir Lower, and 3.1% in other districts (Fig. 3). The p-value was calculated as < 0.001, showing a statistically significant association between age group and disease occurrence.

Month-wise prevalence of CL

Although cases of CL were reported throughout the year, monthly data collection suggested that majority of cases (10.8%) were reported in March 2022, followed by December 2022 (9.7%), while the minimum number of cases were reported in February 2023 (0.83%). Figure 4 shows the number of cases reported each month during the study period of 14 months, from January 2022 to February 2023. Statistical analysis revealed a highly significant difference in number of cases across months, with a p-value of < 0.001.

Seasonal trends in prevalence of CL

The highest prevalence of CL in patients with active lesions was recorded in spring 2022 (n = 556, 27.3%), followed by summer 2022 (n = 445, 21.9%) and then autumn 2022 (n = 387, 19.0%) (Table S1). However, during the winter months, the study observed a reduction in the prevalence of CL, with winter 2022 (n = 309, 15.2%) and winter 2023 (n = 338, 16.6%) (Table S1). The circular heatmap in Fig. 5 reveals significant correlations between environmental factors and CL incidence. The highest number of cases were documented during spring and summer months when temperature ranges between 20 °C and 30 °C and relative humidity levels were moderate (40–60%). Changes in rainfall patterns were observed to coincide with fluctuations in disease prevalence throughout the study period. The statistical analysis yielded a p-value < 0.001, indicating significant seasonal variation in number of cases.

Prevalence of CL with respect to localization of the lesions

Although CL lesions were found throughout the body, from head to toe, the most affected area was head, including the nose, ears, chin, cheeks, and neck, followed by the upper and lower extremities, and then other body parts (Table 2). The frequency of lesions on head was recorded to be 1145 (56.2%), on the upper extremities 317 (15.58%), on the lower extremities 338 (16.61%) and in mixed 208 (10.2%). About 88.4% of the patients were found with 1–2 lesions, and 10.2% had multiple lesions.

Variability in duration of lesions among patients of CL

The duration of lesions in patients with CL varied significantly (p < 0.001). Twenty-two percent of the patients (n = 450) had lesions for ≤ 1 month, about half of the patients (n = 1050, 51.6%) experienced lesions for 2–3 months, while 400 patients (19.65%) had lesions for 4–6 months, and in 123 patients (6.04%), lesions lasted for 7–12 months. However, the frequency of patients with lesions lasting more than 12 months was notably very low; only 12 patients (0.6%) had lesions extending beyond twelve months (Table 2).

Discussion

CL is a vector-borne parasitic disease, widely distributed in communities throughout the Mediterranean regions, Middle East and South Asia [18]. During the study, a total of 2035 cases of CL were recorded in the north-western region of Pakistan, especially in district Bajaur, district Malakand and district Dir Lower. Children under the age of 20, particularly males, were predominantly affected by CL. The high burden of CL was recorded from district Bajaur and district Malakand, while it was lower in district Dir Lower. The highest prevalence of CL was recorded in spring and summer, followed by autumn and winter, due to favorable environmental conditions for sandflies. The number of cases detected in the study was significantly higher than the previous reports from nearby regions. Iqbal et al. reported 374 cases of CL in 2021 from North Waziristan. Similarly, Irum et al. reported 750 cases from Waziristan, Anwar et al. collected 150 cases from Charsadda in 2019, while Khattak et al. reported 48 cases from Peshawar after a survey from March 2019 to July 2020 [4, 8, 19, 20]. Davoodi et al reported 161 cases of CL from Fars Province, southern Iran; however, in this study, we reported 2035 active cases of CL [21]. This high prevalence of CL in the region may be due to several factors, including rapid population growth, frequent population mobility, increasing urbanization, and poor health standards. Furthermore, advancements in diseases diagnosis and increased awareness regarding early treatment may be contributing factors. The highest prevalence of the disease was recorded in district Bajaur, which has been a war-affected zone for the past few decades, and most of the residents are internally displaced.

The current study suggested a high prevalence of CL in summer and spring, but lower in autumn and winter. Similarly, some studies have also shown a similar pattern of seasonal trends in the prevalence of CL, including Kayani et al., Haq et al., Irum et al. and Nili et al. who reported a higher prevalence of CL in summer than winter [3, 14, 19, 22]. This is likely due to the use of minimal, lightweight clothing and an increase in outdoor sleeping habits which are favorable for sandfly bites. However, in contrast to these findings, some researchers observed an opposite pattern, with higher prevalence in winter than in summer, as reported by Iqbal et al., Tadayonfar et al., and Saadene et al. [4, 23, 24]. This seasonal variation across different studies might be attributed to local climatic conditions, their effects on the activities of the vector, and the incubation period of the disease. Sandflies are highly active in humid environments and bread in places with sufficient moisture [5, 25]. The spring and summer seasons in Pakistan are hot and humid, and people in villages often sleep outdoors. Moreover, the nocturnal activity of sandflies is more closely associated with humidity than with temperature [20].

Iqbal et al., Anwar et al., Gashaw et al., Ashraf et al. and Ali et al. reported a high prevalence of CL in children and young individuals under 20 years of age and concluded that this high correlation of CL in children is due to the outdoor activities of children with minimal precautions [4, 8, 26–28]. The present study also revealed a similar trend of CL with children and young individuals, particularly those under 20 years, being predominantly affected by CL. This may be attributed to several factors, including increased outdoor exposure, immature immune responses, behavioral factors that increase the risk, and low socioeconomic conditions that limit access to preventive measures and healthcare.

CL is more common in males than females, as reported by Iqbal et al., Khurshaid et al., Irum et al., Malik et al. and Yizengaw et al. [4, 6, 19, 29, 30]. Although the present study did not observe any significant difference between males and females in Bajaur, in the other districts, the male to female ratio was comparatively higher. This is likely due to several factors, including higher occupational exposure, increased mobility and travel, less protective clothing and greater outdoor activities among males compared to females. Among lesions throughout the body, the highest prevalence was observed in the facial region, consistent with studies by Glans et al., Ullah et al., Bamorovat et al., and Karami et al. [7, 31–33]. This could be due to sleeping outdoors during the hot summer season with an exposed face, or migration due to war, which might have compromised their defense against sandfly bites during the evening and night.

In the present study the mean number of lesions per patient was calculated to be 1.80, which means that majority of the patients 88.4% were observed with only 1–2 lesions. This is somewhat higher than previous studies reported in the region such as a mean of 1.5 reported by Ullah et al. [34] and 1.65 reported by Iqbal et al. [4]. This variation in lesion counts may be due to the difference in host immunity, vector feeding behavior, shelter conditions and occupational exposure. Furthermore, the mean lesion duration recorded in our study was approximately 3.3 months, which is notably shorter than previous studies reported in the region such as 9.35 months [34], 6.15 months [20] and 4.03 months [35]. This reduction in the duration of lesions may be due to increased public health awareness, improved access to health facilities, early clinical diagnosis and treatment.

Environmental and climatic factors also play a key role in the transmission dynamics of CL, primarily by influencing the habitat, breeding cycles and activity of sandfly vectors [36]. Our study observed a peak in the number of cutaneous leishmaniasis cases during the spring and summer, likely due to moderate temperatures (20–30 °C) and relative humidity levels (40–60%), conditions favorable for sandfly proliferation. Similarly, a study from Fars Province of Iran also reported that environmental factors such as temperature, humidity and vegetation cover are significantly correlated with the incidence of CL [22]. Our study also observed that periods with increased in rainfall were closely followed by fluctuations in case number (Fig. 5), suggesting that precipitation likely affects the breeding and transmission patterns of sandflies. Similarly, a study from Colombia also reported that precipitation play a dual role, adequate rainfall creates ideal breeding conditions for sandflies, while heavy rainfall disrupt their life cycle thus affecting the transmission rate [37].

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations, resulting from its cross-sectional design and limited geographic scope. Establishing a temporal trend for leishmaniasis remained challenging in this study. We recommend that a cohort study be conducted in the future to explore the causal relationship between the risk factors and outcomes. Moreover, molecular characterization of Leishmania parasites and vector sandflies is necessary to better understand the epidemiology and transmission dynamics of CL in the area.

Conclusion

The study highlights the significance of CL as a major public health problem especially, in Bajaur, Malakand and Dir Lower. Thus, our findings emphasize the need for healthcare workers to increase awareness among the residents, improving the treatment and diagnostic facilities to prevent long term morbidity thereby reducing transmission of the disease.
