Lipoprotein(a), apolipoprotein B and other lipid biomarkers in Asian healthcare workers
Authors: Wann Jia Loh, Mon Hnin Tun, Brenda Shak, Soon Kieng Phua, Geraldine Lim, Carolyn Yap, Joan Khoo, Troy Puar, Chew Kiat Heng, Roger Foo, Gerald F Watts, Rinkoo Dalan, Tar Choon Aw

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionApolipoprotein B [apoB] and lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] concentrations are the two prime lipoprotein indices recommended by some expert consensus to assess and manage cardiovascular risk. However, their distributions, associations, inter-relationships, and clinical relevance remain un-investigated in the majority of Asian populations, particularly among healthcare workers.Aim and methodsThe distributions and relationships of serum Lp(a), apoB, and other lipid biomarker concentrations in 1,927 Asian consenting healthcare workers across ethnicities, sexes, and body mass index (BMI) were analysed. The percentage of apoB content of Lp(a) relative to apoB particle concentrations (Lp(a)-to-apoB proportion) was calculated.ResultsParticipants’ mean age was 39.4 years, mostly females (79.7%). Ethnicities were Chinese (57.2%), Malay (16.9%), Indian (9.2%), Filipino (12.8%) and others (3.8%). Distribution of Lp(a) was positively skewed to the right for all ethnicities. The median Lp(a) was 16.4 nmol/L (IQR 7.9, 41.8) and ranged between < 7 to 470 nmol/L. The proportion of participants with Lp(a) ≥ 75 nmol/L was 13.9%, and with Lp(a) ≥ 125 nmol/L was 7.8%. Multivariable linear regression analysis showed that being female, older age, and Indian ethnicity were associated with higher Lp(a) levels, whereas being male, older age, Indian ethnicity, and higher BMI were associated with higher mean apoB levels. In this cohort, 11.4% of individuals had Lp(a)-to-apoB proportion > 5%, whilst 3.3% had Lp(a)-to-apoB proportion of > 10%. Among individuals with Lp(a) ≥ 250 nmol/L, the median Lp(a)-to-apoB proportion was 14.9% (IQR 12.6,19.7). Across Lp(a) deciles, the Lp(a)-to-apoB proportions were inversely correlated with LDL-C, non-HDL-C, remnant cholesterol, and triglyceride concentrations.ConclusionAbnormal Lp(a) (≥ 75 nmol/L) was found in 13.9% of a predominantly female Asian healthcare cohort. In individuals with Lp(a) ≥ 250 nmol/L, Lp(a) particles contributed to the circulating apoB levels by a median of 15%. These findings support the notion that Lp(a) should be integrated into routine lipid assessment in Asian populations, including healthcare workers.Trial registrationclinicaltrial.gov NCT06304415.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-026-02912-7.Keywords: Lipoprotein(a), Lp(a), Apolipoprotein B, ApoBAsians, Healthcare workers, Healthcare staff, Lipids, Lipoproteins, Cardiovascular risk

Introduction

A recent analysis of the UK Biobank population-based prospective cohort study found that lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] and total apolipoprotein B (apoB) concentrations were the two strongest lipid biomarkers that were independently associated with an increased risk of coronary artery disease, more so than very low density lipoproteins (VLDL) and low density lipoproteins (LDL) particles concentrations [1]. Elevated Lp(a) is a risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) and calcific aortic valve stenosis, with recommendations for routine Lp(a) testing in clinical practice sequentially echoed by various expert bodies [2–7]. While testing is prioritised in individuals at high and very high cardiovascular risk and their family members, [2] the European, Canadian and National Lipid Association guidelines and a few others have also recommended testing of Lp(a) in all adults in the general population to facilitate early cardiovascular prevention strategies [4, 5, 8]. The high prevalence of elevated Lp(a) ≥ 125 nmol/L (≈ 50 mg/dL), affecting 20% of the general population globally and 30% of patients with ASCVD, supports the notion that this common risk factor should not continue to be ignored [3, 9].

Successful integration of Lp(a) into patient care is challenging and requires a prior understanding of the lipid distribution and potential impact of screening programs of Lp(a) [2]. As a starting point, the lipid distribution of healthcare workers who are fronting the testing and management of any new and existing health policies would be incredibly informative in multiple ways, e.g. well-being, informing implementation strategies, and awareness. The distributions of Lp(a) concentration vary significantly across ethnicities [10, 11]. The total apoB concentration reflects the total number of circulating atherogenic particles and is considered a better lipid biomarker of cardiovascular risk than LDL-C or total cholesterol alone [12]. Due to the additional pro-atherogenic effects of apolipoprotein(a) and pro-inflammatory effects of oxidised phospholipids carried by Lp(a) particles, an assessment of the contribution of Lp(a) to apoB concentrations is helpful to understand the potential additional cardiovascular risk posed by elevated Lp(a) [13–15].

Therefore, an understanding of the distribution of Lp(a), apoB and other lipid biomarkers, as well as the prevalence of elevated Lp(a) in various ethnic populations is crucial for ethnic dependent-disease risk analysis, clinical relevance, and regional implementation studies; yet this remains uninvestigated in most ethnicities in Asia and not reported before in Southeast Asia. Apart from ethnicity, the epidemiology and distribution of Lp(a) and apoB also remain unclear across different genders and body mass index (BMI) groups in Asians. This study hypothesised that among healthcare workers in Asia, Lp(a) contributes to clinically significant apoB concentrations across various ethnicities. Therefore, this study aimed to analyse the distribution of Lp(a) and apoB using a particle-based approach, and to investigate the relationship of the contribution of Lp(a) to the total apoB particle concentrations across varying LDL-C, triglyceride, non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C), and remnant cholesterol concentrations in Southeast Asian healthcare workers.

Methods

Study population and design

The LPACO (LP(a) in healthCare wOrkers) study is a prospective, observational multi-ethnic study of adults working in healthcare settings in Singapore that aims to investigate the distribution of blood lipid biomarkers including Lp(a) (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06304415). The study was conducted in accordance with Helsinki principles, approved by the institutional review board (CIRB 2023–2651), and all participants signed a written informed consent form. Open invitations were sent to all adult staff (age 21 and above) working in Changi General Hospital, other restructured hospitals, clinics, and medical institutes. The participants of this study included staffs from nursing, allied health (e.g. pharmacist, physiotherapist, radiographer), administrative (e.g. executive, manager, coordinator), ancillary (e.g. healthcare assistant, patient service associate, cooks), and medical doctors of all ranks.

A total of 1927 individuals participated in this study from the 1 st of April till 31 st Dec 2024. Mode of recruitment were either via an on-site dedicated research clinic or at health screening events for healthcare workers. Changi General Hospital is a busy tertiary hospital with over 1000 inpatient beds located in eastern Singapore serving 20% of the population. In mid-2024, a once-yearly health-screening event was held in Changi General Hospital, where a total of 1402 out of 2673 healthcare staff who joined the health-screening event consented to participate in the LPACO study. The rest of the study participants consented to join LPACO at the research clinic and consisted of healthcare staff working in any part of Singapore, with the majority working in Changi General hospital. Study participants answered a dedicated questionnaire that included full medical history and family history and medications via face-to-face interviews with research coordinators. The study participants’ reported comorbidities and medications were cross-checked with the electronic medical records whenever available.

Definitions

Lipid-lowering medications include statins, fibrates, ezetimibe, bile acid sequestrants, and agents inhibiting proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) pathways. Dietary history included omega-3 fish oil supplements, but they were not classified as lipid-lowering medications. Using the definition by the European Atherosclerosis Society (EAS) and National Lipid Association (NLA), elevated Lp(a) was defined as serum Lp(a) concentration ≥ 125 nmol/L and abnormal or mildly elevated Lp(a) was Lp(a) ≥ 75 nmol/L [3, 16]. As previously published, serum Lp(a) concentration ≥ 120 nmol/L and ≥ 70 nmol/L were clinically relevant thresholds associated with increased cardiovascular risk, the prevalence of abnormal Lp(a) at these thresholds are also reported [17]. Regarding apoB concentrations, thresholds apoB ≥ 90 mg/dL were used because this was the median apoB for National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) cohort [12] as well as the LPACO cohort. Triglyceride levels < 1.2 mmol/L (100 mg/dL) were regarded as optimal [18]. Proportions of participants with elevated LDL-C ≥ 3.4 mmol/L were described. Smoker refers to participants who were actively smoking. The alcohol variable was coded as present when participants answered that alcohol was regularly consumed. The exercise variable was coded as present when exercise was undertaken regularly.

Biochemical parameters

Random venous blood samples (fasting not required) were collected at the time of study recruitment, at a single time point, for measurement of blood lipid profiles in the hospital laboratory. Serum Lp(a) concentrations were measured using the Roche particle-enhanced turbidimetric immunoassay [Tina-quant Lp(a) Gen.2 (Latex) on the Cobas C502] with coefficient variation (CV) of < 2.2% [19]. The lower quantitation limit of detection for Lp(a) was 7 nmol/L, and there were 27 individuals with < 7 nmol/L. For statistical analysis, the value < 7 nmol/L was substituted with half of limit of detection value. The apolipoprotein B (apoB) was measured by immunoturbidimetry (APOBT v2) on the Roche Cobas C502 with CV < 2.4%. All other lipids and lipoproteins were measured on the Roche Cobas C702 by enzyme colorimetry (total cholesterol, HDL-C [CV < 1.8%]), triglyceride (CV < 2.0%). Direct low-density lipoprotein cholesterol concentrations (LDL-C) were measured directly by homogeneous enzymatic colorimetry with a CV of < 2.1%. Serum creatinine (CV 1.2%) and glucose (CV 1%) were measured by enzymatic method on the Roche Cobas 8000 analyser. HbA1c was measured on the Roche Cobas C513 by turbidimetric inhibition immunoassay with a CV of < 1.2%.

Non-HDL-C was calculated by subtracting the HDL-C from the total cholesterol value. The remnant cholesterol concentration was calculated by subtracting the direct LDL-C from the non-HDL-C. The estimation of apoB content from Lp(a) particles was calculated using the division of Lp(a) by apoB (both in nmol/L) multiplied by 100 and is referred to as Lp(a)-to-apoB proportions in this report. As apoB was measured in mg/dL, a conversion factor to nmol/l was used by multiplying by 20, as endorsed by the American Heart Association [20] which assumes an estimated molecular weight of 500 kDA [21, 22].

Statistical analyses

The age groups were classified into 4 groups: 20–29 years,30–39 years, 40–49 years, 50 years and above. The body mass index groups were divided into 3 groups: BMI < 23 kg/m2 (normal weight), BMI 23 to < 27.5 kg/m2 (overweight) and BMI ≥ 27.5 kg/m2 (obesity). To evaluate the relationship between Lp(a)-to-apoB proportions with Lp(a) stratified by sex and age, Lp(a) concentrations are divided into 3 groups for this analysis: 75–124 nmol/L, 125–249 nmol/L, and ≥ 250 nmol/L. The ethnic groups were Chinese, Malay, Indian, Filipino, and Others. The ‘Others’ ethnicities group was of a small sample size (n = 74, 3.8%) and consisted of individuals from Myanmar, Eurasian, Sikh, Javanese, Boyanese, Bhutanese, Thai, Vietnamese, Arab, Singhalese, Sri Lankan, Ceylonese, Iban, and Kadazan. The triglyceride concentrations were classified into 2 groups according to severity: triglyceride < 1.2 mmol/L (n = 1088, 56.4%) and triglyceride ≥ 1.2 mmol/L (n = 840, 43.6%).

Continuous variables not conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Continuous variables with normal distributions were expressed as means and standard deviation (SD). Chi-square test was used for categorical variables, t-test for comparison of means, and Mann–Whitney U-test for comparison of medians of different groups. ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test was used to assess statistical significance across multiple groups. Differences among groups were first assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction. As the distributions of Lp(a) are significantly skewed to the right, natural logarithmic transformation of Lp(a) was used to normalise distributions for regression analyses. Multivariable linear regression analysis was used to assess the association between age, BMI, sex, and ethnicity with ln-transformed Lp(a) and apoB, respectively. Statistical analyses were undertaken using STATA version 16.1 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). A two-sided p value of < 0.05 was taken as statistically significant. Graphs and visualizations were generated using Python version 3.11.12.

The predominant age group was the 30–39 years group (38%), followed by the 20–29 years (21.4%), 40–49 years (21.2%), and ≥ 50 years (19.4%). The majority of study participants (75%) did not report any major comorbidities; Only 3.8% had a known diabetes mellitus diagnosis, 7.8% had known hypertension, 9.4% had known hyperlipidaemia, 0.5% ischaemic heart disease, 0.3% stroke, 0.2% peripheral artery disease, and 0.1% had chronic kidney disease. Likewise, only a minority of study participants reported taking lipid-lowering agents: 7.2% were on statins, 0.2% on fibrates, and 0.3% on ezetimibe. The youngest participant taking statins was 32 years old. All individuals taking fibrate or ezetimibe were also taking a statin. As a supplement, 15.5% reported consuming omega-3 fish oil. None were on agents that inhibit the PCSK9 pathway, such as evolocumab, alirocumab, or inclisiran. Similarly, none reported taking glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists or sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 inhibitors. Among the study cohort, 38 participants reported a thyroid-related medical history such as hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, subclinical thyroid state, or thyroid nodule.

Lipoprotein(a)

Distribution of Lp(a) concentrations was positively skewed for all major ethnicities, ranging from < 7 nmol/L to 470 nmol/L, and percentiles are shown in Fig. 1. The median Lp(a) was 16.4 nmol/L (IQR 7.9, 41.8), shown in Table 1. The proportion of participants with, Lp(a) ≥ 70 nmol/L was 15.2%, Lp(a) ≥ 75 nmol/L was 13.9%, Lp(a) ≥ 120 nmol/L was 8.2% and Lp(a) ≥ 125 nmol/L was 7.8%. Among the 5 groups of ethnicities, the Indian group had the highest median Lp(a) level, followed by Others, Malays, Chinese, and Filipino (31 nmol/L, 22.1 nmol/L, 18.9 nmol/L, 15.4 nmol/L, and 11.1 nmol/L, respectively) with a statistical significance for trend across ethnicities (p < 0.001, Table 1).

Distribution patterns of Lp(a) and apoB concentrations

Female participants had higher median Lp(a) level than males (16.9 vs 14.2 nmol/L, p = 0.016, shown in Fig. 1 and Table 2). The proportions of individuals with Lp(a) ≥ 75 nmol/L and Lp(a) ≥ 125 nmol/L were higher in females than in males (Supplementary Fig. 3). The distributions of Lp(a) and apoB concentrations in females and males are shown in Table 2. For females (n = 1536), the median Lp(a) was highest at the age group ≥ 50 years at 19.5 nmol/L. Among females, the ethnicities with the highest median Lp(a) were Indian, followed by Others, Malay, Chinese, and Filipino. Conversely, for males (n = 391), the highest median Lp(a) was at the youngest age group of 20–29 years. Among males, the ethnicities with higher median Lp(a) in descending order were Indian, followed by Malay, Chinese, Others, and Filipino. Multivariable regression analysis found that females, older age and ethnicities were associated with higher log-transformed Lp(a) levels whereas BMI was not associated with Lp(a) (Supplementary Table 3).

Apolipoprotein B

ApoB concentrations were normally distributed for all ethnicities, ranging from 30 to 171 mg/dL (percentiles shown in Fig. 1). The mean apoB was 91.4 ± 21.7 mg/dL, median apoB was 89 (IQR 76,104) mg/dL (Table 1). The proportions of participants with elevated apoB ≥ 90 mg/dL was 49.2%, and those with apoB ≥ 120 mg/dL were 10.8%. Among ethnicities, Indian group had the highest median apoB level (95 mg/dL), followed by Malay, Filipino, Chinese and Others (92 mg/dL, 90 mg/dL, 87 mg/dL and 82 mg/dL respectively), p < 0.001 (Table 1).

Among females, the 95th and 99th percentiles of apoB concentration were 128 mg/dL and 153 mg/dL, respectively (Fig. 1). Among females, the range of apoB was from 30 mg/dL to 166 mg/dL, 47.2% with apoB ≥ 90 mg/dL, and 9.8% with elevated apoB ≥ 120 mg/dL (Table 2). The apoB distribution was similar for females not on lipid-lowering medications, 9.8% (140/1435) with elevated apoB ≥ 120 mg/dL (Table 2).

Among males, apoB ranged from 42 to 171 mg/dL, and 14.8% had elevated apoB ≥ 120 mg/dL (Table 2). The 95th and 99th percentiles of apoB concentration were 138 mg/dL and 157 mg/dL, respectively, in the male group (Fig. 1). Among males not on lipid-lowering medications, 16.4% with elevated apoB ≥ 120 mg/dL. Multivariable regression analysis showed that higher age (p < 0.001), higher BMI (p < 0.001), male sex (p < 0.001), and Indian ethnicity(p = 0.011), when compared to Chinese ethnicity, were variables associated with higher mean apoB levels (Supplementary Table 3). Adjusting for statin use in the regression analyses did not alter these relationships.

Cholesterol concentrations from direct LDL, non-HDL, and remnant cholesterol

The distributions of direct LDL-C, non-HDL cholesterol, and remnant cholesterol concentrations were normal, unlike that of Lp(a), as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. The mean LDL-C was 3.1 ± 0.8 mmol/L, the mean non-HDL-C was 3.5 ± 0.9 mmol/L, and the mean remnant cholesterol concentration was 0.39 ± 0.3 mmol/L (Table 1). Compared with males, females had lower mean LDL-C (3.2 vs 3.1 mmol/L, p = 0.021), non-HDL-C (3.7 vs 3.5 mmol/L, p < 0.001), and remnant cholesterol concentration (0.48 mmol/L vs 0.38 mmol/L, p < 0.001) (Table 2). The mean LDL-C was lower in patients taking lipid-lowering medications compared with those not taking lipid-lowering medications (2.5 ± 0.7 mmol/L vs 3.1 ± 0.8 mmol/L, p < 0.001).

The patterns for these lipid distributions in median and interquartile ranges stratified by age groups, BMI ranges, and ethnicities are shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 2. Comparing between ethnicities, the highest mean LDL-C was in the Malays (3.21 mmol/L), followed by Indian (3.18 mmol/L), Filipinos (3.11 mmol/L), and Chinese (3.08 mmol/L). For non-HDL-C, Indians had the highest mean non-HDL-C (3.64 mmol/L), followed by Malays (3.61 mmol/L), Filipinos (3.53 mmol/L), Chinese (3.45 mmol/L), and Others group (3.4 mmol/L). For remnant cholesterol concentrations, Indians had the highest mean concentrations at 0.46 mmol/L, followed by Filipinos (0.42 mmol/L), Malays (0.40 mmol/L), Others (0.39 mmol/L), and Chinese (0.38 mmol/L). Chinese participants had lower values than those of all other ethnic groups for non-HDL-C (p = 0.004) and remnant cholesterol (p < 0.001).

Lp(a) in relation to apoB concentration

There was a positive correlation between Lp(a) with apoB (regression coefficient β = 0.92, p < 0.0001) and no significant correlation between Lp(a) and triglyceride. The median proportion of Lp(a) to apoB (nmol/L over nmol/L) was 0.9 (IQR 0.5, 2.3), range 0.2–25.7 (Table 1). The Lp(a)-to-apoB proportions increase with increasing Lp(a) concentrations (p < 0.001, Fig. 4). Within the highest decile of Lp(a) (168 to 470 nmol/L), the median proportion of Lp(a)-to-apoB was 10.3% (IQR 9.1%,12.6%) (Supplementary Table 1). In 11.4% of the study cohort, Lp(a) particles accounted for at least 5% of their total apoB. In 3.3% of the study cohort, Lp(a) particles accounted for at least 10% of their total apoB. In individuals with Lp(a) > 95th percentile (168 nmol/L), the median Lp(a)-to-apoB % was 10.34% (IQR: 9.15, 12.64%). The Lp(a)-to-apoB proportion of > 7% affected 7.5% (n = 146) of this cohort.

Across the Lp(a) deciles, the median Lp(a)-to-apoB proportions were statistically significantly different across ethnicities and sexes but not age group (p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 1). Lp(a) and apoB proportions in relation to triglyceride concentration

As shown in Fig. 4, the relationship of Lp(a) and apoB was affected by different triglyceride concentrations, with a significant positive association observed when triglyceride < 1.2 mmol/L (β = 1.16, p < 0.001, n = 1087), a moderate association when 1.2 ≤ triglyceride < 5 mmol/L (β = 0.79, p = 0.012, n = 825), and no significant association when triglyceride ≥ 5 mmol/L (β = 2.38, p = 0.228, n = 15). When stratified by triglyceride, the Lp(a)-to-apoB proportions was inversely correlated with triglyceride concentrations, with the lowest triglyceride concentration group (triglyceride < 1.2 mmol/L) having a median Lp(a)-to-apoB of 1.06 (0.54, 2.59), followed by the 1.2 ≤ triglyceride < 5 mmol/L group with a median of 0.76 (0.45, 1.94), and the highest triglyceride concentration group (triglyceride ≥ 5) having a median of 0.87 (0.32, 3.07) across all the Lp(a) groups. Across Lp(a) deciles, Lp(a)-to-apoB was inversely correlated with non-HDL, LDL-C, and remnant cholesterol concentrations (p < 0.001), reflecting the reduced contribution of Lp(a) towards total circulating apoB with the increase in other non-Lp(a) apoB-containing particle concentrations.

Discussion

In this multi-ethnic cohort of Asian healthcare workers, Lp(a) has a right-skewed distribution, unlike the normal distributions of other lipid biomarkers. Lp(a) contributed 0–25% of total apoB, depending on Lp(a) concentrations in this study. The prevalence of elevated Lp(a) ≥ 125 nmol/L of 7.8% (or Lp(a) ≥ 120 nmol/L of 8.2%) reported here coincided with the prevalence of Lp(a) > 50 mg/dL (≈100–125 nmol/L) of 8.4% in a recently published large study of 2.9 million health screening results in China [23]. Nevertheless, the prevalence of elevated Lp(a) ≥ 125 nmol/L among healthcare workers is a notable finding, the prevalence being similar to that of diabetes mellitus in Singapore’s local general population (8.5%) [24]. This data on Southeast Asians support the observation that the prevalence of elevated Lp(a) is lower in other Asian ancestries (3 to < 20%) than in White and Black ancestries (20–30%) [11, 17, 19, 23]. As Lp(a) is linearly associated with increased ASCVD risk, the prevalence of 13.9% at the lower threshold of abnormal Lp(a) ≥ 75 nmol/L (30 mg/dL) among healthcare workers is worth noting. Apart from ethnic variations, this study showed that female sex and older age (likely contributed by menopause [25]), but not BMI, were associated with increasing Lp(a) concentrations. Additionally, the contribution of Lp(a) to apoB concentrations was markedly increased from 5.2% to 14.9% with increasing Lp(a) categories (75–124 nmol/L, 125–249 nmol/L, and ≥ 250 nmol/L), highlighting that the highest applicability of this ratio in a clinical setting is when Lp(a) concentration is very high. This study also reported that the contribution of Lp(a) to circulating apoB concentrations decreases with age, highlighting the relative contribution of other apoB-containing lipoproteins at older age.

In a recently published study of a US medical claims database of approximately 270,000 individuals with ASCVD, it was found that higher Lp(a) was associated with recurrent ASCVD events; the Lp(a) levels were higher in patients with Black ancestry, followed by Hispanic and White [26]. The Interheart study of 7 ethnicities reported that median Lp(a) was highest among Blacks, South Asians and Latin Americans, followed by Southeast Asians and Arabs, and lowest among Europeans and Chinese [10]. This current study showed that the median Lp(a) of Southeast Asian healthcare workers (16.3 nmol/L) was lower than the reported median Lp(a) of the general population of Blacks (75 nmol/L) and Whites (19 nmol/L) but similar to that of the Chinese (16 nmol/L) from the UK Biobank [11]. As Southeast Asians comprises of multiple ancestries with markedly different genetic make-up, it is worth noting that the Lp(a) distribution significantly differed among major ethnicities in this region suggesting the importance of assessment of individual ethnicities; In this study, the median Lp(a) of Indians (31 nmol/L) was approximately 3 times higher than of Filipino (11.1 nmol/L), and 2 times of Chinese (15.4 nmol/L). Similarly, the same pattern of ethnic variation of Lp(a) distribution among Indians (highest), Malays, and Chinese was also observed in other local cohort studies in patients with ischaemic heart disease (prevalence Lp(a) > 120 nmol/L of 15.7%) [19], and patients with angiographic coronary artery disease (prevalence Lp(a) > 120 nmol/L of 10.7%) [17]. Thus, the dyslipidaemia of Lp(a) and apoB affected minority ethnicities of Singapore (Indians and Malays) more than the predominant ethnicity (Chinese). This important observation could help inform local cardiovascular modification strategies.

Each atherogenic lipid particle carries one apoB100, with variable cholesterol content across circulating lipid particle types [12]. This unique property of apoB makes it an informative tool and generally more accurate than solitary assessments of non-HDL-C, LDL-C, or remnant cholesterol for evaluating overall atherogenic lipid status [12, 27]. However, apoB, when interpreted singularly, has limited value because apoB alone is unable to differentiate Lp(a), LDL-C, and triglyceride-rich apo-B100-containing lipoproteins (VLDL and IDL). Recent genetic analyses suggest that per-particle Lp(a) is 6 times more atherogenic than an LDL particle, whereas triglyceride-rich lipoproteins and remnants are 4 times more atherogenic than LDL [15, 28]. However, LDL particles remain the predominant atherogenic lipoproteins among all apoB-100 carrying lipoproteins because of their overabundance [1]. Interpretation of apoB should include Lp(a) because of the additional cardiovascular risk conferred by Lp(a) beyond apoB; A recent analysis of UK Biobank data found that with every 100 nmol/L increase in concentrations Lp(a) compared with apoB, Lp(a) was associated with a 3.3-fold increased risk of coronary artery disease [1]. Taken together, the interpretation of apoB in conjunction with LDL-C, Lp(a), and possibly non-HDL-C is helpful in identifying discordance among lipid measurements [12].

With increasing Lp(a) particle concentration, the contribution of Lp(a) towards total circulating apoB concentrations increases. In the highest Lp(a) decile (Lp(a) ≥ 168 nmol/L), Lp(a)-to-apoB proportions are > 10% and thus may be helpful to gauge therapeutic interventions pending the availability of effective Lp(a)-lowering agents. Although Lp(a) increases modestly with age, the sub-analysis suggests that Lp(a) contribution to apoB decreases with increasing age, particularly in the Lp(a) 75–125 nmol/L group, highlighting the importance of other apoB-containing lipoproteins with increasing age. However, this study is limited by a small sample size of patients with extremely high Lp(a) levels, and larger studies are needed to confirm this observation. The Lp(a)-to-apoB ratio may be helpful in some scenarios; for example, a low Lp(a)-to-apoB ratio paired with a high Lp(a) level may indicate a need to focus on triglyceride-rich lipoproteins, such as VLDL-C and chylomicrons. This is because Lp(a)-to-apoB proportions are affected by triglyceride-rich lipoproteins concentrations, which could confound interpretation. As shown in this study and in a study by Chilazi et al. (2022), this relationship is modified by triglyceride-rich lipoprotein concentrations, owing to the greater contribution of VLDL and LDL particles to total apoB concentrations [21]. Using data from an extensive US database of laboratory readings, the authors reported that median Lp(a)-to-apoB proportions of 2.7% in a cohort with a median Lp(a) of 47 nmol/L [21]. The authors suggested that a Lp(a)-to-apoB proportion threshold of > 7% would be clinically relevant because it corresponded to an elevated Lp(a) level above 125 nmol/L [21]. However, in this study, the Lp(a)-to-apoB variable is unlikely to be equally as meaningful as a general tool, as the median Lp(a) of 16.3 nmol/L in this study cohort was much lower, with a correspondingly low median Lp(a)-to-apoB of 0.9% observed. Currently, the role of Lp(a)-to-apoB proportion as a biomarker is not mentioned in any medical guideline. Further work is necessary to investigate the clinical utility of the Lp(a)-to-apoB proportion as a biomarker and its threshold in clinical practice among patients with elevated Lp(a) and in high cardiovascular risk cohorts.

Lipid thresholds for borderline-intermediate cardiovascular risk population include LDL-C < 2.6 mmol/L, non-HDL-C < 3.4 mmol/L, and apoB < 90 mg/dL [12], noting there are other suggested apoB thresholds (e.g., 100 mg/dL) [8]. The 2024 NLA consensus reported a median apoB of 90 mg/dL in untreated 12,696 adults from NHANES, which is the same as the median observed in this study [12]. However, some studies suggest that considerations of ethnicity influence (or population-specific models) in distribution and cardiovascular risk for treatment targets or risk prediction algorithms are necessary concerning non-HDL-C targets, given the varying triglyceride concentrations and non-HDL-C levels across populations [29–31]. Similarly, further work is needed to evaluate the adequacy and utility of apoB thresholds across other ethnicities and populations, including healthcare worker cohorts [32]. This is because the mean LDL-C in the LPACO cohort, 3.1 mmol/L (including or excluding participants on lipid-lowering treatment), is significantly lower than the Singapore National Population Health Survey (2024) report, which reported a mean LDL-C of 4.6 mmol/L for newly diagnosed hypercholesterolaemia [33]. The prevalence of hypercholesterolaemia (defined as LDL-C ≥ 4.1 mmol/L) in the LPACO cohort is also lower than that of Singapore’s general population (11.1% vs 30.5%) [33]. Apart from Lp(a), apoB, and non-HDL-C, a meta-analysis has shown that a proxy for triglyceride-rich lipoproteins, using calculated remnant cholesterol concentrations ≥ 0.65 mmol/l, is associated with a 1.5-fold increased ASCVD risk and mortality compared with those with lower remnant cholesterol concentrations [34, 35]. However, with the expanding list of lipid biomarkers into a ‘lipid panel’ in clinical practice, further rationalization of indications, monitoring frequency, and cost-effectiveness is required, taking into account clinical utility and feasibility [27, 31]. Regardless, integration of Lp(a) testing and management into routine clinical practice is highly recommended [2–7]. Co-ordinated strategies are needed to address the significant gaps of under-testing, poor awareness and misconceptions on Lp(a) among healthcare professionals and patients [36–39].

This study’s strengths included providing key insights into lipid distributions among the 4 major ethnicities that hold the fort of Singapore’s healthcare workforce and may help inform the implementation of Lp(a) in clinical practice. Studies of healthcare workers, with a predominant focus on women, such as the Nurses’ Health Study, have been highly useful in elucidating associations between risk factors and outcomes and in informing global health policies for women [40]. While the modifiable risk factors, such as smoking, may differ significantly between healthcare workers and non-healthcare workers, Lp(a) is considered a non-modifiable risk factor as 80–90% of the Lp(a) concentration is genetically determined [14]. Therefore, this study’s findings of the prevalence of elevated Lp(a) remain useful as a starting point for implementation and intervention studies and future policies related to Lp(a). This study is predominantly focused on women (79.7%). Because this study focuses on adults working in healthcare settings, a higher proportion of women was expected to be recruited. However, a high rate of women's recruitment is also a strength of this study, as it helps supplement the major deficit in women's representation in clinical studies and the medical literature, particularly among young and working women [41]. A better understanding of cardiovascular risk profiles, including lipid profiles in women, risk factors specific to women, and common risk factors that are more harmful to women, is necessary to facilitate the adoption of individualized precision care [25, 41, 42]. For example, Ridker et al. (2024) found that the combined measurement of LDL-C, Lp(a), and C-reactive protein (CRP) had a better predictive value for cardiovascular risk in women over a 30-year period than conventional 10-year risk prediction models [43]. Other strengths of this study include the short recruitment period, which allows analysis of lipid profiles relevant to current living status, and the uniform measurement using a relatively isoform-insensitive Lp(a) assay that quantifies Lp(a) in molar rather than mass concentration. Future studies, including longitudinal studies in Asian populations and women, are needed to evaluate the causal relationships and intervention effects of Lp(a) and apoB on cardiovascular disease risk.

This study has limitations. A limitation of this study is that the findings may not be generalisable to the local or regional general populations. This study cohort primarily consisted of consenting Southeast Asian healthcare workers, who, on average, have healthier lifestyles (e.g., low smoking rates, regular exercise, low alcohol consumption), higher health awareness and health literacy, and possibly different stress levels than the broader unselected general population. Another limitation of this study was that ultracentrifugation, or nuclear magnetic resonance, was not used to accurately quantify lipid concentrations of LDL, VLDL, IDL, and apoB. However, this method was not necessary for this study, as this study’s aim was to provide a descriptive analysis of these lipid biomarkers using common, commercially available, robust immunoassays to facilitate future work on their clinical applicability. Blood tests were collected regardless of fasting status to reflect real-world data, as fasting is no longer mandatory for lipid profile testing [44]. The mild implication of non-fasting blood would be that the total apoB concentration measured includes both apoB-48 and apoB-100. However, the contribution of apoB-48 to apoB measurement is likely minimal unless there is severe chylomicronaemia [21]. When taken in the fed state, the total apoB measured by immunoassay would include apoB-48 containing lipoproteins (chylomicrons and remnants), although these are not considered directly atherogenic. The small sample size of male participants in this study precludes statistical sub-analysis of sex differences of Lp(a) and apoB ratios, especially among individuals with high Lp(a) groups, and further studies are required.

Conclusion

In summary, elevated Lp(a) concentrations affect a significant proportion of this predominantly female cohort of Asian healthcare workers. Lp(a) contributes to circulating total apoB concentrations in individuals with high and extremely high Lp(a) concentrations. This study’s findings suggest the need to integrate Lp(a) testing at least once for healthcare workers and underscore the need for further study in the general Asian populations. While the results of ongoing cardiovascular outcome trials of effective and specific Lp(a)-lowering treatments are awaited, the management of elevated Lp(a) focuses heavily on overall modifiable cardiovascular risk factor control to facilitate overall ASCVD risk reduction [3, 14]. This would include reducing LDL-C to healthier levels, avoiding excess body weight, and controlling blood pressure, all of which are common cardiovascular risk factors even among healthcare workers.
