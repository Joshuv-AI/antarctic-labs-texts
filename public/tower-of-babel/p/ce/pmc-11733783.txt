Racial and ethnic minority representation in dementia risk factor research: a scoping review of cohort studies
Authors: Arjun Krishnan, Anupa Pathak, Thomas Blyth Nicholas, Jeffrey Lee, Louise Waite, Fiona Stanaway
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite a potentially greater burden of dementia, racial and ethnic minority populations around the world may be more likely to be excluded from research examining risk factors for incident dementia. We aimed to systematically investigate and quantify racial and ethnic minority representation in dementia risk factor research.MethodsWe performed a two-stage systematic search of databases—MEDLINE (Ovid SP), Embase (Ovid SP) and Scopus—from inception to March 2021 to identify population-based cohort studies looking at risk factors for dementia incidence. We included cohort studies which were population-based and incorporated a clinical dementia diagnosis.ResultsOut of the 97 identified cohort studies, fewer than half (40 studies; 41%) reported the race or ethnicity of participants and just under one-third (29 studies; 30%) reported the inclusion of racial and ethnic minority groups. We found that inadequate reporting frequently prevented assessment of selection bias and only six studies that included racial and ethnic minority participants were at low risk for measurement bias in dementia diagnosis. In cohort studies including a multiethnic cohort, only 182 out of 337 publications incorporated race or ethnicity in data analysis—predominantly (90%) through adjustment for race or ethnicity as a confounder. Only 14 publications (4.2% of all publications reviewed) provided evidence about drivers of any observed inequalities.ConclusionsRacial and ethnic minority representation in dementia risk factor research is inadequate. Comparisons of dementia risk between different racial and ethnic groups are likely hampered by significant selection and measurement bias. Moreover, the focus on ‘adjusting out’ the effect of race and ethnicity as a confounder prevents understanding of underlying drivers of observed inequalities. There is a pressing need to fundamentally change the way race, ethnicity and the inclusion of racial and ethnic minorities are considered in research if health inequalities are to be adequately addressed.

Introduction

Dementia is a global health problem with an expected increased burden within the coming years. Dementia burden may be disproportionately greater within certain racial and ethnic minority populations, with socioeconomic disadvantage an important driver.1 Studies in the USA, for instance, frequently report a higher incidence of dementia among African American and Hispanic adults2 3 while data from the UK show a higher burden within the African Caribbean population.4

The 2020 Lancet Commission report on dementia prevention, intervention and care emphasises the importance of examining and addressing sociodemographic risk factors within racial and ethnic minority groups which may be driving differences in dementia incidence.1 However, older persons from racial and ethnic minority backgrounds are frequently under-represented in research.5 6 Consequently, the burden of dementia in many racial and ethnic minority groups may not have been quantified and, by extension, there is likely limited examination of how differences in risk factors may be contributing to unequal burdens of disease.7 8

Further complicating our understanding of differences in dementia risk across different racial and ethnic groups is the impact of measurement and selection bias that can influence comparisons between groups. This encompasses factors such as underdiagnosis and delays in diagnosis when administrative data are used to identify dementia as well as differences in recruitment, loss to follow-up and missing data that result in selection bias. Equally pertinent is examining the way race and ethnicity are incorporated in data analysis. This includes practices such as amalgamating diverse groups into a single non-meaningful category and the frequent practice of only adjusting for race or ethnicity as a confounding variable rather than investigating drivers of observed disparities.9 10 Evaluating the extent to which these practices are adopted in dementia risk factor research is, therefore, an essential consideration in examining how well-represented racial and ethnic minority groups are within this research space.

To our knowledge, the extent of representation of racial and ethnic minority participants in dementia risk factor research has not been examined or well-characterised previously. Recent studies have focused primarily on identifying barriers to adequate inclusion of racial and ethnic minority populations as well as evaluating strategies to address under-representation.11 12 Robustly quantifying and deconstructing how racial and ethnic minority groups are represented within the current body of dementia risk factor research and scrutinising how factors such as reporting of measurement and selection bias can influence estimates of dementia burden and risk is equally important if the conclusions drawn from this kind of research is to inform future public health policy and interventions. Our review was thus conducted to understand the limitations in the evidence base and identify how future research should be conducted to better quantify and understand the drivers of racial and ethnic inequities in dementia burden.

Methods

The protocol for this review has been published and elucidates in detail our methodological approach which is described in brief below.13

Search strategy and eligibility criteria

Given the immense volume of research within this space, our search strategy incorporated two separate stages (figure 1). We first identified systematic reviews of risk factors for dementia to efficiently locate potential cohort studies for inclusion in our review. We supplemented this by examining lists of cohort studies of ageing and neurodegenerative disease published online and confirmed the comprehensiveness of our list with an expert in the field (author LW). The second phase of our search aimed to identify more recent cohort studies that may have been missed in the first search. We selected a start date for this search based on the search end date of our included systematic reviews. We chose the earliest date out of those systematic reviews which had focused on major dementia risk factors and had incorporated a comprehensive search strategy.

PRISMA diagram of two-stage systematic search. PRISMA, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses

Searches were conducted through MEDLINE (via Ovid SP), EMBASE (via Ovid SP) and Scopus and the search strategies were developed in partnership with a Medical Librarian (online supplemental appendix 1). The first stage of our search protocol identified systematic reviews from the inception of databases through to 5 August 2020. Our search of primary cohort study publications retrieved records from 1 June 2017 to 11 March 2021. All records located through our systematic search were imported into Covidence. Two reviewers (AK and FS) independently screened titles and abstracts and assessed full-text articles to evaluate eligibility for inclusion. A third reviewer (LW) was consulted where needed.

We included population-based cohort studies of ageing looking at any risk factor for dementia incidence. We excluded studies that specifically recruited individuals with particular conditions (such as diabetes or hypertension) at baseline. There were no geographical or language restrictions. We excluded studies that only measured cognitive impairment. Dementia diagnosis requires some form of clinical assessment and needs to be defined according to established criteria such the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders or International Classification of Disease codes. Given the known undercounting of dementia based on hospital admissions and deaths,14 we only included data linkage studies that incorporated a combination of inpatient (eg, hospital admission records) and outpatient (eg, primary care visits) data sources. We included both cohort studies that did and did not incorporate racial or ethnic minority representation to establish an appropriate denominator for estimating the frequency of inclusion.

Only cohort studies with at least three relevant publications were selected for inclusion to ensure that our analysis was addressing the larger and more influential cohort studies within the field—particularly where consideration of racial and ethnic minority populations is concerned. These criteria were introduced as our comprehensive search yielded a significant number of cohort studies—often unnamed—with single or few publications which would significantly impact our reported proportions in a way that would not be reflective of the overall evidence base (see online supplemental appendix 3 for list of excluded studies). In instances where multiple publications originated from the same cohort study, we collected information from both the original cohort profile report (or demographic description from cohort study websites if available) as well as publications about dementia risk factors. Furthermore, to ensure that the denominators reported in our analyses of publications from cohort studies which included racial and ethnic minority participants were accurate, we performed an additional search of cohort study websites and databases specifically searching for dementia-related publications from these cohort studies through to February 2022.

Data analysis

Data from systematic reviews and individual cohort study publications were extracted by three reviewers (AK, FS and TBN) using standardised forms.

We defined racial and ethnic minorities as groups who are numerically smaller and possess ethnic, religious or linguistic characteristics that are different from those in the majority population within a given location.15 Further details of our evaluation framework have been published previously.13 In brief, we first evaluated if the race or ethnicity of cohort study participants was described and whether this was measured appropriately. We then examined if selection and recruitment directly or indirectly excluded or underincluded racial and ethnic minority participants. Finally, we assessed the potential for measurement and selection bias in the comparison of findings by race and ethnicity and evaluated how race and ethnicity were included in the statistical analyses. We also performed logistic regression to examine if cohort study characteristics (location, funding source and year of publication) were associated with greater racial and ethnic minority representation.

Patient and public involvement

There was no patient or public involvement in conducting this review.

Results

Our search of online databases yielded 8864 records (after removal of duplicates) that underwent title and abstract screening (figure 1). We reviewed 1429 records for full-text screening—of which 968 were excluded. We reviewed primary studies included in 340 systematic reviews (phase 1 of search) and 121 individual publications from cohort studies (phase 2) to ultimately produce a list of 97 cohort studies that met our inclusion criteria (online supplemental appendices 2 and 3). Most studies were based in Europe (45 studies; 46%) and North America (31 studies; 32%).

Less than half of the included studies (40 cohort studies; 41%) reported the race or ethnicity of participants (figure 2). Of these, only 29 studies included or reported racial and ethnic minority persons in their description of participants (table 1).1644 The majority of these studies were from North America and the racial or ethnic groups most frequently included were African Americans and Hispanic Americans. In the remaining 11 studies, while a description of the ethnicity of the sample was included (eg, all white Danish), this did not include any participants from racial or ethnic minority groups.4555 Two of these studies directly excluded or underincluded racial or ethnic minority participants by limiting inclusion by country of birth and English language proficiency48 50 while 5 studies indirectly excluded or underincluded racial and ethnic minority participants as a result of being undertaken in locations where fewer racial and ethnic minority groups were present than there would be in the population as a whole.4547 5153

Among the 29 cohort studies reporting inclusion of racial or ethnic minority groups, 9 studies (31%) directly excluded or underincluded racial and ethnic minority participants.17 20 24 30 35 37 40 42 44 These studies (from North America, Australia and the UK) restricted inclusion on the basis of citizenship status,24 35 37 or incorporated fluency with English as part of their eligibility criteria.17 20 30 37 40 42 44 Five cohort studies (17%) indirectly excluded or underincluded minority participants as they reported recruiting samples from geographic regions (eg, rural or remote areas) or time periods where racial or ethnic minorities were under-represented.22 27 31 32 38 In two studies,34 41 a determination of direct/indirect exclusion or underinclusion of racial and ethnic minority participants could not be made due to lack of information on the ethnic composition of the study area, study eligibility criteria and recruitment processes. The remaining 13 cohort studies did not report any study eligibility criteria or recruitment processes that were likely to exclude or underinclude racial or ethnic minority participants.16 18 19 21 23 25 26 28 29 33 36 39 43

Measurement and reporting of race or ethnicity

Only 15 studies out of 40 (38%) reporting the race or ethnicity of participants (15% out of the total 97 included studies) used the recommended self-reported approach to measuring ethnicity or race.1619 21 23 26 28 3133 35 38 39 42 44 50 The remainder of studies either used country of birth as a proxy (3 studies; 8%)24 27 36 or made no explicit mention of how race and ethnicity was measured (16 studies; 40%). Two studies indicated that researchers evaluated ethnicity through interviews with no specific details provided.20 52 One study used information from military records25 and another collected information from telephone directories and cultural clubs.29

We also considered how race and ethnicity were categorised within the 29 cohort studies including racial and ethnic minority participants. The Framingham Heart Study (FHS) represented a unique case where though the latter waves of recruitment (OMNI 1 and OMNI2) included racial and ethnic minority participants, all of the publications we evaluated were based on the original cohort which was racially and ethnically homogeneous.22 As such, we excluded FHS and its publications from the remainder of our analysis. Out of the remaining 28 studies, 4 cohort studies focused on a single racial or ethnic group (eg, Japanese American) and hence there was no categorisation of ethnicity.25 26 29 33 In a further six studies, although there was variation in the way race or ethnicity was categorised within individual publications, in the majority of analyses several diverse minority groups were amalgamated into a single group.16 17 27 34 38 44 For instance, several studies from the USA and the UK grouped all racial and ethnic minority persons into a single ‘non-white’ category for analysis.16 17 38 44

Selection and measurement bias

We evaluated the risk of selection bias by race or ethnicity in the 24 cohort studies which included more than one racial or ethnic group and as a result could potentially make some sort of comparison by race and/or ethnicity (table 2). In most instances (15 studies, 63%), the risk of selection bias was unclear due to a lack of reporting of recruitment methods, response rates, follow-up and missing data by race and ethnicity. Of the remaining nine studies, eight were deemed to be at high risk of selection bias while only one study was classified as low risk. The studies assessed as high risk had greater missing data and lower response rates among racial and ethnic minority participants.

The risk of measurement bias for the outcome of dementia was evaluated in the 28 cohort studies reporting inclusion of racial and ethnic minority participants (table 2). 13 of these studies (39%) were deemed to have an unclear risk of bias due to a lack of sufficient information about how dementia was assessed including whether assessments were blinded to race or ethnicity. Nine studies were deemed at high risk of bias including three studies with dementia ascertainment through linked data. We classified these studies as high risk as linked administrative data relies on the use of health services and would, therefore, underestimate dementia in racial and ethnic minority populations with reduced access to health services.56 57 Six studies—all from the USA—were determined to be at low risk of bias due to the use of culturally and linguistically adapted assessments. Among these, three studies which included a multiethnic sample also ensured that subjective diagnoses of dementia (such as those made through a consensus panel) were made blind to the race or ethnicity of participants.31 32 43

Analysis of race or ethnicity data

We also examined all publications related to dementia risk factors from the 28 cohort studies including racial and ethnic minority participants to evaluate how ethnicity was incorporated into data analyses. We analysed this aspect by publication rather than cohort study as different publications from the same cohort study often used different approaches. We evaluated a total of 394 publications from 28 cohort studies, including 337 publications from 24 cohort studies that included more than one racial or ethnic group (figure 2). The remaining 57 publications came from the 4 cohort studies that included a single racial or ethnic group (eg, Japanese American).25 26 29 33 We make this distinction as only studies including more than one racial or ethnic group make comparisons by race or ethnicity as part of their analysis. Of the 337 publications from multiethnic samples, 91 studies (27%) did not make any mention of race or ethnicity (including in the table of characteristics) and did not consider race or ethnicity in the data analysis. 64 of the 337 publications (19%) reported the diversity of the sample but did not consider race or ethnicity in the data analysis. Of the 182 remaining publications which did consider race or ethnicity in the data analysis, the majority (119 publications; 65%) analysed ethnicity or race solely as a confounder. A further 45 publications out of 182 (25%), predominantly analysed ethnicity or race as a confounder but also performed analyses stratified by ethnicity or race or tested for interactions by race or ethnicity. Only 14 publications of the 337 (4.2%) conducted analyses aimed at understanding potential drivers of racial and ethnic disparities in dementia.

Studies which reported the inclusion of racial and ethnic minority participants came from one of six countries: Australia, the USA, the UK, Singapore, Sweden and Israel (table 1 and figure 3). Only two cohort studies in Australia included racial and ethnic minorities with the proportion being 40%–45% in the Health in Men Study (HIMS)24 and 29% in the Sydney Memory and Ageing Study.37 Neither study specifically reported the proportion of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander participants included. Not considering studies which focused exclusively on a single racial or ethnic group, cohort studies from the USA varied considerably in the proportion of racial and ethnic minority participants included in the sample. Proportions ranged from 2% in the Monogahela Valley Independent Elders Survey30 through to 67%–80% in the Washington Heights Inwood and Columbia Aging Project (WHICAP).43 Nine cohort studies included a sample of racial and ethnic minority participants that was in the range of approximately 25%.16 18 20 21 23 28 31 39 42 From the two UK cohort studies,41 44 only a single publication from each reported the ethnic composition to an extent which allowed assessment of how representative the sample may be with 10% in the Whitehall II Study and 33% from the UK Clinical Practice Datalink (CPRD).58 59 The Singapore Longitudinal Ageing Studies34—the only study from Asia with a diverse sample—reported approximately 5% of the sample to be from racial or ethnic minority backgrounds; comprising mainly Malays and Indians. Only one publication from the Swedish National Patient Registry—the only study from Europe—reported 14% of the sample to be migrants with no further characterisation of their specific race or ethnicity.60 The Israeli Ischaemic Heart Disease Study reported racial and ethnic minority status based on country of birth with 85% of participants born in central and eastern Europe, the Balkan countries, the Middle East and Northern Africa.27

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive review of racial and ethnic minority representation in cohort studies of dementia risk factors. In addition to quantifying the lack of adequate representation, our results are unique as they also assess the potential for selection and measurement bias that could be impacting on comparisons made between ethnic and racial groups. We have also examined the adequacy of analytical approaches used to understand drivers of observed disparities. Our results show that, to date, there has been minimal representation of racial and ethnic minority groups in dementia research, and even fewer studies have produced results that could be used to address any observed racial and ethnic inequalities. Our results point towards global inadequacies in the way that race and ethnicity are considered and handled in dementia risk factor research. First and foremost, the proportion of studies which have reported the race or ethnicity of their participants is low (41%) and the proportion of studies which have included racial or ethnic minority participants is even lower (30%).

Aside from in the USA, we found that very few publications from cohort studies in areas of the world with a racially and ethnically diverse ageing population included a potentially representative proportion of racial and ethnic minority participants. For instance, Australia is one of the most ethnically diverse countries in the world with 40% of the population reporting non-Anglo Celtic ancestry, 22% non-European ancestry and 3.2% identifying as Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples.61 62 The population aged 65 years and above is particularly diverse, with 37% born outside of Australia.63 Only HIMS included a sample that could potentially be representative of the national population; though this assessment cannot be determined exactly as publications from this study only used an ‘overseas-born’ category as a proxy for ethnicity which is common practice in Australia.24 In the UK, non-white racial and ethnic minorities account for approximately 6.4% of the population over 65 years of age.64 Only a single publication from each of the UK studies—CPRD and WHII—reported inclusion of a potentially representative sample.58 59 In the USA, approximately 23% of the population aged over 65 are racial and ethnic minority members.65 Nine cohort studies included a sample of racial and ethnic minority participants that was approximately representative of the population.16 18 20 21 23 28 31 39 42 However, it is important to note that included racial and ethnic minority groups were predominantly African Americans and Hispanic Americans, with a notable absence of Asian participants who account for approximately 5% of the older population in the USA.65

Concerningly, despite the growing racial and ethnic diversity of the older population in countries such as Australia, Canada and the UK, almost the entirety of evidence relating to differences in risk factors and dementia burden within racial and ethnic minority participants comes from the USA and is restricted to African American, Hispanic and Japanese American minority groups. Other specific racial and ethnic minority populations from around the world have been almost entirely neglected in dementia research. This is despite indications that some racial and ethnic minority populations—such as Black and South Asian groups in the UK—may experience a greater burden of dementia.66 67 Even more concerning, our findings show that Indigenous populations have been entirely excluded from longitudinal dementia research. Indigenous populations frequently experience greater levels of disadvantage that may greatly elevate their risk of dementia, and they are, therefore, important populations to include in research.68 In Australia, for instance, dementia incidence data from the Koori Growing Old Well Study have recently been published and indicate a substantially higher burden of dementia than the rest of the Australian population.69 70 Indigenous populations in other parts of the world have been similarly neglected in dementia risk factor research and there is clearly a need for such disparities to be addressed as a matter of priority.

Another common issue in the field is the lack of granularity in ethnic and racial groups reported in many studies. For example, in the only two Australian studies reporting and including a multiethnic sample, only a proxy of ethnicity—country of birth—was used and all migrants from Northern Europe, Asia, North America, South America, Africa and the Middle East were grouped into a non-Australian-born category and Indigenous Australians were grouped with the rest of the Australian-born category.24 37 In the two studies from the UK, participants were also dichotomised as ‘white’ and ‘non-white’.41 44 While this amalgamation of diverse groups is usually driven by small sample sizes, it is important to acknowledge that the categorisation of race and ethnicity in this way is only useful for interpreting risk factor and outcome relationships for the majority population as it removes heterogeneity.10 71 It does not provide meaningful results for racial and ethnic minority group members when groups with diverse risk factor and outcome profiles are combined into the one category.10 71

Inclusion of racial and ethnic minority populations in meaningful data analysis requires a sufficient sample size of these participants and this can often be expensive—particularly where translation into other languages is required—and practically challenging. The approach some studies in the USA have taken—such as the Honolulu-Asia Ageing Study (HAAS)25 investigating dementia risk factors and incidence specifically within Japanese-American men living in Hawaii or the Sacramento Area Latino Study on Aging33 looking at Hispanic populations in rural and suburban countries in California—has been to recruit participants exclusively from a specific racial or ethnic minority population. The benefit of such an approach is that the potential for measurement bias is substantially mitigated by the protocolised use of culturally specific study materials and processes. It is also of value in identifying important risk factors for dementia within a particular racial or ethnic group. However, including only a single racial or ethnic population in studies precludes direct comparison of disease burden and makes understanding the root causes of ethnic inequalities more difficult. An alternative to this may be to tailor recruitment in population-based cohort studies to address either known ethnic inequalities in dementia or expected inequalities based on the distribution of other risk factors, comorbidities and level of disadvantage. This conceptually driven approach may warrant oversampling of certain racial or ethnic populations to ensure that subsequent analyses based on ethnicity are sufficiently powered to derive robust results. This could be a useful approach in countries such as Australia where the diverse older population is not dominated by a small number of racial or ethnic groups.

Another approach to tackling under-representation of racial and ethnic minorities in health research is policy based and demonstrated by the 1993 National Institutes of Health Revitalization Act from the USA, which mandates the inclusion of racial and ethnic minority populations in federally funded biomedical research.72 Such an approach may offer benefit for countries such as the UK and Australia which currently hold no such legislation despite having a racially and ethnically diverse ageing population.

Our study also importantly highlights a lack of adequate reporting of the potential for selection or measurement bias when making comparisons by race and ethnicity. The importance of adequate reporting is underlined by our findings that in the few instances where missing data and loss to follow-up by race and ethnicity were reported, the risk of selection bias was high due to increased missing data and loss to follow-up in racial and ethnic minority participants. Similarly, given known cultural and racial biases in dementia diagnosis,73 74 it is an essential starting point for investigating racial and ethnic inequalities in dementia that we are sure that our measures of dementia incidence are not biased by race and ethnicity. However, few studies even considered the potential for this bias in their study design processes.

Finally, another key finding from our results is the almost ubiquitous practice of adjusting for race and ethnicity as a confounder along with other demographic variables such as age, sex and education status—with 90% of all publications from cohort studies including a multiethnic sample using this approach in their analyses. Rather than ‘adjusting out’ the effect of race and ethnicity, drawing attention to differences in dementia incidence and identifying what the potential underlying determinants may be is essential to generate the evidence needed to address inequalities.9 For instance, the WHICAP43—which included a large number of African American and Hispanic American participants—was able to clearly demonstrate by estimation of population attributable risks for different risk factors that a much greater amount of the burden of dementia in the Hispanic American population was due to diabetes.75 This kind of data is invaluable as it does not just provide data on what risk factors are driving dementia but also what risk factors are driving dementia disparities. This is essential information for policy-makers that allows a targeted focus on the drivers of inequalities in disease incidence.76

While our review incorporates a sensitive search strategy that has systematically identified a comprehensive list of cohort studies, there is a possibility that cohort studies conceived more recently may not have been identified or included due to too few publications. However, our work remains relevant to the main body of dementia risk factor research and the list of studies we have evaluated has been checked by a content expert (LW) to confirm comprehensive coverage of the field. Furthermore, we did not perform risk of bias assessments as this was a scoping review and our evaluation framework focused more so on the impact of how selection and measurement bias are reported—considerations which hold greater relevance when reporting differences in dementia risk by race and ethnicity. To our knowledge, this is the first work to robustly characterise how racial and ethnic minority populations are represented in dementia risk factor research—including detailed consideration of how race and ethnicity are incorporated into data analysis. Our findings specifically highlight where important changes are needed in future research practice if inequalities are to be better understood and addressed.

Exclusion of racial and ethnic minority populations from health research is arguably a form of institutional racism.6 The consequences of this are all the more dire when we consider the evidence of significantly poorer health outcomes some racial and ethnic minority populations experience across the spectrum of disease. The recent large disparities in mortality from COVID-19 in the USA and UK are a telling example of this.77 Much evidence around racial and ethnic inequalities in dementia is poorly characterised and will continue to remain so until we change the way we consider race and ethnicity as well as the importance of inclusion and representation. Aside from limiting the external validity and generalisability of research findings, exclusion or underinclusion of racial and ethnic minority groups—be it inadvertent or not—has serious implications for social justice as the outcomes of health research are often tied to the allocation of resources for services, public health interventions and future research. The lack of focus on producing research results aimed at understanding drivers of disparities, even when racial and ethnic minority participants are included in research, further exacerbates the lack of evidence in this field. The impetus is therefore on researchers and governing bodies throughout the world to, if necessary, enact measures at a systematic level to improve racial and ethnic minority representation in research so that research findings and translated public health initiatives serve all members of the population.
