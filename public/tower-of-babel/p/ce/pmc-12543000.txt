Development and Validation of a Simulation Model–Based Tool to Support Individualized Physical Activity Discussions and Prescriptions for Breast Cancer Survivors
Authors: Jinani C Jayasekera, Oliver WA Wilson, Clyde Schechter, Jennifer L Caswell Jin, Kaitlyn M Wojcik, Nicolien T van Ravesteyn, Jonathan Wall, Jacob Schneider, Lia L D'Addario, Janise M Roh, Swarnavo Sarkar, Lisa Cadmus-Bertram, John P Pierce, Amy Trentham-Dietz, Lawrence H Kushi, Charles E Matthews
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSEClinical guidelines recommend offering individualized physical activity prescriptions to cancer survivors. However, there are limited tools to support individualized physical activity discussions and prescriptions. We developed and validated a simulation model–based tool to estimate individualized survival outcomes for postdiagnosis physical activity among postmenopausal breast cancer survivors.METHODSWe adapted an established simulation modeling approach developed within the Cancer Intervention and Surveillance Modeling Network to estimate breast cancer–specific and all-cause survival associated with postdiagnosis physical activity for 50- to 75-year-old (postmenopausal) women with stage I to III invasive breast cancer. Model estimates were generated for 60,480 subgroups based on age, weight status (BMI), stage, tumor subtype, treatment, aerobic (<30 min/wk [no/minimal], ≥30 to <150 min/wk [insufficient], ≥150 to <300 min/wk [active], ≥300 min/wk [highly active]), and muscle-strengthening (<2 or ≥2 d/wk) activity. The outcomes were 10-year survival and absolute survival benefits for different levels of physical activity by individual characteristics and treatment. Model inputs were derived from trials, cohort studies, registry, and surveillance data. External validation used independent data.RESULTSSurvival rates and absolute benefits for physical activity varied by age, weight status, stage, tumor subtype, and amount and type of activity. For example, the 10-year breast cancer–specific and all-cause survival for no/minimal activity in a 65- to 69-year-old-woman with stage II, hormone receptor–positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2–negative breast cancer with obesity was 79.2% and 72.2%, respectively. Increasing aerobic activity from no/minimal to insufficient activity with <2 d/wk of muscle-strengthening was associated with absolute increases in 10-year breast cancer–specific and all-cause survival by 2.8 and 3.4 percentage points, respectively. The model closely replicated survival rates in independent data.CONCLUSIONSimulation model–based estimates could support clinical tools for guideline-recommended individualized discussions and physical activity prescriptions for breast cancer survivors.

INTRODUCTION

Physical activity may offer several clinical benefits to breast cancer survivors including improvements in quality of life, functional ability, cardiovascular health, and survival.1-8 However, national-level data suggest that only 38% of US women diagnosed with breast cancer engage in guideline-recommended levels of aerobic physical activity (eg, walking), whereas only about 17% meet muscle-strengthening (eg, resistance-training) guidelines.9 Studies show that patient-provider discussions could increase physical activity participation and referral uptake among breast cancer survivors.10

Accordingly, current clinical guidelines recommend that clinicians offer individualized physical activity prescriptions to cancer survivors.11,12 The new ASCO Cancer Treatment and Survivorship Care Plan for Breast Cancer includes a component for clinicians to discuss physical activity with their patients.13 However, in the United States, <50% of breast cancer survivors currently report having discussed or received advice about physical activity from a health care provider14 and fewer than 10% of survivors use referrals to exercise programs.15 Clinicians report lack of time, confidence, and knowledge as major barriers to physical activity discussions in clinical settings.16

Clinical decision tools considering patient characteristics, treatment, and health outcomes associated with physical activity could increase clinician's confidence to discuss, educate, encourage, and provide physical activity prescriptions for breast cancer survivors.17-19 However, to our knowledge, currently, there are no tools that provide individualized outcomes associated with different amounts and types of physical activity for breast cancer survivors seen in clinical practice.20,21

In this study, we adapted an established discrete-event simulation modeling approach22-25 to generate individualized survival outcomes and absolute benefits associated with different levels of physical activity for women aged 50-75 years diagnosed with stage I to III breast cancer in the United States. We aimed to provide simulation model–based estimates for a clinical decision tool that could help clinicians discuss the benefits of physical activity considering a woman's clinical and tumor characteristics, breast cancer treatment, and survival. The overarching goal of this study was to support guideline-recommended patient-provider discussions to gradually increase postdiagnosis physical activity and improve overall survival among breast cancer survivors.

METHODS

Overview of the Simulation Model

We adapted a previously developed and validated discrete-event simulation modeling approach for breast cancer, which was created in collaboration with the Cancer Intervention and Surveillance Modeling Network.22-25 This model has been used to simulate clinical trial protocols and estimate treatment outcomes for populations of US women diagnosed with early-stage breast cancer.22-25 In this study, we used a similar approach to model the effects of postdiagnosis physical activity on breast cancer–specific and all-cause survival for US women diagnosed with stage I to III invasive breast cancer (Supplementary Methods I).

We simulated women with combinations of age, stage at diagnosis (American Joint Committee on Cancer, Version 6), estrogen (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) status, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) status, weight status (derived from BMI), and breast cancer treatment from the joint frequency distributions of these characteristics as a simple random sample. Breast cancer–specific and all-cause survival was modeled for different amounts and types of physical activities considering age, weight, stage, tumor subtype (ie, ER/PR/HER2 status), and systemic therapy which included hormonal therapy (tamoxifen, aromatase inhibitors), chemotherapy (cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, and fluorouracil; anthracycline; taxanes), and HER2-targeted therapy (trastuzumab). The model was calibrated to the SEER breast cancer mortality rates (Data Supplement, Fig S1).26

Modeled Population

The simulation included female patients age 50-75 years (postmenopausal) diagnosed with stage I to III invasive breast cancer. Due to limited data, we were unable to model the effects of physical activity in women with ductal carcinoma in situ or metastatic breast cancer. Women starting at age 50 years were included in this analysis as 83% of invasive breast cancers are diagnosed in this age group,27 and age 50 years is commonly used as a proxy for postmenopausal status. Furthermore, the clinical considerations for recommending physical activity may differ based on the distributions of tumor characteristics and treatment in women younger than 50 years (premenopausal).

Physical Activity

Physical activity was simulated according to the Kaiser Permanente Pathways Study,28 in which physical activity was repeatedly measured at 1, 2, and 6 years after a primary breast cancer diagnosis using the validated Arizona Activity Frequency Questionnaire (AAFQ).29,30 The AAFQ provides self-reported frequency, intensity, and duration of participation for 43 aerobic (household/recreational/transport domains) and recreational muscle-strengthening activities.

The amounts of aerobic and muscle-strengthening activities were determined based on the 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans and Exercise Guidelines for Cancer Survivors.12,31 Accordingly, aerobic activity included no/minimal activity (equivalent to <30 min/wk of moderate-intensity physical activity [MPA]), insufficient activity (≥30 to <150 min/wk of MPA), active (≥150 to <300 min/wk of MPA), and highly active (≥300 min/wk of MPA, ie, ≥double the recommended aerobic guidelines). Guidelines also recommend muscle-strengthening activities on ≥2 d/wk.12 Accordingly, muscle-strengthening included not meeting guidelines (<2 d/wk) and meeting guidelines (≥2 d/wk). Survival outcomes were estimated for eight levels of physical activity considering these different amounts of weekly aerobic and muscle-strengthening activities.

Modeled Outcomes

Simulated patients were randomly assigned to eight levels of weekly aerobic and muscle-strengthening activities 6 months after breast cancer diagnosis and then followed until death. The simulated women could show adherence to an assigned level of activity up to 1, 2, or 6 years and then move to an age-specific level of physical activity based on the national distribution in US women with a history of breast cancer. Survival with up to 2 years of adherence to a physical activity level was presented in the base-case analysis. Simulated women could die of breast cancer or die of other causes depending on their combination of unique clinical/tumor characteristics, treatment, and physical activity. The primary end point of interest was time from diagnosis to death.

Statistical Analysis

Distributions of clinical (age, weight) and tumor (stage, tumor subtype) characteristics and physical activity levels in the simulated sample were compared with national-level data. Ten-year breast cancer and all-cause survival rates were estimated using Kaplan-Meier curves for overall and patient subgroups. Absolute benefits were calculated by comparing 10-year survival rates across increasing levels of physical activity (eg, increasing aerobic activity from no/minimal to active).

Subgroup Analysis

Breast cancer–specific and all-cause survival rates were estimated for 60,480 subgroups defined by combinations of physical activity, age, tumor subtype, stage, weight, and systemic therapy. These results were included in a web calculator to display results for all possible combinations of individual characteristics and treatment.36 Absolute benefits in 10-year breast cancer survival were also summarized for increasing aerobic activity from no/minimal to insufficient with <2 days/week of muscle-strengthening and insufficient to active with ≥2 days/week of muscle-strengthening.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the effect of varying two input parameters on model outcomes: duration of adherence to physical activity recommendations and distribution of breast cancer adjuvant treatment modalities. In the first sensitivity analysis, the input parameters were changed to assume that the women's adherence to an assigned level of physical activity varied from 1 year (worst case) to 6 years (best case) after breast cancer diagnosis. In the second sensitivity analysis, the input parameters were modified to reflect the universal receipt of optimal treatment using published data.33,34,37

Model Validation

Model validation was conducted using publicly available data from the Women's Healthy Eating and Living (WHEL) study.38,39 WHEL data were not used for model input parameters. Breast cancer–specific and all-cause survival rates in the simulated sample were compared with the rates observed in the WHEL study for meeting guideline-recommended levels of aerobic physical activity (≥150 min/wk).31 See the details in Supplementary Methods II.

All analyses were conducted using Stata (version-18, College Station, TX).40 This study was approved by the National Institutes of Health Institutional Review Board and was considered as exempt research based on the use of deidentified pre-existing data (IRB002299).

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

We simulated approximately 20 million 50- to 75-year-old women diagnosed with stage I to III invasive breast cancer based on computational feasibility to generate estimates for different physical activity levels, treatment, and patient characteristics. The simulation model closely replicated the distributions of patient characteristics and physical activity levels in US national data (Data Supplement, Table S2). The mean age of the overall simulated sample was 61 years, and 33.8% had obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2). Most tumors were ER- and PR-positive (70.7%), HER2-negative (86.4%), and stage I (52.9%).

Physical Activity and Breast Cancer–Specific Survival

Overall, the 10-year breast cancer–specific survival was 88.8% among 50- to 75-year-old women with stage I to III breast cancer (Table 2). No/minimal aerobic activity (ie, <30 min/wk) with <2 d/wk of muscle-strengthening activity was associated with a 10-year breast cancer-specific survival rate of 84.5%. Increasing aerobic activity from no/minimal to active (ie, ≥150 to <300 min/wk aerobic activity) with <2 days/week of muscle-strengthening increased the 10-year breast cancer–specific survival to 89.3%, for an absolute increase of 4.8 percentage points (%pts).

Physical Activity and All-Cause Survival

Overall, the 10-year all-cause survival for women diagnosed with stage I to III breast cancer was 82.9% (Data Supplement, Table S3). Increasing aerobic activity from no/minimal to active with <2 d/wk of muscle-strengthening activity was associated with an increase in 10-year all-cause survival from 78.8% to 84.1%, for an absolute increase in all-cause survival by 5.3%pts. Absolute benefits of physical activity on 10-year all-cause survival also increased with age. In ages 50-54 and 70-75 years, increasing aerobic activity from no/minimal to active with <2 days/week of muscle-strengthening was associated with absolute increases in 10-year all-cause survival by 2.6%pts and 8.9%pts, respectively.

Summary of Results

Older age (66-75 years) and obesity were consistently associated with absolute benefits of ≥2%pts in 10-year breast cancer survival for increasing aerobic activity from no/minimal to insufficient with <2 days/week of muscle-strengthening in stages I to III, HR+ and HR–, and HER2-negative breast cancer (Table 4). Increasing aerobic activity from insufficient to active with ≥2 days/week of muscle-strengthening was associated with absolute benefits ranging from 2%pts to 10%pts in stages I and II, HR+/HR–, and HER2-negative breast cancer (Data Supplement, Table S5). The largest benefits were seen in stage III breast cancer.

Sensitivity Analysis

Survival rates associated with optimal treatment were higher compared with the observed rates with real-world treatment patterns (Data Supplement, Table S6). Compared with the base-case analysis (ie, adherence to a physical activity regimen up to 2 years), adherence to 1 year of the physical activity regimen was associated with lower survival, whereas adherence up to 6 years was associated with higher survival.

Model Validation

The median age of women included in the WHEL study was 52 years, and most were diagnosed with HR+ (79.2%), stage I or II (95.7%) breast cancer (Data Supplement, Table S7). The 10-year breast cancer survival rates among 50- to 70-year-old women diagnosed with HR+, stage I to III breast cancer, meeting aerobic activity guidelines (≥150 min/wk), were 95.2% (95% CI, 93.1% to 96.7%) in the WHEL study and 95.1% in the simulation. Similarly, the 10-year all-cause survival rates in the WHEL study and the simulation were 92.8% (90.2%-94.7%) and 91.1%, respectively. Furthermore, the model closely replicated WHEL results for meeting aerobic guidelines for women stratified by age and stage (Data Supplement, Table S8).

DISCUSSION

Current clinical guidelines recommend that clinicians offer individualized physical activity prescriptions to cancer survivors.11,12 Studies report that health care providers need to be better equipped to discuss and provide individualized physical activity prescriptions to patients seen in clinical settings.19,41 There are several resources, including the American College of Sports Medicine's Moving Through Cancer initiative42 and the Exercise in Cancer Evaluation and Decision Support algorithm18 to help health care providers assess, advise, and recommend rehabilitation or exercise services to cancer survivors. However, to our knowledge, currently, there are no tools to provide individualized survival outcomes associated with physical activity for breast cancer survivors.

In this study, we aimed to adapt an established simulation modeling approach24,43,44 to estimate survival rates for different levels of aerobic and muscle-strengthening activities considering breast cancer treatment and individual characteristics of breast cancer survivors. These simulation model–based estimates may help clinicians in tailoring physical activity recommendations according to the individualized benefits associated with different levels of physical activity. For instance, the model results showed a survival benefit of ≥2%pts for older breast cancer survivors with obesity engaging in at least ≥30 to <150 min/wk of aerobic activity (ie, less than meeting guidelines) compared with no/minimal activity. These results are consistent with the exercise guidelines recommending that older adults who cannot engage in recommended levels of exercise participate in activity levels allowed by their abilities and conditions.31 Furthermore, our results indicate that the absolute benefits of physical activity could increase with age. Older women with breast cancer are at higher risk of treatment-related adverse events and comorbidities.45,46 However, physical activity could improve comorbid conditions and reduce treatment-related adverse effects in older adults.4,7,12,47 As a result, physical activity may offer greater benefits to older breast cancer survivors.

A potential concern for clinicians with the use of clinical tools to support physical activity prescriptions could be that it may prolong the duration of time-constrained clinical consultations. However, recent data suggest that tools for patient education and counseling may not increase the overall length of clinical encounters.48 Moreover, there are several evidence-based practices (eg, 5As framework49) to support efficient counseling while maintaining high-quality, patient-centered care in time-limited settings. Importantly, various members of the health care team including exercise physiologists, nurses, and patient navigators may also use the tool for exercise discussions.17-19

There was no single data source to estimate the effects of physical activity on survival for all combinations of treatment and individual characteristics. For instance, US national surveillance data provided information on physical activity participation, but not tumor characteristics or breast cancer treatment.32 National registry data provided information on demographic and tumor characteristics, but not physical activity.26 Moreover, certain data sources lacked adequate sample size, power, or follow-up to generate long-term outcomes (eg, survival) associated with physical activity. Simulation modeling provided a useful approach to combine information from different high-quality data sources to estimate survival for postdiagnosis physical activity by individual clinical and tumor characteristics.24,43,44

Our model results are consistent with observational studies showing that physical activity is associated with higher cancer-specific and all-cause survival.6,50 Many plausible biological mechanisms may explain the benefits of physical activity on breast cancer survival.51-53 Studies suggest that physical activity may influence survival (1) through direct biological effects that counter cancer progression (eg, inflammation), (2) by acting as an adjuvant to improve cancer treatment efficacy and tolerability, and/or (3) by providing a marker of better health status.53 Physical activity, especially in advanced disease, may indicate better prognosis in breast cancer survivors.

It is important to note the role of reverse causality and healthy exerciser bias in the interpretation of observed associations (ie, women who are less likely to die of breast cancer are more likely to engage in physical activity). As informed by Wade et al,54 we attempted to move closer to a causal inference by deriving input parameters from a prospective study with repeated measures of physical activity gathered up to 6 years starting from the date of diagnosis; modeling the effects of physical activity on mortality considering the effects of tumor characteristics, treatment, and weight; and validating model results using independent data from the WHEL study.38

However, our study had several limitations. First, there were limited data to model the effects of physical activity on metabolic risk factors including abdominal obesity, high blood pressure, fasting glucose, triglycerides, and cholesterol levels. We used weight to capture the indirect effects of metabolic risk on survival. Second, data sources that captured the variation of weight with increasing age were lacking. Therefore, we used national cross-sectional survey data to create a natural history of weight conditional on the women's increasing age. Third, there were limited national-level data on age, comorbidity, and weight-specific other-cause mortality rates for physical activity. However, we used age- and weight-specific other cause mortality rates for physical activity from National Health Interview Survey data assuming average comorbidity.35 Fourth, all input parameters were derived using data from the United States. Therefore, model results may only be generalizable to women diagnosed with breast cancer in the United States. We assumed that the distributions of the clinical and tumor characteristics seen in SEER were similar to those distributions in the overall population of US women diagnosed with breast cancer. Fifth, there were also limited data to model the effects of physical activity on recurrence or second primary cancers among breast cancer survivors. Finally, input parameters derived using self-reported data (eg, physical activity) cannot rule out potential biases because of measurement errors, selection bias, social desirability bias, and/or recall bias. However, we used previously validated physical activity data from the Pathways Study,29 and our model results were similar to the independent data provided by the WHEL study.38 Future studies may consider objective measures of physical activity collected using accelerometers or smartphones.

Overall, simulation modeling provided a powerful computational tool to synthesize existing data and generate individualized outcomes for physical activity. The use of SEER data in our model allowed us to simulate clinical and tumor characteristics of women seen in real-world settings. We also presented individualized survival estimates for eight levels of aerobic and muscle-strengthening activity, offering insights beyond what is currently available in the existing literature.6,8 Clinicians could use these model estimates to support evidence-based discussions, make referrals, and/or provide guideline-recommended physical activity prescriptions for breast cancer survivors. In a future study, we will add individualized messages and additional resources alongside the model-based estimates to facilitate the integration of the tool into a clinical workflow.
