Pretreated mesenchymal stromal cells and their secretome for kidney disease: mechanisms and applications
Authors: Qi Liu, Guodong Liu, Dong Sun, Shulin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The issue of kidney disease represents a significant global health challenge. While current treatment options may provide symptomatic relief, they are limited by several factors. Consequently, there is a pressing need to create more effective therapeutic strategies. Mesenchymal stromal cell (MSCs) and their secretome have attracted considerable attention in the field of regenerative medicine owing to their multidirectional differentiation potential, immunomodulatory properties, and paracrine effects, which offer a promising solution to this challenge. However, direct transplantation of MSCs and their secretome faces problems such as low survival rate and unstable therapeutic effect in practical applications. These challenges have prompted researchers to explore strategies to enhance the therapeutic potential of MSCs and their secretory factors through pretreatment. This review summarizes the current research progress on pretreated MSCs and their secretome in the treatment of kidney diseases and discusses how various pretreatment approaches can enhance their therapeutic efficacy and clinical application in renal disorders, thereby providing insights for the future optimization and therapeutic use of MSCs.

Introduction

Kidney diseases, ranging from acute kidney injury (AKI) to end-stage renal disease, impact more than 850 million individuals globally and lead to 5 to 10 million deaths annually [1, 2]. Numerous studies indicate that a considerable portion of individuals with AKI eventually progresses to chronic kidney disease(CKD) [3–5], thereby exacerbating the burden associated with kidney disease. The prevalence and mortality rates of kidney diseases have made them a significant global health concern [6]. Treating kidney diseases requires a large amount of medical resources and costs, Managing kidney diseases demands significant medical resources and expenses, imposing a substantial strain on patients, their families, and the economy as a whole [1, 7, 8]. The treatment options for kidney disease are diverse [8], but each of them has its own limitations. Kidney transplantation faces donor shortage, postoperative complications and economic burden [7]. Dialysis treatment cannot completely replace kidney function and has a variety of complications risk [9]. Drug treatment has limited effect and may have side effects [10]. Given the severity and growing prevalence of kidney diseases, there remains an urgent need for more effective therapeutic strategies.

With biological characteristics such as the potential for multi-directional differentiation, immunomodulatory properties, self-renewal ability, homing characteristics, and secretory functions, MSCs [11] play a crucial role in the field of regenerative medicine. MSCs can be isolated from various tissues, including bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cords, placentas, amniotic fluid, and dental pulp [12, 13]. MSCs secrete a range of bioactive components, including growth factors, cytokines, and chemokines, which influence surrounding cells and promote tissue repair and regeneration [14, 15]. In addition, MSCs can directly interact with other cell types, including immune and endothelial cells, through cell surface receptor–ligand interactions, thereby transmitting signals and regulating cellular functions [16]. Through these mechanisms, MSCs can suppress inflammation, prevent apoptosis, mitigate oxidative stress, inhibit fibrosis, facilitate angiogenesis, and modulate both autophagy and immune responses [16, 17]. As a result of these processes, MSCs offer innovative concepts and approaches for addressing a range of diseases[18–21], including kidney disease [22]. Nonetheless, recent research indicates that in real-world applications, the direct transplantation of MSCs suffers from a low success rate, diminished survival rates, and compromised paracrine functionality [17].

The MSC secretome refers to the collective array of bioactive substances secreted by MSCs, including cytokines, exosomes, microvesicles, proteins, and other molecules [23, 24]. Increasing evidence indicates that MSC-derived secretome exerts significant therapeutic effects in kidney diseases by promoting tissue repair through anti-fibrotic, anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and pro-angiogenic actions, thereby improving renal function and slowing disease progression [25–27]. Additional studies have discovered that the structure of the MSC secretome is intricate and may be significantly affected by the conditions of the culture. Emerging studies suggest that pretreatment strategies can further optimize both the composition and biological functions of the MSC secretome [28].

This review summarizes the current research progress and underlying mechanisms of various pretreatment strategies applied to MSCs and their secretome in the treatment of different kidney diseases, with the aim of providing insights for future therapeutic development in this field (Master table: pretreatment methods for MSCs in the treatment of different kidney diseases).

Application of pretreated MSCs in kidney diseases

Pretreated MSCs and their secretome have shown considerable therapeutic effects. Pretreatment significantly improves MSC homing, migration, survival, and stimulates MSCs to secrete exosomes with increased levels of beneficial factors and reduced levels of detrimental factors. Key underlying mechanisms involve inhibiting oxidative stress, inflammation, fibrosis, cell apoptosis and improving angiogenesis and renal function (Fig. 1:Mechanisms of Preconditioned MSCs in Ameliorating Kidney Disease).

AKI

Acute kidney injury involves tubular epithelial cell death from ischemic or toxic insults, triggering mitochondrial dysfunction, severe oxidative stress, and inflammatory cascades that initiate fibrosis and potential progression to chronic kidney disease. Current management relies on supportive fluid resuscitation, diuretics, and renal replacement therapy like dialysis—approaches that alleviate symptoms but fail to regenerate damaged tissue and carry significant mortality risks. While MSC therapy offers promise through paracrine mechanisms like exosomal miRNA-mediated, anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects, its clinical application faces critical challenges including poor cell survival and homing efficiency to kidneys. Physical pretreatment strategies for MSCs in AKI

Enhancing the therapeutic potential of MSCs through physical pretreatment involves altering their physiological characteristics and strengthening their regenerative capabilities. Various types of physical stimuli have been investigated for the purpose of preconditioning MSCs, including:

Hypoxia pretreatment

Culturing MSCs under reduced oxygen conditions (1–5% O₂) mimics their physiological niche and enhances cell survival, proliferation, and secretion of pro-regenerative factors, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and anti-inflammatory cytokines [29]. Hypoxia pretreatment has been shown to alter the protein composition of extracellular vesicles derived from adipose-derived MSCs, enhancing their antioxidant capacity [30]. By up-regulating the gene expression of VEGF and SDF1, hypoxic conditions can significantly improve the angiogenesis potential of MSCs, and EVs from MSCs cultured under hypoxic conditions showed stronger angiogenesis-promoting effects in vitro experiments [31].

Pretreatment with hypoxia boosts the chemotactic ability, survival rates, and paracrine functions of MSCs via the SDF-1-CXCR4/CXCR7 signaling pathway, leading to improved homing efficiency in vivo and improves the therapeutic effect of renal I/R injury [32]. Hypoxia pretreatment with CoCl2 enhances MSC migration by activating HIF-1α and upregulating CXCR4 expression [33]. Additionally, another study has shown that pretreatment with hypoxia improves, at least to some extent, the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs by boosting their antioxidant, antiapoptotic, and angiogenic properties, although the underlying mechanisms were not further explored [34]. Further studies have demonstrated that AMSCs pretreated with hypoxic and conditioned media showed enhanced renal protection in vitro and in vivo. These effects were attributed to increased secretion of protective factors and attenuation of inflammatory responses [35]. In a sepsis-induced AKI model, exosomes from hypoxia-pretreated ADSCs were found to prevent microvascular dysfunction by delivering mmu_circ_0001295, which may contribute to improved outcomes in sepsis [36]. Meanwhile, an other research investigation found that MSCs under hypoxic conditions increased renal tubular autophagy, which consequently reduced tubular cell death, mitigated interstitial inflammation, and lessened impairment of renal function [37].

In addition to the above physical pretreatment method, the researchers have also explored the other two pretreatment methods. Although there is no relevant research on the treatment of kidney disease, it also provides some inspiration for the future MSCs pretreatment strategy:

Hydrostatic pressure

Exposing MSCs to controlled hydrostatic pressure can enhance their differentiation potential, particularly in applications related to tissue engineering and organ regeneration. MSCs pretreated with hypoxia respond to periodic hydrostatic pressure of specific amplitude and frequency, promoting their differentiation into osteoblasts [38]. Hydrostatic pressure influences the volume, clustering, and differentiation of MSCs into cartilage, and it can improve the potential of MSCs for cartilage differentiation [39].

Electromagnetic stimulation

A single-pulse electromagnetic field stimulates the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs obtained from bone marrow (BMSCs) and promotes bone repair, neovascularization, and the proliferation of cells in necrotic bone in mice [40]. The EMF exerts significant, enduring effects on both the proliferation and differentiation of BMSCs. When combined with cell sheet technology, this approach offers a novel strategy for addressing the challenges associated with fracture nonunion [41]. EMF significantly enhance the migration of MSCs through increasing intracellular calcium concentrations and activating the FAK/Rho GTPase signaling pathway, which are crucial for cellular movement and function [42]. The proliferation and differentiation of MSCs are positively influenced by EMF, and these effects can be seen over different treatment periods [43].

Chemical pretreatment strategies for MSCs in AKI

Chemical pretreatment represents another important strategy to enhance the survival, proliferation, and differentiation potential of MSCs. This approach involves exposing MSCs to specific biochemical factors prior to transplantation or therapeutic application. Several major chemical preconditioning strategies have been reported.

Small molecule induction

Certain small molecules can change the fate of MSCs [44, 45]. Based on the changes in MSCs’ properties caused by these small molecules, researchers have developed a series of methods to improve MSCs and their secretome for treating kidney disease.

The reparative capacity of MSCs in renal ischemia–reperfusion (I/R) injury is enhanced by 14 S,21R-diHDHA via activation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, which promotes MSC survival and increases the secretion of renoprotective factors [46].

All-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) pretreatment enhances the renoprotective potential of AMSCs. It plays a role by restoring redox balance, reducing inflammation and apoptosis through downregulating TGF-β1, IL − 6, and caspase − 3, while upregulating HIF-1α and VEGF [47]. Subsequent investigations indicated that pretreatment with ATRA stimulates the production of hyaluronic acid (HA) by MSCs, activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in renal tubular epithelial cells via the HA/CD44 interaction, thus improving the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs in treating AKI [48]. Additionally, Wharton’s jelly-derived MSCs (WJ-MSCs) pretreated with ATRA can enhance their renal protection, which may be related to upregulation of Wnt/β-catenin pathway, reduction of apoptosis, inflammation and oxidative stress [49].

The effectiveness of ADSCs-MVs pretreated with Dexmedetomidine (DEX) has been shown to reduce apoptosis in tubular cells and alleviate renal dysfunction by inhibiting the miR-122-5p/Bax/PARP apoptosis signaling pathway, while simultaneously boosting the expression of erythropoietin (EPO )[50].

EPO pretreatment of bone marrow–derived MSCs accelerates AKI repair through multiple mechanisms, including regulation of SIRT1 and apoptosis-related protein expression, as well as direct anti-inflammatory effects [51]. However, the mechanism of EPO-mediated SIRT1 signaling activation require further investigation.

Fenoldopam Mesylate (FD) has been reported to enhance the growth, differentiation, and colony-forming capabilities of MSCs. It activates the cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB) and the AKT signaling pathway, enhances the expression of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) and heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), and facilitates the nuclear translocation of NRF2, thereby providing additional cytoprotective benefits [52]. Pretreatment with S-nitroso-N-acetylpenicillamine (SNAP) has the potential to boost the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs in cases of renal ischemia-reperfusion injury. This approach may enhance MSCs’ survival, their ability to migrate, and overall renal function within an ischemic kidney environment by stimulating the AKT signaling pathway and increasing the levels of cytoprotective factors [53].

Moreover, targeting specific molecular pathways can further potentiate MSC therapy. AMSCs pretreated with CD44-targeted hyaluronic acid can enhance their protective effect against renal ischemic injury, with mechanisms related to the activation of angiogenesis, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, and the inhibition of oxidative stress, apoptosis, inflammation, and fibrosis [54]. Finally, valproic acid (VPA) pretreatment of umbilical cord WJ-MSCs can enhance cell migration by activating the AKT/P13K and SDF1/CXCR4 pathways, alleviate renal inflammation, and have a protective effect against IRI [55].

Cytokine and growth factor stimulation

The immunomodulatory capabilities of MSCs are not an intrinsic characteristic; rather, they necessitate a certain level of inflammatory response or additional stimulation to be effectively activated [56]. Cytokine priming enhances MSC immunomodulatory function, as shown across various inflammatory models, although kidney-specific studies remain limited [57–60].

Pretreatment with interleukin-17 A (IL-17 A) has been demonstrated to enhance the proliferation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) through the activation of the COX-2/PGE2 signaling pathway. This immunomodulatory effect contributes to an enhanced therapeutic potential of MSCs in IRI-AKI mice, thereby improving renal protection and recovery [61].

In addition, epigenetic modulation has been explored as a chemical pretreatment strategy for MSCs and has promising results. The differentiation potential of MSCs can be enhanced through epigenetic modulation, which is achieved by employing a hypomethylating agent (HMA) in conjunction with a histone deacetylase inhibitor (HDACi). This approach considerably boosts the expression of essential regulatory genes, including Runx-2, BDNF, and Sox-9, which in turn facilitates multilineage differentiation. These chemical preconditioning strategy optimize the properties of MSCs, improving their therapeutic potential in regenerative medicine [62]. Regulatory functions in the differentiation of dental pulp MSCs (DPSCs) are influenced by epigenetic mechanisms. Modifying these epigenetic factors can enhance the differentiation of DPSCs into osteoblasts. Specifically, an EZH2 inhibitor directly increases the expression of genes like Runx2, BMP2, and Alpl. Meanwhile, the histone deacetylase inhibitor TSA enhances the activation of these genes through its interaction with signaling pathways within an osteo-inductive medium (OIM) [63].Although the application of MSCs with epigenetic modifications and their secretome in kidney disease is currently lacking, it also provides a new idea for the future of pre-treated MSCs in kidney disease.

Engineering pretreatment strategies for MSCs in AKI

Engineering pretreatment modifies MSCs through gene-editing technologies to enhance their specific functions. Key approaches include:

Gene overexpression enhances cellular therapeutic capacity by activating specific genes (such as growth factors or anti-apoptotic proteins), essentially “arming” cells to boost their secretion efficiency and survival capabilities. Glycosylation engineering, in contrast, focuses on fine-tuning glycosylation modifications of proteins. By regulating glycosylase enzymes, it redefines the cell’s surface “identity recognition” and “communication” patterns—akin to “upgrading cellular gear” to improve targeting accuracy, prolong circulation time in vivo, or modulate immune responses.

Through these approaches, the survival and homing of MSCs were improved, and the anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptosis and antioxidant effects of their secretome are further strengthened.

Gene overexpression

MSCs overexpressing Lipocalin 2 have been shown to ameliorate kidney damage induced by cisplatin and increase the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs by promoting the expression of HGF, IGF, FGF and VEGF [64]. Similarly, VEGF-modified MSCs also can enhance the protective effect against cisplatin-induced AKI through anti-apoptosis and promoting proliferation [65].

Overexpression of heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) protects BMSCs from apoptosis in the AKI microenvironment and ultimately improves renal function. The mechanism is related to antioxidant, inhibition of downstream p53 and p38 MAPK phosphorylation, and anti-inflammatory actions [66]. Further studies revealed that HO-1overexpression enhances the proliferation and differentiation of BMSCs in the AKI microenvironment, which may improve renal function in AKI rats by stimulating PI3K/Akt and MEK/ERK signaling pathways [67]. In addition, HO-1–overexpressing MSCs have been shown to improve survival rates and renal function while reducing inflammation in sepsis-associated AKI mice, with these effects largely mediated by activation of the JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway [68].

BMSCs with high levels of ISL1 expression alleviate renal damage induced by ischemia-reperfusion by decreasing oxidative stress and apoptosis through paracrine pathways [69]. Further studies on ISL1-rich medium revealed that it reduces oxidative stress and apoptosis induced by H₂O₂ in human renal proximal tubular cells (HKC). Proteomic studies, along with corresponding in vitro analyses, identified Haptoglobin (HP) as a key paracrine element that offers protection to HKC. These findings suggest that HP may represent a promising cell-free therapeutic target for reducing apoptosis and oxidative stress in renal ischemia–reperfusion injury.

Overexpression of the prion protein (PrP^C) has been reported to protect may safeguard against renal ischemia-reperfusion injury by boosting ATP synthesis and promoting mitochondrial biogenesis, thereby revitalizing ADMSCs and enhancing their regenerative capabilities [70].

Klotho gene overexpression significantly enhanced the proliferation, secretion and migration of BMSCs. Moreover, Klotho gene overexpression significantly promoted the recovery from rhabdomyolysis-induced AKI by elevating the levels of EPO and IGF-1 and reducing the expression of KIM-1 and HIF-1 [71].

IDO-overexpressing MSCs-Exo significantly promotes renal function recovery in IRI mice by regulating macrophage polarization, with its reparative capacity far exceeding that of ordinary MSC-Exo [72].

The introduction of kallikrein-modified MSCs into the kidney offers significant advantages in safeguarding against kidney damage caused by ischemia through the inhibition of both apoptosis and inflammation [73].

In hemorrhagic shock-induced AKI, MSCs overexpressing PBX1 have demonstrated renoprotective effects by inhibiting NF-κB-mediated NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby reducing inflammation and tissue damage [74].

Small extracellular vesicles derived from ACE2-modified human umbilical cord MSCs (MSC-ACE2-EVs) demonstrate enhanced anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-apoptotic properties both in vitro and in vivo. These vesicles provide protection to HK-2 cells and kidneys against damage caused by renal ischemia–reperfusion injury. This protective effect may be linked to the activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway [75].

Glycosylation engineering

Genetic modifications targeting CD44 fucosylation have also demonstrated beneficial effects in ischemic kidney injury by enhancing MSC homing capabilities and promoting macrophage polarization toward a reparative phenotype [76].

Improvement of culture condition

Although MSCs are conventionally cultured in two-dimensional (2D) monolayers, their removal from the native three-dimensional (3D) microenvironment can induce detrimental functional alterations [77–81]. Therefore, simulating its three-dimensional environment for culture has become a possible way to augment the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs. In addition to 3D culture systems, modifying the composition of the culture medium represents another approach for optimizing MSC-based therapy. Cultivating MSCs in low serum medium is another optimized culture strategy.

Studies have found that a hollow-fiber 3D culture system provides a strategy for efficient continuous production of MSC-exos, which has stronger therapeutic potential for cisplatin-induced AKI [82]. Similarly, 3D spheroid culture strategy improved the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs against AKI, characterized by reducing apoptosis and tissue damage, increasing angiogenesis, and improving renal function [83]. These advantages can be linked to the modulation of SLC7A11 and GPX4, which assists in reducing ferroptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells.

Progress in bioprinting technology has also played a role in therapies based on MSCs. EVs sourced from microfiber-bioprinted MSCs significantly accelerate NRK-52E cell proliferation and migration while attenuating hypoxia-reoxygenation (H/R) injury in vitro. Across in vivo and in vitro models, 3D-EVs demonstrated superior efficacy to 2D-EVs, an enhancement plausibly mediated by KLF15 signaling pathways [84].

Optimized culture and pretreatment conditions (hypoxia and spherical culture) can significantly induce miR-210 overexpression and increase exosome production. Exosomes abundant in miR-210 can enhance the survival rate of injured kidney cells, decrease apoptosis and the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and facilitate cell migration [85].

In summary, MSC pretreatment for acute kidney injury (AKI) is not merely a technical adjustment, but rather a strategic approach aimed at enhancing the survival and function of MSCs in the injury microenvironment. Through the coordinated action of multiple mechanisms, it improves the adaptability and therapeutic efficacy of MSCs under acute injury conditions.

As outlined in this chapter, effective pretreatment strategies systematically address the key challenges in AKI treatment: (1) By activating signaling pathways such as HIF-1α or PI3K/Akt, they enhance the survival of MSCs and their targeted migration to the injury site under inflammatory and hypoxic conditions. (2) Through cytokine prestimulation or genetic engineering, the immunomodulatory function of MSCs is pre-enhanced, promoting the rapid release of key mediators such as IDO and PGE₂ to suppress excessive inflammatory responses. (3) By upregulating antioxidant pathways (e.g., Nrf2/HO-1) and anti-apoptotic proteins, MSCs are better equipped to counteract oxidative damage, thereby protecting renal tubular epithelial cells. These mechanisms work synergistically to collectively promote renal tissue repair and functional recovery.

AKI to CKD transition

Unlike the acute oxidative and inflammatory responses described in AKI, the transition from AKI to CKD is driven by maladaptive repair characterized by sustained inflammation, epigenetic reprogramming of renal cells, and progressive fibroblast activation that drives irreversible extracellular matrix deposition. Current interventions—primarily RAAS inhibitors—merely slow functional decline without stopping fibrosis progression, while MSC-based therapies show theoretical potential through paracrine modulation of TGF-β/Smad and Wnt pathways yet remain limited by hostile fibrotic microenvironments that induce MSC senescence and impair secretory function. Overcoming these barriers requires innovations in preconditioning strategies, such as hypoxia-primed exosomes, as well as engineered delivery systems capable of disrupting established fibrotic niches (Table 2, Pretreated MSCs and Their Secretome in AKI to CKD transition).

Pretreated MSCs and their secretome in AKI to CKD transition

Physical pretreatment strategies for AKI to CKD transition

Hypoxia pretreatment

Hypoxia preconditioning in MSCs can reduce inflammation and renal fibrosis in rats that have experienced ischemia-reperfusion., thereby partially averting the progression from AKI to CKD [86]. Additionally, hypoxia-pretreated extracellular vesicles from MSCs may impede renal fibrosis by enhancing mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation (FAO) mediated by CPT1A, probably via the regulation of mitochondrial balance [87]. Together, these findings highlight the beneficial role of hypoxic preconditioning of MSCs and their exosomes in mitigating the AKI-to-CKD transition.

Chemical pretreatment strategies for AKI to CKD transition

Small molecule induction

Astilbin(AST) pretreatment enhanced the therapeutic effect of MSCs in AKI and AKI-to-CKD mice, and induced macrophages to polarize to M2-like phenotype through PTGS2-mediated pathway [88].

Cytokine and growth factor stimulation

Pretreatment of MSCs with interferon-γ (IFN-γ) induces Tregs by increasing indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) secretion, thereby alleviating renal fibrosiss [89]. Additionally, IFN-γ pretreatment can enhance the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs in the context of experimental renal fibrosis, and this effect is associated with the promotion of PGE2 secretion and the induction of macrophage polarization [90].

Engineering pretreatment strategies for MSCs in AKI to CKD transition

Gene overexpression

Klotho gene-modified BMSCs can inhibit the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, enhance the anti-fibrosis effect, improve the proliferation ability of BMSCs, inhibit macrophage infiltration in kidney with AKI [91].

Overexpression of Oct-4 enhances the efficacy of extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from MSCs in treating AKI. This significantly improves renal function and alleviates renal fibrosis by reducing apoptosis and promoting cell proliferation [92].

Overall, Current studies show that MSCs and their secretory group are enhanced with immunomodulatory ability after pretreatment, and their ability to fight inflammation and fibrosis in the kidney is improved, showing the superior therapeutic outcomes compared with untreated MSCs.

CKD

Chronic kidney disease features progressive nephron loss and irreversible fibrosis driven by sustained inflammation, metabolic dysregulation (e.g., mitochondrial FAO impairment), and pathological activation of myofibroblasts via TGF-β/Smad3 and Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Current management relies on RAAS blockade to reduce proteinuria and SGLT2 inhibitors to slow eGFR decline—although these can only delay the progression to end-stage renal failure. MSC-based therapies aim to disrupt this vicious cycle via paracrine secretion of anti-fibrotic miRNAs and immunomodulation of macrophage polarization, yet face critical barriers including hostile senescent microenvironments that induce MSC dysfunction, poor retention in sclerotic tissue, and inconsistent efficacy across heterogeneous CKD etiologies.Preprocessing methods can to some extent ameliorate these issues (Table 3, Pretreated MSCs and Their Secretome in CKD).

Physical pretreatment strategies for MSCs in CKD

Hypoxia pretreatment

Evidence indicates that exosomes derived from MSCs cultured under hypoxic conditions are more effective in alleviating renal dysfunctions in a unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) model, as compared to those derived under normoxic circumstances. The underlying mechanisms include reducing the levels of pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6, inhibiting cell apoptosis, and alleviating renal fibrosis [93].

Chemical pretreatment strategies for MSCs in CKD

Small molecule induction

Melatonin pretreatment has been shown to enhance the homing and survival capacity of BMSCs in CKD models, thereby promoting kidney regeneration [94]. Studies suggest that melatonin pretreatment can improve its efficacy, which may provide a new strategy for diabetic nephropathy (DN) treatment by regulating pathological factors and activating autophagy [95].

Beyond melatonin, the conditioned medium obtained from umbilical cord MSCs (UC-MSCs) that were preconditioned with deferoxamine has shown enhanced therapeutic efficacy for DN when compared to conventional MSC-conditioned medium. It works through downregulating pro-fibrotic and pro-inflammatory factors such as TGF-β1, IL-6, and caspase-3, and upregulating HIF-1α and VEGF, ultimately contributing to renal protection [96].

Enginering pretreatment strategies for MSCs in CKD

Gene overexpression

HGF-transfected MSCs can help alleviate renal fibrosis after ureteral obstruction, providing a basis for cell regeneration therapy of renal diseases [97]. Using the same UUO rat model, an additional study revealed that inhibition of renal epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) underpins the mechanism, suggesting a foundation for treating renal fibrosis [98].

Similarly, Research indicates that VEGF-transfected MSCs protect kidneys against UUO-induced EMT and fibrosis. This protection occurs by modulating inflammatory factors and preserving renal structure and function [99].

Glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF)-modified AMSCs can enhance the repair of damaged endothelial cells, promote angiogenesis, improve renal function in UUO mice, and reduce renal fibrosis [100]. In view of the paracrine role of MSCs, subsequent research confirmed that exosomes derived from GDNF-AMSCs ameliorate renal fibrosis. The study further identified activation of the SIRT1/eNOS pathway as the underlying mechanism, enhancing endothelial cell function in vitro [101].

Microparticles (MPs) derived from human EPO mRNA-transfected MSCs have also been found to inhibit EMT and ameliorate renal interstitial fibrosis in UUO models, highlighting another potential avenue for therapeutic intervention [102].

MicroRNA-modified MSCs have emerged as a powerful tool for CKD treatment. The therapeutic effect of BMSCs on H2O2-injured podocytes seemed to be further enhanced when miR-124a was overexpressed. This effect may be achieved by regulating apoptosis-related genes, autophagy-related genes, and inhibiting PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway [103]. Mir-146a-5p-modified UC-MSCs enhanced renal protection in STZ-induced DN rats by promoting M2 macrophage polarization [104]. In CKD-IR rats, iPS-MSC–derived cells overexpressing miR-19a and miR-20a demonstrated significantly enhanced efficacy compared to unmodified iPS-MSCs. The therapeutic benefit was mediated through the suppression of inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptotic signaling pathways, as well as the preservation of mitochondrial function. Collectively, these actions provided significant advantages in recovering residual renal function and maintaining renal structural integrity [105].

In addition, lentiviral-mediated transfection of relaxin-2 (RLX) and green fluorescent protein (GFP) genes into BMSCs generated BMSCs-eRLX + GFP capable of sustained expression of anti-fibrotic and renoprotective hormones. These engineered MSCs showed superior therapeutic efficacy in both AKI and hypertensive CKD models compared with unmodified BMSCs or ACE inhibitor treatment alone. BMSCs-eRLX + GFP still showed a good long-term safety profile 9 months after the end of treatment, and no major long-term adverse reactions were observed [106].

Improvement of culture condition strategies for MSCs in CKD

Recent studies suggest that combined serum-free culture and hypoxic pretreatment can enhance the anti-fibrotic efficacy of MSCs in renal fibrosis models. Although this study lacks experimental data related to renal function and has certain limitations, it shows a new idea of co-culture for MSCs pretreatment [107].

Overall, pretreated MSCs exhibit enhanced tolerance and anti-fibrotic efficacy. However, repeated cell injections are impractical in chronic diseases, making the “cell-derived products” generated through pre-treatment particularly crucial. Multiple studies indicate that the secretory profiles of pre-treated MSCs improve mitochondrial function and reduce fibrosis in CKD kidneys. Furthermore, optimal pre-treatment protocols can be tailored to different etiologies (e.g., diabetic nephropathy, hypertensive nephropathy) and disease stages of CKD. For instance, antioxidant stress-preconditioning may be prioritized for diabetic nephropathy, while advanced fibrosis-focused strategies employing engineered approaches could prove more effective.

Physical pretreatment strategies for MSCs in other kidney diseases

Hypoxia pretreatment

Hypoxia pretreatment can alleviate MSC dysfunction, aging, and DNA hydroxymethylation in atherosclerotic renovascular disease (ARAS), thereby optimizing the efficacy of autologous MSC therapy. Hypoxia pretreatment improves MSC function and is associated with epigenetic changes, but currently, it is not possible to directly correlate specific genes with the activation or inhibition of MSC regenerative function [108].

In a complementary study, intrarenal arterial administration of AMSCs improved diastolic blood pressure, reduced renal fibrosis and inflammation, and alleviated medullary hypoxia in ARAS models. While hypoxic preconditioning did not further enhance renal function compared with normoxic MSCs, it significantly reduced 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) levels in genes associated with inflammation and fibrosis, suggesting a disease-specific epigenetic regulatory effect [109].

Chemical pretreatment strategies for MSCs in other kidney diseases

Small molecule induction

In the context of kidney transplantation, EPO pretreatment of BMSCs can enhance its ameliorative effect on cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity through various mechanisms, including improved cell survival, migration, and immunomodulation [110]. Mechanistically, EPO-enhanced MSC migration is associated with activation of the SDF-1/CXCR4 signaling pathway, while resistance to rejection appears related to modulation of the Th1/Th2 immune balance. Interestingly, the studies revealed that a concentration of 500 IU/mL EPO is most effective in facilitating the migration and survival of BMSCs [111].

Rapamycin has been found to reverse the senescent phenotype and enhance immunoregulation in MSCs from Murphy Roths Large Lymphoproliferative mice and systemic lupus erythematosus patients by inhibiting the mTOR signaling pathway [112]. Rapamycin not only rejuvenates these MSCs but also enhances their immunoregulatory properties, suggesting a potential therapeutic approach for autoimmune kidney diseases.

Dimethyloxaloylglycine (DMOG) pretreatment has demonstrated significant benefits in lupus nephritis (LN) models. It improves the anti-inflammatory properties and homing capabilities of hUC-MSCs, decreases the enlargement of lymph nodes and the weight of kidneys in LN mice, lowers urinary protein levels, enhances renal morphology and structure, and prevents inflammatory cell infiltration and renal fibrosis. The mechanism is related to down-regulation of TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway [113].

Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) and MSCs alone can improve renal injury in LN, but when used in combination, The therapeutic effectiveness of MSCs can be diminished by HCQ; however, pretreating MSCs with HCQ may accelerate improvements in renal morphology and function. Additionally, HCQ pretreatment boosts the migratory capacity of MSCs, which may be related to CXCL10 and CXCR3 pathways, while altering serum IL-10, IL-2 and IgM levels, regulating Th1/Th2 balance and B-cell antibody secretion [114].

Engineering pretreatment strategies for MSCs in other kidney diseases

Over expression

In rat kidney transplant model, adipose-derived MSCs genetically modified to express OX40Ig significantly prolonged graft survival, reduced rejection severity, and modulated cytokine expression profiles, thereby enhancing immunosuppressive efficacy [115].

Similarly, IDO-overexpressing MSCs augment the functional expression of CD4⁺CD25⁺Foxp3⁺ regulatory T cells (Tregs), thereby promoting allograft tolerance [116].

In addition, survivin-modified MSCs demonstrated enhanced protective effects against renal ischemia–reperfusion injury in transplantation-related AKI models, primarily through improved cell survival and anti-apoptotic signaling [117].

Improvement of culture condition strategies for MSCs in other kidney diseases

AMSCs cultured in low serum medium confer better renal protection in rat GBM-GN models by reprogramming macrophages toward an immunomodulatory phenotype [118]. Similarly, serum-free MSCs (SF-MSCs) exert anti-apoptotic effects via paracrine signaling, thereby improving renal function in contrast-induced nephropathy (CIN) models, making them a promising candidate for CIN treatment [119].

Beyond conventional AKI and CKD, personalized pre-treatment strategies demonstrate revolutionary potential in managing other kidney disorders with highly heterogeneous etiologies. The core promise lies in tailoring MSC activation protocols to patients’ unique immune profiles and disease-specific pathophysiological mechanisms, thereby achieving true precision medicine.

Problems and prospects

The Necessity and Advantages of Pretreatment Strategies: The implementation of pretreatment strategies for MSCs and their secretome is necessary due to hostile microenvironments in kidney diseases characterized by oxidative stress, inflammation, and fibrosis severely compromise the survival (homing efficiency < 10%) and paracrine function of MSCs. Preconditioning (e.g., hypoxia, cytokine stimulating, or genetic engineering) delivers three core advantages: (1) Enhanced bioactivity (e.g., HIF-1α-driven VEGF upregulation boosting angiogenesis); (2) Augmented microenvironmental resilience (e.g., HO-1 overexpression reducing apoptosis); (3) Optimized secretome composition (e.g., IFN-γ priming enriching immunomodulatory exosomal miRNAs like miR-146a). Although pretreatment increases production complexity and costs, this investment is clinically justified by:

Superior efficacy: Pretreated MSCs achieve significantly better functional recovery than untreated cells in diabetic nephropathy and transplantation models, potentially reducing long-term dialysis dependence.

Dose-sparing effects: Engineered MSCs require substantially lower cell doses to achieve efficacy, mitigating infusion-related risks.

Economic sustainability: Single administrations of optimized pretreated MSCs can replace multiple injections of naive cells, offsetting initial costs through reduced hospitalization and follow-up interventions.

Therefore, despite added complexity, pretreatment shows a valuable effect for developing clinically effective and economically viable MSC therapies.

Delivery methods: The current delivery methods of MSCs generally include renal artery injection, renal intravenous injection, and renal cortex injection, etc. Studies have mentioned the advantages of renal artery injection [120]. However, current conventional delivery methods often result in the entrapment of MSCs and their secretome in the liver and lungs, with only a minority reaching the target organs. New and more efficient delivery approaches still need to be explored. Recent research demonstrates that a nanoplatform utilizing hyaluronic acid combined with chitosan nanoparticles can improve both the safety and effectiveness of BM-MSCs in addressing nephrotoxicity caused by cisplatin. This approach enhances the stability and longevity of MSCs, presenting a promising strategy for treating kidney damage [121]. GF-1 C-modified hydrogels provide a favorable microenvironment for AMSCs, enhancing their therapeutic efficacy in AKI models [122]. Enhancements in these delivery techniques significantly boost the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs, potentially serving as a dependable approach to further enhance the effectiveness of MSCs therapy.

Standardization and production challenges: The therapeutic efficacy of MSCs and their secretome (exosomes, conditioned medium) is highly dependent on cell source, culture process, and preparation procedures, while currently lacking unified quality control standards. For a given disease, the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs and their secretomes from different sources varies considerably. Moreover, the prevalent storage methods, which mainly involve cryopreservation, require validation regarding their impact on therapeutic efficacy. Achieving clinical-grade consistency in pretreated MSC therapies demands a holistic standardization strategy encompassing: (1) rigorous cell sourcing via donor screening (age < 40 years, no genetic disorders) and master cell banking with ≤P5 passage limits to maintain phenotype (CD73⁺/CD90⁺/CD105⁺ >95%; ISCT criteria); (2) locked-down pretreatment parameters such as hypoxia (1–5% O₂, 24–48 h), cytokine priming (e.g., IFN-γ 20–50 ng/mL, 48 h), or genetic modification (MOI = 10, > 70% efficiency), monitored by real-time sensors or qPCR; (3) secretome quality control per ISEV guidelines—validating exosome size (80–150 nm), marker expression (CD81⁺/TSG101⁺), and potency metrics (e.g., ≥ 50% TGF-β inhibition); and (4) clinical translation protocols employing body surface area-based dosing and GMP-compliant cryopreservation. Adherence to FDA/EMA regulatory frameworks and correlating product attributes (e.g., exosomal miR-21 levels) with functional outcomes (e.g., eGFR slope) will mitigate batch variability while enabling predictable therapeutic efficacy.

Long-term Safety and Efficacy: Many current studies have mentioned the tumorigenicity of MSC therapy, and preconditioning the secretory group of MSCs may be one way to address this issue in the future. However, some chemical and genetic pretreatment approaches may have new safety concerns, such as off-target effects and immunogenic reactions. The variability of pretreatment strategies often due to inconsistent cellular responses to the same pretreatment, which can be influenced by factors such as donor heterogeneity, cell passage number, and culture conditions. This outcome heterogeneity has big challenges for reproducibility, scalability, and clinical translation, highlighting the need for rigorous standardization and quality control in MSC pretreatment protocol [123].

Clinical Translation: The therapeutic effects of MSCs have already received some positive support at the animal model level from the aforementioned studies. However, due to the limitations of animal models, whether its therapeutic effects are applicable to humans remains to be explored. Currently, an increasing number of researchers are gradually focusing on the significance of organoid model applications, which may also represent one of the potential directions for overcoming clinical application limitations in the future. In addition, given the diversity of current preprocessing methods, there is still no consensus on which preprocessing method offers the best cost-effectiveness in clinical applications. On the other hand, few studies have explored the optimal dosage and timing for the application of MSCs and their exosomes, which are the most direct and unavoidable issues in clinical practice. Despite the promising results of various MSC pretreatment methods, there is a significant lack of standardization regarding key parameters such as chemical concentration, exposure duration, and the timing of application. These variables can influence the functional output of MSCs, including their viability, paracrine activity, immunomodulatory effects. For example, low-dose hypoxic pretreatment strategy may enhance the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs, whereas excessive or prolonged exposure may trigger oxidative stress. Similarly, cytokine or pharmacological pretreatment strategies must be carefully adjusted, as both low-dose or high-dose pretreatment can lead to inconsistent or even side effects. Therefore, future studies should focus on optimal pretreatment parameters, including chemical concentrations, treatment times, dosage, duration.

Existing technologies struggle to track the survival, distribution, and functional status of transplanted MSCs in real-time, resulting in unclear therapeutic mechanisms and a lack of evidence-based dosage optimization, which exacerbates the severity of this issue. While pretreatment strategies have great promise in enhancing MSC efficacy, it is important to balance this optimism with caution. For example, genetic modification can effectively enhance specific functions of MSCs, but it also raises concerns about potential genetic instability and may face strict regulatory barriers in clinical translation. Chemical pretreatments may be easier to control, but their long-term effects remain unknown. Therefore, we should carefully assess the risk–benefit before clinical application.

Compared to basic research, the clinical application of MSCs and their exosomes faces more problems that need to be addressed, so a more cautious attitude and greater efforts are required.

Balancing Efficacy and Safety: The effective dose established in animal studies (typically based on cell number/body weight) is difficult to extrapolate directly to humans. Current early-phase clinical trials often employ empirical dosing, lacking a consensus on a unified “dose-response” relationship. Future research must systematically investigate: (1) Dose Translation: More scientific interspecies scaling based on body surface area or pharmacokinetic modeling; (2) Administration Strategy: The comparative benefits of a single high-dose infusion versus fractionated repeated dosing, and its correlation with the optimal therapeutic time window (e.g., within 24–72 h post-AKI diagnosis); (3) Personalized Dosing: The possibility of dose adjustment based on patient weight, disease severity, or inflammatory status. Studies in other fields, such as the BOOST-2 trial for stem cells in myocardial infarction, which explored different dose cohorts, provide a methodological reference for nephrology.

Long-term Safety and Tumorigenicity Risk Assessment: The long-term safety of cell-based products, particularly the risk of tumorigenicity, is a paramount concern for regulators and clinicians. Current assessment faces several challenges: (1) Insufficient Follow-up Duration: Most phase I/II trials are limited to 1–2 years of follow-up, inadequate for evaluating long-term risks; (2) Complex Mechanisms: Risks arise not only from the malignant transformation potential of the cells themselves (related to donor age and in vitro expansion passages) but also from off-target organ engraftment, aberrant differentiation, or induction of immune dysregulation. Consequently, a multi-tiered evaluation system is essential: Preclinically, standard in vivo tumorigenicity assays (e.g., subcutaneous implantation in immunodeficient mice) and genomic stability analysis must be completed; Clinically, a long-term registry spanning 5–10 years should be established to monitor tumor incidence, autoimmune diseases, and ectopic tissue formation.

Regulatory Pathways and Compliance Challenges: Cell products are typically categorized as Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products (ATMPs), subject to stricter regulatory frameworks than conventional drugs (e.g., the Biologics License Application (BLA) pathway with the FDA or the Centralized ATMP Authorization in the EU). Key hurdles include: (1) Complexity of Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls (CMC): Defining the characteristics of living cells as a “product” is inherently difficult; (2) Potency Assays: The need to develop in vitro bioactivity tests predictive of clinical efficacy, which is crucial for demonstrating product “quality”; (3) Clinical Endpoints: Agreement with regulators on acceptable surrogate endpoints (e.g., slope of eGFR decline) to accelerate approval. Early engagement with regulatory agencies through meetings like the FDA’s INTERACT program is critical to delineate development requirements.

Broader Prospects for Clinical Translation of Pretreated MSCs: In addition to these technical parameters, a forward-thinking approach must also emphasize the importance of international consensus. Global alignment facilitated by organizations such as ISCT, ISEV, and ISO will be crucial in establishing universally accepted criteria for MSC pretreatment, production, and quality assessment, thereby facilitating multi-center clinical trials and regulatory approval across different regions. Lessons can also be drawn from other advanced cell therapies such as CAR-T or iPSC-derived products. Such cell therapies have strict GMP compliance, standardized potency testing, and clearly defined release criteria, which accelerating clinical translation. Cross-disciplinary innovations are expected to further transform the field. Automated bioreactor systems may allow reproducible pretreatment processes, while bioengineering approaches such as 3D bioprinting can provide more physiologically relevant conditioning microenvironments. Moreover, AI-driven quality control have potential for real-time monitoring of cell state, optimize pretreatment parameters, and finally improving both safety and cost-effectiveness. Beyond technical issues, policy and regulatory factors must also be mentioned. Cost-effectiveness analyses, reimbursement pathways, and supportive government–industry partnerships can play a significant role in determining the clinical accessibility of pretreated MSC products.

Exploration of New Pretreatment Methods: Through recent research, it has become evident that one of the key focuses in MSC pretreatment methods is to simulate or precondition their native microenvironment. Whether through hypoxia, cytokine co-culture, 3D culture, or optimized culture conditions, these approaches revolve around this central theme. Additionally, targeted interventions based on known signaling pathways represent another strategy. By activating or inhibiting critical pathways regulating MSC survival, proliferation, migration, differentiation, and paracrine functions, the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs can be enhanced. Engineering-based pretreatment and certain chemical pretreatment methods follow this rationale. Therefore, it is foreseeable that advancements in understanding MSC biological characteristics and molecular pathways will further inspire the development of novel pretreatment strategies. The current advancements in artificial intelligence technology may provide some assistance for future predictions regarding preprocessing-related target sites and methods.

Possibility of combination therapy: some studies have explored the use of multiple pretreatment strategies, and evidence suggests that combining two pretreatment methods can enhance the therapeutic effects of MSCs compared to a single approach. Therefore, the combination of pretreatment methods to modify the therapeutic effect of MSCs may be a direction for future research.

Conclusions

Compared to directly administered naïve MSCs or their secretome, pretreated MSCs and their derived products demonstrate superior survival rates and enhanced homing capabilities. This advancement partially addresses the critical limitation of poor microenvironmental adaptability inherent in conventional MSC therapies. To further optimize this strategy, future research should prioritize the following actionable directions:
