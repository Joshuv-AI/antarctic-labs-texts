Ophthalmic Comorbidities in Patients with Chronic Plaque Psoriasis: A Hospital-Based Cross-Sectional Study of 100 Patients
Authors: Kritika Katoch, Rattan Kumar Sharma, Vikram K. Mahajan, Rajeev Tuli
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To study the characteristics of ophthalmic comorbidities in patients with chronic plaque psoriasis.Methods:We assessed the clinical and epidemiological attributes and characteristics of ophthalmic comorbidities of patients who had chronic plaque psoriasis for 15–25 years (mean ± standard deviation [SD], 3.9 ± 4.8 years). Out of 100 patients, 57 were males and 43 were females. In this cohort, the age range was from 19 to 77 years (mean ± SD, 41.8 ± 13.9 years). The severity of psoriasis was defined by the psoriasis area severity index (PASI) score as mild (PASI ≤6), moderate (PASI >6–12), or severe (PASI >12).Results:Psoriasis was mild in 77, moderate in 13, and severe in 10 patients, respectively. Three patients had asymmetric oligoarthritis. In a cohort of 100 patients, there were 23 patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis and 77 patients with mild psoriasis. In the moderate-to-severe psoriasis group, 18 out of the 23 patients also had ophthalmic abnormalities. In the mild psoriasis group, 53 of 77 patients also had ophthalmic abnormalities. Itching (n = 12; psoriasis was mild in 7, moderate-to-severe in 5 patients), decreased vision (n = 12; psoriasis was mild in 10, moderate-to-severe in 2 patients), redness (n = 9; psoriasis was mild in 5, moderate-to-severe in 5 patients;), watering/discharge (n = 8; psoriasis was mild in 6, moderate-to-severe in 2 patients), and pain or burning (n = 6; psoriasis was mild in 4, moderate-to-severe in 2 patients) were commonly complained by 31 patients. The major ophthalmic comorbidities in order of frequency observed were keratoconjunctivitis sicca/dry eye (n = 41), blepharitis (n = 29), meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) (n = 18), conjunctivitis (n = 10), corneal abnormalities (n = 13), cataract (n = 14), retention cyst (n = 2), and anterior uveitis in 2 patients (without psoriatic arthritis). Common corneal abnormalities were punctuate keratitis (n = 7), corneal opacities (n = 4), band-shaped keratopathy (n = 1), and superficial vascularization (n = 1). Posterior segment manifestations of arteriosclerotic changes (n = 4), media haze (n = 2), and macular degeneration (n = 1) were likely to be age-related.Conclusions:The ophthalmic comorbidities, such as blepharitis, MGD, conjunctivitis, keratoconjunctivitis sicca, corneal abnormalities, and presenile cataract, can occur in patients with psoriasis. These are largely asymptomatic and subtle findings that are often missed. They require an early consult to ophthalmology for appropriate management. The major limitations in this study are that it is a single-center, cross-sectional study design that has a low number of patients, lacks a healthy patient control group, and there is a lack of long-term follow-up.

INTRODUCTION

Psoriasis is a chronic, relapsing, and potentially debilitating inflammatory dermatosis affecting 0.2%–4.8% of the population worldwide. It has an estimated prevalence of 2.2% to 2.63% in the US with approximately 150,000 newly diagnosed cases per year.12 All forms of psoriasis eventually evolve into chronic plaque psoriasis, which is characterized clinically by well-demarcated, erythematous, scaly plaques of varying severity. Patients with psoriasis are at increased risk of developing other diseases due to shared genetic pathways, common immune mechanisms, treatment-related toxicities, and the associated psychological stress of the disease. Psoriasis can directly affect the eyes or can be seen as a complication of treatment in about 10% of patients.34 Ophthalmic complications of psoriasis include madarosis, cicatricial ectropion, trichiasis, loss of lid (due to erythema), edema, and psoriatic plaques around the eyes. Obstructive meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD), resultant blepharitis, and keratoconjunctivitis sicca (dry eye syndrome) are also common complications.3456789 Lid involvement can extend to involve the conjunctiva which may progress to chronic nonspecific conjunctivitis. This can also lead to symblepharon and keratoconjunctivitis sicca. Keratoconjunctivitis sicca has been reported in up to 18.7% of patients with psoriasis and up to 2.7% of psoriatic arthritis patients.1011 Complications include punctate keratitis, filaments, epithelial thickening, recurrent erosions, vascularization, ulceration, scarring, and even corneal melting, especially at the periphery.7 Uveitis in psoriatic patients tends to be bilateral, prolonged, and more severe, especially in those patients who have psoriatic arthritis.12 Although uncommon, retinal vasculitis, cystoid macular edema, and papillitis have also been reported.13 In addition, topical and systemic treatment modalities can also affect the eyes.141516 However, ophthalmic comorbidities/symptoms in psoriasis largely remain under-looked, and the quality-of-life surveys for psoriasis rarely address this problem. We studied the characteristics of ophthalmic comorbidities in patients with chronic plaque psoriasis.

METHODS

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Date 06/07/2017/No. HFW-H-DRPGMC/Ethics/2017-37). All procedures followed were in accordance with ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation (institutional and national) and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2013. After the necessary approvals from the institutional ethics committee, we started enrolling patients with chronic plaque psoriasis with or without the presence of psoriatic arthritis. These patients presented to the dermatology clinic between July 2017 and June 2018 and provided written informed consent for enrollment. The exclusion criteria included: Children <18 years, pregnant and lactating women, patients using contact lenses, or patients with the following diagnoses: Diabetes mellitus, hypertension, collagen vascular disorders, sarcoidosis, or Behçet’s disease.

We collected demographic data, patient and family history, medical history, and clinical details for the type of psoriasis, duration, severity, presence of arthritis, and the nature of treatment taken. Psoriasis is a clinical diagnosis that is characterized by the presence of characteristic skin lesions, and its severity is assessed by the psoriasis area severity index (PASI) score. It is divided as follows: Mild (PASI ≤6), moderate (PASI >6–12), or severe (PASI >12).17 The Moll and Wright criteria guided the psoriatic arthritis diagnosis.18 This includes distal interphalangeal joint arthritis, asymmetrical mono/oligoarthritis, symmetrical polyarthritis, axial arthritis, and arthritis mutilans.

The history of ocular complications was recorded, and on physical examination, patients were examined to check for the presence of abnormal head posture, psoriatic lesions over the periocular skin, ocular position, and extraocular movements. A detailed clinical examination for periocular skin and ocular abnormalities was performed.19 Standard Snellen’s and Jaeger’s charts were used to assess the best-corrected visual acuity. The intraocular pressure was measured by the puff test with a noncontact applanation tonometer and/or a Schiotz tonometer. The ocular surface, eyelids, meibomian glands, conjunctiva, cornea, and the tear film were examined to assess for inflammation and any abnormalities under the slit-lamp biomicroscope (Haag Streit BM 900). The grading of severity of meibomian gland disease was assessed as described by Foulks and Bron [Box 1].20 Schirmer’s ɪ test and tear film break-up time (TBUT) were measured by using a cobalt blue filter on the slit-lamp examination to assess for keratoconjunctivitis sicca. Briefly, a Schirmer’s strip (5 mm × 35 mm Whatman filter paper strip) was left in the lower fornix of the open eye at its lateral 1/3rd and medial 2/3rd junction for Schirmer’s I test. After 5 min, the length of filter paper wetting was measured, and wetting >10 mm was considered normal. TBUT was performed after 30 min; a sterile fluorescein strip (containing 1 mg fluorescein sodium) moistened with normal saline was placed on the inferior bulbar conjunctiva. The interval between the appearance of the first dry spot and a complete blink was measured for three successive attempts, and their average was taken as a final measurement. All the patients with reduced TBUT were further assessed under the slit-lamp biomicroscope for ocular surface disease using the rose Bengal 1% solution or sodium fluorescein dye 2% solution in each eye. The presence of punctate staining was suggestive of excess mucus, dead or dying cells, or conjunctival de-epithelialization. Examination of the anterior segment of the eye was performed under ×1 to ×1.6 magnification for the presence of hypopyon, hyphema, keratic precipitates, aqueous cells, flare, pupillary membrane, fibrinous reaction, and any other abnormalities of the iris.

Based on the anatomical involvement, the eye the uveitis was classified into anterior (inflammation of anterior uveal tract involving iris and pars plicata of ciliary body), intermediate (inflammation in the anterior vitreous, ciliary body, and the peripheral retina), posterior (inflammation of the choroid), and panuveitis (inflammation of all the layers of the uvea) according to the Uveitis Nomenclature Working Group Grading system.21 The anterior uveitis was further sub classified depending on its clinical course into; (i) acute anterior uveitis manifesting with episodes of sudden onset and of limited duration, (ii) recurrent anterior uveitis of 3 or more months duration with recurrent episodes punctuated by periods of inactivity without treatment, and (iii) chronic uveitis that persists and relapses in <3 months after discontinuation of treatment.21

When present, cataracts were evaluated by the Lens Opacities Classification System III and classified into nuclear sclerotic, cortical, or posterior subcapsular type.22 A presenile cataract was defined as a form of cataract with early onset before the age of 50 years.

After dilating the pupils with tropicamide (1%), the posterior segment was examined by indirect and direct lens (Heine beta 200S ophthalmoscope) ophthalmoscopy. A detailed fundus examination was performed with a slit-lamp biomicroscope with +90 diopter (D) aspheric Volk lens, and when needed, the indirect ophthalmoscopy 20 D aspheric lens was used. Retinal angiography was performed only if the fundus showed any abnormalities. All patients were treated according to the standard of care requirements.

Statistical analysis

MS OfficeTM Excel® software was used to tabulate and organize demographic data, clinical examination findings for chronic plaque psoriasis, and the associated ophthalmic comorbidities for the 100 patients who completed the study. The continuous data is presented as means and standard deviation (SD), while the categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. The Mann–Whitney nonparametric test was used for variables that were not distributed normally. The χ2 test/Student’s t-test was used for statistical analysis of the categorical and parametric data. P = 0.05 calculated at 5% level (95% confidence interval) was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Seven patients did not complete the evaluation procedure and were excluded from the final analysis. The other 100 patients comprised 57 males and 43 females (M:F = 1.32:1) aged 19 − 77 years (mean ± SD: 41.8 ± 13.9 years) [Table 1]. The duration of psoriasis varied from 15 days to 25 years (mean ± SD: 3.9 ± 4.9 years), and 27 patients had the disease for >5 years. The severity of psoriasis was moderate-to-severe (PASI >6) in 23 patients, and three patients had associated asymmetric oligoarthritis. Fifty-eight patients were treated with emollients, corticosteroids, and tar 3% ointment topically, while 27 patients were taking oral methotrexate 15 mg/week intermittently alone or in combination with topical/supportive treatments.

DISCUSSION

Overall, the clinical and demographic profile of our patients is similar with previous studies.232425 The reported prevalence of ophthalmic comorbidities in psoriasis is nearly 50% to 80% and is more frequent compared to healthy controls, and at least one ophthalmic abnormality was reported in 67% of patients.2627282930 However, there is an increased risk of ophthalmic involvement affecting up to 10% of patients with psoriatic arthritis, and this may even precede articular changes.34 Although only 31% patients had subjective ocular complaints such as itching, burning, and pain, decreased vision, redness, and watering of the eyes, one or more ophthalmic comorbidities were observed in 71% patients after ophthalmic examination in our study. Kilic et al.31 observed blepharitis, conjunctivitis, episcleritis, corneal involvement (punctate epithelial erosions, vascularization, corneal opacity, or filaments), anterior uveitis, pigment dispersion, cataract, and cystoid macular edema more frequently in patients than in the control group. Ocular inflammation was seen in 31% of 112 psoriasis patients in an earlier study. The inflammation presented as conjunctivitis in 19.6%, iritis in 7.1%, keratoconjunctivitis sicca in 2.7%, and episcleritis in 1.8% patients.32 Similarly, the prevalence of keratoconjunctivitis sicca in 16%−39%, blepharitis in 16%−24%, pterygium in 34.9%, cataract in 30.2%, pinguecula in 13.9%, keratitis in 11.6%, and conjunctival hyperemia in 9.3% patients, respectively, have been reported in two separate studies.333 However, Cruz et al.33 found a statistically higher frequency of blepharitis and dry eye in psoriasis patients as compared to the controls. The dry eye might have been responsible for the media haze seen in our two patients, as no other known etiology could be identified. Blepharitis overlaps substantially with MGD, affecting psoriasis patients across all ages and ethnic groups. This happens more often in patients with scalp involvement as compared to healthy controls and we also noticed this in our study as 40.8% patients presented with scalp lesions as well.283334

Chronic, nonspecific conjunctivitis is another commonly reported abnormality, and conjunctival injection has been observed more often in psoriasis patients than in healthy controls.373335 The prevalence of conjunctival injection, pinguecula, and the results of OSDI, TBUT, positive Schirmer’s ɪ test, and rose bengal staining were also significantly greater among patients with psoriasis than in the control group in an Egyptian study.35 Conjunctivitis was present in 10% of our patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis, and it appears to have a positive correlation with disease severity. We observed keratoconjunctivitis sicca in 57.7% of psoriasis patients as compared to the reported prevalence of 32% in the normal populations.36 Bhargava and Kumar10 observed that the values of Schirmer’s ɪ test and TBUT were low in patients with psoriasis as compared to controls. Although we noted that the mean Schirmer’s ɪ test and TBUT values decreased as the duration of psoriasis and the PASI score increased, it was not statistically significant. This is different from what has previously been seen in other studies.35 However, keratoconjunctivitis sicca was seen with greater frequency in patients with a longer and more severe disease course. Whether it could be attributed to the commonly implicated factors such as aging, abnormalities of the outer lipid layer, middle aqueous layer, and inner mucous layer, or the psoriasis per se remains conjectural.610373839 However, psoriasis-associated noncicatricial obstructive MGD from hyperkeratinization of gland duct epithelium seems more plausible, as has been suggested previously.610 Apparently, these noninflammatory changes in the long term may also result in ductal dilation, gland atrophy, and decreased number of functional meibocytes, poor functioning and hyposecretion of viscous meibum, disrupting in turn the stability of the tear film and compromising ocular surface health. Furthermore, some degree of fibrosis from psoriasis-associated chronic inflammation and resultant noncicatricial hyposecretory state could be another disruptor for meibomian gland function.

One patient each had punctate keratitis, corneal opacities, superficial vascularization, and band-shaped keratopathy, respectively. These patients had moderate-to-severe psoriasis of >5 years; this association was statistically significant and is in sync with the reported literature.334041 Anterior uveitis is another comorbidity occurring in 7%−25% of patients with psoriatic arthritis which is considered a risk factor for ocular inflammation. Anterior uveitis, a risk factor for ocular inflammation, is another comorbidity that occurs in 7%–25% of patients with psoriatic arthritis.3101442 However, it can also occur independently of arthropathy, and it tends to be anterior, bilateral, and chronic (mean duration 11.2 weeks) irrespective of the severity of psoriasis.1227414243 We also observed similar variability in two patients with acute anterior uveitis (three episodes in one case) presenting with mild psoriasis without arthritis. Whereas, the other three patients with psoriatic arthritis had keratoconjunctivitis sicca, band-shaped keratopathy, or MGD alone. We hypothesize that band-shaped keratopathy in one of the patients with psoriatic arthritis could be from a previous episode of uveitis.

Although the prevalence of cataract among psoriasis patients usually does not exceed that of the normal population, 5.3%−29.7% of patients with psoriasis that are <40 years may have cataract.34445 The presence of presenile cataract, mainly the posterior subcapsular type, in 7 (28%) out of the 25 eyes examined reflects the necessity of regular ophthalmic examination in psoriasis patients, specifically when they present with diminished vision.

Ocular complications may also be from antipsoriasis therapies such as retinoids (dry eye, corneal opacities, and cataract), psoralens, ultraviolet-A (cataract, dry eye, conjunctival hyperemia, and macular burns), and prolonged use of corticosteroids (cataract, glaucoma, dry eye, retinal emboli events, and central serous choroidopathy). Furthermore, periorbital edema, ocular pain, blurred vision, photophobia, conjunctivitis, blepharitis, decreased reflex tear secretion, and nonarteritic ischemic optic neuropathy have also been described as being associated with high-dose methotrexate chemotherapy for cancer.464748 However, the association between ophthalmic comorbidities and low-dose methotrexate used for psoriasis treatment remains poorly elucidated. The association of keratoconjunctivitis sicca in 20 (74%) out of 27 patients on systemic methotrexate treatment was statistically significant as compared to 22 (44%) of 50 patients on topical therapies (P < 0.05). However, it remains possible that their moderate-to-severe psoriasis might have primarily caused it, as they have only received low-dose methotrexate treatment intermittently. Nevertheless, a significant correlation between the objective indicators of keratitis sicca and methotrexate has been reported in patients of autoimmune rheumatic diseases treated similarly with methotrexate.4950 Thus, it remains distinctly possible that methotrexate might have played some role in causing or exacerbating symptoms of dry eye in psoriasis patients as well. Furthermore, accumulation of methotrexate is higher in mucosal epithelial cells having high mitotic activity compared to others, and being polyglutaminated, its intracellular presence is prolonged and might have been another cause keratoconjunctivitis sicca, attributable perhaps to direct drug toxicity.51

Although the correlation between ophthalmic comorbidities and gender, age, or duration of psoriasis remains poorly correlated, ophthalmic comorbidities are significantly more common with increasing severity of psoriasis likely due to a greater inflammatory burden.3293133 Similarly, the flare value was reportedly higher in patients over the age of 40 who have moderate-to-severe psoriasis and in those with a disease duration >10 years.5253 Although not statistically significant, the ophthalmic comorbidities in 73.9% of our patients who had psoriasis for more than 5 years were more prevalent as compared to 50% of patients who had psoriasis for <5 years. Similarly, a significantly higher number (83.3%) of our patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis had ophthalmic comorbidities, particularly corneal opacities and keratoconjunctivitis sicca, compared to 49% patients with mild disease.

Ophthalmic comorbidities in patients with psoriasis are not uncommon but their magnitude perhaps remains under-evaluated, particularly for timely diagnosis and management. Another possible reason could be that the ophthalmic comorbidities of psoriasis are too subtle and remain underdiagnosed and underreported without a dedicated ophthalmic evaluation. Blepharitis, MGD, conjunctivitis, keratoconjunctivitis sicca, corneal abnormalities, and presenile cataract are more frequent. Although seen infrequently in this study, uveitis is a potential sight-threatening complication necessitating early detection and treatment to prevent irreversible vision loss. However, the association of otherwise age-related posterior segment manifestations such as arteriosclerotic changes, media haze, macular degeneration, and psoriasis remains uncertain. A single-center, cross-sectional study design, with a small number of patients in each group for stratification and a lack of healthy controls, and long-term follow-up are the major limitations. Follow-up for the therapeutic outcome was not a part of the study. Better-designed prospective studies addressing these limitations will aid in understanding the complete spectrum of ocular comorbidities in psoriasis. Nevertheless, a high degree of clinical suspicion and urgency for an early ophthalmic evaluation is needed for a timely diagnosis and proper management in psoriasis patients with ophthalmic comorbidities.
