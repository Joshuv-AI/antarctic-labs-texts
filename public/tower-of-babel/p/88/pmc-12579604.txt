Quantitative Analysis of Monocyte-Derived Macrophage NFκB Signaling in Cancer Co-culture Models Using Luciferase-Based Biosensing
Authors: Colette Li, Megha Anand, Garrett McPheron, Maeve Stiles, Elizabeth Wayne
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and PurposeIn this study, we investigate the plasticity of tumor-associated macrophages, which originate from circulating monocytes and are associated with poor cancer prognosis. The differentiation of monocytes into macrophages is a dynamic and spatiotemporal process, as is the resulting macrophages’ polarization. However, traditional methods for measuring polarization, such as qPCR and flow cytometry, provide only static information about polarization. To supplement these methods, we present a novel bioluminescent method that allows for time-resolved measurement of NFκB activation in macrophages while in co-culture with cancer cells. By using a monocyte cell line whose NFκB responsive element is labeled with firefly luciferase, we obtain a quantitative and temporal characterization of macrophage polarization in response to tumor-derived signals.Materials and MethodsTo quantify the effect of tumor cell signaling THP-1 monocytes encoded with a firefly luciferase NFκB response element were co-cultured with cancer cells. We investigated the impact of the following factors on NFκB signaling cancer cell type (HCT116 or MDA-MB-231), ratio of the number of cancer cells to macrophages in co-culture, and the THP-1 cell differentiation state (monocyte or monocyte-derived macrophage). Bioluminescence was measured over three days. Descriptive features of the bioluminescence response curves were then extracted to compare effects between cancer types.ResultsWe observed that the MDA-MB-231 cancer cells induced lower but more persistent NFκB activation in THP-1 monocyte-derived macrophages than was observed in HCT116 co-culture. Higher number of cancer cells (lower macrophage ratio) elicited higher AUC values in HCT116 co-culture compared to low cancer cell conditions. There was no difference between high and low macrophage ratios within the MDA-MB-231 co-culture condition. Moreover, the addition of macrophage differentiation stimuli modulated the NFκB profile in the co-culture. In contrast, MDA-MB-231 cancer cells elicited lower but more persistent NFκB activation profiles. This study highlights how bioluminescence reporter assays can be used to extract meaningful metrics about monocyte/macrophage behavior during tumor progression. Introduction

Monocytes are versatile innate immune cells that influence tumor progression and therapeutic responses [1, 2]. Monocytes circulate through blood vasculature and extravasate into peripheral organs, connecting systemic inflammation to local tumor environments (TME). Once in the inflamed tissue, monocytes can differentiate into macrophages, dendritic cells, or myeloid derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [3–5]. These cells perform varying effector functions including cytokine production, phagocytosis, extracellular matrix remodeling, angiogenesis, and antigen presentation. The quality of the responses contribute to the immune landscape, resulting in the production of a tumor suppressive or a tumor-supporting environment.

Tumors also shape monocyte infiltration and polarization, shifting them towards an immunosuppressive, pro-tumoral phenotype. Tumors signal to the bone marrow to increase monocyte production and survival in a manner dependent on MCSF receptor expression [6]. Resulting increases in numbers of peripheral circulating monocytes, clinically measured as a lower lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR) has been correlated with both a progression free survival and a lower overall survival [7–9].

Monocytes and monocyte derived macrophages (ModMs) can also interfere with chemotherapy efficacy. Monocytes with high expression of programmed death 1 (PD-L1) correlated with poor response to chemotherapy in NSCLC patients [10, 11]. Due to their involvement in tumor immunosuppression, monocytes have been therapeutically targeted. Reduction of monocyte tumor infiltration using anti-CSF strategies have resulted in better prognosis and improvement in chemotherapy and immunotherapy treatment strategies [12]. Other strategies have employed monocytes as drug delivery carriers, synergizing their migration into the tumor with the added need for specialized transport and delivery of immunomodulatory drugs.

Biochemical and mechanical components of the TME give instructions to monocytes, influencing the temporal dynamics of transcriptional programming [13–15]. One of the most common is nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NFκB). NFκB is family of transcription activating proteins known as master regulators of inflammation; it can be activated by viral or bacterial products, cytokines, growth factors, lipids, reactive oxygen species, DNA damage, ER stress, and oncogenic stress. NFκB activation and downstream signaling leads to changes in cell survival, immune cell development, and regulating inflammatory responses [16]. NFκB consist of five members: p50/p105, p-52/p100, RelA/p65, RelB and c-Rel, which bind to promoter regions in DNA to commence transcription of a specialized set of genes. Despite the extensiveness of mechanisms to initiate upstream NFκB signaling, there are only two distinct pathways of downstream processing: canoncial or noncanonical. Canonical activation is characterized by RelA/p50 while non-canonical activation is triggered by RelB/p52 related transcriptional activity [17, 18]. Canonical NFκB signaling is rapidly activated and is usually transient due to strong degradation of free IκBα [19]. Non-canonical activation operates differently, exhibiting slow and persistent activation due to the additional time required for stabilization, accumulation (protein synthesis), and degradation steps in the activation pathway [20, 21]. Canonical NFκB activation is initiated via pattern recognition receptor signaling (i.e., TLRs, RLRs, NLRs) and allows cells to respond quickly to infections and cellular stress/damage. In contrast, non-canonical NFκB signaling is activated by a narrower range of signals (e.g. BaFF, Lymphotoxin beta (LTβ), CD40) and controls cell survival, immune cell development and immune function.

Tumor infiltrating monocytes and tumor associated macrophages exhibit altered NFκB signaling. For example, macrophages can have prolonged nuclear presence of NFκB protein where other cells such as fibroblasts experience periodic cycling [22]. In colorectal cancers, NFκB signaling through the p50 subunit has been implicated in promoting a tumor promoting phenotype in macrophages [17]. In addition, NFκB signaling inhibition leads to increased M1, pro-inflammatory activation and has been used as a therapeutic target [22, 23].

The tributary nature of NFκB activation combined with the constrained downstream signaling make NFκB both an attractive and complicated system to model. Fluorescence imaging shows that NFκB dimer nuclear translocation is known to oscillate at close to 90 min intervals [24]. These oscillations are dependent on stimuli type and concentration. Luciferase response element reporters have been used to assay the downstream transcription that occurs following NFκB nuclear localization [25]. Response element systems facilitate measurement of NFκB activity under various conditions without the need to perform genetic (i.e., qPCR) or protein analysis (i.e., ELISA) tests [26–28].

While there is substantial peer-reviewed literature about the genomic and proteomic changes that occur in tumor-infiltrated monocytes, there is a need for real-time measurements that retain information about the magnitude and duration of activation to provide more insight. In this study, we co-culture human THP-1 monocytes NFκB luciferase reporter with cancer cells to measure the temporal response in NFκB activation during the early phases of monocyte infiltration into the tumor microenvironment. We hypothesize that the kinetics of NFκB activation can aid in measuring how monocytes transition into tumor environments. This study explores monocyte co-culture between two distinct cell lines, human colorectal HCT116 and human breast cancer MDA-MB-231 which have high or low levels of non-canonical NFκB activation signaling, respectively [23].

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

THP-1 NFκB FLuc cells were created by transducing THP-1 acute monocytic leukemia cells (American Type Culture Collection) with NFκB Firefly luciferase reporter lentivirus (Cellomics Technology, LLC.) at a multiplicity of infection of 50. The transduced cells were selected using puromycin. THP-1 Nuclight Red cells were created by transducing THP-1 wt cells with Incucyte Nuclight Red lentivirus (Sartorius AG). All THP-1 cell types were used before 20 passages and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum in an incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2. HCT116 colorectal carcinoma cells (ATCC) were cultured in 5A medium with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum and MDA-MB-231 breast adenocarcinoma cells (ATCC) in DMEM medium with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum.

Macrophage and Tumor Cell 2D Co-culture BTS

We seeded 10,000 THP-1 NFκB FLuc or THP-1 Nuclight Red cells in white clear-bottomed 96 well plates. If the cells were first differentiated then Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (Peprotech), M-CSF (STEMCELL Technologies), or GM-CSF (STEMCELL Technologies) were added at 50 ng/mL and allowed to incubate overnight before the tumor cells were added. The number of HCT116 or MDA-MB-231 were varied in order to create macrophage to tumor cell ratios of 10:1, 1:1, and 1:10. The cells were cultured in a 50/50 mixture of RPMI 1640 and 5A or DMEM with 1 mM D-Luciferin added, unless otherwise specified, due to a mixture producing the clearest separation of NFκB Luciferase curves (Supplemental Fig. S1) and optimal THP1 cell survival as evidenced by fluorescence imaging (Supplemental Figure S2).The plates were centrifuged for 200 g for 10 min. Then luminescence was measured using a spectrophotometer between 490 and 605 nm with an integration time of 10 s. Every 12 h media was replaced, and luminescence was measured. Luminescence was normalized with the protein concentration in the media at each time point measured using a BCA protein assay (Thermo Scientific) using the manufacturer protocol.

Macrophage Polarization Curves

Twenty thousand THP-1 NFκB FLuc cells were seeded in white clear-bottomed 96 well plates. The cells were differentiated using 10 ng/mL PMA overnight and then rested for 48 h. The media was then replaced with RPMI with 1 mM D-Luciferin and the baseline was measured for 24 h before cytokines or other stimulants were added. Luminescence was monitored for 72 h using the method specified above and normalized by protein concentration in the media using a BCA protein assay.

qPCR Analysis

RNA was isolated from lysate samples using RNeasy Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the concentration was quantified using a NanoDrop. The RNA was converted to cDNA using High-Capacity RNA-to-cDNA kit (applied biosystems by ThermoFisher Scientific) by the manufacturer’s instructions. qCR was performed with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix on Applied Bioscience ViiA 7 Real-Time PCR System. mRNA quantification was done using the ΔΔCT method and normalized to 18 s for the in vitro samples and ACTB for ex vivo tumor samples. The primers used in this study are in Table S1.

Calculation of Temporal Activation Curve Metrics

Following data collection from the luciferase time-course measurements, several metrics were calculated. Area under the curve (AUC) was calculated using the using the numeral approximation methods “trapezoidal rule” using Python [29]. Time to maximum response (TimeMAX) was calculated by identifying the location of maximum RLU intensity using np.argmax() in python. Response curves were separated by manual segmentation into Rising, resolving, and ending stages. The slopes for each stage were calculated by computing the change in RLU intensity divided by the time interval being assessed, similar to other published methods [30].

Statistical Analysis

Figures were created and analyzed using GraphPad Prism (San Diego, California). One- or two-way ANOVA followed by post-hoc Tukey tests or Sidak’s multiple comparisons for multiple comparisons were performed to determine significance. Significance in figures is connoted as follows: p < .0001(****), p < .001 (***), p < .01(**), p < .05 (*). Microsoft Excel (Redmond, Washington) was used to compute the area under the curve, slopes, protein concentration off a standard curve, and to normalize the luminescence values.

Results

ModMs Polarize to NFκB Polarization Strongest Under TNFα

First, we investigated NFκB polarization temporal response profile under mono-culture and single-dose conditions. We exposed THP-1 NFκB Luciferase reporter cells to pro-inflammatory cytokines and measured luciferase activity (Figure 1A). Each stimuli triggers NFkB activation through different pathways, highlighting the diversity of NFκB responsiveness. LPS, IFNγ, R848, and TNFα trigger NFκB activation via TLR4, RIP kinase, TLR7/8, or TNFR, respectively [31–33]. The temporal curves demonstrate a quick, sharp spike in activation following stimulation (Figure 1B). By comparing different cytokine stimuli, we sought to demonstrate how the temporal measurement of macrophage polarization can provide more opportunity for granular analysis in comparison to a single time-point assessment. Analysis of the temporal profile indicated that IFNγ and LPS had a higher maximum response (Figure 1C) than R848 (Resiquimod), a TLR7/8 agnoinst. TimeMAX measures how long it takes to reach the maximum NFκB signal. The results appear to not be significant with all cytokines peaking at or before 12 h (Figure 1D). R848 activates NFκB via TLR7/8/MyD88-dependent signaling and had the smallest peak response and reached peak activity the fastest (Figure 1E) [33]. However, when we computed the area under the curve (AUC) to reflect the total activation window of NFκB activity R848 is more comparable to LPS/IFNγ due to its long activation window. TNFα caused a large spike in activation with a maximum response significantly higher than every other condition.

Bioluminescent Response Curve Reveals Signatures of Polarization Due to Tumor Co-Culture Signals

Upon entrance into the tumor microenvironment, monocytes undergo transcription changes that influence their cell fate. To understand how tumor cells can modulate monocyte NFkB activation, we measured NFκB activation profiles of monocytes co-cultured with different cancer cell lines (Figure 2A). We investigated two cell lines linked with strong NFκB activation HCT116, a colorectal cancer cell line, or MDA-MB-231, a triple negative breast cancer cell line. Further we explored co-culture at two monocyte concentrations. The number of monocytes cultured was kept constant but the number of cancer cells added was changed so that we could control for the impact of monocyte numbers on initial NFκB luciferase activity. Monocytes cultured with MDA-MB-231 experienced a minimal initial spike in NFκB activation at either low macrophage ratio (Figure 2B) or high macrophage ratio (Figure 2D). These monocytes did see a gradual increase in NFκB activation at by Day 3. In contrast, monocytes cultured at high ratios (90%) with HCT116 demonstrated a significant spike in NFκB activation followed by a return to baseline expression (Figure 2C). The low macrophage condition when co-cultured with HCT116 cells did not experience a spike in activation (Figure 2E), suggesting the number of cancer cells present may impact the strength of NFκB signaling.

In conjunction, monocytes labeled with EF-1 Nuclight Red (Essen Bioscience) were fluorescently tracked to independently measure cell count during experiments. The fluorescence signal across each MDA-MB-231 condition was generally constant, the NFκB activation was slowly increasing over time (Figure 2F). The fluorescence data reveals that in the HCT116 low macrophage condition, NFκB activation was highest when the cell count was lowest and vice versa (Figure 2G). These findings suggest NFκB activation had a slow and gradual profile in MDA-MB-231-monocyte co-culture but a fast and transient profile in HCT116: monocyte co-culture.

Differentiation Stimulus Impact the THP-1 Monocyte-Derived Macrophage NFκB Activation Response to Tumor Co-culture Signals

The response to cancer cell exposure can vary depending on the differentiation mechanisms involved. To further understand this variability, we investigated the impact of differentiation stimuli on the response of macrophages to surrounding cancer cells. We examined the effects of two growth factors, GM-CSF and M-CSF, as well as Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) on monocyte differentiation into macrophages. PMA is commonly used as an in vitro monocyte differentiation additive and results in the activation of the canonical NFκB pathway [34–36]. In comparison, the growth factors GM-CSF and M-CSF induce differentiation via non-canonical NFκB pathway [37, 38].

At high cancer cell ratios both HCT116 (grey) and MDA-MB-231 (blue) co-cultures with PMA-differentiated macrophages (PMA-ModMs) showed a notable early spike in NFκB activity (Figure 3B). GM-CSF and M-CSF-MoDMs led to slower activation peaks in both co-cultures. In MDA-MB-231 cultures, GM-CSF and M-CSF-MoDMs had gradually increasing NFκB activity at the end of the experiment. At low cancer cell burden (high macrophage ratio) the difference between differentiation groups is less obvious (Figure 3C). All HCT116 co-culture groups had an increase in NFκB activity around Day 1. In contrast, except for PMA-ModMs the MDA-MB-231 co-culture groups are relatively flat throughout the experiment.

A Monocytic reporter line cells were differentiated using PMA, GM-CSF, or M-CSF for 24 h before adding cancer cells in a 10:1 (low macrophage percentage), or 1:10 ratio (high macrophage percentage). B Subsequently, NFκB activation was monitored over 72 h using BTS, with the HCT116 and MDA-MB-231 curves represented in black and blue, respectively. The experiments have an n = 8 biological replicates, except for the HCT116 co-cultures without a differentiation agent, which had n = 14 biological replicates

The difference in ratios suggest the unique cancer cell derived signals impact the rate of NFkB activation. GM-CSF-MoDMs and M-CSF-MoDMs displayed similar NFκB temporal profiles. Moreover, these groups were similar to the no-agent, control group. This is consistent with findings that cancer cells secrete these factors [39, 40].

Across all differentiation treatment conditions, HCT116-associated macrophages had a shorter time to reach a maximum response, TimeMAX (Figure 4A and B). This finding was true regardless of the number of macrophages present. Amongst the low macrophage groups, PMA-ModMs exhibited short TimeMAX than control and GM-CSF and M-CSF differentiated ModMs. AUC values were calculated by integrating the region under the temporal response curve to encapsulate the entire spectrum of NFkB activity [41]. PMA-ModMs were the only differentiation condition that led to differences in overall NFkB activity (Figure 4C). PMA-ModMs cultured with MDA-MB-231 cocultures had higher AUC values compared to HCT116 in both high (Figure 4D) and low macrophage ratios (Figure 4C). The fast activation time and low AUC values suggests the combination of PMA differentiation and HCT116 coculture induced fast and rapid NFκB activation. GM-CSF and M-CSF ModMs had similar overall NFkB activity to the control groups but both cancer types and MoDMs ratios. Analyzing features of the temporal response curve can highlight treatment conditions that render unique activation patterns that may not have been obvious from the original data.

Temporal Repolarization Curves Can be Used to Determine Immunosuppression

To further investigate the rates of change within the NFκB temporal profiles, we computed the slope (change in RLU/time) at three phases (Figure 5A). The rising slope captures the initial changes in NFκB activation. HCT116-associated ModMs had a higher rising slope compared to MDA-MB-231-associated ModMs (Figure 5B). The resolving slope helps evaluate how quickly NFκB becomes deactivated. Once again, HCT116 associated ModMs elicit a dramatic decrease in NFκB compared to MDA-MB-231 (Figure 5C). At higher macrophage ratios, both the rising slope and the resolving slope effect was diminished. The ending slope helps us understand whether NFκB activity approaches a baseline level. Here, MDA-MB-231-associated ModMs exhibit a strong positive slope at both low and high macrophage ratios (Figure 5D). In comparison, HCT116-associated ModMs have a neutral ending slope signifying a steady of NFκB activation.

B The rising slope is a measure of the speed and strength of the NFκB activation peak for each sample, calculated by determining the change in NFκB activation between baseline and the peak over the time taken to reach the maximum. C The resolving slope indicates how quickly the activation returns to a baseline and is calculated by determining the rate of NFκB activation as RLUs between the peak timepoint and the timepoint at which the curve returns to about baseline value over that time. D The ending slope calculates the rate of RLU production over time in the last 24 h of measurement. Unpaired t test with Welch’s correction p < .0001(****), p < .001 (***), p < .01(**), p < .05 (*). We analyzed gene expression changes in the co-cultures (Figure 5) to evaluate key proteins that would be involved in inducting NFκB activation observed in previous experiments. The MDA-MB-231 co-cultures demonstrated significant increases in TNFα, IL-10, and IL-6, with an apparent increase in CD40. Activating CD40 leads to non-canonical NFκB activation [42]. However, TNFα, a pro-inflammatory cytokine associated with canonical activation, was downregulated less in the MDA-MB-231 co-cultures than in the HCT116 ones. The most notable difference was in IL-6 expression which been linked most frequently with canonical NFκBactivation [43] (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Understanding how tumors modulate the activity of monocyte-derived macrophages is of great importance. In this study, we introduced a novel strategy for measuring the time-resolved activation of NFκB in ModMs while in co-culture with cancer cells. This approach quantifies the impact of the tumor cell signaling on monocyte activation which is critical for understanding how extracellular cues drive TAM formation. NFκB was chosen as the reporter due to its central but heterogeneous role in cancer. Within the tumor microenvironment, both cancer cells and immune components express NFκB and is linked to tumor development and progression. The in vitro temporal kinetics method utilized in this study enables study of crosstalk signaling in a high-throughput manner.

Our study focuses on the signaling effect of cancer cells on NFkB activation within surrounding monocytes or MoDMs. We demonstrated how the temporal NFκB activation curves in THP-1 cells vary by the cancer cell type and ratio as well as the type of differentiation stimulation. We compared macrophages differentiated with growth factors or PMA to ones differentiated by the tumor cells themselves. GM-CSF and M-CSF differentiated macrophages had very similar profiles as well as feature-based quantifications having a significant difference from each other, even though these should have initially primed the macrophages to have opposing polarizations. Despite these initial differences the THP-1 cells end with similar NFkB activation curves, demonstrating how the tumor cell signaling reprograms the macrophages to a more self-benefiting phenotype.

The presence of a high tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) burden has been associated with poor prognosis in breast [44] and colorectal cancers [45, 46]. Therefore, we measured NFkB activity at high and low macrophage concentrations. Our results showed that ModMs cultured at a high cancer cell (low macrophage) concentration had the highest levels of NFκB activity. Low macrophage concentrations also produced the highest rising and resolving slopes in HCT116-associated ModMs.

TimeMax, AUC, and trending slope data are analytical tools that have been used in pharmacologic studies of drug dosing [47–49]. In this study, we apply these techniques to NFκB responses to assess how cancer cells polarize tumor associated macrophages. We found that HCT116 colorectal cancer cells inducing fast and strong NFκB activation profiles. Moreover, MDA-MB-231 co-culture yielded longer TimeMax, smaller rising slopes but larger ending slopes. qPCR analysis after 24 hrs of revealed MDA-MB-231-ModMs co-culture had an increase in IL-6 production which is associated with canonical NfκBactivation [50]. A hypothesis based on these trends is that HCT116 and MDA-MB-231 induce canonical and non-canonical NFκB activation within associated ModMs respectively. However, to validate these further mechanistic studies we need to confirm the presence of RelA/p50 and RelB/p52. It is also critical to consider that both NFκB pathways have been reported in tumor progression [51]. The strength of the analytic tools presented here is the ability to perform crosstalk signaling experiments in a living, real-time context. In addition, future studies should explore the relevance of the activation curves on cell functions such as phagocytosis, proliferation, or cytokine secretion.

The NFκB pathways are very well studied and the use of NFκB luciferase reporters to perform screening has been established. Our study builds upon this knowledge in important ways. Here, we used NFκB response measurements from macrophages to indirectly understand crosstalk signaling from surrounding cancer cells. In addition, we apply quantitative methods to interpret the entire temporal activation profile rather than single timepoint analysis. Future work should employ similar analysis with other luciferase tagged transcriptional factors such as STAT1 and STAT6.

Conclusion

Our study presents a novel method for temporal measurement of tumor-associated macrophage polarization in vitro. Analyzing global features from the NFκB luciferase temporal response curves provides information that distinguish activation states mediated by tumor cell signaling. There is also potential for this technique to be used for diagnostic and therapeutic screening purposes. Finally, we propose that this method can be applied to patient samples, allowing for the creation of immunogenicity curves for specific patients and for screening different treatment effects.
