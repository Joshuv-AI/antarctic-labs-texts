Continuous ofatumumab treatment until the early third trimester in multiple sclerosis with preserved neonatal immunity
Authors: Kenzo Sakurai, Hideyuki Takeuchi, Tomohiro Katsuta, Hiroyuki Aiba, Soichiro Shibata, Kei Kaburagi, Shintaro Akamatsu, Naoki Takao, Yoshihisa Yamano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Evidence regarding neonatal immune outcomes following late-gestation exposure to ofatumumab remains limited. We report a 35-year-old woman with relapsing multiple sclerosis who continued ofatumumab until 28 weeks of gestation following individualized shared decision-making based on informed patient preference. The patient remained clinically and radiologically stable throughout pregnancy. Sustained maternal B-cell depletion enabled early postpartum resumption of maintenance therapy without re-induction. Although neonatal immune status at birth was unavailable, her infant showed normal B-cell counts during follow-up and completed routine vaccinations, including live vaccines, without complications. This case provides clinically relevant longitudinal neonatal immune and vaccination data following late-gestation ofatumumab exposure.

Introduction

Ofatumumab is a fully human anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody that effectively suppresses relapse activity and disease progression in multiple sclerosis (MS). 1 Its high efficacy and convenient once-monthly subcutaneous administration have established it as a preferred disease-modifying therapy (DMT) for relapsing MS. 2 However, since MS predominantly affects women of childbearing age, managing treatment during pregnancy remains a significant challenge. 3 Current guidelines often advise avoiding treatment during pregnancy due to concerns about fetal exposure and transient neonatal B-cell depletion. 4 Immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) monoclonal antibodies, including ofatumumab, cross the placenta increasingly during the third trimester. Although ofatumumab has a relatively short serum half-life, its pharmacodynamic effect persists for approximately 24.6 weeks after discontinuation. 5 While emerging reports suggest severe neonatal immunodeficiency is rare, data on longitudinal B-cell recovery and the safety of routine vaccinations following exposure in the third trimester are extremely scarce. 6 Consequently, there is no consensus on peripartum management. This report describes maternal disease control together with longitudinal neonatal immune and vaccination outcomes following ofatumumab exposure in the early third trimester.

Case description

A 35-year-old Japanese woman with relapsing MS was treated with ofatumumab starting at age 31. Glatiramer acetate had been discontinued because of intolerable injection-related adverse effects, and she subsequently experienced breakthrough relapses while receiving dimethyl fumarate. Under ofatumumab, she achieved complete clinical and radiological stability for 4 years. At age 34, she became pregnant. Because previous therapies had failed to provide durable disease control, continuation versus early discontinuation of ofatumumab was discussed in detail. Although earlier discontinuation was considered a reasonable option given the prolonged pharmacodynamic effect of ofatumumab, treatment was continued until 28 weeks of gestation through individualized shared decision-making based on the patient's informed preference. At 24 weeks, her Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score was 2.0, and MRI showed no active disease (Figure 1). Laboratory testing at 27 weeks confirmed maternal B-cell depletion (<1%).

The patient delivered a healthy infant at 39 weeks and 3 days. Maternal B-cell levels were 0.2% on postpartum day 3. Breastfeeding was initiated immediately, and ofatumumab was resumed on postpartum day 13 without a re-induction phase. Subsequent maternal MRI and B-cell monitoring showed sustained disease control. The infant was appropriate for gestational age. Although neonatal B-cell measurements at birth were unavailable, at 1 month, the infant's B-cell proportion was 15.5%, and at 6 months, it reached 19.0%. All routine vaccinations, including live vaccines, were administered between 2 and 6 months of age without adverse events or infectious complications (Figure 2). At 7 months, the infant showed normal growth and development.

Discussion

This case does not advocate routine continuation of ofatumumab into the third trimester. Rather, it provides longitudinal neonatal immune and vaccination data in the uncommon circumstance where late gestational exposure occurred following individualized shared decision-making. Placental transfer of IgG1 antibodies increases significantly during the late second and third trimesters, raising concern regarding fetal exposure to anti-CD20 therapies. 7 Compared with intravenous anti-CD20 antibodies, monthly low-dose subcutaneous ofatumumab produces more gradual systemic exposure and less extensive depletion of deep tissue B-cell compartments. 8 Furthermore, the approximately 11-week interval between the final dose and delivery (approximately four to five half-lives) likely reduced maternal drug concentrations during peak placental IgG transfer and may have contributed to the favorable neonatal immune outcome. These pharmacological characteristics may explain the normal infant B-cell proportions at 1 and 6 months and the successful completion of routine vaccinations, including live vaccines.

Earlier discontinuation at pregnancy recognition would also have been a reasonable option because the patient's sustained disease stability most likely reflected the prolonged pharmacodynamic effect of pre-pregnancy B-cell depletion rather than continued late-gestation dosing. Nevertheless, because previous therapies had failed to provide durable disease control and the patient strongly wished to minimize the perceived risk of relapse during pregnancy and particularly during the early postpartum period, treatment until 28 weeks was chosen through individualized shared decision-making based on the patient's informed preference. Sustained maternal B-cell depletion at delivery also provided a strategic clinical benefit, as it allowed for early postpartum resumption on day 13 without the need for a re-induction phase. Given that the risk of MS relapse increases significantly in the first 3 months postpartum, 9 this strategy may have contributed to maintaining maternal neurological stability. Additionally, the minimal transfer of IgG1 into breast milk and its subsequent degradation in the infant's gastrointestinal tract further support the safety of resuming ofatumumab during breastfeeding. 10

This report is limited by its single-case design and the absence of B-cell data immediately at birth, neonatal immunoglobulin levels, and post-vaccination antibody titers. Accordingly, this report should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing evidence.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that neonatal immune competence and routine vaccination can be preserved following ofatumumab exposure in the early third trimester. However, given the prolonged pharmacodynamic effect of ofatumumab, earlier discontinuation at pregnancy recognition may be a reasonable strategy for patients with stable disease. Treatment decisions should be individualized through shared decision-making, balancing maternal disease control with fetal safety.
