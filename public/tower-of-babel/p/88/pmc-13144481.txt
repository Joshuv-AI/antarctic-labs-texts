Impact of AI misinformation on diagnostic accuracy and confidence calibration in novice medical students
Authors: Da Teng, Lihua Tan, Qiyuan Cao, Yanwei Xia, Na Zhang, Jiantao Li, Dan Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
For novice medical learners, do the benefits of correct AI explanations outweigh the risks of plausible misinformation? In a randomized trial with 111 students, we found they do not. Our results reveal a significant and problematic asymmetry: misleading AI explanations significantly degraded diagnostic accuracy, while correct explanations offered no significant improvement over a no-explanation control. Misleading explanations reduced diagnostic accuracy and showed no evidence of confidence calibration, such that confidence did not reliably distinguish correct from incorrect responses. This study provides crucial empirical evidence that, without proper safeguards, the harm caused by AI-generated falsehoods in this population and task is more potent and robust than the benefit derived from correct guidance. This finding highlights a fundamental safety challenge for AI in medical education, demanding a strategic pivot towards building learners’ critical appraisal skills. Trial registration: Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR), ChiCTR2500111932, registered on 7 November 2025.

Introduction

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into education, particularly specialized fields such as medical education, is expanding rapidly1. AI offers significant potential benefits, such as providing personalized learning pathways, delivering instant feedback, and aiding in the comprehension of complex concepts2,3. These capabilities suggest that AI can revolutionize traditional teaching models and enhance learning efficiency4,5. However, this technological promise is shadowed by a significant reliability crisis, where the persuasive power of AI-generated text can create a high-risk “plausibility trap" for unsuspecting learners. For novices like junior medical students, who are still developing their foundational knowledge and critical appraisal skills, these sophisticated falsehoods are not merely confusing; they risk being internalized as authoritative knowledge by learners unable to distinguish plausibility from accuracy.6.

The danger of this “plausibility trap” is not merely theoretical but a documented failure mode of advanced AI systems7. Consider a real-world example observed in GPT-4’s performance on medical licensing questions involving diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), a life-threatening emergency where a novice’s reasoning is critical. A fundamental, life-saving principle of DKA management is that the patient’s total body potassium is always severely depleted due to massive urinary losses, even when initial serum (blood) potassium levels paradoxically appear normal or even elevated8–10. However, the AI can craft persuasive “half-truths” that are highly misleading: it correctly identifies DKA but then leverages a complex physiological detail—that acidosis temporarily shifts potassium out of cells—to erroneously argue that “decreased total body potassium” is an unlikely finding. This sophisticated misdirection, which constructs a flawed conclusion from factually correct premises, exemplifies a well-documented failure pattern repeatedly identified in AI medical analyses11–13. For a novice learner, such a coherent but incorrect narrative can lead them to confidently dismiss a core principle of emergency medicine. This potential for AI to generate persuasive misinformation highlights a fundamental challenge for medical education. It raises a fundamental and practical question for the implementation of AI in education: Is the harm caused by misleading information more powerful than the benefit derived from correct information?

To empirically answer this question, we conducted a three-group randomized controlled trial (RCT). The study was designed to directly compare the effects of plausible but misleading AI explanations, correct AI explanations, and a no-explanation control on the performance of novice medical learners. This approach allows us to isolate the impact of different information environments on students’ diagnostic accuracy and confidence calibration (metacognitive monitoring). Specifically, this research addresses two fundamental questions:RQ1: What are the effects of correct and misleading AI-generated explanations on the diagnostic accuracy of novice medical students, compared to a baseline without explanations?RQ2: How do these different explanation types influence students’ confidence calibration (i.e., the alignment between their confidence and diagnostic accuracy)?

Results

We first report the primary comparison of diagnostic performance between experimental conditions (control vs correct explanation vs misleading explanation), focusing on diagnostic accuracy (RQ1) and confidence/calibration (RQ2). We then describe the sample and confirm baseline equivalence across groups. Next, we analyze commission error patterns to provide evidence consistent with the mechanism by which misleading explanations guided participant responses. Finally, we report exploratory item-level descriptives to characterize variability across the stimulus set.

Mixed-effects model analysis of diagnostic performance

To account for the hierarchical structure of our data (2775 item-level trials contributed by 111 participants), primary diagnostic performance outcomes were analyzed using mixed-effects models, which appropriately handle non-independent observations by modeling relevant clustering factors (participants and/or questions) as random effects, depending on the outcome.

Diagnostic accuracy (RQ1)

To investigate the effects of correct and misleading AI-generated explanations on diagnostic accuracy, we fitted a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a binomial distribution and logit link to predict response correctness. The explanation group was included as a fixed effect. The initial model specified random intercepts for both participant_id and question_id; however, a singular fit was detected for the participant random effect, and this non-informative random intercept was removed to yield a more stable final model with a random intercept for question_id (final specification: is_correct ~ group + (1∣question_id)). Results are summarized in Table 1 and visualized in Fig. 1B. Compared to the Control group (accuracy = 21.0%), the misleading explanation group performed significantly worse (accuracy = 9.2%, OR = 0.36, 95% CI [0.27, 0.47], p < 0.001), indicating that plausible misinformation actively harmed student performance. Main effect of explanation group on confidence

Averaging over response correctness, overall confidence differed markedly by group (Fig. 1a). Estimated marginal means (EMMs) were substantially higher in both explanation conditions (Correct Expl.: EMM = 1.90, SE = 0.075, 95% CI [1.75, 2.05]; Misleading Expl.: EMM = 1.92, SE = 0.079, 95% CI [1.76, 2.08]) than in the Control group (EMM = 1.42, SE = 0.075, 95% CI [1.28, 1.57]). Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons confirmed that both explanation groups reported higher confidence than Control (Correct − Control: Δ = +0.47, p < 0.0001; Misleading − Control: Δ = +0.50, p < 0.0001), while the two explanation groups did not differ from each other (Misleading − Correct: Δ = +0.02, p = 0.978). Because the model includes a group × correctness interaction, these EMMs should be interpreted as marginal means averaged over correct and incorrect responses (i.e., an overall vertical shift in confidence). Taken together, exposure to any AI-generated explanation—regardless of whether it was correct—inflated learners’ confidence relative to receiving no explanation.

To determine whether this confidence inflation held independent of correctness, we examined EMMs within each correctness level. Pairwise group comparisons within each level were Tukey-adjusted (family size = 3 comparisons per level). For incorrect responses, confidence remained higher than Control in both explanation groups (incorrect: control EMM = 1.44 [1.29, 1.59]; Correct Expl. EMM = 1.80 [1.65, 1.95], p = 0.0021 vs Control; Misleading Expl. EMM = 1.94 [1.79, 2.08], p < 0.0001 vs Control). The same pattern held for correct responses (Correct: Control EMM = 1.41 [1.25, 1.57]; Correct Expl. EMM = 1.99 [1.83, 2.16], p < 0.0001 vs Control; Misleading Expl. EMM = 1.90 [1.71, 2.09], p = 0.0004 vs Control). Within each correctness level, Correct vs Misleading did not differ (both p > 0.39), indicating comparable overall confidence levels across the two explanation conditions.

Confidence calibration: despite similar overall confidence, calibration patterns differed sharply across groups. The group × correctness interaction was significant (χ2(2) = 13.79, p = 0.001), indicating that explanation type altered the relationship between performance and confidence. Simple effects within groups showed no evidence of calibration in Control (Incorrect − Correct: Δ = +0.03, p = 0.547) or in the Misleading Explanation group (Δ = +0.04, p = 0.594). In contrast, the Correct Explanation group showed reliable calibration (Incorrect − Correct: Δ = −0.19, p < 0.0001).

Taken together, misleading explanations reduced diagnostic accuracy and showed no evidence of confidence calibration, whereas correct explanations did not improve accuracy but preserved calibration.

Sample description and baseline equivalence

The final analysis included data from 111 participants (N = 111), randomly and equally assigned to three groups: the Control Group, the Correct Explanation Group, and the Misleading Explanation Group (n = 37 in each group). Four participants were excluded due to abnormal response times.

Baseline demographic characteristics were compared to verify the equivalence of the groups prior to experimental intervention, as summarized in Fig. 2. Statistical tests indicated no significant differences between the groups in terms of age (F(2, 108) = 0.15, p = 0.86), gender distribution (χ2(2) = 0.92, p = 0.63), or distribution across provinces (χ2(14) = 13.32, p = 0.50). Error pattern analysis: commission errors

To assess whether misleading explanations steered participants toward specific incorrect answers, we analyzed the rate of commission errors (instances where participants accepted the AI’s misinformation and selected the specific target distractor). Among all incorrect answers from the Misleading Explanation Group, a substantial 70.27% were commission errors. In contrast, this same distractor accounted for only 24.65% of errors in the Control Group and 30.38% in the Correct Explanation Group. A Chi-square test revealed a highly significant association between the experimental group and the likelihood of committing a commission error (χ2(2) = 469.76, p < 0.0001), indicating a large effect (Cramer’s V = 0.411). This provides evidence consistent with a steering effect of misleading explanations toward the targeted distractor (Fig. 3), thereby supporting the proposed mechanism by which plausible misinformation guided participant responses.Fig. 3Systematic persuasion: how misleading AI explanations induce specific errors.a Accuracy degrades with misinformation persuasiveness. Discussion

This study was designed to answer a critical, practical question for medical education in the era of AI: do the benefits of correct AI-generated explanations outweigh the risks of plausible but incorrect ones? Our findings reveal a clear asymmetry: misleading explanations significantly reduced diagnostic accuracy relative to the no-explanation control and frequently steered learners toward the targeted incorrect option (commission errors). In contrast, authoritative correct explanations did not yield a statistically significant improvement in accuracy over the control.

Regarding diagnostic accuracy, we found that plausible misinformation was potently harmful, significantly degrading students’ diagnostic accuracy below the no-explanation baseline. In contrast, authoritative correct explanations offered no statistically significant benefit over the control.

Regarding confidence calibration, this performance harm was compounded by a severe breakdown in confidence calibration. Misleading explanations led to a state of significant miscalibration, where students’ confidence was no longer aligned with their actual performance. As evidenced by the flat confidence slope in the misleading group (Fig. 1), confidence ratings became invariant to correctness; the flawed explanations provided a coherent, albeit fallacious, rationale that led students to report high confidence for incorrect answers14–17. This decoupling mirrors earlier findings where clinicians maintained high confidence in incorrect diagnoses even after consulting online evidence systems18, suggesting a pervasive vulnerability in technology-assisted reasoning. This state is particularly dangerous in high-stakes domains like medicine, because confidence no longer reliably distinguishes correct from incorrect responses.

Theoretically, this vulnerability aligns with established research on automation bias—defined by Mosier and Skitka as the tendency to use automated cues as a heuristic replacement for vigilant information seeking19. While previous studies have shown this bias leads to commission errors primarily in monitoring tasks20–23, our results highlight a specific danger in knowledge-intensive contexts. Here, the observed danger extends beyond mere over-reliance on a tool; the data are consistent with an internalization of persuasive falsehoods, resulting in high-confidence errors that may be more harmful to a novice learner than simple ignorance. This results in a firmly held misconception—a state arguably more harmful to a novice learner than simple ignorance24.

These findings have several pressing implications. From an educational perspective, our results suggest that integrating AI as a passive “knowledge provider” is inherently risky for novice learners25. Given the profound impact of misinformation observed in our study—even on learners who were not explicitly primed to trust the AI–the extent to which simple warnings can mitigate such persuasive falsehoods remains an open empirical question and should be tested directly. Therefore, we propose a fundamental shift in pedagogy toward AI-Auditing Training within medical curricula, structured activities that teach learners to critically evaluate and verify AI-generated explanations rather than treating them as authoritative. Rather than merely warning students about hallucinations, educators should design active learning modules where students are tasked with “auditing” AI-generated explanations to identify subtle errors—effectively treating the AI not as a professor, but as a knowledgeable but unreliable junior colleague. Specifically, we recommend the implementation of “AI-Auditing Drills”: simulation exercises where learners must verify AI outputs against trusted primary literature before accepting them. This approach does more than test knowledge; it calibrates confidence. By explicitly exposing students to the “fluent but false” failure modes of LLMs (as demonstrated in our STME analysis), curricula can immunize learners against the “plausibility trap,” (see Table 3 for a deconstruction of a highly persuasive example), transforming automation bias into constructive skepticism essential for patient safety. For developers of medical AI, this study highlights a significant safety flaw in current conversational interfaces: the lack of uncertainty signaling26. The “confident” tone of the misleading explanations in our study could be one contributing factor. To mitigate this, we recommend that educational AI tools incorporate “Epistemic Uncertainty Markers”. Instead of prioritizing conversational fluency, medical AI systems should be designed with “Cognitive Forcing Functions”—design elements that deliberately slow down the user to encourage analytical thinking. For example, when an LLM provides a diagnostic rationale, the interface could highlight low-confidence logical steps or require the user to click through to verify citations before the full conclusion is revealed. Moving from “black-box” answers to “transparent reasoning” interfaces is not just a feature request; it is a safety requirement to prevent the propagation of medical misinformation. For educational research, our findings issue a methodological caution: self-reported confidence is a highly unreliable metric for assessing learning in environments featuring potentially unreliable AI. High confidence may not reflect genuine competence but rather an uncritical acceptance of a flawed argument, necessitating the use of more objective measures of knowledge and reasoning in future studies27,28.

These conclusions must be contextualized within the study’s limitations29. First and foremost, our study was designed to measure the effects of AI explanations, not the underlying cognitive mechanisms. Second, our exploratory analysis of the relationship between question difficulty and an explanation’s persuasiveness (STME) was underpowered due to a small item sample (N = 25). No firm conclusions can be drawn from it beyond highlighting a potential area for future, larger-scale investigation. Third, our use of 100% correct vs 100% incorrect explanations was an experimental necessity to maximize effect detection. Real-world AI outputs often contain more subtle, nuanced errors. Consequently, the magnitude of the effects observed here may represent an upper bound. Finally, our findings are based on a cohort of junior medical students from a single cultural context; generalizability to learners with different levels of expertise and in other domains requires further study.

In conclusion, this study provides compelling empirical evidence for a profound asymmetry in the impact of AI-generated explanations on novice medical students. Plausible but incorrect explanations function not as benign errors, but as a potent source of misinformation, actively degrading performance and fostering a dangerous state of miscalibrated confidence. These findings issue a clear call to action: for educators, to prioritize the cultivation of critical evaluative skills over the mere adoption of new technology; and for AI developers, to shift the paradigm from engineering conversational fluency to designing transparent, responsible reasoning systems. For the next generation of clinicians, our work underscores the pedagogical imperative to cultivate not just knowledge, but the skills required to deconstruct the authoritative allure of AI and navigate an increasingly complex information ecosystem.

Methods

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of this educational research study, which focused on medical student learners rather than patient participants.

Study design

Trial registration: This trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR) on 7 November 2025, with registration number ChiCTR2500111932. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines30,31. It employed a three-arm, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial (RCT) design. This trial was designed as a superiority study to determine whether misleading AI explanations cause significantly worse outcomes compared to no-explanation control. It aimed to rigorously evaluate the causal effects of different sources of explanatory information. The focus was on junior medical students’ knowledge-application accuracy and confidence calibration. Evaluation occurred in a standardized medical knowledge testing scenario28. This scenario simulated key features of the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) clinical vignette sections, such as stem complexity, distractor plausibility, and focus. Potential harms were defined as (1) decreased diagnostic accuracy (primary outcome) and (2) a failure of confidence calibration (secondary outcome). In this study, we operationalize confidence calibration (often referred to in cognitive science as metacognitive monitoring) as the extent to which a learner’s confidence aligns with their diagnostic accuracy (i.e., being confident when correct and unsure when incorrect). These were systematically assessed for all participants. The core of this design lies in simulating the information environments learners might encounter (no assistance, reliable assistance, or seemingly authoritative but flawed assistance) and quantifying their impact on key learning outcomes.

Participants

The target population for this study was Chinese junior medical students. This group was operationally defined as undergraduate medical students who had completed basic medical science courses (e.g., Physiology, Biochemistry, Pathology) but had not yet commenced major clinical rotations (e.g., Internal Medicine, Surgery, Obstetrics/Gynecology, Pediatrics), typically corresponding to students in years 3–5 of a 5-year medical program. The rationale for selecting this group is that they possess a foundational level of medical knowledge but lack deep clinical experience and systematic preparation for licensure exams (such as the National Medical Examination and USMLE), making them potentially more susceptible to the influence of external explanatory information.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) meeting the operational definition of a “junior medical student"; (2) being a native Chinese speaker or possessing equivalent reading comprehension; (3) agreeing to participate voluntarily and being able to complete the online experiment independently; and (4) having access to a stable internet connection and a compatible device (PC or tablet recommended).

Exclusion criteria were: (1) having completed major clinical rotations or who had more than 6 months of clinical practice; (2) systematic participation in commercial preparatory courses for major medical licensure exams; (3) previous participation in research on highly similar topics; (4) being a non-clinical medical specialty student; or (5) failing to demonstrate genuine engagement with the task. Engagement was assessed by pre-determined response time criteria established a priori: total completion times of less than 3 min (suggesting superficial engagement) or 60 min or more (suggesting significant interruptions) led to data exclusion.

Participants were recruited via online channels (e.g., student societies in medical schools across multiple provinces). Recruitment materials clearly stated the research purpose, procedure, estimated duration, voluntary nature, and anonymity. An a priori sample size estimation was conducted using G* Power (v3.1) for a one-way ANOVA (α = 0.05, power = 0.80, 3 groups)34. Targeting a medium effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.30), informed by meta-analyses of instructional explanations35, this estimation indicated a minimum required total sample size of 111 participants. A total of 115 participants were initially recruited. No interim analysis or stopping guidelines were prespecified; the trial was completed after reaching the target sample size. Simple randomization was used to allocate participants into one of the three experimental groups (Control, Correct Explanation, Misleading Explanation) in a 1:1:1 ratio. Four participants were excluded post-randomization based on the response time criteria. This resulted in a final sample of 111 participants (N = 111, n = 37 per group) included in the analysis. The trial was completed after reaching its recruitment target and was not stopped prematurely. Materials

The experiment utilized a test comprising 25 single-best-answer multiple-choice questions in Chinese. These questions were selected and curated to simulate the cognitive demands of a standardized medical knowledge assessment, such as the USMLE. The selection and development of experimental materials, including the questions and their associated explanations, followed a rigorous multi-stage process designed to isolate the causal effects of AI-generated information.

The process began with an initial screening of 191 candidate questions drawn from publicly available USMLE-style question banks and prior analyses of GPT-4 error patterns7. Two senior medical experts (each with over 15 years of clinical and teaching experience) finalized a set of 25 questions based on four criteria: (1) coverage of core medical science subjects (e.g., pathology, pharmacology, physiology); (2) high intrinsic cognitive challenge, requiring multi-step reasoning rather than simple fact recall; (3) the presence of a highly plausible but incorrect distractor option suitable for targeted misinformation; and (4) successful translation and cultural adaptation confirmed by bilingual medical experts to ensure conceptual equivalence.

Procedure

The experiment was conducted online via the Wenjuanxing platform (https://www.wjx.cn/) over a 48-h period. After providing informed consent and completing baseline questionnaires, the platform automatically assigned participants to one of the three groups. This assignment was performed using a computer-generated simple randomization sequence generated by an independent statistician without any blocking or stratification, ensuring an equal probability of allocation to each group. Allocation concealment was achieved through the online platform’s automated assignment system, which ensured that researchers were unaware of the next allocation until after participant enrollment. They then proceeded to the testing phase, which consisted of 25 multiple-choice questions presented in random order with a total time limit of 60 min.

Statistical analysis

The online experimental platform required participants to provide a response for each of the 25 questions before submission. The hierarchical structure of the data consisted of 2775 trials nested within 111 participants. Consequently, the final dataset for the 111 included participants was complete, with no missing item-level data. Therefore, no procedures for handling or imputing missing data were necessary. To account for the hierarchical structure of our data (multiple responses nested within individuals), we analyzed our primary outcomes using mixed-effects models. This approach correctly handles the non-independence of observations by modeling relevant clustering factors (participants and/or questions) as random effects depending on the specific outcome. All analyses were conducted in R (v4.5.0) using the lme436, lmerTest37, and emmeans38 packages. The significance level was set at α = 0.05.

To analyze the binary outcome of response correctness (correct = 1, incorrect = 0), we fitted a GLMM with a binomial distribution and a logit link function. The model included the explanation condition (Control, Correct, Misleading) as a fixed effect. The initial model specified random intercepts for both participant_id and question_id. The participant random intercept was omitted due to a singular fit (variance ≈ 0); results were materially unchanged when it was retained. Following standard procedures for addressing singular fits39, this non-informative random effect was removed to achieve a more parsimonious and stable final model: is_correct ~ explanation_condition + (1∣question_id). Odds ratios (OR) were reported as the effect size.

To analyze confidence ratings, we fitted an LMM36. The model included the explanatory condition, response correctness (correct vs incorrect), and their crucial two-way interaction as fixed effects. This interaction term allows us to test whether the relationship between response correctness and confidence (i.e., confidence calibration) differs across the experimental groups. To account for the repeated-measures structure, a random intercept for participant_id was included. The final model structure was: confidence ~ explanation_condition × response_correctness + (1∣participant_id). To address potential over-adjustment, we report the model without a question-level random intercept. To address concerns about potential over-adjustment, we report the model without a question-level random intercept. As a robustness check, adding a question-level intercept yielded materially unchanged fixed-effect inferences (see Table 2). Post-hoc comparisons of EMMs were conducted using emmeans40. p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Tukey method; when probing group differences within each level of response correctness, the Tukey adjustment was applied separately within each correctness stratum (three pairwise comparisons per stratum)41. Finally, overall model fit was assessed using AIC, BIC, and marginal/conditional R-squared values42.
