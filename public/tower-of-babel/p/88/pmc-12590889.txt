Interplay between red blood cells and circulating tumor cells: clinical and molecular evidence of a putative pro-metastatic axis
Authors: Julia Richert, Marta Popęda, Anna Muchlińska, Robert Wenta, Barbara Bollin-Matysiak, Aleksandra Parteka, Beata Pieczyńska-Uziębło, Tomasz Kryczka, Anna Żaczek, Grażyna Suchodolska, Elżbieta Senkus, Marcin Matuszewski, Kevin Miszewski, Natalia Bednarz-Knoll
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Recent evidence suggests that red blood cells (RBCs) may actively regulate course of tumor disease, whereas red cell distribution width (RDW) might even serve as surrogate marker of tumor progression. Here, we investigated the clinical and molecular significance of RDW in breast (BrCa) and d’Amico high-risk prostate cancer (PCa) patients. Higher RDW (RDWhigh) was associated with metastatic disease, poorer survival, therapy resistance, elevated circulating tumor cell (CTC) counts, proinflammatory mediators profiles and some comorbidities such as cardiological disorders or diabetes. Gene expression analysis of primary tumors from patients characterized by RDWhigh revealed deregulation of genes involved in inflammation, immune modulation, cell adhesion, and migration. Using imaging flow cytometry (imFC), we identified for the first time direct RBC-CTC interactions (CTCRBC) in almost 9% of PCa patients. The presence of CTCRBC correlated with lymph node involvement and shorter time-to-biochemical recurrence. Those CTCs interacting with RBCs displayed predominantly epithelial phenotype and lacked protrusions. Collectively, these results provide clinical and molecular evidence that RBCs can modulate tumor progression both indirectly, potentially through systemic inflammation, and directly, via physical interactions with CTCs. Importantly, our findings highlight the potential of RDW, a routinely measured blood parameter, as a simple and clinically meaningful biomarker of tumor progression and dissemination.

Background

Recent evidence suggests that red blood cells (RBCs) - traditionally considered as passive carriers of oxygen - may play an active role in tumor progression [1]. Pereira-Vega et al. showed that RBCs may actively facilitate tumor aggressiveness and metastatic behavior by affecting tumor cells morphology and phenotype in breast and lung cancer [1]. One of the most striking discoveries in this study was the upregulation of PAK4, a kinase promoting cytoskeletal reorganization, cell polarity, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), in tumor cells exposed to RBCs collected from patients with metastatic disease. In parallel, RBC-primed tumor cells displayed pro-migratory morphological changes, such as more frequently occurring protrusions. In addition, RBCs distribution width (RDW), marker of RBCs heterogeneity, was proven to serve as a simple prognostic marker indicating poorer progression-free survival and overall survival, and correlate with the higher number of circulating tumor cells (CTCs) [1].

RDWhigh as surrogate marker of breast and prostate cancer progression

To further exploit erythrocytes potential impact on tumor progression, we have investigated our cohorts of cancer patients for whom clinical (incl. pre-treatment blood count), molecular, and CTC data were collected in the past and ongoing studies. The cohorts included non-metastatic and metastatic BrCa [2, 3] and d’Amico high risk prostate cancer (PCa) patients [4]. In both tumor types, RDW and RBCs count, two distinct but complementary parameters reflecting RBCs (but also bone marrow) physiology, revealed a weak inverse correlation (R2 = −0.245, p < 0.001 and R2 = −0.255, p = 0.003 for BrCa and PCa, respectively). RBC low counts, indicators of (sub)clinical anemia, might be caused by tumor disease or affect its course, and occur more frequently in metastatic patients [5], whereas high RDW (RDWhigh) usually reflects inflammation, malnutrition or bone marrow dysfunction [6]. Pereira-Vega et al. [1] showed that tumor cells coculturing with RBCs, particularly those derived from blood of metastatic patients, boosted invasiveness of tumor cells. In our cohort of 245 BrCa patients RDW was higher in M1 patients (p = 0.044, Fig. 1A), while mean RBC counts remained comparable in M0 and M1 patients (data not shown). Thus, we assumed that protumorigenic effect of RBCs derived from metastatic patients might be rather associated with their shape or functional/molecular status than count, and, in turn, focused our analysis on RDW.

Similarly to Pereira-Vega et al. [1], RDW was clinically relevant in both cohorts of our patients. Patients were classified into two subgroups – those with RDWhigh, defined as the upper quartile (≥13.6) and RDWlow (defined below this threshold). RDWhigh occurred more frequently in BrCa patients who died (p = 0.047). RDWhigh was also more frequent in patients who developed distant metastases (p = 0.003) and those where ≥5 CTCs were detected per 1 million PBMCs in peripheral blood (p = 0.041; Fig. 1B). RDW tended also to be higher in BrCa patients not responding to therapy (p = 0.069, Fig. 1C). In the cohort of additional 33 BrCa patients [3], the higher RDW correlated weakly-to-moderately with proinflammatory plasma-derived cytokines: ENA78/CXCL5 (Pearson’s correlation, R2 = 0.489, p = 0.004), eotaxin/CCL11 (R2 = 0.528, p = 0.002) and IL2R (R2 = 0.351, p = 0.045).

In 125 d’Amico high risk PCa patients, RDWhigh (determined also with the cutoff ≥13.6), showed borderline association with the shorter time-to-biochemical recurrence assessed within 3 years after radical prostatectomy (time-to-BR, p = 0.085, Fig. 1D). RDWhigh was also observed more frequently in patients with preoperative serum PSA ≥10 ng/ml (p = 0.023), as well as in those with co-existing diabetes (p = 0.035) or cardiological diseases (p = 0.052) (Fig. 1E). However, no such association was found with other documented diseases (e.g., hypocholesterolemia or thyroid disorders) or with the presence of CTCs (data not shown). More striking, RDWhigh occurred more frequently in PCa patients who developed distant metastases after radical prostatectomy (p = 0.014, Fig. 1E). In PCa, the higher RDW correlated also with the proinflammatory mediators: DKK1 (Pearson’s correlation, R2 = 0.349, p < 0.001), osteonectin/SPARC (Pearson’s correlation, R2 = 0.270, p = 0.004) and osteoprotegerin (Pearson’s correlation, R2 = 0.302, p = 0.001) measured using MILLIPLEX MAP Human Cancer/Metastasis Biomarker Magnetic Bead Panel (Merck) in preoperative peripheral blood-derived plasma.

Based on those and recent [1] results, it might be reasoned that preoperative RDW might be a simple indicator not only of tumor progression but even specifically of its dissemination. However, our outcomes might also suggest that RDWhigh is in fact the result of potentially pre-anemic condition manifested by the lower RBCs counts or inflammation-associated comorbidities, both of which might influence tumor development. Thus, such clinical conditions, where cancer and other diseases coexist, should be investigated carefully in the larger cohorts of patients in terms of tumor progression, its response to therapy, and the genuine role of RBCs in those processes.

Molecular profile of primary tumors from breast and prostate cancer patients characterized by RDWhigh

Pereira-Vega et al. showed that RBCs-primed breast (MDA-MB-231) and lung (H1975) cancer cell lines might gain pro-invasive molecular profile (including increased adhesion, disruption of the endothelial barrier, and invasiveness) [1]. As our and literature data suggest that the putative tumor-supportive effect of RBCs might be addressed by measuring RDW, we investigated primary tumors resected from BrCa and PCa patients in the context of RDWhigh (23 RDWlow vs 9 RDWhigh BrCa and 48 RDWlow vs 21 RDWhigh PCa) for multigene expression analysis using nCounter® PanCancer Immune Profiling Panel and nCounter® PanCancer Progression Panel (NanoString), respectively [7, 8]. |log2FC|≥0.5 and p-values ≤0.05 were considered as statistically significant. RDWhigh BrCa revealed downregulation of CDKN1A, CXCL10, FCGR3A, and MAPKAPK2, and upregulation of CCL21, EpCAM, IL11RA, IL34, KIT, and TNFSF10 genes, which are associated with inflammatory response (GO0006954), regulation of immune system (GO0002684), proliferation (GO0008284), pseudopodium assembly (GO0031274) or migration (GO00030335), potentially underlying the pro-invasive character of those tumors. RDWhigh PCa were characterized by downregulation of AKAP12, ARHGDIB, BGN, CD2AP, CLIC4, DDR2, DSC2, FAM174B, GIMAP4, GRHL2, ITGA8, MS4A4A, PLA2G2A, PTRF, RGCC genes, among which 7 annotated to anchoring junctions (GO0070161), consistent with the impaired cell-cell adhesion potentially contributing to RBC-CTC interaction.

CTCs-RBCs clusters and their clinical relevance in prostate cancer

The results from PCa cohort confirm the observation that RBCs might indeed induce promigratory, or even specifically EMT-related processes supporting tumor dissemination proposed by Pereira-Vega et al. [1]. Thus, it could be reasonable to investigate whether they can bind directly to CTCs during their transit via bloodstream. To the best of our knowledge, clusters of CTCs and RBCs (CTCRBC) derived from blood have never been presented in the literature. It might be caused by technical limitations of imaging technologies involved in the standard CTC identification: they frequently lack sufficient image resolution for detailed analysis and/or are limited by the number of fluorescent channels, which disables simultaneous codetection of different cell types. Imaging flow cytometry (imFC), newly implemented by our team into the detection of CTCs [9], bypasses those limitations by offering high-throughput, multiparameter imaging analysis both in bright field and 9 fluorescent channels [2]. Using imFC, RBCs could be identified based solely on their morphological features such as biconcave disc shape and size (Fig. 1F) [10]. Therefore, we looked into our library of over 3000 CTCs detected using imFC to search for their potential direct interactions with RBCs (http://www.CTCAtlas.org). We have not observed CTCRBC in PB of 210 breast, 24 ovarian, and 29 lung cancer patients [2, 11], but putatively detected them (Fig. 1G) in 12 of 134 (8.9%) d’Amico high risk PCa patients investigated both in tumor draining vein blood (TDVB) and/or PB. In total, we have detected 70 CTCRBC in TDVB and 11 in PB in those patients. Putative CTCRBC did not correlate with the time from blood collection-to-its processing, RBCs count or RDW. Although the number of patients with CTCRBC was limited, and the findings should be interpreted with caution, it might be reasoned that CTCRBC potentially contribute to the worse clinical outcome, as their presence was significantly associated with lymph node involvement (p = 0.041, Fig. 1H) and correlated with the shorter time-to-BR (p = 0.041, Fig. 1I). RBCs occurred to bind to both single and clustered CTCs (Fig. 1G), and in 44% of CTCRBCs they co-localized with platelets. CTCs found in direct contact with RBCs were primarily of epithelial phenotype (p = 0.025, Fig. 1J) and all without any detected protrusions [11], which stays in contrast to the previous outcomes postulating rather mesenchymal-like and migratory character of RBC-primed tumor cells [1]. Primary tumors seeding CTCRBC (n = 4) expressed higher levels of ADAM1TS (log2FC = 2.11), and lower levels of CCL5 and CD34 (log2FC = −0.78 and log2FC = −1.17, respectively), in comparison to those seeding CTCs not clustering with erythrocytes (n = 4), however, those changes did not reach statistical significance (all p = 0.133). CD34 is a marker of endothelial cells, facilitating the recognition of vascular vessels, but we did not see any difference in vascularity of primary tumors in the context of CTCRBC [12].

Conclusions

Our results corroborate the highly interesting finding of Pereira-Vega et al. [1] that RBCs might interplay with tumor cells potentially boosting their invasiveness and indicating the worse course of malignancy. They also suggest that patients with coexisting cancer and inflammation-associated diseases such as cardiological diseases or diabetes, might have different disease course, which gives a rationale for future studies oriented on the crosstalk of coexisting multi-diseases. Of note, our and literature data advocate that some characteristics of RBCs, such as RDW, defining RBCs’ physiology, might be a simple and early indicator of tumor dissemination [1, 13, 14]. Importantly, since RDW is a routinely measured, cost-free blood parameter, its prognostic value can be readily integrated into clinical practice without additional resources, offering immediate biomedical applicability. Our study provides also – to the best of our knowledge – the first evidence that CTC might directly bind to RBCs in the bloodstream, potentially modulating their fate or metastatic potential. This observation, together with the recent study by Pereira-Vega et al. [1], opens research directions towards investigating tumor cells in the context of all blood components and offers translational opportunities for patient risk stratification and therapeutic monitoring [15].
