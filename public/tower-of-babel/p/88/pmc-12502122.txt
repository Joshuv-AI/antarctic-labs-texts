I1 Exploring Beekeeping Innovations and Science: Proposals and Conclusions from the 2nd Conference in Sarajevo

Marta Leite1,2, Ana R. Mateus2,3,4, Midhat Jasic5, Andreia Freitas1,2

1Associated Laboratory for Green Chemistry (LAQV) of the Network of Chemistry and Technology (REQUIMTE), Porto, Portugal; 2National Institute for Agricultural and Veterinary Research (INIAV), Vila Do Conde, Portugal; 3Associated Laboratory for Green Chemistry (LAQV) of the Network of Chemistry and Technology (REQUIMTE), University of Coimbra, Faculty of Pharmacy, Coimbra, Portugal; 4Faculty of Pharmacy, University of Coimbra, Coimbra, Portugal; 5Faculty of Pharmacy, University of Tuzla, Bosnia and Herzegovina

Correspondence: Marta Leite

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(28):I1

On 6 and 7 May 2025, the COST Action BeSafeBeeHoney held its 2nd International Conference — BeeForward: Exploring Beekeeping Innovations and Science, at the Hollywood Hotel, Sarajevo, Bosnia and Herzegovina. As in 2024, the event was once again a success: around a hundred researchers, beekeepers and policy makers discussed the latest challenges and opportunities for bee health, honey quality and agricultural sustainability.

The Conference reaffirmed its interdisciplinary nature, successfully uniting stakeholders across the beekeeping and bee product value chain. The event provided a platform for presenting both scientific research and professional practice, while fostering dialogue among veterinarians, food technologists, nutritionists, medical doctors, agronomists, pharmacists, experienced beekeepers, and related professionals. Discussions highlighted the critical role of beekeeping as a driver of rural development, poverty reduction, and youth engagement, while emphasizing the need for innovation, competitiveness, and harmonization with European regulations.

Key recommendations addressed pressing challenges in legislation, standardization, and quality assurance of bee products. Participants underlined the importance of strengthening surveillance for bee health, providing institutional support for queen rearing, and promoting high-value products such as royal jelly, propolis, and geographically designated honeys. Greater attention was also called to organic production pathways, the adoption of good beekeeping and manufacturing practices, and the development of harmonized sensory evaluation methodologies to ensure consumer trust and market differentiation.

The conference further emphasized the need for standardization and evidence-based validation of apitherapy, recognizing its potential to support pharmaceutical, cosmetic, and related industries. Education emerged as a central theme, underscoring the necessity of continuous training for beekeepers and professionals across the production, processing, and distribution chain to enhance sustainability, quality, and innovation.

By bringing together diverse expertise and perspectives, the Second Conference provided a comprehensive set of recommendations toward advancing beekeeping practices, protecting bee health, and improving the competitiveness of bee products in both domestic and international markets.

S1 Tackling honey fraud together: how to detect adulteration with syrups using portable devices

Marco Ciulu

Department of Biotechnology – University of Verona, Italy

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(28):S1

Honey fraud has a significant impact on the beekeeping sector. It is estimated that around half of the samples available on the market derive from illicit activities such as adulteration with exogenous syrups or origin falsification. Honeys produced in countries with inadequate controls or quality standards can easily reach Europe by triangulation through third countries and then, be marketed at very low prices. Several beekeepers’ associations have organized protests in different European countries calling for strengthening border controls, banning ultrafiltration and consolidating official analytical methods to identify adulterated honeys and trace origin. In this context, several analytical techniques can be used to control imports and, more in general, honeys on the market, such as EA/LC-IRMS, HPAEC-PAD, LC-HRMS, or 1HNMR. Although highly performing, such techniques are often very expensive, time-consuming, and require the intervention of highly skilled personnel. Instead, the fight against fraud needs reliable, fast, implementable on-site screening approaches that can determine which samples are suspect and which are authentic, with a certain degree of confidence.

For this purpose, spectroscopic techniques such as FT-NIR and FT-Raman can prove very useful, also by virtue of the possible acquisition by portable instrumentation, for the rapid obtaining of actual fingerprints containing the information necessary for authentication and origin tracking. Studies supporting this claim, however, are often limited to a small number of samples, and the proposed models do not consider the wide variability in terms of chemical composition given by the different floral and local origins. In collaboration with Dr. Andrea Mara of the University of Sassari and thanks to the participation of several members of CA22105, a collaborative research project with the provisional title of DISCERNER (Development of an on-sIte SCreening stratEgy for the authentication and tRaceability of hoNeys in the European maRket) aimed at building authentication and classification models using portable devices was initiated.

S2 Understanding bee’s stressors involves communicating like bees

Noa Simon Delso

BeeLife European Beekeeping Coordination, Brussels, Belgium

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(28):S2

Working like the bees involves communicating like the bees. The importance to understand how each of us can contribute to Beeforward. In a honey bee colony, survival hinges on two things: efficient division of labour and seamless communication. While each bee plays a distinct role—from foraging to nursing to guarding—what truly holds the colony together is a sophisticated information exchange system. The hive is a living model of cooperation, where a single emitter can relay a signal—be it a waggle dance or pheromone—that is decoded, interpreted, and acted upon by many. This is a model we, as scientists, policymakers, and stakeholders, must learn from. Today, publicly funded research and environmental monitoring produce a wealth of data. From landscape-scale studies on pesticide residues to molecular analyses of bee health, the volume and complexity of data have never been greater. The European Commission has repeatedly emphasised the need for this data to follow the FAIR principles—Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable—to ensure it can serve the broader community. However, data that cannot be understood or acted upon is no better than a lost waggle dance. This keynote will explore how working like bees—specialized, task-oriented, but always aligned to the common good—also means communicating like bees. We must transform data into intelligible signals that can be acted upon by different actors: researchers, beekeepers, regulators, and citizens alike. This requires investing not just in data generation, but in clarity, interoperability, and context. We can only foster a collaborative environment where science truly informs policy and practice by mimicking the hive’s communication logic—one emitter, many receivers, standard and seamless understanding. Together, we can create a living, breathing data ecosystem that supports pollinators and ensures a sustainable future for all.

S4 Leveraging Behavioural Insights for a More Sustainable Agriculture

Marieke Cornelia Baaken

Osnabrück University, Department of Environmental Economics, Institute of Environmental Systems Research

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(28):S4

The relationship between agricultural production and the environment is complex and multi-faceted. While agriculture fundamentally depends on a well-functioning environment, current practices are a major driver of ecosystem degradation, leading to biodiversity loss (including declines in pollinators such as bees), soil degradation, water pollution, and climate change. At the same time, a more sustainable agriculture has the potential to reduce—and, in some cases, even reverse—these negative impacts by fostering ecological synergies already present within the system. One such synergy exists between crop production and apiculture (i.e., beekeeping), which are closely connected and can mutually benefit each other. Pollination is essential for crop production and is significantly enhanced by honeybees, resulting in yield increases of up to 40% for pollinator-dependent crops. These crops, in return, provide important food resources for bees, supporting the productivity of hives and, ultimately, beekeepers. The interests of farmers, beekeepers, and the broader public align in the provision of public goods such as climate and environmental protection. However, realizing these synergies requires a transformation of the agricultural system toward a more sustainable trajectory. A key component of this transformation is the behaviour of stakeholders in the agri-food chain, including farmers. Within my presentation, I provide insights into relevant agri-environmental practices, sustainable agriculture, and farmers’ decision-making. Where appropriate, I explore how these insights connect to and have implications for apiculture, and how they may inform more targeted and effective policy design.

O1 Refining Analytical Methodologies for Determination of Volatile Substances in Honey Using HS-GC-MS: Initial Insights

Biljana Arsić, Danijela Kostić, Gordana Stojanović

University of Niš, Faculty of Sciences and Mathematics, Department of Chemistry, Niš, Serbia

Correspondence: Biljana Arsić

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(28):O1

Honey is a natural substance with distinct properties influenced by the various groups of compounds it contains. Published studies suggest that the volatile profile of honey can help identify its botanical origin. In this study, a headspace gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (HS-GC-MS) method was developed for the simultaneous qualitative and quantitative analysis of volatile components in honey. To optimize the procedure, honey samples were subjected to different conditions during the HS-GC-MS analysis, including: 1) the addition of water, 2) the addition of a saturated aqueous sodium chloride solution, and 3) no added solvents or substances. The optimized HS-GC-MS conditions included approximately 2 g of honey, a solvent delay of 2 minutes, a temperature regime starting at 50 °C for 2 minutes, then increasing at 5°C/min to 150 °C, and at 10°C/min up to 200°C. HS-GC-MS analysis was carried out using a 7890/7000B GC/MS/MS triple quadrupole system (Agilent Technologies, USA) with a Combi PAL autosampler. A fused silica capillary column (HP-5MS, 5% phenylmethylsiloxane, 30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 µm film thickness) was used with helium as the carrier gas (1 cm³/min). Compounds were identified by comparing their mass spectra to those in the Wiley 6, NIST20, and Adams databases, using the AMDIS32 software (Automated Mass Spectral Deconvolution and Identification System). The developed method was applied to 50 honey samples from across the Republic of Serbia, of six different botanical origins: sunflower honey, flower honey, meadow honey, linden honey, acacia honey, and forest honey.

Flavonoids as key medicinal compounds in bee honey and their possible role in adulteration control

Harun Kurtagić

Federal Institute of Agriculture, Sarajevo, Bosnia and Herzegovina

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(28):O2

Honey has been used during the history of civilization more often under the term healing. Today, honey is perceived primarily as a food, while its medicinal properties are questioned except of manuka. A lot of scientific research has been done on this topic. Bees are exposed to many dangers of anthropogenic origin such as increasing environmental pollution, etc. As a consequence of ecological changes, the bee is exposed to many diseases and has become one of the endangered species, and possibility of honey production has been reduced. On the other hand, honey is in demand on the market, but adulterated honey appears, as a very serious challenge for consumers, beekeepers, as well as for scientists. Due to seemingly simple structure of honey, adulteration causes enormous damage to beekeeping and consumers. Up to date there is no scientific evidence that there are effective, cheap and fast solutions for adulteration control. The available methods are limited, expensive and time-consuming. An intention of this paper is to point out a future alternative in adulteration control that relies on the natural properties of honey. Polyphenols, especially flavonoids, are a chance in controlling honey adulteration because only plants can produce them and they are concentrated in the parts of plants where pollen, nectar and honeydew are produced. Global science should be established a database of flavonoid profiles of honey plants and their presence in honey by mass spectrometry. By controlling flavonoids in honey, the control of adulteration and the botanical origin could be carried out.

O3 An Overview of Spanish Honey Production: Diversity, Characteristics, and Valorisation

María Carmen Seijo Coello1, Ana Diéguez-Antón1, María Shantal Rodríguez-Flores1, Amelia González-Porto2, Olga Escuredo1

1Facultade de Ciencias, Universidade de Vigo, Campus As Lagoas, 32004 Ourense, Spain; 2Instituto Regional de Investigación y Desarrollo Agroalimentario y Forestal de Castilla-La Mancha, Marchamalo, Spain

Correspondence: María Carmen Seijo Coello

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(28):O3

The position of the Iberian Peninsula, surrounded by the Atlantic Ocean and the Mediterranean Sea, together with its almost insular nature, makes Spain one of the countries with the greatest biodiversity in the European Union. Currently, it has approximately 3 million beehives and 36,494 beekeepers distributed throughout the country. Beekeeping in the north is based on small, stationary apiaries and vertical Langstroth-type hives, while in the Mediterranean and southern areas, beekeeping tends to be more intensive, with a greater number of hives, and transhumant management. This context gives rise to a great diversity of honey production, with different botanical origins, physicochemical characteristics and organoleptic properties. This study presents the first results of a national research project aimed at assessing the value of Spanish honey according to its properties and geographical provenance. Over 400 honey samples from the 2023 and 2024 harvests have been analysed using various analytical procedures. Preliminary results indicate that Spanish honey presents a remarkable diversity in both composition and botanical origin. The physicochemical analyses confirmed significant variations in parameters such as moisture content, pH, electrical conductivity, colour and enzymatic content, among others. Additionally, sensory evaluations highlighted distinctive aromatic profiles, ranging from floral and fruity notes in lighter honey types to toasted and resinous nuances in darker types. Antioxidant activities are also very varied, ranging from typically high values in dark honey to almost 10% in the clearest ones. Future phases of the project include further analysis looking for biomarkers to authenticate botanical origin.

Cyril and Methodius” – Skopje; Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Skopje, Macedonia, the former Yugoslav Republic of; 9Faculty of Natural Sciences, University of Tirana, Blv. “Zogu i Parë”, Tirana, Albania; 10Jožef Stefan Institute, Ljubljana, Slovenia; 11Trinity College Dublin, Dublin, Ireland; 12Institute of Food Safety, Animal Health and Environment “BIOR", Riga, Latvia; 13LHICA, Science and Veterinary Science Faculties, Santiago de Compostela University, Santiago de Compostela, Spain; 14Centre for Functional Ecology, Associate Laboratory TERRA, Department of Life Sciences, University of Coimbra, Coimbra, Portugal; 15Croatian Veterinary Institute, Veterinary Institute Križevci, Križevci, Croatia; 16ITSAP-Institut de L’abeille, Avignon, France; 17School of Life Sciences, Pharmacy and Chemistry, Kingston University, Kingston upon Thames, KT1 2EE, London, United Kingdom; 18Institute of Food Safety, Animal Health and Environment “BIOR”, Riga, Latvia

Correspondence: Adrián Fuente-Ballesteros

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(28):O4

The contamination of bee products with pesticide residues is a growing concern due to its potential impact on bee health, product safety, and consumer exposure. This study presents a comprehensive review of pesticide residues detected in bee-related matrices across Europe and associated countries such as Turkey over the past six years. The analysis covers key pesticide families, including acaricides, insecticides, herbicides, fungicides, inert ingredients, and other chemical-related compounds. Acaricides, such as coumaphos, tau-fluvalinate, and amitraz, remain predominant due to their use in varroa mite control, while neonicotinoids and organophosphates continue to raise concerns for bee colony health. Among neonicotinoids, acetamiprid, imidacloprid and thiamethoxam are often detected in the hive and bee products. Additionally, the presence of emerging contaminants and banned pesticides underscore the complexity of residue accumulation in hive matrices. Residue levels varied among honey, pollen, beeswax, and bee bread, reflecting the influence of physicochemical properties and environmental exposure pathways.

Analytical methodologies based on targeted analysis with tandem liquid chromatography or gas chromatography–mass spectrometry remain the gold standard for pesticide detection in bee products. This review emphasizes critical challenges in monitoring pesticide residues, including regulatory gaps, the need for harmonized surveillance protocols, and the necessity for improved risk assessment frameworks. These findings call for stricter monitoring programs, updated regulatory policies, and the development of innovative analytical approaches to assess pesticide exposure in beekeeping environments. Future research should focus on alternative pest management strategies to the use of pesticides, and the long-term implications of pesticide mixtures on bee colony dynamics.

O5 Analytical Strategies for Multi-Detection of Antibiotic Residues in Honey

Rosa Ferreira1,2, Helena Rodrigues1,2, Marta Leite2,3, M. Beatriz Oliveira1,3, Andreia Freitas2,3

1University of Porto, Faculty of Pharmacy, Rua de Jorge Viterbo Ferreira 228, 4050-313, Porto, Portugal, Porto, Portugal; 2National Institute for Agricultural and Veterinary Research (INIAV), Rua dos Lagidos, Lugar da Madalena, 4485-655, Vila do Conde, Portugal, Vila do Conde, Portugal; 3Associated Laboratory for Green Chemistry (LAQV) of the Network of Chemistry and Technology (REQUIMTE), R. D. Manuel II, 4051-401 Porto, Portugal, Porto, Portugal

Correspondence: Rosa Ferreira

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(28):O5

The detection of antibiotics in honey samples is not merely a matter of food safety; it is a critical step in health promotion. Honey produced by honeybees can contain antibiotic residues used in beekeeping to combat bees’ bacterial diseases. The presence of these residues affects the quality and safety of the product, posing significant risks to human health. Despite the ban on the use of antibiotics as growth promoters in livestock in the European Union since 2006, their use is still permitted in other parts of the world.

A systematic review concerning the optimization and validation of analytical methodologies for the determination of antibiotics in honey was therefore performed to fulfil a comprehensive assessment of the current multi-detection technologies in this topic. PubMed and Scopus were used as databases for article search with a defined string of keywords, which included “honey,” “validation,” “antibiotics,” and “analytical methods.” In this matter, data showed Solid Phase Extraction (SPE) methods are the procedures most used for the extraction of different families of antibiotics. For detection purposes, the golden method in analytical chemistry, the liquid chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (LC-MS) was also found to be the most prominent technique for determining multi-class antibiotics.

There are still many challenges regarding the detection of antibiotics in honey. However, the importance of quality control in this type of food has been alerted throughout the EU to promote their monitoring and, consequently, to prevent public health issues. In addition, long-term follow-up of strategies for honey monitoring is essential.

O6 Potential Health Risks and Bio-Indicator Role of Bee Bread: Essential and Toxic Metal Analysis in Bingöl Province-Türkiye

Ayşegül Erdoğan1, Mehmet Emin Şeker2, Duygu Nur Çobanoğlu3, İlginç Kızılpınar Temizer4

1 Ege University, İzmir, Turkey; 2 Manisa Celal Bayar University, Manisa, Turkey; 3 Bingöl University, Bingöl, Turkey; 4 Giresun University, Giresun, Turkey

Correspondence: Ayşegül Erdoğan

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(28):O6

Bee bread (BB) is a nutrient-rich superfood packed with proteins, vitamins, and bioactive compounds that support immunity and promote energy. The elemental composition of BB, including essential minerals and trace metals, can provide significant health benefits while also requiring mindful consumption. Thus, a reliable and precise method must be chosen to analyse essential and potentially toxic metals in bee bread samples. In this study, elements were measured in five BB samples from Bingöl province using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). The microwave-assisted digestion system was also used for the digestion of bee bread samples. In addition, the accuracy of the method was evaluated by examining a standard reference material, speciﬁcally Ulva lactuca (BCR®279 – sea lettuce). The minimum and maximum metal contents were in the range: Na: 456.35-2535.51; Mg: 1372.01; Ca: 203.40-261.44; Se: 0.05-0.15; Mn:19.64-57.98; Fe: 456.35-2535.51; Cu: 7.68-19.31; Zn: 36.43-73.58; Cr: 3.31-121.15; As: 0.06-1.26; Ni:<0.0001-8.31; Cd: 0.03-0.07; and Pb: <0.001-1.26 µgg-1. The obtained values were utilized to estimate the recommended daily allowance (RDA), target hazard quotient (THQ), hazard index (HI), and cancer risk (CR) associated with the consumption of bee bread containing potentially toxic elements (PTEs). The findings suggest a potential carcinogenic risk associated with Cr and Ni in certain bee bread samples, underscoring the need for strict monitoring and control measures to ensure its safety and quality while mitigating potential health risks. Consequently, BB serves as a bio-indicator for abiotic stress in the hive, reflecting environmental pollution levels and exposure to PTEs that may impact colony health.

O7 The dynamics of pathogens in honey bee colonies in relation to different apicultural methods and treatment approaches

Lucija Žvokelj, Suzana Skerbiš, Metka Pislak Ocepek, Monika Kozar, Barbara Hočevar, Rene Rus, Mira Jenko Rogelj, Laura Šimenc Kramar, Ivan Toplak

Veterinary Faculty, Ljubljana, Slovenia

Correspondence: Lucija Žvokelj

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(28):O7

Brood interruption is a natural mechanism of the honey bee colony that reduces the number of pathogens. For long-term sustainable beekeeping, one of the most promising methods seems to be to allow colonies a period without brood even during the peak season. These methods will also be crucial, at least in the initial phase, for the control of new pests approaching European countries, with a focus on the Tropilaelaps mite.

In our applied research, we focused on two different ways of caging queens and applying oxalic acid to colonies with brood present. To monitor the effects of environmental conditions, we conducted the experiment in two different climates. We placed 30 comparable bee colonies in two different locations, in the Osrednjeslovenski and Obalno-Kraška regions. We determined the effects of the different methods by observing the effectiveness of the treatment and the effects on colony development and honey yield.

Since there are few studies on the dynamics of the concentration of the most important pathogens in bee colonies, we monitored the bee colonies individually four times from fall 2023 to spring 2025 and determined the number of Nosema spp. and the most common bee viruses during this period. This gave us an interesting insight into the dynamics of pathogen concentration at colony level. Our results also showed that brood interruption has no effect on colony strength and successful overwintering.

O9 Analysis of smart scales data for efficient beehive monitoring

Maj Smerkol1, Žan Šešet2, Lucija Žvokelj3, Metka Pislak Ocepek3, Miha Finžgar4, Samo Simončič4, Luka Mali5, Maja Ivana Smodiš Škerl6, Anton Gradišek1

1Jožef Stefan Institute, Ljubljana, Slovenia; 2Faculty of Mathematics and Physics, University of Ljubljana, Ljubljana, Slovenia; 3Veterinary Faculty, University of Ljubljana, Ljubljana, Slovenia; 4Senso4s, Trzin, Slovenia; 5Faculty of Electrical Engineering, University of Ljubljana, Ljubljana, Slovenia; 6Agricultural Institute of Slovenia, Ljubljana, Slovenia

Correspondence: Anton Gradišek

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(28):O9

Beekeepers often use automatic scales to measure the weight of the beehive to determine how good the harvest was in a given day. Commercial scale systems typically provide the user with only a single value per day, usually in the evening. While this may be sufficient for an experienced beekeeper, the analysis of weight data with a better temporal resolution offers a clear advantage in that it can provide a more detailed information about the hive. We developed an artificial intelligence-based module to identify different types of events in a beehive, such as days with a good/bad harvest, interventions of a beekeeper, or anomalies. The module comprises a rule-based module with a simple set of rules and a machine-learning module which was trained on a labelled dataset. We used four types of datasets: open-source data with a period of sampling once per hour, data from two pilot studies using custom-made and modified commercial scales to sample up to once per 10 min, and a synthetic dataset [1] that contained a balanced number of events for a more efficient testing of the algorithm. This study is a part of a broader research to better understand the events in a beehive through sensors in order to optimize the interventions of a beekeeper.

O10 From Ground to Canopy: Vertical Foraging, Bee Diversity, and the Hidden Impact of Lime Trees

Aleksandra Langowska1, Weronika Banaszak-Cibicka1, Łukasz Dylewski2, Joanna T. Białas2, Piotr Zduniak3

1Laboratory of Apidology, Department of Zoology, Poznań University of Life Sciences, Poznań, Poland; 2Department of Zoology, Poznań University of Life Sciences, Poznań, Poland; 3Department of Avian Biology and Ecology, Faculty of Biology, Adam Mickiewicz University, Poznań, Poland

Correspondence: Aleksandra Langowska

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(28):O10

Pollinators exhibit distinct foraging patterns, yet research on their vertical stratification across landscapes remains limited. Urban and non-urban environments offer diverse floral resources, but pollinators’ use of these resources varies with ecological traits, habitat structure, and species-specific interactions. We examined bee abundance, species richness, and functional diversity across ground, mid-story, and canopy levels, analysing environmental and ecological factors shaping vertical stratification.

Species richness was highest at ground and canopy levels, while bee abundance peaked in the canopy. Larger-bodied species were more prevalent at higher strata, suggesting body size influences vertical foraging. Land cover significantly shaped vertical space use, highlighting the role of habitat structure in foraging behaviour.
