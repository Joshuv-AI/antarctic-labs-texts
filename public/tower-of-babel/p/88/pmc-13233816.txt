Adults’ dental cone beam computed tomography images dataset for detecting and classifying missing teeth
Authors: Lan Feng, Zhi Li, Qihang Gu, Yaqi Wang, Xiaoyang Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The scarcity of high-quality Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) datasets with Three-Dimensional (3D) annotations hinders advancements in automated dental implant planning. Existing datasets lack precise 3D localization of tooth loss sites, particularly in complex cases presenting metal artifacts or existing implants. To address this, we curated a CBCT dataset from 158 patients across three institutions. Cases were rigorously screened for image quality (scores 2–4) and annotated using 3D Slicer, with artifact characteristics explicitly delineated. The dataset comprises a total of 158 CBCT volumes featuring 501 missing tooth sites, of which 85 volumes containing 114 specific sites were selected for 3D annotation (4,994 slices annotated). This resource provides 3D annotations of missing teeth to facilitate the training of deep learning models for automated implant planning. Furthermore, we established comprehensive baselines through clinical evaluations and deep learning modeling. These publicly available data aim to foster artificial intelligence (AI)-driven dental rehabilitation research.

Background & Summary

Tooth loss affects over 20% of adults aged 45 + globally1, with consequences extending beyond oral health to systemic conditions like diabetes and cardiovascular diseases2. It also has negative psychological and cognitive effects3. Despite the high demand for dental implants, three barriers limit accessibility: (1) a shortage of specialists, (2) prohibitive costs, and (3) variability in manual surgical planning due to clinician experience, which may compromise long-term success rates due to inconsistent implant positioning. Current dental implant therapy is undergoing a digital transformation, evolving from digital guides to robot-assisted surgery, improving efficiency and precision. The integration of advanced technologies such as 3D imaging and computer-assisted design has revolutionized the approach to implantology, allowing for more precise and individualized treatment plans4. However, these advanced digital methods still heavily rely on clinicians and technicians for treatment planning.

The integration of Deep Learning (DL) into implant design offers transformative potential to address these challenges. Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) is the primary method for dental implant surgical planning. However, in routine clinical practice, conditions characterized by extensive metal artifacts, fixed prostheses, or large edentulous areas significantly challenge the accurate localization of missing teeth, as shown in Fig. 1(a). New annotation methods must leverage intrinsic jawbone features to directly map tooth loss sites in CBCT images. While recent pioneering studies have significantly advanced automated dental analysis, the development of clinically robust DL models for implantology can be further enhanced by expanding existing datasets to include comprehensive 3D annotations, complex real-world artifacts, and open-source accessibility. For instance, Gerhardt et al.5 achieved high accuracy by using 3D convolutional neural networks to segment existing teeth and subsequently conjectured missing positions. However, they often explicitly exclude complex clinical scenarios with severe artifacts. To directly target the edentulous space, Al-Asali et al.6 proposed a method for segmenting missing tooth regions from 150 CBCT volumes. Due to limitations in their network architecture, their training and validation were ultimately restricted to a two-dimensional manner. Furthermore, it is noted that the relevant datasets for these advanced studies remain proprietary and closed-source. Existing resources primarily consist of two-dimensional (2D) data. For instance, Iyer et al. released a dataset of 379 panoramic radiographs containing missing teeth without annotations7, and another dataset provided 1,153 real-world 2D photographs with manually annotated missing tooth sites8. While useful for basic detection, these 2D datasets are clinically insufficient for dental implantology, which is an inherently three-dimensional (3D) surgical discipline. Simply identifying the absence of a tooth in 2D does not provide the crucial spatial parameters required for surgical planning, such as the spatial position, width, height, and bone density of the available alveolar ridge. Therefore, 3D annotation on CBCT images is necessary.

Furthermore, as highlighted in a recent scoping review, there is currently a notable lack of fully automated and scientifically validated digital workflows for 3D virtual implant placement. Existing advanced AI tools capable of such comprehensive spatial planning remain largely proprietary and closed-source9 which limits reproducibility and broader scientific advancement. There is currently a lack of publicly available 3D CBCT datasets specific to missing teeth on an international scale10. Our dataset, comprising 158 3D CBCT volumes (4,994 slices annotated), bridges this gap as the first multi-center resource to provide precise 3D boundary annotations, enabling the open-source training of algorithms to recognize complex anatomical variations. Additionally, our CBCT dental missing teeth dataset contains a notable presence of metal artifacts. The public release of this dataset will significantly contribute to advancing the development of deep learning models focused on improving CBCT image quality. By classifying and annotating missing-tooth sites according to their anatomical locations within the jaw, it becomes possible to develop location-specific AI design strategies and surgical pathways tailored to different regions in future applications. Methods

The construction of the missing teeth dataset was systematically organized into three primary stages: data collection, data screening, and data annotation. To ensure the high quality of the CBCT images, we implemented a thorough cleaning and filtering process, and ultimately selected images from 158 patients to form our final dataset. These patients ranged in age from 20–85 years and included a total of 501 tooth loss sites. To ensure precise annotations, three dentists annotated the dataset using 3D Slicer to generate corresponding three-dimensional masks. The final dataset comprised 114 tooth loss sites annotated with 3D masks, and 387 unannotated sites. Each 3D mask was composed of numerous 2D slices that collectively provided detailed anatomical information about the missing teeth regions. All relevant data have been released publicly to promote transparency and foster collaborative advancements in dental implantology. Subsequently, we performed technical validation of the collected data from both clinical and deep learning viewpoints. Ethics statement

This study was conducted with the approval of the Ethics Committee of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital (Approval No: 20250222), and all patients signed the informed consent form. Ethical approval encompasses the collection of CBCT imaging data, the reconstruction of three-dimensional models of patients’ oral anatomy, and sharing of these imaging datasets. The research strictly adhered to the ethical guidelines outlined in the World Health Assembly’s Declaration of Helsinki and the CIOMS International Ethical Guidelines for health-related research involving humans. To protect patient confidentiality, all personally identifiable information has been anonymized, retaining only gender and age. Additionally, informed consent was obtained from all participants before their inclusion in the study.

Data collection

This study collected CBCT data from 158 missing teeth patients across three medical institutions. The data were acquired using three different brands of CBCT systems: Meyer SS-X9010DPro, Newtom VGi, and Kavo OP 3D Vision V17.

CBCT scanners from different manufacturers exhibit inherent variability in acquired image quality due to disparities in radiation protocols and reconstruction algorithms11. Under constant tube current conditions, variations in tube voltage demonstrated limited efficacy in enhancing image quality metrics12. With respect to tube current modulation, reducing tube current increases image noise and decreases structural clarity13. In terms of voxel size, a smaller voxel size is associated with higher accuracy in CBCT images and a higher patient radiation dose14. The Newtom system operated at the highest tube current and smallest voxel size during image acquisition, yet yielded images with the most clearly delineated anatomical structures and the lowest background noise. In contrast, the Kavo system utilized the lowest tube current and biggest voxel size, producing images exhibit the least distinct anatomical definition and the highest background noise levels. While small-field-of-view (FOV) CBCT is commonly utilized in routine clinical practice for localized diagnosis and measuring specific edentulous sites, medium FOV CBCTs offer significant advantages for the future of automated design and execution in dental implant treatments. Full-arch scans are particularly crucial for managing complex clinical scenarios, such as continuous multi-tooth implant therapies, full-arch reconstructions, and the automated design and manufacturing of surgical guides. To address these advanced clinical needs, all CBCT examinations included in our dataset are full-arch scans. We intentionally incorporated medium FOV CBCT data from three different manufacturers with three distinct FOV dimensions. This deliberate diversity in imaging equipment and spatial dimensions is highly beneficial for improving the robustness and generalization capability of AI models.

Data screening

Cases with significant artifacts and unclear anatomical structures were excluded from further analysis. Patients with missing teeth often present with poor dental health and have typically undergone at least one dental treatment procedure. Consequently, their oral cavities may contain crown restorations or dental implants, and some patients may be undergoing orthodontic treatment with metal brackets. These metallic structures cause significant attenuation or complete absorption of X-rays, reducing the number of X-ray photons that penetrate these areas. This phenomenon leads to decreased image contrast and the appearance of dark regions or black streaks in the radiographic images15. For the current dataset, our image quality assessment criteria draw inspiration from the seminal work of Pallasch et al., whose investigation employed photon-counting detector CT imaging in patients with intraoral metallic restorations to systematically evaluate the impact of image quality on cervical tissue visualization16. Building upon existing assessment standards, we developed a dedicated CBCT evaluation system for missing teeth cases by addressing two critical aspects: (1) spatial distribution patterns of metal artifacts relative to edentulous areas; (2) structural continuity of anatomical margins. The detailed grading criteria are as follows:

5 = Excellent image quality: Sharp and well-defined anatomical structures with no artifacts.

4 = Good image quality: Continuous anatomical structures with minor artifacts, excluding metal-induced artifacts.

3 = Moderate image quality: Presence of moderate artifacts, including dental metallic materials outside the same jaw quadrant as the missing tooth site.

2 = Poor image quality: Moderate artifacts present, including dental metallic materials located in the same jaw quadrant relative to the missing tooth site.

1 = Very poor image quality: Extensive artifacts severely obscuring anatomical structures.

Cases achieving a score of 2 points or higher were included in the final dataset, resulting in the selection of 158 cases. The images included in this dataset are scored on a scale of 2 to 4. The absence of images rated 5 in this dataset stems from the clinical necessity to balance diagnostic image clarity with reduced radiation exposure for patients, resulting in a trade-off that sacrifices maximal resolution for lower dosimetry. Data annotation

The dataset contains 158 CBCT volumes, encompassing a total of 501 missing tooth sites. Among these, 4,994 CBCT slices with annotation were generated (3,004 from Meyer, 641 from Newtom, 1,349 from Kavo). The term slice refers to one axial cross-section within a CBCT volume. For each annotated case, edentulous regions were labeled as 3D cuboid bounding boxes in the CBCT coordinate space. Each cuboid spans multiple consecutive slices; therefore, only the slices that intersect a cuboid contain a visible annotation in the slice-wise representation. Selective annotation was implemented under two clinical-technical constraints: (1) The prevalence of multi-tooth continuous loss patterns in real-world patient populations, and (2) Current limitations in 3D annotation protocols and the efficacy of deep learning model training that hinder precise localization in complex missing tooth scenarios. This strategy specifically excluded cases with > 2 consecutive missing tooth positions, except two mandibular anterior cases where three consecutive annotation was permitted to ensure the sample size. Each CBCT scan contains up to three consecutive missing tooth positions, which facilitates the preliminary debugging of the deep learning model. The data annotation procedure was conducted using 3D Slicer, an open-source software platform renowned for its capabilities in medical image processing, visualization, and analysis. Widely utilized in both research and clinical environments, 3D Slicer supports multiple medical imaging formats, including DICOM, and provides a rich set of tools for tasks such as image segmentation, annotation, measurement, and 3D reconstruction17. Annotations were made by delineating the tooth loss sites using 3D cuboid structures. The cuboid annotations enable deep learning models to parse the markings layer by layer from three perspectives, thereby enhancing the precision and comprehensiveness of the analysis. Each cuboid’s upper edge was meticulously aligned with the upper border of the alveolar bone at the site of the missing tooth. The other edges of the cuboid were adjusted to harmonize with the height and width of neighboring teeth, ensuring that the annotations faithfully represent the spatial relationships within the jawbone. The annotation process involved a panel of five oral medicine experts. Specifically, three experts were randomly assigned 28 anonymized images each and independently performed the image marking tasks. An additional two experts were designated to review the annotations produced by their peers. When reviewing experts identified ambiguities or discrepancies, all five reviewers convened to discuss and resolve these issues, reaching a consensus. This collaborative approach ensured that only uniformly agreed-upon annotations were accepted as the final results. All annotation information was meticulously recorded and saved in JSON format, ensuring structured and accessible data storage for future analysis and model training.

Data Records

The dataset is available at ScienceDB (10.57760/sciencedb.26465)18. We provide the entire dataset described above for download and utilization by the wider research community. The annotated and unannotated datasets are stored in separate two folders. CBCT images are stored in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format, and annotation files are maintained in JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) format. Data derived from three distinct CBCT systems are housed in directories named Meyer, Newtom, and Kavo. Within these primary directories, each patient’s CBCT images and associated annotation files are stored in dedicated subdirectories. Annotation files for different tooth loss locations are categorized as follows: “R01.mrk.json” for maxillary anterior missing tooth, “R02.mrk.json” for mandibular anterior missing tooth, “R03.mrk.json” for maxillary posterior missing tooth, “R04.mrk.json” for mandibular posterior missing tooth. In cases where a single patient has multiple missing tooth sites within the same jaw region, these are denoted with parenthetical numerals, such as “R04(2).mrk.json,” indicating the second missing tooth site in the mandibular posterior region for that particular patient. This dataset comprises a total of 501 tooth loss sites, with 188 sites collected from Meyer, 179 from Newtom, and 134 from Kavo. We used the Fédération Dentaire Internationale (FDI) tooth notation system to number all teeth and record the sites of tooth loss. FDI tooth notation system is the internationally standardized method for designating permanent and deciduous (primary) human teeth. This two-digit system precisely identifies each tooth by dividing the dentition into four quadrants. The first digit indicates the quadrant: 1 = Permanent upper right; 2 = Permanent upper left; 3 = Permanent lower left; 4 = Permanent lower right. The second digit specifies the tooth position within the quadrant, numbered sequentially from the midline (central incisor) to the posterior (third molar), ranging from 1–8 for permanent teeth. In this dataset, the tooth position with the highest frequency of cases is 36, with 48 cases; the least frequency is 43, with only 5 cases.

For this dataset, four labeling categories were defined based on the anatomical jaw region of missing teeth: (1) Maxillary anterior region, (2) Mandibular anterior region, (3) Maxillary posterior region, and (4) Mandibular posterior region. Anatomically, teeth numbered 13–23 belong to the maxillary anterior region, while teeth numbered 33–43 are classified as the mandibular anterior region. Additionally, teeth numbered 14–17 and 24–27 are located in the maxillary posterior region, and those numbered 34–37 and 44–47 are in the mandibular posterior region.

Analysis of tooth loss patterns within this dataset reveals that the majority of missing teeth are located in mandibular posterior region, followed by the maxillary posterior, maxillary anterior, and mandibular anterior regions. Technical Validation

Clinical observational study

To reflect the diagnostic performance under clinical conditions characterized by high patient loads and significant stress, this study recruited five junior dentists (≤ 3 years experience) and five senior dentists (≥10 years experience). They annotated and classified the tooth loss sites on 10 images within a 15-minute limit. Experimental verification

Using the aforementioned dataset, we conduct training and testing of the deep learning model by employing cross-sections of three-dimensional images as an example. The dataset comprised 3D CBCT scans of 85 patients from three manufacturers (Kavo: 14 patients, Meyer: 62 patients, Newtom: 9 patients). Each 3D volume was systematically converted into axial cross-sectional slices, maintaining anatomical continuity and metadata integrity (spatial direction matrices, voxel spacing, origin coordinates).
