Comparison of Sternotomy Access Versus Thoracotomy Access in the Surgical Treatment of Aortic Coarctation: A Propensity Score-matched Study
Authors: Ilya A Soynov, Ksenya A Rzayeva, Yuriy N Gorbatykh, Yuriy Y Kulyabin, Artem V Gorbatykh, Ilya A Velyukhanov, Nataliya R Nichay, Serezha N Manukian, Saihuna A Magbulova, Aleksey N Arkhipov, Aleksander V Bogachev-Prokophiev

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe search for the “Holy Grail” of surgical repair for aortic coarctation involves finding an access and correction method that successfully and consistently avoids aortic recoarctation and arterial hypertension. The controversy persists as there is still no consensus on the best surgical approach and technique to achieve these objectives.ObjectiveThe objective of this study is to compare sternotomy and left thoracotomy as surgical techniques for treating patients with aortic coarctation and hypoplasia of the distal aortic arch.MethodsFrom January 2008 to December 2020, 103 surgical procedures were performed using an extended oblique anastomosis. The patients were divided into 2 groups: oblique, extended anastomosis from thoracotomy access (n−68; 66%) and oblique extended anastomosis from sternotomy access (n−35; 34%). After performing a propensity score analysis (1:1) for the entire sample, 25 patients from sternotomy access were matched with 25 patients from thoracotomy access.ResultsThe mortality rate in the thoracotomy group was 4%, with 1 patient, while it was 8% with 2 patients in the sternotomy group, p > 0.99. There were no significant differences in early postoperative complications between the two groups. Recurrence of aortic coarctation was observed more frequently in the thoracotomy group (20.8% vs 0%). Low weight was identified as the only risk factor for aortic re-coarctation.ConclusionThe mortality rates and early postoperative complications associated with the surgical repair of aortic coarctation were similar between the sternotomy and thoracotomy approaches. Nevertheless, sternotomy approach may be beneficial in reducing aortic recoarctation.Keywords: Coarctation of the aorta, Thoracotomy access, Sternotomy access, Aortic recoarctation

1. Introduction

The search for the “Holy Grail” of surgical repair for aortic coarctation involves finding an access and correction method that successfully and consistently avoids aortic recoarctation and arterial hypertension [1]. The controversy persists as there is still no consensus on the best surgical approach and technique to achieve these objectives. Some centers advocate left thoracotomy for patients with coarctation of the aorta and mild hypoplasia of distal aortic arch [1], while others prefer a median sternotomy with cardiopulmonary bypass [2]. Cardiac surgery centers performing left thoracotomy cite ease of access, fewer early postoperative complications, and lower risk of aortic angulation due to excessive tissue tension [1,3,4]. Opponents of left thoracotomy cite fewer long-term complications [5]. The purpose of the current study was to compare two surgical approaches: sternotomy and left thoracotomy in patients with aortic coarctation and distal aortic arch hypoplasia.

2. Methods

This single-center, retrospective study was carried out at Meshalkin National Medical Research Center. From January 2008 to December 2020, a total of 489 surgical procedures performed on patients with aortic coarctation in the Department of Congenital Heart Diseases. Of these surgeries, 103 (21%) utilized the extended oblique anastomosis technique, which satisfied the inclusion criteria indicated in Fig. 1.

Study design.

All patients were divided into two groups: oblique, extended anastomosis from thoracotomy access (n−68; 66%) and oblique extended anastomosis from sternotomy access (n−35; 34%). The study was approved by Local Institutional review board and was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. The main objective of this study is to compare the incidence of recoarctation of the aorta as the primary endpoint between patients undergoing surgical repair using sternotomy and left thoracotomy approaches. The secondary objectives include evaluating the presence of arterial hypertension and assessing the remodeling of the aortic arch, specifically focusing on the classification of gothic and crenel-type aortic arches.

The diagnosis of aortic coarctation was established through a comprehensive assessment that included physical examination as well as the application of diagnostic criteria based on transthoracic echocardiography. The study was carried out on ultrasonic systems “Philips Ultrasound” (Bothell, WA, USA), “Vivid iq” (GE Medical, Milwaukee, WI, USA). Inhospital mortality was assessed as the development of death within 30 days from the date of surgery. Acute damage to two or more organs was used as the criteria for assessing multiple organ failure.

The assessment and calculation of the inotropic index was carried out within 24 h after surgery using the formula of Gaies et al. [6]. Paradoxical arterial hypertension was determined by the elevation of blood pressure beyond the 95th percentile of the distributions of these parameters, considering the respective growth indicators during the first 24–48 h [7]. Injury of the recurrent nerve was diagnosed as paresis of the left vocal cord using bronchoscopy on Olympus XP 60 (Olympus Medical Systems Corp. Japan). Compression of the bronchi or trachea was established as any narrowing of the bronchi or trachea that occurred after surgery using bronchoscopy and confirmed using MS CT examination. In diagnosing arterial hypertension based on 24-h monitoring data, the criteria included an elevation in the average values of systolic blood pressure (SBP) and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) beyond the 95th percentile of the distributions of these parameters, considering the respective growth indicators [7]. Z-score was calculated using the Petterson calculator, recommended for newborns and young children [8]. A Z-value less than −2 indicates severe hypoplasia (in this case, the aortic arch). During the long-term follow-up period, CT scans were conducted to verify or rule out both recoarctation and the presence of any remodeling of the aortic arch. Aortic recoarctation was defined as repeated narrowing of the aortic arch with a Z-value < −2 or more. Aortic arch remodeling was assessed according to the criteria of Ou et al. [9]. In the long-term follow-up at our center, all patients underwent an examination by a pediatric cardiologist (at least 3 times), 24-h blood pressure monitoring to determine arterial hypertension, echocardiography (at least 3 times), and CT scans for the diagnosis of aortic aneurysms and recoarctation.

Surgical details.

Surgical repair from the thoracotomy approach.

Surgical access to the aorta was performed through the third intercostal space from a left posterolateral thoracotomy. A prerequisite was the mobilization of the aorta, which included the isolation of the aortic arch to the brachiocephalic trunk, the left common carotid and left subclavian arteries, the aortic isthmus, the patent ductus arteriosus, the area of narrowing and the descending aorta. The ductus arteriosus was doubly ligated and divided. To avoid the development of recoarctation, all ductal tissue was resected within the “healthy” tissue. After mobilization, the aorta was clamped with Satinsky clamp between the right brachiocephalic trunk and the left common carotid artery, with simultaneous occlusion of the left common carotid artery and the left subclavian artery. The descending aorta occluded with the distal Satinsky clamp below the narrowed segment of the aortic arch. All coarctation and ductal tissue was resected. To expand the perimeter of the anastomosis, along the inferior curvature of aortic arch was made an incision, not reaching 2–3 mm to the branches of the proximal Satinsky clamp. The distal end of the transected aorta, if necessary, was dissected along the outer wall to achieve optimal compliance when performing anastomosis. The last stage was the formation of an end-to-end anastomosis with a continuous entwined suture in the direction under the aortic arch (“extended” anastomosis).

Surgical repair from the sternotomy approach.

All patients were operated under general combined anesthesia. For induction, sevoran 6–7 vol/%, fentanyl at a dosage of 5–6 μg/kg, and arduan 0.06 mg/kg were used. For maintenance anesthesia, sevoran (1–1.5 vol/%), fentanyl 5–7 μg/(kg h), and Arduan 0.03 μg/(kg h) were used. Blood pressure monitoring was carried out in the right radial and femoral arteries. Cerebral saturation was assessed using the INVOS 5100 device (Somanetics, USA) throughout the procedure. To perform CPB, Dideco Lilliput I systems (Sorin, Italy) were used. The initial fill volume of the extracorporeal circuit was 200–220 ml and included donor packed red blood cells (to maintain a hematocrit of at least 30%), fresh frozen plasma 10 ml/kg, 20% albumin 5 ml/kg, sodium bicarbonate 4%, mannitol and heparin. To gain access to the heart and great vessels, a median sternotomy was performed. To ensure systemic perfusion during the surgical procedure, two different methods of aortic cannulation were employed based on the individual anatomy of the ascending aorta and the chosen method of protection. These methods included direct cannulation into the ascending aorta and double cannulation. In cases where direct cannulation into the ascending aorta was feasible and appropriate, a cannula was inserted directly into the ascending aorta. Alternatively, in situations where direct cannulation was not suitable, a heparinized polytetrafluoroethylene GoreTex graft (GORE-TEX® Vascular Grafts, W. L. Gore & Associates, Inc., Flagstaff, AZ, USA) was used as an alternative conduit. In this scenario, one cannula was inserted into the ascending aorta through the graft, while a second cannula was placed into the thoracic descending aorta to perfuse the lower half of the body. This approach involved perfusing the posterior pericardium to ensure adequate blood flow to the lower body during the surgical procedure. The selection of the appropriate cannulation method was based on individual patient factors and surgical considerations. Depending on the concomitant pathology or its absence, separate cannulation of the vena cava was performed or a monocannula was installed in the right atrium. If necessary, a left ventricular drain was placed in the right upper lobe pulmonary vein. During the procedure, CPB was established with a volumetric perfusion rate of 150 ml/kg. Cooling was initiated for a minimum of 20 min, gradually lowering the patient’s rectal temperature to specific target ranges depending on the technique utilized. For deep hypothermic circulatory arrest, the rectal temperature was reduced to 18 °C. In cases where antegrade cerebral perfusion was employed, the rectal temperature was maintained between 25 and 32 °C. With double aortic cannulation, the rectal temperature was raised to a maximum of 32 °C. Throughout the cooling and rewarming process, a temperature gradient of no more than 5 °C was maintained between the perfusate and the patient’s temperature. The blood gas composition was managed in α-stat mode throughout the procedure, with regular monitoring conducted at intervals of every 20 min. Following aortic occlusion, myocardial protection was achieved by administering Brettschneider’s crystalloid cardioplegic solution (Custodiol Dr. Franz Kohler Chemie, Alsbach-Hahnlein, Germany) in an antegrade manner into the aortic root. This cardioplegic solution was administered at a dosage of 40 ml/kg, aimed at ensuring adequate myocardial protection during the procedure. Reconstruction of the aortic arch was carried out using an oblique extended anastomosis. The ascending aorta was meticulously dissected and mobilized, extending from the aortic root to the aortic arch and continuing down to the origin of the third pair of intercostal arteries in the descending aorta. The brachiocephalic arteries were highly mobilized to eliminate tension on the aortic tissue. The distal portion of the descending aorta was occluded using a Satinsky clamp, positioning it below the narrowed segment of the aortic arch. Complete excision of the coarctation and any ductal tissue was performed during the surgical procedure. To facilitate an optimal anastomosis, the perimeter of the aorta was expanded by making an incision along the inferior curvature, allowing access to the proximal part of the ascending aorta. If deemed necessary, the distal end of the transected aorta was carefully dissected along the outer wall to attain optimal compliance. The final stage involved the creation of an oblique extended anastomosis, also known as an “extended” anastomosis.

The choice of surgical approach was based on surgeon preference. All operations were performed by 3 experienced surgeons.

2.1. Statistical analysis

The surgical data were analyzed using Stata 14 software (StataCorp LP, College Station, USA). Prior to analysis, the normality of all variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilks test. Continuous data are reported as the median and interquartile range (25th; 75th percentiles), while categorical data are presented as numbers and percentages. Group comparisons were conducted using the Fisher test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. Propensity score matching analysis was employed to minimize the influence of sampling bias and potential confounding among patients. Through multivariate logistic regression analysis, pseudorandomization was performed for each patient to determine the probability of undergoing a lateral thoracotomy. The baseline variables used for pseudorandomization in this study included age, sex, body weight, body surface area, and distal aortic arch Z score. The propensity score matching technique was employed using the nearest neighbor matching algorithm with a caliper of 0.2. A 5 to 1 comparison was implemented, with a case–control ratio of 1:1. To assess the balance between variables before and after matching, standardized percentage bias was examined. The balance was further evaluated using standardized differences (d). Categorical variables were compared using the McNemar test, while the paired T-test was utilized for continuous variables with normal distribution. The results of the logistic regression analysis were reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Results before propensity score matching analysis

Hospital mortality was noted in 1 patient in the thoracotomy group (1.4%) and 2 (5.7%) patients in the sternotomy group (P = 0.26). The time of mechanical ventilation in the thoracotomy group was 26 (14; 72) h and that in the sternotomy group was 72 (23; 180) h (P = 0.028). Median intensive care unit (ICU) stay duration was 4 (2; 7) days in the thoracotomy group and 6 (4; 10) days in the sternotomy group (P = 0.022). Acute kidney injury was observed in 5 patients in the thoracotomy group (7.3%) and in 11 patients in the sternotomy group (31.4%) (P = 0.003). Pneumonia was noted in 12 (17.6%) patients in the thoracotomy group versus 4 (11.4%) patients in the sternotomy group (P = 0.56). Multiorgan failure syndrome developed in 3 patients in the thoracotomy group (4.4%) and in 3 patients in the sternotomy group (8.5%) (P = 0.42). Chylothorax was noted in 1 (1.4%) patients in the thoracotomy group versus 0 (0%) patients in the sternotomy group (P > 0.99). Recurrent nerve injury developed in 4 patients in the thoracotomy group (5.9%) and in 2 patients in the sternotomy group (5.7%) (P > 0.99). Bronchial compression was observed in 1 patient in the thoracotomy group (1.4%) and in 4 patients in the sternotomy group (11.4%) (P = 0.044). Neurological complications developed just in 5 patients in the sternotomy group (14.3%) (P = 0.008). Paradoxical hypertension was noted in 32 (47%) patients in the thoracotomy group versus 12 (34.3%) patients in the sternotomy group (P = 0.30). Median hospitalization time duration was 18 (14.5; 22) days in the thoracotomy group and 20 (17; 30) days in the sternotomy group (P = 0.011).

There was no mortality in the long-term period. Recoarctation developed just in 8 patients in the thoracotomy group (12.1%) (P = 0.048). Arterial hypertension was noted in 15 (22.7%) patients in the thoracotomy group versus 8 (24.2%) patients in the sternotomy group (P > 0.99).

4. Discussion

Coarctation of the aorta frequently coexists with hypoplasia of the distal aortic arch [10]. Different surgical approaches have been advocated for addressing both the coarctation and hypoplasia. Some authors argue in favor of a lateral thoracotomy approach, emphasizing the avoidance of CPB and the associated complications such as bleeding, renal dysfunction, and neurological complications [11–14]. We note in our study that with lateral thoracotomy there is shorter operating time and less bleeding, which may affect early postoperative results. Conversely, other authors support the use of median sternotomy with CPB due to its low risk of aortic recoarctation, ability to address concurrent congenital heart defects, and potential for improved long-term quality of life [15,16]. These varying perspectives reflect the ongoing debate regarding the optimal surgical approach for coarctation of the aorta and hypoplasia of the distal aortic arch. In our study, we compared two surgical approaches (correction of aortic coarctation from median sternotomy and lateral thoracotomy) of one surgical technique (aggressive oblique extended anastomosis). Extended oblique anastomosis is the “gold standard” for the treatment of aortic coarctation in modern pediatric cardiac surgery [3,17]. The advantages of this technique include complete resection of the narrowed portion of the aorta along with ductal tissue, wide anastomosis, and the potential for increased repair [16,17]. In our study, we used the same surgical technique for different surgical approaches to minimize data heterogeneity. In our study, the overall mortality rate did not exceed 8%, which falls within the range reported in other studies considering different surgical approaches, where mortality rates range from 0.9% to 15% [16,18]. Notably, all cases of mortality in our study were attributed to causes unrelated to cardiac issues: in the lateral thoracotomy group, the patient died from neonatal pneumonia, while in the sternotomy group, two patients succumbed to necrotizing enterocolitis. We also identified long-term mechanical ventilation after surgery as the only risk factor for mortality. Although we did not observe a significant difference in immediate postoperative complications between the two surgical groups, it is important to highlight that compression of the left bronchus was more commonly observed with the sternotomy approach, consistent with findings from the study by Ishimaru et al. [19]. The only identified risk factor for compression of the left bronchus was a weight less than 2.5 kg. Nakamura et al. provide an explanation for this phenomenon, attributing the narrowing of the bronchus to the limited mobility of the aorta resulting from an aggressive technique during anastomosis formation [20]. To address the issue of left bronchus compression following aortic coarctation correction, Arcieri et al. propose a solution known as “posterior aortopexy” [21]. In our study, all patients underwent aortopexy along with additional mobilization of the descending aorta, which enabled successful weaning from mechanical ventilation with minimal respiratory distress. It is worth mentioning that patients who underwent sternotomy access required more prolonged use of cardiotonic support, likely due to the comprehensive treatment of aortic coarctation and associated anomalies, which consequently resulted in more severe cardiopulmonary compromise. A crucial aspect to evaluate in each surgery is the growth dynamics of the aortic arch. Disproportionate growth of the arch can lead to recoarctation syndrome, characterized by severe arterial hypertension [5]. In our study, we assessed the growth dynamics of the aortic arch using the Z score parameter. We observed proportional growth of the aortic arch in both surgical groups. However, the more aggressive approach of forming an oblique extended anastomosis through the sternotomy access allowed for effective expansion of the entire aortic arch. On the other hand, an oblique extended anastomosis technique primarily expanded the distal part of the aortic arch. This finding suggests that the choice of surgical approach can influence the extent and effectiveness of aortic arch expansion during the procedure. While many studies report effective growth of a hypoplastic aortic arch following successful coarctation of the aorta correction, it is important to consider potential factors that can impact growth dynamics. Purse stringing of the suture and fibrosis at the level of the anastomotic suture may contribute to residual hypoplasia of the aortic arch and, consequently, disproportionate growth of the anastomosis [22,23]. In our long-term follow-up, no significant difference was observed between the groups in terms of arterial hypertension manifestation. Additionally, Seo et al. suggested in their study that higher tissue tension might lead to the development of a gothic aortic arch [24]. These findings emphasize the complexity of aortic arch growth and highlight the potential influence of surgical techniques and tissue tension on long-term outcomes. In our study, we initially hypothesized that a more aggressive anastomosis technique would result in a higher incidence of aortic arch angulation. However, our findings did not support this hypothesis. We found no significant difference between the groups in terms of gothic and Crenel-type aortic arches, which are often associated with arterial hypertension. The only identified risk factor in our study was a left ventricular myocardial mass index greater than 59 g/m2. Furthermore, during long-term follow-up, we did not observe any differences between the groups in the manifestation of arterial hypertension. Seo et al., in their study, suggested that higher tissue tension could contribute to the development of a gothic aortic arch [24]. In our study, based on data from Seo and colleagues, we assumed that more aggressive anastomosis should lead to more frequent aortic arch angulation. However, after conducting research, we did not confirm our hypothesis. We did not find a difference between the groups in such indicators as gothic and Crenel-type aortic arches, which most often lead to arterial hypertension. The only risk factor in our study was a left ventricular myocardial mass index. Coartcation of the aorta is not a simple mechanical obstruction, but a systemic disease that leads to changes in the elastic framework, changes in the neuroregulation of the aorta and mixed arteries, as well as hypertrophy of the left ventricular myocardium [25]. Thus, Leandro et al. demonstrated in their study that left ventricular mass was increased, despite the absence of a significant increase in blood pressure during 24-h monitoring [26]. Similar data were obtained by Ou et al., where 40 normotensive patients had a significant increase in the left ventricular mass index after correction of aortic coarctation [27]. Crepaz et al. [28] found that left ventricular mass index has a direct relationship with increased blood pressure in patients after correction of aortic coarctation.

Another long-term complication was aortic recoarctation, which was significantly more common in the thoracotomy group (20.8%), while there was no recoarctation in the sternotomy group. The recoarctation rate, depending on the method, can vary from 0 to 100% [29]. Most often, recoarctation occurs during balloon angioplasty and direct and indirect isthmoplasty [30]. The lowest incidence of recoarctation is observed in techniques with excision of the aortic isthmus along with ductal tissue [16]. However, the incidence of recoarctation varies depending on the surgical approach. Thus, according to Sakurai et al., the rate of recoarctation with sternotomy access was 8%, while the rate of recoarctation with lateral thoracotomy was 39% [22]. In a study by Rakhra et al., the risk factor for aortic recoarctation was an oblique extended anastomosis performed from a lateral thoracotomy [31]. In our study, the only risk factor for aortic recoarctation was patients with low weight who underwent surgery through a lateral thoracotomy. According to clinical recommendations, we started treatment of aortic recoarctation with balloon dilatation. The effectiveness of balloon dilatation in our study was 60%. In 40%, due to severe fibrosis at the level of the anastomosis, repeated surgical correction was performed using an oblique extended anastomosis from a sternotomy approach.

The study was retrospective with a small sample of patients, which may limit the obtained results. A prospective, randomized study may provide new data. The study was performed in a single center, which is also a limitation.

5. Conclusions

The mortality rates and early postoperative complications in patients undergoing surgical repair for aortic coarctation were found to be comparable between the sternotomy and thoracotomy approaches. Nevertheless, sternotomy approach may be beneficial in reducing aortic recoarctation.
