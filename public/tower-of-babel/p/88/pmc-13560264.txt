An interpretable XGboost algorithm for predicting 30-day mortality in acute pancreatitis using routine biomarkers
Authors: Jun Zhou, Ying Chen, Jingping Liu, Wenyang Hou, Bin Pei, Mengxiao Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop and validate a machine learning (ML) algorithm for predicting 30-day mortality in adult patients with acute pancreatitis (AP) using routinely readily available laboratory parameters.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed 965 consecutive AP patients hospitalized between January 2017 and December 2019. Feature selection was performed using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, followed by the evaluation of eleven machine learning algorithms. Model performance was assessed using via the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The optimal model was further interpreted using SHapley additive explanation (SHAP).ResultsThe overall 30-day mortality rate was 4.5% (43/965). Among the evaluated algorithms, extreme gradient boosting (XGboost) demonstrated the best performance in both the development and validation cohorts. SHAP analysis identified creatine kinase (CK) as the most influential predictor, followed by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), age, prothrombin time (PT), and carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9). A simplified algorithm incorporating only the top five features achieved near-perfect discrimination in the development cohort (AUC = 1.000) and maintained robust performance in the validation cohort (AUC = 0.847). This five-feature XGboost algorithm showed superior performance compared to conventional prognostic scores in the development cohort.ConclusionAn interpretable XGboost algorithm incorporating five key parameters (CK, LDH, age, PT, CA19-9) shows potential to predict predicts 30-day mortality in AP patients. This single-center retrospective model demonstrates promising internal performance but requires prospective multicenter external validation before clinical implementation. The web-based platform is provided as a research prototype for proof-of-concept and must not be used for real-time clinical decision-making at this stage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12874-026-02900-6.

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a prevalent gastrointestinal emergency worldwide, with an incidence that is steadily increasing—currently estimated at approximately 34 cases per 100,000 population annually [1]. Clinically, AP presents with a broad spectrum of severity, ranging from mild, self-limiting episodes to life-threatening conditions characterized by systemic inflammation and multi-organ failure. Approximately 20–30% of cases progress to severe acute pancreatitis (SAP), which is defined by persistent organ dysfunction or local complications and is associated with significantly worse outcomes and higher mortality [2]. Timely risk stratification is therefore critical for guiding therapeutic decisions and improving prognosis.

However, a critical gap persists regarding the lack of a simple, early, and highly accurate tool for predicting mortality in AP patients. Conventional scoring systems—such as the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE-II), Ranson, and Bedside Index for Severity in Acute Pancreatitis (BISAP)—are limited in routine clinical practice. These limitations stem from their reliance on complex or serial measurements, delayed result availability, and suboptimal sensitivity and specificity, particularly during the initial hours after admission. Accordingly, there is an urgent need for a prognostic model that utilizes only routine admission laboratory data, combined with machine learning (ML), to provide immediate and accurate risk stratification.

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI), particularly ML, has demonstrated considerable promise in enhancing clinical prediction tasks, including diagnostics, prognosis estimation, and survival forecasting [3, 4]. Despite its expanding application in critical care, ML has not been extensively explored for early mortality risk prediction in AP. This study aims to address this gap by identifying key laboratory predictors of mortality in AP and by leveraging eleven ML algorithms to develop an accurate, interpretable, and clinically applicable algorithm for predicting 30-day mortality in hospitalized AP patients.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study included adult patients diagnosed with first-onset acute pancreatitis (AP) who were admitted to the First Affiliated Hospital With Nanjing Medical University between January 2017 and December 2019. Inclusion criteria were as follows:① age ≥ 18 years; ② diagnosis of acute pancreatitis (AP) based on the 2012 Atlanta criteria [5] (presence of at least two of the following: abdominal pain, serum amylase or lipase ≥ 3 times the upper limit of normal, and characteristic imaging findings);③ admission within 24 h of symptom onset;④ availability of complete admission laboratory data. Exclusion criteria: age < 18 years; history of chronic pancreatitis or pancreatic cancer; pregnancy or lactation; severe pre-existing organ dysfunction; recent major trauma or surgery; immunocompromised status; and incomplete routine laboratory data. Ultimately, 965 eligible patients were included. To develop an early and objective risk stratification tool, the analysis focused exclusively on laboratory parameters obtained at admission. No formal sample size calculation was performed; all consecutive eligible patients during the study period were included.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University (approval number: 2023-SR-043), with a waiver of informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Feature selection and model development

Patients were randomly allocated to a development cohort (70%) and a validation cohort (30%) using stratified sampling via scikit-learn (v1.2.2) to ensure a balanced distribution of the outcome variable. All continuous features were then normalized using min-max scaling to (0, 1) based on the development cohort, with the same parameters applied to the validation cohort. To address class imbalance, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied exclusively to the development cohort, while the validation cohort was left unaltered to ensure unbiased performance evaluation. All feature selection procedures were conducted strictly within the development cohort to prevent data leakage. LASSO regression was performed on the development cohort using 10-fold cross-validation to determine the optimal regularization parameter (λ), which was selected according to the “one standard error” (1-SE) rule. Predictors with non-zero coefficients at the optimal λ were retained, yielding a set of 22 candidate features [6, 7]. These selected features were then fixed and used to train all subsequent machine learning models without further modification; the validation cohort was not accessed during this process.

Eleven machine learning algorithms were constructed, including Naive-Bayes (NB), K-nearest Neighbor (KNN), logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), decision tree (DT), artificial neural network (ANN), support vector machine (SVM), gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT), light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), adaptive boosting (AdaBoost), and extreme gradient boosting (XGboost).

Hyperparameter optimization for each algorithm was performed using 5-fold cross-validation within the development cohort. A grid search was employed to explore the hyperparameter space, with the optimal configuration selected based on the highest mean area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) across the five folds. The final model for each algorithm was then retrained on the entire development cohort using its respective optimal hyperparameters.

Model performance was evaluated using the AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and F1 score. The algorithm demonstrating the highest AUC in the validation cohort was designated as the optimal model.

Outcome definition

The primary endpoint was 30-day all-cause mortality, measured from the date of hospital admission. Survival status was ascertained from hospital electronic medical records for in-hospital deaths and via structured telephone follow-up at 30 days for patients discharged alive. Outcome assessment was blinded to the model predictions, as mortality status was ascertained from electronic medical records and telephone follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM) and R software (version 4.3.1). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range], as appropriate; categorical variables are presented as frequency (percentage). Between-group comparisons were made using the Mann-Whitney U test or Pearson’s chi-square test. Variables with missing values exceeding 5% were excluded from the analysis; for the remaining variables with minimal missing data (< 5%), imputation was performed using random forest-based methods. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In the development cohort, significant prognostic variables (p < 0.05) included age, vital signs (temperature, pulse, respiratory rate, systolic blood pressure, urine output), therapeutic interventions (oxygen support, dopamine, adrenaline, norepinephrine), complications (pleural effusion, chronic renal failure, pancreatic necrosis), and a broad range of laboratory values. These laboratory parameters comprised inflammatory markers (CRP, WBC, LY, NE, LY, Mo percentage, NE percentage, RDW, PLT, PCT, MPV), coagulation profiles (PT, PT-INR, APTT, FIB, TT, D-dimer), and metabolic indicators (ALB, Ca, UREA). No significant associations were observed for sex, BMI, diastolic blood pressure, lifestyle factors, comorbidities, or the following laboratory results: hematological indices (monocyte count, RBC, HB, HCT, MCV, MCH, MCHC, PDW), hepatic enzymes (AFP, ALT, ALP, GGT, TBIL, IBIL, TG, albumin/globulin ratio, GLU), electrolyte panels (K, Na, Cl), or pancreatic enzymes (AMY, uAMY) (Table 1).

Model selection and validation

Following SMOTE processing, LASSO regression was applied to the development cohort and identified 22 key variables, which were subsequently used to train eleven machine learning algorithms (Fig. 2A–B). XGboost achieved the highest area under the curve in both the development (AUC = 1.000) and validation cohorts (AUC = 0.792). We further validated the model using cross-validation, and XGBoost continued to demonstrate robust performance (AUC = 0.995). Although its perfect performance in the development cohort may indicate overfitting, it remained the best-performing algorithm in the validation cohort and was therefore selected as the final algorithm (Fig. 3A–B; Table 2).

Discussion

AP can progress from mild, self-limiting illness (mortality < 1%) to severe disease with mortality exceeding 30% [8, 9]. Consequently, prompt identification of at-risk individuals constitutes a critical priority in clinical management.

ML algorithms have demonstrated markedly enhanced predictive accuracy compared to conventional models in critical care settings, as evidenced by recent investigations [10, 11]. Within AP management, these computational approaches show particular promise for severity stratification and complication forecasting. Pearce et al. validated ML’s superiority over admission APACHE II scores in identifying SAP, while Wei et al. established CatBoost-based algorithms as optimal tools for mortality prognostication in ICU-admitted AP patients [12, 13].This evidence is reinforced by well-designed studies conducted in related fields. For instance, Soldera et al. successfully employed XGboost and SHAP to predict major adverse cardiovascular events after liver transplantation, emphasizing three critical pillars for reliable clinical prediction: rigorous feature selection to avoid overfitting, comprehensive model calibration, and external validation [14]. The present investigation substantiates these observations, confirming machine learning’s consistent superiority over traditional scoring methodologies, although the differences were not statistically significant in the validation cohort (DeLong test, all p > 0.05).

Our investigation employed a comprehensive analysis of admission laboratory parameters to identify key prognostic indicators. Through LASSO regression, we refined 56 initial laboratory parameters to 22 pivotal predictors. These biomarkers were subsequently incorporated into eleven distinct machine learning algorithms.

Following rigorous validation, the XGboost algorithm demonstrated optimal performance. To enhance clinical applicability, SHAP analysis facilitated further model simplification, revealing that only five cost-effective, routinely available biomarkers - CK, LDH, age, PT, and CA19-9, are required for better prediction. This streamlined XGboost algorithm was consequently implemented in an accessible web-based calculator to support clinical decision-making.

CK serves as a diagnostic biomarker for injuries or pathologies affecting skeletal muscle, myocardial tissue, or cerebral structures, with its levels correlating with pathological severity. In AP, an elevated CK level predominantly indicates skeletal muscle injury secondary to intra-abdominal hypertension (IAH) and abdominal compartment syndrome (ACS), rather than primary myocardial damage. Severe AP is often complicated by systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) and capillary leak, which promote progressive IAH. This elevated intra-abdominal pressure impairs skeletal muscle perfusion, leading to ischemic injury and rhabdomyolysis, and consequent CK release. However, the precise mechanisms underlying CK elevation in AP remain to be elucidated and require further investigation. Therefore, elevated CK in AP may serve as a marker of IAH/ACS severity—a well-established independent predictor of multiorgan failure and mortality—rather than a direct indicator of cardiac injury [15]. Previous investigations have established independent correlations between elevated CK/CK-MB levels and critical endpoints including mortality, SAP development, and infected pancreatic necrosis (INP) [16, 17]. In our analysis, CK emerged as the foremost prognostic indicator for 30-day mortality, aligning with established clinical evidence. Additionally, LDH and advanced age were confirmed as significant mortality predictors, corroborating prior research [18].

Emerging as the fourth most significant predictive factor, PT (a key extrinsic pathway coagulation indicator) is markedly prolonged in AP. This prolongation is especially evident in severe AP and correlates positively with worsening disease severity [19, 20].

CA19-9 also emerged as a crucial predictor, showing an association with 30-day mortality risk. Its elevation could reflect both the severity of acute pancreatitis and the potential presence of pancreatic cancer, thereby elevating short-term mortality [21].

We acknowledge that malignancy or biliary obstruction may confound this association, as we cannot fully exclude these diseases based on admission data alone.

In the context of AP, CA19-9 elevation is well-recognized to occur in benign conditions due to two primary mechanisms: disruption of pancreatic acinar architecture by inflammation and transient biliary obstruction secondary to pancreatic head edema. Thus, CA19-9 elevation in AP primarily reflects the severity of pancreatic inflammation and biliary involvement, rather than occult malignancy.

To facilitate real-world clinical adoption, we have developed a web-based calculator that implements the five-feature XGboost algorithm. In routine practice, this tool could be integrated into hospital electronic health record systems. Upon admission of a patient meeting AP diagnostic criteria, the system would automatically extract the five predictors (CK, LDH, age, PT, CA19-9) from admission laboratory data and compute the 30-day mortality risk score in real time. The result could be displayed on the physician’s dashboard, facilitating early identification of high-risk patients and guiding more intensive monitoring or early intervention. Given the limited validation cohort size (only 13 deaths) and the absence of decision-curve analysis, these suggestions remain hypothetical and should be tested in future prospective studies before real-world deployment.

The results must be interpreted alongside study limitations. First, the single-center retrospective design and modest sample size (965 patients, 43 events) represent inherent constraints. While we employed LASSO regression for feature selection, SMOTE for class imbalance, and cross-validation to mitigate overfitting, the small sample size may still affect model stability. Second, the lack of external validation remains a critical limitation that precludes generalization of the model to other populations or clinical settings. Third, our exclusion of patients with significant comorbidities, while reducing confounding, may restrict applicability to real-world populations where multimorbidity is common. External validation in multicenter, real-world cohorts is needed. Finally, critical prognostic factors such as temporal parameter evolution (e.g., 48-hour changes) and CT-based scoring characteristics were not incorporated. These limitations strongly caution against overinterpretation of the model’s real-world clinical utility at this stage. Collectively, the model should be considered internally validated only, pending rigorous external validation in larger, prospective multicenter cohorts.

Conclusion

We developed an interpretable XGboost-based prognostic model for estimating 30-day mortality risk in adult AP patients using five simple, cost-effective admission biomarkers (CK, LDH, age, PT, and CA19-9). The model demonstrates favorable discriminative ability in our single-center retrospective cohort. However, it is critical to reiterate that prospective multicenter external validation is still lacking. The model’s performance and generalizability must be rigorously tested in large, prospective, multicenter cohorts before any clinical implementation or real-world use.

Overinterpretation of its clinical utility should be strictly avoided at this proof-of-concept stage.
