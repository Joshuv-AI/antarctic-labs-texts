Current status and future challenges in the use of Earth Observation data for Civil Protection activities: the Italian case study
Authors: Andrea Taramelli, Roberta Bonì, Marco Salvadore, Silvia Ricolfi, Valentina Cima, Ignacio Gatti, Alessandra Nguyen Xuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores the integration of Earth Observation (EO) data within Italy’s civil protection and disaster risk management (DRM) framework, with a focus on enhancing response, preparedness, and recovery efforts. Recognizing the critical role of satellite and in-situ EO products in monitoring hazards such as earthquakes, volcanic activity, meteo-hydrological (i.e., landslides, floods, land subsidence, droughts), wildfires, and technological hazards, the research evaluates current European Copernicus services and products, and their applicability to Italian civil protection needs. Through the creation of a comprehensive database of the Italian Civil Protection Department’s (DPC) needs and technical requirements, the study identifies existing gaps and unexploited opportunities for EO data utilisation. It emphasizes the importance of a user need-driven approach, with potential applicability across Europe, to inform future mission planning, service improvements, and the ongoing development of the Italian IRIDE EO constellation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49067-0.Subject terms: Environmental sciences, Hydrology, Natural hazards

Introduction

Civil protection and disaster risk management (DRM) refer to the policies, bodies, and mechanisms that a country or region has in place to protect the people, the environment and the functioning of critical infrastructure1 from impacts due to natural hazards, such as earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, extreme weather events, as well as human-induced, such as industrial accidents. Earth Observation (EO) data (including satellite and in-situ data like ground-based, or airborne) can be transformed into valuable information sources to support actions across all phases of the disaster management cycle (pre-disaster, emergency, and post-disaster)2. Indeed, EO services and products enable rapid response and timely delivery of images of the areas affected by the event in response phase2, but also geospatial information for risk mapping3 in the preparedness phase and for post-event monitoring4.

Starting in 2000, an initiative named “International Charter Space and major disaster” has been coordinated by space agencies around the world. It pioneered the provision of free satellite data and map products to authorised users (i.e., Civil Protection Authority) for response and monitoring activities of major natural and human-induced disasters5. In Europe, starting in 2008 an initiative named “Global Monitoring for Environment and Security” (GMES) was developed to provide operational information for monitoring and management of the environment and civil security6. In 2012, the GMES programme was renamed Copernicus programme. The Program is led by the European Union, represented by the European Commission, and is driven by policies to support them and by requirements from users7–10. The coordinator of the space component is the European Space Agency (ESA) and other European institutions are involved as entrusted entities for the core services such as the European Organisation for the Exploitation of Meteorological Satellites (EUMETSAT), the European Environment Agency (EEA), and the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecast (ECMWF)11. Copernicus offers a comprehensive suite of services providing critical data and information across various environmental and security domains. The program aims to collect data to monitor the Earth using satellite-based and ground-based data, and it includes six thematic services: land, marine, atmosphere, climate change, emergency management, and security. The Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS) focuses on air quality and atmospheric composition, while the Copernicus Marine Environment Monitoring Service (CMEMS) delivers insights into our oceans. For terrestrial observations, the Copernicus Land Monitoring Service (CLMS), a collaborative effort between the European Environment Agency (EEA) and the European Commission’s Joint Research Centre (JRC), provides land cover and land use data. Climate-related information is supplied by the Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S), implemented by ECMWF. Additionally, Copernicus addresses security concerns through its dedicated Copernicus Security service (CSS) and provides rapid mapping and early warning capabilities via the Copernicus Emergency Management Service (CEMS), also implemented by the JRC, for effective disaster response.

The Italian national territory is threatened by a wide range of natural hazards, such as earthquakes, volcanic activity, landslides, floods, droughts, wildfires, and technological hazards12. From 2000 to 2025, more than 50,000 deaths due to natural and technological disasters have been reported in Italy in the Emergency Event Database (EM-DAT). Between 2003 and 2020, around 378 deaths due to natural hazards were reported in Italy12. L’Aquila earthquake casualties in April 2009 accounted for more than 300 of that totals, with economic losses estimated at over $2.5 billion, 100,000 buildings damaged and 67,000 people left homeless13. Another big earthquake in Amatrice (Lazio region) in August 2016 caused $11 billion in direct and indirect economic losses14. Regions like Emilia-Romagna have suffered frequent events like the 2012 earthquake or the 2024 alluvial floods, leading to widespread damage to infrastructures, private properties, and agriculture, prompting substantial EU solidarity funding. In Italy, frequent landslides or mass movements cause victims and severe damage15. For instance, Messina province mass movement events between 2009 and 2010 caused 37 deaths and severe damage to urbanised areas, infrastructures (national highways, rural roads, and railroads, cultivated and pasture lands)16. Another landslide in Rigopiano (Abruzzo region) in 2017 caused 29 deaths and also imposed considerable social and economic costs on the affected mountain communities17. In Italy, significant problems can be caused also by recurring periods of ground uplift and subsidence (e.g. Campi Flegrei, Campania region)18, or slow slip processes leading to aseismic creep (e.g. Mt. Etna, Sicilia)19 associated with volcano dynamics. Italy is also susceptible to frequent and impactful flood events classified from riverine floods to destructive flash floods and coastal inundations, examples of Po river flood in 201420,21 or Emilia-Romagna in 2023 with 15 deaths, 36,000 people evacuated and billions of Euros of economic losses22, as well as droughts that affects areas like agriculture, water supply, energy production, and ecosystems (e.g. severe 2022 drought in northern Italy)23. Extreme temperatures and wildfire are also other hazards to be considered, the second one with some important events in drier regions in southern Italy, for instance, the summer of 2017, with 140,000 hectares burnt areas24. Significant industrial base, dense population, and extensive infrastructure are susceptible to a range of technological hazards. Industrial incidents, including fires, explosions, and chemical leaks, are more common. Maritime accidents involve significant loss of life during irregular migration by sea, especially in southern Italy, near areas like Lampedusa (Sicily region) or the Costa Concordia wreck offshore the Giglio Island25.

As previously mentioned, EO products derived from satellites offer invaluable insights into various natural hazards. Both optical and Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) sensors are used to detect and monitor floods, ground deformation, gas emissions, wildfire spread, landslide mapping, and drought detection26,27. EO products from satellites are a key component in the Italian Civil Protection Department (DPC) operational toolkit28. DPC operates a comprehensive risk management system, coordinating national, regional, and local responses to disasters. In the framework of the National Civil Protection Service (Legislative Decree No 1 of 2 January 2018, ‘Civil Protection Code’29), DPC is responsible for a wide array of sectors pertaining to civil protection, ranging from prevention, forecast and assessment, early warning and alert systems to emergency response and recovery from emergency. In this code, the mentioned risks are seismic, volcanic, tidal, hydraulic, hydrogeological, adverse weather events, water deficit and forest fires (Article 16 (1)). In addition, there are other risks for which civil protection action can be carried out, without prejudice to the competences of the entities normally responsible and the resulting activities. They are chemical, nuclear, radiological, technological, industrial, transport, environmental and sanitation risks and uncontrolled re-entry of space objects and debris (Article 16 (2))29.

The European Copernicus Programme, plays a crucial and increasingly vital role in supporting the DPC across all phases of disaster management, by providing services in six thematic areas, including emergency15, with a vast amount of free and open data (Fig. 1). Since 2012, a total of 120 activations of the Copernicus Emergency Management Service (CEMS) have been recorded in Italy. These activations cover a wide range of hazard types, with floods representing the most frequent events, followed by wildfires and, to a lesser extent, earthquakes, mass movements, storms, and volcanic activity. The spatial distribution of activations is heterogeneous, with regions such as Emilia-Romagna (17 activations), Sardegna (12), and Sicilia (10) showing the highest number of requests, reflecting both exposure and hazard-specific vulnerability. Conversely, northern regions generally exhibit lower activation numbers, often associated with more localised or hazard-specific events. The temporal trend indicates a progressive increase in the use of CEMS, particularly in the emergency response phase, while preparedness and recovery activations remain comparatively limited (Fig. 1).

However, the diverse range of EO products, from various sensors and processing chains and Copernicus services, is designed for different purposes and scales of analysis. This makes it difficult for Civil Protection authorities, such as DPC (especially at regional and local levels), to seamlessly combine, compare, and integrate data from various sources to provide a comprehensive overview.

Furthermore, the Council of the European Union (European Council 8343/25) has recently emphasised the importance of EO and other space-based data for resilience and crisis preparedness. It highlights the need for increased collaboration and the use of all available satellite data, whether public, private, or commercial, to improve responses to climate change, disasters, and crises affecting both European and global security30. Indeed, recognizing a swifter response to crisis situations is achieved if near real-time access to satellite data, including high-resolution satellite data, is coordinated and calibrated to specific needs. The coordinated use of satellite data while satellite data infrastructure is being developed should ensure that it is integrated into or interoperable with existing systems. This is recognised as a critical issue in reducing the gaps in information about the Earth. A future Earth Observation Governmental Service (EOGS) is under development to be made available to EU Member States and the EU in the next EU multi-annual financial framework (2028–2034)31.

This paper aims to analyse the need of the Civil Protection Authority and the current European Copernicus offer to (1) address the future missions and services improvements, (2) identify the unexploited uptake of EO data, and 3) determine gaps of information that will support the Italian IRIDE EO constellation development based on users’ demand32. For the first time, a dedicated database of DPC needs and technical requirements has been created. The work innovates by providing insights into potential products that address these needs and identifying possible solutions to enhance current offerings, thereby placing user requirements at the core of designing new products and improving the existing Copernicus services. Overall, the proposed approach could be extended and applied to other European countries.

Methodology

This section describes the proposed methodology to evaluate the current potential and future challenges in the use of Copernicus EO data to support the disaster cycle activities of the DPC. This analysis aims to identify both the potential and any gaps between DPC needs and the currently available data, to enhance the effectiveness of risk management operations. Specifically, the proposed methodology consists of three phases (Fig. 2): (1) identification of needs and technical requirements for products relevant to the activities of the DPC; (2) assessment of the potential of Copernicus services and products. (3) gap analysis between Copernicus products and the technical requirements defined by the DPC.

Flowchart of the methodology. Figure created using GIMP 2 (https://www.gimp.org/).

The first phase aims to develop a database of needs and technical requirements for the products requested by the DPC for the various thematic areas. The starting point of the database was the result of the “National Plan for the development of Earth Observation Capabilities” (PNOT, 2020) as described in detail in Taramelli et al., 20207, which was prepared in 2020 by the Italian Earth Observation Working Group, established by the Interministerial Committee for Space and Aerospace Research Policies (COMINT). The objective of PNOT (2020) was to identify the list of priorities for national programs to be developed in the Space Economy sector. PNOT (2020) describes the results of the analysis conducted by the Earth Observation working group regarding existing national and European missions and systems for upstream and midstream activities, also considering the work carried out in the Space Economy Mirror Copernicus, concerning the needs expressed by institutional users (Buyers group) for the development of national operational downstream services. PNOT (2020) provides a knowledge framework of national priorities in the Space Economy, highlighting the needs expressed by the DPC and the existing capabilities within national and European Earth observation programs. It, therefore, gathers most of the needs expressed by the DPC and was used as a starting point to develop the database. In the context of this work, the database has been created by extracting the needs and technical requirements of products relevant only to DPC activities, updating the information available in PNOT 2020 to 2025 and integrating thematic areas (e.g., nuclear risk) that were not previously considered.

For each requirement, a unique identification code has been introduced, consisting of two letters representing the initials of the considered risk, one letter representing the initial of the disaster management phase (i.e., “P”, “E” and “R” stand for “preparedness and prevention” phase, “emergency response” phase, “recovery” phase, respectively), and a progressive number (e.g., the first need related to seismic risk and pertaining to prevention activities is assigned the identification code SRP1). Regarding the technical requirements, the information was collected using the structure previously proposed in the context of PNOT 2020 and is organised into four sections: (1) product user requirements (e.g., requirement description, and object to be observed), (2) SAR sensor, (3) optical sensor, (4) in situ sensor7,8,32. Example data are provided in the supplementary material (Supplementary Table S1). The second phase of the research focuses on analysing Copernicus services and products that can potentially meet DPC needs, without considering the technical requirements, which are addressed in the final phase of the research.

Unique identification codes have been introduced for Copernicus products (https://cis2.eea.europa.eu/product/list/) as indicated below: “initials of the Copernicus service providing the product” “_” “progressive number”. For example, for the first product provided by the CAMS service, the identification code will be “CAMS_1”. Potential Copernicus products useful for the needs of the DPC have been identified. The relationships between needs and Copernicus products are represented through Sankey diagrams33, highlighting the connection between the phases of the DPC activities, needs, services, and Copernicus products at this stage of the analysis. These diagrams allow for rapid identification of needs that are not met by any Copernicus product and that may require the development of new products.

The Sankey diagrams obtained in the second phase of the research do not take into account the match between the technical requirements expressed by the DPC and the technical characteristics of the available Copernicus products. Thus, it is necessary to compare the technical characteristics to understand whether the technical requirement of the DPC is fully, partially, or not satisfied.

The third phase of the work aims to analyse the technical requirements of the products requested and already provided by the DPC. The result of the gap analysis is reported through a data matrix that classifies the needs into three categories listed below: (1) Fully satisfied technical requirements; Copernicus products have the same technical characteristics required by the DPC. (2) Partially satisfied technical requirements; Copernicus products are available, but they do not have the same technical characteristics as requested by the DPC. (3) Unsatisfied technical requirements; Copernicus products to meet the DPC’s needs are not available. If no available Copernicus products fully meet the technical requirements, new products currently under development may be considered as potential future solutions32.

Results

User needs and technical requirements of products across all phases of the disaster management cycle

In the context of disaster risk management, clearly defining the objects to be observed is fundamental for the effective design and implementation of monitoring systems and operational requirements. These objects correspond to the phenomena, features, or parameters that must be tracked to assess hazard occurrence and impact. They include both natural processes such as ground displacement, flood extent, lava flows, pyroclastic deposits, volcanic ash accumulation, wildfire spread, and extreme weather impacts and anthropogenic elements, including buildings and infrastructure.

For seismic events, earthquakes with magnitudes exceeding 5.5 can produce strong ground shaking and trigger secondary processes that damage structures and infrastructure. Accurate mapping of affected areas is essential, distinguishing zones with visible damage from those appearing intact and assessing the severity of impacts at multiple scales, from regional surveys at moderate resolution to detailed building-level inspections. This includes identifying collapsed structures, debris distribution, and secondary hazards such as landslides or soil liquefaction34.

Floods and coastal inundations require mapping of inundated areas and their secondary impacts on buildings and infrastructure. Hydrometric measurements, flow dynamics, and remote sensing data are critical for defining the extent, severity, and temporal evolution of flooding, enabling both emergency response and post-event recovery.

Volcanic hazards including lava flows, pyroclastic flows, ash fall, and volcano-tectonic earthquakes can severely affect structures and infrastructures. Spatially detailed monitoring is necessary to capture both the direct impacts of eruptive phenomena and secondary effects, such as remobilization of volcanic ash by rainfall or slope instability, supporting timely risk assessment and mitigation planning.

Wildfires propagate rapidly across vegetated and human-modified areas, requiring continuous observation to delineate the affected zones, assess the severity of damage, and estimate potential risks to infrastructure and population.

Extreme weather events such as intense thunderstorms, tornadoes, windstorms, snowstorms, and freezing rain can cause damage patterns that are either highly localised or span extensive regions. Freezing rain is particularly hazardous due to ice accretion on electrical lines, roads, and other infrastructure, which can lead to severe disruptions. Overall, the systematic mapping and characterization of affected areas, combined with quantitative assessment of damage, is fundamental for supporting emergency response, informing risk mitigation strategies, and guiding post-event recovery actions.

Starting from this framework, the ten DPC thematic areas of risk were analysed to identify 67 operational needs across the three phases of the DPC emergency management cycle: Preparedness and Prevention, Emergency, and Recovery. For each need, a unique identification code was created (Supplementary Table S1). Figure 3 presents the distribution of needs across thematic areas and emergency phases. Meteorological and hydrological risk accounts for the largest share of needs (22%, 15 needs), followed by seismic risk (18%, 12 needs). Volcanic, environmental, and tsunami risks each account for 10% of the total, while forest fire, industrial and nuclear, healthcare, and dam risks contribute less than 10% each.

Polar chart of the number of DPC needs for the thematic areas subdivided for the phase of the emergency management cycle. Figure created using AnyChart (https://www.anychart.com/products/anychart/gallery/Polar_Charts/).

The distribution of needs across the emergency management cycle highlights the varying priorities of different hazard types. Meteorological and hydrological risk shows the highest number of needs for preparedness and prevention, reflecting the importance of proactive monitoring and mitigation. All thematic areas include pre-emergency needs, except industrial risk, which focuses solely on the emergency phase. In the post-emergency phase, seismic risk exhibits the greatest number of needs, whereas healthcare and dam risks are primarily concerned with preparedness and prevention.

The technical requirements for Copernicus products vary according to the type of risk and the phase of the emergency management cycle. For seismic risk, approximately 50% of the identified needs require high spatial resolution data (< 1 m) to support detailed mapping of damage, debris volumes, and hazardous materials. Medium-resolution products (≈ 3 m) are sufficient for pre- and post-event monitoring. Temporal requirements differ: most needs call for a revisit time of ≤ 2 days to enable rapid post-event response, while deformation monitoring can rely on a 16-day revisit. Data delivery is divided between systematic approaches, used for continuous pre-event monitoring, and on-demand services for rapid post-event assessment (Table 1).

For meteorological and hydrological risk, 30% of requirements demand high-resolution products (≤ 1 m), mainly for delineating flooded areas, mapping damage, and tracking extreme weather events. The remaining 70% can be satisfied with medium-resolution products ranging from 3 m to 1 km, suitable for monitoring coastal and ground movements. Temporal resolution also varies half of the requirements necessitate a revisit time of ≤ 2 days for rapid response, except for fog and coastal monitoring, which require observations every 5–15 min.

Forest fire risk monitoring relies primarily on 10 m spatial resolution products for land cover, forest areas, and vegetation changes. Fire emissions, however, can be adequately monitored at coarser resolutions of 4 km.

For volcanic risk, ground movement monitoring requires 3 m resolution with a 16-day revisit cycle, whereas transport of volcanic materials demands coarser resolutions: 5–7 km for aerosols and 15 m for ash dispersion models, with hourly updates. Detailed damage mapping requires 1–3 m resolution and a revisit time ≤ 2 days. Some volcanic gas measurements, such as CO₂ and SO₂, still lack fully defined technical requirements.

In tsunami risk, monitoring infrastructure and coastal morphology relies on high-resolution Digital Terrain Models (DTM) (1–3 m) with a frequency of 180 days for DTMs and < 2 days for post-event updates. Coastal vulnerability requires < 5 m resolution with a 10-minute frequency, while wave motion monitoring can be performed at 250 m resolution with hourly updates. Systematic data delivery is preferred, with rapid damage mapping requiring both systematic pre-event and on-demand post-event products.

For healthcare risk, air quality monitoring requires 4–7 km resolution with hourly frequency on an on-demand national scale. Monitoring urban heat islands, focusing on land cover and temperature, needs 300 m resolution with daily, weekly, and monthly systematic observations at the national level.

Industrial risk needs cover fire, toxic cloud, pollutant, and damage monitoring. Fire monitoring requires 10 m resolution products at the local scale for systematic land cover assessment. Toxic cloud monitoring relies on 4–7 km resolution aerosol products with hourly frequency on a national scale.

Finally, environmental risk requires a combination of high- and medium-resolution products. High-resolution (< 1 m) systematic products are needed for annual soil degradation monitoring, while hourly on-demand products support air quality assessments. Other environmental variables generally require 5–10 m resolution daily systematic products, except for groundwater recharge estimation and greenhouse gas monitoring, which rely on 1 km resolution with daily and hourly systematic delivery, respectively.

The technical specifications of these sensors, such as spatial and temporal resolution and delivery modality, have been compared with the capabilities of the sensors needed to generate Copernicus products. Potential of the European Copernicus programme for civil protection needs

Having established a comprehensive understanding of user needs and their technical requirements, we investigated the potential Copernicus services and products to address the demand of DPC. As a single user need can be associated with more than one Copernicus service, the service-level analysis resulted in 92 service–user need associations. The results indicate that CEMS accounts for the largest share (37 associations, 40%), followed by CLMS (24, 26%) (Fig. 5a). CEMS provides the largest overall contribution, mainly related to meteorological and hydrological risks, and also accounts for the highest number of products addressing seismic, tsunami, forest fire, and industrial risks.

CEMS products accounting for the largest share (69 products), followed by CLMS (34), C3S (11), CAMS (10), CMEMS (3) and CSS (3) (Fig. 5b). No Copernicus products are currently available to address the user needs related to earthquake damage mapping and volume estimation (SRE7), including rubble volume estimation (SRE8) and toxic waste volume estimation (SRE9) (Fig. 5c).

For meteo-hydrological risk, the identified needs are supported by a combination of CEMS, CLMS, and C3S products, which enable risk assessment, coastal monitoring, heatwave analysis, ground deformation monitoring (e.g., subsidence and landslides), as well as snow, ice, and fog mapping.

For volcanic risk, Copernicus products support damage mapping through CEMS, ground deformation monitoring through CLMS, and volcanic gas and ash monitoring through CAMS.

For tsunami risk, the identified needs can be addressed using CMEMS products for ocean parameters (e.g., wave height), along with CEMS and CLMS products for flood mapping and coastal and infrastructure characterization.

For forest fire risk, CEMS provides the main support for mapping burned areas and assessing damage, while CLMS contributes with land cover and vegetation information, and CAMS supports emission monitoring and fire evolution analysis. Key products include “EFFIS Climate Forecasts” and land cover datasets such as “Burnt Areas” and “Tree Cover Density.”

For healthcare risk, Copernicus services contribute through CAMS for air quality monitoring, CLMS for land use and land cover information, and C3S for meteorological variables relevant to heat stress and environmental conditions.

For environmental risk, the needs are addressed through a combination of CEMS (damage assessment), CLMS (land use and pollution sources), C3S (meteorological variables), and CAMS (air quality and greenhouse gas monitoring), with additional contributions from CSS for specific applications.

For industrial risk, Copernicus products support the identification and monitoring of fires, toxic releases, and damage, mainly through CEMS for affected area mapping and CAMS for emission assessment.

For nuclear risk, Copernicus services support the assessment of population exposure and the monitoring of toxic clouds through CEMS and CAMS, although gaps remain in continuous monitoring of surface radioactivity.

Finally, for large dam risk, Copernicus products contribute to infrastructure monitoring and stability assessment through CLMS and CSS.

For seismic risk, the user needs are identified as follows: SRP1 seismic risk assessment; SRP2 monitoring of seismic areas during the interseismic phase; SRP3 high-resolution radar monitoring of strategic buildings and infrastructure; SRE4 pre-event situation; SRE5 damage mapping and identification of unaffected areas; SRE6 assessment of damage extent and severity; SRE7 earthquake damage mapping and volume estimation; SRE8 estimation of rubble volume; SRE9 estimation of toxic waste volume; SRE10 real-time monitoring of landslides and subsidence; SRE11 detailed post-event ground motion mapping; SRR12 damage assessment and reconstruction monitoring. In the context of seismic risk activities, no Copernicus product currently satisfies the requirement SRP1 for vulnerability and exposure mapping at the Italian national scale, and no technical specifications are available to fully assess this gap. European Ground Motion Service (EGMS) products meet the spatial resolution needs of SRP2 and SRP3 (i.e., monitoring seismic areas and strategic buildings/infrastructure in the interseismic phase) but fail to meet the required temporal frequency, as they are only updated annually32,35. Emergency phase support products, such as the Emergency Reference Map, fully satisfy requirement SRE4. SRE5 and SRE6, concerning delineation of affected areas and damage grading, are also covered by CEMS rapid mapping services with requested spatial and temporal resolutions. Significant unmet needs persist for SRE7, SRE8, and SRE9, which involve the estimation of damaged buildings, rubble, and toxic waste volumes. No Copernicus products currently meet these demands. Requirements SRE10 and SRE11, regarding post-seismic deformation monitoring and secondary effects, are only partially addressed by CEMS ground deformation analysis. The COSMO-SkyMed-based MapItaly program36,37, managed by ASI and e-GEOS in collaboration with DPC, may offer partial coverage of SRP2, SRP3, SRE10 and SRE11 thanks to its X-band SAR data acquisition, but some considerations need to be done for mountain areas in the Italian peninsula38. MapItaly allows to access a 16-day interferometric mapping service and archive regularly and intensively with specific interferometric historical data38, which has been done mainly with imagery in StripMode39,40.

Regarding the meteo-hydrological risks requirements, in the case of landslide mapping rapid kinematic phenomena, an integrated remote and in situ monitoring system is required41, and ad-hoc Copernicus products on the speed and direction of the landslide phenomenon are not available. Regarding risk assessment in the pre-event phase, some products from CLMS and CEMS were identified, but it was not possible to determine whether they meet the requirements due to a lack of technical specifications from DPC. The same applies to the monitoring of snow and ice, avalanches, and flood alerts. As for coastal characterization and monitoring, the identified CLMS and CEMS products do not meet the required spatial resolution and service frequency. The requirements for forecasting and monitoring drought events and heatwaves are only partially met, as some products offer sufficient service frequency but fall short in terms of the expected spatial resolution. Similarly, EGMS products only partially meet the requirements for monitoring landslides and subsidence. Regarding flood extent delineation, damage assessment, and rapid mapping during extreme weather events, several technical limitations currently prevent full compliance with user requirements. The CEMS Rapid Mapping flood delineation service was originally designed to provide fast, situational overviews during emergencies. However, its reliance on SAR-based flood monitoring imposes constraints in urban and densely vegetated areas, making highly precise flood depth estimates unrealistic. Recent advancements have addressed these limitations by integrating new algorithms with hydraulic models. This combined approach allows for the simulation of flood dynamics, producing physically realistic representations of flooded surfaces. It preserves the rapid mapping capability essential for emergency response while improving the accuracy of water distribution and generating flood depth estimates that better reflect the terrain’s true characteristics42.

In the case of forest fire risk, it is addressed by a wide range of Copernicus services, and most user technical requirements are at least partially satisfied (see Supplementary Table S1). As expected, CEMS provides comprehensive coverage across the entire cycle, while CLMS mainly contributes to the “Preparedness and Prevention” phase, and CAMS to the “Emergency” phase.

Key products for the “Preparedness and Prevention” phase include the EFFIS Monthly/Seasonal Forecast, the Fire Danger Forecast, and land cover and vegetation products such as Burnt Area and Tree Cover Density. These products are relevant for identifying areas and periods at risk of wildfires. For monitoring fire emissions, several CAMS products are available and partially address the need for “Monitoring fire evolution”, alongside CEMS service. In addition, the CAMS Global Fire product, named Global Fire Assimilation System (GFAS) plays a key role during the emergency phase by estimating biomass burning emissions based on fire radiative power. The EFFIS Active Fire Detection product is particularly useful for the emergency response. The “Recovery” phase focuses on a single user need (i.e., assessment of fire damage), which is addressed exclusively by CEMS products. Overall, all wildfire-related needs are covered by at least one product; however, some limitations remain in terms of technical specifications, particularly spatial resolution and update frequency. For example, national forests mapping and change detection require updates every 30 days, whereas CLMS products are typically updated annually. While CEMS products generally meet update frequency requirements, they do not always achieve the desired spatial resolution. For instance, EFFIS Active Fire Detection provides data at 375 m resolution, compared to the required 10 m. Similarly, CAMS emission products have a spatial resolution of 0.1 degrees (~ 10 km), which is coarser than the 4 km resolution required for estimating fire emissions. Although all requirements related to forest fire risk are at least partially fulfilled, a single product is often insufficient to meet al.l user needs. Instead, combining multiple datasets can help bridge these gaps. For example, national forest mapping and change assessment require 10 m resolution and 30-day updates. CLMS High Resolution Layers provide vegetation data at 10 m resolution (e.g., Tree Cover Density, Forest Type, Dominant Leaf Type) but are updated every three years. Land cover products are available at 10 m (e.g., Corine Land Cover Plus Backbone) or 100 m (e.g., Dynamic Land Cover, Corine Land Cover) with annual or multi-year updates. Additional alternatives, such as the IRIDE Land Cover & Use service, are under consideration43. Some products offer more frequent updates. For instance, Fraction of Vegetation Cover is available at 300 m resolution with 10-day estimates and near-real-time products delivered five days after processing. The Burnt Area product is also provided at 300 m resolution with daily estimates and next-day availability. While this diversity of datasets allows partial fulfilment of user requirements, full compliance often requires integrating multiple sources to achieve the necessary spatial and temporal detail. The need for monitoring fire evolution is not fully satisfied, as no specific product provides systematic data at 10 m resolution with a 7–30-day update frequency. Products such as Global Fire Emissions, EFFIS Rapid Damage Assessment, Burnt Areas, and Fire Danger Forecast are available at coarser resolutions than required. However, CEMS wildfire delineation and grading products are generated on demand, with a latency of 5–15 days and a mapping scale of 1:25,000. In contrast, technical requirements for damage assessment are largely met. This phase relies on on-demand CEMS products, including wildfire delineation and grading, post-disaster landslide risk assessment, and soil erosion risk assessment, typically delivered within five days. The Fire Severity product is also relevant, offering 10 m resolution updates every seven days. However, it is not directly downloadable, except as an annual summary44.

Regarding volcanic risk, only one user need falls under the “Preparedness and Prevention” phase, and it is associated with CLMS and CEMS products for ground motion monitoring (see Supplementary Table S1). The remaining needs fall under the “Emergency Management” phase and are linked to CEMS and CLMS products for damage mapping and ground motion monitoring, as well as to CAMS products for monitoring gases and ash emitted during eruptions. Although Copernicus products have been identified for each user need, CAMS products do not meet the required specifications. For the need related to estimating the concentration of volcanic gases in the plume, it was not possible to assess compliance because of missing technical details. Regarding ground movements monitoring before and after eruptions, the EGMS currently provides deformation products updated annually with a maximum resolution of 5 m × 20 m, which only partially meets the requirements. The CEMS Ground Deformation Analysis product partially meets the requirements for ground movement monitoring during the emergency phase, as it is provided on demand but with a latency slightly higher than required. As for air quality monitoring and volcanic ash concentration, the technical requirements are not met by the available Copernicus services. In contrast, CEMS products for delineating affected areas and assessing damage fully meet the requirements.

Regarding tsunami risk, most user needs fall under the “Preparedness and Prevention” phase and are associated with CMEMS products for wave height monitoring, as well as with CEMS and CLMS products for flood mapping, and for the characterization of coastlines and coastal infrastructure (see Supplementary Table S1). The remaining needs fall within the “Emergency Management” and “Recovery” phases and are all linked to CEMS products for damage mapping and assessment. Concerning the requirements related to flood maps and damage mapping during and after the event, it was not possible to assess whether the identified products meet the requirements due to a lack of information on service frequency and spatial resolution. For requirements concerning wave height monitoring and coastal vulnerability, no systematic products with adequate temporal frequency and spatial resolution were identified. However, requirements for emergency-phase damage mapping and assessment are fully met by CEMS, while those concerning coastal characterization are only partially met, as they are not systematic but produced on demand.

In the case of healthcare risk, requirements for air quality assessment related to manure spills (i.e., particulate matter concentration), soil moisture monitoring, and urban heat islands monitoring fall under the “Preparedness and Prevention” phase (see Supplementary Table S1). In all cases, user needs are covered by CAMS products (air quality), CLMS products (land use and land cover), and C3S products (meteorological variables). In some cases, a single product may not be sufficient to fully meet user requirements, but combining multiple products can help address these limitations. Specifically, CAMS products can support air quality monitoring for pollutants including NO₂, O₃, SO₂, NH₃, PM10, and PM2.545. These products offer a minimum spatial resolution of 0.1° (~ 10 km), compared to the required resolution of 4–7 km, and provide update frequencies ranging from daily to monthly at the European scale, thus meeting temporal requirements. C3S products can also support the prevention phase of health risks by providing specific information on variables such as volumetric soil moisture, which influences emission levels, and temperature for the heat islands monitoring46. However, for the latter, the spatial resolution of the main product (i.e., Heat and cold spells in Europe derived from climate projections) does not fully meet the required 300 m resolution.

Regarding environmental risk, all needs within the “Preparedness and Prevention” phase are covered by Copernicus services (see Supplementary Table S1). These are mainly associated with CEMS products for damage assessment, CLMS products for land use and land cover information and the identification of sources of environmental pollution, C3S for monitoring meteorological variables, and CAMS for air quality products (i.e., the presence of pollutants and greenhouse gases in the atmosphere). The “Emergency Management” phase is covered by CEMS and CSS products. As with other thematic areas, a sole product may not be sufficient to fully meet user needs, but combining multiple products can help address these gaps. For groundwater recharge estimation, CEMS products, particularly the European Drought Observatory (EDO), can support monitoring. These products provide information on soil moisture status, abnormal drought conditions, and extreme events, with update frequencies as high as daily, thus meeting the required temporal resolution. Additionally, the C3S product “Hydrological Climate Variables – Precipitation” provides data on a monthly basis. To assess aquifer vulnerability to anthropogenic and agricultural pressures, a temporal resolution of 1 year and a spatial resolution of 10 m are required. CLMS products such as CORINE Land Cover and Dynamic Land Cover, land cover classification data can help identify more vulnerable areas. However, they offer a 100-meter spatial resolution with updates every 6 years, which does not fully meet these requirements. C3S products providing temperature information and hydrological change data do not meet the required resolutions either but can complement land cover data in vulnerability assessments. For air quality assessments in past (reanalysis), programmatic (plan scenarios), and/or future scenarios (climate change), and for monitoring greenhouse gases and other Essential Climate Variables (ECVs), an update frequency of 1 h and spatial resolutions of 4–7 km and 1 km respectively are required. CAMS products such as “European air quality forecast and reanalysis” provide data on emissions of greenhouse gases and atmospheric pollutants such as NO₂, O₃, SO₂, NH₃, PM10, and PM2.5, with a spatial resolution of up to 10 km and daily updates. CLMS can also assist in soil moisture monitoring by providing information on land cover class and vegetation fraction, both of which influence emission levels. Lastly, C3S provides reanalysis and projection products for meteorological variables, including volumetric soil moisture. For monitoring soil degradation, the spatial resolution provided by CLMS products is lower than the required < 1 m. For monitoring oil spills and identifying pollutants, as well as risks of environmental crimes and incidents (illegal dumping, discharges on land, at sea, and in rivers), the technical requirements are met by current product offerings. The most relevant products are from CEMS (“Risk and Recovery Mapping” component) for damage and impact assessment and CSS for environmental assessment, maritime surveillance, and protection of critical infrastructure. These products can be updated on request, especially during emergencies, with a latency of 5–15 days (CEMS) and less than 1 h (CSS).

Regarding nuclear risk, of the four identified user needs, three fall under the “Emergency Management” phase and are associated with CEMS and CAMS products, respectively, for assessing population exposure to radiation and for identifying and monitoring of toxic clouds (see Supplementary Table S1). For the only need classified under the “Preparedness and Prevention” phase, there are currently no Copernicus products available for permanent monitoring of surface radioactivity levels. Although the DPC did not define specific technical requirements for nuclear risk-related needs, some Copernicus services were identified that could potentially be applied in this context. The assessment of population exposure to radiation could be supported by the CEMS Global Human Settlement Layer and Impact assessment/exposure analysis on assets and population products. The identification of toxic clouds resulting from nuclear disasters could be addressed by CAMS products related to atmospheric forecasts and reanalysis.

As for industrial risk, user need include the identification and monitoring of industrial fires, the release of toxic substances and pollutants, and the assessment of damage resulting from industrial accidents (see Supplementary Table S1). All these needs fall under the “Emergency Management” phase and are covered by Copernicus services. Specifically, most are covered by CEMS products for identifying affected areas and assessing damage, and by CAMS for evaluating emissions following incidents. Although no specific update frequency is provided for this need, CEMS products meet the required spatial resolution of 10 m. The CAMS product on global biomass burning emissions, based on fire radiative power, can support the monitoring of toxic substance releases and pollutant identification in the atmosphere. It offers an update frequency ranging from daily to weekly, and a spatial resolution of 0.1° (~ 10 km), which does not meet the required 4–7 km resolution. For identifying pollutants in various environmental matrices and evaluating industrial accident damage, several EO products can be integrated for monitoring purposes. Although no technical requirements are defined for these needs, CAMS, CEMS, CLMS, and CSS services are considered relevant for monitoring.

Concerning the large dam risk, all needs fall within the “Preparedness and Prevention” phase and are associated with CLMS and CSS products, respectively, for periodic dam stability assessments and infrastructure condition analysis (see Supplementary Table S1). As with nuclear risk, no specific technical requirements were provided by the DPC. In Italy, for public safety purposes, large dams fall under the direct supervision of the State, through the General Directorate for Dams and Water Infrastructure (DG Dighe) of the Ministry of Infrastructure and Transport (MIT). Nevertheless, some Copernicus products were identified as relevant. Periodic evaluation of dam stability, from a hydrogeological-hydraulic and seismic point of view, could be supported by CLMS products, and infrastructure condition analysis could be addressed by CSS products.

The results of the gap analysis are summarised in the supplementary material (see Supplementary Table S1) for all thematic areas, with a focus on the technical requirements (spatial and temporal resolution) specified by the DPC for the required products and sensors (i.e., optical, SAR and in situ), and those provided by the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem.

Gap analysis of sensor requirements for civil protection applications

Regarding the technical requirements of optical, SAR, and in situ sensors required to meet DPC needs, the results of the analysis show that most require systematic delivery and distribution across different spatial and temporal resolutions. For seismic risk, all variables analysed require high spatial (< 10 m) and temporal (daily or weekly) resolution with on-demand delivery during the event. Regarding hydro-meteorological risk monitoring, systematic and on-demand delivery with daily revisit time from high-resolution (< 1 m, 1–10 m) optical and SAR sensors is required in most cases. For the remaining needs, a weekly revisit time with medium resolution (10–300 m) from SAR sensors is required. For volcanic risk, almost all requirements call for high spatial resolution (< 10 m) and a daily revisit time, except for emissions monitoring, which requires low resolution with an hourly revisit time (> 1 km). For forest fire risk, only one requirement calls for a spatial resolution of less than 1 m from optical and SAR sensors, while the other requirements highlight the need for daily optical data and weekly to monthly SAR data at a slightly lower resolution (1–10 m). For environmental risk, the analysis shows the need for optical and SAR data with high spatial resolution (< 1 m, 1–10 m) and monthly optical and in-situ data with a spatial resolution of 1–10 m. For health risk, the only requirement highlighted by the analysis is the need for weekly optical data at high resolution (< 1 m). For industrial risk, the need for weekly optical data at a resolution of 1–10 m and hourly optical data at low resolution (> 1 km) is highlighted. In contrast, for nuclear and dam risk monitoring, the technical requirements for optical, SAR, and in situ data are not established.

Discussion

In the literature, there has been extensive use of Earth observation in the context of disaster management47–49. For over a decade, Copernicus services and data have proven to be reliable support for all phases of the emergency management cycle: preparedness, prevention, disaster risk reduction, emergency response, and recovery, especially with a dedicated service named CEMS50,51. The exploitation of the Copernicus data and products is evident in a wide range of risks, such as earthquakes3, floods52, sea level rise53, volcanic deformation54, land subsidence55, landslides56, forest fires52, and Natech events57. In addition to the Sentinel missions, which represent the backbone of Copernicus services, providing systematic, global, and long-term observations, Copernicus Contributing Missions (CCMs) offer complementary, on-demand data, with very high-resolution optical and radar imagery58. These third-party missions, operated by national space agencies, commercial satellite providers, and international organizations, play a key role in addressing specific observational requirements that cannot be met by the Sentinel missions alone.

This system, combining systematic coverage and on-demand access, is crucial for ensuring timely and tailored responses in disaster management contexts and also sets the foundation for emerging services development such as EOGS.

In Italy, CEMS rapid mapping was activated for the earthquake in Northern Italy, Emilia-Romagna Region, on 20 May 2012 and 2016, when an earthquake occurred in the centre of Italy involving a large territory including several Regions (i.e., Lazio, Abruzzo, Umbria). In these cases, the DPC requested support to retrieve damage information on the affected areas. In 2016, the CEMS damage grading maps included five classes (i.e., destroyed, highly damaged, moderately damaged, negligible to slight damage, no visible damage) according to the European Macroseismic Scale (EMS-98)59. The results of this experience have highlighted the need to redefine the damage interpretation guidelines, shifting from ground-based field assessments to damage visible on vertical imagery to take into account some limitations, such as geometric resolution, radiometric resolution, and the subjectivity of the interpretation60,61.

For flood events, 40 CEMS activations were recorded for the Italian territory. The ongoing CEMS efforts focus on enhancing flood mapping in urban, forested, and coastal areas through advanced techniques like Interferometric SAR and high-resolution LiDAR data. Integration of Copernicus Global flood monitoring information into flood products is considered relevant to improve situational awareness, with rapid web-based access provided by new web viewers. Additional efforts are ongoing to incorporate dike break scenarios, flood depth into CEMS Rapid Mapping, and to streamline situational reporting, ensuring delivery within 4 hours62. Some authors have used COSMO-SkyMed (CSK) and optical and CEMS products as benchmarks to obtain continuous flood monitoring in near real-time on demand data63. Indeed, the CSK programme operates in three modes (routine, crisis, and very urgent) with increasing request frequencies and decreasing response times (up to 18 h for very urgent). Only Italian authorities can change modes or impose area vetoes. Performance metrics include reaction time, information age, response time, and revisit time, with delivery time excluded from response time36.

Regarding the risks associated with snow and ice, scientific literature presents numerous proofs of concept for applying Copernicus missions to monitor ice and snow dynamics64. This is particularly relevant in alpine environments, where snow avalanches pose a significant threat. Cryosphere monitoring is one of Copernicus’s primary application areas, with its dedicated space component comprising the Sentinel satellite constellation series, including Sentinel-1, Sentinel-2, and Sentinel-3 missions65. For alpine monitoring, specifically concerning snow avalanches, remote sensing techniques significantly enhance surveillance by providing continuous, large-scale coverage of snowpack stability and terrain characteristics66. Optical and radar imagery, such as that from Sentinel-1 (C-band SAR) and Sentinel-2 (Multispectral Imager), allow for the detection of crucial parameters like snow cover, slope, and vegetation, all of which are factors influencing avalanche risk. For instance, Sentinel-1 SAR data has been utilised to generate maps of snowmelt areas in the European Alps, demonstrating its excellent capability for operational monitoring64. The Sentinel-2 constellation also provides comprehensive coverage of the Alps within five days, offering high spatial resolution data65. Efforts are ongoing to integrate diverse remote sensing technologies, including multi-sensor data fusion from Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2, to improve avalanche detection and gain insights into the broader geological environment. These advancements support more robust avalanche forecasting and disaster management in vulnerable mountainous regions66. Furthermore, the EUMETSAT Satellite Application Facility (H-SAF), established in 2005 to support operational hydrology and water management, is crucial for obtaining reliable snow cover information through optical and microwave remote sensing. H-SAF disseminates several snow products relevant for alpine monitoring, including H10, H12, H13, H34, and H3567. The H10 and H34 products, designed for snow detection, are based on multi-channel analysis from the SEVIRI instrument aboard Meteosat satellites, leveraging snow’s high reflectivity in the visible spectrum. The H12 and H35 products, which indicate effective snow cover, utilize visible and shortwave near-infrared data from AVHRR instruments on NOAA and MetOp satellites, incorporating topographic correction for mountainous areas. For Snow Water Equivalent (SWE), the H13 product is derived from microwave sensors like SSMI/S, employing the Helsinki University of Technology (HUT) snow emission model, with adjustments for both flat/forested and mountainous regions. H-SAF employs distinct algorithms for mountainous and flat areas, which are then merged to create a unified snow product67. Continuous validation of these products is conducted using ground observations and other satellite data, with specific studies evaluating snow mapping accuracy in mountain regions68 and validating H12 using Sentinel-2 imagery69.

For wildfires, Copernicus services, particularly CEMS, offer comprehensive coverage across all phases of the emergency cycle. Copernicus products have proven effective in fire mapping, risk assessment, and wildfire management70–73, though in some cases, such as CAMS products or EFFIS climate forecasts, their spatial resolution may be insufficient for national Italian civil protection requirements. The integration of Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 data has enhanced burned area mapping and fire severity assessment74–80, enabling effective monitoring and detection of even small fires81. CLMS products, such as Tree Cover Density (TCD) and CORINE Land Cover (CLC), have been utilised to identify the most damaged vegetation types and the correlation between tree cover density and fire severity76, or to filter out non-natural areas77, thereby helping to identify vulnerable areas and support restoration planning strategies. C3S data can help assess wildfire risk due to heat stress and to forecast fire danger82. The integration of CAMS Global Fire Assimilation System (GFAS) products, which provide fire radiative power data, can contribute to fire danger forecasting, including global climatic and environmental impacts of wildfires82,83.

Products from Copernicus and Copernicus Contributing Missions are widely used for monitoring and managing volcanic risks, thanks to their capabilities to observe ground deformations, lava flows, gas emissions, and other volcanic dynamics. Sentinel-1 has been deployed in several volcanic areas to track, through Interferometric synthetic aperture radar (InSAR), mean ground deformation rates and ground displacement time series, as demonstrated by studies on Santorini84(Greece), Methana Volcano54(Greece) and Campi Flegrei85(Italy). In particular, the Campi Flegrei caldera (southern Italy) is an active volcanic system, with its last eruption occurring in 1538. The area is affected by vertical ground deformation, expressed as subsidence and uplift, commonly referred to as bradyseism. Over the past two decades, the caldera has undergone a progressive uplift phase, accompanied by increased seismicity and geochemical anomalies. Ground deformation is routinely monitored by the DPC using GNSS observations and InSAR measurements.

Sentinel-2 has been used to identify and map lava flows and volcanic deposits, supporting the identification of the most vulnerable areas and the dynamics of eruptions86. Sentinel-5P (TROPOMI), instead, has been used for the monitoring of volcanic gases (e.g., SO₂), in the context of the study of atmospheric emissions and monitoring of chemical variations in volcanic plumes87. Among CCMs, missions such as TerraSAR-X and Radarsat-2, often integrated with Sentinel, that improve the capabilities of deformation monitoring and mapping of volcanic deposits, demonstrating an effective multi-sensor approach (e.g., by including also in-situ data) to study complex phenomena88.

CLMS products can also support monitoring when integrated with CAMS products by helping identify emission sources. For example, in the case of fires caused by industrial accidents, EO products from CEMS can support the detection of active fires, burned areas, fire severity, and classification/delineation of fire perimeters89.

Relevant CLMS products include Land Cover datasets, integrated with products for detecting wetlands and vegetation indices. These have periodic update frequencies, with some data available daily or every 10 days.

Recent events highlighted the potential of EO data for oil spill disasters25,90.

Further discussion is needed on how existing Copernicus services could contribute to a comprehensive vulnerability assessment, beyond the elements of risk already characterised through exposure and hazards products. In the analysis of the entire DPC emergency management cycle, several elements emerge that could serve as a basis for developing vulnerability-oriented services. This perspective aligns with the recent European Union Space Strategies for Security and Defence 2023, and has been reaffirmed by the DG-DEFIS Commissioner (European Commission, 2025)91, which emphasizes enhancing geospatial intelligence, as highlighted in the “A Secure Europe” initiatives within the Council of the European Union. These initiatives aim to harness space data in support of autonomous European decision-making, including through situational awareness services such as EOGS and the ESA initiative European Resilience from Space (ERS).

While EO is strong for hazard and exposure mapping for all risks identified in this analysis, its full potential in informing vulnerability assessments is often underutilised or not explicitly requested as a “need”. At the European scale, emerging initiatives are beginning to address different dimensions of vulnerability, for instance, the Risk Data Hub (RDH) of the Disaster Risk Management Knowledge Centre (DRMKC) of the JRC. This platform analyses five types of vulnerability dimensions (physical, social, environmental, economic and political) across different administrative levels (Country, NUTS2 and NUTS3)92. However, the local scale remains unexplored, even if there are potential applications that could lead to significant advancements.

Built on the experience of Copernicus, Italy is developing IRIDE, a new Earth Observation program representing a novel generation of hybrid constellation, bridging national initiatives with European space governance and set to be completed by 2026. This constellation of 35 Low Earth Orbit satellites (SAR, optical, panchromatic, hyperspectral, and infrared sensors) aims to enhance Italy’s EO capabilities, offering rapid revisit times, high spatial resolution and providing valuable data for civil defence in areas like hydrogeological disruption, fires, coastal protection, and critical infrastructure monitoring93. The program consists of sub-constellations of satellites (upstream segment), ground-based operational infrastructure (downstream segment), and services specifically designed for the Italian Public Administration (service segment).

Furthermore, user needs identified in this study help to address the limitations of the current system and lay the groundwork for next-generation EO services such as EOGS aligned with ERS program objectives such as providing frequent high-resolution images94. In Italy, this program complements initiatives such as MapItaly from the Italian Space Agency (ASI), which performs interferometric investigations to analyse and monitor the evolution of potentially risky natural phenomena (landslides, subsidence, seismic or volcanic phenomena) using SAR (COSMO-Sky Med satellite). Requirements identified as partially or not satisfied in this analysis could be addressed in the near future by IRIDE’s new capabilities, once all the new satellites and services are available. For example, in the case of SAR data, monitoring north-south displacement remains a challenge, but efforts in constellation design have been undertaken to investigate this issue32,35. Cotugno et al. 202535 exploring new future potential configuration, such as 49° mid-inclination orbit in the right-looking StripMap mode for IRIDE NIMBUS X-Band SAR, give insight into an enhanced 3D deformation mapping by capturing North-South displacements, which are limited in traditional orbit configuration systems.

The work is centred on the aims introduced by the recent Italian Law No. 89 of June 13, 202595 and the future EU Space Act96 and the legislative proposal by the European Commission on the safety resilience and sustainability of space activities in the Union, that introduces a harmonised framework for space activities across Member States, promote new technologies related to EO within the scope of activities for the prediction and prevention of risks associated with natural and human-made phenomena and overcome fragmentation of national laws markets and services at European level. Meanwhile, the Commission is moving towards new investments and identifying several key areas to enhance the EU competitiveness, closing the innovation gap, dual-use technologies, decarbonisation, reducing excessive dependence on third countries, and improving security and resilience.
