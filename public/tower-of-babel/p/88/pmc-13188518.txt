The influence of the nursing work environment on organizational commitment among nurses in healthcare settings: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hamad Shajiri, Ahmed Nahari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNurses play a central role in healthcare delivery, and the conditions in which they work have important implications for organizational performance and patient care. The nursing work environment is a key determinant of nurses’ attitudes and behaviors, including organizational commitment, which is essential for workforce stability and quality of care. Therefore, this study examined the influence of the nursing work environment on organizational commitment among nurses working in healthcare facilities across Saudi Arabia.MethodsA cross-sectional quantitative study was conducted across healthcare facilities in the five regions of Saudi Arabia. A convenience sample of 149 registered nurses completed an online questionnaire between April and August 2025 using the Practice Environment Scale of the Nursing Work Index (PES-NWI) and the Three-Component Model (TCM) of Organizational Commitment questionnaire, both of which are validated instruments with established reliability and validity. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and multiple linear regression to examine the relationships between the nursing work environment and affective, continuance, and normative organizational commitment.ResultsParticipants reported a moderately favorable nursing work environment and moderate levels of organizational commitment, including affective, continuance, and normative commitment. The nursing work environment was significantly and positively associated with affective commitment (r = .436, p < .001) and showed a weaker but still statistically significant association with normative commitment (r = .249, p = .002). In contrast, no significant association was found between the work environment and continuance commitment (r = .079, p = .341). Multiple regression analysis indicated that the work environment significantly influenced affective commitment (β = 0.469, 95% CI [0.712, 1.491], p < .001, R² = 0.255) and normative commitment (β = 0.324, 95% CI [0.351, 1.198], p < .001, R² = 0.130), whereas neither the work environment nor the control variables significantly influenced continuance commitment (R² = 0.057).ConclusionsThe findings indicate that the nursing work environment plays an important role in shaping nurses’ emotional attachment and sense of obligation to their organizations. However, it was not significantly associated with continuance commitment. Improving workplace conditions may therefore strengthen key dimensions of organizational commitment and support nurse retention and organizational stability within healthcare settings, which is particularly important in the Saudi healthcare context where workforce sustainability and quality of care remain national priorities. Future longitudinal and intervention-based studies are needed to further examine causal relationships and evaluate strategies aimed at strengthening the nursing work environment and organizational commitment.

Background

Organizational commitment is a key workforce outcome for healthcare organizations because it reflects nurses’ psychological attachment to their employer and their willingness to remain as active members of the organization. It refers to the psychological attachment that employees feel toward their organization and their willingness to remain as active members. Meyer and Allen’s three-component model conceptualized organizational commitment as consisting of affective commitment (emotional attachment to the organization), continuance commitment (perceived costs associated with leaving), and normative commitment (a sense of moral obligation to remain) [1]. In practical terms, affective commitment reflects “wanting to stay,” continuance commitment reflects “needing to stay,” and normative commitment reflects “feeling obligated to stay.” High levels of organizational commitment among nurses have been linked to a number of positive outcomes including improved job performance, higher quality patient care, a stronger patient safety culture, and lower turnover intention [2–4]. Conversely, low organizational commitment is associated with disengagement, lower performance, and increased turnover intention, which can threaten workforce stability and continuity of care.

In Saudi Arabia, workforce retention remains a pressing concern. According to the Saudi Health Council [5], nursing turnover rates are estimated at approximately 20% and are higher than rates reported in other developed health systems such as the UK, Canada, Australia, Germany and Japan, which highlights ongoing retention challenges within the national healthcare system. In addition, a study conducted in a major public hospital in Saudi Arabia reported that 47.88% of nurses agreed with all organizational commitment items, suggesting opportunities to further strengthen commitment levels across healthcare settings [6]. These issues highlight the importance of identifying modifiable organizational factors that may strengthen nurses’ commitment and reduce turnover risk. Within Saudi Arabian healthcare settings, existing evidence indicates that organizational commitment is influenced by factors such as leadership styles, work engagement, job satisfaction, relational coordination, and sociodemographic characteristics, including age and nationality [7–9].

Nurses represent the largest group of healthcare professionals, and they are considered primary providers of continuance patient care. Thus, the conditions under which they work can have significant effects on both patient outcomes and organizational performance. The nursing work environment consists of physical, psychological, and social conditions that influence nurses’ daily practices including staffing levels, workload, leadership quality, professional development opportunities, interpersonal relationships, and organizational policies. International evidence consistently shows that supportive nurse work environments are associated with better nurse and organizational outcomes. A large meta-analysis found significant associations between the nurse work environment and multiple outcome domains (including nurse job outcomes and patient outcomes), highlighting the broad relevance of work environment quality across healthcare systems [10]. A supportive nursing work environment is central to high-quality healthcare delivery, contributing to nurse well-being, patient safety, and organizational performance [7, 10, 11]. On the other hand, unsupportive work environments contribute to burnout, job dissatisfaction, and increased turnover, all of which threaten the stability of healthcare systems [11–13]. These associations have also been observed internationally, where work environment characteristics and job resources have been linked to organizational attitudes (including organizational commitment) among nurses [10, 14, 15].

In this study, the nursing work environment was assessed using the Practice Environment Scale of the Nursing Work Index (PES-NWI), which captures key modifiable dimensions such as nurse participation in hospital affairs, nursing foundations for quality of care, nurse manager ability/leadership/support, staffing and resource adequacy, and collegial nurse–physician relations [16]. In Saudi Arabia, several studies have identified key challenges affecting nurses’ work environments, including inadequate staffing, heavy workloads, limited organizational support, leadership and management issues, and limited professional development opportunities [6, 12, 17–20]. These challenges not only affect nurses’ daily work practices but also can shape their psychological organizational attachment and retention decisions.

The nursing work environment is generally considered an important contextual factor in relation to nurses’ organizational commitment. Work settings characterized by effective leadership, adequate staffing, collaborative interdisciplinary relationships, participation in decision-making, and sufficient resources tend to promote positive attitudes toward the organization and strengthen nurses’ emotional attachment and loyalty [10, 14, 16]. Current evidence suggests that favorable work environments are associated with reduced turnover intention and higher levels of organizational commitment [15]. In Saudi Arabia, research examining the nursing work environment has primarily focused on nurses’ perceptions, engagement, and retention-related attitudes rather than directly assessing organizational commitment [11, 21]. Other studies have focused on job satisfaction, work-related stress, burnout, and job performance as key work-related variables linked to nurses’ attitudes and retention-related outcomes, including organizational commitment [11]. Common organizational approaches that have been used to strengthen nurses’ commitment include improving leadership support, ensuring adequate staffing and resources, supporting professional development, and building collaborative practice environments, factors that align closely with modifiable work environment dimensions.

Although existing studies have examined related aspects of nurses’ work experiences, comprehensive evidence examining how the overall nursing work environment relates to the three distinct components of organizational commitment (affective, continuance, and normative) remains limited in Saudi Arabian healthcare settings. Studies have largely focused on mediating variables or isolated organizational factors, with limited direct examination of how the work environment influences each dimension of organizational commitment. Importantly, examining affective, continuance, and normative commitment separately may help distinguish whether workplace conditions primarily strengthen nurses’ emotional attachment and sense of obligation (affective/normative) versus perceptions of the costs of leaving (continuance), which may be shaped by different mechanisms. Clarifying these relationships can support strategies to enhance nurse retention and care quality. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the influence of the nursing work environment on affective, continuance, and normative commitment among nurses working in healthcare facilities in Saudi Arabia.

Theoretical framework

This study was guided by the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model, which explains how work environment characteristics influence employee motivation, well-being, and organizational outcomes through the balance of job demands and job resources [22]. Given that the nursing work environment includes both demands (e.g., workload and staffing pressures) and resources (e.g., leadership support, participation in decision-making, and professional development opportunities), the JD-R model provides a logical explanation for why variations in the work environment may be associated with differences in organizational commitment. In this study, the nursing work environment is conceptualized as a combination of demands and resources that is expected to influence nurses’ affective, continuance, and normative organizational commitment. Sociodemographic and work-related characteristics (e.g., age, gender, education, nationality, years of experience, salary, healthcare facility type, sector, and region) were included as contextual factors that may also affect nurses’ perceptions of their work environment and organizational commitment.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a quantitative, cross-sectional correlational design. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies.

Participant and setting

The study was conducted in various healthcare facilities across different regions of Saudi Arabia. Participants were recruited using a non-probability convenience sampling technique through the online distribution of the survey. Healthcare facilities across the five main geographic regions of Saudi Arabia (Central, Western, Eastern, Northern, and Southern) were intentionally included to enhance geographic coverage and improve the national scope of the study. Within each region, participation was voluntary and based on convenience sampling. Data was collected between April and August 2025. Registered nurses working in clinical settings were invited to participate. The target population consisted of registered nurses currently employed in clinical settings in Saudi Arabia. The inclusion criteria were: (1) registered nurses currently working in clinical settings in Saudi Arabia; (2) having at least one year of clinical experience; and (3) being able to complete the questionnaire in English. Nurses who held higher administrative positions or worked in quality, education, or research departments were excluded from the study. Based on a priori power analysis conducted using G*Power (version 3.1), the estimated sample size was 130 participants to achieve a statistical power of at least 80%, assuming a type I error rate of 0.05, a medium effect size (f² = 0.15), and an anticipated 5% rate of missing data. To enhance data quality and account for potentially incomplete or invalid responses, the target sample size was increased by at least 10%. Ultimately, a total of 149 complete and eligible responses were obtained and included in the final analysis.

Measurements

The survey instrument consisted of two main sections. The first section collected sociodemographic information, including age, gender, level of education, nationality, years of experience, salary, healthcare sector, and region. The second section assessed the nursing work environment and organizational commitment among nurses in Saudi Arabia.

Measurement of work environment

The Practice Environment Scale of the Nursing Work Index (PES-NWI) was used to measure the nursing work environment. The PES-NWI was selected due to its strong theoretical alignment with nursing practice environments, widespread international validation, and robust psychometric properties. The PES-NWI is a previously published and widely validated instrument developed by Lake [16] and consists of 31 items grouped into five subscales: Nurse Participation in Hospital Affairs (9 items), Nursing Foundations for Quality of Care (10 items), Nurse Manager Ability, Leadership, and Support of Nurses (5 items), Staffing and Resource Adequacy (4 items), and Collegial Nurse–Physician Relations (3 items). Responses are measured on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 4 (“strongly agree”), with higher scores indicating a more favorable nursing work environment. Subscale scores are calculated using composite mean scores, with a midpoint of 2.5 [16]. Mean scores were interpreted relative to the midpoint of the four-point scale (2.5), with values near the midpoint considered indicative of moderate perceptions of the nursing work environment. The PES-NWI is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing the nursing practice environment, with strong construct validity demonstrated across international settings and high internal consistency, as reflected by Cronbach’s alpha values typically ranging from 0.70 to 0.90 [16, 23–25]. In the present study, the original published version of the PES-NWI was used without modification and demonstrated acceptable to excellent internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values for the subscales ranging from 0.70 to 0.90 and an overall alpha of 0.96, indicating high internal consistency.

Measurement of organizational commitment

Organizational commitment was measured using the Three-Component Model (TCM) scale, a previously published and validated instrument developed by Meyer and Allen [26]. The TCM scale was selected because it provides a multidimensional assessment of affective, continuance, and normative commitment, consistent with the study’s research questions. The scale consists of 18 items divided into three subscales: Affective Commitment (6 items), Continuance Commitment (6 items), and Normative Commitment (6 items). Responses were measured on a seven-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 7 (“strongly agree”), with higher scores indicating higher levels of organizational commitment. Subscale scores were calculated using mean values, as recommended by the original scale developers [26]. Mean scores were interpreted relative to the theoretical midpoint of the seven-point scale, with values near the midpoint considered indicative of moderate levels of organizational commitment. The TCM scale is a valid and reliable measure of organizational commitment, with strong psychometric support across healthcare and other settings and robust internal consistency for each component, indicated by Cronbach’s alpha values generally between 0.70 and 0.90 [1, 2]. In the present study, the original published version of the TCM scale was used and demonstrated acceptable to good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.71 to 0.82 across the three subscales.

The original English versions of both instruments were used. English is widely used as the professional language of communication in Saudi healthcare settings, and expatriate nurses represent the majority of the nursing workforce in the country (refer). Therefore, using the English versions of the instruments was intended to enhance inclusivity and ensure accessibility for a diverse multinational nursing population.

Ethical considerations and data analysis

This study obtained ethical approval from the participating healthcare facilities prior to data collection. Data collection was conducted through an online questionnaire. The questionnaire link was distributed to participants via official emails sent by their managers following institutional approval. Participants were provided with an online information sheet outlining the study’s purpose, voluntary participation, and the right to withdraw at any time without consequences. Informed consent was obtained prior to questionnaire completion. To ensure confidentiality and privacy, no personal identifiers were collected. All responses were anonymized, coded, and securely stored, and the data were used exclusively for research purposes. The questionnaire was administered using Qualtrics.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize continuance variables using means and standard deviations and categorical variables using frequencies and percentages. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) and point-biserial correlation coefficients were used to assess the relationships between sociodemographic characteristics, the nursing work environment, and the components of organizational commitment. Correlation analyses examined initial associations between variables, and multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to assess the independent influence of the nursing work environment on each component of organizational commitment, thereby addressing the research questions. Prior to conducting regression analyses, assumptions of normality, linearity, homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity were assessed and met. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 31 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 149 nurses participated in the study. As shown in Table 1, most participants were female (61.7%), married (74.5%), Saudi nationals (69.8%), and held undergraduate degrees (69.1%). The mean age was 35.91 years (SD = 6.40), and the average nursing experience was 11.65 years (SD = 6.47). Most participants were employed in governmental Ministry of Health facilities (94.6%).

As presented in Table 2, the overall mean score of the PES-NWI was 2.93 (SD = 0.55), with similar mean scores across all subscales. These values indicate generally favorable perceptions of the nursing work environment relative to the midpoint of the scale. Commitment levels were moderate, with mean scores of 4.38 (SD = 1.28) for affective commitment, 4.32 (SD = 1.37) for continuance commitment, and 4.36 (SD = 1.30) for normative commitment, relative to the theoretical midpoint of the seven-point scale.

Associations among sociodemographic variables, work environment, and organizational commitment

As shown in Table 3, gender was the only sociodemographic factor significantly associated with the work environment (r = .182, p = .026), with female nurses reporting more favorable perceptions. No other sociodemographic variables were significantly associated with the work environment or any component of organizational commitment (all p > .05).

The nursing work environment demonstrated a moderate positive correlation with affective commitment (r = .436, p < .001) and a weaker but statistically significant correlation with normative commitment (r = .249, p = .002). Importantly, no significant association was observed between the work environment and continuance commitment (r = .079, p = .341). Strong positive correlations were observed among the organizational commitment components, particularly between affective and normative commitment (r = .536, p < .001).

Factors influencing organizational commitment

As shown in Table 4, the multiple linear regression model significantly predicted affective commitment, explaining 25.5% of the variance (R² = 0.255, F(9, 122) = 4.629, p < .001). After controlling for sociodemographic variables, both the nursing work environment (β = 0.464, t = 5.601, p < .001, 95% CI [0.712, 1.491]) and practice sector (β = 0.179, t = 2.120, p = .036, 95% CI [0.073, 2.150]) emerged as significant positive predictors of affective commitment. The nursing work environment was the strongest predictor of affective commitment in the model. These findings indicate that a more favorable nursing work environment and working in MOH facilities, as compared to non-MOH facilities, independently and significantly contributed to higher levels of affective commitment among nurses. None of the remaining sociodemographic variables were significant predictors (all p > .05).

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the influence of the work environment on organizational commitment among nurses working in healthcare facilities across Saudi Arabia. The findings showed that participants perceived the nursing work environment as moderately favorable and reported moderate levels of organizational commitment across affective, continuance, and normative components. The nursing work environment was significantly associated with, and showed a significant influence on, affective and normative commitment; however, no significant association or influence was observed for continuance commitment. These findings extend existing evidence within the Saudi context by demonstrating that the nursing work environment differentially influences distinct dimensions of organizational commitment, reinforcing the importance of examining commitment as a multidimensional construct rather than a single outcome [1, 8, 14].

The generally favorable perceptions of the nursing practice environment found in this study suggest that nurses worked in supportive organizational and professional environments across healthcare facilities in Saudi Arabia. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting positive or moderately favorable nursing work environments in Saudi Arabia [11, 17, 21]. Several factors may help explain these favorable perceptions. A number of national initiatives under Saudi Vision 2030 have been established to strengthen the nursing workforce, including increasing specialized nurses and enhancing practice standards [18]. Moreover, increasing national emphasis on workforce development, leadership preparation, and professional training may have contributed to enhanced perceptions of organizational support and improved work environments [18, 27]. Despite the notable progress in this area, continued efforts are needed to further strengthen key aspects of the work environment, including staffing adequacy, resource availability, participation in hospital affairs, and multidisciplinary collaboration.

In line with the generally favorable perceptions of the nursing practice environment, the current study found that nurses reported moderate levels of organizational commitment across affective, continuance, and normative components. This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted in Saudi Arabia, which have reported moderate levels of organizational commitment among nurses across the affective, continuance, and normative components [6, 8, 9]. However, some international studies conducted in resource-rich healthcare systems have reported comparatively higher affective commitment levels, suggesting that contextual and organizational differences may influence the strength of emotional attachment to organizations [15, 28]. These moderate levels of commitment suggest that nurses maintain a balanced relationship with their organizations that reflects emotional attachment, perceived obligation, and consideration of the costs of leaving, rather than strong reliance on a single commitment component. Prior research indicates that nurses’ organizational commitment is influenced by their overall workplace experiences, including leadership quality, organizational support, and job satisfaction [8, 9].

Collectively, these findings suggest that moderate commitment across all components may reflect both supportive and challenging aspects of the nursing work environment. This pattern highlights opportunities for targeted organizational improvements to enhance sustained commitment and workforce stability.

The findings of this study showed that nurses who perceived a more positive work environment demonstrated stronger affective commitment toward their organizations. Moreover, the nursing work environment emerged as the strongest and most significant predictor of affective commitment, highlighting the crucial role of workplace conditions in shaping nurses’ emotional attachment to their organizations. This finding aligns with previous studies reporting significant positive relationships between work environment characteristics and affective commitment among nurses [28]. Prior research has shown that positive work environment features, such as adequate staffing, emotional support from supervisors, and effective leadership, are linked to higher affective commitment [15]. Additionally, affective commitment has been linked to organizational justice, managers’ mental health, and appreciative leadership [29, 30]. These findings confirm prior international evidence while extending it within the Saudi healthcare context, where empirical examination of affective commitment in relation to comprehensive work environment measures remains limited. Together, these findings suggest that affective commitment is particularly responsive to job resources that enhance motivation, recognition, and emotional support. From a practical perspective, interventions targeting leadership development, staffing adequacy, supportive supervision, and participatory decision-making may be particularly effective in strengthening nurses’ affective commitment.

The current study also found that a more favorable nursing work environment was associated with higher levels of normative commitment. Furthermore, the nursing work environment remained a significant factor influencing normative commitment even when other sociodemographic and work-related characteristics were considered. Although this influence was more modest compared to that observed for affective commitment, the findings highlight the role of workplace conditions in shaping nurses’ moral commitment to remain with their organizations [1]. While normative commitment has been less frequently examined in Saudi Arabia, international research suggests that leadership practices, ethical climate, and organizational values contribute to strengthening nurses’ sense of obligation and loyalty [15]. The present findings extend this literature by providing Saudi-based evidence that workplace resources contribute not only to emotional attachment but also to perceived obligation. In addition, organizational and professional norms that emphasize loyalty, responsibility, and collective commitment within Saudi healthcare settings may further reinforce normative commitment, particularly when nurses perceive their work environment as supportive [1, 3, 6, 18]. These findings suggest that organizational policies promoting fairness, recognition, ethical leadership, and team cohesion may enhance nurses’ sense of duty and long-term organizational loyalty.

In contrast to affective and normative commitment, the present study did not find a significant association or influence of the nursing work environment on continuance commitment. This finding suggests that nurses’ decisions to remain in their organizations based on perceived costs of leaving were not strongly influenced by day-to-day workplace conditions. This result is consistent with previous studies reporting non-significant or minimal relationships between work environment factors and continuance commitment among nurses [28]. In addition, continuance commitment is conceptualized as a cost-based form of attachment (i.e., staying because leaving is perceived as costly) [1, 31, 32]. These cost-related considerations are primarily influenced by structural, financial, and career-related factors that extend beyond immediate workplace conditions, which may explain the absence of a significant relationship. Accordingly, improving the nursing work environment alone may be insufficient to strengthen continuance commitment, underscoring the importance of broader workforce and compensation policies.

An additional finding was that gender was the only sociodemographic variable significantly associated with perceptions of the work environment, with female nurses reporting slightly more favorable perceptions. This finding may reflect differences in workplace experiences, expectations, or role distribution within healthcare settings. Further research is warranted to explore gender-related perceptions of organizational resources and their implications for workforce management.

The findings can be interpreted through the JD-R framework. The significant influence of the nursing work environment on affective and normative commitment suggests that job resources (such as supportive leadership, adequate staffing, participation in decision-making, and positive collegial relationships) enhance motivational and value-based forms of commitment. According to the JD-R model, job resources foster positive work attitudes and motivation, which aligns with the observed effects on affective and normative commitment [22, 33]. By distinguishing between affective, normative, and continuance commitment, this study extends the JD-R framework by demonstrating that job resources are more strongly aligned with motivational and value-based forms of commitment than with cost-based attachment. In contrast, continuance commitment is primarily driven by cost-based considerations and structural factors, which fall outside the motivational mechanisms emphasized by the JD-R model. Thus, the findings support the JD-R framework as an explanatory lens for affective and normative commitment while highlighting its more limited applicability to continuance commitment.

Overall, this study contributes to the literature by providing context-specific evidence from Saudi Arabia that distinguishes the differential effects of the nursing work environment across commitment dimensions, strengthens theoretical integration using the JD-R framework, and identifies practical organizational targets (such as leadership development, staffing adequacy, and participatory governance) to enhance workforce stability.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the nursing work environment and organizational commitment. Second, the use of a non-probability convenience sampling method may limit the generalizability of the findings and introduces the possibility of selection bias, as participation was voluntary and may have attracted nurses with particular perspectives. Although healthcare facilities from all five major geographic regions of Saudi Arabia were intentionally included to enhance national coverage, the sample was not proportionally stratified across regions. Data were collected at a single point in time, which limits the ability to capture changes in nurses’ perceptions over time. The study relied on self-reported questionnaire data, which may be subject to response and social desirability bias. Cultural and professional norms may also influence how nurses report perceptions of organizational commitment and workplace conditions. Additionally, because the questionnaire was administered in English, nurses with limited English proficiency may have been underrepresented, which could affect generalizability. Future research using longitudinal, mixed-methods, or intervention-based designs is recommended to further examine causal pathways and provide deeper contextual insight.

Conclusions

This study examined the influence of the nursing work environment on organizational commitment among nurses in Saudi Arabian healthcare facilities. The findings showed that nurses perceived their work environment as favorable and reported moderate levels of organizational commitment. A supportive work environment was significantly associated with affective commitment and, to a lesser extent, normative commitment, while no significant influence was observed on continuance commitment. These results indicate that workplace conditions play a key role in fostering nurses’ emotional attachment and sense of obligation to their organizations, whereas continuance commitment may be shaped by factors beyond the immediate work environment. Improving leadership support, staffing adequacy, and collegial relationships may strengthen nurses’ organizational commitment and contribute to workforce stability and patient care quality. Further research is needed to examine the determinants of continuance commitment and to explore longitudinal relationships between work environment and organizational commitment.
