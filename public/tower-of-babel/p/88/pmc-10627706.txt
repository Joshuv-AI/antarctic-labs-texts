Effects of music therapy on pain relief during fundus screening in infants: Randomized controlled clinical trial
Authors: Renyi Chen, Shijie Duan, Yin Wang, Fengqin He, Li Ren, Wentao Peng
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:To determine the efficacy of music therapy on pain relief during fundus screening in infants.Methods:The sample consisted of infants aged 0 to 3 months who required fundus screening. Infants were randomized to fast music, slow music, and control groups. All groups underwent fundus screening under topical anesthesia. Music therapy was provided to the music groups prior to, during, and after the operation. The patient’s heart rate (HR), transcutaneous oxygen saturation, and crying decibel were measured. The Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability scale was used for pain measurement.Results:A total of 300 subjects’ data were collected. The quantitative analysis revealed that in both music groups, peripheral capillary oxygen saturation and satisfaction levels increased while pain scores decreased (P < .05). The slow music group’s HR was shown to have significantly decreased (P < .05).Conclusion:Music therapy can effectively reduce pain and crying, and increase blood oxygen saturation during fundus examination of infants. Music with a rhythm of 60 to 80 beats per minute can decrease HR. Music therapy must be remembered to increase infants’ comfort during fundus examination.

1. Introduction

With the improvement in neonatal survival rates in recent years, the incidence of retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), which can lead to blindness, has also increased.[1] The most effective and widely used method for diagnosing ROP is fundus screening. A fundus digital camera is required for retinal imaging throughout the procedure. Preterm and low birth weight infants with birth weights of <2000 g, as well as newborns with risk factors for ocular illness, should be evaluated by an ophthalmologist as soon as feasible after birth, according to the professional consensus on neonatal fundus screening in China.[2]

According to current research, ROP examination is a painful procedure for infants, and peripheral tissue perfusion may be affected during the procedure.[3] Despite the ROP examination being a frequent source of procedural discomfort, there are no established guidelines for its application.[4] Poor pain management may cause anxiety in children and parents because pain, a distressing sensory and emotional state, has a significant impact on a child’s life.[5] Because of this, some recent research have raised concerns about the use of non-pharmacologic interventions to lessen the discomfort associated with eye exams.[6,7]

Music therapy, an easy-to-apply and widely available tool, is becoming increasingly valued by clinicians as modern nursing advances. For hundreds of years, music has been recognized for its ability to influence human behavior. Recent research has confirmed that music has a wide range of effects on emotion, pain, and anxiety.[8] Appropriate music includes a calm, rhythmic beat at 60 to 80 beats per minute for at least 15 to 30 minutes, which can significantly reduce the pain of the operation.[9] Another study discovered that combining music therapy and distraction cards had no discernible effect on reducing children’s anxiety and pain perception during phlebotomy.[10] Despite the fact that there have been few studies on whether music therapy has a pain relief effect in infant fundus screening procedures, some similar studies have confirmed the effect of music therapy on pain relief during operations such as blood sampling.[11,12]

This study aims to evaluate the effects of different types of music on pain relief during fundus examination in infants.

2. Methods/design

2.1. Ethics, consent, and permissions

The trial received a favorable opinion from the Ethics Committee of West China Second Hospital, Sichuan University and study approval from the study hospital, and was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trials Registry (ChiCTR2100051726). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents. This prospective, randomized trial was conducted in the pediatric outpatient department of the West China Second Hospital of Sichuan University in China over a period of 14 months (from October 2021 to December 2022).

2.2. Participants

Following the voluntary principle, we recruited infants with gestational age < 32 weeks or birth weight < 2000 g or with high-risk factors as participants in the ophthalmology clinic of a Chinese pediatric hospital using a convenience sampling method. Patients with hearing impairment were excluded from the study.

2.3. Randomization, and blinding

All eligible patients will be divided into 3 groups at random in a ratio of 1:1:1 to represent the number of subjects in each group. An impartial statistician who is not involved in the data analysis process will create the block randomization sequence using IBM SPSS Statistic 23 software.

Considering the impact of the earphones on the infants’ hearing, the researchers chose external music and set the music decibels. The experimenter and data collector did not know the grouping information or the music list.

2.4. Procedure

The infants remained in a quiet space. The nurse administered 3 Mydrin-P eye drops to the patient, 1 drop every 5 minutes, to enlarge their pupils. After the patient had rested for 30 minutes, the nurse administered 2 mL of oxybuprocaine obucaine hydrochloride eye drops as a surface anesthetic, which was immediately followed by an ophthalmologist utilizing a binocular indirect examination ophthalmoscope to examine the patient.

2.5. Intervention

Two music lists were produced by the authors, one of which was played to patients who were allocated to the slow music group. This music list had a pace of 60 to 80 beats per minute. Patients in the fast music group were given the other list, which contained music with a tempo of 80 to 100 beats per minute. The session, which lasted 45 to 60 minutes, was attended by the patients in the rapid music group. The music was kept at a level of 50 to 60 dB to preserve the patients’ hearing.

Since many patients receiving fundus screening in an outpatient setting are more than 28 days, we assess their level of pain using the FLACC scale rather than the Neonatal Infant Pain Scale. Several studies have shown that the FLACC scale is an effective and reliable tool for assessing pain in children aged 0 to 7 years after surgery or for other pain.[13,14] Five factors comprise the FLACC scale: Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, and Consolability. Each factor is graded on a range of 0 to 2, adding up to a final score between 0 and 10. A score of 1 to 3 denotes minimal pain, a score of 4 to 6, moderate pain, and a score of 7 to 10, severe pain.

2.7. Sample size estimation

There is no consensus in the literature regarding what might represent a clinically significant difference in FLACC scores. So we performed a preexperiment and 30 children were included. We chose to use a standard deviation of 1.2 points. Thus, using an alpha error of 0.05 and a power of 85%, it was estimated that 86 patients per group. Given a potential drop-out rate of 10%, 95 patients were needed in each group.

Finally, due to the high number of enrollments, a total of 300 patients ultimately met the inclusion criteria and participated in the study; 3 patients were excluded (voluntary withdrawal from the trial prior after randomization). The flow chart of this study is shown in Figure 1.

2.8. Statistical analysis

The Windows version of IBM SPSS Statistic 23 was used to conduct the statistical analysis. Using the LSD-t test and Kruskal–Wallis test, it was determined whether there were any differences between the 3 groups in terms of pain, SpO2, HR, and satisfaction measurements. For all analyses, the level of significance was fixed at P < .05.

3. Results

In the final 300 participants, 99 were in the control group, 100 were in the rapid music group, and 101 were in the slow music group, including 155 females (51.7% of the total) and 145 boys (48.3%). The kids’ average age was 42.69 ± 19.76 days (range, 3–90 days). the birth weight of the children was 2166.4 ± 763.46 g (range, 845–4340 g). 4. Discussion

We gathered data from a total of 300 experimenters, which is a sizable amount of data. In our study, the 2 music groups showed better SpO2, lower pain scores, and higher levels of happiness. The slow music group also showed a reduction in HR.

Neonatal facilities are increasingly using music to enhance behavioral or physiological results. Live music is said to calm down parents and babies, improve oral feeding transition time, sucking success, and vital indicators like steady physiological measurements and better oxygen saturation.[15,16] During the pre- and postoperative periods, it was advised that music therapy consist of soothing, gentle tones of 60 to 80 beats per minute (to match the advised HR) for at least 15 to 30 minutes.[15] The majority of these research focused on hospitalized neonates. Our research has shown that brief music therapy has a similar impact during outpatient exams. Fast music (60–80 BPM) and slow music (60–80 BPM) both served similar purposes in our study, however slow rhythm music is more effective in lowering HR. We therefore believe that slow music can lower patients’ HRs.

Although there are several scales available to evaluate infant pain, no scale can precisely measure the pain brought on by the procedure on the baby because pain is a subjective sensation. The median posterior sensitivity and specificity of the FLACC in a combined model with conditional dependence were 89.94%, indicating that FLACC offers outstanding sensitivity and specificity for assessing newborn discomfort.[9] Infants who did not get music therapy had FLACC pain levels of about 6 to 7 when topical anesthetics were used, whereas the infants in the 2 music groups had lower pain scores of about 4 to 6. Crying is the most common way for infants to convey their suffering, and it is a key sign of distress.[17] In this study, we only examined the crying decibels for 3 groups of infants at different times; during the examination, the crying decibels of the babies in the 2 music groups were lower than those in the control group. It is possible that crying’s decibel level doesn’t accurately represent its misery. Another drawback is that we only conducted short-term music therapy for the emerging field of repeated and long-term music therapy, which requires additional study. It is important to note that the music group had much higher satisfaction; we interpret this to imply that music therapy is simple to embrace and has many treatment adjustments.[18]

This study has several limitations. First, we used a convenience sampling method, which might have resulted in sample selection bias. Second, because of constraints, we were not strictly blind, which caused the parents of these infants to know which group they were in, which affected their satisfaction. Third, because this study was conducted in an outpatient setting without a follow-up system to track participants’ ensuing conditions, we did not undertake follow-up. We should continue to advance in these areas in the future by establishing a follow-up mechanism and better music therapy approaches.

In conclusion, music has the ability to relieve pain, minimize crying, and increase blood oxygen saturation when a baby’s fundus is being examined. Music with a beat of 60 to 80 beats per minute can also lower HR. Music therapy is advised as an adjuvant intervention for pain management because it is a well-tolerated, secure, and affordable treatment.
