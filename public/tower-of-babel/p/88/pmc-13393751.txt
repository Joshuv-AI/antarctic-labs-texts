Single-cell sequencing reveals NK cell dysregulation during chronic HIV infection and identifies transitional NK cell cytotoxicity related to HIV reservoir decay
Authors: Wen-Jing Cao, Bao-Peng Yang, Si-Yuan Chen, Cheng Zhen, Xue-Meng Li, Xiang-Yi Cao, Lin-Yu Wan, Li-Ping Zhang, Mengyuan Zhang, Jia-Min Gao, Chao Zhang, Yan-Mei Jiao, Xing Fan, Ruo-Nan Xu, Hui-Huang Huang, Fu-Sheng Wang, Jin-Wen Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundNatural killer (NK) cells, as an integral component of the innate immune system, assume a pivotal role in combating viral infections. Our understanding of how human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection affects NK cell heterogeneity remains limited.MethodsWe conducted single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on peripheral blood NK cells from five healthy controls and seventeen male people living with HIV (PLWH), both before and after antiretroviral therapy (ART). Biological sex was extracted from medical records, and gender identity was not collected. O-link proteomic analysis was employed to quantify alterations in plasma inflammatory cytokines. Cell-associated HIV-DNA was measured in CD4+ T cells.FindingsAmong ten identified NK cell subsets, c7-Type I interferon (IFN)-responding cells were increased in untreated participants, correlating negatively with CD4+ T cell counts and positively with inflammation. The c3-CD56dim NK-IRS2 subset, enriched in lipopolysaccharide (LPS) response genes, markedly declined and showed extensive transcriptional remodelling following HIV infection. Two proliferating NK cell subsets, c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 and c9-CD56dim NK-MKI67, increased following HIV infection, enriched in S and G2/M phases cells, respectively, with distinct metabolic profiles. Notably, the cytotoxicity score of the c5-Transitional NK subset before ART was associated with HIV-DNA decline over time.InterpretationOur findings offer a comprehensive understanding of the cellular and molecular heterogeneity of NK cell immune response during HIV infection and offer insights for NK cell-based immunotherapies.FundingSupported by grants from the National Natural Science Foundation of China (No. 82371766, 82572006, 82101837, and 82271857).Keywords: HIV, NK cells, scRNA-seq, HIV reservoir

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed from database inception to July 3, 2025, using combinations of the terms “HIV” OR “PLWH”, “natural killer cell” OR “NK cell”, “single-cell RNA sequencing” OR “scRNA-seq”, and “antiretroviral therapy” OR “ART”. We also screened the reference lists of relevant articles. Original human studies on NK cell phenotype, function, or transcriptional states in HIV infection were included, and studies without NK cell-specific analyses were excluded. Natural killer (NK) cells play a critical role in anti-human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) immunity. Although antiretroviral therapy (ART) effectively suppresses the HIV viral load to undetectable levels, the persistent HIV reservoir is a barrier to achieving clinical functional cure. Several studies based on animal models have obtained evidence linking NK cell activity to disease progression and HIV reservoir decay. Paradoxically, HIV infection directly compromises NK cell antiviral function through phenotypic and functional alterations. Consequently, it is urgently needed to obtain a comprehensive single-cell resolution profiling of NK cell subsets before and after ART to elucidate their role in viral control and reservoir dynamics.

Added value of this study

Our research uncovers the NK cell heterogeneity in individuals with chronic HIV infection at the single-cell level. The c7-Type I interferon (IFN)-responding NK cell subset is significantly elevated in untreated participants and is closely associated with HIV disease progression. The c3-CD56dim NK-IRS2, characterised by an enrichment of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) response-related pathway, exhibited a significant reduction and underwent extensive transcriptional reprogramming following HIV infection. Importantly, the cytotoxicity of the c5-Transitional NK subset shows a significant positive correlation with HIV-DNA decay.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our study provides a comprehensive dissection of the cellular and molecular heterogeneity of NK cells during chronic HIV infection, and further identifies a transitional NK cell subset whose cytotoxic potential is linked to the decay of HIV reservoir upon ART initiation. These findings illuminate potential immune correlates of HIV reservoir dynamics and may contribute to the design of NK cell-based immunotherapeutic strategies aimed at achieving a functional cure.

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection remains a major global public health challenge. While antiretroviral therapy (ART) has rendered HIV a manageable chronic condition, significant obstacles remain in achieving a clinical functional cure and meeting the 2030 sustainable development goal of eradicating the HIV epidemic.1 Natural killer (NK) cells, key components of the innate immune system, play essential roles in the immune response against HIV infections and in determining the disease progression.2 They act as the first line of defence with potent cytotoxicity regulated independently of antigen presentation through a balance of activating and inhibitory receptors, and also interact closely with other immune cell types to regulate adaptive immune response.3, 4, 5

In addition to direct cytolysis and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity,6 NK cells secrete β-chemokines (CCL3, CCL4, and CCL5) which competitively bind CCR5, thereby blocking HIV entry into host cells.7,8 Moreover, the expression patterns of NK cell killer immunoglobulin-like receptor (KIR) genes and their human leucocyte antigen (HLA) ligands are closely associated with HIV disease progression. Notably, co-expression of the activating receptor KIR3DS1 and HLA-Bw4-80I is associated with slower HIV disease progression.9,10 Asian cohorts with the HLA-B46+ phenotype exhibit higher HIV viral loads and more rapid disease progression compared to HLA-B46− individuals.11 Beyond host genetics, HIV can acquire sequence polymorphisms that modulate NK cell recognition. Alter et al. identified KIR-associated HIV-1 polymorphisms that enhance engagement of inhibitory KIRs and reduce the antiviral activity of KIR-positive NK cells.12 In HIV infection, including the acute phase, NK cell perturbations are characterised by a redistribution of subsets with expansion of a dysfunctional CD56−CD16+ population, which is associated with impaired cytokine production and cytotoxic function.13,14 In addition, NK cell education and HIV-associated changes in HLA expression may jointly shape NK responsiveness to autologous HIV-infected targets, providing additional context for persistent functional alterations observed during chronic infection.15 Furthermore, antibody-dependent activation can downregulate NKp46 via a metalloproteinase-dependent mechanism, potentially attenuating NKp46-dependent natural cytotoxicity during HIV infection.16 Recently, growing attention has been given to leveraging NK cells as a potential immunotherapeutic strategy to control HIV replication. In humanised mouse and rhesus macaque models, increased NK cell response, particularly in conjunction with latency-reversing agents, has been shown to reduce viral reservoirs and delay viral rebound post-treatment cessation.17,18 Additionally, the frequency of CXCR5+ NK cells in the lymph nodes of people living with HIV (PLWH) is negatively correlated with viral replication.19 A phase I clinical trial by Miller et al. demonstrated that haploidentical NK cell infusion combined with interleukin (IL)-2 or N-803 can reduce virus-producing cells in lymph nodes.20

Traditionally, NK cells are categorised into CD56bright and CD56dim subsets. The CD56dim subset is further classified by markers like CD57, CD94-NKG2A, and CD62L, reflecting different maturation levels.21, 22, 23 Recent advancements in cytometric methodologies, particularly cytometry by time-of-flight and spectral flow cytometry, have provided valuable insights into NK cell heterogeneity and functionality.24, 25, 26, 27 Nonetheless, methodologies above are limited to assessing gene expression profiles at the cellular level, thereby constraining our comprehension of transcriptional heterogeneity. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) provides a powerful solution, facilitating the exploration of transcriptomic alterations in innate immune cells at the single-cell level. Related technologies have been widely applied to various diseases and have led to the successful identification of numerous significant functional impairments and substantial alterations in NK cells. For instance, the expansion of specific NK cell subsets has been observed in various cancer patients, and the functional and metabolic characteristics of impaired NK cells have been described,28 while transforming growth factor (TGF)-β-mediated NK cell dysfunction has been documented in patients with severe coronavirus disease 2019.29 In HIV research, scRNA-seq has also emerged as a valuable tool for dissecting NK cell alterations at single-cell resolution. By performing scRNA-seq on peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from four individuals during hyperacute HIV infection, Kazer et al. found that proliferating NK cells may be associated with subsequent viral control.30 Wang et al. also revealed impaired NK cell responses, as evidenced by significant alterations in NK cell receptor and cytokine signalling gene expression in chronic HIV infection.31 Although prior PBMC-based scRNA-seq studies in PLWH included NK cells, the limited numbers of NK cells captured in those datasets precluded more precise, in-depth NK subset resolution. Therefore, NK-focused single-cell profiling during HIV infection remains relatively limited and warrants further investigation.

Here, we constructed the largest unbiased and comprehensive single-cell atlas of human blood NK cells during HIV infection. We longitudinally assessed how HIV infection and ART initiation affect the heterogeneity of the NK cell subset. We also identified key molecular pathways that are altered in NK cell subsets following HIV infection and the remaining perturbation after ART. Of particular importance, the cytotoxicity scores of NK cell subsets, especially the transitional NK cell subset, are correlated with the decay of HIV-DNA. In summary, our dataset is a valuable resource for better understanding the mechanisms underlying the dysfunctional NK cell immune response during chronic HIV infection and providing valuable insights into NK-cell-based immunotherapy for HIV infection.

Methods

Study participants

The cohort for the scRNA-seq analysis consisted of 17 PLWH and 5 healthy controls (HCs) (Table S1). Subsequently, an additional 5 PLWH and 4 HCs were enrolled to facilitate functional validation. Detailed characteristics of all individuals involved in this research are provided in Table S2. All participants were recruited from the Fifth Medical Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital in Beijing, China. Given that the epidemiological profile of HIV in Beijing is predominantly concentrated among men who have sex with men, this study included only male participants. Biological sex (male/female) was extracted from medical records; gender identity was not collected. One sample was obtained from each PLWH during the untreated phase and again after they had undergone ART for over one year, respectively. The “untreated” PLWH who displayed typical progressive disease and had not received ART, and they were classified as “cART (combination ART)” when their plasma HIV-RNA levels fell below the detection threshold following more than one year of ART. PLWH were excluded from the study if they had co-infections with the hepatitis B or C virus, or were in a moribund status.

Ethics

Blood samples were collected with the approval of the Fifth Medical Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital Research Ethics Committee (KY-2023-10-67-1). Study participants provided written informed consent following the Declaration of Helsinki.

Human sample collection

PBMCs were isolated from ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) anticoagulated venous blood by Ficoll-Hypaque (Merck KGaA, Cat# GE17-1440-02) density gradient centrifugation. Briefly, blood samples were centrifuged at 450 x g for 10 min. The supernatant (plasma) was collected and preserved at −80 °C. PBMCs were then isolated by Ficoll through density gradient centrifugation at 700 x g, with the acceleration set to 4 and the deceleration set to 1. Following centrifugation, cells from the plasma-Ficoll interface were collected and subsequently transferred to a new tube, then washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 450 x g for 10 min. The resulting precipitates were resuspended in the cryopreservation liquid containing 90% foetal bovine serum (FBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 100010023) and 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Merck KGaA, Cat# 200-664-3). Finally, PBMCs were stored at −80 °C for 24 h before being transferred to liquid nitrogen for long-term preservation.

Detection of plasma HIV-RNA

The HIV real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) Fluorescence Quantitative Detection Kit was used according to the manufacturer's instructions to quantify HIV-RNA levels in plasma. PCR was performed using a CFX96 RT-PCR system (Bio-Rad, Hercules).

Flow cytometry

PBMCs were thawed and incubated for 3 h at 37 °C in the RPMI-1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 11875093) supplemented with 10% FBS in a CO2 incubator. For phenotypic analysis, PBMCs were stained extracellularly by incubating with antibodies specific to the respective makers for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Fluorescently conjugated monoclonal antibodies and reagents utilised included Fixable Viability Stain 700, anti-CD3, anti-CD14, anti-CD20, anti-CD16, anti-CD56, anti-CD52, anti-CD49f, anti-NKG2C, anti-FcεRIγ, and Brilliant Stain Buffer (BD Pharmingen, Cat# 566385). For the subsequent intracellular marker staining, cells were permeabilized using the Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 00-5223-00), followed by incubation with the indicated antibodies including anti-ki67, anti-MX1, and anti-STMN1. For functional assays, PBMCs from untreated PLWH were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS and stimulated with IL-12 (10 ng/mL; PeproTech, Cat# 200-12H-50UG), IL-15 (10 ng/mL; BioLegend, Cat# 570304), and IL-18 (50 ng/mL; BioLegend, Cat# 592104) for 20 h,32 or co-cultured with the K562 cell line at an effector (PBMC) -to-target (K562) ratio of 5:1. To assess cytokine production by stimulated cells, CD107a was added at 6 h, and GolgiStop (BD Biosciences, Cat# 554724) was added at 5 h before cell harvest. Before or after stimulation, cells were harvested for the detection of intracellular interferon (IFN)-γ, granzyme B, perforin. Detailed information on antibodies is listed in Table S3. Flow cytometry was performed on a BD FACSymphony™ A5 Cell Analyser flow cytometer, and data were analysed using FlowJo software.

Plasma protein profiling using Olink multiplex panel

Plasma samples from the cohort were analysed by using the Olink inflammation panel, which measures 92 proteins associated with the immune response, following the manufacturer's instructions for the Olink multiplex assay (Olink Bioscience, Sweden). The Olink panel employs proximity extension assay technology, which relies on pairs of DNA-labelled antibodies that bind to target proteins and generate unique reporter molecules, quantifiable by RT-PCR. The Olink panel provides normalised protein expression units (NPX), which are log2-transformed values proportional to protein concentration. A variation of one NPX indicates a twofold increase in the protein concentration.

NK cell and CD4+ T cell isolation

After thawing the frozen PBMCs, the magnetic bead-based enrichment was employed to isolate NK cells and CD4+ T cells from PBMCs using the NK Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat# 130-092-657) and CD4+ T-cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat# 130-096-553) following the manufacturer's instructions, respectively. Briefly, PBMCs were prepared, incubated with a biotin-labelled antibody cocktail, and labelled with magnetic microbeads. Magnetic separation in a field-based column enriched NK cells or CD4+ T cells via flow-through containing unlabelled cells. After magnetic enrichment, isolated NK cells were used for the scRNA-seq, and isolated CD4+ T-cells HIV-DNA detection.

Quantitative detection of HIV-DNA

Total cellular DNA was extracted from purified CD4+ T cells by utilising the Qiagen QIA Symphony DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, USA). HIV-DNA quantification was subsequently performed using the SUPBIO HIV Quantitative Detection Kit (SUPBIO, Guangzhou, China), and the actin gene was simultaneously used under the same PCR conditions as a cellular reference gene for quantitative PCR amplification. Both kits were used according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Intact proviral DNA assay (IPDA)

Workflows of IPDA are as previously described.33 Briefly, extracted genomic DNA from CD4+ T cells was mixed with primers, probes, ddPCR Supermix for Probes (no dUTPs), and nuclease-free water, followed by droplet formation and PCR amplification. Sequences of the relevant primers are shown in Table S4. A 35 μL enzymatic reaction system was composed of genomic DNA, 0.2 μL of probe and primer (both 10 μmol/L), 7 μL of Digital LightCycler 5 × DNA Master, and DEPC-treated water. Enzyme activation was performed following the protocol of the Digital LightCycler® System (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Subsequently, 45 amplification cycles were carried out, with each cycle sequentially involving denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s and annealing/extension at 59 °C for 1 min. The threshold for positive signals was determined by integrating data from negative controls (template-free reactions and genomic DNA isolated from CD4+ T cells of HCs).

Detection of cytomegalovirus (CMV) lgG antibodies

HCMV-specific IgG antibodies were assessed in plasma samples by a commercially available ELISA kit (Abcam; Cat# ab108724) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Results were determined based on the following parameters (in Standard Units): cut-off, 10; grey zone, 9–11; negative, <9; positive, >11.

Sample fixation and fixed RNA profiling sequencing

NK cells isolated by negative magnetic bead selection were fixed and processed following the user guide for Fixation of Cells & Nuclei for Chromium Fixed RNA Profiling (CG000478-Rev A, 10 × Genomics) to capture and profile approximately 7000 cells per sample. Gene expression libraries were generated using the Chromium Fixed RNA Profiling workflow with the Human Transcriptome Probe Set, a probe-hybridisation/ligation–based assay that targets around 18,532 genes. The method for cell counting and viability detection involves sample staining with a fluorescent dye (AO/PI Staining Solution kit, CS2-0106-5 mL, Nexcelom Biosciences) and cell quantification with an automated fluorescent cell counter (Cellaca counter, MX-112-0127, Nexcelom Biosciences). Subsequently, probe hybridisation was conducted, followed by partitioning in Gel Bead-in-Emulsion (GEM), ligation, and extension within the GEMs, culminating in library construction. Libraries were then loaded onto a high-throughput sequencing platform NextSeq500 (Illumina) to generate large amounts of RNA sequencing data.

Quality control and normalisation of scRNA-seq data

Gene-cell metrics generated from each sample were utilised as input for Seurat (Version v4.1.1)34 to implement expression-level quality control, adhering to the following criteria: (1) capture of 600 to 10,000 genes per cell, (2) Unique Molecular Identifier counts ranging from 1000 to 20,000, and (3) a mitochondrial gene proportion of less than 10%. Doublet cells were assessed by Scrublet35 and then removed.

Normalisation, batch effect correction, and unsupervised clustering

The 10 × Genomics Fixed RNA Profiling sequencing data underwent normalisation and batch effect correction through standard bioinformatics procedures. Initially, the NormalizeData function in Seurat was used to normalise the gene expression matrix, ensuring a uniform total expression level across cells post-normalisation, followed by a logarithmic transformation. To correct batch effects in the normalised expression matrix, we used the Harmony algorithm, which utilises a graph-based approach to align cells from different batches. Harmony constructs a global graph that integrates cells from all batches while preserving local structures within each batch. By optimising the graph's edges to minimise batch-related discrepancies, Harmony effectively corrects for batch effects while preserving biological variability. Next, we used the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) algorithm to visualise and cluster the reduced dimensionality of all samples. Following this, the FindAllMarkers function was applied to pinpoint signature genes that characterise each immune cell subset. Clusters exhibiting high expression of cell-specific genes (NCAM1, NKG7, GNLY, and KLRF1), as widely accepted, were identified as NK cell subsets. In contrast, clusters with high expression levels of other immune cell markers, including B cells (CD79A and MS4A1), monocytes (LYZ and CST3), platelets (PPBP), NKT cells (CD3D, CD8A, and TRAC), and an unnamed unconventional cell subset (MT-ND4, MT-CO3, and MT-ATP6) were eliminated.

Group distribution of NK cell clusters

We calculated the ratio of observed to expected cell numbers (Ro/e) for each cluster across various groups to quantify the cell type preference. A Ro/e value greater than 1 signifies that cells of the specific NK cell cluster are more frequently observed than random expectations in this group, that is, enriched. Conversely, a Ro/e value less than 1 signifies that cells of the given NK cell cluster are observed with less frequency than random expectations in this group, indicating depletion.

Differential gene expression and enrichment analysis

To perform differential expression analyses, we used the FindAllMarkers function. Based on the identified differential expression gene (DEG), enrichment analyses were conducted using databases such as Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), to provide insights into the biological functions and pathways linked to the observed gene expression changes. Results were visualised using various plot types, including UMAP plots, bubble heatmaps, and violin plots, to interpret the data and identify key patterns and trends in the gene expression profiles.

Module score of known NK cell subsets

Based on established database definitions from Bulk-RNA sequencing, we identified distinct gene sets (modules) that define these NK cell subsets: CD56bright, CD56dim, and adaptive NK cells. We utilised the AddModuleScore function in Seurat to assess the gene expression profiles of individual NK cells at the single-cell level. Briefly, we calculated the average expression score of specific gene sets in each cell and compared it to a background gene set, generating a module score. This score indicates the enrichment level of the specific gene set within each cell.

Calculation of functional signature score

To systematically evaluate the functional variations of NK cell subsets, we defined cytotoxicity, inflammatory, and stress-related gene sets based on the previous study.28 The cytotoxicity gene sets were defined as GZMA, GZMB, GZMH, GZMM, GZMK, GNLY, PRF1, and CTSW. The cytokine and chemokine gene set was defined as IFNG, LTB, CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, XCL1, and XCL2. The cytokine and chemokine receptor gene set were defined as IL7R, IL2RB, IL21R, CXCR3, CXCR4, CCR7, CXCR1, S1PR5, SELL, CXCR2, CMKLR1, CX3CR1, CCR5, and KIT. We used the R package AUCell36 to calculate the scores of each specific gene set, which is ranking-based and independent of the gene expression units and normalisation procedures. We built the ranked expression matrix using the AUCell_buildRankings function, then calculated the AUC value using the AUCell_calcAUC function.

Pseudotime trajectory inference

Monocle3 (v0.1.3) was utilised to investigate transcriptional trajectories of ten NK cell clusters, and we constructed a pseudotime trajectory based on the UMAP plot. Initially, we imported the gene expression matrix from Seurat into Monocle 3 and mapped it to a two-dimensional space using the UMAP algorithm. Subsequently, we identified the c6-CD56bright NK subset as the starting point for developmental trajectory analysis using the learn_graph function. Next, we used the order_cells function to sequence cells along the pseudotime, ensuring their alignment with the biological developmental trajectory. Finally, we generated a scatter plot of cells using the plot_cells function, which included pseudotime order information and supported robust biological analyses.

Transcription factor analysis

We used the Regulon Specificity Score (RSS) method to assess transcription factor (TF) binding activities in the genome by calculating the regulatory score of specific sequences. TFs binding site activities were evaluated based on the significance and distribution of RSS. A high RSS value typically indicates a high-affinity binding site for the TF.

Cell cycle analysis

The cell cycle encompasses the entire process from the completion of one cell division to the end of the next, delineated by distinct phases, including G0/1, S, and G2/M. We used the CellCycleScoring function included in Seurat to calculate the cell cycle scores for each cell, followed by visualisation of the results using a UMAP plot.

Statistical analysis

Detailed information on software and algorithms used for scRNA-seq data analysis and flow cytometry data analysis is listed in Table S3. Differential expression thresholds are set at log2FC > 0.25. Statistical analyses used in this study included the two-sided Mann–Whitney test, Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test, and as described in the figure legends. Statistical correlations were estimated by Spearman correlation. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure is employed for multiple hypothesis testing correction to control the false discovery rate (FDR). p < 0.05 indicates a statistically significant difference (∗: p < 0.05, ∗∗: p < 0.01, ∗∗∗: p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗: p < 0.0001).

Results

scRNA-seq profiling of NK cells in PLWH

To characterise the NK cell immune response during HIV infection, we applied scRNA-seq to characterise the transcriptome of peripheral blood NK cells collected from 17 paired PLWHs before and after initiation of ART (designated as untreated and cART in subsequent figures, respectively) as well as five HCs (Fig. 1A, Table S1). Detailed clinical information of the PLWH participants is shown in Table S2.

Subsequently, a comprehensive sequencing data analysis was conducted by combining Olink and HIV-DNA datasets. (B) UMAP plots with distinct colours depicting different NK cell subsets. (C) UMAP plots illustrating the expression levels of four NK-lineage-defining markers (NCAM1, NKG7, KLRF1, and GNLY) across all subsets. Each circle represents an individual sample, with blue-filled circles and hollow circles representing the same participant. Statistical analysis was performed using the two-sided Mann–Whitney test and Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test. The p values were adjusted for FDR using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

NK cells were isolated from peripheral blood by magnetic-activated cell sorting, with the purity confirmation by flow cytometry (Figure S1A), and subjected to scRNA-seq using the Chromium Fixed RNA Profiling platform. Following stringent quality control (Figure S1B), 265,356 high-quality single-cell transcriptomes were retained for UMAP analysis to map cells into two dimensions for clustering. Non-NK cells, including B cells (CD79A, MS4A1), monocytes (LYZ, CST3), platelets (PPBP), NKT cells (CD3D, CD8A, TRAC), and an unknown cell type (MT-ND4, MT-CO3, MT-ATP6), were excluded. The remaining 262,697 cell transcriptomes, which encompassed samples from HCs (30,653 cells), PLWH untreated (108,074 cells), and cART (123,970 cells) (Figure S1C), were clustered into 10 NK cell subsets (Fig. 1B). NK cell lineage markers (NCAM1, NKG7, KLRF1, and GNLY) were visualised by UMAP (Fig. 1C).

As shown in Fig. 1C and D, cluster 6 was identified as “c6-CD56bright NK” cells based on high expression of NCAM1 (CD56), along with SELL (CD62L), and GZMK (granzyme K). Consistently, c6-CD56bright NK exhibited the highest enrichment for the CD56bright NK cell gene signature (Figure S1D). Cluster 5 shared transcriptionally similarity with cluster 6, but showed upregulation of CD56dim NK cells associated genes such as FCGR3A (CD16) and downregulated KLRC1, SELL, and GZMK (Fig. 1D, Figure S1F), referring to previously described “c5-Transitional NK”.37, 38, 39 This subset displayed intermediate expression of signature genes of both CD56bright and CD56dim NK cells (Figure S1D-E). The remaining eight clusters were classified as CD56dim NK cells and named based on their predominant gene expression profiles. In detail, c1-CD56dim NK-CD52 and c2-CD56dim NK-RAP2A cells (RAP2A, a small GTP-binding protein), which highly express CD52, IL32, CD3E, and CD2, were regarded as adaptive-like NK cells.40 In parallel, several underappreciated NK cell subsets were also identified by unique transcriptional phenotypes. For example, c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 and c9-CD56dim NK−MKI67 were distinguished by high expression of proliferation markers such as STMN1 (a microtubule-destabilising phosphoprotein) and MKI67. Additionally, c3-CD56dim NK-IRS2 cells specifically expressed IRS2 and JUN, which are related to activation status. We also identified a small fraction of c7-Type I IFN-responding cells with strong expression of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), including IFI44L, IFI6, and MX1. Additionally, the c0-CD56dim NK-PTGDS and c4-CD56dim NK-ZNF331 are respectively marked by expression of PTGDS, involved in the prostaglandin metabolic pathway, and ZNF331, a zinc finger protein often aberrantly methylated in tumours (Fig. 1D, Figure S1F).

Next, we compared the distribution of NK cell subsets following HIV infection. Compared to HCs, untreated individuals exhibited a significant enrichment of c7-Type I IFN-responding cells (2.18% vs 0.72%, p = 0.0032) and c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 subset (1.63% vs 0.97%, p = 0.0005), with a non-significant trend toward increased c9-CD56dim NK-MKI67 subset (0.95% vs 0.69%, p = 0.08) (Fig. 1E–F, Figure S1C). Although ART reduced the frequency of c7-Type I IFN-responding cells, the level remained elevated (1.21% vs 0.72%, p = 0.06); and the c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 subset also failed to normalise (1.41% vs 0.97%, p = 0.0150) (Fig. 1F). In contrast, adaptive-like subsets c1-CD56dim NK-CD52 and c2-CD56dim NK-RAP2A increased cART compared to untreated individuals (18.99% vs 15.27%, p = 0.0174; 19.68% vs 16.18%, p = 0.0395, respectively) (Fig. 1F). Notably, c3-CD56dim NK-IRS2 subset was the only subset significantly reduced in both untreated (p = 0.0086) and ART-suppressed individuals (p = 0.0005) relative to controls (Fig. 1F). These results indicated that abnormal NK cell subset distribution induced by HIV infection persists and does not fully recover with ART.

Functional divergence of NK cell subsets

We further inspected the gene expression signature to decipher the functional divergence. We found that CD56dim NK cell subsets exhibited a spectrum of molecules, such as perforin (PRF1) and granzymes (GZMM, GZMH, GZMB, and GZMA), whereas c6-CD56bright NK cells displayed decreased cytotoxicity scores despite strong expression of GZMK, GNLY, and CTSW (Fig. 2A, Figure S2A). The c5-Transitional NK subset expressed intermediate levels of cytotoxic molecules (Fig. 2A). We next assessed cytokine and chemokine molecule expression across NK cell subsets. c6-CD56bright NK cells were characterised by robust transcription of LTB, XCL1, and XCL2. By contrast, c3-CD56dim NK-IRS2 cells exhibited high expression of CCL3, CCL4, and IFNG (Fig. 2A), suggesting that the subsets have varying abilities to produce cytokines and chemokines. It also should be noted that IL32 and CCL5 are highly expressed in c1-CD56dim NK-CD52, c2-CD56dim NK-RAP2A subsets. Several studies showed that CD56bright NK cells exhibited a highly expressed cytokine receptor signature of NK cell precursors, including KIT and IL7R.41,42 Here, both c5-Transitional NK and c6-CD56bright NK subsets exhibited heightened expression levels of IL10RB, IL18RAP, IL2RB, and IL18R1 (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, the CXC chemokine receptor family also had a high discriminability. In particular, the c6-CD56bright NK subset preferentially expressed CCR7, whereas CCR5 was enriched in c5-Transitional NK cells (Fig. 2A).

We further explored the classic activating and inhibitory receptors of NK cell subsets. c3-CD56dim NK-IRS2 exhibited intermediate expression of NCR1 (NKp46), CD160, and NCR3 (NKp30) (Fig. 2A). KIR2DL4, which can be activated and induce the release of IFN-γ and TNF-α, was predominantly expressed by the c6-CD56bright NK subset (Fig. 2A). In contrast, the c4-CD56dim NK-ZNF331 subset expressed markedly higher levels of inhibitory receptors, including LAG3, PDCD1, KLRG1, and TIGIT (Fig. 2A). While the c3-CD56dim NK-IRS2 subset was distinguished by enriched expression of SIGLEC7 and HAVCR2 (TIM3), suggesting a unique inhibitory receptor profile (Fig. 2A).

In terms of various dimensions of gene expression, a gradual gradient of differences consistently exists among several subsets. To more intuitively demonstrate the correlations within the transcriptional landscape, we employ pseudotime analysis to delineate the differentiation hierarchy among diverse NK cell subsets precisely. The c6-CD56bright NK subset serves as the foundational cluster and gives rise to two distinct trajectories (Figure S2B). The c5-Transitional NK acts as a critical juncture for differentiation divergence, where a brief trajectory terminates in c4-CD56dim NK-ZNF331 (Figure S2B). Conversely, the longer trajectory encompasses multiple branching points, leading to c3-CD56dim NK-IRS2 and establishing a linear connection with c0-CD56dim NK-PTGDS, c2-CD56dim NK-RAP2A, c1-CD56dim NK-CD52, c9-CD56dim NK-MKI67, and c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 sequentially (Figure S2B). The expression levels of the NK cell marker CD16 (FCGR3A) progressively increase along pseudotime, whereas CD56 (NCAM1) displays an inverse pattern. Furthermore, as differentiation events advance, we observe a gradual decline in the expression of cytokine receptor genes (IL7R), adhesion/homing receptor genes (SELL), and killer cell lectin-like receptor genes such as KLRC1 (Figure S2C), aligning with the overarching trends of cell differentiation and maturation.

Meanwhile, the biological process enrichment analysis based on GO exploratively indicated potential functional tendencies across subsets, although these results are not demonstrative. Two adaptive-like NK cell subsets (c1-CD56dim NK-CD52 and c2-CD56dim NK-RAP2A) were enriched in genes involved in the regulation of T-cell activation and cell surface receptor signalling pathway (Fig. 2B). The c4-CD56dim NK-ZNF331, c5-Transitional NK, and c6-CD56bright NK cells displayed suggestive enrichment for differentiation-related processes (Fig. 2B). The c3-CD56dim NK-IRS2 exhibited selective enrichment for lipopolysaccharide-response genes, suggesting a potential antibacterial-responsive tendency (Fig. 2B). The c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 subset was primarily associated with the DNA replication process, while c9-CD56dim NK-MKI67 was linked to enhanced regulation of viral process (Fig. 2B). Notably, the c7-Type I IFN-responding cells showed enrichment consistent with antiviral defence signatures (Fig. 2B). Together, these findings provide exploratory insights into the potential functional diversity of NK cell subsets in chronic HIV infection.

c7-Type I IFN-responding cells were associated with HIV disease progression

Chronic inflammation is a hallmark of HIV infection that develops as the disease progresses.43,44 We assessed the clinical relevance of plasma inflammation-related proteins (IRPs) by quantifying 92 proteins using the Olink multiplex inflammation panel. Compared to HCs, 24 IRPs were differentially expressed before ART and 12 IRPs after ART (Figure S3A). As expected, 8 IRPs, including AXIN1, CCL20, CD8A, FGF-19, CXCL9/10/11, and TNFRSF9, remained abnormal after ART (Figure S3B). We then explored the correlation between NK subsets and clinical indices, as well as IRPs, in untreated individuals. We found that the proportion of c7-Type I IFN-responding cells was positively correlated with inflammation-related chemokines and cytokines such as CXCL9/10/11, TNF, IL-12B, IL-18, CCL19, and the costimulatory receptor TNFRSF9 (Fig. 3A). Additionally, the proportion of c7-Type I IFN-responding cells was significantly negatively correlated with absolute CD4+ T cell counts and the CD4/CD8 ratio, and positively correlated with HIV viral load (Fig. 3A). Simultaneously, we observed that the proportion of the c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 subset exhibited a significant negative correlation with both CD4 and CD8 T cell counts (Fig. 3A). Notably, following ART, correlations between NK cell subsets and clinical parameters were completely lost (Figure S3C). Under ART, the proportion of the c7-Type I IFN-responding cell subset showed a positive correlation with CCL25, a trend not observed before ART. Meanwhile, the proportions of c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 and c4-CD56dim NK-ZNF331 subsets exhibited a significant positive correlation with the chemokine CXCL10 (Figure S3C). Concurrently, the c4-CD56dim NK-ZNF331 subset also showed positive correlations with CXCL10 and CXCL11 after ART (Figure S3C).

Among all NK cell subsets, c7-Type I IFN-responding cells appeared most strongly associated with HIV disease progression and inflammation. Therefore, we further explored the characteristics of this subset. In c7-Type I IFN-responding cells, several ISGs were specifically upregulated (Figs. 1D and 3B), suggesting that these NK cells were activated by IFNs. We confirmed the increased proportion of IFN-responding NK cell subset, identified by MX1+NK cells, in untreated participants using flow cytometry, consistent with our scRNA-seq results (Fig. 3C and D). To elucidate the immune responses of this NK cell subset, we performed GO analysis. The c7-Type I IFN-responding cells were tightly involved in a process related to viral defence (Fig. 3E). Utilising SCENIC tools for the RSS analysis, we predicted the upregulated TF, which is integral to type I IFN response pathways in this subset (Figure S3D). Among them, IRF9 is a key regulator of type I IFN-dependent immune responses, while STAT2 mediates the cellular IFN response.45

For the IFN response assessment, we intersected our data with 628 ISGs categorised into five functional clusters,46 and it turns out that 62 ISGs were significantly upregulated (Fig. 3F). Most ISGs (53 of 62) exert direct antiviral activity, including IFIT and IFITM genes (Fig. 3F), which with broad-spectrum antiviral functions.47, 48, 49 To validate the functional properties of c7-Type I IFN-responding NK cells, we assessed effector molecule expression in MX1+ and MX1− NK cells using both cytokine stimulation and K562 co-culture assays. Under cytokine stimulation, MX1+ NK cells showed significantly higher levels of CD107a, perforin, granzyme B, and IFN-γ than MX1− NK cells (Fig. 3G and Figure S4A-C). In the K562 co-culture assay, MX1+ cells maintained significantly higher perforin, granzyme B, and IFN-γ expression, whereas the differences in CD107a between MX1+ and MX1− cells were no longer statistically significant (Figure S4D).

Two proliferating NK cell subsets showed distinct metabolism patterns

We identified two proliferating NK cell subsets (c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 and c9-CD56dim NK-MKI67) (Fig. 1B). DEG analysis revealed that the expression of TYMS, HELLS, MCM, and E2F1, which are markers associated with S phase entry and DNA replication, were upregulated in the c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 subset (Fig. 4A). High activity of E2F8 and E2F7, members of the cell cycle-associated E2F transcription factor family, was also observed in the c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 subset (Figure S5A and C). In contrast, the c9-CD56dim NK-MKI67 subset is characterised by high expression of MKI67, H1FX, CENPF, and HIST1H1D, which are markers for cell proliferation, nucleosome chains condensation, and mitosis (Fig. 4A). GLIS2, NFYC, NELFE, NFYA, and HCFC1 were identified as the most specific regulons associated with the c9-CD56dim NK-MKI67 subset (Figure S5B and D).

Cell division is a metabolically demanding process that requires substantial energy and biomass synthesis. Metabolism adjusts to these demands, regulating related proteins and influencing the cell cycle.50 To characterise the metabolic profile, we used the scMetabolism package to calculate the active metabolic pathways score.51 Pyrimidine metabolism, as well as amino acid and peptide metabolism, were predominantly enriched in the c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 subset, while nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism and ether lipid metabolism were enriched in c9-CD56dim NK-MKI67 (Fig. 4B). Notably, the citrate cycle level of c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 subset is very high, and both subsets exhibited high levels of oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 4B).

GO analysis indicated that the c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 subset showed enrichment for upregulated genes related to DNA replication and recombination, alongside downregulated genes linked to immune response-activating signalling pathways (Fig. 4C). In contrast, the c9-CD56dim NK-MKI67 subset displayed upregulation in genes involving viral processes (Fig. 4D). Cell cycle scores, previously defined as the G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases,52 were used to determine cycle stages. We found that c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 and c9-CD56dim NK-MKI67 exhibited significantly higher S and G2/M scores, respectively (Fig. 4E–G). Almost all cells of the c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 subset were in S phase, and 60.15% of the c9-CD56dim NK-MKI67 subset were in G2/M phase (Fig. 4H). Flow cytometry confirmed that following HIV infection, the c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 subset (identified as STMN1+ NK cells) increased, whereas the c9-CD56dim NK-MKI67 subset (identified as ki67+ NK cells) showed no significant change (Fig. 4I–K).

Transcriptional remodelling of NK subsets following HIV infection

Transcriptional variations during HIV infection drive altered cellular functions, prompting us to investigate the infection-related transcriptional differences among the 10 subsets in the DEG analysis between samples from HCs and PLWH. Notably, c4-CD56dim NK-ZNF331 and c3-CD56dim NK-IRS2 subsets consistently exhibited the highest number of DEGs compared to HCs, followed by c5-Transitional NK and c0-CD56dim NK-PTGDS subsets, regardless of ART status (Fig. 5A and B). We then performed GO enrichment analysis and identified the top 3 enriched pathways of each NK cell subset (Fig. 5C and D). In untreated individuals, most NK cell subsets showed enrichment of upregulated genes related to “Defense response to virus”, “Defense response to symbiont”, and “Response to Virus” (Fig. 5C). Given that c3-CD56dim NK-IRS2 and c4-CD56dim NK-ZNF331 subset exhibited the most pronounced transcriptional remodelling, we further analysed the top 10 significantly enriched GO terms of these subsets and found these two subsets also upregulated genes associated with the “Interferon-mediated signalling pathway”, “Negative regulation of viral process”, etc. (Figure S6A and B). However, compared with HCs, no significant functional enrichment of these two subsets was observed after ART (Fig. 5D). We further investigated the immune characteristics of the two NK cell subsets, and analysis of DEGs across 10 clusters confirmed that the c3-CD56dim NK-IRS2 subset upregulated IRS2, CD69, JUN, FOS, and NFKBIZ (Figure S6C), which are associated with cell activation or immediate early response. GO analysis indicated that upregulated DEGs of the c3-CD56dim NK-IRS2 subset were enriched for cell differentiation and responses to lipopolysaccharide and molecules of bacterial origin (Figure S6D). The c4-CD56dim NK-ZNF331 exhibited high expression of genes associated with immune suppression and exhaustion, such as KLRG1, CD160, and TIGIT (Figure S6C). DEGs of c4-CD56dim NK-ZNF331 were enriched for functional annotations related to cell differentiation (Figure S6E).

Hallmarks of c0-CD56dim NK-PTGDS are the elevated expression of genes associated with prostaglandin metabolism, including prostaglandin D2 synthase (PTGDS) and alde-keto reductase superfamily member (AKR1C3) (Figure S6F). Several connectin-associated genes (MYOM2) and genes implicated in cell adhesion (SPON2) also showed high expression (Figure S6F). The c0-CD56dim NK-PTGDS subset exhibited relatively high cytotoxic molecules, accompanied by elevated expression of TGF-β, as well as IL-2 and IL-12 receptor, suggesting that this subset may be co-regulated by opposing cytokine signals (Fig. 2A). In addition, chemokine receptor genes, such as S1PR5, CMKLR1, CXCR1/2, and CX3CR1, showed the most pronounced expression (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, we identified specific activities of multiple regulon genes in c0-CD56dim NK-PTGDS, such as IKZF2, NFE2L1, and BCL11B (Figure S6G), which are tightly related to cell differentiation. Interestingly, GO analysis of this subset revealed downregulation of cell differentiation processes, alongside enhanced cell killing and activation function (Figure S6H-I).

To further assess the effects of cART on NK cell transcriptional programs, we performed a paired comparison of samples from untreated and cART-treated samples within the same individuals. The analysis revealed that untreated subjects exhibited enriched antiviral pathways across all NK cell populations, including “defense response to virus”, “defense response to symbiont”, and “response to virus” (Figure S6J). Together, these results indicate that while cART partially reverses the transcriptional alterations in NK cells induced by chronic HIV infection, it does not completely restore them to a normal state.

Unique transcriptomic profile of adaptive-like NK cells

Previous studies indicated that memory NK cells are expanded in PLWH.53,54 CMV is a potent driver of adaptive NK cell expansion, and all samples from our scRNA-seq analysis were CMV IgG seropositive (Table S1 and Table S2); however, no correlation was observed between CMV IgG status and the above two subsets (Figure S7A–D). We established a module score using bulk RNA data on adaptive NK cells from existing literature,55 and our analysis revealed that the c1-CD56dim NK-CD52 and c2-CD56dim NK-RAP2A subsets exhibited the highest adaptive NK cell module scores (Fig. 6A). We further examined the signature genes and TFs associated with adaptive NK cells. Increased expression of CD52, IL32, CD3E, B3GAT1 (CD57), ZBTB38 (a methylation-sensitive transcriptional regulator), PRDM1, BATF, BCL11B, and CD2; as well as the loss of FCER1G (FcεRIγ), ZBTB16 (PLZF), and SYK correlates with a memory NK cell phenotype,39,56,57 leading us to classify the c1-CD56dim NK-CD52 and c2-CD56dim NK-RAP2A as phenotypically adaptive-like NK cells (Fig. 6B). RSS analysis pinpointed the most specific TFs. Four of the top five lists for c1-CD56dim NK-CD52 and c2-CD56dim NK-RAP2A are consistent: SETBP, GLIS2, BCL11B, and KLF12 (Fig. 6C). BCL11B is essential for NK cell differentiation toward an adaptive state.57 BATF, specific to the c2-CD56dim NK-RAP2A subset, is part of the AP-1 TF family, recognised for shaping chromatin accessibility in adaptive NK cells.58 Correspondingly, the TF specific to c1-CD56dim NK-CD52 is PPARA, which mainly participates in lipid metabolism regulation. Interestingly, although both c1-CD56dim NK-CD52 and c2-CD56dim NK-RAP2A subsets shared pathways such as cell surface receptor signalling, regulation of T cell activation, T-cell receptor signalling pathway, etc., the c2-CD56dim NK-RAP2A subset exhibits stronger enrichment across these shared pathways (Fig. 6D).

We defined the adaptive NK cell subsets using the classic markers NKG2C and FcεRIγ. Flow cytometry analysis showed that the proportion of FcεRIγ-adaptive NK cells in PLWH receiving cART was significantly higher than that in and HC and untreated PLWH (63.50% vs 41.40%, p = 0.0097; 63.50% vs 49.15%, p = 0.0192, respectively) (Fig. 6E). And the proportion of NKG2C+FcεRIγ− adaptive NK cell subsets in PLWH receiving cART was also significantly higher than that in untreated PLWH (27.14% vs 18.71%, p = 0.0136) (Fig. 6F). To further validate the alteration of memory NK cells during HIV infection, consistent with transcriptome data, flow cytometry analysis showed that the proportion of CD52+ NK cells in cART-treated PLWH was significantly higher than that in HCs (1.58% vs 0.61%, p = 0.0068), while no significant difference was observed between untreated PLWH and HCs (1.04% vs 0.61%, p = 0.24) (Fig. 6G). In addition, CD52+ NK cells had high NKG2C expression compared to CD52- NK cells (Figure S8A). Both untreated and cART-treated PLWH exhibited a significantly higher proportion of CD52+NKG2C+ NK cells compared to HCs (0.33% vs 0.09%, p = 0.0002; 0.60% vs 0.09%, p < 0.0001, respectively) (Fig. 6H).

We further investigated the impact of HIV infection on the transcriptional profiles of both subsets. In untreated individuals, both subsets exhibited shared upregulation of several genes, including STAT1 (involved in interferon-mediated immune responses), BPGM (involved in glycolysis), and TYMP (involved in nucleotide metabolism and angiogenesis). In contrast, ST5 (involved in vesicle trafficking regulation) was downregulated in both subsets. Following ART, BPGM remained upregulated and ST5 continued to be downregulated in both subsets. Additionally, COL11A2 (a component of the extracellular matrix) was also downregulated in both subsets after cART (Figure S8B). These shared transcriptional changes indicate that both adaptive-like NK subsets may undergo similar functional reprogramming in response to HIV infection.

NK cell cytotoxicity may contribute to the reduction of HIV-DNA

HIV reservoir is the main barrier to achieving a functional cure. Here, we observed a significant reduction in HIV-DNA level following ART (Figure S9A). To investigate the role of NK cells in HIV reservoir dynamics, we analysed correlations between the frequencies of 10 NK cell subsets and HIV-DNA levels before and after ART. However, apart from the negative correlation between untreated c1-CD56dim NK-CD52 subset and intact HIV proviruses, no other significant negative correlations were observed (Fig. 7A). In contrast, the frequencies of c4-CD56dim NK-ZNF331 and c9-CD56dim NK-MKI67 NK cell subsets in untreated individuals were positively correlated with HIV-DNA levels after ART (Fig. 7A). Then, we incorporated the cytotoxicity score into this analysis and found that the cytotoxicity scores of several NK cell subsets before ART showed negative correlations with the fold change of HIV-DNA; particularly the c5-Transitional NK subset, which also showed significant negative correlations with the fold change of both HIV-DNA and intact HIV proviruses (Fig. 7B). Further investigation identified that PRF1, a key effector in NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, may contributing to HIV-DNA decline (Fig. 7C).

The cellular and molecular characteristics of the c5-Transitional NK cell subset were further analysed. The core signature encoding genes include GZMK (a member of serine proteases from cytotoxic lymphocytes), FOS (a regulator of proliferation and differentiation), TCF7 (a transcription factor associated with T cell differentiation), SELL (a surface adhesion molecule), and KLRC1 (a classical killer cell lectin-like receptor) (Figure S9B). Compared with other NK cell subsets, the c5-Transitional NK cell subset is enriched for functional annotations related to mononuclear/lymphocyte/T-cell differentiation, T-cell activation, and cell–cell adhesion, while exhibiting downregulation of pathways associated with leucocyte-mediated cytotoxicity and immunity (Figure S9C and D). Moreover, we also observed that before ART, the c5-Transitional NK cell subset upregulated the viral response process, which was consistent with previous results, and also upregulated annotations related to regulating the viral life cycle and viral genome replication, while downregulating the secretion of hormone-related peptide substances (Fig. 7C, Figure S9E and F). However, after ART, the c5-Transitional NK cell subset is enriched in protein synthesis/localization events, relieving negative regulation of protein kinase activity (Fig. 7C, Figure S9E and F).

To functionally validate the c5-Transitional NK cell subset, we first sought a surface marker that could selectively enrich c5-Transitional NK cells. UpSet analysis of cluster-specific differentially expressed genes identified 22 genes uniquely differentially expressed in the c5-Transitional NK cell subset (Figure S10A). Among these candidate, ITGA6 (CD49f) showed the most selective enrichment, as demonstrated by cluster-level expression analysis (Figure S10B) as well as its restricted distribution on UMAP (Fig. 7D) and violin plots (Fig. 7E). We therefore sorted CD49f+ and CD49f− NK cells from one healthy individual and profiled them using 10 × Genomic 3’ scRNA-seq. Compared with CD49f− NK cells, CD49f+ NK cells exhibited increased expression of c5-associated genes (Figure S10C), confirming that CD49f could enrich the c5-Transitional NK cell subsets. Additionally, CD49f+ NK cells exhibited a gene expression profile highly consistent with the core functional identity of the c5 subset originally defined in Fig. 2A (Figure S10D). Functionally, compared with canonical CD56bright and CD56dim NK cells, CD49f+ NK cells showed intermediate levels of perforin and granzyme B both before (Figure S10E) and after stimulation (Figure S10F), further supporting the notion that the c5 subset represents a transitional state between these two major NK-cell compartments. Interestingly, CD49f+ NK cells exhibited higher CD107a expression than both CD56bright and CD56dim NK cells after stimulation (Figure S10F). Finally, perforin expression in CD49f+ NK cells was inversely correlated with fold changes in both total HIV-DNA and intact HIV-DNA following ART initiation (Fig. 7F), suggesting that the cytotoxic capacity of c5-like NK cells may be linked to early HIV reservoir decay.

Discussion

As a key component of the innate immune system, NK cells play a pivotal role in controlling HIV infection by eliminating infected cells via cytotoxicity and cytokine secretion.59 Although scRNA-seq has significantly advanced our understanding of human NK cell diversity in healthy individuals and cancer patients,28,39 comprehensive studies in PLWH are still lacking. To achieve this, we enrolled paired untreated and cART PLWHs to construct the most comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic atlas of NK cells to date in chronic HIV infection and identified 10 distinct subsets. Our findings highlight the profound impact of HIV infection on the cellular and molecular heterogeneity of NK cells.

Chronic HIV infection leads to the impairment of the gastrointestinal barriers, and the leakage of microbial products to circulation is one of the reasons responsible for chronic immune activation and inflammation. Although ART successfully suppressed viral replication, the inflammation cannot be fully recovered.60 We identified a c3-CD56dim NK-IRS2 subset with high expression of immediate early response genes such as JUN, EGR1, and FOS. Interestingly, the c3-CD56dim NK-IRS2 subset is enriched in lipopolysaccharide (LPS) response-related pathway, and compared with healthy controls, the c3-CD56dim NK-IRS2 subset has one of the highest numbers of DEG, regardless of ART. Meanwhile, ART treatment cannot restore the frequency of the c3-CD56dim NK-IRS2 subset. The increased translocation of microbial products during HIV infection, such as LPS, may bind to toll-like receptor 4 and CD14 receptors and lead to the transcriptional disturbance of the c3-CD56dim NK-IRS2 subset. The other subset with the highest numbers of DEG compared to healthy controls is the c4-CD56dim NK-ZNF331 subset. In ART-treated individuals, the frequency of the c4-CD56dim NK-ZNF331 subset is positively correlated with inflammatory cytokines such as CXCL10 and CXCL11, which are among the inflammatory cytokines that fail to normalise following ART treatment. Thus, the persistent gastrointestinal barriers and resulting inflammation may closely participate in the dysregulation of NK cells during HIV infection.

CXCR3, the receptor for CXCL9/10/11, is highly expressed in less differentiated c6-CD56bright NK cells. These cells may migrate to the inflammatory site under the chemotactic influence of CXCL9/10/11, where they can exert immunomodulatory function through secreting cytokines. In addition, the high expression of CCR7 in c6-CD56bright NK cells enables these cells to migrate to the lymph node, where intense HIV replication and latency occur. Previous studies have shown the persistence of HIV replication in lymph nodes from ART-treated rhesus macaques and PLWH.61 The ongoing viral replication and inflammatory milieu may also lead to the phenotypic shift of CD56bright NK cells and give rise to more differentiated cytotoxic CD56dim NK cells to inhibit viral replication. In addition, we have previously shown that the frequency of CXCR5+ NK cells was negatively correlated with HIV viral replication in lymph nodes, highlighting the importance of NK cells in controlling HIV replication in the sanctuary site.19 Interestingly, we found that the cytotoxicity of NK cell subsets was closely associated with HIV-DNA decay, especially the c5-Transitional NK cell subset. This subset has been previously described as an intermediate population between CD56bright and CD56dim NK cells,28,39,56,58 and intermediate gene expression patterns, such as cytotoxic molecules, cytokines and chemokines, chemokine receptors, etc., have been observed. This intermediate expression pattern endows the c5-Transitional NK cell subset with the dual advantages of CD56bright NK cells in tissue chemotaxis and CD56dim NK cells in cytotoxicity, making it a potential therapeutic target for achieving a functional cure for HIV. However, our study does not demonstrate a causative link, and thus, the underlying mechanisms and direct contributions of this NK cell subset remain unclear. To further characterise this subset, we identified CD49f (integrin α6, encoded by ITGA6) as a surface marker enabling the enrichment of the c5-Transitional NK cell subsets. CD49f is not unique to NK cells and is known to be expressed across multiple immune and non-immune cell types. Given that CD49f is involved in adhesion, tissue residency, migratory behaviour, or developmental state,62 its expression on the c5 subset may enable it to function as a cytotoxic precursor with unique migratory or tissue-homing properties. In addition, the c5-Transitional NK cell subset may influence the HIV reservoir indirectly, such as cytokine secretion, modulation of dendritic cell and T cell responses, and acting as precursors that differentiate into more effective cytotoxic NK subsets upon activation.4,5 Meanwhile, Bone et al. reported that the HIV reservoir size is inversely associated with memory-enriched CD57+CD56bright NK cells.63 The c5-Transitional NK cells may also share features with memory-enriched CD56bright NK cells.

Type I IFN (IFN-I) is a pivotal cytokine in the early stage of viral infection. These IFN-I are primarily produced by plasmacytoid dendritic cells and other immune cells in response to viral stimuli. IFN-I exerts its antiviral activity through the induction of ISGs, which encode proteins with immunomodulatory and antiviral functions. In our study, in untreated participants, we observed a significant increase in c7-Type I IFN-responding cells, which demonstrated high expression levels of STAT1/2 activity and also ISGs such as IFIT and IFITM genes. This subset has not been previously identified in large-scale single-cell sequencing studies of NK cells in healthy individuals or cancer patients. It appears to be a unique population that emerges in response to viral infections, particularly in the context of specific IFN responses. Similarly, Angin et al. also identified an CD8+ Tem cells with marked upregulation of IFN-response gene in PLWH by scRNA-seq.64 In the context of chronic HIV infection, the innate immune responses are insufficient to completely inhibit viral replication. The persistent IFN secretion and IFN-mediated immune activation may become one of the main pathogenic mechanisms of HIV disease.65 Therefore, the biological relevance of the c7-Type I IFN-responding subset remains ambiguous: these cells may reflect a transient antiviral-primed state or, alternatively, a detrimental signature of chronic immune activation. Hence, this phenomenon may indicate transcriptional activation driven by systemic IFN signalling, serving as a detrimental biomarker of sustained immune system activation rather than evidence of viral clearance. Consistently, we observed that the frequency of c7-Type I IFN-responding cells was positively correlated with HIV viral load and levels of inflammatory cytokines, including CXCL9/10/11, TNF, CCL19, TNFRSF9, IL12B, and IL18. Additionally, a negative correlation was observed between the frequency of these cells with CD4 count, as well as CD4/CD8 ratio.

While NK cells are typically classified as innate immune cells, increasing evidence suggests that NK cells can also exhibit immune memory functions, possess a prolonged lifespan, and demonstrate a swift response to reinfection.66 Comparable subsets have also been documented in the context of HIV infection, and direct interactions between NKG2C-positive NK cells and infected target cells have been described.54,67 In alignment with the aforementioned report, we observed two adaptive-like NK cell subsets, c1-CD56dim NK-CD52 and c2-CD56dim NK-RAP2A, which were significantly increased during HIV infection. These subsets were enriched in relatively high levels of classic adaptive marker genes such as CD52, IL32, CD3E, and ZBTB38.28,68 The phenomenon that CMV activity stimulates the expansion of adaptive NK cells has been thoroughly explored.66,69 Therefore, it must be noted that all participants enrolled in our study were CMV IgG seropositive, we cannot evaluate CMV serostatus effects and cannot interpret adaptive-like NK expansions independently of CMV exposure. The compromised immune function in PLWH may sporadically facilitate CMV reactivation and may induce the proliferation of adaptive NK cells. While CMV seropositivity is a prerequisite for adaptive NK cell development, the degree of expansion does not always correlate directly with antibody titres. For example, Maucourant et al. reported that neither the percentage nor the absolute counts of NKG2C+CD57+ adaptive NK cells showed any correlation with CMV IgG levels in sera.70 Furthermore, the emergence of memory NK cells may also be related to the HIV-associated inflammatory milieu.71 Notably, we observed that these two adaptive-like subsets exhibit elevated expression levels of CCL5, which can competitively inhibit HIV binding to CCR5 and interfere with the viral entry process.7 In addition, these subsets show high expression levels of the calmodulin-type characteristic marker S100A4/6, which is recruited to immune synapses and colocalized with myosin IIa after NK cell activation, as reported in CD56dim CD57+ memory NK cells.72 Consistent with other studies, we also found that the frequencies of adaptive NK cell subsets do not recover after ART.73 Importantly, emerging evidence indicates that adaptive NK cells are associated with the control of HIV reservoirs, especially in elite controllers.68 Núria Climent et al. also reported a HIV infection case who did not experience viral rebound after ceasing medication for 15 years, during which the viral reservoir size decreased and negatively correlated with the frequency of memory-like NKG2C+CD57+ NK cells.74 Sánchez-Gaona et al. identified a subset of memory-like NK cells that coexpress NKG2A and NKG2C.68 This specific NK cell subset demonstrated a heightened antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity response against HIV-infected cells. As c5-Transitional NK cells express KLRC1 (NKG2A), raising the possibility of partial transcriptional overlap with this NKG2A+NKG2C+ memory-like population. Targeted approaches to enhance the function of adaptive NK cells have been explored. Astorga-Gamaza et al. showed that targeting KLRG1 with specific antibodies can enhance the cytotoxicity of adaptive NK cells toward HIV-infected cells, reactivate the latent HIV, and reduce the intact HIV genomes in individuals undergoing ART treatment.75 Collectively, adaptive NK cells represent a potential therapeutic target for achieving a functional cure for HIV.

Previous studies have further indicated that during the acute phase of HIV infection, individuals possessing a higher proportion of proliferating NK cells can sustain viral suppression below detectable levels even after cessation of medication for 2.75 years.30 Our study identified two proliferative subsets, c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 and c9-CD56dim NK-MKI67, which showed distinct enrichment of metabolism-related gene signature in transcriptome-based pathway analyses. The c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 subset displayed relatively higher expression of glycolysis-associated gene programs, whereas the c9-CD56dim NK-MKI67 subset showed relatively higher enrichment of oxidative phosphorylation-associated signatures. This metabolic divergence based on transcriptional programs may indicate their adaptive mechanisms in response to distinct functional requirements during viral infections or varying energetic demands at different phases of the cell cycle. Notably, the frequency of c8-CD56dim NK-STMN1 remained elevated and demonstrated a positive correlation with plasma CXCL10 levels following ART, indicating that the inflammatory milieu may also contribute to the expansion of proliferating NK cells. However, the biological significance of these two proliferating NK subsets remains unsolved, particularly since only STMN1+ NK cells expand post-infection while MKI67+ NK cells do not. Future studies using alternative strategies (e.g., surface surrogates) and functional assays will be required to test whether these proliferating states confer distinct metabolic or functional advantages.

There are some limitations to our study. First, the individuals enrolled in our study received ART for approximately one year. The question remains unresolved regarding whether long-term ART can fully restore the NK cell immune dysregulation. Second, all participants enrolled in our study are male and of Han ethnicity. Considering the impact of sex and ethnicity on immunological and HIV outcomes,76 our data warrant further validation in a cohort that includes women and other ethnic backgrounds. As this was a single-center study conducted in China and the sample size was modest though sufficient for statistical analyses, the generalisability of the findings may be limited, underscoring the need for larger multi-center cohorts to confirm their robustness and external validity. In addition, our analysis focused solely on NK cell subsets in peripheral blood, excluding crucial lymph nodes and gut-associated lymphoid tissue are the primary sites for HIV replication and latency. Niehrs et al. reported that CD56bright NK cells access tissue niches at steady state, whereas CD56dim NK cells, constrained to vasculature at steady state, entered lymph nodes upon inflammation.77 Thus, the peripheral blood NK cells may also enter the tissue compartments and promote the decay of the HIV reservoir. Future studies should focus on characterising NK cells in these tissues and comparing them with peripheral blood NK cells to gain a comprehensive understanding of NK cell characteristics in chronic HIV infection. Meanwhile, although CD49f can be used as a marker to enrich for a c5-like NK cell fraction, it does not enable clean prospective isolation of a homogeneous c5 subset, and the number of CD49f+ NK cells after cell sorting was insufficient for functional assays. Furthermore, the Flex assay is a probe-based and targets a predefined transcriptome probe set rather than providing genome-wide coverage. As probe design and performance can differ across genes, detection sensitivity and apparent expression levels may vary between targets. Finally, our study lacks a comparator group of post-treatment controllers or elite controllers. Including these groups would provide valuable context for interpreting NK cell transcriptional programs in relation to natural or therapy-mediated viral control.

In summary, our study systematically elucidates the cellular and molecular heterogeneity of NK cells during chronic HIV infection. The single-cell atlas of NK cells presented here can also serve as a reference for future studies on NK cells in HIV infection. In addition, our data suggest that NK cells may involve in modulating HIV reservoir decay following ART initiation. These findings support the development of NK cell-based immunotherapeutic approaches,78,79 especially those directed at adaptive-like or transitional NK-cell subsets, and highlight the need to evaluate their impact on reservoir reduction in carefully designed clinical studies. We believe that a detailed understanding of NK cell response during HIV infection could provide a foundation for the development of NK cell-based immunotherapies for the functional cure of HIV.
