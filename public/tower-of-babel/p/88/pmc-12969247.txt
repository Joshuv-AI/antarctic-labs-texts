Results of a Phase 1 Study Assessing the Effect of CIN‐102, a Novel Formulation of the Dopamine Receptor Antagonist Domperidone Designed to Treat Gastroparesis, on Cardiac Repolarization in Healthy Volunteers
Authors: Mary Bond, Brian Murphy, Brendan Doran, Borje Darpo, Hongqi Xue, Leela Vrishabhendra, Jon Isaacsohn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCIN‐102 is a deuterated form of domperidone in development for the treatment of acute, recurrent gastroparesis. This thorough QT study assessed the effects of CIN‐102 on cardiac repolarization in 62 healthy volunteers. In this 4‐period randomized crossover study, participants were administered single doses of 30‐mg CIN‐102 (representing therapeutic exposures), 100‐mg CIN‐102 (representing supratherapeutic exposures), placebo, and moxifloxacin (positive control). Continuous 12‐lead electrocardiograms (ECGs) and time‐matched blood samples were collected to determine plasma levels of deuterated domperidone and its major metabolites. The primary endpoint was placebo‐corrected change‐from‐baseline QTc corrected by Fridericia's formula (ΔΔQTcF). By‐time‐point and concentration‐QTc analyses excluded an effect on ΔΔQTcF exceeding 10 ms for both doses of CIN‐102 at all time points up to deuterated domperidone plasma concentrations of ≈92 ng/mL (≈6 times the therapeutic steady‐state concentration). There were no clinically relevant effects of CIN‐102 on heart rate or cardiac conduction, and no deaths or serious adverse events occurred. This thorough QT study demonstrated that at doses producing therapeutic and supratherapeutic exposures, CIN‐102 does not have a clinically meaningful effect on ECG parameters, including QT interval. This modified formulation of domperidone provides a potential gastroparesis treatment while decreasing the risk of QT prolongation associated with the traditional formulation of domperidone.Keywords: CIN‐102, deuterium, domperidone, gastroparesis, QT prolongation

Gastroparesis is a chronic disorder characterized by impaired gastric emptying in the absence of physical gastric outlet obstruction. Typical symptoms include early satiety, postprandial fullness, nausea, vomiting, upper abdominal discomfort, and bloating. 1 Gastroparesis affects approximately 5 million adult Americans 2 ; however, the actual prevalence may be much higher, as many with gastroparesis‐like symptoms have never had a confirmatory gastric emptying test and remain undiagnosed. 3 , 4

Metoclopramide is a dopamine D2‐receptor antagonist and is the only US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)‐approved agent for the treatment of gastroparesis. 5 However, metoclopramide carries a black‐box warning from the FDA due to the risk of central nervous system side effects, including tardive dyskinesia. 5 Because the risk of tardive dyskinesia increases with duration of treatment and cumulative dosage, treatment with metoclopramide is not recommended for longer than 12 weeks. 6 Another treatment for gastroparesis is erythromycin, which improves gastric emptying; however, the effectiveness of oral erythromycin diminishes with continued use. 5 Antiemetic agents and tricyclic antidepressants can improve associated nausea and vomiting but do not improve gastric emptying. 5

Like metoclopramide, domperidone is a D2‐receptor antagonist but with lower central nervous system side effects than metoclopramide. Domperidone has marketing authorization outside the United States but is available only via physician‐initiated Investigational New Drug protocol in the United States. 5 , 7 A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis found that domperidone was superior to metoclopramide in improvement of gastroparesis symptoms. 8 In addition, domperidone does not readily cross the blood‐brain barrier at therapeutic concentrations, thereby diminishing central nervous system adverse events commonly seen with metoclopramide. 9 However, cardiovascular risks, including arrhythmia, sudden death, and cardiac arrest, have previously been associated with the use of domperidone. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 From a review of the literature, including a thorough QT study and case‐controlled studies with respect to the incidence of QT prolongation and sudden cardiac death, the data appear to indicate that the risk of QT prolongation is related to high plasma levels of domperidone. 10 , 12 , 14 The FDA Expanded Access to Investigational Drugs program website also indicates that increased plasma levels of domperidone are associated with a higher risk of serious events. 7 CIN‐102 is a deuterated form of domperidone formulated with select excipients designed to extend the pharmacokinetic (PK) half‐life to maintain effects during a dosing interval while blunting peak concentrations to decrease the potential for concentration‐related adverse cardiac events. By reducing the maximum observed plasma concentration (Cmax), CIN‐102 has the potential for fewer QT effects at therapeutic doses and, therefore, be a safer alternative for the treatment of gastroparesis. This thorough QT study evaluated potential electrocardiogram (ECG) effects of CIN‐102 doses, producing therapeutic and supratherapeutic exposures of deuterated domperidone and its prevalent circulating metabolites, including concentration‐QTc (C‐QTc) analysis, in healthy volunteers.

Methods

This thorough QT study was designed in accordance with the FDA guidance for the clinical evaluation of QT interval prolongation and proarrhythmic potential for non‐antiarrhythmic drugs 15 and conducted following the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Good Clinical Practice guidelines of the International Council for Harmonization. The study was conducted at Medpace Clinical Pharmacology Unit, a fully owned subsidiary of Medpace, Inc. (Cincinnati, OH, USA) from April 2021 through July 2021. An external committee independent of the sponsor and principal investigator (Advarra Institutional Review Board, Columbia, MD, USA) approved the protocol before enrollment. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before any study procedures were performed.

Participants

Typical inclusion/exclusion criteria for healthy volunteer studies were applied. More specifically, volunteers were eligible if they were between the ages of 18 and 55 years and in good health, as determined by medical and psychiatric history, physical examination, ECG, vital signs, and routine laboratory tests (blood chemistry, hematology, and urinalysis).

Study Design

The study was performed using a crossover approach, so that each participant served as their own control. Each participant received each of the following treatments in a randomized fashion (one treatment per study period):

The study drug was dispensed in a double‐blind (CIN‐102) or open‐label (moxifloxacin) manner. The 30‐mg CIN‐102 dose represents the approximate steady‐state therapeutic Cmax, while 100 mg represents the steady‐state supratherapeutic Cmax (three to six times therapeutic exposure) in patients. Consistent with regulatory guidance, a single dose of each treatment was considered adequate and appropriate to characterize the relationship between plasma concentration and QT interval, as CIN‐102 does not exhibit nonlinear or time‐dependent pharmacokinetics (PK). Moxifloxacin was selected as the positive control because it is known to prolong the QT interval. Participants fasted for a minimum of 8 h before and 4 h after the administration of each treatment. There was a minimum 10‐day washout period between treatments.

Participants checked in to the study center the day prior to dosing and remained in the clinic for approximately 48 h after dosing in each treatment period. Blood samples for PK analysis were obtained prior to and throughout the 48 h following dosing in each period. Participants received a follow‐up telephone call 72 ±24 h after the final treatment period to assess adverse events (AEs) and changes to concomitant medications.

Cardiodynamic Assessments

Continuous 12‐lead ECGs were recorded using a Mortara Surveyor telemetry system starting at least 1 h prior to dosing and ending approximately 48 h postdose. Up to 10 replicates were extracted from the continuous 12‐lead ECG telemetry or Holter system at the following time points: at 3 time points within 1 h prior to each dose (−45, −30, and −15 min) and at 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h after each dose. Participants were required to remain in a supine position for a minimum of 10 min prior to and 5 min following each time point. The central ECG laboratory (ERT/Clario, Philadelphia, PA) used TQT Plus, an advanced computer‐assisted and statistical process, to extract up to 10 ECG replicates from continuous recordings. The QT and preceding respiratory rate for each beat were used for heart rate (HR) correction using Fridericia's formula (QTcF).

The primary endpoint was the placebo‐corrected change from baseline (ΔΔ)QTcF. Secondary endpoints were the (1) change from baseline (Δ)QTcF, ΔHR, ΔPR interval, and ΔQRS duration; (2) ΔΔHR, ΔΔPR interval, and ΔΔQRS duration; (3) categorical outliers for QTcF, HR, PR interval, and QRS duration; and (4) frequency of treatment‐emergent changes of T‐wave morphology and the presence of U waves.

An assessment of hysteresis was based on joint graphical displays of the least‐squares (LS) mean ΔΔQTcF at each postdose time point from the by‐time‐point analysis and the mean concentrations of deuterated domperidone and CIN‐102‐M3 (a prevalent circulating metabolite) at the same time points.

PK Analyses

Blood samples for PK analysis were collected prior to dosing and throughout the treatment period (at the same time points immediately after the ECG extractions). Plasma concentrations of deuterated domperidone, CIN‐102‐M3, and/or moxifloxacin were measured using validated liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry methods by Medpace Bioanalytical Laboratories, LLC (Cincinnati, OH, USA). Based on the results of a subsequent human absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion study, retained samples from this study were assayed for a second prevalent metabolite (CIN‐102‐M1) at a later date. The lower limits of quantitation were 0.01, 10, 0.5, and 25 ng/mL for CIN‐102, CIN‐102‐M3, CIN‐102‐M1, and moxifloxacin, respectively. Plasma PK parameters for deuterated domperidone, CIN‐102‐M3, and CIN‐102‐M1 included Cmax, time to Cmax (Tmax), and area under the plasma concentration–time curve (AUC) from time 0 to 12 h postdose (AUC0‐12h), AUC from time 0 to 24 h postdose (AUC0‐24h), AUC from 0 to 48 h postdose (AUC0‐48h), AUC from time 0 to the last quantifiable plasma concentration (AUC0‐t), apparent first‐order terminal elimination rate constant (λz), and apparent first‐order terminal elimination half‐life (T1/2). Cmax, Tmax, and AUC0‐24h were also calculated for moxifloxacin.

Safety Analyses

Safety was assessed throughout the study based on AEs, vital signs, physical examinations, clinical laboratory evaluations, and ECG findings.

Statistical Analyses

We estimated that a sample size of 44 evaluable participants would provide 90% power to exclude a QTc effect of more than a 10 ms ΔΔQTcF caused by CIN‐102 at clinically relevant plasma levels based on the upper bound of the two‐sided 90% confidence interval of the model‐predicted QTc effect at the observed geometric mean Cmax of deuterated domperidone. The sample size calculation assumed a one‐sided significance level of 0.05, an underlying effect of CIN‐102 of 5 ms, and a standard deviation of 8 ms for ΔQTcF for both CIN‐102 and placebo.

Intensive PK sampling and continuous telemetry were performed to provide high‐quality, well‐controlled, robust, and time‐matched PK and ECG data to support the cardiodynamic analysis. Linear mixed‐effects modeling was used to quantify the relationship between plasma concentrations of deuterated domperidone, its metabolite CIN‐102‐M3, and ΔQTcF. The full model specified ΔQTcF as the dependent variable, with plasma concentrations of deuterated domperidone and CIN‐102‐M3 as explanatory variables; centered baseline QTcF (defined as the individual participant's baseline QTcF minus the population mean for the corresponding period) as a covariate; treatment assignment (active = 1, placebo = 0) and time (i.e., nominal postdose time point) as fixed effects; and random effects on the intercept and slopes for each participant. In all calculations, for participants who received placebo, the concentrations were set to 0. Plasma concentrations of deuterated domperidone and CIN‐102‐M3 below the quantifiable limit after dosing were set to half the lower limit of quantitation in the C‐QTc analysis. An unstructured covariance matrix was specified for the random effects. If model convergence could not be achieved even after appropriate rescaling of the concentration data, the random effect on the slopes and intercept was sequentially removed until convergence was attained. Degrees of freedom were determined using the Kenward–Roger method. From the final model, the slopes (i.e., regression parameters for deuterated domperidone concentrations and CIN‐102‐M3) and the treatment effect–specific intercept (defined as the difference between active and placebo) were estimated along with their two‐sided 90% CI.

Assay sensitivity was evaluated by conducting a C‐QTc analysis of the effect of moxifloxacin on ΔQTcF using a linear mixed‐effects model similar to that used in the primary analysis. Assay sensitivity was considered met if the slope of the C‐QTc relationship/ΔQTcF was statistically significant at 10% level (two‐sided test), and the predicted QT effect (i.e., defined as the lower bound of the two‐sided 90% CI for ΔΔQTcF) exceeded 5 ms at the observed geometric mean Cmax of 400‐mg moxifloxacin. At each postdose time point, the effect of CIN‐102 on ΔΔQTcF, ΔΔHR, ΔΔPR, and ΔΔQRS was evaluated. LS mean, SE, and 90% CI for active and placebo treatment groups were based on a mixed model for repeated measures, with ΔQTcF as the dependent variable; period, sequence, time, treatment (30‐mg CIN‐102, 100‐mg CIN‐102, moxifloxacin, and placebo), and time‐by‐treatment interactions as fixed effects; and baseline QTcF as a covariate. For HR, PR, and QRS intervals, the analysis was based on the change from baseline post‐dosing values (ΔHR, ΔPR, and ΔQRS). The same (by‐time‐point analysis) model was used as described for QTcF. PK parameters were calculated with Phoenix WinNonlin (Certera USA, Inc.) version 8.0 or higher and verified with SAS Version 9.4 or higher. The linear‐log trapezoidal method was used to calculate AUC values. Linear regression of the log concentration versus time was performed using at least three data points to estimate the apparent first‐order terminal elimination constant.

Results

Participants

Sixty‐two participants were randomized and received at least one dose of study drug. Forty‐eight participants received a single dose of all four treatments and 14 participants discontinued early. The primary reasons for early discontinuation were AE (four participants), study terminated by sponsor due to attainment of protocol‐specified study number of completers (four participants), withdrawal by participant (three participants), and loss to follow‐up (one participant).

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics are presented in Table S1. The mean age of participants was approximately 39 years, and the mean body mass index (BMI) of the participants was approximately 26 kg/m2. Baseline ECG parameters were within expectations for a healthy population among treatment groups, with mean HR between 59.9 and 61.0 beats per minute (bpm), mean QTcF between 404.0 and 406.1, mean PR between 152.3 and 153.7 ms, and mean QRS between 103.0 and 103.7 ms.

Pharmacokinetics

A single oral dose of CIN‐102 was rapidly absorbed with a median Tmax of 1.5 to 2 h postdose for both the 30‐ and 100‐mg dose levels (Figure 1 and Table 1). The mean Cmax of deuterated domperidone was approximately 17 and 52 ng/mL for the 30‐ and 100‐mg dose levels, respectively, representing both a therapeutic and supratherapeutic range of exposures. Plasma concentrations of deuterated domperidone generally declined from peak in a biphasic manner with mean T1/2 of approximately 16 to 18 h across treatment groups.

Two prevalent circulating metabolites of CIN‐102 are formed in humans: CIN‐102‐M1 and CIN‐102‐M3. Both metabolites are pharmacologically inactive and have demonstrated no QT liability based on human ether‐à‐go‐go‐related gene (HERG) data where the IC50 was not detected at the highest concentration tested for CIN‐102‐M3 (100 µM, which is equivalent to approximately 52,500 ng/mL) or CIN‐102‐M1 (30 µM, which is equivalent to approximately 6300 ng/mL). The plasma concentrations of both metabolites were measured, and PK parameters were calculated in this study due to their prevalence. CIN‐102‐M3 was formed rapidly with a median Tmax of 2 h postdose for both treatment groups (Figure 1 and Table 1). The mean Cmax of CIN‐102‐M3 was approximately 83 and 248 ng/mL for the 30‐ and 100‐mg dose levels, respectively, again representing a broad range of exposures. The half‐life of CIN‐102‐M3 ranged from approximately 2 to 3 h for both treatment groups. CIN‐102‐M1 also formed rapidly with a median Tmax of 1.5 h postdose. The mean Cmax of CIN‐102‐M1 was approximately 16 and 39 ng/mL for the 30‐ and 100‐mg dose levels, respectively. Overall, the observed plasma concentrations for all analytes cover a broad range appropriate for the assessment of the effects of CIN‐102 on the QT interval.

Cardiodynamics

The LS mean ΔQTcF of CIN‐102 was similar to placebo at all postdose time points, with a somewhat larger effect in the 100‐mg treatment group between 3 and 6 h postdose (Figure 2A). The LS mean ΔΔQTcF of 30‐mg CIN‐102 was between −0.3 and 1.8 ms at 0.5 and 2 hours, respectively.The LS mean ΔΔQTcF of 100‐mg CIN‐102 was between −0.1 and 3.7 ms at 0.5 h and 3 h postdose, respectively (Figure 2B). The upper bound of the 90% CI of ΔΔQTcF was <10 ms for both CIN‐102 doses at all time points, demonstrating that an effect on ΔΔQTcF exceeding 10 ms can be excluded for both doses. After dosing with 400‐mg oral moxifloxacin, a clear increase of LS mean ΔΔQTcF was observed with a peak value of 13.3 ms (90% CI, 11.52–15.00) at 3 h postdose. The largest effect of 100‐mg CIN‐102 on ΔΔQTcF (LS mean: 3.7 ms [90% CI, 1.95–5.41]) was not observed at or near the Tmax of the deuterated domperidone (mean 1.95 h [SD 1.06]) or CIN‐102‐M3 (mean 2.28 h [SD 0.97]) (Figure 3).

No participants had a QTcF >480 ms or a ΔQTcF >30 ms. There were six participants at 19 time points after moxifloxacin administration and one participant at one time point after placebo administration with QTcF >450 ms and ≤480 ms. No participants met the criteria for prolonged QTcF in the CIN‐102 treatment groups.

There were no clinically relevant effects of CIN‐102 on HR, PR interval, or QRS duration. The LS mean ΔHR for CIN‐102 was similar to placebo, and the LS mean ΔΔHR following CIN‐102 dosing ranged within ±1.4 bpm, from −1.4 to 1.1 bpm (at 3 and 24 h postdose, respectively, both in the 30‐mg treatment group).The LS mean ΔΔPR varied between ±2.3 ms, from 1.3 to −2.3 ns at 8 and 24 h postdose, respectively, and the LS mean ΔΔQRS was within ±1.1 ms across all postdose time points. Two participants had flat T waves following 100‐mg dose of CIN‐102.

By visual inspection of LS mean ΔΔQTcF and the deuterated domperidone and CIN‐102‐M3 plasma concentrations across time, hysteresis was ruled out, and additional C‐QTc models (such as a model with an effect compartment) were not explored.

Three models to predict the QT effect were explored: a full model with both deuterated domperidone and CIN‐102‐M3 (Model A), a model with deuterated domperidone alone (Model B), and a model with CIN‐102‐M3 alone (Model C). Based on the Pearson correlation coefficient calculation, the Pearson correlation coefficient between deuterated domperidone and CIN‐102‐M3 was 0.813 (<0.95), and these two analytes can therefore be included in the same C‐QTc model. Scatter plots of deuterated domperidone and CIN‐102‐M3 concentrations versus ΔQTcF, including both a linear regression line and a locally weighted scatter plot smoothing regression line (90% CI), were used to assess the assumption of a linear C‐QTc relationship. The model with CIN‐102‐M3 alone was selected as the primary due to the smallest Akaike information criterion value. These data indicated that a linear model of the C‐QTc relationship may appropriately capture the observed data. The modeling effort was not repeated with the CIN‐102‐M1 data, given the qualitative and quantitative similarity to the parent compound and the lack of QT liability observed with this metabolite based on the (HERG) data (data on file).

The model with CIN‐102‐M3 (Model C) was selected as primary. Based on the C‐QTc analysis (Figure 4A), an effect on ΔΔQTcF exceeding 10 ms can be excluded up to deuterated domperidone plasma concentrations of ≈92 ng/mL (representing approximately six times the therapeutic steady‐state Cmax of deuterated domperidone) and the effect on ΔΔQTcF can be predicted to 1.10 ms (90% CI, 0.58–1.61) and 3.50 ms (90% CI, 2.18–4.82) for 30‐mg and 100‐mg treatment groups, respectively (Table 2). The C‐QTc analysis of CIN‐102‐M3 (Figure 4B) demonstrated that an effect on ΔΔQTcF exceeding 10 ms can be excluded up to plasma concentrations of ≈480 ng/mL (representing approximately five times the therapeutic steady‐state Cmax of CIN‐102‐M3), and the effect on ΔΔQTcF can be predicted to 1.43 ms (90% CI, 0.90–1.96) and 3.58 ms (90% CI, 2.34–4.83) for 30‐mg and 100‐mg treatment groups, respectively (Table 3). The slope of the C‐QTc relationship for moxifloxacin was statistically significant and the lower bound of the 90% CI of the model‐predicted effect on ΔΔQTcF exceeded 5 ms at the geometric mean peak concentration (1552.6 ng/mL), demonstrating assay sensitivity (Figure 4C).

Safety

There were no deaths or serious AEs during the study. Overall, one participant experienced an AE that was considered related to CIN‐102 (Table 4). This AE of ventricular tachycardia occurred in the 30‐mg CIN‐102 group and was considered moderate in severity. Overall, three participants experienced an AE that led to the discontinuation of the study drug: one participant after receiving 30‐mg CIN‐102 (ventricular tachycardia), and two participants after receiving 400‐mg moxifloxacin (ventricular tachycardia in one participant and nausea, vomiting, and rash in the other). There were no clinically meaningful changes in vital signs, physical examinations, or QTcF on 12‐lead safety ECGs during the study and no AEs related to abnormal vital signs.

Discussion

The current first‐line agent for treating gastroparesis is metoclopramide; however, modest efficacy and risk of severe central nervous system side effects hamper its use in disease management. 5 While domperidone is an effective prokinetic and antiemetic, 8 it is currently only available in the US through an investigator‐initiated Investigational New Drug application. 5 , 7 A 2017 study evaluated survey results from 1423 adult patients with gastroparesis and found that 23% to 28% of patients were satisfied with current prokinetic medications and that AEs with these medications were common (34%–63% of at least moderate severity). 16 This study also found that more than half of the patients ranked their health as fair or poor, and 30.4% of patients were not working because of their gastroparesis. 16 The need for better tolerated and effective gastroparesis treatments remains an unmet need and an opportunity for developing novel treatment options. 17

The risk of sudden cardiac death due to QTc prolongation with the use of domperidone is increased at high exposures. 18 CIN‐102 is a deuterated form of domperidone with selected excipients in which four hydrogen atoms on the aromatic ring have been substituted with deuterium atoms, allowing a more stable bond with carbon. 19 , 20 , 21 This novel formulation was designed with the goal of lowering Cmax, while maintaining therapeutically effective AUC values in an effort to reduce the risk of adverse cardiac effects. These formulation modifications do not affect D2 receptor antagonist activity. The original formulation of domperidone blocks cardiac potassium channels, 22 prolongs action potential duration, 23 and alters the stability of HR. 18 Available HERG data confirm the potential QT effects of deuterated domperidone; however, no potential QT liability was observed for the prevalent metabolites (CIN‐102‐M3 and CIN‐102‐M1; data on file).

This thorough QT study evaluated potential ECG effects of therapeutic and supratherapeutic exposures of deuterated domperidone and its CIN‐102‐M3 metabolite, including C‐QTc analysis, in healthy volunteers. At the 30‐mg dose, deuterated domperidone reached plasma concentrations comparable to the Cmax in patients with gastroparesis receiving the current maximum therapeutic dose. At the 100‐mg dose, deuterated domperidone reached a mean Cmax approximately three times the maximum concentration reached with the maximum therapeutic dose, representing high clinical exposure to the drug that may occur when the maximum therapeutic dose is increased by intrinsic (e.g., renal or liver impairment) or extrinsic (e.g., drug interaction or food effect). 24 The observed exposures covered a broad range (up to six times the therapeutic steady‐state concentration) appropriate for the assessment of the effects of CIN‐102 and CIN‐102‐M3 on the QT interval. These two CIN‐102 doses allowed us to understand the relationship between plasma concentrations of deuterated domperidone and the QT interval across the full continuum of clinically relevant exposures. Because a thorough QT study is intended as an initial assessment of a drug's potential to prolong QT and to inform the need for ECG monitoring in subsequent patient trials, it is typically conducted in healthy subjects with fewer potential confounding factors and without an increased baseline risk of arrhythmias. This approach allows for a clear characterization of the relationship between plasma concentrations and associated changes in QT, or lack thereof.

The highest mean plasma concentrations for deuterated domperidone occurred at 1.5 and 2 h postdose for the 30‐mg and 100‐mg treatment groups, respectively. The highest mean plasma concentrations of CIN‐102‐M3 were observed at 2 h postdose for both the 30‐mg and 100‐mg treatment groups, and the highest mean plasma concentrations of CIN‐102‐M1 were observed at 1.5 h postdose for the 30‐mg and 100‐mg treatment groups. The LS mean ΔΔQTcF within the first 4 h of dosing ranged from −0.3 to 1.8 ms for the 30‐mg dose at 0.5 and 2 h postdose and −0.1 ms to 3.7 ms at 0.5 and 3 h postdose for the 100‐mg dose. The threshold level for regulatory concern is approximately 5 ms with an upper bound 95% CI of 10 ms, 15 and CIN‐102 fell below this threshold at both doses. Moxifloxacin exhibited a lower bound CI above 5 ms at the geometric mean Cmax, meeting the criteria as a positive control and validating the assay sensitivity. No participants in the CIN‐102 treatment groups met the criteria for prolonged QTcF (>450 ms). However, six participants in the positive control group did meet the criteria for prolonged QTcF, further validating the sensitivity of our analysis. CIN‐102 did not have a clinically relevant effect on HR or cardiac conduction (i.e., PR interval and QRS duration).

Single oral doses of 30‐ and 100‐mg CIN‐102 were safe and well tolerated by healthy volunteers. The most frequent treatment‐emergent AEs were ventricular tachycardia in three participants (one participant following 30‐mg CIN‐102 and two participants following 400‐mg moxifloxacin) and nausea in three participants (two participants following moxifloxacin and one participant following placebo). The incident of ventricular tachycardia following 30‐mg CIN‐102 was noted on continuous telemetry approximately 26 h postdose (plasma deuterated domperidone concentrations were 3.03 and 1.97 ng/mL at 24 and 36 h postdose, respectively; plasma CIN‐102‐M3 concentrations were below the lower limit of quantification for both 24 and 36 h postdose; Cmax was 11.10 and 32.1 ng/mL for plasma deuterated domperidone and CIN‐102‐M3, respectively). No treatment was needed, and the event was considered resolved on the same date. Nausea is also a common side effect of moxifloxacin. 25

Cardiovascular risks, including arrhythmia, sudden death, and cardiac arrest, have previously been associated with the use of domperidone. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 From a review of the literature, including a thorough QT study and case‐controlled studies with respect to the incidence of QT prolongation and sudden cardiac death, the data appear to indicate that the risk of QT prolongation is related to high plasma levels of domperidone. 10 , 12 , 14 CIN‐102 was designed to lower peak plasma concentration to reduce the risk of adverse cardiac effects related to high plasma concentrations of domperidone while maintaining the blockade of D2 receptors. The results of this study demonstrate the magnitude of the safety margin for QT prolongation with this novel formulation.

Conclusions

Following the administration of CIN‐102, the mean effect on the QTcF interval did not exceed 5 ms, constituting a negative thorough QT/QTc study. 15 At clinically relevant exposures, this modified/deuterated formulation of domperidone has achieved its goal of providing a potential treatment for gastroparesis while decreasing the risk of QT prolongation associated with the traditional/non‐deuterated formulation of domperidone. These findings strongly support the continued clinical development of CIN‐102 for the treatment and relief of symptoms associated with acute and recurrent gastroparesis.
