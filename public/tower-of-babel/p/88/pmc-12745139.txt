Thirty years of experience with full-root Ross procedure in the pediatric population—Timing matters!
Authors: Viktoria HM Weixler, Alison J Howell, Bana Samman, Makaela Milton, Gloria Ho, Kyle Runeckles, Mimi X Deng, Christoph Haller, Rachel D Vanderlaan, David J Barron, Osami Honjo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe optimal timing of Ross procedure in pediatric patients remains unclear. We aimed to analyze how age, diagnosis, autograft support, and Konno-incision impact outcome.MethodsPatients (0-18 years) undergoing primary/secondary Ross/Ross-Konno from August 1993 to November 2023 were included. Mortality, autograft replacement rate, and greater than mild autograft regurgitation were analyzed in the full cohort and subgroups (age, diagnosis, primary/secondary Ross, Ross/Ross-Konno, autograft support) using time-to-event methods. Propensity-score matching was performed to adjust for differences in baseline characteristics of patients undergoing supported/nonsupported Ross procedures.ResultsIn total, 196 patients underwent Ross/Ross-Konno procedures (N = 117/79; 60/40%) with median age/weight of 9.4 years [interquartile range, 3.4-14.1 years]/31.7 kg [12.9-57 kg] and median follow-up of 8.8 years [1.6-14.8 years]. Twenty-year freedom from death, autograft replacement rate, and rate of greater than mild autograft regurgitation was 88.7% (95% confidence interval, 79.8%-93.8%), 13.2% (7.1%-24.7%), and 17.7% (11.3%-27.8%), respectively. Lowest 20-year freedom from death was observed among neonates/infants 47.6% (7.5%-80.8%)/58.9% (34.5%-76.8%) compared with older patients (97.6%; 90.6%-99.4%, P < .001). In total, 83.3% of all deceased neonates/infants had associated mitral valve disease. Patients with isolated aortic valve stenosis and/or Konno-incision were at greater mortality risk (P = .02). 20-year autograft replacement rate was highest among adolescents (12-18 years): 30.7% (15.5-60.9), without statistical significance (P = .08), and greater in secondary Ross: 23.8% (11.4-49.7, P = .03). Matched pairs (supported vs nonsupported Ross, n = 30 each) did not show differences in primary outcomes.ConclusionsThe Ross procedure in pediatric patients demonstrated good long-term outcomes. Mortality risk is greater in neonates/infants and patients with isolated aortic stenosis. Adolescent patients/patients undergoing secondary Ross procedures were associated with greater autograft replacement rates.Key Words: Ross operation, autograft support, congenital aortic valve disease

The best timing of the Ross procedure is between 1-12 years of age.

Central Message

The Ross procedure has the lowest mortality and at the same time lowest risk for autograft replacements when done after 1 year of age and before reaching adolescence.

Perspective

The Ross procedure can be performed safely, with excellent early-, mid-, and long-term outcomes. Choosing the right timing for surgery is essential, considering the greatest mortality is found in the neonates and infants and the greatest risk for autograft replacements in adolescents and patients undergoing secondary Ross procedures.

The Ross procedure is the first-line treatment for pediatric aortic valve disease, given the autograft's growth potential, long-term durability, favorable hemodynamics, and avoidance of anticoagulation.1 Despite potential benefits, the long-term fate of the pulmonary autograft in aortic position, especially in growing children, remains unclear, because the procedure only entered clinical practice in the mid-to-late 1990s to early 2000s. Some studies propose that the Ross procedure should be performed early so that the autograft can grow with the patient and the autograft leaflets can adapt to the high-pressure circulation.2,3 Others suggest postponing the surgery into adolescence/adulthood, which allows for autograft support.4,5 Although an increase of autograft dimensions over time in growing children has been observed, it is difficult to differentiate whether those changes are somatic growth or pathologic dilatation.6 Furthermore, it is unclear whether autograft dilatation automatically leads to leaflet malcoaptation and autograft regurgitation (AR).7 Autograft support has been suggested to prevent autograft dilatation8; however, this strategy cannot be applied to growing children.

Because the overall survival of children with critical aortic valve disease has been improved, our interest has shifted to delineate the best age, timing, and optimal candidacy for the Ross procedure to achieve greater durability of the autograft. The aim of this study was to identify the major determinants for important clinical outcomes (ie, death, autograft replacement, autograft dilatation/regurgitation), with special interest on age at surgery, diagnosis, timing (primary vs secondary), autograft support, and Konno incision. In addition, propensity-score matching was performed to analyze the impact of autograft support on AR and replacement rates. Furthermore, we investigated changes in autograft dimensions over time and tried to understand whether there is a connection between autograft growth or dilatation and degree of regurgitation.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Population

After institutional review ethics board approval (no. 1000068502; approval date: March 2, 2020) a retrospective study was conducted and informed consent was waived. All pediatric patients (0-18 years) undergoing primary or secondary (Ross procedure as first surgical intervention or after initially aortic valve repair) Ross/Ross-Konno procedures from August 1993 to November 2023 were included.

Surgical Techniques

The full-root autograft implantation technique was used in all cases as described previously.9 Konno incision was performed in cases of a small native aortic root and/or left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO). The pulmonary autograft was either implanted nonsupported or supported externally by various materials, such as GoreTex (W. L. Gore & Associates, Inc), Dacron, Teflon (The Chemours Company) strip, or autologous pericardium, in selected cases. Various types of right ventricle to pulmonary artery (RV-PA) conduits from aortic/pulmonary homografts, heterografts, or porcine valved conduits were used.

End Points

Primary end points were mortality and postoperative complications. Early mortality was defined as mortality during the same hospitalization, and late mortality is death after hospital discharge. Major complications (eg, renal failure requiring dialysis, pacemaker implantation, neurologic impairment, mechanical circulatory support, phrenic nerve injury, bleeding requiring re-exploration), length of ventilation, intensive care unit, and hospital stays were noted.

Secondary end points were the incidence of autograft replacements and AR greater than mild and change in autograft dimensions over time. Valve-sparing neoaortic valve repairs after initial Ross were noted as well but not included in the analysis.

Preoperative, early postoperative, and last-available echocardiograms were reviewed and analyzed by a specialized pediatric cardiologist (A.J.H.). All study end points/outcome measures were analyzed within the whole cohort as well as subgroups by (1) age groups (ie, <1 month, 1 month to 1 year, 1-12 years, >12-18 years), (2) primary or secondary Ross, (3) diagnosis (ie, isolated aortic valve stenosis (AS), isolated AR, or combined aortic valve disease), (4) Ross/Ross-Konno procedure, and (5) supported versus nonsupported Ross procedures. All study end points were additionally analyzed in propensity-matched groups of patients who underwent supported versus nonsupported Ross procedures.

Echocardiographic Analysis

Echocardiographic data were collected from a subset of patients who had digitally stored images available for analysis (SyngoDynamics; n = 156/196). Autograft dimensions were measured in centimeters and z score at 4 different aortic levels: annular, sinotubular junction, aortic sinus, and ascending aorta. Change in autograft dimensions were assessed from the initial postoperative study to last follow-up.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize clinical characteristics, with continuous data presented as medians and first/third quartiles and categorical data as frequencies (%). Between-group (age group at surgery, diagnosis, autograft support, primary vs secondary Ross) differences in continuous and categorical variables were assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum and Fisher exact tests, respectively. The Kaplan-Meier survival method was applied to estimate survival rates, and log-rank tests were used to compare the survival curves between groups. The Gray subdistribution method was applied to characterize (1) autograft reoperation and (2) AR greater than mild regarding cumulative incidence rates. Death was considered as a competing risk. Between-group differences in the cumulative incidence rates were evaluated using the Gray tests. Administrative censoring at 20 years was applied to all time-to-event analyses. Propensity score matching was used to compare patients who underwent supported versus nonsupported procedures. Propensity scores of autograft support type were estimated using logistic regression, including age, body surface area, and diagnosis in the model. Upon estimation of propensity scores, patients undergoing supported/nonsupported Ross procedures were matched on a 1:1 basis. We used standardized mean differences to quantify and assess covariate imbalance of the match-paired sample. To account for the pairing, the effect of the autograft support was quantified and evaluated using the stratified log-rank test for mortality, and stratified Gray tests for autograft replacements and AR greater than mild. Change in autograft dimensions (initial postoperative study to last follow-up) was assessed using linear regression. The overall effect of age group (neonates, infants, 1-12 years, >12-18 years) was evaluated using the Wald test. The reference category for the age group was 1 month or less. Statistical analysis was performed using R, version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Baseline Characteristics and Preoperative Procedures

A total of 196 patients underwent Ross (60%; n = 117/196)/Ross-Konno (40%; n = 79/196) procedures. Median age and weight were 9.4 years [interquartile range, 3.4-14.1 years] and 31.7 kg [12.9-57 kg], respectively. Thirty-two patients (16.3%) were younger than the age of 1 year. The diagnosis was combined aortic valve disease in most patients (68; n = 133/196), with more than one half (65%) initially undergoing balloon valvuloplasty and 46 patients (23.5%) having previous surgical aortic valve repairs. The remaining 150 patients (76.5%) underwent a primary Ross procedure. There was no difference in baseline characteristics among the subgroups except for age and weight/height/body surface area being greater in the supported Ross group and lower in the Ross-Konno and the isolated AS groups (P < .01). Mitral valve disease/Shone complex was present in 20.4% (40/196) of all patients and in one half of neonates/infants (16/32) (Tables 1 and 2, Table E1).

Intraoperative and Early Postoperative Outcome

Median cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and aortic crossclamp (ACC) times were 188 minutes [157-238 minutes] and 150 minutes [128-183 minutes], with no differences between the patients who had supported (n = 33) versus nonsupported Ross procedures (n = 163; P > .30) and Ross versus Ross-Konno procedures (P > .40). Secondary versus primary Ross procedures had longer CPB durations (216 minutes [170-255] vs 179 minutes [156-230], P = .03). CPB and ACC times were the longest in neonates and infants compared to older patients (P < .01). Patients with isolated AS had a greater incidence of LVOTO and Konno-incision (80%, 31/39) compared with patients with isolated AR or combined disease (P < .01). Neonates and infants had a greater incidence of LVOTO and Konno incision compared with older children (P < .01). Patients undergoing a supported Ross procedure had fewer Konno incisions compared with the nonsupported cohort (21% vs 44%, P = .02). Thirty-three patients (33/196; 16.8%) underwent autograft-supported Ross procedures, for which different materials were used, with Dacron being the most frequent (23/33, 70%), We used full inclusion/wrapping for the most patients (24/33; 72.7%). Nine patients received annular stabilization with a Teflon strip (9/33; 27.3%) (Table 1, Table E2).

Hospital mortality in the entire cohort was 4.6% (9/196) with the majority (66.7%) being neonates or infants. 83.3% (10/12) of all deceased neonates/infants had associated mitral valve disease. Neonates and infants and patients with isolated AS showed longer ventilation times, intensive care unit and hospital stays compared with other groups (P < .02). Postoperative complications such as neurologic injury was highest in the neonates and infants (P < .01, Table 3, Tables E3 and E4). Patients undergoing primary versus secondary Ross procedures did not differ in postoperative variables.

Midterm Outcome

Survival

Median follow-up time among all patients was 8.8 years [1.6-14.8]. There were 9 hospital deaths and 7 late deaths (see Table E5 for more details causes of death). Most deceased patients were younger than 1 year of age (12/16; 75%). The 10- and 20-year survival rates in the entire cohort were 91.5% [86.2%-94.8%] and 88.7% [79.8%-93.8%] (Figure 1, A). When we compared different age groups, neonatal and infant groups (<1 month and <1 year) showed significantly lower survival than other groups (1-12 years, >12-18 years) (P < .01) (Figure 1, B). Patients with isolated AS demonstrated significantly lower survival than patients with other diagnosis (P = .02) (Figure 1, C). Patients undergoing Ross-Konno procedure demonstrated worse survival than patients after the Ross procedure (P < .01) (Figure 1, D). Patients undergoing primary versus secondary Ross did not show any survival differences.

Autograft Durability and Regurgitation

A total of 11 patients received autograft replacements after the initial Ross operation, and another 6 patients underwent valve-sparing neoaortic valve repairs. The 10- and 20-year cumulative incidence of autograft replacements within the entire cohort were 4.7% [2.2-10.4] and 13.2% [7.1-24.7] (Figure 2, A). Autograft replacement rate was greater after secondary Ross procedure than primary (P = .03), and greater in Ross-procedure compared with Ross-Konno procedure (P = .04). Autograft replacement rate was the greatest in adolescent patients, without statistical significance (P = .08) (Figures 2, B-D). Autograft replacement rate did not differ by autograft support or by diagnosis (P > .3). There was no difference in autograft replacement rate among adolescent patients who underwent supported versus nonsupported Ross: 27.3% [7.7%, 97.2%] versus 30.1% [13.9%, 65.4%] (P = .8). The 10- and 20-year cumulative incidence of greater than mild AR within the entire cohort was 11.5% [7.2-18.4] and 17.7% [11.3-27.8] and did not differ between any of the subgroups. At last follow-up, most patients (163/187; 87.2%) had none or mild AR. A total of 24 patients had either moderate (n = 20) or severe (n = 4) AR at last follow-up.

RV-PA Conduit Reoperations/Reinterventions

The 10- and 20-year cumulative incidence of surgical RV-PA conduit replacements in the entire cohort was 2.5% [0.8-7.7] and 19.9% [11.6-34.2].

Echocardiographic Analysis

When we compared different age groups, autograft dimensions measured at different levels increased the most in the neonatal group (<1 month of age) with the largest change (from postoperative to last follow-up) found in aortic valve annulus size. Average change at annular level in the neonatal group was 0.7 ± 0.3 cm compared with significantly lower changes in other age groups (P < .01), and z score on average increased by +3.2 [0.9-5.4] compared with the other age groups (P = .20, Figure 3).

Propensity-Score Matching: Supported Versus Nonsupported Ross Procedure

Our 1:1 propensity-score matching (supported vs nonsupported Ross) created 30 well-matched pairs with acceptable covariate imbalance (Figure E1). The 10- and 20-year cumulative incidence of autograft replacements did not differ between patients who received supported versus nonsupported Ross procedures: 9.1% [1.4%-58.9%] and 27.3% [7.7%-97.2%] versus 5.1% [1.3%-19.7%] and 30.1% [13.9%-65.4%]; P = .3. Survival and AR rates were equally comparable between matched pairs (P > .5).

Discussion

The study evaluated several factors such as age, surgical timing, and diagnosis influencing medium to long-term mortality and autograft durability after the Ross procedure. The major findings are as follows: (1) neonates and infants have a greater risk of mortality; (2) adolescents have higher autograft replacement rates; (3) secondary Ross is associated with a higher autograft replacement rate compared to primary Ross; and (4) autograft support did not prevent autograft replacement. Although autograft dimensions increased the most in neonates/infants, it is not linked to greater rates of AR.

Survival

This study found that early survival is highly dependent on age at surgery, which is consistent with other studies. Although overall hospital mortality in the entire cohort was 4.6% (9/196), neonates and infants had a 10 times greater early mortality (18.8%, 6/32) compared with older children (1.8%, 3/164, P < .001). Other studies report similar operative mortality in neonatal and infantile Ross procedures ranging from 7% to 28%.10, 11, 12, 13 This result can be explained by several factors. First, the myocardium at this age is more vulnerable to stress and ischemia from the relatively lengthy procedure.14 In addition, many of these patients have pre-existing left ventricular dysfunction and/or endocardial fibroelastosis.13 Second, critical AS in neonates and infants is often associated with mitral valve disease/Shone complex, which was present in 50% of our neonatal/infant cohort and is a strong predictor for poor outcomes (>80% deceased in our study).15 Lastly, neonatal Ross can be technically more challenging; however, given the few other options in this fragile group, it might be the last resource. It is important to note that the Ross procedure in neonates and infants becomes safer as the expertise level increases and when done at highly specialized centers. In our cohort, we found a decrease in early mortality from the first era (1993-2007) compared with the second (2008-2023): 36.4% (4/11) versus 9.5% (2/21), P = .10.

The 10-/15-/20-year overall freedom from mortality in our cohort was excellent (91.5%/91.5%/88.7%), and consistent with other reports (90%-94.4%/80.9%-94.4%/94.4%).16, 17, 18 The study showed greater mortality rates after Ross-Konno procedures compared with Ross procedures (P < .001) and in patients with isolated AS compared with AR or combined aortic valve disease (P = .02), which can probably be explained by the fact that the high operative risk seen in neonates/infants with a small aortic annulus and/or LVOTO. We did not find a difference in survival based on the timing of surgery (primary vs secondary Ross), which is different from Buratto and colleagues,16 who suggested a survival benefit after secondary Ross.

Autograft Replacement

The study demonstrated a similar 20-year overall incidence for autograft replacements (13.2% [7.1-24.7]) compared with literature of around 84.9% [61.2-94.7].17 Secondary was found to have a significantly greater rate of autograft replacement (P = .03) compared with primary Ross, which is different to other group's findings indicating that the secondary Ross is associated with a lower autograft replacement rate due to the presumed protective effect of the scar tissue.16 One reason for this difference could be that in the aforementioned study, they matched patients undergoing primary versus secondary Ross procedure to overcome differences in baseline characteristics. As we used unmatched secondary versus primary Ross-groups, the secondary Ross patients were slightly older, which may have contributed to the greater autograft replacement rate. This goes along with the study by Nelson and colleagues,18 who also found the greatest rate of autograft reoperations in the adolescent patients and the lowest rate in infant and neonate patients, which they explained by the similarity in histology of pulmonary and aortic valve at birth leading to a more durable autograft. On contrary, Buratto and colleagues19 showed a significantly lower autograft reoperation rate in patients between 10 and 18 years old (87.8% ± 9.0%) compared with younger children (infants: 58.4% ± 16.2%). They explained the benefit of delaying the Ross procedure into adolescence is having the option to support the autograft, which they believed is important to protect from dilatation and reoperation. In our study at this point, no differences between supported versus nonsupported Ross procedures in terms of autograft replacement risk were found (P = .30). The majority of patients aged between 12 and 18 years old underwent supported Ross procedures in our cohort (n = 24, 73%) and when comparing just this subgroup of patients in terms of autograft support, there was no difference in autograft replacements between the groups (P = .80). However, numbers of supported Ross procedures within the entire group are low (33/196; 16.8%) and follow-up times in the supported group were rather short (median of 4.3 years); therefore, no definite conclusions can be drawn regarding the impact of autograft support.

Autograft Dilatation/Change in Dimensions Over Time

Echocardiographic analysis showed that autograft dimensions increase the most in neonates. That being said, they also demonstrated to have the lowest rate of autograft replacements and did not show any significant differences in the rate of greater than mild AR compared with other groups. Other groups7,10 also suggested that even though neonates and infants show a greater increase in autograft dimensions over time, this does not necessarily result in autograft dysfunction, and it is still uncertain whether this increase is somatic growth or pathologic dilatation.6 Interestingly, in our study, neonates not only showed an increase in autograft dimensions measured in centimeters but also an increase in z scores, suggesting that it is not only growth but also some degree of dilatation, which seems to not affect autograft function.

Limitations

This study has several limitations because of its retrospective uncontrolled design. One important limitation is the difference in follow-up durations among age groups, with the shortest in the neonates and infants. One might argue that with longer follow-up greater rates of autograft replacements could have been noticed, even though Nelson and colleagues18 had not found them either with median follow-up of >10 years. Given the fact that neonates/infants undergoing Ross operations are usually critically ill patients with hemodynamic compromises, comparing them with patients undergoing elective Ross procedures might not be fair. Another limitation is that surgical practice and expertise have changed dramatically over a 30-year study period, which potentially influenced the results. Also, the different techniques of autograft support used should be considered a limitation as it makes comparison between patients undergoing supported versus nonsupported Ross procedures hard.

Conclusions

The Ross operation in the pediatric population can be performed with overall good early- and long-term survival and low autograft replacement rates. Neonates and infants are at greater risk, especially for early mortality. However, given the fact that the Ross operation might be their only option, it should still be considered, especially looking at their favorable long-term outcome with low autograft replacement rates. Adolescent patients, in contrast, demonstrated greater rates of autograft replacements. If possible, ideal timing for the Ross operation appears to be beyond the first year of life and before reaching adolescence.
