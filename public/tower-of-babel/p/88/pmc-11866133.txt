A case of flexor tenosynovitis caused by Cyberlindnera rhodanensis – First reported human infection
Authors: Sarah Kim, Stephanie Spivack
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report a case of an immunocompetent individual who was diagnosed with fungal tenosynovitis of the wrist caused by Cyberlindnera rhodanensis, a yeast in the Saccharomycetales order that has never been reported to cause infections in humans. The yeast grew in routine culture and was sent to a reference laboratory for identification and susceptibility testing. The patient ultimately received six surgeries and five months of antifungal therapy, including micafungin and voriconazole, before clinical resolution.Keywords: Tenosynovitis, Emerging pathogen, Cyberlindnera rhodanensis, Saccharomycetes, Saccharomycetales

Introduction

Fungal tenosynovitis is very uncommon, and most reported cases have occurred in immunocompromised individuals. Recognized causative pathogens include Candida several species (spp.), Cryptococcus, and dimorphic fungi including Histoplasma and Coccidioides. We report a case of flexor tenosynovitis caused by Cyberlindnera rhodanensis, which has never been reported to cause disease in humans. C. rhodanensis has been reported as a cause of bovine mastitis, and other species of Cyberlindnera have been reported as an uncommon cause of human disease. This case highlights an index case of a potential emerging fungal pathogen.

Case presentation

A 58-year-old male presented to the orthopedic surgery clinic (day 0) with swelling in his right wrist and numbness in his right hand that had slowly worsened over one year. He had been seen by orthopedic surgery two years prior (day −724) for a mass near his right elbow which was felt to be a ganglion cyst on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Surgical excision was planned but cancelled due to the COVID-19 pandemic and the elbow lesion resolved without intervention. Seven months prior to presentation (day −269), he was treated as an inpatient for COVID-19 with remdesivir, baricitinib, and steroids.

His medical history was significant for hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and migraines; he had no prior surgeries. His medications included hydrochlorothiazide, atorvastatin, aspirin, and bumetanide. He did not use tobacco, alcohol, or other illicit drugs. He was originally from Puerto Rico and had no recent travel. He worked in construction but did not recall any injuries or abrasions to his wrist or hand. He had no exposure to animals. He denied a history of recurrent infections.

His physical exam on day 0 revealed swelling in the volar ulnar aspect of the wrist. An ultrasound of the area did not show a definitive mass. His symptoms did not improve with conservative management, and an MRI was performed on day 202. This revealed tenosynovitis involving all the long flexor tendons, myositis, a 2 × 1.3 centimeter (cm) thick-walled septated fluid collection in the hypothenar musculature, edema of the entire hamate, and erosion of the hook of the hamate (Fig. 1). He underwent right wrist flexor tenosynovectomy on day 264 and was found to have tenosynovitis of all nine flexor tendons, with a significant amount of synovium and rice bodies that were removed.

MRI of R wrist showing tenosynovitis. Axial T1 post view with synovial thickening of flexor tendons.

Our hospital's microbiology lab identified a yeast in routine aerobic culture on sheep's blood agar, but the MALDI-TOF MS system failed to identify the organism. The isolate was sent to Mayo Clinic, where MALDI-TOF MS again was unable to identify the organism. D2 rRNA gene Sanger sequencing was used, and the organism was identified as Cyberlindnera rhodanensis. The sequence was 309 base pairs in length and was a 100 percent match to the NCBI GenBank entry for C. rhodanensis (GenBank accession number AB772186.1). Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) testing was performed, though no interpretations were available (Table 1). This was regarded as a contaminant and not treated.

After the surgery, the patient continued to have swelling in the wrist. He was treated with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), a prednisone taper, three triamcinolone injections, a wrist brace, and a fiberglass cast, without improvement.

Seven months after the first surgery and fourteen months after the initial presentation (day 468), he was admitted to the hospital with worsening swelling. Computed tomography (CT) imaging showed severe tenosynovitis and a multi-loculated thick-walled abscess in the right distal forearm (Fig. 2). He underwent incision and drainage on day 469 with gross purulence and necrotic tissue noted in the operating room (OR). He was discharged with empiric cefuroxime, doxycycline and ciprofloxacin. At his one-week postoperative visit, his aerobic cultures were growing Candida freyschussii and fluconazole was initiated. At his two-week post-operative visit, fungal cultures on Sabouraud dextrose agar were growing a yeast, which again was unable to be identified on MALDI-TOF MS and sent to Mayo Clinic. There, D2 rRNA Sanger sequencing was again used, identifying the organism as Cyberlindnera rhodanensis. Fluconazole was switched to voriconazole 200 milligrams (mg) twice daily and he was referred to infectious diseases.

CT scan of wrist showing tenosynovitis and abscess. A, Axial view and B, coronal view showing thick-walled collection abutting flexor tendons (red arrows).

At his infectious diseases visit on day 494, he continued to have purulent drainage from his incision. A fourth-generation screening test for HIV was negative. Voriconazole was continued with therapeutic drug monitoring. A voriconazole trough was elevated at 6.9 μg/milliliter (mcg/mL), and his dose was reduced to 200mg every morning and 100mg every evening. A subsequent trough was 1.9 mcg/mL and considered therapeutic. However, he continued to report thick drainage from his wound. He underwent another incision and drainage one month later, on day 518. He was found to have significant tenosynovitis in all tendons with necrosis at the proximal end of flexor pollicis longus tendon.

He then developed right hand pain and numbness in his fingers, with continued purulent drainage from his wound. He was admitted to the hospital on day 537, where he underwent two more incision and drainage procedures on days 537 and 539, with thick purulence around his volar tendons noted in the OR. Cultures ultimately grew methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) and no yeast. He was discharged with an 8-week course of IV cefazolin 2 g every 8 hours and micafungin 100 mg daily. A vitamin D level was noted to be very low at 11.9 ng/milliliter (normal range 30–100) and was repleted.

After discharge, he again developed dehiscence of his wound with drainage, but cultures taken in the office on day 560 were negative, and the wound closed with supportive care. He was transitioned back to voriconazole 200mg every morning and 100mg every evening after completing his 8-week course of cefazolin and micafungin. He ultimately completed approximately five months of antifungal therapy through approximately day 630 and was doing well at follow-up visits.

Discussion

We present a case of fungal tenosynovitis caused by Cyberlindnera rhodanensis in an immunocompetent host. The patient ultimately needed six surgeries and five months of antifungal therapy over two years before clinical resolution. Although no MIC interpretations were available, we chose voriconazole due to its low MIC, using Clinical & Laboratory Standards Institue (CLSI) breakpoints for other Candida species as a surrogate.

This case is unusual for several reasons. C. rhodanensis, also known as Pichia rhodanensis or Saccharomyces rhodanensis, has never been reported to cause disease in humans. It belongs to the class of Saccharomycetes and the order of Saccharomycetales, and thus is related to the ubiquitous yeast Candida [1]. Fungal tenosynovitis, and Candida tenosynovitis in particular, is extremely rare. Cases in immunocompetent individuals are generally preceded by trauma or surgery, which our patient did not have. As demonstrated by this case, diagnosis of fungal tenosynovitis is often delayed, and clinical improvement can be slow despite antifungal therapy and source control with debridement. Additionally, our patient's chronic wound and multiple interventions predisposed him to developing a superinfection with Staphylococcus aureus, which further complicated his course.

A retrospective single-center review at the Mayo Clinic identified ten cases of fungal tenosynovitis between 1990 and 2013. Seven were caused by Histoplasma capsulatum, two by Coccidioides posadasii/immitis, and one by Cryptococcus neoformans [2]. Eight of the ten patients were immunosuppressed. All patients had localized pain, swelling and erythema. The average time from symptom development to diagnosis was six months. They were all treated with synovectomy and debridement. Most patients underwent only one surgery, but three patients required multiple debridements. After surgery, all patients were treated with systemic antifungal therapy with a mean duration of 8.2 months. Three patients had a relapse of their infection after completing antifungal therapy with two occurring in the setting of immunosuppression treatment.

Cyberlindnera rhodanensis has never been reported to cause disease in humans. There is one reported case of Cyberlindnera rhodanensis causing mastitis in a bovine in Brazil in 2017 [3]. The cow was not immunocompromised, and Cyberlindnera rhodanensis grew from milk cultures incubated in bovine blood agar. The yeast was then identified with PCR and gene sequencing.

There have been reports of human infection with another member of the Cyberlindnera family, Cyberlindnera fabianii. In one center in South Korea, eleven patients had C. fabianii isolated from blood, urine, or sputum using MALDI-TOF and molecular analysis. All patients had indwelling catheters such as Foley catheters, chemotherapy ports and central venous catheters. However, it was unclear whether the positive cultures represented true infection or colonization. These isolates were susceptible to amphotericin B, flucytosine, itraconazole, fluconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole, anidulafungin, micafungin and caspofungin. Because no MIC interpretations were available, susceptibility was determined according to CLSI breakpoints of other Candida species [4].

Cyberlindnera is in the Saccharomyces family, which includes Candida species. While Candida commonly causes human disease, Candida tenosynovitis is rare, with only nine cases described in the literature. Of these, five were immunocompromised from various conditions, including Buckley's hyperimmunoglobulinemia [5], advanced HIV [6], chronic steroid use [7,8], and calcineurin inhibitor and steroid use [9]. Of these five cases, one had preceding trauma with a distal radius fracture after a fall. Two were noted to have disseminated Candida infections [5,7]. The remaining four cases were in immunocompetent individuals, all of whom had an injury or intervention prior to developing infection. In one case, the patient had a crush injury to the finger complicated by traumatic ganglion cyst treated with multiple steroid injections and trigger finger releases [10]. In the second, infection developed after tenosynovectomy for trigger finger in a patient who worked with crops [11]. In the third, infection developed after two triamcinolone injections and a pulley release surgery for trigger finger [12]. In the fourth, the patient injected drugs into the hand and wrist [13]. In our case, the patient had no clear trauma prior to the development of infection, but we speculate that he may have had micro-trauma to the hand related to his work in construction.

Finally, our patient was incidentally found to be severely deficient in vitamin D. Vitamin D deficiency has not been clearly linked to an increased risk of infection, though vitamin D can modulate the immune system at multiple levels [14]. In vitro and in a mouse model, vitamin D3 was found to have an inhibitory effect against Candida species [15]. Studies of the clinical importance of this finding are ongoing.

In conclusion, this case highlights a potential emerging fungal pathogen, as well as the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges associated with fungal tenosynovitis. As this case demonstrates, it is critical to use caution when determining whether an organism isolated in culture represents contaminant, colonization, or true infection. This is especially important as the taxonomy of fungal organisms continues to change and new technologies allow more organisms to be identified.
