Synthesis of Crystalline Copper Oxide (CuO) Nanoparticles via a Combined Polyol and Thermal Decomposition in Air
Authors: Mohammad Jahid Hasan, Matthew Moreno, Paige Law, Vikram Kapoor, Esteban Ureña-Benavides
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Copper(II) oxide (CuO) nanoparticles are of growing interest due to their versatility in catalysis, energy storage, and environmental remediation. In this work, a novel air-assisted polyol–thermal decomposition method was developed to synthesize crystalline CuO nanoparticles with a controlled size. The reaction used copper(II) acetate in 1,4-butanediol at 140 °C under varying airflow conditions and reaction times, followed by calcination at 400 °C in air. Continuous air bubbling minimized the formation of Cu2O and metallic Cu, while maximizing the CuO yield with shortened reaction times. The optimal conditions involved a 4 h polyol reaction while purging air at 1800 cm3/min, followed by 4 h of calcination. This method resulted in polycrystalline monoclinic CuO nanoparticles with a size of 73 ± 32 nm, as observed by TEM and XRD. FT-IR and Raman spectroscopy verified the compositional purity of the nanoparticles. To enhance colloidal stability, a citrate coating reaction of CuO was optimized using sodium citrate dihydrate or citric acid in either water or 1,4-butanediol. The optimal coating conditions employed sodium citrate in water with bath sonication and overhead stirring, yielding a zeta potential of −40.6 ± 0.4 mV at pH 7. This work provides a practical and tunable method for producing high-quality CuO nanoparticles suitable for diverse applications.

1. Introduction

Metal oxide nanoparticles represent an important class of materials in nanotechnology, owing to their diverse physicochemical properties and wide-ranging applications. Their adjustable electronic, magnetic, optical, and catalytic behaviors have contributed to significant developments in areas such as energy conversion and storage, biomedical engineering, chemical sensing, and environmental remediation [1,2,3,4,5,6]. Among them, copper(II) oxide (CuO), also known as cupric oxide, nanoparticles have attracted significant attention due to their unique structural, optical, and catalytic properties, making them promising candidates for applications in photovoltaics, catalysis, gas sensing, biomedicine, energy storage, and environmental remediation [7,8,9,10,11,12]. In many of these applications, particularly those involving liquid-phase processing or biological environments, colloidal stability plays a critical role in maintaining uniform dispersion, preventing aggregation, and preserving the effective surface area and reactivity. Specifically, colloidally stable CuO nanoparticles are highly desirable for applications in catalysis, antimicrobial and other biomedical systems, electrochemical sensing, and printable functional coatings, where consistent performance and reliability depend on stable dispersions [13,14,15]. Their performance in these applications is highly dependent on their size, shape, crystallinity, and surface properties, which, in turn, are governed by the synthesis conditions. Therefore, developing a robust and scalable synthesis method that enables control over these parameters is crucial for advancing CuO-based technologies.

Several synthetic techniques have been reported for CuO nanoparticle preparation [16,17], including physical synthesis [18], chemical precipitation [10], sol–gel synthesis [19], hydrothermal and solvothermal methods [20,21], microwave-assisted processes [22], direct thermal decomposition [23], polyol synthesis [24,25] and green synthesis [26,27]. Each synthesis route presents specific advantages and limitations. Chemical precipitation is a simple, low-cost method for high-yield CuO synthesis but offers limited control over crystallinity and size. Sol–gel provides better uniformity and purity but requires longer processing and costly or hazardous precursors. The hydrothermal method produces highly crystalline CuO nanoparticles with a controlled morphology but is limited by long reaction times and high-pressure requirements. Thermal decomposition is simple and scalable but faces challenges in achieving a uniform particle size and often requires inert conditions for safety reasons. Green synthesis, which employs biological agents such as plant extracts, bacteria, fungi, or algae as natural reducing and stabilizing agents, offers an eco-friendly and biocompatible approach, though reproducibility may be affected by variability in biological sources. Solvothermal synthesis offers better size control at lower pressures but typically requires long reaction times and high pressures. Polyol synthesis offers many of the advantages of solvothermal methods but allows for high-temperature liquid-phase reactions to be carried at near-ambient pressures. Moreover, the hydroxyl groups provide nucleation sites for the nanoparticles while the solvent itself stabilizes their growth. Given the wide range of available synthesis methods, each with specific advantages and limitations, there is a critical need to systematically evaluate and optimize these techniques to produce CuO nanoparticles with well-defined properties.

Solvothermal and polyol synthetic methods of CuO nanoparticles are versatile processes that enable the controlled crystallization of copper precursors in solvents such as ethanol, ethylene glycol, propylene glycol, and other organic media under an elevated temperature. The morphology and shape of CuO nanoparticles are strongly influenced by reaction parameters such as temperature, time, and the type of ligands used [28,29,30,31,32]. Some ligands like citric acid can control the growth of CuO and favor spherical shapes [31,32], whereas using PEG200 in aqueous media often leads to elongated nanowires [28]. At moderate temperatures (~150 °C), CuO forms readily through alcohothermal synthesis, but higher temperatures (≥180 °C) can cause a reduction to Cu2O and metallic Cu [29,33]. Additionally, at lower temperatures, Cu(OH)2 frequently appears as an intermediate that later converts into CuO, highlighting the importance of the reaction time [29]. Therefore, CuO synthesis requires the careful optimization of ligands and thermal conditions to achieve a controlled morphology and phase purity. Nogueira et al. (2016) synthesized CuO nanoparticles, with a nanosphere morphology and particle sizes ranging from 10 to 20 nm, by solvothermally treating a copper acetate solution in ethanol at 110 °C for 20 h in a Teflon-lined autoclave [34]. Furthermore, Alla et al. (2016) synthesized CuO–MgO nanocomposites by dissolving CuCl2·2H2O and MgCl2·6H2O in a mixed solvent of ethylene glycol and deionized water, adjusting the pH to 12 with NaOH, and performing solvothermal treatment at 180 °C for 24 h, followed by washing, drying, and calcination at 450 °C [35]. According to the literature, sealed-autoclave solvothermal reactions often produce well-crystallized CuO nanoparticles directly due to the high temperature and autogenous pressure, whereas open-system reactions sometimes require post-calcination to achieve full crystallinity [34,35,36]. Overall, many solvothermal methods often result in mixed phases with various Cu oxidation states, highlighting the difficulties in producing pure CuO.

The colloidal stability of CuO nanoparticles is a key factor influencing their performance in a wide range of applications, including catalysis, antimicrobial activity, and environmental remediation, as aggregation can reduce surface area, alter reactivity, and compromise reproducibility. Various surface-coating agents, such as citrate, polyvinylpyrrolidone, polyethylene glycol, and surfactants, have been employed to improve nanoparticle dispersion and stability in solution [13,37,38]. Among these, citrate is particularly attractive because it provides strong electrostatic stabilization, is biocompatible, and allows for easy functionalization [37]. Optimizing the citrate coating process is therefore essential to achieve uniform particle size, stable dispersion, and consistent performance of CuO nanoparticles in subsequent applications.

In this study, multiple synthesis strategies were explored to optimize the production of crystalline CuO nanoparticles with a controlled size. The authors investigated the polyol synthesis of CuO using copper(II) citrate in 1,4-butanediol at 140 °C under varying airflow rates as a novel approach to minimize Cu2O and metallic Cu formation, maximize CuO yield, and shorten the reaction time, compared to conventional solvothermal and polyol methods. Butanediol was chosen as both the solvent and capping agent due to its high boiling point and ability to control nanoparticle growth, while its mild reducing nature is herein minimized through moderate air bubbling to prevent the reduction of CuO to Cu2O or Cu(0); a post-synthesis thermal treatment at 400 °C was then applied to enhance particle stability. The annealing temperature of 400 °C was selected based on literature reports indicating that this temperature yields phase-pure, highly crystalline monoclinic CuO, while lower temperatures may result in incomplete oxidation or mixed phases [39,40]. Additionally, the citrate coating of CuO nanoparticles was optimized to improve their colloidal stability. A comprehensive suite of characterization techniques was employed including TEM for particle size and morphology, XRD for crystallinity and phase analysis, FTIR and Raman spectroscopy for structural features, and DLS with a zeta potential for hydrodynamic size and colloidal stability. This work presents a comparative evaluation of CuO synthesis methods and provides valuable insights for the production of CuO nanoparticles.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Materials

Copper(II) acetate (≥99.0%), CuO nanopowders, and ethyl alcohol (≥99.5%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and used without further purification. Sodium citrate dihydrate (≥99.0%), citric acid (≥99.5%), and 1,4-butanediol were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA) and used as received. Ultrapure type I water (Milli-Q system) (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) was used for all aqueous preparations. All reagents were of analytical grade or higher.

2.2. Synthesis of CuO Nanoparticles

Copper oxide (CuO) nanoparticles were first synthesized through a polyol method using copper(II) acetate as the precursor and 1,4-butanediol as both solvent and ligand. Multiple reaction conditions were explored and optimized to achieve controlled particle size, purity, and enhanced stability. In the control experiment, 2.18 g of copper(II) acetate was dissolved in 240 mL of 1,4-butanediol in a 500 mL round-bottom flask equipped with a reflux condenser. The mixture was stirred magnetically at 1000 rpm and maintained at 140 °C for 20 h without air purging. Upon completion, the reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 30 min to remove residual solvent and unreacted copper salts. The collected precipitate was washed twice with ethanol and subsequently dried in a vacuum oven at room temperature overnight. To study the effect of temperature, a second reaction was conducted at 160 °C for 20 h under otherwise identical conditions and purified similarly.

In a third variant, to see the effect of air bubbling, the reaction was conducted at 140 °C for 20 h with low-rate air bubbling (flow rate 900 cm3/min) using an Ultra Zero Grade Air cylinder (Size 300 High Pressure Steel Cylinder, CGA 590, Airgas, Radnor, PA, USA). In a fourth variant, a moderate air flow (1800 cm3/min) was introduced, and the reaction was conducted at 140 °C for 4 h. All samples were collected and purified following the same protocol as the control reaction.

To improve nanoparticle stability and crystallinity, the nanoparticles obtained from both 20 h and 4 h reactions were subjected to calcination in a muffle furnace (Thermolyne, F6010, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Hampton, NH, USA) at 400 °C for 4 h. The resulting calcined powders were stored in airtight containers for subsequent characterization. It is important to note that calcination of the pre-synthesized CuO nanoparticles did not require an inert atmosphere, which simplified the procedure and reduced complexity. Direct calcination of the copper(II) acetate precursor in air should not be attempted as it may result in fire.

2.3. Citrate Coating of CuO Nanoparticles

Citrate coating of CuO nanoparticles was first optimized using commercial particles to determine the most effective coating conditions, after which those conditions were applied to the synthesized CuO nanoparticles. For optimization, both sodium citrate dihydrate and citric acid were tested separately as capping agents in either water or 1,4-butanediol, conducted in separate reaction batches. For coating with sodium citrate in water, 2 g of CuO nanoparticles were dispersed in 500 mL of deionized (DI) water in a 1 L glass beaker under magnetic stirring at 700 rpm for 10 min. The suspension was then sonicated using a probe sonicator (amplitude 30; 10 s ON, 10 s OFF) for 10 min to ensure uniform dispersion and minimize aggregation. The pH of the suspension was adjusted to 7 using 0.1 M HCl. The mixture was heated to 70 °C and maintained at this temperature for 4 h. After cooling to room temperature, the citrate-coated CuO nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 15 min and washed three times with DI water to remove unbound citrate. Finally, the purified nanoparticles were dried using a freeze-dryer (Labconco FreeZone 4.5 L, Marshal scientific, Hampton, NH, USA) and stored in airtight containers at room temperature for further use. Similarly, citrate coating of CuO was performed using sodium citrate in 1,4-butanediol, citric acid in water, and citric acid in 1,4-butanediol, following the same procedure.

To further enhance the citrate coating, an alternative method was employed in which the reaction mixture (CuO + sodium citrate + water) was placed in a beaker within a bath sonicator (Fisherbrand, FB11205, Fisher Scientific, Hampton, NH, USA) equipped with integrated heating and a separate overhead stirrer. The method allowed the assessment of the effect of bath sonication on the coating process. Continuous bath sonication was done at a frequency of 86 kHz to enhance citrate adsorption onto the CuO surface and to prevent nanoparticle aggregation during the reaction. A picture of the experimental setup is provided in Supplementary Information Figure S1.

2.4. Characterization of Nanoparticles

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images of the nanoparticles synthesized under various conditions were acquired using a JEOL JEM-2010F microscope (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). TEM samples were prepared by drop-casting a 0.001 wt% nanoparticle suspension onto copper carbon mesh grids (CF300-Cu, Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA) and allowing them to dry at room temperature in a fume hood. The particle size distribution of the synthesized CuO nanoparticles, along with commercial CuO, was determined by analyzing nanoparticle sizes measured from TEM images. Furthermore, energy-dispersive analysis of X-rays (EDAX) was performed using a Hitachi S5500 field emission scanning electron microscope (Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) in SEM mode to analyze the atomic compositions of the synthesized nanoparticles. About 10 µL of each synthesized CuO aqueous dispersion (0.01 wt%) was placed on a silicon wafer and dried overnight, after which the substrate was analyzed in the SEM.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of the nanoparticle powder samples was carried out using a Panalytical Empyrean diffractometer (Malvern Panalytical Ltd., Malvern, UK). The dried CuO NPs analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) from 2θ = 10° to 80° with a step size of 0.003° and a step time of 0.6 s, while the samples were rotated at 4 revolutions per minute at 25 °C using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.1540 nm).

Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy of the CuO nanoparticle powder samples was performed using a Shimadzu IRAffinity-1S spectrometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Spectra were recorded over a total of 100 scans to ensure an adequate signal-to-noise ratio.

Raman spectra were obtained using a Horiba LabRam HR-Evolution system (Horiba Scientific, Piscataway, NJ, USA) equipped with a 600 grooves/mm grating, 532 nm laser excitation, CCD camera detection, and an MPlan N 100×/0.9 objective lens from Olympus (Waltham, MA, USA). The spectra were collected with an acquisition time of 1 s and 100 accumulations to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential measurements were conducted using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS series instrument (Malvern Panalytical Ltd., Malvern, UK) with nanoparticle suspensions at 0.01 wt% and 0.1 wt%, respectively, at pH 7.

3. Results

3.1. Outcome of Synthesis Reactions

A first assessment of the reaction results was done through the visual appearance of CuO nanoparticles synthesized under different conditions, along with calcined CuO, commercial CuO, and citrate-coated CuO nanoparticles, as shown in Figure 1. The citrate-coated nanoparticles represent the optimized coating conditions with details discussed in Section 3.2. The visual inspection is useful as a qualitative assessment before completing the more rigorous characterization presented in the following sections. Initially, the synthesis of CuO nanoparticles in 1,4-butanediol was carried out at 140 °C and 160 °C without air bubbling. The reaction conducted at 140 °C for 20 h resulted in a bluish nanoparticle suspension (Figure 1a), indicating an incomplete reaction and formation of Cu(OH)2. In contrast, the same reaction performed at 160 °C for 24 h led to the formation of reddish nanoparticles (Figure 1b), suggesting the presence of elemental copper (Cu) or Cu2O as major products. While elevated synthesis temperatures are generally favorable for converting Cu(OH)2 to CuO, they may also promote the reduction of copper in a polyol medium with insufficient oxygen availability [29,41]. An early study by Hong et al. (2002) reported the alcohothermal synthesis of CuO nanoparticles at various low temperatures (i.e., 90, 110, 150, and 180 °C) with a reaction time of 20 h [33]. They reported that the alcohothermal synthesis of CuO nanoparticles was complete at reaction temperatures between 110 °C and 150 °C; however, at 180 °C and above, the formation of Cu2O or metallic Cu was observed, likely due to the reduction of CuO. Therefore, in this study, air was bubbled through the reaction mixture to minimize the reduction of Cu2+ to Cu+ or Cu0, thereby limiting the formation of Cu2O and metallic Cu and increasing the yield of CuO. The polyol reaction of copper(II) acetate in 1,4-butanediol at 140 °C with low air purging produced a blackish nanoparticle suspension after 20 h (Figure 1c), suggesting the formation of CuO nanoparticles. Interestingly, the same reaction with medium air purging yielded a similar blackish suspension in just 4 h (Figure 1d), suggesting that increased air flow accelerates the formation of CuO and significantly reduces the reaction time. However, the air bubbling needed to be controlled and maintained at a moderate flow rate, as a high airflow caused the rapid evaporation and escape of butanediol, leading to significant solvent loss. Comparatively, the aqueous suspensions of calcined CuO (Figure 1d,f), commercial CuO (Figure 1g), and citrate-coated CuO nanoparticles prepared under the best optimized coating conditions (see Section 3.2) (Figure 1h) also appeared black, as expected. All synthesized products from each batch were further characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD) to confirm their chemical composition and crystalline structure.

Figure 2 presents the XRD patterns of calcined CuO NPs, while the XRD of all other samples are shown in Supporting Information Figures S2–S4. The results indicate that the nanoparticles synthesized using the combined polyol and thermal decomposition methods (Figure 2a,b) exhibited distinct peaks at 32.6°, 35.7°, 38.9°, 48.9°, 53.6°, 58.4°, 61.7°, 66.4°, 68.1°, 72.5°, and 75.2°, corresponding to the (110), (−111), (111), (−202), (020), (202), (−113), (−311), (220), (311), and (−222) crystalline planes of monoclinic CuO (JCPDS card no. 48-1548) [42,43]. The citrate-coated CuO nanoparticles (4 h reaction + 4 h calcination + optimized citrate coating) displayed the same diffraction peaks as their uncoated counterparts (Figure 2c), suggesting that the coating process did not affect the crystalline structure of the CuO nanoparticles. However, the XRD patterns of the nanoparticles synthesized using the polyol method alone (Supplementary Information, Figure S2) show diffraction peaks at 2θ = 11.1°, 13.7°, 14.9°, 16.1°, 19.6°, 21.3°, 23.9°, 26.6°, 32.3°, 32.9°, 34.6°, 37.5°, 39.7°, 42.2°, 46.8°, 47.9°, 49.1°, 50.7°, 55.6°, 59.1°, and 63.1°. The low-angle reflections at 11.15–26.63° are consistent with residual copper acetate [44], while the peaks at 16.1° and 23.9° are characteristic of Cu(OH)2 (JCPDS Card No. 78-2076) [45]. The reflection at 42.2° can be indexed to cubic Cu2O [46,47] (JCPDS no. 05-0667), indicating partial reduction during the polyol reaction. Peaks observed near 32–39° and at higher angles (46.8°, 49.1°, 55.6°, 59.1°, and 63.1°) are close to the reported reflections of monoclinic CuO; however, the absence or weak intensity of the most prominent CuO peaks at ~35.7° and ~38.9° suggests that CuO is not the sole or fully developed phase. The coexistence of these reflections indicates a multiphase system arising from competing hydrolysis, dehydration, and reduction processes during synthesis; therefore, a subsequent calcination step is required to achieve phase-pure CuO.

The XRD pattern of the bluish particles obtained from the control reaction between copper(II) acetate and 1,4-butanediol at 140 °C for 20 h (without air bubbling) provided multiple peaks (Supplementary Information, Figure S3) indicating the presence of unreacted copper salt as well as hydroxide and oxide intermediates, consistent with an incomplete reaction. On the other hand, the XRD pattern of the reddish particles obtained from the reaction at 160 °C (without air bubbling) demonstrated peaks at 43.3°, 50.4°, and 74.1° (Supplementary Information, Figure S4), corresponding to the (111), (200), and (220) planes of metallic copper (JCPDS card no. 04-0836) [46,48]. This suggests the reduction of CuO to metallic Cu at an elevated temperature [29]. In contrast, the commercial CuO and the citrate-coated CuO nanoparticles (Supplementary Information, Figure S2e,f) exhibited identical diffraction peaks at 32.6°, 35.7°, 38.9°, 48.9°, 53.6°, 58.4°, 61.7°, 66.4°, 68.1°, 72.5°, and 75.2°, corresponding to the (110), (−111), (111), (−202), (020), (202), (−113), (−311), (220), (311), and (−222) crystal planes of monoclinic CuO (JCPDS card no.48-1548), identical to the calcined particles synthesized in our lab [42]. Once again, this indicates that the coating process did not alter the crystalline structure of the CuO nanoparticles and further validates our synthesis methods.

3.2. Optimization of Citrate Coating of CuO NPs

The colloidal stability of CuO nanoparticles is an important property, especially when the nanoparticles need to remain stable in suspension for applications such as wastewater treatment and microbial disinfection [49,50]. Nanoparticles with a zeta potential greater than +30 mV or less than −30 mV are generally considered colloidally stable [51]. CuO nanoparticles typically exhibit a positive surface charge of around +20 mV at pH 7, depending on the synthesis conditions [52]. However, their surface charge can be modified by coating with citrate, which renders their surface negatively charged. In this study, the citrate coating of CuO nanoparticles was first optimized by the surface functionalization of commercial CuO nanoparticles using either sodium citrate dihydrate or citric acid in water or 1,4-butanediol, separately, at 70 °C for 4 h, as described in Section 2.3. The surface charge and the hydrodynamic diameter (particle size) of the citrate-coated CuO NPs from each coating reaction are provided in Table 1. The results showed that the citrate-coated nanoparticles exhibited average hydrodynamic diameters ranging from 265 to 293 nm, which is comparable to the size of the uncoated commercial CuO nanoparticles (260 ± 69 nm). Therefore, the coating is likely occurring around clusters of nanoparticles as opposed to individual primary particles. Moreover, the zeta potential of commercial CuO nanoparticles at pH 7 was measured to be 2.5 ± 0.9 mV, indicating poor colloidal stability under neutral conditions. In contrast, the zeta potential (ZP) values of the citrate-coated CuO nanoparticles under various coating conditions ranged from −21 to −29 mV, confirming the increased negative surface charge resulting from the citrate coating. However, the choice of solvent or citrate source, whether sodium citrate dihydrate or citric acid, had minimal impact on the surface charge of the coated nanoparticles. Based on these observations, the citrate coating of CuO in water is recommended for its simplicity and ease of purification. To further improve the coating efficiency in aqueous media, the reaction was carried out in a sonication bath with continuous sonication and overhead stirring, which helped maintain the colloidal stability of CuO nanoparticles during the coating reaction and enhanced citrate adsorption on their surfaces. This method resulted in a significantly increased surface charge, with a zeta potential of −40.6 ± 0.4 mV, indicating more effective citrate coating due to the use of bath sonication. Yet, surprisingly, the hydrodynamic diameter was of a similar magnitude as all the other coating reactions, suggesting the sonication did not break clusters of the commercial CuO nanoparticles. It is possible these particles may be sintered. TEM images of the commercial CuO nanoparticles shown in Figure S5 do appear merged together with very irregular shapes, supporting evidence for sintering in the commercial samples. Nevertheless, among all the citrate coating approaches tested, the most effective method was coating CuO nanoparticles with sodium citrate in water at 70 °C under continuous bath sonication and overhead stirring; this optimized condition was subsequently applied to the citrate coating of the synthesized CuO nanoparticles, as discussed in Section 3.4.

3.3. Aggregate Size and Zeta Potential of the Synthesized CuO Nanoparticles

The aggregate sizes and zeta potentials of CuO nanoparticles synthesized under various reaction conditions are presented in Table 2. The measurements indicated that all synthesized nanoparticles (both before and after calcination) exhibited a very low surface charge, with zeta potential values ranging from 0 to 3 mV at pH 7, suggesting poor colloidal stability under neutral conditions. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis showed that the Z-average hydrodynamic diameters of the synthesized nanoparticles ranged from 260 to 290 nm for all samples, except for the sample synthesized with a 20 h reaction followed by 4 h of calcination, which exhibited a significantly larger size of 515 ± 113 nm. In general, the sizes measured by DLS were larger than those obtained from the TEM analysis (discussed in Section 3.4), but were of similar size as the commercial CuO NPs in Table 1. This discrepancy between TEM and DLS is attributed to the low surface charge of the nanoparticles, which led to rapid aggregation during the hydrodynamic diameter measurement despite the probe sonication immediately prior to the analysis. The reduced colloidal stability caused particle growth and aggregation within the short measurement timescale, resulting in higher apparent sizes. To improve colloidal stability at neutral pH, the calcined particles were further coated with sodium citrate in water using a bath sonicator, following the procedure described in Section 2. The results demonstrated that citrate coating significantly increased the negative surface charge of the particles. Specifically, the CuO_20 h reaction followed by calcination and citrate coating exhibited an exceptionally negative zeta potential of −48.0 ± 0.9 mV, while the CuO_4 h reaction followed by calcination and citrate coating showed −43.6 ± 0.5 mV. This increase in negative charge indicates an improved colloidal stability at a neutral pH compared to uncoated particles. Moreover, citrate coating also reduced the aggregate size in both calcined samples to 146 ± 34 nm for the 20 h reaction, and 189 ± 70 nm for the 4 h reaction, both smaller than their uncoated counterparts and the commercial CuO nanoparticles, with or without coating, with the difference of the means being statistically significant (p ≤ 0.05). These results suggest that the synthesized CuO nanoparticles not only offer enhanced stability but also exhibit smaller aggregate sizes compared to commercial CuO nanoparticles, highlighting the effectiveness of the synthesis approach developed in this study.

3.4. Morphological Study with TEM Imaging

The TEM analysis revealed the morphological characteristics and particle size distribution of the synthesized CuO nanoparticles (Figure 3). The nanoparticles obtained after the 20 h polyol reaction followed by 4 h of calcination exhibited an average diameter of 80 ± 38 nm, with individual crystallites (grain size) ranging from 14 to 21 nm (Figure 3a,b). In contrast, the nanoparticles that were synthesized under shorter reaction conditions (4 h polyol + 4 h calcination) showed a slightly reduced particle size with an average diameter of 73 ± 32 nm, and grain sizes varying between 12 and 22 nm (Figure 3c,d). High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) imaging confirmed a consistent lattice spacing of 0.25 nm in both samples, corresponding to the (111) planes of monoclinic CuO [29,53]. In addition to that, a lattice spacing of 0.29 nm was observed in the 4 h reaction (Figure 3d), corresponding to the (110) plane of monoclinic CuO [29,54]. The observed grain size range in both samples suggests a polycrystalline nature of the particles. The TEM results were further compared with standard commercial CuO nanoparticles (Supplementary Information, Figure S5). The size of the commercial CuO was found to be 176 ± 79 nm based on the TEM analysis (Figure S5a,b). Citrate coating did not significantly alter the size of the commercial CuO nanoparticles (Figure S5c,d). Both citrate-coated and uncoated Sigma-Aldrich CuO samples exhibited lattice spacings of approximately 0.24 nm and 0.28 nm, corresponding to the (111) and (110) planes of monoclinic CuO, respectively [29,54]. Moreover, the insets in panels a and c show the Selected Area Electron Diffraction (SAED) patterns of the corresponding samples, with rings representing specific diffraction peaks with a random orientation, indicating the polycrystalline nature of the nanoparticles [55,56]. SAED patterns showing discrete points indicate there is a preferred orientation of the crystals within the field of view. Overall, TEM images revealed that the synthesized nanoparticles exhibited a smaller average particle size and narrower size distribution compared to the tested commercial CuO nanoparticles, with the difference being statistically significant (p ≤ 0.05), validating the effectiveness of the combined polyol and calcination method in producing CuO nanoparticles with a controlled size and crystallinity. However, commercially available CuO powders can vary widely in size, ranging from tens of nanometers to a few microns [57]; therefore, the synthesized CuO nanoparticles in this work are still comparable to commercially available products.

The reported particle sizes and synthesis conditions for CuO nanoparticles vary considerably in the literature, largely depending on the preparation method, reaction temperature, and any subsequent post-treatment steps. For instance, sol–gel synthesis followed by calcination at temperatures above ~250 °C typically results in submicron CuO aggregates. These aggregates are generally composed of primary crystallites in the range of ~20–40 nm, while the overall particle (aggregate) size can fall between 100–140 nm, depending on factors such as pH and annealing conditions [58]. Sonochemical methods in aqueous media have produced more uniform CuO NPs around ~36 nm in diameter under optimized surfactant and reducing agent conditions [59]. The direct thermal decomposition of copper(II) acetate has been used to synthesize CuO nanoparticles due to its simplicity; however, this approach generally yields large, aggregated particles. For example, Gomaa et al. reported that the direct thermal decomposition of copper acetate at elevated temperatures produced CuO clusters with sizes (particle size ≈ 600 nm) significantly larger than typical wet-chemical products [60]. In contrast, our two-step synthesis, involving a pre-polyol reaction followed by calcination, produced smaller and more uniform CuO nanoparticles (particle size < 80 nm). The polyol reaction likely facilitates controlled nucleation and limits particle growth during the subsequent thermal treatment, resulting in a narrower size distribution and improved particle dispersion compared to direct thermal decomposition. This demonstrates that the pre-treatment of the precursor can be an effective strategy for tuning the particle size in CuO nanoparticle synthesis.

3.5. Atomic Composition with EDAX Analysis

The atomic compositions of the synthesized CuO nanoparticles, along with commercial CuO, were determined by EDAX and are summarized in Table 3 and Figures S6–S8. The commercial CuO exhibited 41.5 ± 6.0% O and 58.5 ± 5.3% Cu, indicating predominantly phase-pure CuO, although the Cu:O atomic ratio was slightly higher than the ideal 1:1. This deviation may be attributed to the limited oxygen detection efficiency of the instrument and a minor presence of Cu2O. The synthesized CuO samples obtained after 20 h reaction followed by calcination showed 41.0 ± 3.5% O and 59.0 ± 4.5% Cu, while the 4 h reaction plus calcination sample showed 41.6 ± 3.8% O and 58.4 ± 3.9% Cu. These values are comparable to those of the commercial CuO, confirming the formation of phase-pure CuO. In contrast, the polyol reactions conducted without subsequent calcination did not yield phase-pure CuO based on the XRD analysis; therefore, EDAX measurements were not performed for these samples.

3.6. Chemical Functional Groups via FT-IR Spectroscopy

Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra (Figure 4) were recorded for various types of CuO nanoparticles to confirm the successful synthesis and identify the main functional groups of each sample. The commercial CuO nanoparticles exhibited two major peaks at 480 cm−1 and 601 cm−1, corresponding to Cu–O stretching vibrations [61,62] characteristic of monoclinic CuO. Coating the commercial nanoparticles with citrate resulted in additional peaks at 1386 and 1575 cm−1, attributed to the symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of carboxylate (–COO−) groups [63], confirming the successful surface functionalization. The polyol synthesized nanoparticles from the 20 h and 4 h polyol reactions without calcination showed multiple peaks. These include a broad peak at 3313 cm−1 due to O–H stretching from residual butanediol and a small amount of hydrated acetate [64,65]. Additional peaks associated to butanediol include 2897 cm−1 and 2848 cm−1 assigned to aliphatic C–H stretching from the –CH2 groups, 1440 cm−1 from –CH2 bending, and 1051 cm−1 from the primary alcohol C–OH stretching vibrations. Evidence of residual copper acetate in the FTIR spectra is not straightforward. A weak peak at 1645 cm−1 may indicate asymmetric –COO− stretching; however, this vibration typically shows up closer to 1615 cm−1. A shift to a higher wavenumber could result from a monodentate coordination structure or complexation with butanediol [65,66]. The corresponding symmetric stretch is expected at 1400–1440 cm−1, which overlaps with the –CH2 bending vibration of butanediol. The polyol synthesized nanoparticles include copper–oxygen bands at 582 cm−1, 514 cm−1, and 466 cm−1 in both cases [67]. These features demonstrate the presence of residual 1,4-butanediol on the CuO surface, as butanediol was used both as a solvent and a capping agent during synthesis. In contrast, the calcined samples (4 h or 20 h polyol reaction followed by 4 h calcination at 400 °C) showed a single strong peak at 599 cm−1, corresponding to the Cu–O stretching vibration [61]. The absence of any organic compound peaks in these samples confirms the thermal decomposition and removal of organic residues, such as butanediol and trace amounts of acetate, during calcination. Overall, the FTIR analysis confirms the formation of pure CuO nanoparticles after calcination and validates the effectiveness of the synthesis and purification strategy.

3.7. Raman Spectroscopy Analysis

Raman spectroscopy (Figure 5) was employed to investigate the vibrational characteristics of the synthesized and commercial CuO nanoparticles. All samples displayed characteristic peaks corresponding to the monoclinic phase of CuO. The nanoparticles synthesized through the 4 h polyol synthesis followed by 4 h calcination exhibited Raman bands at 274, 324, and 610 cm−1. These peaks are assigned to the Ag and Bg vibrational modes of monoclinic CuO, which are associated with the Cu–O bond stretching and bending vibrations within the CuO lattice framework [68,69]. In addition, the 20 h polyol synthesis and 4 h calcination showed slightly shifted peaks at 279, 328, and 613 cm−1. These shifts toward higher wavenumbers suggest improved crystallinity and reduced structural defects compared to the 4 h polyol synthesis. The commercial CuO nanoparticles exhibited Raman peaks at 271, 317, and 605 cm−1, consistent with the literature values for bulk CuO [70,71]. In contrast, the citrate coating on the commercial CuO nanoparticles resulted in slightly red-shifted (shifted towards lower wavenumbers) Raman bands at 268, 315, and 605 cm−1. The red-shift and slight peak broadening observed may be attributed to surface modifications induced by the citrate molecules, which can influence local lattice vibrations through surface strain. Overall, the variation in Raman shifts across the samples reflects the influence of synthesis conditions and surface functionalization on the structural ordering of CuO nanoparticles.

4. Conclusions

This study demonstrates a novel air-assisted polyol–thermal decomposition method for producing phase-pure, crystalline CuO nanoparticles with a controlled size and enhanced colloidal stability. The introduction of moderate air (1800 cm3/min) bubbling during the polyol reaction effectively reduced the formation of Cu2O and metallic Cu, while simultaneously maximizing CuO production and reducing the overall reaction time. However, calcination remains necessary in order to obtain phase-pure CuO nanoparticles. Under optimized conditions, consisting of 4 h of the polyol reaction at 140 °C with moderate air purging (1800 cm3/min) followed by 4 h of calcination, it was possible to obtain CuO nanoparticles with well-defined polycrystalline monoclinic structures and an average particle size of 73 ± 32 nm measured by TEM. Comprehensive characterization using XRD, FT-IR, and Raman spectroscopy confirmed the phase purity and crystallinity of the synthesized nanoparticles, which are comparable to those of commercial CuO. Additionally, surface functionalization via the citrate coating of calcined nanoparticles effectively enhanced their colloidal stability, as evidenced by a highly negative zeta potential. The synthesized nanoparticles exhibited aggregate sizes that were 71 nm (27%) to 114 nm (44%) smaller than the tested commercial particles, thereby validating the air-assisted polyol method. Overall, the tunable synthesis and coating strategies herein presented provide a versatile platform for producing CuO nanoparticles through simple methods, potentially tailoring these nanoparticles towards various technological applications, including water treatment, catalysis, sensing, and energy storage.
