Knowledge and practice of the management of breakthrough cancer pain among general practitioners providing palliative care in Shanghai, China: a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Ying Yu, Panpan Zhang, Danxia Chen, Sun Fang Jiang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study examined knowledge and practice of breakthrough cancer pain (BTcP) management among general practitioners (GPs) providing palliative care in Shanghai.DesignCross-sectional study using a self-administered questionnaire.Setting and participantsA total of 393 GPs providing palliative care in 32 community health service centres in Shanghai were recruited by stratified cluster random sampling between 1 May and 30 June 2022.Outcome measuresKnowledge and practice concerning BTcP management.ResultsA total of 375 questionnaires were collected and declared valid. The median knowledge score was 11 out of 21 points. Only 36.3% (n=136) of the participating GPs scored 11 points or more, which was categorised as good knowledge. Only 24.8% (n=93) of the GPs used Davies’ adapted diagnostic algorithm as the gold standard for diagnosis. All of the GPs assessed the intensity of BTcP; however, less than a quarter of the GPs evaluated the relationship between background pain and BTcP (22.1%, n=83), the impact of BTcP on activities of daily living (24.0%, n=90), the impact of BTcP on social relationships (14.4%, n=54) and the impact of BTcP on mood (10.1%, n=38). 56.5% (n=212) of the GPs reported that they used pain tools in the assessment of BTcP. All of the GPs prescribed immediate-release morphine as rescue medication for relieving BTcP; however, 60.5% (n=227) prescribed the dose based on their personal experiences, irrespective of the basal opioid dose, and 57.3% (n=215) did not conduct dose titration after providing the initial dose. No GPs reported that they ever administered interventional treatment to their patients.ConclusionsInsufficient knowledge and inappropriate behaviours in BTcP diagnosis, assessment and treatment were identified. There is an urgent need to improve BTcP management among GPs providing palliative care in Shanghai.

Introduction

The incidence and morbidity of cancer have been increasing in recent years in China, and cancer has been the leading cause of death since 2010.1 Despite the WHO analgesic ladder for cancer pain management, cancer pain remains a common and distressing condition in patients with cancer. Breakthrough cancer pain (BTcP), a special type of cancer pain, refers to a transient exacerbation of pain that occurs either spontaneously or in relation to a specific predictable or unpredictable trigger, despite relatively stable and adequately controlled background pain.2 BTcP is associated with a variety of physical, psychological, and social complications and decreased quality of life.3 BTcP is a common problem in patients with cancer. The results of a recent systematic review reported an overall BTcP prevalence of 59.2% (39.9%–80.5%).4 A lower prevalence of BTcP was observed in cancer outpatients, and a higher prevalence was observed in patients receiving palliative care or advanced cancer patients admitted to hospice.4 5 Despite the high prevalence of BTcP, its management remains challenging, resulting from underdiagnosis and undertreatment.6 7

Palliative care is defined as active and integral care oriented to patients with diseases that no longer response to healing therapies. The role of palliative care is aimed at improving the lives of these individuals and their family members by relieving pain and biopsychosocial and spiritual issues.8 Palliative care in China starts later than that in Western countries but still remains immature after more than 30 years of development.9 Shanghai, a developed region of China, is a pilot area for palliative care in China. Unlike other regions of China, the general practitioners (GPs) who work in community health service centres (CHCs) are the main force providing palliative care in Shanghai. In recent years, palliative care has developed rapidly in Shanghai. To gain further insight into BTcP management in palliative care settings in CHCs in Shanghai, we conducted a survey of GPs’ knowledge and behaviour towards BTcP management in palliative care settings, aiming to provide a groundwork for improving BTcP management in Shanghai in the future.

Methods

Study population and sample

This cross-sectional survey was conducted using stratified cluster random sampling between 1 May and 30 June 2022. Shanghai was divided into 16 administrative regions. According to administrative region-stratified sampling, two CHCs were randomly selected from each administrative region. Potential participants in each study CHC were recruited from the job number list of GPs providing palliative care. All eligible individuals in these 32 sampled CHCs were invited to participate. They were provided the WeChat barcode for the questionnaires and voluntarily scanned the WeChat barcode to complete the survey. GPs who refused to complete the investigation were excluded from this study.

Measurement tool

The questionnaire for this study was developed based on clinical practice guidelines for BTcP management2 10 11 and was pilot tested on 30 randomly selected respondents. The items were found to be reliable, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.875, indicating acceptable internal consistency. The questionnaire collected general information from the respondents, such as their gender, age, region, professional title, education level, pattern of palliative care and years of experience in palliative care. Furthermore, knowledge regarding BTcP management was tested through 21 single-choice questions. Each correctly answered question was given a score of 1 point, and the total score was 21 points. The total cumulative score derived from the knowledge section was categorised into good or poor knowledge based on the median score, where a score less than the median score was categorised as poor and a score equal to or more than the median score was categorised as good. In the end, behaviour towards BTcP management was observed through 10 single-choice questions, 4 multiple-choice questions and 1 question scored using 5-point Likert scale (online supplemental table 1).

Data collection

This cross-sectional study was conducted via the WeChat platform. All items in the questionnaire were mandatory, and the questionnaire could not be submitted if not completed. An IP address could be used to submit the questionnaire only once.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated according to the following formula:

n=μα/22P(1−P)δ2

n refers to the required sample size. μα/2 is the μ value when the cumulative probability from left to right is 1−α/2 (both sides) in the standard normal distribution. P represents the accuracy rate of all the questions in the presurvey, where δ is the allowable error. Based on the presurvey results of 30 GP respondents (P=0.265, 1−P=0.735, α=0.05) and a μα/2=1.96, a 5% margin of error was set. Through this, the calculations were altered as follows: δ=0.05, and the required sample size was deemed to be at least 300. At a shedding rate of 20%, the total sample size was at least 360.

Analyses were performed with SPSS V.16.0. Continuous variables are presented as mean±SD, categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages) and were analysed using the χ2 test. A value of p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Practice

As shown in table 3, only 24.8% (93/375) of the GPs used Davies’ adapted diagnostic algorithm as the gold standard for distinguishing between background pain and BTcP. All of the GPs assessed the intensity of BTcP; however, less than a quarter of the GPs evaluated the relationship between background pain and BTcP (22.1%, 83/375), the impact of BTcP on activities of daily living (24.0%, 90/375), the impact of BTcP on social relationships (14.4%, 54/375) and the impact of BTcP on mood (10.1%, 38/375). When asked whether they used pain scales to assess BTcP, 56.5% (212/375) of the GPs reported that they used tools in clinical practice. The most commonly used assessment tool was the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) (online supplemental figure 1). Regretfully, no GPs reported that they used patient diaries. GPs with longer experience in palliative care or higher levels of professional titles were significantly more likely to use pain tools (online supplemental table 3).

When patients suffered from BTcP, all of the GPs added immediate-release opioids in addition to their treatment for background pain (pharmacological therapy for background pain is shown in online supplemental table 4). Regarding therapeutic options of immediate-release opioids as rescue medication, all of the GPs prescribed morphine as rescue medication. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report knowledge and behaviour regarding BTcP management among GPs providing palliative care in Shanghai. We identified insufficient knowledge and inappropriate behaviour among the GPs in the management of BTcP involved in diagnosis, assessment and treatment.

BTcP diagnosis and assessment

Despite the lack of a validated tool for diagnosis, the literature recommends the use of Davies’ algorithm. However, according to our survey, only 21.1% of the GPs understood this algorithm, and only 24.8% of the GPs used it as the gold standard for diagnosis. These results revealed that the majority of the GPs not only lacked awareness of this tool but also did not follow the BTcP diagnosis protocol, which may lead to its underdiagnosis and suboptimal undertreatment. A similar result was observed among pain-unit physicians in Spain.12

BTcP is a very heterogeneous condition characterised by both interindividual and intraindividual variability. The diagnostic breakthrough pain algorithm had a good positive predictive value but limited sensitivity using a cut-off score of ‘mild’ to define controlled background pain,13 suggesting that a comprehensive clinical assessment remained the preferred method to diagnose BTcP. In the present study, all of the GPs assessed the intensity of BTcP, whereas most of them actually ignored collecting other information on BTcP, especially the relationship between background pain and BTcP and the impacts of BTcP on patients’ daily lives. Compared with our results, nurses from Europe paid more attention to assessing the impact of BTcP on patients’ everyday activities and the factors that exacerbate BTcP,14 which led to better performance in the assessment of BTcP.

One of the strategies proposed to overcome inadequate assessment is the use of specific validated pain tools.15 Nevertheless, pain tools do not appear to be widely used in clinical practice. A European survey revealed that only 15% of patients reported that their clinician measured their cancer pain using a pain scale.16 In this study, 56.5% of the GPs reported that they used pain tools in clinical practice. NRS and visual analogue scale, which are unidimensional tools, have been confirmed to be valid, reliable, and appropriate for use in cancer pain management but only measure the intensity of pain.17 18 Different from unidimensional scales, multidimensional tools and patient diaries collect a range of information related to BTcP,19–21 providing a more comprehensive picture of patients. Our results found that the majority of GPs did not understand the advantages of multidimensional scales and patient diaries and were likely to use unidimensional scales rather than multidimensional tools or patient diaries, which was consistent with a previous investigation.14 It seems reasonable that the lack of knowledge may limit the use of multidimensional scales or patient diaries in clinical practice, which may account for the inadequate assessment of BTcP in this study.

BTcP treatment

Rescue medication, with the characteristics of a fast onset and short duration, is the cornerstone of the management of patients with cancer and BTcP.22 A Korean nationwide survey demonstrated that 56% of patients did not receive short-acting analgesics for BTcP.23 Another investigation which enrolled patients from European countries reported that almost quarter of BTcP patients received morphine in addition to their treatment for background pain.24 Inspiringly, our study found that all of the GPs prescribed immediate-release opioids in addition to treatment for background pain.

Morphine is a traditional first-line rescue medication, but morphine administered orally has a slow onset of action, which may be useful in the management of BTcP episodes lasting for more than 60 min and predictable BTcP. Rapid-onset opioids (ROOs) have been confirmed to be more effective and provide more rapid analgesia than traditional immediate-release oral morphine and are recommended as the first option for BTcP.25–27 In this study, however, all of the GPs prescribed morphine as rescue medication rather than ROOs, which was related to the fact that morphine is the only available rescue medication in CHCs in Shanghai. Regarding the routes of administration, the majority of the GPs preferred morphine administered by subcutaneous route for BTcP patients, suggesting that they understood the pharmacokinetic characteristics of subcutaneous injection of morphine. Historically, treatment of BTcP has involved opioid supplementation with doses of 5%–20% of the total daily dose of the opioid already being taken to relieve background pain.28 For adequate BTcP analgesia, it is also important for opioids to be titrated over time to meet the needs of each patient.29 The opioid ‘rescue medication’ dose should be determined by individual titration.2 However, in this study, the dose of rescue medication given to BTcP patients seemed to be inappropriate. More than 60% of the GPs prescribed initial rescue medication based on their personal experiences, irrespective of the basal opioid dose, and 57.3% did not conduct dose titration, which was consistent with the low correct response rate to the initial rescue dose calculation and dose titration items in the knowledge questionnaire. We speculated that the lack of knowledge was an important factor that led to inappropriate prescriptions. In addition, incorrect diagnosis, poor pain assessment or cultural factors may account for inappropriate prescriptions of rescue medication.30 31

In addition, interventional techniques, such as trigger-point injection, epidural or intrathecal drug infusion, neural blockade, transcutaneous nerve stimulation and neuroablation, are also important approaches to improve BTcP and may offer effective analgesia with less systemic toxicity and dependency than first-line and second-line oral and parenteral agents.32 The implementation of interventional techniques requires the participation of specialists for advanced pain management techniques and an interdisciplinary team containing an anaesthetist with expertise. However, CHCs in Shanghai lack pain management specialists and interdisciplinary teams, which may account for the limited use of interventional technologies by the population included in this study. In addition, the role of interventions to manage pain in patients with cancer is poorly acknowledged and appreciated. It is often misunderstood that interventions for cancer-related pain should be performed only after other options have been exhausted.33

Study limitations

This study had some limitations. First, the cross-sectional study could not determine a causal relationship between knowledge and practice. Second, our survey recruited voluntary participants, which likely produced a sampling bias. This might make generalisation of the results difficult due to the voluntary nature. Third, univariate analysis identified several factors influencing GPs’ knowledge and behaviour about BTcP but could not exclude potential confounders because multivariate analysis was not conducted.

Conclusion

Our study provides a reflection of the knowledge and practice of BTcP management among GPs in primary palliative care settings in Shanghai. We found that the GPs had insufficient knowledge and inappropriate behaviours regarding BTcP diagnosis, assessment and treatment. The insufficiency in palliative care education and training and the shortage of specialised palliative care professionals may account for the poor management of BTcP in Shanghai.9 It is time to take action to improve GPs’ knowledge and to correct their inappropriate behaviours. The present study indicates an urgent need to improve BTcP management in palliative care settings in Shanghai.
