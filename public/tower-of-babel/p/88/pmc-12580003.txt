Introduction

Vesicovaginal fistula (VVF) is an abnormal connection between the bladder and vagina, causing persistent urine leakage. It is mainly caused by gynecological surgery, pelvic tumor surgery, radiotherapy, or obstructed labor. The prevalence of VVF after hysterectomy is reported to be 0.8–1.0 per 1000 women [1, 2]. VVFs impair physical, mental, social, and sexual quality of life. Diagnosis is made by dye test, cystoscopy, cystography, CT, and MRI. Simple fistulas are solitary lesions less than 0.5 cm without prior radiotherapy, whereas complex ones include failed prior repairs, sizes 0.5–2.5 cm, chronic disease, or prior radiotherapy [3].

Although there is no standardized protocol for treatment timing or methods, conservative drainage with a catheter may be effective for simple fistulas; however, the response rate is low. If not repaired, surgical options are considered [4]. Low‐pressure intermittent suction is used to prevent perforation, adhesion, and bleeding, primarily for draining fluid in conditions like ileus and pneumothorax.

We report successful conservative treatment using low‐pressure intermittent suction via an indwelling bladder catheter.

Case Report

A 78‐year‐old female underwent Prolift‐type anterior transvaginal mesh (TVM) surgery for a stage III cystocele. During surgery, cystoscopy after the puncture for the mesh arms revealed a bladder injury on the outer side of the right ureteral orifice (Figure 1A). We performed two‐layer closure with 3–0 Vicryl via a transvaginal approach (Figure 1B). A Martius flap would have been preferable due to the fragility of surrounding tissue, but we lacked the necessary knowledge and skills at that time. After confirming no obvious leakage by intravesical indigo carmine injection, mesh was inserted. Cystography on postoperative day 14 showed no leakage, and the urethral catheter was removed. However, urinary incontinence recurred on postoperative day 17. Vaginal examination revealed that the cervical side of the vaginal wound was indistinct, and cystoscopy demonstrated mucosal traction near the posterior wall of the right ureteral orifice. These findings were suggestive of VVF; however, no definitive fistulous opening was identified in the bladder mucosa (Figure 1C). A 20Fr foley catheter was placed; however, there was no improvement in urinary incontinence. As the patient declined reoperation, conservative treatment was continued.

Previously, we experienced a case of a neobladder‐skin fistula that had been successfully treated with low‐pressure intermittent suction; therefore, we decided to use the same method with this patient. We replaced the urethral catheter with a 20Fr catheter with a terminal hole and short tip, which is originally for nephrostomy, so that we could have the most effective drainage, and directly inserted the tube of the electric low‐pressure suction device (Figure 2) and started intermittent suction (−20 cm water column for 5 min and pause for 1 min) on the third day after VVF onset. The amount of urinary incontinence showed gradual improvement (Figure 3). On the eleventh day after VVF onset, contrast‐enhanced CT showed residual VVF (Figure 1D,E). Urinary incontinence disappeared on day 22 after VVF onset. We gradually reduced the suction pressure and stopped on day 37 after VVF onset. On day 65, cystoscopy revealed no mucosal defects (Figure 4F). A cystogram was performed on day 72, and injection of contrast medium revealed no evidence of leakage (Figure 4G). A tampon dye test showed no evidence of leakage. The urethral catheter was removed on day 78, and the patient has remained free of recurrence for 10 years to date.

Discussion

VVF is one of the most common types of urinary fistula in women. The prevalence rate of VVF due to TVM surgery was reported as 0.29%–1.1% [5, 6, 7]. In one of these previous studies [5], VVF was caused by bladder injury due to the position of the needle in the bladder, which is similar to the present case.

There is no standard algorithm of treatment for VVF. In cases where urethral catheterization is initially performed for VVF, Michael et al. [8] recommend that surgical therapy 10–12 weeks after catheter removal should be done if only a small amount of urine is output from the catheter and that continued catheterization should be considered if most of the urine is output from the catheter. Thus, eliminating leakage through the VVF is a key factor for the success of conservative treatment with indwelling catheterization.

Conservative treatment with catheterization alone for VVF has been reported to have a cure rate of 8% [9]. Factors reported to be associated with success include early initiation of treatment [10], small fistula size [11, 12], nonmalignant disease [8], and prior radiation therapy [8]. A review of conservative closure cases reported that 39% of patients had spontaneous closure if a urinary catheter was placed within 3 weeks after injury. On the other hand, when it was placed more than 6 weeks after injury, only 3% had spontaneous closure, suggesting the importance of early drainage [6]. Smaller fistulas are more likely to close, with a study reporting a 50%–60% success rate for fistulas less than 2 cm in diameter [7], and another study reporting spontaneous healing of small‐diameter fistulas with indwelling urinary catheterization only in about 39% of cases [8].

In the present case, we could not detect the fistula by cystoscopy, suggesting that it was small. We placed the foley catheter on the next day after VVF occurrence. The small fistula size and early catheter placement after the onset of VVF may have influenced the success of the treatment. There is no general consensus for the timing of catheter removal after conservative treatment. In the present case, VVF developed despite 2 weeks of postoperative catheterization; therefore, we continued catheterization for a long time (11 weeks) after urinary incontinence stopped. Further studies should define optimal catheter duration.

Regarding low‐pressure intermittent suction, there is only one report of its use in the treatment of VVF [13]. In two of the cases, low‐pressure suction (−10 cm water column) was added, which may have contributed to healing. In other studies, intermittent suction has been used to prevent mucosal [14] and organ [15] damage. In this case, low‐pressure intermittent suctioning was employed because urinary incontinence persisted despite urinary catheterization alone. Long‐term continuous or high‐pressure suction was avoided due to the risks of organ damage and obstruction due to the bladder mucosal adhesions. After initiating low‐pressure intermittent suction, urinary incontinence decreased, ultimately reaching a state of no urinary leakage, which was important in the successful conservative treatment.

Conclusion

We experienced a case of VVF after TVM surgery that was successfully treated conservatively by placement of a urethral catheter with low‐pressure intermittent suction. This method may be a feasible option for small and fresh fistulas refractory to catheterization alone. However, this study was limited as it was a single case. Further case studies and comparisons with other treatment methods are necessary.
