Liberal Versus Restrictive Transfusion Strategy in Patients With Subarachnoid Hemorrhage: A Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Pargol Balali, Lamya Ibrahim, Monisha A. Kumar, Brett Cucchiara, Steven Messe, Scott E. Kasner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe effect of different thresholds for packed red blood cell transfusion on neurologic outcomes in patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage and anemia is uncertain. This meta‐analysis aimed to evaluate the effect of liberal versus restrictive transfusion strategies on functional outcomes in subarachnoid hemorrhage.MethodsWe systematically searched MEDLINE (inception–March 26, 2025) for randomized controlled trials comparing functional neurological outcomes in adults with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage assigned to receive packed red blood cell transfusion using high versus low hemoglobin thresholds. Risk of bias was assessed using Cochrane Risk of Bias tool 2.0. Unfavorable neurologic outcomes were defined based on trial‐specific primary neurologic outcome definitions (Glasgow Outcome Scale Extended score ≤5 or modified Rankin Scale score ≥3 or modified Rankin Scale score ≥4). An exploratory analysis using Glasgow Outcome Scale Extended score ≤5 or modified Rankin Scale score ≥3 as unfavorable neurologic outcome was also performed. We calculated the pooled risk ratio (RR) with 95% CI for unfavorable neurologic outcome using random effect models and assessed heterogeneity using Cochran's Q test.ResultsOf 1628 studies, 3 trials (n = 953 patients) met the inclusion criteria. Over a follow‐up of 3–12 months, 181/470 patients (38.5%) randomized to liberal transfusion strategies (hemoglobin<9–11.5g/dL) compared with 214/483 patients (44.3%) in the restrictive strategy group (hemoglobin<7–10g/dL) experienced unfavorable neurologic outcomes. Liberal (versus restrictive) packed red blood cell transfusion strategies resulted in a nonsignificant reduction (RR, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.77–1.01; P = 0.06; I2 = 0%) in unfavorable neurologic outcomes per trial‐specific definitions and RR = 0.89 (95% CI, 0.79‐0.99; P = 0.04; I2 = 0%) when unfavorable outcome was defined as Glasgow Outcome Scale Extended score ≤5 or modified Rankin Scale score ≥3. Sensitivity analysis, excluding the small pilot trial (n = 44) with a higher hemoglobin threshold, did not change the summary effect.ConclusionsOur study demonstrated a trend toward better neurologic outcomes with liberal transfusion strategies in patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage. Further studies are required to determine the optimal transfusion strategy for this high‐risk population and identify subgroups that are most likely to benefit from the liberal strategy.

Observational studies have shown that anemia is associated with poor outcomes in patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). 1 , 2 Liberal packed red blood cell (pRBC) transfusion strategies may improve brain tissue oxygenation in these patients who are at risk of impaired cerebral blood flow. 3 However, it is possible that anemia merely reflects an increased severity of the event or underlying frailty and is thus associated with outcome indirectly. In addition, blood transfusion is associated with adverse effects such as lung injury, infections, and thromboembolic events. 4 Thus, the optimal hemoglobin threshold for transfusion remains uncertain, and clinical trials have shown inconsistent results. 5 , 6 We aimed to summarize available evidence regarding the association between different pRBC transfusion hemoglobin thresholds and neurological outcomes after SAH.

Methods

This systematic review was not registered prospectively, but it was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses guidelines. 7 All data used in this study were extracted from published manuscripts, which are publicly available. A systematic search in MEDLINE from inception to March 26, 2025, identified randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing functional neurologic outcomes in adult patients with aneurysmal SAH assigned to receive pRBC transfusion using high versus low hemoglobin thresholds (Table S1). After excluding duplicates and non‐RCT studies (marked by the Cochrane automated RCT classifier tool) using Covidence software, 2 reviewers (P.B. and L.I.) screened the titles and abstracts, and discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer (S.K.). Our primary outcome was an unfavorable neurologic outcome per trial‐specific primary neurologic outcome definitions (Glasgow Outcome Scale Extended [GOS‐E] score ≤5 at 6‐month follow‐up in the TRAIN (Transfusion Strategies in Acute Brain Injured Patients) trial, 5 , 8 modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score ≥4 at 1‐year follow‐up in the SAHARA (Subarachnoid Hemorrhage Red Cell Transfusion Strategies and Outcome) trial, 6 and mRS score ≥3 at 3‐month follow‐up in the Naidech et al. study). 9 GOS‐E scores range from 1–8, with 1 indicating death and increasing scores reflecting better functional outcome. mRS scores range from 0–6, with 6 indicating death and lower scores representing better functional outcomes. Our secondary outcomes were cerebral ischemia, thromboembolic events (including any reported new cerebral ischemia, acute myocardial infarction, intestinal ischemia, acute peripheral limb ischemia, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism), length of intensive care unit stay, and length of hospital stay. Additionally, we performed an exploratory analysis for unfavorable neurologic outcomes using an mRS score ≥3 threshold, as it aligns better with the GOS‐E score of ≤5 threshold, and represents a more homogenous definition of functional dependence (unable to do one or more of the life roles). 10 , 11 P.B. and L.I. independently extracted the study characteristics (eg, author, year, trial design, setting, blinding, and intervention), participant details (sample size, inclusion criteria, and featured baseline characteristics), outcome data using intention‐to‐treat analysis (excluding patients with missing data on the primary outcome), and percentage of missing outcome data. We did not impute any data beyond those already imputed by the individual trials. Corresponding authors of papers with missing data were contacted by email. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool 2.0 12 by 2 reviewers (P.B. and L.I.), and disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (S.K.). For the meta‐analysis comparing liberal versus restrictive transfusion strategies, we included only studies where hemoglobin thresholds were clearly defined, and neurologic outcomes were assessed at ≥3 months. We assessed the quality of evidence for each outcome based on the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) system using GRADEpro GDT tool. 13

We performed a meta‐analysis to calculate the pooled risk ratio (RR) with 95% CI for the study binary outcomes and pooled mean difference (95% CI) for continuous outcomes using Mantel–Haenszel and inverse variance random effect models, respectively. Results of individual studies for each outcome, along with the summary effect of the meta‐analysis, were presented using forest plots. For the meta‐analysis, the mean ± SD was calculated based on the median, interquartile range, and group sample size using a previously published online tool. 14 Cochran's Q test was used to assess the heterogeneity among the studies and was reported using I2 statistics. Publication bias was assessed by contour‐enhanced funnel plots; however, due to the small number of included studies, no statistical tests for funnel plot asymmetry were performed. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant, and analysis was performed using Review Manager 5.4.1 software.

Results

A total of 1628 studies were identified, of which 857 studies were excluded (853 studies were marked as non‐RCT by the Cochrane RCT classifier automated tool, and 4 were duplicates). Of 771 screened studies, 3 met our eligibility criteria, 6 , 8 , 9 , and a total of 953 patients were included in this analysis (Figure 1 and Table S2). Table summarizes the key features of the included studies. All included studies were categorized as having a nonserious risk of bias. The detailed assessment of the risk of biases is presented in Figure S1. Contour‐enhanced funnel plots assessing the risk of publication bias for each outcome are presented in Figure S2. Over a follow‐up of 3–12 months, 181 out of 470 patients (38.5%) assigned to a liberal transfusion strategy (hemoglobin<9–11.5 g/dL) had unfavorable neurologic outcomes, compared with 214 out of 483 patients (44.3%) assigned to a restrictive strategy (hemoglobin<7–10 g/dL). Risk of unfavorable neurologic outcome per each trial's primary outcome definition was nonsignificantly lower with a liberal transfusion strategy (RR, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.77–1.01; P = 0.06) with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) [Figure 2, panel A]. Exploratory analysis using score cutoffs indicating functional neurologic dependence (ie, GOS‐E score ≤5 or mRS score ≥3) showed a significant reduction in unfavorable neurologic outcome among those randomized to a liberal versus restrictive transfusion strategy (RR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.79–0.99; P = 0.04; I2 = 0%; Figure 2, panel B). The estimated absolute risk reduction in unfavorable neurologic outcome with a liberal transfusion strategy was 6.5% (number needed to treat = 15).

Discussion

This meta‐analysis of 3 randomized trials showed that there is a likelihood of modest reduction or no reduction in unfavorable neurologic outcomes with liberal transfusion strategies in patients with SAH, using study‐specific primary outcome definitions, and a modest but significant reduction in unfavorable neurologic outcomes using a harmonized outcome definition (mRS score ≥3 or equivalent GOS‐E score as the threshold for unfavorable neurologic outcome).

A recent meta‐analysis of RCTs including patients with acute brain injury (ie, traumatic brain injury [>80% of the included patients], intracerebral hemorrhage, and SAH) did not demonstrate definitive evidence of the beneficial effect of liberal transfusion strategies on functional neurologic outcome. However, their sensitivity analysis, limiting to the studies with low risk of bias, suggesting a potential neurological benefit with higher hemoglobin threshold transfusion strategies. 15 Notably, patients in the SAHARA and Naidech et al trials appeared to be less ill than those in the TRAIN trial (median Glasgow Coma Scale scores of 13–14 versus 7, respectively). 6 , 8 , 9 However, the relative effect size of higher (versus lower) hemoglobin thresholds on functional neurologic outcome remained essentially similar comparing TRAIN and SAHARA trials (RR = 0.87 versus 0.89), which is reassuring regarding the generalizability of the potential treatment effect to patients with lower baseline neurological scores. The numerically lower rate of cerebral ischemia in those treated with a liberal transfusion strategy provides a potential mechanism for improved outcomes by reducing brain tissue hypoxia and metabolic distress, which have been previously observed in patients with SAH and anemia. 16

This study has several limitations. The TRAIN trial included a heterogeneous population (SAH, traumatic brain injury, and intracerebral hemorrhage), though stratified randomization by type of brain injury resulted in a fair randomized comparison and relatively balanced patient characteristics in trial arms for the SAH subgroup included in this meta‐analysis. Of note, patients with SAH in the TRAIN trial who were randomized to the liberal transfusion strategy were older (mean age 60 versus 57 years) and had higher levels of blood glucose on admission (median glucose 166 versus 152 mg/dL) than those in the restrictive group. 8 As older age and higher glucose levels on admission have been reported to be associated with worse functional outcome after SAH, these differences would be expected to bias the results in favor of the restrictive strategy. 17 , 18 The included studies used different cutoffs as their hemoglobin thresholds. It is possible, though we believe far less likely, that there is an abrupt threshold response with outcome, which could be missed using the current available trial‐level data. Moreover, neurologic outcomes were assessed using different tools, score cutoffs, and follow‐up duration across studies, which limits the quantitative harmonization of the trials but should offer consistent directionality. As previous studies showed that functional neurologic outcomes continue improving significantly from 3 to 6 months and 1 year, 19 , 20 we assume that the divergence between the functional neurologic outcomes in each treatment arm continues increasing over time. Thus, shorter follow‐up times (if they have any effect on the result) would bias our results toward the null. The Naidech et al. trial differed from the other 2 studies given its shorter follow‐up duration (3 versus 6–12 months), higher rate of missing outcome data (9% versus ≈1%), and significantly higher hemoglobin thresholds for pRBC transfusion in liberal versus restrictive strategy (≤10 versus ≤11.5 g/dL compared with <7–8 g/dL versus <9–10 g/dL in other trials). 5 , 6 , 9 The latter may have caused an overlap regarding the hemoglobin threshold included in the restrictive strategy group in this study with those randomized to the liberal strategy group in other trials. However, sensitivity analysis excluding this pilot study did not alter the results of our meta‐analysis. In addition, we could not include this study for some of the secondary outcomes, given that the data were unavailable. 9 Lastly, there may be double‐counting of patients for thromboembolic events in TRAIN and SAHARA trials, because we did not have the data regarding the composite outcome of thromboembolic events for these trials, and some patients in each trial might have experienced more than one thromboembolic event. Therefore, this outcome should be interpreted cautiously. 6 , 8 However, most of the difference in thromboembolic events was due to cerebral ischemia, which was analyzed separately. In addition, given that the same method was used in both treatment arms, we do not believe that it would bias our results.

In summary, although current evidence remains insufficient to conclude that a more liberal transfusion strategy reduces unfavorable neurologic outcomes in patients with SAH, meta‐analysis of completed trials suggests the possibility of a clinically meaningful treatment effect. Before making any recommendation for clinical practice, individual participant data meta‐analysis and future randomized trials with uniform definitions for negative outcomes and extended follow‐up periods of 12 months or longer are warranted to provide a more definitive guidance regarding optimal transfusion strategies for this high‐risk population and potentially identify subgroups that are most likely to benefit.
