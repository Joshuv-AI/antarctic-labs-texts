Genome evolution and regulatory dynamics underlying salt stress tolerance in the halophyte Halogeton arachnoideus
Authors: Kaiming Xu, Peiying Ye, Lei Zhang, Jianquan Liu, Fuping Tian, Shuyu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlants inhabiting extreme environments provide valuable genetic resources for studying stress-resistance mechanisms. Halogeton arachnoideus (Amaranthaceae sensu lato) is a typical halophyte native to saline habitats, yet the molecular mechanisms underpinning its adaptation remain unclear. Here, we present a high-quality, chromosome-scale genome assembly using PacBio HiFi long-read sequencing and high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) technologies. The genome is highly repetitive and dominated by long terminal repeat retrotransposons, which shape genome architecture and potentially modulate gene regulatory landscapes. Although no lineage-specific whole-genome duplication (WGD) event is detected, genes derived from ancient WGD and tandem duplications likely provide evolutionary substrates for salt-associated responses. Transcriptomic analyses under moderate and high salinity treatments reveal extensive transcriptional remodeling. Gene regulatory networks analyses uncover stress-induced rewiring and decentralization. Transcription factor families (MYB, AP2/ERF, WRKY, and bHLH) constitute major components of the salt-responsive regulatory landscape, while NAC and C2H2 factors display concentration-specific regulatory activity. We also detect a shift in regulatory hubs from early to initial acclimation stress phases, pointing to a coordinated but temporally coarse regulatory transition. Together, this integrative genomic and transcriptomic framework provides insights into the evolutionary processes and regulatory organization associated with salt responsiveness in H. arachnoideus and provides a foundation for improving salt-tolerant crops.Subject terms: Evolution, Genomics

Introduction

Extreme environments have recurrently shaped the evolutionary trajectories of plants, offering powerful natural models for disentangling the genomic and regulatory innovations that underlie adaptation to persistent abiotic stress. Among these stressors, soil salinization has emerged as one of the most pervasive and intensifying threats to plant productivity, exacerbated by anthropogenic activities and global climate change1. According to the 2024 report of the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, more than 1.381 billion hectares of land, accounting for approximately 10.7% of the global terrestrial area, are affected by salinization2. Elevated concentrations of sodium (Na⁺) and chloride (Cl−) ions disrupt ion homeostasis, induce osmotic stress, and compromise nutrient uptake3–6. These physiological disturbances substantially limit the survival and performance of most plant species, particularly glycophytic crops, leading to reductions in arable land area, crop yield, and product quality7,8.

Despite these challenges, a small but phylogenetically diverse set of angiosperms, known as halophytes, have independently evolved the capacity to survive and reproduce in environments with salt concentrations exceeding 200 mM NaCl9–11. Deciphering how these species persist and thrive under such constraints is essential for understanding the molecular basis of environmental adaptation and for guiding the development of stress-resilient crops12–14.

Within halophytic lineages, Amaranthaceae sensu lato (Amaranthaceae s.l.) is particularly enriched in salt-tolerant species exhibiting striking morphological and physiological innovations, such as succulence and ion compartmentalization. Halogeton arachnoideus, a representative member of this halophytic lineage, is widely distributed across arid and semi-arid regions of northwestern China, Mongolia, and Central Asia, where saline-alkaline soils are prevalent. It is phylogenetically related to economically important crops such as spinach (Spinacia oleracea) and beet (Beta vulgaris)15,16, and holds cultural significance as a traditional alkali source in the preparation of Lanzhou beef noodles, a traditional dish from China. Its phylogenetic position within a halophyte-rich lineage, combined with its extreme ecological niche, makes H. arachnoideus an excellent system for exploring the genomic and regulatory foundations of salt tolerance.

One mechanism thought to contribute to such adaptation is gene duplication. Both whole-genome duplications (WGDs) and localized gene duplication events (e.g., tandem) have been recognized as key drivers of genome evolution, including the evolution of complex traits such as salinity tolerance17–21. In the halophyte Achnatherum splendens, for instance, a WGD event provides genetic redundancy and may enable the emergence of gene functions associated with tolerance to ionic and osmotic stress22. Similar patterns have been reported in mangrove species, while WGD-retained genes have facilitated adaptation to seawater salinity23. Tandem duplications (TDs) have also been implicated in enhanced salt tolerance in halophytes such as Tamarix chinensis24 and Thellungiella salsuginea25.

Building on this foundation, gene duplication is increasingly understood to influence the architecture of gene regulatory networks (GRNs) controlling stress-responsive transcriptional programs. Salinity stress, in particular, induces rapid transcriptional reprogramming, requiring activation and repression of numerous genes mediated by diverse transcription factors (TFs). Recent studies have revealed conserved salt-responsive GRNs in which certain TF families, such as WRKY, act as central regulatory hubs, facilitating plant adaptation to fluctuating environments26. Additional work has identified a GRF-centered regulatory network that orchestrates transcriptional responses to salinity27. GRNs integrate TFs and their target genes to form dynamic and modular systems capable of fine-tuned stress responses across a broad temporal scale, ranging from immediate early reactions to long-term acclimation28. Such regulatory flexibility enables plants to maintain cellular homeostasis and physiological function under fluctuating environmental conditions. Alterations in network topology, such as the emergence of WGD-derived TF hubs, or the stress-specific recruitment of additional TF families, can therefore reprogram plant responses to salinity. However, the extent and functional implications of this regulatory rewiring remain largely unexplored in halophytic plants.

To address this knowledge gap, we generated a chromosome-scale, highly contiguous reference assembly of H. arachnoideus cv. “Tenggeli”, a domesticated cultivar derived from wild H. arachnoideus, by combining PacBio high-fidelity (HiFi) long-read sequencing technology with high-throughput chromatin conformation capture (Hi-C). Leveraging this resource, we performed transcriptomic profiling under moderate (200 mM NaCl) and high (400 mM NaCl) salinity treatments and constructed GRNs to examine transcriptional responses to salt stress. Through the integrative analysis of genome structure, gene duplication patterns, stress-responsive gene expression, and GRN architecture, our study elucidates how these genomic features collectively shape salt-responsive regulation in H. arachnoideus. These findings provide insights into the evolutionary genomics of salt adaptation in this halophyte and offer valuable genetic resources for future efforts to develop salt-resilient crops through molecular breeding.

Results

Assembly of a high-quality, chromosome-scale H. arachnoideus genome

Given its wide distribution across high-pH saline–alkaline habitats (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1a), a chromosome-scale genome assembly is essential for investigating the genomic basis of salt tolerance in H. arachnoideus. We sequenced the cultivar H. arachnoideus cv. “Tenggeli” (Supplementary Fig. 1b) using PacBio HiFi, Hi-C, and Illumina paired-end technologies. In total, ~235 Gb of raw sequencing data were generated, including 50 Gb (~59.4×) of PacBio HiFi reads and 120 Gb ( ~ 142.6×) of Hi-C data for de novo assembly, along with 55 Gb ( ~ 65.3×) of Illumina paired-end reads for genome survey (Supplementary Table 1). A 19-mer analysis estimated the genome size to be approximately 770 Mb, with a heterozygosity rate of 0.81% and a repeat content of 58.85% (Supplementary Fig. 2).

The final assembly spanned approximately 841.6 Mb, exceeding the estimated genome size, likely reflecting differences in repeat resolution between sequencing platforms. The assembly achieved a scaffold N50 of 87.38 Mb (Supplementary Table 2), and 97.71% ( ~ 822.3 Mb) of the assembly was anchored onto nine pseudochromosomes ranging from 72 to 118 Mb (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 3, and Supplementary Table 3). Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) analysis using the embryophyta_odb10 dataset indicated a high level of completeness (97.50%; Supplementary Table 4), comparable to that of related Amaranthaceae s.l. species such as Haloxylon ammodendron (97.8%) and Sp. oleracea (97.2%) (Supplementary Table 5). Furthermore, the base-level quality score reached a consensus quality value (QV) of 45.99, underscoring the assembly’s high contiguity and accuracy (Supplementary Table 2).

Structural and functional annotations of H. arachnoideus genome

Using an integrative annotation pipeline that combined homology-based, transcriptome-based, and ab initio gene prediction methods, we identified 33,850 high-confidence protein-coding genes, with an average gene length of 4692.6 bp (Supplementary Tables 2 and 6). Over 99% of these genes were successfully localized on the assembled pseudochromosomes. Annotation completeness was supported by a BUSCO score of 97.4% (Supplementary Table 7). Functional annotation assigned putative functions to 93% of protein-coding genes based on homology to entries in multiple public databases (Supplementary Table 8).

In addition to protein-coding genes, we annotated non-coding RNA genes, including 2358 transfer RNA (tRNA), 1598 microRNAs (miRNAs), 810 small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), and 224 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) (Supplementary Table 9). Our analysis also identified 1276 TFs distributed across 69 TF families (Supplementary Table 10). Several TF families that are commonly associated with abiotic stress responses, such as bHLH (104 members), AP2/ERF (86), MYB (115), WRKY (50), and NAC (64). These families together accounted for ~32.84% of all predicted TFs, highlighting their central role in orchestrating salt-tolerance pathways and broader environmental adaptation mechanisms in H. arachnoideus.

LTR expansion and its implications for genome dynamics

A remarkably high proportion of repetitive sequences (73.01%) was identified in the H. arachnoideus genome (Supplementary Table 11). On a chromosomal scale, these repeats were predominantly enriched in gene-poor regions, while being significantly depleted in high-density gene clusters toward the chromosomal ends (Fig. 1b). Transposable elements (TEs) constituted a major fraction of the repeats, occupying 66.61% of the genome, dominated by long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons. Gypsy and Copia elements contributing 30.47% and 3.48% of the genome, respectively. An additional 7.98% (69.42 Mb) of the genome was made up of unclassified LTRs, likely representing lineage-specific insertions (Supplementary Table 11).

Analysis of LTR insertion times suggested a recent surge of transposition, with 67.56% of these elements inserted within the past one million years (Fig. 1c), supporting the inference that recent bursts of LTR activity have significantly contributed to genome expansion in H. arachnoideus. To further support this hypothesis, we performed comparative analyses across representative Amaranthaceae s.l. species with high-quality chromosome-scale assemblies. Total LTR content showed a strong positive correlation with genome size (Supplementary Table 12). Consistently, H. arachnoideus has an intermediate genome size ( ~ 842 Mb, ~43% LTR) among related species (e.g., B. vulgaris, ~567 Mb, ~22% LTR; Sp. oleracea, ~894 Mb, ~57% LTR) underscores the importance of lineage-specific TE proliferation in shaping genome architecture.

To further explore LTR evolutionary diversity, we conducted phylogenetic analysis of reverse transcriptase (RT) sequences, which identified 15 distinct evolutionary lineages within the H. arachnoideus genome (Fig. 1d). Gypsy elements were distributed across several evolutionary lineages, including Reina, Retand, Tekay, Athila, CRM, Galadriel and Ogre, whereas Copia elements were primarily assigned to Ale, Ivana, SIRE, and Bianca. This diversity highlights the multiple independent contributions of different LTR lineages to genome evolution.

Beyond their structural roles, LTRs also influence gene regulation. Genomic analyses revealed their physical association with protein-coding genes, as a considerable number of genes involved in metabolic pathways were found to have LTR element located promoter regions (up to 2 kb upstream) (Supplementary Fig. 4a). To assess the functional implications of this association, we examined gene expression under salt stress (see salt-stress transcriptome analysis below), transcriptomic analysis showed that only a subset of these LTR-associated genes (defined as genes with LTR within 2 kb upstream) showed differential expression (Supplementary Fig. 4b), suggesting LTR elements likely exert selective, gene-specific, or conditional regulatory influences rather than global effects on gene expression. Further supporting this notion, the promoter regions of salt-responsive differentially expressed genes (DEGs) showed marked depletion of LTR elements compared to the genomic background (Supplementary Fig. 4c). More notably, when compared to the general background of all LTR-associated genes, these promoters were further depleted in terms of both LTR number and sequence coverage (Supplementary Fig. 4d, e). These findings collectively support a model of an adaptive regulatory landscape, in which LTR-rich regions maintain transcriptional homeostasis, possibly through epigenetic silencing mechanisms, while LTR-depleted regions retain greater transcriptional plasticity, allowing flexible responses to environmental stresses such as salinity.

Phylogenetic position and gene family evolution in H. arachnoideus

To elucidate the evolutionary trajectory of H. arachnoideus and its genomic adaptations, we conducted comparative analyses among H. arachnoideus and eight representative angiosperm species, including five from the Amaranthaceae s.l. family (Salicornia europaea, Suaeda aralocaspica, Ha. ammodendron, Sp. oleracea, and B. vulgaris), and three additional species (Solanum lycopersicum, Glycine max, and Arabidopsis thaliana). Orthogroup clustering identified 7983 gene families shared across all nine species (Supplementary Fig. 5). Using 784 single-copy orthologs, we then reconstructed a maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogeny with A. thaliana as the outgroup (Fig. 2a). The resulting topology within and among the major clades was congruent with previous studies16,29, confirming that Halogeton is closely related to Haloxylon, a genus well adapted to arid and saline environments30. The divergence time between these two lineages was estimated at approximately 11.38 million years ago (MYA, 95% CI: 7.30–16.44), shortly after the Middle Miocene Climate Optimum ( ~ 15 MYA), a period characterized by elevated global temperatures and sea levels31. The subsequent global cooling and regional aridification, particularly in Central Asia, likely imposed selection pressures that drove niche differentiation and lineage-specific adaptations in this clade.

Gene family evolution analyses revealed that H. arachnoideus underwent lineage-specific gene turnover following its divergence from related taxa, with 778 expanded and 1898 contracted gene families (Fig. 2a). Expanded gene families were particularly enriched in functional categories related to stress-response, genome stability and DNA repair mechanisms, and energy-related nucleotide transport (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Data 1), whereas the contracted ones were overrepresented in developmental regulation and cellular signaling (Supplementary Data 2). These patterns suggest that the expansion of stress-related gene families, coupled with the contraction of growth-associated pathways, reflects a genomic reconfiguration that may favor enhanced tolerance to saline environments.

To evaluate whether well-known salt-tolerance genes followed this expansion trend, we examined the High Affinity K⁺ Transporter 1 (HKT1) gene family, which encodes high-affinity K⁺ transporters critical for sodium exclusion and ion balance in halophytes32. Interestingly, the HKT1 family did not exhibit any evidence of gene copy number expansion in H. arachnoideus, yet its HKT1 orthologs exhibited significant transcriptional responses to salt stress (Supplementary Fig. 6). This indicates that rather than expanding its repertoire through gene duplication, H. arachnoideus likely enhances salt tolerance primarily through transcriptional activation of key HKT1 pathway genes.

Whole-genome duplication history in H. arachnoideus

To explore the WGD history of H. arachnoideus, we analyzed synonymous substitution rate (Ks) distributions for paralogous and orthologous gene pairs in H. arachnoideus, Ha. ammodendron, and B. vulgaris. Ha. ammodendron was selected as the closest phylogenetically related species with a chromosome-scale genome assembly, enabling robust detection of collinearity blocks and WGD signatures, while B. vulgaris served as a benchmark due to its well-resolved WGD history29,33. As expected, the Ks distribution in B. vulgaris showed a prominent peak corresponding to the γ whole-genome hexaploidization event shared among core eudicots. Parallel Ks peaks observed in H. arachnoideus and Ha. ammodendron suggest that both species have retained signatures of this ancient γ-WGD event (Fig. 2c). No evidence was found for any lineage-specific WGD events in either species after their divergence. Extensive one to one collinearity among the three species further reinforced this conclusion, although several structural rearrangements were detected, reflecting ongoing chromosomal evolution (Supplementary Fig. 7).

As expected for an ancient WGD, extensive genome fractionation has markedly reduced paralog retention, consistent with the BUSCO results showing that 95.6% of conserved orthologs occur as single-copy in this genome (Supplementary Table 7). Nevertheless, intragenomic synteny analysis revealed that several large duplicated blocks with conserved gene collinearity remain detectable across the genome, providing structural evidence of the ancient WGD event (Fig. 2d). Homologous gene pairs within these segments exhibit relatively high Ks values, consistent with their origin from ancient duplication events and supporting the presence of an ancestral WGD in H. arachnoideus (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Gene duplications may have contributed to salt tolerance

To assess how different duplication events have shaped salt tolerance in H. arachnoideus, we classified gene duplicates into WGD, TD, proximal duplication (PD), transposed duplication (TRD), and dispersed duplication (DSD) (Supplementary Table 13). Gene family expansion was dominated by small-scale duplications (Supplementary Table 14). Among all duplication types, WGD-derived genes exhibited the lowest nonsynonymous to synonymous substitution rate ratio (Ka/Ks) (Fig. 3a), and were enriched for housekeeping functions related to DNA-templated transcription and protein phosphorylation (Supplementary Data 3).

Comparative analysis revealed that TF families as a whole were significantly enriched among WGD-derived genes relative to non-TF genes (P < 0.001; Supplementary Data 4). Notably, AP2/ERF, bZIP, C2H2, and WRKY TF families were overrepresented in the WGD category (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Data 4) but not in small-scale duplication modes (DSD, PD, TRD, or TD; Supplementary Data 5). Moreover, they exhibited significantly lower Ka/Ks ratios than TF families originating from small-scale duplications (Supplementary Fig. 9), collectively supporting their role as likely dosage-sensitive regulators under strong evolutionary constraint to maintain stoichiometric balance.

Transcriptomic profiling under salt stress revealed that WGD-derived genes were significantly over-represented among salt-induced DEGs compared with non-WGD genes (Fig. 3c). Moreover, the proportion of WGD-derived DEGs increased markedly under high salinity (400 mM NaCl) relative to moderate-salinity (200 mM NaCl), highlighting the potential importance of deeply conserved, dosage-sensitive regulatory modules in orchestrating robust transcriptional responses to extreme salt stress.

In contrast, genes originating from small-scale duplication events, particularly TDs, showed distinct evolutionary and functional characteristics. TD genes showed elevated Ka/Ks ratios (Fig. 3a), indicative of relaxed selective constraints or potential adaptive divergence. Functionally, they were predominantly involved in responses to oxidative stress, detoxification, and secondary metabolism (Supplementary Data 6), pointing to specialization in stress-associated processes. Unlike WGD-derived genes, the relative abundance of TD-associated DEGs did not proportionally increase with rising salinity levels (Fig. 3c), suggesting that TD genes may function across a broader range of salinity conditions, possibly through coordinated or context-dependent regulation, rather than acting as key drivers of acute stress responses.

Physiological and transcriptomic response to salt stress

To investigate the physiological and transcriptional basis of salinity adaptation, two-month-old H. arachnoideus seedlings were exposed to moderate salinity (200 mM NaCl) and high salinity (400 mM NaCl), simulating ecologically relevant stress conditions (Supplementary Fig. 10). This dual treatment was designed to capture the transition from routine acclimation at moderate salinity to the emergency defense responses activated near the species’ tolerance threshold. Net Na⁺ fluxes, used as a proxy for physiological response, revealed distinct ion regulation dynamics between roots and leaf tissues of seedlings (Fig. 4a). During the initial exposure phase (0–6 h), Na⁺ efflux occurred primarily in roots, with increased fluxes under elevated salinity. As the treatment progressed (6–24 h), Na⁺ was progressively translocated to the leaves, reflecting systemic ion redistribution.

Approximately 230.48 Gb of high-quality RNA-seq data were generated from leaf and root tissues under control and salt-treated conditions, with uniform sequencing depth, high base quality, and strong concordance among biological replicates (Supplementary Data 7 and 8). Principal component analysis revealed clear separation by tissue type and sampling time, reflecting both spatial and temporal transcriptional responses to salt stress (Supplementary Fig. 11). Global transcriptome analysis revealed substantial transcriptional reprogramming in response to salt stress, clearly differentiating treated samples from controls (Supplementary Fig. 12). DEG analysis identified distinct stress-responsive patterns among different time points, salt concentrations, and tissues (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14, and Supplementary Data 9), with leaves and roots exhibiting contrasting response magnitudes and temporal trajectories.

Under moderate salinity, roots showed a rapid and transient transcriptional activation, with 1035 DEGs at 6 h, followed by a marked decrease to 261 DEGs at 24 h, suggesting initial stress perception followed by partial acclimation. In contrast, leaves showed a delayed yet progressive response, with DEG numbers rising from 495 to 871 across the same interval, consistent with the observed Na⁺ response dynamics in roots and leaves. Under high salinity, these tissue-specific trajectories diverged even further. Leaves displayed an amplified and delayed response, as DEG numbers surged more than fourfold, from 367 at 6 h to 1578 at 24 h, signifying a strong transcriptional mobilization once the stress signal was perceived. Roots, meanwhile, sustained their activation, with DEGs increasing from 676 to 933, reflecting prolonged physiological stress that required continuous transcriptional reconfiguration.

Dynamic gene expression underlying salt stress adaptation

We identified eight similar temporal expression patterns (Cluster1–Cluster8, hereafter, C1–C8) across roots and leaves tissues under both moderate and high salinity treatments (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses revealed that C2, C4, C5, C6, and C8 showed a significant enrichment trend in specific biological pathways, clear patterns could be identified. These dynamic expression clusters were significantly associated with core biological processes, including general biological regulation, rhythmic and circadian process, transcription and gene expression regulation, photosynthesis and energy metabolism and metabolic and biosynthetic process (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Data 10), underscoring their fundamental roles in plant salt tolerance mechanisms.

Under moderate salinity, both tissues showed similar enrichment, with pathways related to general biological regulation, transcription and gene expression regulation, and metabolic and biosynthetic processes (C6) showed a transient upregulation followed by recovery, whereas genes associated with rhythmic and circadian processes (C4) displayed a delayed downregulation pattern (Fig. 4c). These patterns suggest that under moderate salinity, the two tissues may adopt comparable response strategies and optimize energy allocation through metabolic trade-offs to cope with stress.

Under high salinity, the enrichment profiles of the two tissues showed the differences. In roots, enrichment was mainly detected in pathways associated with photosynthesis and energy metabolism (C5), indicating that root tissues employ a concentration-dependent adaptive strategy in which moderate and high salinity activate distinct functional pathways to maintain cellular homeostasis. In contrast, leaves retained an overall pattern similar to that under moderate salinity but exhibited more dispersed gene expression and substantially fewer significantly enriched pathways. This contraction of GO enrichment suggests that under high salinity, leaf transcriptional activity becomes restricted to a limited set of core metabolic and protective processes.

Gene regulatory networks of salt-responsive transcriptional modulators

Our analysis revealed that TF families function through diverse and dynamic regulatory mechanisms to cope with salt stress, as evidenced by the varying composition and abundance of TF families across distinct temporal expression clusters and tissue types (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Data 11). Several prominent salt-responsive TF families, including MYB, WRKY, NAC, bHLH, and AP2/ERF, were enriched across multiple temporal expression clusters. Furthermore, clear tissue-specific enrichment patterns were detected, with the activation of bHLH, C2C2, GRAS, and MYB families in roots aligning with their known functions in regulating ion homeostasis and hormone signaling34–37. Conversely, the enrichment of WRKY, HB, and ZF-HD families in leaves suggests specialized roles in mitigating oxidative damage and fine-tuning photosynthetic processes under salt stress38–40.

To explore putative regulatory architecture, we constructed GRNs from time-series transcriptome data using the GENIE3 algorithm (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Data 12 and 13). These GRNs represent putative TF-target relationships inferred from co-expression patterns, and thus serve as hypothesis-generating models41–43. Under high salinity, the GRN expanded in size (more nodes and edges) and became more modular, as indicated by a higher weighted modularity (Q) (Supplementary Table 15). This restructuring was accompanied by longer characteristic path lengths, an increase in connected components, and the emergence of numerous smaller subnetworks. This transition from a centralized architecture toward a decentralized, modular organization may represent a distinct regulatory regime activated under severe stress. Across stress conditions, AP2/ERF, MYB, WRKY, and bHLH TF families consistently have high connectivity in the networks, targeting broad repertoires of downstream genes. While additional TF families showed stress-dependent recruitment (e.g., C2C2 under moderate salinity; WRKY and NAC under high salinity). The incorporation of additional TF families, such as bZIP, zf-HD, and B3, further expanded the distributed regulatory architecture.

By integrating gene duplication patterns with salt-responsive GRNs, we found significant enrichment of both WGD- and TD-genes within these networks (Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18), indicating that both ancient and recent duplication events may have contributed to the regulatory architecture of salt stress responses, albeit without clear evidence of functional enrichment in specific network positions.

We further inferred TF-TF interactions at 6 h and 24 h to summarize early and late response phases. Distinct regulatory patterns emerged under both moderate and high salinity (Fig. 5c, d). Under moderate stress, the networks exhibited a relatively centralized but loosely connected structure, indicating limited coordination among TF families. In contrast, the high stress-induced network showed increased regulatory connectivity, with 24 h exhibiting higher interactivity than 6 h, supporting the notion of a progressive intensification of transcriptional regulation in response to increasing salt stress. MYB and AP2/ERF TF families maintained active regulatory interactions across both time points, whereas bHLHs were primarily involved at 24 h. WRKY and NAC TF families were broadly engaged at 24 h and emerged as key regulators specifically under high salinity. The differential regulatory patterns between these two time points suggest a potential shift in transcriptional strategies over time.

Discussion

Plant adaptation to saline environments represents a remarkable case of environmental tolerance driven by complex genomic and regulatory innovations. In this study, we present a chromosome-scale genome of the halophyte H. arachnoideus and combined comparative genomics with salt-stress transcriptomics to dissect the potential mechanisms underlying its salt tolerance. Our analyses highlight several evolutionary features associated with salt tolerance, including LTR dynamics, gene duplication modes, and the reconfiguration of transcriptional regulatory networks, which together outline a genomic context potentially associated with transcriptional coordination under stress.

A notable characteristic of the H. arachnoideus genome is the pronounced depletion of LTR elements in the promoter regions of salt-responsive genes. While LTR elements are known to generate genetic variation and influence the expression of adjacent genes19,44,45, their proximity to gene promoters may compromise transcriptional fidelity by disrupting cis-regulatory elements, altering chromatin accessibility, or triggering epigenetic silencing20,46,47. The observed pattern is consistent with the action of purifying selection acting to preserve the integrity of regulatory circuits against deleterious TE insertions20. At the same time, alternative explanations such as insertional bias or residual assembly uncertainty in repetitive contexts cannot be fully excluded. Nonetheless, the specific underrepresentation of LTRs around promoters of salt-responsive genes, rather than a genome-wide uniform pattern, suggests that minimizing TE presence in these regulatory regions may help maintain precise and reliable transcriptional activation under stress. For H. arachnoideus, maintaining robust transcriptional activation of stress-response genes under challenging environmental conditions may take precedence over the potential evolutionary benefits conferred by TE-mediated regulatory innovation, although this interpretation remains inferential and will require further functional and population-level validation.

Complementing this regulatory stability shaped by TE dynamics, patterns of gene duplication may also be relevant to the evolution of salt tolerance in H. arachnoideus. Both WGDs and small-scale events, such as TDs, have long been recognized as important contributors to plant stress responses, despite fundamental differences in their origins, gene retention rates, and functional divergence patterns24,48–50. In diploid lineages, the remnants of ancient WGDs usually undergo extensive genome fractionation, a process involving chromosomal rearrangements, gene conversion events, TE activity, and epigenetic modifications, leading to the preferential retention of a subset of duplicated gene51,52. In H. arachnoideus, the ancient WGD shared with other Amaranthaceae s.l. lineages have been heavily fractionated, yet the surviving duplicates remain significantly enriched for salt-responsive TF families, particularly those involved in regulatory networks (e.g., AP2/ERF, bZIP, WRKY). This pattern aligns with the gene balance hypothesis, which posits that dosage-sensitive genes involved in complex networks are preferentially preserved post-WGD53,54. While our evidence is correlative, the preferential retention and salt responsiveness of these ancestral duplicates suggest that they may provide a potential basis for the regulatory repertoire that could support coordinated stress transcriptional programs in extreme environments.

In contrast, TD-derived genes in H. arachnoideus exhibited more variable expression patterns across different salt concentrations, suggesting their involvement in transcriptional plasticity and context-dependent regulatory responses. Such expression dynamics support the view that TDs, though often lineage-specific and less conserved, serve as important sources of adaptive novelty, especially in species without recent polyploidization55. The flexibility afforded by TDs may allow rapid rewiring of stress-response pathways in response to fluctuating environmental conditions56. Notably, both WGD- and TD-derived genes were significantly over-represented within salt-responsive GRNs inferred from transcriptomic data. This co-enrichment motivates a working model in which WGD-derived genes primarily contribute to regulatory stability and centrality, whereas TD-derived genes preferentially support environmental fine-tuning and modular expansion. However, this division-of-labor model remains hypothetical and necessitates further validation.

The GRNs inferred from our transcriptomic data provide an initial integrative framework for exploring the transcriptional programs associated with salt responsiveness in H. arachnoideus. These networks highlight putative interactions between TFs and their downstream targets, offering testable hypotheses about how coordinated transcriptional responses may emerge under stress57. Several TF families, including AP2/ERF, MYB, WRKY, and bHLH, exhibit high connectivity in these networks and are associated with genes involved in cellular homeostasis and stress-related regulatory processes58. In parallel, tissue-specific and stress intensity-dependent recruitment of auxiliary TF families appears to modulate the transcriptional landscape, providing a potential mechanism for fine-tuning gene expression across organs and stress regimes.

Under high salinity conditions, the inferred GRN of H. arachnoideus showed a more decentralized and modular topology, with more predicted TF-target links and less apparent dominance by a small set of highly connected nodes. In principle, such a decentralized organization may provide redundant control mechanisms and promote parallel regulatory routes, thereby reshaping the coordination of stress responses. These features could be compatible with a regulatory organization that may support flexible coordination of transcriptional responses under variable or extreme conditions, but this interpretation remains inferential and requires validation from multiple independent lines of evidence. At the same time, we emphasize that these inferences are based on GENIE3-derived networks, which are predictive in nature and can include false positives. Network complexity and connectivity should therefore be interpreted as indicators of enhanced transcriptional coordination potential rather than direct evidence of adaptive regulatory evolution. The GRNs presented here represent putative regulatory relationships, and their biological relevance, including the central roles of specific TFs and the functional consequences of network modularity, will require future validation using experimental approaches such as ChIP-seq and reporter assays. This will also represent an important direction for our future research.

Interpretation of these GRNs is constrained by the temporal resolution of the transcriptomic dataset, which included two time points. This design enables the detection of major shifts in the regulatory landscape between these phases, but is not sufficient to resolve the full sequence of TF deployment or finer transient regulatory transitions. Future studies incorporating denser time-series sampling will be necessary to provide a more comprehensive view of the stress-response process. In addition, untreated controls were sampled only at 0 h, meaning that potential temporal effects, such as diurnal variation, cannot be fully separated from stress-induced responses. Although such effects are expected to be limited given the short treatment duration, this limitation highlights the importance of including time-matched controls in future long-term experiments to improve interpretability.

In summary, this study provides a foundational view of the genomic architecture and regulatory organization associated with extreme salt tolerance in H. arachnoideus. Future work integrating high-resolution time-course experiments, epigenomic profiling, functional validation, and population-level analyses will be essential to translate insights from H. arachnoideus to broader efforts in breeding and engineering salt-tolerant crops.

Methods

Plant materials and salt treatment

Tender leaf and root tissues were collected for genomic sequencing from a mature individual of H. arachnoideu cv. “Tenggeli”, which was cultivated under natural lighting and photoperiod conditions at the Lanzhou Horticultural Experimental Station (Gansu, China) (Supplementary Fig. 1b). For salt stress treatments, seeds of H. arachnoideus cv. “Tenggeli” were first surface sterilized in 4% sodium hypochlorite solution for 15 min, followed by thoroughly rinsed with sterile water. After a 3-day chilling period at 4 °C, the seeds were transferred to moist Petri dishes and allowed to germinate at 25 °C until the radicles reached approximately 1 cm in length. The germinated seedlings were subsequently transferred into plant cultivation boxes and grown in a greenhouse at the State Key Laboratory of Grassland and Agro-ecosystems, College of Ecology, Lanzhou University. Growth conditions were controlled at a temperature of 24 ± 1 °C, relative humidity of 45%–55%, and a 14 h light/10 h dark photoperiod.

Two-month-old seedlings were subjected to either moderate (200 mM NaCl) or high (400 mM NaCl) salinity stress (Supplementary Fig. 10). The 200 mM concentration represents a critical threshold for halophyte identification, while the high salinity treatment was applied to investigate the molecular mechanisms under extreme salinity, serving as a standard benchmark for salt tolerance in halophytes such as Suaeda salsa59, Spergularia marina60, and Limonium bicolor61.

To investigate physiological and genome-wide responses, leaf and root tissues from salt-treated seedlings were harvested at 6 and 24 h post-treatment under different salinity concentrations. Untreated controls were sampled at 0 h only and served as the common baseline control for all treatment. Net Na+ fluxes were measured using Non-invasive Micro-test Technology. Samples designated for transcriptomic analysis were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction and library preparation. Three biologically independent replicates were included for each treatment.

Genome, transcriptome sequencing, and genome survey

Genomic DNA was extracted from young leaves with a modified CTAB method62 and subsequently employed for multi-platform sequencing, including Illumina paired-end sequencing, PacBio high-fidelity (HiFi) sequencing, and high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) data sequencing. DNA purity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Illumina paired-end libraries with insert sizes of ~300 bp were generated and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina, San Diego, USA) with a read length of 150 bp. Genome survey was implemented using the k-mer method in Jellyfish v2.2.1063 and GenomeScope v2.064, with parameters of 19-mers to estimate genome sizes, heterozygosity, and repetitive content.

For HiFi sequencing, 20 kb SMRTbell libraries were prepared with the SMRTbell Prep Kit 3.0 (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, USA) following standard PacBio protocol and sequenced on the PacBio Revio (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, USA) platform in the circular consensus sequencing (CCS) mode. After sequencing, consensus HiFi reads were generated using CCS software (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/ccs) with default parameters. For Hi-C library construction, fresh leaf tissues were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde, processed according to the standard Hi-C protocol, including chromatin digestion with DpnII. The resulting libraries were sequenced on the MGI-SEQ 2000 (MGI Tech, Shenzhen, China) platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads for chromatin interaction analysis.

Total RNA was isolated from leaf and root tissues using RNA Easy Fast Plant Tissue Kit (TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing, China). For genome annotation, RNA was extracted from leaf and root tissues of untreated mature plants. For salt stress transcriptomic analysis, RNA was extracted from leaf and root tissues across all treatment groups. RNA integrity and concentration were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA), and only samples with an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) > 7.0 were used for library construction. RNA-seq libraries were constructed using the MGIEasy Fast RNA Library Prep Kit (MGI Tech, Shenzhen, China) and sequenced on the MGI-SEQ 2000 (MGI Tech, Shenzhen, China) platform (2 × 150 bp), generating approximately 6 Gb of raw data per sample.

Genome assembly and chromosome scaffolding

The H. arachnoideus genome was initially assembled from HiFi reads using Hifiasm v0.20.0-r63965 with default parameters to generate contig sequences. This draft assembly was scaffolded to pseudochromosome-level using Hi-C reads, which were quality-controlled with Fastp v.0.20.066 with default parameters, aligned to the contig-level assembly using BWA-MEM v0.7.17-r118867 with parameters “-5SP”. Duplicate reads were removed with samblaster v0.1.2668, and low-quality or artefactual pairs were filtered using samtools v1.1069 and the HapHiC v1.0.670 utility “filter_bam” with parameters “1 –nm 3”. Scaffold clustering and ordering were performed using HapHiC with default settings. The final files (.hic and .assembly) were imported into Juicebox v1.11.0871 for manual check and adjustment.

Genome annotation

Repetitive sequences were annotated using the Extensive De Novo TE Annotator (EDTA) pipeline v2.2.272 with parameters “–sensitive 1 –anno 1” to generate the non-redundant de novo TE libraries and annotated the intact long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons. TEsorter v1.273 with default parameters was employed to reclassify elements initially annotated as “LTR/unknown” by the EDTA pipeline, ensuring a more accurate identification of LTRs.

For a comprehensive prediction of protein-coding genes, repetitive sequences within the genome assembly were first masked using RepeatMasker v4.1.0 (https://github.com/Dfam-consortium/RepeatMasker). Subsequently, gene annotation was conducted through an integrated approach combining homology-based, transcriptome-based, and ab initio predictions. For homology-based gene prediction, publicly available protein sequences from four Amaranthaceae species, Sa. europaea74 (https://www.salicorniadb.org/), Sp. oleracea75 (http://spinachbase.org/ftp/genome/Monoe-Viroflay/), B. vulgaris76 (BioProject PRJNA780534), and Ha. ammodendron77 (BioProject PRJNA1221346), were aligned against the genome assembly to identify potential homologous region. The resulting alignments were further refined using GeneWise v2.4.178 with default parameters to predict precise gene models. For transcriptome-based prediction, the quality of RNA-seq reads was first assessed using FastQC v0.11.9 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). The raw reads were then processed with Fastp v0.20.0 with default parameters to remove adaptor sequences and low-quality reads. The trimmed RNA-seq reads from leaf and root tissues were aligned to the assembled genome using HISAT2 v2.1.079 with parameters “–max-intronlen 20000 –dta –score-min L, 0.0, −0.4”, and transcripts were assembled with StringTie v2.2.180 with default parameters, and subsequently processed using TransDecoder v5.5.0 under default settings (https://github.com/TransDecoder/TransDecoder) to predict open reading frames, and redundant gene models with amino acid sequence identities greater than 80% were filtered out to ensure non-redundancy. For ab initio gene prediction, AUGUSTUS v3.3.381 was used with parameters “–allow_hinted_splicesites = gcag,atac –min_intron_len = 30 –softmasking = 1 –gff3 = on”, incorporating gene model training based on evidence from homology and transcriptome-based predictions. The gene models derived from TransDecoder, AUGUSTUS, and GeneWise were integrated using EvidenceModeler82 with default parameters to generate a comprehensive, non-redundant consensus gene set. Finally, the consensus gene set was analyzed using iTAK83 to identify TF genes.

Functional annotation was performed by aligning the predicted protein sequences using DIAMOND v2.0.1584 against multiple databases, including the Non-redundant Protein Sequence Database (NR)85, InterProScan86, Swiss-Prot87, Clusters of Orthologous Groups and Eukaryotic Orthologous Groups (COG/KOG)88, and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway database89. Additionally, GO annotation was performed using the KEGG Orthology Based Annotation System (KOBAS, accessible at http://bioinfo.org/kobas/annotate/)90.

Non-coding RNAs, including miRNA and snRNA, were predicted using INFERNAL v1.1.5 against the Rfam database v.15.0 (http://rfam.xfam.org/)91. tRNAs were annotated using tRNAscan-SE v2.0.1292. rRNAs were annotated by mapping Sp. oleracea rRNA sequences to the H. arachnoideus genome using BLASTN v2.12.0 (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi).

Assessment of genome assembly and annotation quality

The genome assembly and annotation quality were thoroughly evaluated using two assessment tools. Base-level accuracy of the assembly was assessed using Merqury v1.4.193, by generating Meryl databases from Illumina short reads to calculate QV scores. Additionally, the completeness and accuracy of genome assembly and gene annotations were evaluated using Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO v5.7.1)94 with the embryophyta_odb10 database.

Insertion time estimation and phylogenetic analysis of LTRs

To estimate the insertion time of transposable elements (TEs), we first extracted full-length long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons from the .intact.gff3 file generated by the EDTA pipeline. The insertion time was calculated using the formula T=k2μ, where K represents the sequence divergence rate between the 5′ and 3′ LTR sequences, and μ indicates the neutral mutation rate set as 7.54 × 10−9 mutations per base pair per year95.

Clade-level classification of LTRs was performed using TEsorter v1.2 with the parameters “-db rexdb-plant”. The reverse transcriptase (RT) domains of LTR-RTs were identified and aligned using scripts provided by TEsorter for phylogenetic analysis. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Maximum Likelihood method implemented in IQ-TREE v2.3.696, and subsequently visualized and polished using FigTree v1.4.4 (https://github.com/rambaut/figtree/).

Gene family clusters and phylogenomic analyses

To infer the evolutionary trajectory of the H. arachnoideus genome, we performed comparative analyses using protein sequences derived from the longest transcripts of H. arachnoideus and eight representative plant species: Sa. europaea, Ha. ammodendron, Sp. oleracea, B. vulgaris, Su. aralocaspica97 (BioProject PRJNA428881), So. lycopersicum98 (BioProject PRJDB16644), G. max99 (BioProject PRJNA19861), and A. thaliana100 (BioProject PRJNA10719). These species were selected to determine the evolutionary placement of H. arachnoideus within a broader phylogenetic framework, ensuring adequate taxonomic coverage and reliable rooting of the phylogenetic tree. Orthologous gene families were identified using OrthoFinder v2.5.2101 based on an all-versus-all sequence similarity search conducted with DIAMOND BLAST using the parameters “-M msa -S diamond -t 24 -a 24”. A ML phylogenetic tree was constructed based on single-copy orthologs using MAFFT v7.526102 and IQ-TREE v2.3.6 with parameter “-m MFP -B 1000 –bnni” for model selection and 1000 bootstrap replicates. A. thaliana served as the outgroup for tree rooting.

Divergence times were estimated using the MCMCtree program of PAML v4.10.7103 with calibration points: G. max and A. thaliana (102.0–112.5) million years ago (Mya) and Sp. oleracea and B. vulgaris (27.9–62.4) Mya. Both calibrated points were obtained from Timetree (https://timetree.org/, accessed 12 Nov 2024). CAFE104 with the parameters “lambda -s -p 0.05” was applied to compute changes in gene families along each lineage of the phylogenetic tree.

Whole-genome duplication analysis

To identify putative whole-genome duplication (WGD) events in the evolutionary history of H. arachnoideus, we analyzed the distribution of synonymous substitution rates (Ks) among paralogous gene pairs, along with genomic synteny relationships. This analysis was conducted using Ha. ammodendron as the closest relative with a publicly available high-quality genome, and B. vulgaris as a well-annotated representative within the broader Amaranthaceae sensu lato family. Ks values were calculated for anchor gene pairs within both intra- and inter-genomic syntenic blocks among H. arachnoideus, Ha. ammodendron, and B. vulgaris. Putative homologous genes were identified through BLASTP searches (E-value < 1e-5) of protein sequences, with Ks values estimated using the WGDI pipeline105 under the Yang-Nielsen (YN) model.

For genomic synteny analysis, H. arachnoideus protein sequences were self-aligned using BLASTP followed by collinearity analysis with MCScanX106. Interspecific synteny between H. arachnoideus and the two species Ha. ammodendron and B. vulgaris was analyzed using all-versus-all protein alignments in MCScanX. Collinear regions visualized using JCVI v1.0.5107.

Gene duplication modes in H. arachnoideus were classified into five categories, whole-genome duplication, TD, PD, TRD, and DSD using DupGen_finder108 with an E-value cutoff of 1e-10.

Transcriptome profiling and differential expression analysis under salt stress

Raw reads were processed to generate clean reads by adapter trimming and quality filtering using Fastp v0.20.0 with default parameters. Each RNA-seq library yielded ~45–65 million clean paired-end reads, consistently maintaining Q30 values > 96% and GC contents of ~44%. Sequencing depth was balanced across all libraries, with no samples showing abnormal deviations (within 0.5× to 2× of the median depth) (Supplementary Data 7). The robustness and reproducibility of the data were further confirmed by high correlation coefficients among biological replicates (Supplementary Data 8). The resulting trimmed reads were aligned to the assembled genome using HISAT2 v2.1.0 with parameters “–max-intronlen 20000 –dta –score-min L,0.0,−0.4”. Transcript abundance was quantified as Transcripts Per Kilobase Million (TPM) using StringTie v2.2.1, which normalizes read counts based on both gene length and the total number of mapped reads. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the pca function in the PCAtools R package v2.18.0 (https://github.com/kevinblighe/PCAtools). To identify genes exhibiting similar expression dynamics, trend analysis and clustering were performed using the Short Time-series Expression Miner (STEM) algorithm available on the OmicShare platform (www.omicshare.com/tools). For differential expression analysis, the prepDE.py script (https://github.com/gpertea/stringtie) was used to convert StringTie output into a count matrix. Differential expression genes were identified using DEseq2 v1.42.0109, with significance defined as an absolute fold change > 2 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 relative to control samples.

Gene regulatory networks construction

To reconstruct salt stress-responsive GRNs, TPM expression matrices of DEGs from the moderate salinity and high salinity treatments were used as input for the GENIE3 R package v1.28.041. Candidate TFs were identified based on the iTAK annotation results. The GENIE3 algorithm was run with nTrees = 1000, and network robustness was assessed using a bootstrap consensus strategy. Specifically, 1000 bootstrap iterations were performed with 80% random subsampling, and only regulatory edges consistently detected in most iterations (frequency > 0.7) were retained. The resulting consensus network showed strong concordance with the full-sample network (Pearson r > 0.9) (Supplementary Data 12), indicating stable inference.

Network organization was assessed by calculating weighted modularity (Q)110 and comparing it with degree-preserving rewired null models using permutation tests (reporting p-values and z-scores). Regulatory edges between TFs and their potential target genes were inferred, and ranked by weight scores. For visualization purposes, only edges with weights greater than 0.05 in a given network, or with a change in regulatory strength greater than 0.03 across time points, were retained and exported. The resulting GRNs were visualized using Cytoscape111.

Gene Ontology enrichment analysis

Enrichment analysis was performed using R package topGO v.2.58.0112, applying both the “classic” and “elim” algorithms with Fisher test. Semantic similarity among GO terms was calculated using the simplifyEnrichment R package113 based on the “classic” topGO results. We selected the “classic” algorithm because the “elim” algorithm is overly conservative, removing a large number of biologically meaningful GO terms and thus preventing effective topological network analysis.

In the case of DEG-based enrichment, genes undetected in the transcriptome were omitted from the background gene set to ensure statistical relevance. The analysis was conducted using the “classic” algorithm and Fisher’s exact test to infer enrichment trends across different clusters. This precaution was followed by a Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) correction applied to all tested GO terms to control the false discovery rate.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed in R v4.4.1. Differences among more than two independent groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test to identify pairwise group differences (Fig. 3a). For comparisons between two independent groups, the Mann–Whitney U test was used to determine the statistical significance of differences (Supplementary Figs. 9, 10, 13, and 14). Correlation was assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (Supplementary Table 12, Supplementary Data 8, and Supplementary Table 15). Fisher’s exact test was applied to evaluate group-wise frequency differences (Fig. 3c) and enrichment relationships for specific gene categories (Figs. 3b and 4e, Supplementary Data 4, 5, and 11). Enrichment analyses were performed using a non-parametric permutation test (Supplementary Fig. 4c–e, Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18, and Supplementary Table 15). Statistical significance was defined as follows: not significant nsP > 0.05, *P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001. To ensure reproducibility, all physiological and transcriptomic experiments were performed with three biological replicates. Each biological replicate was defined as an independently grown and treated individual plant, from which samples were collected and measured independently.
