A STING-activating biomimetic mineralized nanovaccine triggers robust anti-tumor immunity
Authors: Suxing Tuo, Yihan Wang, Meng Tian, Lixia Su, Jing Dong, Kefeng Lv, Wencan He, Qing Feng, Shan Chen, Daozhu Dong, Xiaoxu Li, Kejun Zhong, Yueming Zhang, Bo Kong, Lan He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumor nanovaccines have attracted great interest recently, due to a variety of advantages including high stability, efficient antigen packing and delivery, and the capacity to elicit sustained anti-tumor immune responses. Despite these advantages, existing nanovaccines are constrained by intricate manufacturing procedures and high production costs, prompting a need to develop simpler and more cost-effective solutions. In this study, we introduce a novel STING-activating tumor nanovaccine, designated OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA. The preparation of OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA nanovaccine is a straightforward process involving the self-assembly of Mn2+ and DNA molecules into nanospheres, which are then crosslinked with both antigenic peptides and antigen-presenting cells (APC)-targeting peptides. Our findings reveal that the OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA nanovaccine facilitates the delivery of antigens to APCs, activates STING signaling pathway effectively and triggering robust cellular immune responses. Results from xenograft mouse tumor model demonstrate a remarkable therapeutic potential of OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA in combating tumors. Collectively, our work presents a new strategy offering OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA as an uncomplicated and economical nanovaccine for potent tumor immunotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-026-04320-3.Keywords: Nanovaccine, STING-activating nanovaccine, Mn-DNA nanospheres, SR-B1, Tumor immunotherapy

Introduction

Tumor vaccines, together with CAR-T therapy and immune checkpoint blockade therapy, have emerged as the three most promising cancer immunotherapy strategies recently. However, a significant proportion of cancer patients still fail to benefit from these immunotherapies. Recent studies have identified STING (stimulator of interferon genes) as a key target in tumor immunotherapy [1–5], primarily through its role in the innate immune system’s antitumor signaling via the tumor DNA–DNA sensor–STING–type I IFN–CD8⁺ T cell axis [6–11]. Given STING’s pivotal function in anti-tumor immunity, substantial efforts have been directed toward agonist development. Nevertheless, first-generation cyclic dinucleotides exhibited limited clinical efficacy because of metabolic instability and suboptimal cellular uptake [12], and a new generation of non-CDN agonists, though promising preclinically, still await full clinical validation. In light of these challenges, STING-based nanovaccines have been described, offering substantial potential in the field of tumor immunotherapy [13–16].

DNA-based nanomaterials have attracted wide attention in recent years, including DNA origami, spherical nucleic acids, and metal-DNA self-assembly nanosphere [17]. The latter method is distinguished by its simplicity and universality, allowing a wide range of applications. Metal-DNA self-assembly nanospheres have been reported in several processes, such as nanozyme [18] and drug delivery [19], however, their application in nanovaccines has not been reported yet. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that the potential of DNA to act as physiological cellular stimuli to activate immune responses after being recognized by DNA sensors remains largely unexploited in nanovaccines.

Recent studies have revealed that Mn2+ effectively stimulates the STING signaling pathway [20]. Mn2+ activates STING pathway by increasing the enzymatic activity and sensitivity of cGAS to double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) to protect host from DNA virus infection. In addition, Mn2+ can also play a similar role in tumors and improve the efficacy of clinical tumor immunotherapy [21, 22]. Of note, it has been reported that incorporation of Mn2+ to nanovaccine design promoted the anti-tumor immune responses [12, 23, 24].

Here, we crosslinked DNA with APC-targeting peptide D-4F (Tp) [25] and OVA257–264 antigen peptide, followed by reacting with Mn2+ to develop a cancer nanovaccine. We named the nanovaccine OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA. The nanovaccine is supposed to exert effective anti-tumor effects based on the following principles: (1) Mn2+ and DNA self-assemble into Mn-DNA nanospheres, which can be taken up by APC cells owing to their small size; (2) the addition of targeting peptides enables OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA to selectively enter APC cells with improved efficiency; (3) OVA antigen peptide can be cross-presented to the surface of APC by MHC-I, recognized by OVA-specific CD8+ T cells and activated anti-tumor immune responses; (4) DNA activates the STING pathway after being recognized by DNA sensors (eg. cGAS); and (5) Mn2+ enhanced the activation of STING pathway. At this stage, an immune microenvironment with a large amount of type I interferon was created, and CD8+ T cells could be effectively activated (Scheme 1). OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA possess the benefits of straightforward synthesis, cost efficiency, and superior biocompatibility. Experimental results showed that OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA targeted APCs efficiently, activated APCs to produce type I interferon, promoted the antigen-presenting function of APCs, and enhanced the cytotoxic function of CD8+ T cells. Notably, tumor-bearing mice injected with OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA showed improved survival rates and smaller tumors with no obvious toxicity. In conclusion, we have developed a novel nanovaccine based on the enhancement of the STING signaling pathway in APC cells, which has remarkable efficacy and great potential in antitumor immunotherapy.

Results

Design, synthesis, and characterization of nanovaccines

The synthesis of OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA nanoparticles is illustrated in Scheme 1. First, we cross-linked peptides and DNA to obtain DNA-PEG-OVA or DNA-PEG-Tp. The OVA peptide or SR-B1 targeting peptide (Tp) was modified with cysteine and reacted with the crosslinker N3-PEG-MAL, and DBCO-modified DNA was added to the solution. Using the click chemical reaction of DBCO with the N3 group and the reaction between the sulfhydryl group of cysteine and maleimide, DNA-PEG-OVA and DNA-PEG-Tp were obtained. MnCl2 was then mixed with DNA-PEG-OVA and DNA-PEG-Tp and reacted at 95 °C for 1 h. OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA nanoparticles were finally obtained after ultrafiltration at 4 °C. Mn-DNA and OVA@Mn-DNA were synthesized in the same manner. More detailed synthesis procedures can be found in Materials and Methods. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 1A) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Fig. 1B) revealed that the nanoparticles were homogeneous and monodisperse. The hydrodynamic sizes of Mn-DNA and OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA nanoparticles were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) to be approximately 220 nm and 190 nm, respectively (Fig. 1C). In addition, the surface charge of the nanoparticles was analyzed using ζ-potential (Fig. 1D). The successful crosslinking of the OVA peptide and targeting peptide slightly reduced the potential of the nanoparticles.In addition, data showed that the loading efficiencies of the DNA and peptide crosslinked into the particles were 58.45% and 90.80%, respectively (Fig. 1E, F).

OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA can be taken up and delivered by cells efficiently

First, we assessed the potential cytotoxicity of the nanoparticles on RAW264.7 cells and observed that OVA@Mn-DNA showed no cytotoxicity at concentrations up to 30 μM (in terms of OVA), suggesting the excellent biocompatibility of the nanoparticles (Fig. 2A). Next, we synthesised fluorescent-labelled OVA(FITC)Tp@Mn-DNA(Cy5) to evaluate its cellular uptake. As a result, FITC-labelled OVA and Cy5-labelled DNA were observed in cells after incubation with OVA(FITC)Tp@Mn-DNA(Cy5) in RAW264.7 cells and bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDC) (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Figure S1). Furthermore, we wanted to verify whether the addition of the SR-B1 targeting peptide could enhance the delivery process. Since receptor-mediated endocytosis improves cellular uptake efficiency [26], the results showed that the OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA(Cy5) group exhibited better delivery efficiency than the OVA@Mn-DNA(Cy5) group (Fig. 2C). Consistently, the flow cytometry results showed that the fluorescence intensity of OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA(Cy5) was greater than that of OVA@Mn-DNA(Cy5) (Fig. 2D, E). Together, these data show that our NP could be efficiently taken up by APCsand SR-B1 targeting peptide further helps the process.

OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA promoted the activation of STING pathway in antigen presenting cells

To determine the optimal dosage of nanoparticles to stimulate cGAS-STING pathway, we first incubated them with RAW264.7 cells at different concentrations. Nanoparticles showed a stimulatory effect on IFNβ production at concentrationsof 1 μM, 3 μM, or 4 μM, and exhibited the greatest stimulatory effect at aconcentration of 2 μM (Fig. 3A). Therefore, 2 μM NPs was used for further experiments. Besides, the results showed that when SR-B1 Tp was added, RAW264.7 produced more IFNβ in OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA-treated group compared with OVA@Mn-DNA group (Fig. 3A). We further validated these findings in the dendritic cell line DC2.4 (Fig. 3B). To further verify the activation effect of nanoparticles on the STING signaling pathway, we detected key signaling proteins of the STING pathway using western blotting (Fig. 3C). OVA@Mn-DNA and OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA treatment enhanced the phosphorylation of STING, IRF3, and TBK1, and OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA groups showed greater stimulatory effects on the phosphorylation of IRF3 and STING than OVA@Mn-DNA, suggesting that the nanoparticles could activate the STING pathway efficiently, and the process can be further promoted by the addition of the targeting peptide.

Dendritic cells (DCs) are important professional antigen-presenting cells (APC) that specialise in inducing T-cell responses. DC2.4 is a DC cell line, and the upregulation of its cell surface co-stimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86 is a good indicator of DC maturation. Therefore, we used this cell line to examine the effect of the nanoparticles on APC function. As shown in Fig. 3D and E, the Mn-DNA and OVA@Mn-DNA groups increased the percentage of CD80+ CD86+ cells in DC2.4. Importantly, the OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA significantly induced DC maturation. In addition, we detected the antigen cross-presentation ability of DC2.4 cells and found that the OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA nanoparticle-treated group also showed the highest percentage of H-2Kb/SIINFEKL+ CD11c+cells, indicating that the antigen presentation of DC2.4 was the greatest under the stimulation of OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA nanoparticles. (Fig. 3F, G).

OVA/Tp@Mn- DNA NPs inhibit tumor growth in murine melanoma tumors

To explore the immunotherapeutic effect of nanovaccines in vivo, we established a B16-OVA xenograft tumor model in C57BL/6J mice and intratumorally injected the nanovaccines (Fig. 4A). B16-OVA tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into five groups and injected with saline (1), OVA peptide (2), Mn-DNA (3), OVA@Mn-DNA (4), and OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA (5). Both OVA@Mn-DNA and OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA treatments inhibited tumor growth. Notably, treatment with OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA significantly reduced tumor growth, suggesting a robust therapeutic effect of the nanovaccines (Fig. 4B, C). Next, we monitored the survival of mice in each group, and it was found that all of the tumor-bearing mice treated with PBS or OVA peptide died within one month, and the mice treated with Mn-DNA or OVA@Mn-DNA survived longer. In contrast, mice injected with the OVA/Tp@Mn-OVA nanovaccine had the highest survival rate, with 66.7% still alive at day 40 (Fig. 4D). In addition, we examined memory T cells in surviving mice. CD3+ CD8+ CD44+ CD62Lhigh was the hallmark of central memory T cell (TCM). Compared with untreated WT mice, the percentage of TCM was significantly higher in the OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA group, suggesting that OVA/Tp@Mn-OVA nanovaccine treatment led to the establishment of a long-term memory phenotype of CD8+ T cells in vivo (Fig. 4E, F).

Next, to examine the biocompatibility of our nanovaccines, the body weights of these differently treated mice were examined, and the results showed that the body weights remained stable during the experiment, showing negligible systemic toxicity of the treatment (Fig. 4G). Hematoxylinand and eosin (H&E) staining was performed. Negligible histological changes were observed in the main organs of differently treated mice, further indicating the high histocompatibility of the nanovaccines (Fig. 4H). Together, these results demonstrate that OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA is a promising candidate for effective tumor immunotherapy.

In vivo immune responses stimulated by nanovaccines

We then assessed the effect of nanoparticles on APC maturation. Treatment with PBS or OVA peptides had a negligible effect on APC maturation. In addition, the stimulatory effects of treatment with Mn-DNA and OVA@Mn-DNA nanoparticles were similar, both being higher than those of the PBS or OVA peptide-treated group, suggesting that Mn2+ and DNA can play an important role in stimulating the maturation of APCs. In contrast, OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA nanoparticles showed the greatest efficiency among all groups (Fig. 5A, B). Consistently, the OVA/Tp@Mn-OVA nanoparticle group showed the highest percentage of H-2Kb/SIINFEKL+ CD11c+ cell population, suggesting that the stimulation of antigenic presentation was most effective in this group (Fig. 5C, D).

We also assessed the immunization effect of nanoparticles by analyzing the proportion of tumor-infiltrating cytotoxic T lymphocytes. Compared with the other groups, the administration of OVA/Tp@Mn-OVA nanovaccine remarkably promoted the infiltration of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) (CD3+CD8+) (Fig. 5E, F). Furthermore, we detected the expression of CD107a, a functional marker of CTL degranulation, after NP treatment. As shown in Supplementary Figure S2, the percentage of CD107+ CTL was the highest abundant after treatment with the OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA nanoparticle group. In summary, these results indicated that OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA NPs promoted the antigen-presenting ability of APC and enhanced anti-tumor immunity.

Discussion

In this study, DNA was utilized for ligand binding to Mn2+, followed by the crosslinking of antigen peptides and APC-targeting peptides to DNA, resulting in the synthesis of OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA nanovaccine particles for tumor immunotherapy. The DNA sensor cGAS senses dsDNA, and the addition of Mn2+ can enhance the activity of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway, which enhances the secretion of IFNβ cytokines. And Mn2+ can promote the maturation of APC and enhance its antigen-presenting ability, which in turn promotes the activation and differentiation of CD8+ T cells within the tumor immune microenvironment. The nanoparticles effectively activated the cGAS-STING signaling pathway, promoted the maturation of dendritic cells, and effectively enhanced cellular presentation. Furthermore, we found that OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA could be used as a therapeutic nanovaccine to inhibit the growth of established tumors and prolong the survival time of animals. A limitation of this work is that we only employed the model antigen peptide OVA, the efficacy of our nanovaccine incorporating tumor antigen peptides remains to be validated. Nevertheless, because the nanoparticle assembly is driven chiefly by coordination between DNA and Mn2⁺, replacing OVA with tumor-antigen peptides should still enable the formation of this nanostructure and exert therapeutic efficacy against tumors. Collectively, based on the coordination assembly of DNA and Mn2+, we synthesized nanovaccine particles with simple and economical materials, efficient action, and targeting properties, which were successfully applied to tumor models in vivo.

Materials and methods

Materials

MnCl2·4H2O was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). ZnCl2 (99%) was purchased from Energy Chemicals. Ultrafiltration tube (100 kD, 500 μl, 15 ml) were purchased from Millipore. All DNA sequences were obtained from Sangon Biotech Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The DNA sequences are: chain a: TACAGATCTACTAGTGATCTATGACTGATCTGTACATGATCTACA; chain b: TGTAGATCATGTACAGATCAGTCATAGATCACTAGTAGATCTGTA; a-DBCO: DBCO-TACAGATCTACTAGTGATCTATGACTGATCTGTACATGATCTACAAAAAA; b-DBCO: AAAAATGTAGATCATGTACAGATCAGTCATAGATCACTAGTAGATCTGTA-DBCO; a-Cy5: Cy5-TACAGATCTACTAGTGATCTATGACTGATCTGTACATGATCTACA; b-Cy5: Cy5-TGTAGATCATGTACAGATCAGTCATAGATCACTAGTAGATCTGTA; a-6-FAM: TACAGATCTACTAGTGATCTATGACTGATCTGTACATGATCTACA-6-FAM; b-6-FAM: TGTAGATCATGTACAGATCAGTCATAGATCACTAGTAGATCTGTA-6-FAM.

N3-PEG2000-MAL was obtained from Shaanxi Ruisen Beier Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The SR-B1 targeting peptide (Tp) (FAEKFKEAVKDYFAKFWDGSGC) was obtained from BankPeptide (Hefei, China). OVA257–264 peptide (CSSSIINFEKL) was obtained from GenScript (Nanjing, China). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was purchased from Yeasen Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). RAW264.7, DC2.4 and B16-OVA cells were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co. Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Crosslinking DNA and peptides

The cysteine-modified OVA peptide and SR-B1 targeting peptide (Tp) with N3-PEG-MAL were cross-linked through the reaction between the sulfhydryl groups of cysteine and maleimide. We cross-linked dsDNA modified with DBCO and N3-PEG-MAL through the click chemistry of N3 and DBCO. Thus, DBCO-modified dsDNA can be linked to cysteine-modified peptides. Specifically, the OVA peptide (100 μM) and Tp (100 μM) were mixed with a ratio of 2:1 in the HEPES buffer (50 mM, PH7.2), then the N3-PEG-MAL (120 μM) was added to the above mixture and shook at 37 °C for 2.5 h prevented from light. An equal volume of DNA-DBCO (50 μM) was then added to the mixture and vortexed at room temperature for 2 h. Thus, DNA was attached to both the OVA peptide and Tp.

Synthesis of OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA nanoparticles

The synthesis method was based on the literature [17, 27]. First, we added 20 μl 25 μM OVA/Tp@DNA, 5 μl 20 mM MnCl2, and 75 μl ddH2O to a 100 μl synthesis system. The mixture was heated at 95 °C for 1 h. Finally, the nanoparticles were obtained and concentrated by ultrafiltration (100 kD) at 10, 000 rpm for 10 min after cooling to 4 °C. Synthesis of Mn-DNA and OVA @Mn-DNA followed the same procedure.

Characterization

TEM (JEM-2100Plus, JEOL, Japan) and SEM (*IGMAHD, UK) was used to characterize the morphologies of nanoparticles. The size and zeta potential were analyzed using a Zetasizer Nano ZS9 (Malvern, UK). To determine the loading efficiency of DNA, the nanoparticles were prepared as described above, excess dsDNA was collected by ultrafiltration, and the wavelength at 260 nm was detected using a UV–vis spectrophotometer. The loading efficiency of dsDNA was calculated using the wavelength of the excess dsDNA and primary dsDNA. To determine the loading efficiency of the OVA peptide, the nanoparticles were prepared as described above, the excess FITC-labeled OVA peptide was collected by ultrafiltration and the fluorescence intensity at approximately 520 nm was detected. The loading efficiency of the peptide was calculated from the fluorescence intensity of the excess peptide and primary peptide.

Cell viability assay

The cytotoxicity of OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) in RAW264.7 cells. First, the cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103/well at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 24 h. Subsequently, the culture medium in each well was replaced with a fresh medium containing OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA NPs at a series of concentrations. Cells in the wells without the addition of OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA NPs were used as controls. After 24 h, 10 μl of CCK8 solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Finally, optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate photometer.

Generation of BMDCs

Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDC) were isolated from 6 to 8 week old C57BL/6 mice. Most of the muscle tissues surrounding the femurs and tibias were removed using scissors and tweezers and transferred to a dish containing RPMI-1640 medium. Both ends of the bones were cut with scissors to expose the lumen, and a syringe was used to flush out the bone marrow using RPMI-1640 medium (HyClone, Beijing, China). Pipet marrow suspensions disintegrate clusters that may be present in the cell suspension. The cell suspension through a 70um cell strainer (Biosharp, Hefei, China). Red blood cell lysis solution (BD Pharmingen, USA) was added to lyse red blood cells. At day 0, bone marrow cells were seeded at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/ml in a 24-well plate in RPMI-1640 medium containing 100 U/ml penicillin–streptomycin (Hyclone, Beijing, China), 10%FBS (BI), murine recombinant GM-CSF (20 ng/ml, PeproTech, USA), and β-mercaptoethanol (50 nM, Acros Organics). Feed cultures at days 3 and 5 without discarding any cells: swirl plates, aspirate 75% of the medium, and addfresh medium containing GM-CSF. Cells were collected for cell uptake experiments on day 7.

Cellular uptake in vitro

RAW264.7, and BMDCs were seeded in a 24-well dish at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. Nanoparticles with Cy5-labeled DNA or FITC-labeled antigen peptide were added into the well and incubated for 4 h. The concentration of nanoparticles was 3 μM. Fluorescence was captured using a confocal laser microscope (A1 Plus, Nikon, Shanghai, China) and measured using flow cytometry (FACSCelesta, BD, USA).

Animal studies in vivo

The animal use protocol listed below has been reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), The Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University, China, Approval Number 2022661. 6–8 week old C57BL/6J female mice were inoculated subcutaneously on the flank with 3.5 × 105 B16-OVA cells C57BL/6J and vaccinated with 20 µg of OVA peptide via intratumor injection when the tumor grew to 150 mm3. Modes of administration in different groups were as follows: ① saline; ② 20 µg OVA peptide; ③ Mn-DNA nanoparticles; ④ OVA@Mn-DNA nanoparticles containing 20 µg OVA peptide; and ⑤ OVA/Tp@Mn-DNA nanoparticles containing 20 µg OVA peptide. 24 h later, the tumor tissues were removed and digested with collagenase IV, collagenase I, and hyaluronidase at 37 °C for 40 min. The ground and filtered cells were detected by flow cytometry. The Zombie AquaTM Fixable Viability Kit, TruStain FcXTM (anti-mouse CD16/32) antibody, APC/Cy7-CD45, CD8, PE-CD11c, PerCP/Cy5.5-CD80, PE/Cy7-CD86, and APC-H-2Kb/SIINFEKL were purchased from BioLegend.

Antitumor effect of the nanovaccines in vivo

For the therapeutic tumor challenge studies, C57BL/6J mice were inoculated subcutaneously on the flank with 3.5 × 105 B16-OVA cells and vaccinated with formulations containing 20 µg of OVA peptide on days 7, 14, and 21 via intratumor injection. Tumor volume was measured every other day via Vernier caliper measurements using the formula: V = L × W2 × 0.5. The body weights of the mice were measured every alternate day. The mice were euthanized at a tumor burden endpoint of 1500 mm3.
