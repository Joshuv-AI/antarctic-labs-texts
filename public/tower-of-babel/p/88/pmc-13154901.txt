Quality of life in older adults with urinary incontinence and its impact on the stigma-care needs network: latent profile and network analysis
Authors: HaoChong He, YuXuan Chen, Jian Liu, XiaoYing Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUrinary incontinence is highly prevalent in older adults, imposing substantial physical, psychological, and economic burdens. Stigma and unmet care needs, particularly psychosocial support, are widespread and interact negatively, significantly compromising quality of life. However, the complex interrelationships among stigma, care needs, and quality of life remain unclear. This study employs network analysis to elucidate these intricate connections, identify key intervention targets, and inform precise strategies to improve outcomes for this population.PurposeTo explore quality-of-life-based heterogeneous subgroups among older adults with urinary incontinence, analyze network structural characteristics of stigmatization and care needs across subgroups, and identify key intervention targets.MethodsThis cross-sectional study evaluated 448 older adults with urinary incontinence at three tertiary hospitals in Guangdong Province from December 2023 to February 2024. Latent profile analysis based on Urinary Incontinence Quality of Life scores was used for stratification. Network analysis constructed models of stigma and care needs for each subgroup. Node centrality and bridging centrality were calculated. Network comparison tests and computer-simulated intervention analyses evaluated intergroup differences and intervention effects.ResultsThe study identified three subgroups: Low Distress Group (15.8%), Moderate Distress Group (63.5%), and High Burden Group (20.7%). Their network structures differed significantly, with Internalized Stigma and Health Education Needs forming the strongest connection and key bridges between systems. Computer simulations pinpointed the optimal intervention targets for each subgroup, such as Health Education Needs for the Moderate Distress group.ConclusionOlder adults with urinary incontinence exhibit heterogeneous subgroups in quality of life, with distinct network structures and core drivers of stigma and care needs across subgroups. This study identifies core intervention targets for subpopulations, thereby providing both a theoretical basis for developing personalized precision care strategies and practical pathways for implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07170-x.

Introduction

Urinary incontinence (UI) is a common geriatric syndrome that poses a significant public health challenge worldwide. Epidemiological studies in China indicate that the prevalence of UI among individuals aged 65 years and older reaches 46.8%, with a marked increase with advancing age. The prevalence rate among women (56.3%) is notably higher than that among men (35.0%) [1, 2].

Older adults with UI generally have high care needs, primarily due to the multifaceted impact of this condition on their lives. Physically, prolonged exposure to urine irritation can lead to incontinence-associated dermatitis, manifesting as skin pain, discomfort, or even lesions, thereby increasing the risk of secondary infections [3]. Psychologically, people often experience intense shame and embarrassment due to their symptoms, sometimes developing anxiety and depression. A study involving 13,830 individuals revealed that 20.29% of those with UI symptoms also had anxiety, and 16.84% had depression—significantly higher rates than in the general population [4]. Socially, UI frequently limits people’s mobility, increases fracture and fall risks, hinders daily household tasks, and impairs normal social and sexual functioning. Some people gradually withdraw from social interactions, intensifying feelings of isolation and further exacerbating psychological burdens [5]. Additionally, the rehabilitation and care costs associated with the condition impose significant economic pressures on both individuals and society. A survey covering 27 EU countries revealed that the total cost of UI reached €69.1 billion in 2023. When care costs are included, the total soars to €80 billion, with an average annual expenditure per person of approximately €1,470.6. Without effective intervention, the total cost is projected to exceed €100.2 billion by 2030 [6].

In recent years, the heavy disease burden of UI has driven increased attention to the care needs of this population. Nevertheless, due to the multidimensional nature of UI-related needs. For instance, fear of leakage and embarrassment often leads to self-restriction of life space and reduced physical activity, which in turn can contribute to deconditioning and social isolation. Consequently, the care requirements of many older adults with UI remain inadequately met [7]. Among these, the need for psychosocial support is particularly prominent. Research indicates that as many as 73.4% of older adults with UI perceive their psychological counseling needs as unmet [8]. This unmet psychological demand is closely linked to the widespread stigma associated with UI. Community survey data indicates this issue is widespread yet frequently concealed. For instance, a UK survey of women aged 40 and above revealed a 34% prevalence of UI, yet only 25% sought medical care. In Germany, a staggering 43% of people concealed their incontinence history, with 25% having lived with the condition for over five years [9]. This widespread concealment and avoidance behavior profoundly reflects people’s shame-driven fear of social judgment, compelling them to withdraw from normal social life and sink into isolation.

Simultaneously, research indicates that stigmatization of illness and care needs do not exist independently but rather reinforce each other in a negative cycle. A study from China found that stigmatization of illness was significantly negatively correlated with self-care ability, exerting a substantial negative impact on self-care by undermining people’s self-efficacy [10]. The shame, hopelessness, and loneliness triggered by stigma inhibit people’s proactive help-seeking behaviors, deepening behavioral restrictions and social barriers. Unmet care needs, in turn, further reinforce people’s self-stigmatization and social isolation [11]. This bidirectional vicious cycle ultimately manifests as a significant decline in people’s quality of life. For older adults with UI, the impact on quality of life is comprehensive and profound. The underreporting of UI and self-management practices are influenced by multiple factors, with shame and fear of social judgment being significant barriers to seeking help [12]. Older adults with UI often deliberately restrict fluid intake, reduce outings, or even abandon social activities. This avoidance behavior leads to multidimensional impairment in their physiological functions, psychological state, and social roles [12, 13]. Therefore, implementing effective interventions to reduce stigma and identifying and meeting the care needs of these individuals are crucial for improving their quality of life.

However, previous studies have primarily focused on linear relationships between variables, making it difficult to reveal the complex interactions that exist within the dynamic system formed by quality of life, stigma, and care needs [14].

The emergence of network analysis offers a novel perspective. As a variable-centered statistical method, network analysis (NA) can reveal key symptom nodes and their interrelationships, providing a basis for precise clinical interventions [15, 16]. The application of network analysis to explore the intricate connections between quality of life, stigma, and care needs has the potential to inform the development of more precise intervention plans by nursing administrators.

Previous studies have not thoroughly explored the underlying mechanisms linking stigma, care needs, and quality of life. This research aims to address this gap by employing psychological network analysis as an exploratory and hypothesis-generating tool. We hypothesize that across different quality-of-life categories, the networks of stigma and care needs will exhibit distinct configurations. By identifying the most central nodes within these networks, this study has the potential to reveal subgroup-specific factors that may serve as high-priority targets for future tailored interventions. Thus, the focus of this study is two fold: to reveal the unique associative structures among stigma, care needs, and quality of life, and to identify key nodes that could inform the development of more precise, person-centred care strategies.

Theoretical framework

To comprehensively elucidate the mechanisms through which different quality-of-life categories influence the stigma-care needs network, this study adopts Bandura’s triadic reciprocal determinism as an integrative theoretical framework [17] This theory emphasizes the continuous, bidirectional interaction among three components: Person (P), Behavior (B), and Environment (E). In this study, the “P” level is operationalized as Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics and care needs. The “B” level is concretely manifested as behavioral expressions of quality of life. While the “E” level is defined as the psychosocial field constituted by stigma.

We hypothesize that adults across different quality-of-life categories exhibit systematic differences in the interaction patterns among the individual-behavior-environment triad, thereby shaping structurally distinct networks of stigmatization and care needs. For instance, in environments with pronounced stigma (E), adults may exhibit marked social avoidance and activity limitations (B) despite clear care needs (P), driven by fear of exposure or evaluative pressure (see Figure S1).

Methods

Design

This study was conducted from December 2023 to February 2024 in three tertiary hospitals in Guangdong Province using a cross-sectional survey design. The study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline [18].

Participants and sampling

Participants were recruited via convenience sampling. Eligible individuals met the following criteria: (1) a diagnosis of UI, as defined by the standardized terminology of the International Continence Society (ICS) [19]; (2) age 60 years or older; (3) consciousness and the ability to complete the questionnaire, either independently or with assistance; and (4) provision of voluntary informed consent. Individuals with severe physical comorbidities or cognitive impairment were excluded. Of the 503 initially screened, 55 were excluded due to ineligibility, resulting in a final sample of 448 participants who completed the study (see Fig. 1).Fig. 1Participants screening flowchart

Participants screening flowchart

The sample size for this study was determined based on requirements from both latent profile analysis (LPA) and network analysis (NA). First, to ensure the reliability and precision of LPA results, the sample size needed to exceed 300 participants [20]. Second, for subsequent NA, the sample size requirement is typically 5 to 6 times the number of variables included [21]. Given that the NA in this study involved seven core variables, each latent subgroup required at least 35 participants. Ultimately, 448 subjects were enrolled. This total sample size not only met the LPA requirement but also ensured that all three subsequently identified subgroups exceeded the minimum threshold of 35 participants, fully satisfying the sample size demands of both statistical methods.

Instruments

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

Based on the literature [1, 2, 4, 5], this study collected sociodemographic characteristics through a self-designed questionnaire, including: gender, age, education level, income, marital status, obesity, constipation frequency, and water intake. Additionally, information on clinical characteristics was gathered, including presence of conditions such as high blood pressure and coronary heart disease, along with detailed data on UI, including UI type and number of leaks.

Incontinence quality of life

The Incontinence Quality of Life Questionnaire (I-QOL) was developed in 1996 by Wagner et al., [22] at the University of Washington in the United States. It is primarily used to assess the quality of life in older adults with UI. The questionnaire covers three dimensions: Behavioral Restrictions (BR), Psychological Impact (PI), and Social Anxiety Disorder (SAD), comprising a total of 22 items. Each item in the I-QOL uses a 5-point Likert scale: 1 indicates “always true”; 2 indicates “often true”; 3 indicates “sometimes true”; 4 indicates “rarely true”; 5 indicates “never true.” The Cronbach’s α coefficient is 0.95, test-retest reliability is 0.93, and discriminant validity is significant (p < 0.001).

Chronic illness stigma scale

The Chronic Illness Stigma Scale was used to evaluate participants’ levels of stigma perception. The original scale was developed by Rao et al., [23] based on the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS). It comprises 24 items covering two dimensions: Internalized Stigma (IS) (13 items) and External Stigma (ES) (11 items). A 5-point Likert scale (1 = Never to 5 = Always) was used, yielding total scores ranging from 24 to 120 points, with higher scores indicating more severe stigmatization. The Chinese version of the scale, adapted by Deng et al., [24], demonstrated good reliability and validity. The content validity index for the entire scale was 0.932, with individual item content validity ranging from 0.800 to 1.000. The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.95, indicating excellent internal consistency and stability of this tool.

Elderly urinary incontinence care needs inventory (EUICNI)

Assessment was conducted using the Elderly Urinary Incontinence Care Needs Inventory developed by Liu (2023) [25] based on the Zuluaga-Raysmith (Z-R) model [26]. This inventory comprises 35 items organized into five dimensions: Daily Living Care Needs (DLCN) (17 items), Medical Care Needs (MCN) (6 items), Health Education Needs (HEN) (7 items), Social Participation Needs (SPN) (2 items), and Psychological Comfort Needs (PCN) (3 items). All items employ a 5-point rating scale (1 = “Not needed” to 5 = “Very needed”), yielding total scores ranging from 35 to 175, where higher scores indicate greater care needs. The scale demonstrates excellent reliability with Cronbach’s α coefficients of 0.901 and split-half reliability of 0.865. Its stable factor structure further validates its strong validity as an assessment tool for care needs among older adults with UI.

Data collection

This study received approval from the institutional ethics committee. Data were collected using a paper-based questionnaire. Prior to administration, all potential participants were informed of the study objectives and procedures, and written informed consent was obtained. Each participant was assigned a unique identifier to protect anonymity. To reduce participant stress and potential bias, the principal investigator remained blinded to individual participation status throughout the research process.

For participants with limited literacy, researchers read each item aloud and recorded their responses. Those who agreed to participate submitted the completed questionnaire along with the signed consent form, while those who declined were asked to return blank documents. To ensure data quality, a validation protocol was implemented, which included: (1) A trap question to detect random responding; (2) Exclusion of questionnaires completed in less than 5 min or more than 30 min; (3) Removal of responses exhibiting patterned answering across all items. All data were double-entered for accuracy prior to statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

First, data analysis was conducted using the SPSS 26.0 software package. Descriptive statistics were performed to analyze subject characteristics.

Pearson correlation analysis was employed to examine the relationship between the I-QOL total score and SSCI and EUICNI. Latent profile analysis was conducted to classify I-QOL subgroups. The optimal model was selected based on fit statistics including AIC, BIC, aBIC, and entropy, along with significant p-values from LMR and BLRT tests [27].

Partial correlation networks were estimated using R (version 4.3.3). In these networks, variables are represented as nodes, and edges between them correspond to the bivariate partial correlations after controlling for all other variables in the network. The thickness of an edge reflects the magnitude of the association, with solid lines denoting positive correlations and dashed lines representing negative ones. Core symptoms and bridge symptoms with distinct characteristics were identified using high centrality indices. We calculated the BEI, defined as the aggregation of edges connecting a node to all nodes in the counterpart community [28]. BEI is considered a more appropriate centrality measure for identifying bridging nodes with positive or negative relationships within a network [29]. Higher positive BEI values indicate increased activation capacity in other communities, while lower negative values suggest greater deactivation capacity [29] The 95% confidence intervals for edge weights and centrality indices were calculated via the Bootstrap method (2000 samples) to assess network accuracy. The stability of centrality indices was evaluated using the correlation stability coefficient (CS-coefficient) [30, 31]. Network Comparison Tests (NCT) were applied across different I-QOL profile networks to investigate differences in network structure, global strength, and edge strength [32]. Finally, computer-simulated interventions were employed to identify the most effective intervention targets using the NodeIdentifyR algorithm (NIRA), which explores the effects of symptom-specific interventions when symptom parameter systems change through simulated data [33].

Network structures of three subgroups

Figure 3. displays the SSCI-EUICNI network structures for the Moderate Distress Group (Fig. 3A), Low Distress Group (Fig. 3B), and High Burden Group (Fig. 3C) among older adults with UI. All networks were constructed based on three I-QOL curves. Across the cross-dimensional connections (between illness stigma and care needs), all three groups exhibited the strongest association as a negative correlation between IS and HEN (Moderate Distress Group: r = −0.121; Low Distress Group: r = −0.088; High Burden Group: r = −0.105). The second-strongest correlations varied across groups: ES and HEN in the Moderate Distress Group (r = 0.044), ES and DLCN in the Low Distress Group (r = 0.069), and IS and DLCN in the High Burden Group (r = 0.059). Within stigmatization dimensions, the correlation between IS and ES significantly strengthened from the Low Distress Group (r = 0.428) to the High Burden Group (r = 0.608), indicating tighter linkage between the two stigmatization dimensions with increasing burden; the correlation was weaker in the Moderate Distress Group (r = 0.094). Within nursing needs, the association between MCN and HEN was strongest across all groups (Moderate Distress Group: r = 0.459; Low Distress Group: r = 0.536; High Burden Group: r = 0.537). The second strongest association was DLCN and MCN in the Moderate Distress Group and High Burden Groups (r = 0.388; r = 0.375), while in the Low Burden Group it was the negative correlation between HEN and PCN (r = −0.258). In summary, the network analysis revealed that while certain strong connections (e.g., between Internalized Stigma and Health Education Needs) were common across all subgroups, the specific patterns of interplay between stigma and care needs varied meaningfully, highlighting subgroup-specific psychological and care dynamics.

Central and bridge symptoms across three subgroups

Analysis of node and bridging centrality across the three subgroups (Fig. 4) indicates that in terms of EI, the core symptom of the stigma network was IS in the Moderate Distress Group (Fig. 4A), while ES was the core symptom in both Low Distress Group and High Burden Group (Fig. 4B and C). In the nursing needs network, the core symptom was MCN in both Moderate Distress Group and High Burden Group, while it was PCN in the Low Distress Group. Regarding BEI, the key bridges connecting stigma to care needs in Moderate Distress Group were IS (BEI = −0.482) and DLCN (BEI = −0.163) (Fig. 4D); For the Low Distress Group, it was IS (BEI = −0.272) and HEN (BEI = −0.599) (Fig. 4E); for the High Burden Group, it was similarly IS (BEI = −0.610) and HEN (BEI = −0.397) (Fig. 4F). The stability of all networks was validated: their core EI and BEI remained stable after case sample reduction (Figure S2 and Figure S5). Both EI and BEI CS-C coefficients exceeded the threshold of 0.25 (Moderate Distress Group: 0.751/0.361; Low Distress Group: 0.357/0.357; High Burden Group: 0.441/0.516). Network comparisons across three subgroups

Based on NCT results, symptom networks across the three distress severity groups exhibited a gradient pattern characterized primarily by differences in local connectivity. The High Burden Group (Network 3) exhibited significant differences in overall network structure compared to the Moderate Distress Group (Network 1) (Test statistic M = 0.432, p = 0.001). while the Low Distress Group (Network 2) showed no significant differences in overall structure with either the Moderate Distress Group (Network 1) or the High Burden Group (Network 3) (Network 1 vs. Network 2: M = 0.370, p = 0.113; Network 2 vs. Network 3: M = 0.261, p = 0.446). No statistically significant differences were found in the global connectivity strength across the three networks (Network 1 vs. Network 3: S = 0.657, p = 0.117; Network 1 vs. Network 2: S = 0.659, p = 0.228; Network 2 vs. Network 3: S = 0.003, p = 0.992). Centrality invariance tests revealed significant or marginally significant differences in disease stigma nodes (IS and ES) across some group comparisons: marginally significant in HEN nodes (p = 0.026); No significant differences in centrality were found for any nodes in Network 2 vs. Network 3. Overall comparisons of EI failed to reach statistical significance across all group pairs (see Supplementary Tables 4–6): Network 1 vs. Network 2: t = −0.556, p = 0.598; Network 2 vs. Network 3: t = 2.222, p = 0.068), and no significant differences in EI were found for individual nodes after Bonferroni correction. Edge invariance tests identified multiple significantly different edges. Comparing the High Burden Group with the Moderate Distress Group, the most prominent difference was observed in the IS-ES edge (p < 0.001), with connection strength increasing from 0.153 in Network 1 to 0.586 in Network 3. Comparing the Low Distress Group with the Moderate Distress Group, significant changes were observed in the ES-DLCN (p < 0.001), ES-HEN (p < 0.001), and HEN-PCN (p = 0.020) edges; Comparing the Low Distress Group with the High Burden Group, significant differences were observed in the ES-DLCN (p = 0.003), ES-HEN (p = 0.007), and IS-SPN (p = 0.049) edges. (see Supplementary Tables 7–9) These changes in edge weights reflect the specificity of symptom interaction patterns across different groups.

Taken together, the network comparison test indicates that the psychological and care need systems are organized differently, particularly between the Moderate and High Burden groups. The increasing strength of the link between internalized and external stigma with greater burden is a notable finding.

Computer simulation-based intervention

Analysis of computer simulation-based interventions revealed distinct network influence patterns across different nursing need dimensions within three subgroups. In the Moderate Distress Group (Profile 1), HEN served as the central hub of the network. Its effect on elevating the total score was strongest during aggravation interventions (rising from 2.44 to 3.26) (Fig. 5A), and it also emerged as the most effective target alongside DLCN during alleviation interventions (total scores both decreasing to 1.79) (Fig. 5D). In the Low Distress Group (Profile 2), node roles shifted: HEN remained a primary risk factor (total score increased from 2.24 to 3.79 after aggravation) (Fig. 5B), while IS became the most effective protective factor (total score decreased to 1.63 after alleviation) (Fig. 5E). In the High Burden Group (Profile 3), PCN and SPN constitute primary risk nodes (e.g., total score increases from 3.04 to 3.91 when PCN worsens) (Fig. 5C), while MCN represent the most effective mitigation target (total score decreases to 1.92) (Fig. 5F). Discussion

To our knowledge, this study represents the first to reveal quality-of-life subtypes among older adults with UI and to identify distinct networks of stigma and care needs across different subgroups. Moving beyond conventional classification methods, it achieves a profound shift from recognizing participant heterogeneity to elucidating the underlying mechanisms within each group.

The study indicates that older adults with UI can be divided into three heterogeneous subgroups—“high,” “medium,” and “low”—based on quality of life. Although previous studies generally suggest that older adults with UI exhibit poorer overall quality of life and psychological status [34], this research further reveals internal compositional differences within the group. The Moderate Distress Group accounted for the highest proportion (63.5%), a distribution consistent with Kralik et al.,‘s findings that most chronic disease patients remain in an intermediate transitional phase between disease perception and gradual adaptation [35]. Thus, “moderate distress” represents a typical psychosocial adaptation state within this population rather than an anomaly. In contrast, participants in the High Burden Group (20.7%) exhibited severe impairment across multiple dimensions including physical, psychological, and social functioning. This aligns closely with Zhao et al., [36]’s findings, which characterize the UI as exhibiting “social cancer” traits. Furthermore, although the Low Distress Group constituted the smallest proportion (15.8%), its existence indicates that some older adults may mitigate the negative impact of UI on their lives to a certain extent through proactive coping strategies, robust social support, or strong disease adaptation capabilities [37].

Analysis of the network structures across the three subgroups revealed that all participants exhibited the strongest negative correlation between IS and HEN, indicating that intrinsic shame is a common key barrier hindering the acquisition of health knowledge. This finding resonates with Bear et al., [38].’s research, which similarly highlighted psychological barriers (such as shame) as critical factors affecting vulnerable populations’ acceptance of health interventions However, network comparison results revealed significant differences in network structures across subgroups (e.g., High Burden Group vs. Moderate Distress Group: M = 0.432, p = 0.001). The most pronounced manifestation was observed in the High Burden Group: participants in this group exhibited an unusually strong association between IS and ES (r = 0.608), with an intensity far exceeding that of the Moderate Distress Group (r = 0.094). This may stem from people shifting from perceiving external negative evaluations to internalizing these evaluations as a solidified self-cognition [39].

The results of EI analysis revealed that the core of the care needs network shifted from PCN in the Low Distress Group to MCN in the moderate- and high-distress groups. This transition indicates that intervention strategies must align with people’s stage of behavioral change. This finding aligns with the conclusions of Yang et al., [40], who demonstrated that timely and effective medical interventions at different stages of disease progression form the foundation for alleviating physical and psychological burdens and promoting functional recovery. Concurrently, BEI analysis revealed that in the Moderate Distress Group, the core bridges were IS and DLCN. This indicates that psychological distress in this group primarily stems from practical difficulties encountered in daily life scenarios such as hygiene, dressing, and mobility. This aligns with Kågström et al., [41]’s conclusion that negative attitudes associated with internalized shame may stem from concrete difficulties and limitations in daily living. However, in both the Low Distress Group and the High Burden Group, the core bridges shifted to IS and HEN. This commonality reveals the dual role of knowledge and cognition in the disease adaptation process. For people in the High Burden Group, intense stigma hinders their proactive pursuit of information and assistance, while unmet need for disease-specific knowledge lead to inadequate disease cognition and management skills, thereby exacerbating internalized shame. This mechanism aligns with Cho and Cho [42]’s findings: without professional guidance, people’s self-directed attempts at potentially ineffective self-management increase frustration and psychological distress, resulting in a negative correlation between self-management behaviors and quality of life. In contrast, the Low Distress Group likely acquired necessary knowledge and skills through effective channels, fostering confidence and self-efficacy in disease management that mitigates the impact of stigma. Stigma constitutes a core barrier preventing patients from seeking professional care. Targeted self-management interventions tailored to adult needs have been proven to effectively enhance treatment adherence and psychosocial adaptation levels [43].

Computer simulation interventions indicate that within the largest Moderate Distress Group, interventions targeting HEN yield the greatest overall benefits, aligning with this node’s central hub status within the network. This finding demonstrates that providing systematic health education to alleviate shame among such people represents an effective pathway to break the cycle of shame-driven avoidance. Such programs could be delivered in primary care or community settings through group workshops or guided self-help materials, effectively breaking the shame-driven avoidance cycle by enhancing self-efficacy, as supported by J. Li et al., [15]. Second, for the Low Distress Group, the simulation intervention revealed the unique value of IS as the most effective protective factor. This implies that at this stage, early intervention through preventive psychological measures to address and reduce initial shame emotions can efficiently maintain their current quality of life and prevent progression to higher distress states [44]. Clinicians could use short, validated stigma scales to identify at-risk individuals and offer targeted cognitive-behavioral strategies or acceptance-based counseling to mitigate early shame, thereby preventing progression to higher distress states [45]. Notably, IS emerged as a protective factor within the High Burden Group. This may stem from people enduring prolonged severe distress, where psychological instincts foster emotional numbness or isolation to reduce pain perception—though this simultaneously leads to social withdrawal [46]. This finding suggests interventions for this group urgently require a multidimensional shift. Prioritizing healthcare service needs remains fundamental [47, 48], while simultaneously requiring coordinated provision of professional psychological support and proactive promotion of social engagement. Timely psychological counseling and social support can effectively alleviate people’s emotional isolation and psychological distress. Critically, as evidenced by Bjørlykhaug et al., [49], actively connecting individuals to social resources and building support networks is associated with breaking the vicious cycle of social withdrawal and may facilitate the restoration of social functioning.

Limitations and outlook

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, due to its cross-sectional nature, the data cannot support inferences regarding the stability of the symptom network or the temporal direction of the observed associations. Additionally, medication histories—including the use of substances known to induce or exacerbate urinary incontinence, such as diuretics and sedatives—were not systematically collected, which may have introduced unmeasured confounding. Furthermore, reliance on self-reported data leaves the findings vulnerable to biases such as recall bias and social desirability bias. To address these limitations, future research should: (1) employ longitudinal designs to test whether interventions targeting core symptoms in specific subgroups (e.g., health education for the moderate distress group) can causally improve outcomes; (2) incorporate comprehensive biomedical assessments, including detailed medication reviews, to enhance the generalizability and specificity of the models; and (3) adopt mixed-methods approaches to contextualize and validate the quantitative networks.

Conclusions

Among older adults with UI, three heterogeneous subgroups exist based on quality of life: “Low Distress,” “Moderate Distress,” and “High Burden.” Network analysis reveals distinct interactive mechanisms within each subgroup. Computer simulation interventions confirm that targeting core needs specific to each subgroup achieves optimal outcomes. This provides both theoretical justification and practical pathways for shifting clinical practice toward personalized precision care.
