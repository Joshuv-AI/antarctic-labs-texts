Investigating solar air collector performance with different absorber surfaces and Piranha fins through numerical simulation
Authors: Ali Basem, Khalil Hajlaoui, Husam Rajab, Aman Sharma, Nashmi H Alrasheedi, Nimesh Raj, Rajeev Sharma, A Ahmadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractResearchers are constantly looking for ways to improve flat solar air heaters, which are a popular way to heat air using solar energy. This study looked at two different designs for the heat-absorbing surface (sinusoidal and plain) and how adding “Piranha fins” affected their performance. This research consisted of two parts. First, solar heaters with different absorber surfaces, including sinusoidal and plain, are investigated in two cases equipped with piranha fins and without fins. The second part will investigate the number of piranha fins (N). The results of the first part showed At a Reynolds number of 10,000, the sinusoidal absorber with Piranha fins achieved 23% efficiency, the sinusoidal absorber without fins reached 30%, and the plain absorber with fins only achieved 0.95% efficiency. The second part of the study demonstrated that increasing the fin count up to seven resulted in significant performance gains, with improvements of 30%, 28.5%, 23%, and 17% compared to the no-fin case for N = 7, N = 5, N = 3, respectively, at Re = 1000.Keywords: Solar energy, Solar air heater, Heat transfer, Thermal performance, Numerical investigationSubject terms: Mechanical engineering, Fluid dynamics

Introduction

Harnessing solar power plays a vital role in delivering environmentally friendly and renewable energy alternatives. Within the broad spectrum of solar energy technologies, solar collectors emerge as key components. These devices are generally divided into two main classifications, depending on the nature of the working fluid they employ [1]. Solar water heaters known as “SWHs” typically use water, while gas collectors referred to as “solar air heaters (SAHs)” utilize air. Generally, SAHs are favored over SWHs due to their application in a variety of engineering fields such as paper and pulp drying, space heating, and HVAC systems, whereas the applications of SWHs are rather restricted [2]. Moreover, SAHs are regarded as safe for utilization as these devices do not face issues like scaling-fouling, corrosion, or leakage, and they have a straightforward design. The effectiveness of SAHs is heavily influenced by factors such as solar radiation, the flow rate, and the shape of the collector surfaces, particularly when fins are used to support the absorber plate [3, 4]. Devices such as solar thermal units are essential for generating cost-effective and eco-friendly heat. These systems capture sunlight and convert it into usable heat energy, which can then serve multiple functions, including space heating in residential, commercial, and industrial settings, as well as water heating applications. By minimizing dependence on conventional fossil-based energy sources, this approach significantly cuts both energy expenditures and carbon emissions. Solar thermal systems present an economically viable and ecologically sound alternative to conventional heating methods, especially in sun-rich areas where solar resources are abundant. Solar collectors contribute significantly to environmental sustainability by promoting self-sufficient energy systems and minimizing ecological harm. Their use supports the transition toward greener energy solutions and reduces reliance on conventional, pollutant-emitting power sources [5, 6].

Passive techniques for improving solar collectors or air heaters involve advanced design features that enhance heat transfer and energy efficiency without relying on external energy inputs. These include optimized orientation and tilt for maximum sunlight exposure, high-quality insulation to minimize heat loss, and selective coatings on absorber plates to improve heat retention. Incorporating extended surfaces like fins [7, 8] and ribs [9, 10] increases the surface area for heat transfer, while turbulators [11, 12], vortex generators [13, 14], and impingement jets [15, 16] create turbulence in the airflow, breaking thermal boundary layers and improving heat exchange.

In a recent study, Hegde et al. [17] explored methods to improve the efficiency of solar air heating systems by employing modified sidewall configurations. Their analysis focused on airflow behaviors within a Reynolds number (Re) interval of 3000 to 24,000, examining thermal performance across various geometries, including both planar and inclined sidewall structures. To model solar-induced heat transfer within the computational domain, the discrete ordinates (DO) radiation approach was implemented. Remarkably, at a Re of 24,000, the configurations identified as SSW 40 and TSW (40–10) demonstrated superior thermal performance, achieving peak efficiencies of 89.25% and 90.62%, respectively. Among the configurations evaluated, the TSW (40–10) design achieved the maximum exergy efficiency, recorded at 4.08%. From an environmental standpoint, the analysis indicated a yearly decrease in carbon dioxide emissions amounting to 1.06 metric tons, along with an estimated annual ecological cost savings of $15.5.

In their study, Chaurasia et al. [18] examined how variations in Re (ranging from 4281 to 18,250), relative pitch of surface protrusions (between 0.375 and 0.625), and relative spacing (from 2 to 4) influence thermal performance and flow resistance within a solar air cooling system utilizing a composite surface roughness configuration. A maximum Nusselt number (Nu) of 4.15 was achieved under conditions involving a relative protrusion spacing of 0.5, a gap ratio of 2, and a Re of 18,250, demonstrating a significant enhancement over the baseline smooth duct configuration. The analysis further revealed a consistent decline in the friction factor as the Re increased. The maximum friction factor, approximately 3.8, was recorded under conditions of a 0.375 relative protrusion pitch, a relative gap width of 4, and a Re of 4281, in contrast to the values observed for a smooth duct. The findings from the experiments indicate that combined roughness patterns yield superior thermal performance compared to single-type roughness configurations. Moreover, predictive relationships linking the Nu with the friction factor were formulated, demonstrating strong consistency with the observed experimental data.

Ben Amara et al. [19] performed a comparative investigation of SAHs featuring spiral and helical flow configurations. Their work assessed both thermal efficiency and pressure loss, offering valuable guidance for the design and enhancement of SAH systems. The analysis was carried out through a computational model implemented in ANSYS Fluent. The findings revealed a strong correlation between the computational predictions and experimental measurements, exhibiting an average discrepancy of only 4.5%. Experimental observations showed that the peak air temperatures reached 76.2 °C for the Helical SAH and 72 °C for the Spiral SAHat a mass flow rate of 0.01 kg/s. According to the simulation data, air temperature rises of 62 °C and 59.4 °C were observed for helical and spiral type, respectively, at a mass flow rate of 0.005 kg/s. Consequently, the investigation emphasizes that the spiral flow channel offers enhanced thermal performance along with reduced pressure drop relative to the helical design.

Afridi et al. [20] carried out a numerical study to assess heat transfer characteristics in a SAH equipped with an absorber plate enhanced by jet impingement. A three-dimensional CFD analysis was performed in ANSYS Fluent, employing the k-ε turbulence model to evaluate thermal efficiency. The configuration of the jet impingement system follows a W-pattern, and the investigation was divided into two distinct stages. Two geometric aspect ratios were analyzed: AR1 defined as the ratio of pitch to height (p/h), and AR2 as the inverse (h/p), where p denotes the spacing between jets and h indicates their elevation relative to the target surface. During the initial stage of the investigation, three distinct aspect ratios—namely 0.75, 0.83, and 0.92—were evaluated under a fixed jet-to-jet vertical spacing of 120 mm. Among the configurations tested across various Re, the setup with an aspect ratio of 0.75 (corresponding to a pitch of 90 mm) achieved superior thermal efficiency at a Re of 7500, outperforming the other cases in terms of heat transfer capability. In a recent study, Abdelrahman et al. [21] investigated the efficiency of two solar air heating systems constructed using repurposed aluminum beverage cans. The analysis focused on two distinct geometrical designs: the first (SAH-1) featured a rectangular layout with a 4:1 length-to-width ratio, while the second (SAH-2) adopted a square shape characterized by an equal length and width, resulting in a 1:1 aspect ratio. Experimental trials took place in Cairo, Egypt, throughout the winter month of February, with the solar collectors positioned at tilt angles of 0°, 15°, 30°, 45°, and 60°. Among these configurations, the SAH-1 unit demonstrated the most favorable results at a 45° inclination, where it achieved a peak absorber temperature of 88.3 °C, delivered a thermal output of 412.7 W per square meter, and attained a total efficiency of 50.9%. To tackle this challenge, Jyoti Pal et al. [22] explored a novel approach involving the integration of a conical jet impingement system with a sinusoidal absorber plate surface to improve thermal performance in solar air heating units. Their analysis considered several key variables: the amplitude-to-length ratio of the wave profile (ranging from 0.079 to 0.314), the ratio of throat jet diameter to plate length (from 0.063 to 0.141), the ratio of base jet diameter (spanning 0.126–0.251), and the Re, which varied between 3200 and 19,200 to represent different flow conditions. Numerical analysis based on CFD was utilized to solve the time-averaged Reynolds–Navier–Stokes equations, incorporating the RNG variant of the k-ɛ turbulence model to assess both thermal behavior and flow resistance. To verify the reliability of the simulation data, experimental procedures were conducted for comparison. The outcomes revealed a notable enhancement in thermal performance, as evidenced by a 6.71-fold rise in the Nu relative to a conventional flat-plate SAH. However, this improvement in heat transfer was accompanied by a 13.87-fold escalation in the frictional resistance within the system. Moreover, the peak value of the thermos-hydraulic performance (THP) index reached 2.79 under specific operating conditions, which included a Re of 3200, a sinusoidal amplitude-to-length ratio of 0.079, along with jet throat and base diameter ratios of 0.126 and 0.220, respectively. Dhamudia and Senapati [23] employed computational fluid dynamics techniques to analyze improvements in thermal performance and airflow behavior within a SAH duct equipped with plus-shaped fins. Their investigation was carried out using a two-dimensional computational domain, where the absorber surface, constructed from aluminum, was exposed to a uniform thermal load of 1000 W/m2. Key variables examined in the analysis included the Re, the ratio of roughness pitch to element height (P/e), and the ratio of roughness height to hydraulic diameter (e/Dh). The investigation yielded meaningful insights, highlighting improvements in average Nu, changes in frictional resistance, and values of the thermo-geometric performance metric (TGPP) and SAH performance index (SAHPI). Additionally, the study assessed the pumping power requirements and provided visual representations of multiple fluid dynamic characteristics within the SAHD configuration. The investigation explored the effects of modifying the relative roughness pitch (P/e) within the range of 7.14–17.85 and adjusting the Re from 4000 to 22,000, while keeping the relative roughness height (e/Dh) fixed at 0.042. Results demonstrated that optimizing heat extraction in the SAHD system led to attaining SAHPI value of 2.54. Fayez Aldawi [24] conducted a comprehensive investigation into the thermal behavior and operational efficiency of a solar air heating system enhanced with spring-shaped wire inserts, utilizing a thoroughly verified three-dimensional CFD modeling technique. A comparative analysis was carried out involving five distinct configurations of spring-shaped wire elements, set against a baseline SAH design lacking any turbulence-inducing components. The study thoroughly examined how variations in parameters such as coil diameter, spacing between turns (pitch), and thickness of the wire influenced the thermal and aerodynamic performance across all five wire geometries. The findings revealed that incorporating the turbulence-inducing element substantially enhanced heat transfer effectiveness within the absorber zone of the SAH. Furthermore, among the various cross-sectional profiles assessed, the rectangular form demonstrated a notably greater improvement in the Nu. Nonetheless, when evaluating both thermal enhancement and fluid resistance, the circular-shaped turbulence promoter emerged as the most effective configuration. Chaudhri et al. [25] examined the influence of transverse wedge shaped roughened absorbers on performance of SAHs. This method delivers cool ambient air across the whole surface of the absorber plate and the roughness features on the heated plate, which significantly improves the heat transfer coefficient (HTC). Shayan et al. [26] performed experimental and numerical investigations on how a rectangular S-shaped pattern of artificial roughness impacts the THP of SAH. Their results indicated that improving heat transfer through a staggered arrangement of artificial roughness was the most effective method. Sharma et al. [27] proposed various methods to improve the thermal characteristics of SAHs that use an impinging air stream along with V patterned wired ribs on the absorber plate. Their study showed that orienting the ribs toward the impinging air stream enhances the local turbulence on the ribbed plate, which in turn boosts the efficiency of the SAHs. Similar observations regarding the effect of absorber surface on heat transfer efficiency of SAHs have been severally confirmed by other investigations [28–32].

As comprehensively reviewed in this section, the geometry and aspect ratio of the adsorbent surface have high effects on the performance of SAHs. Although many experimental and numerical studies have focused on different surface types, no research has investigated the effect of a sinusoidal profile equipped with piranha fins compared to a flat surface. Accordingly, in the present study, a comprehensive numerical study has been conducted on the use of different adsorbent surfaces. In addition, a parametric study has been conducted to determine the effect of the adsorbent type and the effect of using piranha fins. A key innovation is the combination of the sinusoidal geometry with piranha-shaped fins. Piranha fins are known to enhance heat transfer due to their ability to create vortices and focused turbulence. We are the first (or one of the few) to integrate these two concepts in a SAH and numerically investigate their synergistic effect. This combination results in unprecedented improvements in thermal performance (efficiency and HTC). This study offers important contributions toward refining the design of these systems, addressing gaps in detail and scope that earlier research has largely overlooked.

Numerical solution and methodology

Geometrical design and domain modeling

Using a two-stage simulation, this research aims to optimize the efficiency of a flat plate solar collector by investigating different absorber surface designs. The numerical simulations in this study were performed using the commercial computational fluid dynamics (CFD) software ANSYS Fluent, version 19.2. This robust software package is widely utilized for modeling complex fluid flow and heat transfer phenomena. Further details about the software can be found at [https://www.ansys.com/products/fluids/ansys-fluent]. Specifically, two simple, sinusoidal designs are considered, with and without the addition of Piranha fins. The absorber surface area is held constant throughout the study. Simulations are conducted under turbulent flow conditions, within a Re range of 5000–20,000. Unlike most previous studies that focus on flat-plate absorbers, our work comprehensively numerically analyzes the THP of a sinusoidal wave absorber in a SAH. This corrugated geometry not only provides a specific absorber surface area, but also purposefully increases turbulence and improves air flow mixing in the boundary layer, which ultimately leads to a significant increase in the heat transfer rate. This is a major innovation in the field of absorber design.

Another key innovation is the combination of sinusoidal geometry with piranha-shaped fins. Piranha fins are known to enhance heat transfer due to their ability to create vortices and focused turbulence. We are the first (or one of the few studies) to integrate these two concepts in a SAH and numerically investigate their synergistic effect. This combination leads to unprecedented improvements in thermal performance (efficiency and HTC).Our research includes an extensive parametric analysis that includes the investigation of the influence of [Re, absorber surface type, and number of fins] on THP of the system. This analysis provides valuable insights into the design optimization of such systems that have not been comprehensively explored in previous studies.

The results of this study, including detailed numerical data on convective HTC, pressure drop, and thermal efficiency for a wide range of configurations, can be used as a valuable design guide for engineers and researchers working on high-performance SAHs. This data will help design more efficient and economical systems.The emphasis on a direct comparison of the efficiency of the sinusoidal absorber with similar flat plate absorbers (which is clearly demonstrated in the results) is an important contribution. This comparison clearly demonstrates the significant superiority of our proposed geometry and provides a strong basis for justifying the use of these more complex designs.

Governing equations

Recognizing the critical role of turbulence in accurately representing fluid dynamics, this section outlines the core governing equations that describe the flow behavior, along with a detailed explanation of the computational approach utilized for the simulations. Several assumptions were made for the numerical analysis: steady-state conditions, incompressibility, turbulent flow (using the k-ε model), and constant thermophysical properties for the heat transfer fluid.

The selection of the turbulence model (k − ε) for this study was based on several key considerations:

Balance between Accuracy and Computational Cost: The k − ε model is a common and efficient two-equation model that has a good ability to predict the behavior of turbulent flows in many engineering applications. In contrast to advanced turbulence models like the RSM or LES, the k − ε approach offers a more computationally efficient alternative. This reduced demand on processing power was a key consideration in facilitating the parametric analysis and evaluation of multiple geometric configurations throughout this research. This model allows for the achievement of acceptable results in a reasonable time.

Ability to Simulate Internal Flows and Boundary Layers: The flow inside a SAH is predominantly an internal flow in which well-developed boundary layers play an important role in heat transfer. The k-ε model is well-known for simulating this type of flow, especially in fully turbulent regions, and provides reliable results.

Extensive Support and Proven Track Record: The k-ε model is one of the most widely used turbulence models in CFD software, and numerous experimental and numerical studies have confirmed its accuracy and efficiency in similar applications (such as flow in channels and heat exchangers)[20–24]. This strong track record increases the credibility of our results.

A mesh sensitivity analysis was performed using several separate computational meshes with different cell densities to assess the grid independence of the numerical solutions as shown in Fig. 2. To ensure the accuracy of the boundary layer modeling and wall heat transfer, exponentially growing prismatic layers were used near all walls (especially the absorber and fin surfaces). The value of in the first cell layer adjacent to the wall was carefully controlled. Based on the analysis and careful meshing of all wall surfaces, including the sinusoidal absorber plate and the inner fins of the channel, the distribution of is in the range between a minimum of 50 and a maximum of 80. This range of (between 50 and 80) falls well within the recommended optimal range for using standard wall functions. This allows the k-epsilon model to be sufficiently accurate in predicting velocity distributions, wall friction, and heat transfer near the wall, and the assumptions of the wall functions to be applied correctly.

Impact of adsorbent surface geometry

This section focuses on analyzing how different absorber surface configurations influence the flow-related characteristics within the solar air collector. It is important to highlight that all other geometric variables considered in the study will remain unchanged throughout this analysis. Heat-absorbing surfaces examined in the first part.

The effect of absorber surface geometry on heat transfer in a flat plate solar collector is shown in Fig. 6. Four different configurations were investigated over a wide range of Re. The outcomes indicate a clear trend: increasing Re increases the convective HTC. This is because higher Re correspond to increased fluid velocity and create more turbulent flow, resulting in more frequent interactions between the fluid and the absorber surface. This in turn facilitates more heat absorption by the fluid. It is noteworthy that the sinusoidal surface design equipped with Piranha fins exhibits superior heat transfer characteristics and exhibits high HTCs compared to the other geometries tested.

This results in more efficient heat transfer from the absorber to the fluid. However, the sinusoidal surface design creates regions where the fluid is effectively turbulent and encounters the fluid agitated by the Piranha fins, both of which contribute to improved heat transfer. It can also be stated that the sinusoidal design creates a smoother flow pattern, allowing for more efficient heat exchange between the absorber and the fluid. This improvement stems from the combined action of the fins and the sinusoidal surface, which generate two distinct turbulent flows. Structured (sinusoidal) surfaces create more flow resistance compared to a smooth surface, which leads to a higher pressure drop. Changes in fluid flow and rotation patterns after passing through piranha fins.

As shown in Fig. 10, the cold fluid experiences the formation of swirling flow patterns or vortices when interacting with wave structures such as the solar collector heat absorber and the fins of Piranhas. This phenomenon enhances the convective heat transfer process between the flowing fluid and the heated absorber surface. However, the result of this increased mixing is a corresponding increase in fluid pressure drop. Therefore, a thermal performance coefficient is used to evaluate the overall performance (η) of the solar collector, taking into account heat transfer and pressure drop.

The thermal performance coefficient (η) is a key criterion for evaluating and selecting effective heat absorber surfaces, especially in SAHs. This coefficient, which represents the balance between heat transfer efficiency and friction, is very important for optimal design. As shown in Fig. 11, sinusoidal absorbers equipped with Piranha fins (case (A)) show the highest efficiency at two Re of 15,000 and 20,000. It is noteworthy that the lowest thermal performance occurs at a Re of 5000. The improved performance of the sinusoidal surface is attributed to the generation of a controlled vortex flow regime, which is facilitated by its smooth and undulating profile. This increases the heat transfer while maintaining an acceptable pressure drop, which indicates optimal performance. At medium Re (10,000), the thermal performance is the same for the sine wave equipped with piranha fins (Case A) (η = 23%), the sine wave without piranha fins (Case B) (η = 30%), and the smooth absorber surface equipped with piranha fins (Case C) (η = 0.93%).

Effect of the number of Piranha fins in the sinusoidal absorber (N)

This research aims to determine the effect of these geometric changes on the heat transfer mechanisms and hydrodynamic behavior.

Figure 12 shows the effect of Re and ratio N on the convective HTC. The data shows a positive correlation between the Re and HTC at all values of N. This increase can be explained by the increase in fluid velocity associated with higher Re, which leads to more intense turbulent mixing and increased contact between the fluid and the absorber surface, thus facilitating more efficient heat exchange. Furthermore, the increase in piranha fins (N) increases the turbulence and motion of the fluid, which leads to a corresponding increase in the HTC. This causes more turbulent motion of the fluid and disrupts the boundary layer, which improves heat transfer.

Conclusion

Numerical analysis and evaluation were performed using the commercial computational fluid dynamics (CFD) software ANSYS Fluent, version 19.2. The research consisted of two distinct phases. The first phase focused on the impact of the absorber surface geometry on pressure drop and heat transfer within the solar collector. The second phase investigated the effect of the number of Piranha fins on the efficiency of the SAH. The findings of the first phase showed that the use of a sinusoidal absorber surface equipped with Piranha fins in the solar heater leads to increased thermal performance and cost-effective improvements for industrial applications, thereby increasing the overall efficiency. The second part of the study demonstrated that increasing the fin count up to seven resulted in significant performance gains, with improvements of 30%, 28.5%, 23%, and 17% compared to the no-fin case for N = 7, N = 5, N = 3, respectively, at Re = 1000.
