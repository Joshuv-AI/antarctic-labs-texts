Induction chemotherapy for locally advanced OPSCC: real-world evidence and HPV-dependent prognostic value of treatment response
Authors: Gulidanna Shayan, Ruichen Li, Ya Liu, Junlin Yi, Yi Zhu, Ye Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe clinical value of induction chemotherapy (IC) in stage III-IV oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) remains controversial. While randomized trials have shown limited survival benefit in unselected populations, it remains unclear whether IC provides benefit in high-risk subgroups and whether early treatment response to IC can serve as a prognostic and stratification tool.MethodsWe conducted a two-center retrospective real-world cohort study of 496 patients with stage III-IV OPSCC treated with definitive radiotherapy with or without IC. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was used to balance baseline characteristics, adjusting for age, sex, smoking, alcohol use, T/N stage (AJCC 7th), tumor subsite, HPV status, clinical extranodal extension (cENE), and use of concurrent chemoradiotherapy. Overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS), and locoregional recurrence-free survival (LRFS) were compared using Kaplan-Meier and Cox proportional hazards models. A clinical nomogram was developed to predict individual DMFS and estimate IC benefit. In IC-treated patients, analyses were stratified by HPV status and IC response further stratified prognosis across endpoints.ResultsIn IPTW-weighted multivariable Cox analysis, IC was independently associated with improved DMFS (HR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.43–0.83, p = 0.016) and PFS (HR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.49–0.90, p = 0.041). Subgroup analysis revealed reduced distant metastasis with IC in N3 patients (HR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.20–0.93, p = 0.033). The nomogram (C-index = 0.697) suggested IC-associated benefit in patients with higher baseline risk. Among IC-treated patients, treatment response further stratified prognosis in an HPV-dependent manner. In the HPV-negative subgroup, patients achieving a complete or partial response (CR/PR) to IC experienced significantly improved OS compared with non-responders (p = 0.019). In multivariable analysis, IC response (CR/PR) was independently associated with superior PFS and LRFS, whereas no response-associated survival benefit was observed in HPV-positive patients.ConclusionIn this real-world cohort of locally advanced OPSCC, IC was not associated with an overall survival benefit after adjustment, but was associated with improved distant metastasis-free survival, particularly in selected high-risk patients. Early IC response further identified a favorable-risk subgroup among HPV-negative patients. Given the retrospective design and treatment heterogeneity, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require prospective validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13014-026-02867-0.Keywords: Oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma, Induction chemotherapy, Distant metastasis-free survival, HPV, Risk stratification, Nomogram, Real-world data, IPTW

Background

The incidence of oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) has risen significantly in recent decades, primarily driven by human papillomavirus (HPV)-associated cases, which now account for the majority of OPSCC diagnoses in high-income countries [1, 2]. Compared with HPV-negative disease, HPV-positive OPSCC is associated with significantly better prognosis [3–5]. For patients with locally advanced OPSCC, the current standard of care is definitive radiotherapy (RT) combined with concurrent cisplatin, typically delivered at 66 Gy in 2.2 Gy per fraction or 70 Gy in 2.0 Gy per fraction, administered five days per week over 6–7 weeks [6].

Induction chemotherapy (IC), administered prior to definitive local therapy, has been extensively studied for its potential to improve distant control and is considered as a screening modality for organ preservation strategy in Head and Neck Squamous Cell Carcinoma(HNSCC), but remains controversial. The TAX 323 and 324 trials demonstrated that the TPF regimen provided superior overall survival (OS) compared with PF [7, 8]. In contrast, the phase III PARADIGM trial failed to show a survival advantage over concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT), and reported higher incidence of febrile neutropenia with IC [9]. Similarly, in the DeCIDE trial, two cycles of TPF induction chemotherapy in patients with N2-3 HNSCC showed no improvement in OS, recurrence-free survival (RFS), or distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) [10]. These mixed results have limited the routine adoption of IC in international guidelines. Notably, most prospective studies enrolled unselected HNSCC patients across different subsites, leaving the site-specific value of IC, particularly in OPSCC, unresolved.

IC provides an opportunity to biologically stratify tumors according to chemosensitivity, thereby informing personalized de-intensification or intensification strategies [11, 12]. The prospective phase II IChoice-01 trial investigated de-escalation strategies based on tumor response to IC in HPV-positive OPSCC. Patients demonstrating a favorable response to IC achieved 100% PFS despite receiving reduced RT dose (60 Gy) without concurrent chemotherapy [13]. These findings underscore the potential role of IC in refining treatment intensity and improving risk-adapted management.

IC is widely used in real-world practice, especially in high-volume centers in China, but its role in OPSCC remains uncertain. Most randomized trials included unselected, multi-subsite HNSCC cohorts. Therefore, which OPSCC patients benefit from IC is still unclear. Whether early IC response stratifies prognosis by HPV status remains unknown. Our primary objective was to evaluate the impact of IC on survival outcomes in a retrospective, multicenter cohort of patients with locally advanced OPSCC. Secondary objectives were (1) to identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit from IC and (2) to determine whether IC response differentially stratifies prognosis by HPV status.

Methods

Study population

We conducted a retrospective, real-world cohort study of consecutive patients with stage III-IV OPSCC, as defined by the AJCC 7th edition, treated at two tertiary medical centers between January 1, 2010, and January 30, 2020. The last follow-up date was July 1, 2024. IHC staining was used for evaluating p16 status. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) individuals aged between 18 and 75 years; (2) pathological confirmation of OPSCC with stage III-IV (AJCC 7th); (3) availability of p16 status; and (4) receipt of treatment with definitive radiation therapy with or without induction chemotherapy. The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients (1) received surgery (2) without HPV status information, (3) those who lack information for clinical extranodal extension (cENE) evaluation, (4) were lost to follow-up, (5) had distant metastasis or recurrent disease; or (6) were enrolled in prospective clinical trials, including those involving immunotherapy, targeted therapy, or radiotherapy dose de-escalation. The patient selection procedure is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Definitions and outcomes

OS was calculated from the date of diagnosis to death from any cause or the last follow-up. PFS was defined as the time from diagnosis to either disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Locoregional recurrence-free survival (LRFS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to the occurrence of local recurrence or death. DMFS was defined as the time from diagnosis to the development of distant metastasis or death. Patients without events at the time of last follow-up were censored in all survival analyses.

Treatment

Patients with stage III-IV OPSCC received definitive radiation therapy with or without induction chemotherapy. IC regimens consisted of TPF (docetaxel/paclitaxel cisplatin + capecitabine/raltitrexed), TP (docetaxel/paclitaxel + cisplatin), or PF (cisplatin + capecitabine/raltitrexed) administered for two to three cycles. The standard doses were as follows:

Docetaxel 75 mg/m² (Day 1) or paclitaxel 175 mg/m² (Day 1);

Docetaxel 75 mg/m² (Day 1) or paclitaxel 175 mg/m² (Day 1);

Cisplatin 25 mg/m² (Days 1–3);

Cisplatin 25 mg/m² (Days 1–3);

Capecitabine 825 mg/m² twice daily (Days 1–14) or raltitrexed 3 mg/m² (Day 1).

Capecitabine 825 mg/m² twice daily (Days 1–14) or raltitrexed 3 mg/m² (Day 1).

The induction chemotherapy regimen was selected at the discretion of the treating multidisciplinary team according to tumor burden, performance status, comorbidities, renal function, baseline hearing function, neuropathy, and institutional practice. Patients considered less suitable for triplet chemotherapy generally received doublet regimens. The number of IC cycles was not determined by a standardized prospective protocol but reflected institutional practice and individualized clinical decision-making.

Each cycle was repeated every 21 days. Following induction chemotherapy, definitive radiotherapy (either radical RT or CCRT) was initiated within 4 weeks. Intensity modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) or volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) at a curative dose of 66–70 Gy in 30–35 fractions. Target delineation utilized post-IC CT simulation to define the gross tumor volume (GTV) and involved lymph nodes, whereas the clinical target volume (CTV) was outlined according to the pre-IC measurements. In cases of complete response (CR) following IC, the tumor base and mucosal surface were still contoured as GTV.

For patients who received IC, definitive radiotherapy was delivered with or without concurrent chemotherapy according to treating physician discretion and patient eligibility. Radiotherapy alone was used when concurrent chemotherapy was contraindicated or not tolerated, including severe IC-related toxicity. Concurrent cisplatin-based chemotherapy (80–100 mg/m2 per cycle, commonly delivered over 3 days) could be administered during radiotherapy when clinically appropriate. The use of concurrent chemotherapy was not protocolized and was determined by the treating physician in routine practice. For patients who did not receive induction chemotherapy, definitive radiotherapy was administered with concurrent cisplatin-based chemotherapy (80–100 mg/m² per cycle). For those ineligible for platinum-based regimens, this included cases with: (1) Age ≥ 70 years with ECOG ≥ 2 and/or CrCl < 60 mL/min; (2) Grade ≥ 2 audiometric hearing loss (≥ 25 dB at contiguous frequencies); (3) Chronic kidney disease (GFR < 50 mL/min); (4) Pre-existing peripheral neuropathy (Grade ≥ 2); and in such cases, radiotherapy alone was employed.

All patients were evaluated four to eight weeks after completion of definitive RT. The tumor responses were evaluated again by the same radiographic method as used at baseline (MRI or CT). The evaluation of tumor response to treatment was based on the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1. on cross-section MRI or CT images. The partial response (PR) referred to a ≥ 30% decrease in both primary sites and lymph nodes.

Treatment-related toxicity assessment

Treatment-related toxicities were retrospectively collected from medical records and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0 (CTCAE v5.0). Hematologic toxicity during induction chemotherapy and subsequent RT/CRT was assessed using available laboratory records. Grade ≥ 3 hematologic toxicity during RT/CRT was calculated at the patient level and defined as the occurrence of grade ≥ 3 leukopenia, anemia, or thrombocytopenia, without double-counting overlapping events. Ototoxicity and peripheral neuropathy were available from routine clinical documentation only at Center A. All non-IC patients were treated at Center A, whereas only 75 IC patients were treated at Center A; therefore, ototoxicity and peripheral neuropathy were reported according to the available cohort.

HPV status

All patients underwent immunohistochemical (IHC) evaluation for p16 expression. Tumors were considered HPV-related if p16 positivity was observed in ≥ 70% of tumor cells by IHC, consistent with established criteria for surrogate assessment of HPV-associated disease [14].

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-squared test. The OS, PFS, LRFS, and DMFS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Cox proportional-hazards models. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was employed to address the inherent limitations of retrospective studies and ensure that the baseline characteristics were well balanced across the different treatment groups. Concurrent chemotherapy use was included to account for treatment heterogeneity in routine practice. An IPTW log-rank test and IPTW Kaplan-Meier time-to-event estimates were calculated, along with HRs and 95% CIs derived from an IPTW Cox model using robust standard errors. A prognostic nomogram for 3-year DMFS was constructed using a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model incorporating T stage, N stage, HPV status, cENE, smoking, alcohol use, and tumor subsite. Model discrimination was assessed using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index). Calibration was evaluated using a bootstrap-corrected calibration plot with 500 resamples (B = 500). Patients were stratified into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups based on tertiles of the nomogram-predicted 3-year DMFS. Individual treatment effects (ITEs) of IC on 3-year DMFS were estimated under the potential outcomes framework using a counterfactual prediction approach implemented in the personalized R package. Patient-specific counterfactual 3-year DMFS probabilities under IC and no IC were generated, and the individualized absolute treatment effect was defined as their difference. A LOESS-smoothed curve was used to visualize the association between baseline predicted 3-year DMFS and the estimated absolute IC-associated benefit. All tests were two-sided with a significance level of 0.05. Analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.3).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 496 consecutive patients with stage III-IV oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) treated at two centers between 2010 and 2020 were included in this real-world cohort study. Of these, 173 received induction chemotherapy (IC group) and 323 did not (non-IC group). Prior to IPTW, substantial imbalances in baseline characteristics were observed between the two groups. The IC group had higher proportions of male patients (88.4% vs. 79.9%), advanced nodal stage (N2-3: 74.0% vs. 66.5%), HPV-negative status (64.2% vs. 47.1%), and smoking history (75.7% vs. 64.1%), all with p < 0.05 and standardized mean differences (SMDs) > 0.2 (Table 1). After applying stabilized and truncated IPTW, covariate balance was substantially improved. Most baseline variables achieved SMDs below 0.1, except for sex (SMD = 0.131), which remained mildly imbalanced. The IPTW-adjusted cohort showed comparable distributions of key clinical characteristics, including age, T/N stage, HPV status, and treatment modality (concurrent chemoradiotherapy in ~ 70% of both groups) (Table 2).

Survival analysis between IC and non-IC group

With a median follow-up of 75.33 months (IQR: 55.30-106.87), survival outcomes were compared between the non-IC and IC groups before and after IPTW adjustment. Before weighting, the IC group showed inferior LRFS compared to the non-IC group (5-year LRFS: 53.7% vs. 64.2%, p = 0.034; HR 1.35, 95% CI: 1.02–1.78). However, no significant differences were observed in OS, PFS, or DMFS (all p > 0.05). After IPTW, no statistically significant differences were observed in LRFS, OS, PFS, or DMFS (all p > 0.05) between IC and non-IC group as shown in Fig. 1.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves. (A-D) Unadjusted cohort before IPTW: OS, PFS, LRFS, and DMFS. (E-H) IPTW-adjusted cohort: OS, PFS, LRFS, and DMFS

To evaluate the impact of IC by HPV status, additional IPTW-adjusted survival analyses were performed separately in the HPV-negative (Supplementary Fig. 2) and HPV-positive (Supplementary Fig. 3) subgroups. No significant differences in OS, PFS, DMFS, or LRFS were observed between IC and non-IC groups in either subgroup. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, subgroup analyses stratified by HPV status, concurrent treatment modality, chemotherapy regimen, and number of induction chemotherapy (IC) cycles consistently demonstrated no significant survival benefit associated with IC across all subgroups.

IPTW-adjusted cox regression and subgroup analysis

Univariate Cox regression analysis identified significant adverse prognostic factors for overall survival (OS), including male sex, age ≥ 60 years, advanced T (T3-T4) and N stage (N3), smoking history, alcohol use, cENE, and non-tonsillar primary site. HPV-positive status and CCRT were associated with improved OS, whereas IC alone showed no significant benefit (Table 3). Similar associations were observed for PFS, DMFS, and LRFS (Table 3).

Nomogram and treatment benefit

To identify patients most likely to benefit from IC, we developed a nomogram integrating clinical predictors, including T and N stage, HPV status, cENE, smoking, alcohol use, and tumor subsite, to estimate the probability of 3-year DMFS (Fig. 3A and B). The nomogram showed good discrimination, with a Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) of 0.697. Calibration plots showed close agreement between predicted and actual 3-year DMFS probabilities (Fig. 3C). Patients were categorized into low (< 8%), intermediate (8–14%), and high (> 14%) risk groups based on the tertile distribution of nomogram-predicted baseline risk scores. Individual-level estimation of treatment benefit from IC suggested that patients in the higher baseline-risk group derived greater absolute benefit from IC (Fig. 3D).

Survival benefit in IC responders

Within the IC cohort, objective responders (CR/PR) had better outcomes than non-responders (SD/PD). Using CR/PR as the reference, SD/PD was associated with inferior OS (HR 1.8 (95% CI: 1.08-3.00), p = 0.022), PFS (HR 1.85 (95% CI: 1.17–2.93), p = 0.007), LRFS (HR 1.79 (95% CI: 1.12–2.85), p = 0.013), and DMFS (HR 2.00 (95% CI: 1.22–3.28), p = 0.005) (Supplementary Fig. 5).

In HPV negative patients, as shown in Fig. 4A-D, patients achieving CR/PR had significantly improved OS (p = 0.019), PFS (p = 0.0021), LRFS (p = 0.0014), and DMFS (p = 0.010) compared to those with SD/PD. In multivariable Cox regression, IC response remained an independent predictor of survival. Compared to CR/PR, SD/PD was associated with inferior outcomes in PFS (HR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.01–3.18, p = 0.047) and LRFS (HR = 1.85, 95% CI: 1.04–3.31, p = 0.037). In contrast, HPV-positive patients exhibited no significant association between IC response and clinical outcomes despite their overall better prognosis (all p > 0.05) (Fig. 4E-H).

Adverse events

In our cohort, the addition of IC was associated with an acceptable toxicity profile compared with the non-IC group. Treatment-related toxicities are summarized in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. During induction chemotherapy, any-grade hematologic toxicity occurred in 78/173 patients (45.1%), while grade 3–4 hematologic toxicity occurred in 11/173 patients (6.4%). Febrile neutropenia occurred in 17/173 patients (9.8%) in the IC group and in 0/323 patients (0.0%) in the non-IC group.

During subsequent RT/CRT, grade ≥ 3 hematologic toxicity, calculated at the patient level as grade ≥ 3 leukopenia, anemia, or thrombocytopenia without double-counting overlapping events, occurred in 7/173 patients (4.0%) in the IC group and 25/323 patients (7.7%) in the non-IC group. Ototoxicity occurred in 0/75 evaluable IC patients (0.0%) and 8/323 non-IC patients (2.5%), while peripheral neuropathy occurred in 2/75 evaluable IC patients (2.7%) and 0/323 non-IC patients (0.0%).

Regarding other RT/CRT-related adverse events, patients in the non-IC group experienced a higher frequency of grade 3 mucositis (41.8% vs. 23.7%) and grade 2 xerostomia (60.7% vs. 48.6%). Other adverse events, including hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity, showed no significant differences between the two groups (Supplementary Tables 1 and Supplementary Table 2).

Feasibility and compliance of RT/CRT

Feasibility and compliance of definitive RT/CRT are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. The mean delivered RT dose was similar between the IC and non-IC groups, with 6868.83 cGy and 6858.4 cGy, respectively. RT interruption or premature discontinuation occurred in 1/173 patients (0.6%) in the IC group and 6/323 patients (1.9%) in the non-IC group. The lowest delivered RT dose was 6360 cGy in the IC group and 5400 cGy in the non-IC group. No patient in the IC group experienced a prolonged RT break. In the non-IC group, two patients had a prolonged treatment break of approximately two weeks due to concurrent chemotherapy-related toxicity and subsequently received compensatory treatment. Concurrent chemotherapy was administered in 123/173 patients (71.1%) in the IC group and 229/323 patients (70.9%) in the non-IC group. Completion of concurrent chemotherapy was achieved in 107/173 patients (61.8%) and 202/323 patients (62.5%), respectively. Concurrent chemotherapy was discontinued due to toxicity in 16/173 patients (9.2%) in the IC group and 27/323 patients (8.4%) in the non-IC group.

Discussion

The role of IC in the management of locally advanced OPSCC remains controversial. Several randomized phase III trials, including DeCIDE and PARADIGM, have shown no significant survival benefit of IC followed by CCRT over CCRT alone in unselected Head and Neck Cancer (HNC) patients. A meta-analysis by the MACH-NC group also indicated no survival benefit of IC in non-metastatic HNC [15]. Population based data from the NCDB further corroborated these findings, showing comparable overall survival between IC and non-IC strategies, with a signal of benefit restricted to select high-risk subsets such as T4/N3 and HPV-negative LA-HNSCC [16]. Accumulating evidence suggests that IC is unlikely to function as a universal treatment intensification strategy, but rather to enhance distant disease control and to identify biologically aggressive subgroups who may derive differential benefit.

In our real-world, dual-institution cohort, with ~ 70% of patients receiving CCRT in both groups, IC did not improve OS in the overall cohort after IPTW to address baseline imbalances. IC was associated with better DMFS and PFS in IPTW-adjusted multivariable models; however, the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a broad survival advantage from IC. The observed benefit was mainly reflected in distant disease control, supporting a potential role of IC in reducing distant failure in selected high-risk patients rather than as a universal treatment intensification strategy.

The toxicity data indicate that clinically relevant hematologic toxicity during IC was observed but remained limited, with grade 3–4 hematologic toxicity occurring in 6.4% of patients. Severe hematologic toxicity during subsequent RT/CRT was not increased in the IC group. Ototoxicity and peripheral neuropathy were infrequent, although these findings should be interpreted cautiously because these toxicities were systematically documented only at one center.

A major concern regarding IC is that toxicity or clinical deterioration may compromise subsequent definitive RT/CRT. In our cohort, however, RT dose delivery, prolonged RT breaks, concurrent chemotherapy administration, concurrent chemotherapy completion, and toxicity-related discontinuation of concurrent chemotherapy were comparable between the IC and non-IC groups as shown in Supplementary Table 3. Notably, no patient in the IC group experienced a prolonged RT break. These findings suggest that IC did not substantially impair the feasibility of subsequent definitive RT/CRT in this selected cohort. Nevertheless, careful patient selection remains essential, as previous real-world studies have shown that reduced RT dose, prolonged treatment breaks, or inability to complete concurrent chemotherapy may negatively affect outcomes [17, 18].

In our view, IC should not be considered a substitute for definitive local treatment. Whenever clinically feasible, definitive RT should be delivered without compromise, and concurrent chemotherapy should be considered according to performance status, comorbidities, renal function, hearing function, neuropathy, and tolerance of IC. However, cumulative toxicity from IC may impair the feasibility of subsequent concurrent systemic treatment. Therefore, the decision to administer concomitant chemotherapy after IC should be individualized.

Accumulating evidence has highlighted IC’s potential to reduce distant metastasis (DM), particularly in high-risk subsets. Bhattasali et al. reported that in patients with low-neck and/or N3 lymphadenopathy, IC followed by CCRT reduced distant failure compared to CCRT alone, leading to improved PFS (49% vs. 74%, adjusted HR = 0.46 [0.22–0.93]; p = 0.03) and a lower 3-year DM rate (38% vs. 18%, HR = 0.32 [0.13–0.82]; p = 0.02) in p16-positive OPSCC [19]. Concordantly, in our study IC was associated with a significant reduction in the risk of distant metastasis in the N3 subgroup (HR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.20–0.93, p = 0.033). Furthermore, risk-adapted stratification based on nomogram-derived baseline risk further refined patient selection for IC. Our model enabled stratification into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups for distant metastasis. Notably, treatment benefit curves indicated that patients in the high-risk group derived greater absolute benefit from IC, while those in the low-risk group showed minimal gain. This risk-adapted analysis supports the notion that the efficacy of IC may depend on baseline metastatic potential, and highlights the potential utility of individualized, nomogram-guided treatment decision-making in OPSCC.

Huang et al. and Fan et al. have indicated that higher grades of imaging extranodal extension (iENE) are associated with an increased risk of distant metastasis, and emphasized the importance of accurate iENE grading [18–22]. Although comprehensive iENE grading was not feasible in our cohort, cENE-based analyses did not show clear benefit from IC in cENE-positive patients, highlighting the need for prospective work with rigorous, multimodal iENE grading.

HPV status has emerged as a critical biological determinant in OPSCC, influencing treatment response and prognosis. HPV-positive tumors generally confer a favorable outcome and strong response to chemoradiation. Our multivariate analysis confirmed known prognostic indicators-tobacco use, advanced TNM staging, increasing age, and HPV-negative status-as independent predictors of inferior outcomes. While HPV positive status remained a robust protective factor, IC did not confer overall survival benefits in either HPV-positive or HPV-negative subgroups.

Recently, de-escalation efforts in HPV-positive disease have yielded mixed results. NRG-HN002 trial showed that reduced-dose radiation with concurrent cisplatin in low-risk HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancer with similar 2-year progression-free survival compared to IMRT alone [23]. However, interim results of NRG-HN005 favored standard therapy. Interestingly, the potential prognostic utility of IC response has also gained traction, particularly in guiding treatment de-intensification mostly in HPV-positive patients. The OPTIMA-II trial demonstrated the feasibility of neoadjuvant nivolumab combined with chemotherapy followed by response-adapted local therapy or transoral robotic surgery (TORS), achieving 2-year PFS of 90% (95% CI, 0.80–0.95) and OS of 91.4% (95% CI, 0.82–0.96) in stage III-IV HPV-positive OPSCC [24]. Notably, a phase II randomized trial (iChoice) demonstrated that de-escalation based on IC response in HPV-positive OPSCC patients was feasible [13]. Ruichen et al. also demonstrated that the response to IC correlated with improved progression-free and overall survival in OPSCC [12].

In our analysis, however, IC response stratified outcomes only in HPV-negative disease. Within this subgroup, IC responders (CR/PR) had significantly improved survival compared to non-responders (SD/PD), whereas in HPV-positive disease IC response did not further stratify prognosis. While current paradigms favor treatment de-escalation in HPV-positive OPSCC, our findings suggest that HPV-negative patients who exhibit a favorable response to IC may also experience improved survival. This positions IC response as both a potential biomarker and therapeutic stratification tool for HPV-negative disease. By contrast, among HPV-positive patients, IC response did not further stratify prognosis, suggesting that response alone may be insufficient as a de-escalation trigger in this subgroup and may need to be complemented by biological markers or prospective validation.

Furthermore, in complex clinical scenarios-such as patients with bulky primary tumors or delayed initiation of radiotherapy-IC may serve as an effective cytoreductive strategy. Tumor volume reduction achieved through IC may improve radiotherapy precision, reduce tumor hypoxia, and potentially mitigate radiation-related toxicities. In our study, although patients in the IC group exhibited a higher incidence of anemia during radiotherapy, lower rates of xerostomia and mucositis were observed, potentially attributable to reduced radiation volume following IC. Furthermore, in the era of immunotherapy, a decreased irradiation volume may contribute to better lymphocyte preservation, thereby enhancing potential synergistic effects with immunotherapy and improving treatment outcomes. Though these hypotheses require prospective validation, they emphasize the utility of IC as both a bridging and debulking modality.

This retrospective, dual-institution study utilized IPTW to minimize baseline covariate imbalances; however, residual discrepancies-particularly in sex distribution-persisted. While IPTW adjusted for measured confounders, the influence of unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. Subgroup analyses were exploratory and limited by small sample sizes (e.g., N3 cohort, n = 58), potentially affecting statistical power and increasing type I/II error risk. Additionally, although most patients (49.71%, n = 86) received 2–3 cycles of TPF-based IC, a portion were treated with TP (38.15%, n = 66) or PF (12.14%, n = 21) regimens, introducing treatment heterogeneity that may have biased outcomes. Notably, given that clinical decision-making in oropharyngeal cancer management still predominantly relies on the AJCC 7th edition staging framework, our study cohort was selected based on these criteria (stage III-IV). While we have provided comparative 8th edition staging data in Table 2 for reference, this inherent selection methodology may introduce certain limitations to outcome interpretation. In our study, HPV status was determined based on p16 immunohistochemistry as a surrogate marker, which may introduce potential misclassification or confounding bias [25]. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of IC regimens reflects real-world clinical practice but also introduces potential selection bias. The variability in the number of IC cycles also represents an important limitation. In particular, prolonged IC may increase cumulative toxicity and potentially compromise the feasibility of subsequent definitive RT/CRT. However, in the present cohort, feasibility data did not suggest a substantial impairment of subsequent RT/CRT delivery after IC. This should be considered when interpreting the results. Given the retrospective design, non-randomized treatment allocation, baseline imbalances, and heterogeneity of IC regimens and cycle numbers, the present findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Conclusion

In this bicentric retrospective cohort of patients with locally advanced OPSCC, IC was not associated with an overall survival benefit after adjustment. IC was associated with improved DMFS, suggesting a possible role in reducing distant failure in selected high-risk patients. Early IC response further stratified prognosis among HPV-negative patients. However, given the retrospective design, treatment heterogeneity, and potential impact of IC toxicity on subsequent RT/CRT, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require prospective validation.
