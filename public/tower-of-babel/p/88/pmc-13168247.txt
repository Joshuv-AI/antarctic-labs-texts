Key natural influences on groundwater storage changes in Central and Southern Arizona
Authors: Behshad Mohajer, James S. Famiglietti, Hrishikesh A. Chandanpurkar, Fengwei Hung, Karem Abdelmohsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Groundwater sustainability in Central and Southern Arizona is threatened by prolonged droughts, rising temperatures, reduced surface water supplies, and groundwater overdraft. Recent studies indicate accelerating declines in regional groundwater storage. While the anthropogenic drivers of these declines are relatively well understood, the role of natural hydroclimatic variability in groundwater storage changes has received less attention. In this study, we utilize NASA’s Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor, GRACE Follow-On (GRACE-FO), together with NASA’s Western Land Data Assimilation System (WLDAS) to evaluate the hydroclimatic controls on Groundwater Storage Anomalies (GWSA) variability in Central and Southern Arizona. We quantify long-term standardized trends, subbasin-scale correlations, and dominant modes of natural variability using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) from 2004 to 2021. Principal components are then used to group groundwater subbasins using k-means clustering, an unsupervised machine learning algorithm, and results are compared with in situ measurements and local management strategies. Our results show substantial spatial heterogeneity across the region. This heterogeneity is characterized by recharge-responsive northern and central subbasins, which are influenced primarily by precipitation and subsurface runoff, and loss-dominated southern subbasins, associated with weaker natural recharge and stronger atmospheric demand. PCA shows that natural hydroclimatic variability is statistically aligned with approximately 16% of the inter-subbasin spatial variance in GRACE/FO groundwater storage trends. Within this natural component, total evapotranspiration (~ 29%), precipitation (~ 23%), and subsurface runoff (~ 20%) represent the largest contributors to the explained variance. The remaining spatial variance may reflect anthropogenic influences, geologic heterogeneity, and residual observational or modeling uncertainties. Our diagnostic framework identifies groundwater subbasin clusters driven by shared hydroclimatic modes. It has the potential to serve as a transferable tool for recharge feasibility analysis, groundwater sustainability assessments, and future local groundwater planning in the Lower Colorado River Basin.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44132-0.

Introduction

The Colorado River Basin (CRB) encompasses nearly 250,000 square miles of the arid and semi-arid American Southwest, draining portions of seven western states and northern Mexico1. The CRB is hydrologically divided into the Upper Colorado River Basin (UCRB) and the Lower Colorado River Basin (LCRB). The LCRB, which encompasses Arizona, California, and Nevada, is home to most of the basin’s urban and agricultural areas and contributes to over 55% of the total consumptive use2,3. Geologically, the LCRB is dominated by deep alluvial aquifers (Fig. S8) composed of interbedded sands and silts with variable hydraulic connectivity4–7. These contrasting geological frameworks produce highly non-uniform recharge rates, groundwater-surface water interactions, and aquifer response to stress, which are increasingly being tested by a historic megadrought8–14.

The most severe drought of the last 1,200 years has accelerated hydrological imbalances across the CRB15–18. Declines in precipitation, snowpack, and reservoir storage, combined with rising atmospheric demand, have substantially reduced natural recharge in the basin19,20. On the other hand, groundwater is increasingly being used to compensate for shortfalls in surface water supply, especially in Central and Southern Arizona21. Anticipated reductions in Colorado River allocations under post-2026 operating guidelines only heighten the urgency for groundwater resilience in this region22.

Central and Southern Arizona—particularly the subbasins within and surrounding the Active Management Areas (AMAs), state-designated groundwater management zones—occupy a unique position within the LCRB, where persistent regional hydrological variability intersects with diverse geological settings and water-use patterns23. This region lies within the Basin and Range Province, an arid region characterized by deep, unconsolidated alluvial aquifers in fault-bounded basins that store substantial volumes of water (see Sect. 5.1 and Fig. S8). However, water supply accessibility varies widely across the region24,25. Recharge in these aquifers depends largely on episodic infiltration along mountain fronts and active flowing river channels, resulting in heterogeneous responses to both natural and anthropogenic drivers26,27. Groundwater historically served as the primary water source in the region until the construction of the Central Arizona Project (CAP) canal in the 1980s, which enabled large-scale imports of Colorado River water28. In recent decades, urban growth, prolonged droughts, and surface water shortages have led to a renewed reliance on groundwater resources in the region29,30,27,22. Although regulatory frameworks and Managed Aquifer Recharge (MAR) programs such as those implemented by the Arizona Water Banking Authority (AWBA) have mitigated aquifer depletion in some areas, groundwater declines persist, particularly outside areas regulated under the Groundwater Management Act (GMA) of 198030. These trends highlight the need for diagnostic approaches that integrate climate variability, aquifer response heterogeneity, and human management signals to identify vulnerabilities and inform sustainable regional groundwater planning21.

Satellite gravimetry has substantially expanded the capacity for large-scale groundwater monitoring31–33. The GRACE mission and its successor GRACE Follow-On (GRACE-FO) —hereafter collectively referred to as GRACE/FO— have enabled continuous observation of changes in total Terrestrial Water Storage (TWS) through measurement of subtle variations in Earth’s gravity field34,35. Groundwater Storage Anomalies (GWSA) are derived from GRACE/FO’s TWS by removing modeled or observed contributions from snow, soil moisture, and surface water, providing monthly estimates of vertically integrated groundwater storage changes21,36–39. The global coverage and temporal consistency of GRACE/FO are particularly valuable in regions with sparse in situ monitoring networks40. However, while GRACE/FO accurately captures total water storage changes, the satellites cannot identify the surface processes that drive those changes41.

Previous studies demonstrated that integrating GRACE/FO observations with land surface models improves our ability to detect groundwater trends and their associated hydroclimatic drivers42–44. Despite these advances, relatively few studies have provided actionable insights for water managers in water resources planning projects. Although GRACE and GRACE/FO observations have significantly advanced the understanding of large-scale groundwater depletion, most prior studies have focused on trend detection or broad attribution of losses to climate versus human forcing75. Few studies have examined how natural hydroclimatic variability differentially influences groundwater storage across subbasins, particularly in highly managed regions where pumping and managed recharge strongly modulate the observed signal. Consequently, few diagnostic frameworks integrate GRACE/FO-derived groundwater observations with land surface model outputs to compare spatially heterogeneous natural hydroclimatic influence at management-relevant scales. Recent evidence shows that hydroclimatic variability exerts strong, nonlinear controls on water availability in the CRB, with precipitation playing a dominant role in regulating runoff efficiency and downstream water supply76. These findings highlight the importance of explicitly considering how climate-driven surface processes impact subsurface storage responses, particularly in arid and semi-arid basins. Here, we address this gap by introducing a subbasin-scale diagnostic framework that couples GRACE/FO groundwater storage anomalies with WLDAS45 hydrometeorological variables to identify dominant hydroclimatic modes.

We present a diagnostic framework that integrates satellite-derived observations of groundwater storage change with land surface model variables to quantify the dominant hydroclimatic influences on groundwater storage change at the subbasin scale. We test our approach in Central and Southern Arizona. Our approach captures both spatial variability and temporal dynamics of surface–subsurface interactions by integrating satellite observations with hydrometeorological variables and consists of three core components: (1) quantification of long-term trends in groundwater and surface conditions; (2) identification of influential climate–groundwater linkages; and (3) extraction of dominant hydroclimatic modes. In addition, we classify groundwater subbasins with similar characteristics using clustering based on principal component scores, enhancing interpretability and supporting spatial planning. This multi-method approach provides a robust assessment of how groundwater storage variability is influenced by natural land-surface processes, offering practical insights for regional groundwater management under evolving water availability and climate conditions.

Standardized correlation of GRACE/FO GWSA and surface fluxes

The Pearson correlations between GRACE/FO-derived GWSA and selected WLDAS hydroclimatic variables at the subbasin scale (Fig. 2) provide a diagnostic assessment of the relationship between natural surface-atmosphere processes and groundwater storage variability across the study area. Positive standardized correlations between GWSA and precipitation rate, deep soil moisture, and subsurface runoff are most pronounced in several northern and central subbasins. In these regions, groundwater storage variations are closely linked to hydrologic inputs, indicating that seasonal to interannual climate variability directly influences subsurface water storage. This pattern is consistent with conditions in undeveloped basins or those with managed recharge facilities that enhance infiltration and deep percolation50,51.

In southern subbasins, standardized correlations between GRACE/FO GWSA and evapotranspiration and vegetation transpiration are strongly negative. Surface runoff also exhibits its strongest negative correlations with GWSA in southern and southeastern subbasins, whereas standardized correlations in northern basins are weak to moderately positive. These subbasins reflect loss-driven regimes characterized by high atmospheric water demand, intensified land surface energy fluxes, and limited natural infiltration capacity. The inverse relationship between groundwater storage and surface fluxes is consistent with increased evaporative demand and reduced recharge signals50. Such signals are unlikely to reverse without supplemental recharge or reductions in groundwater extraction52,53.

Additionally, to assess the influence of serial correlation, we computed cross-correlations at lags ranging from − 3 to + 3 months for all target subbasins and reassessed zero-lag significance using a first-order Auto Regressive AR (1) model. Median lagged responses indicate that evapotranspiration and vegetation transpiration typically led GWSA by approximately one to two months, while rain rate and surface runoff also show their strongest correlations at positive lags, indicating leading behavior relative to groundwater storage. Deep soil moisture peaks near zero lag, whereas subsurface runoff remains positively correlated across multiple lags. Although the strong persistence of GWSA reduces nominal statistical significance after degree of freedom correction, a non-trivial subset of subbasins retains detectable zero-lag coupling. Complete distributions, heatmaps, autocorrelation histograms, and significance summaries are provided in the supplementary information Text S3 and Fig. S3–S5.

Interpretation of these standardized correlations may benefit from careful consideration of differences in data representation. GWSA anomalies integrate both natural hydroclimatic variability and anthropogenic influences (e.g., pumping, artificial recharge, and broader aquifer management) through its measurement of total terrestrial water storage change. In contrast, WLDAS variables are derived from a physically based land-surface model that represents only natural water and energy fluxes, excluding human water uses47. This distinction is particularly important in AMAs, where artificial recharge from CAP deliveries contributes substantially to storage. In such basins, positive GRACE/FO trends or weak correlations with natural variables may partially reflect MAR (Fig. S6) or other human interventions that decouple groundwater storage changes from natural climate forcing. Additionally, intensive groundwater withdrawals can dampen or reverse the natural correlation signal in heavily pumped subbasins that lack significant artificial recharge (see Sect. 3.2). For example, in agricultural areas with high extraction rates and limited water imports, GRACE/FO GWSA may show declining trends even where WLDAS variables indicate favorable natural recharge conditions21.

Overall, these findings underscore the importance of integrating human water-use information into future analyses to partition the respective contributions of climate forcing and anthropogenic factors. Without such integration, correlations between GRACE/FO GWSA and natural variables risk conflating climate–groundwater coupling with infrastructure-driven storage changes, especially in regions where policy and management interventions strongly mediate the hydrologic signal.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal component analysis of the subbasin-wide correlation matrix shows three dominant and physically interpretable hydroclimatic modes governing groundwater storage variability in the study area (Table S1). These PCA patterns indicate that groundwater subbasins do not respond uniformly to climate forcings. PC1 represents a hydroclimate coherence mode capturing the overall strength of coupling between GWSA and precipitation, soil moisture, runoff, and evapotranspiration. In contrast, PC2 and PC3 resolve the recharge–loss contrast that co-varies with subbasin-scale sensitivity. Specifically, PC2 represents a mode of variability contrasting atmospheric-flux variables (higher PC2 scores associated with evapotranspiration and precipitation) with runoff- and plant-water-use processes (lower PC2 scores associated with surface runoff, subsurface runoff, and vegetation transpiration). PC3 primarily reflects deep-soil storage control, with higher PC3 scores indicating stronger coupling between GWSA and deep soil moisture. In contrast, low PC2 and/or low PC3 values indicate loss-dominated modes, in which atmospheric demand, plant water use, or rapid runoff decouple groundwater storage from natural recharge. Together, these patterns delineate a recharge-responsive north-central tier and a loss-dominated southern tier, with AMA basins showing management-modulated signals.

Spatial patterns of PCA score maps (Fig. 3) show coherent regional groupings across the analyzed subbasins. PC1 scores are relatively low in basins such as the East Salt River Valley (ESR), the West Salt River Valley (WSR), and the Harquahala (HAR) Basin, indicating weak overall hydroclimatic coupling. In contrast, higher PC1 scores are observed in the Aguirre Valley (AGV) and Santa Cruz AMA (SCA). Avra Valley (AVR) and Maricopa–Stanfield (MST) exhibit positive to near-neutral PC1 values, suggesting intermediate coupling strength. PC2 further distinguishes subbasins dominated by atmospheric flux (characterized by low or negative scores in HAR, Gila Bend (GIL), and Agua Fria (AGF)) from basins with stronger runoff and plant water use pathways, such as SCA. PC3 highlights variability associated with deep-soil storage, with near-zero scores in ESR, modestly positive scores in MST, Eloy (ELO), Vekol Valley (VEK), and Gila Bend (GIL), and higher positive values observed in Santa Rosa Valley (SRO).

To assess the relationship between these hydroclimatic modes and long-term groundwater change, we regressed GRACE/FO-derived GWSA trends across the selected subbasins against the first three principal components. This analysis indicates that approximately 16% of the inter-subbasin spatial variance in groundwater storage trends is statistically associated with the leading hydroclimatic principal components. This value reflects alignment between groundwater trend patterns and dominant climate–groundwater covariance modes, rather than direct volumetric attribution of groundwater change to natural forcing. Within this explained variance, total evapotranspiration accounts for the largest share (≈ 28.6%), followed by rainfall (≈ 23.4%) and subsurface runoff (≈ 19.8%). Vegetation transpiration (≈ 13.0%), surface runoff (≈ 10.0%), and deep soil moisture (≈ 5.2%) each have smaller contributions. These values are consistent with Arizona’s hydroclimatic conditions, where high atmospheric demand drives substantial water loss, while precipitation and mountain-front recharge serve as essential inputs, and deep infiltration signals are spatially limited and attenuated at the basin scale.

Within the explained variance (~ 16%), recharge-related variables represent approximately 58% of the climate-associated component, compared with 42% associated with loss-related variables. Given the modest coefficient of determination, these proportions are indicative of relative hydroclimatic structure rather than quantitative attribution of groundwater decline. Nevertheless, they reinforce the broader finding that spatial variability in recharge potential helps explain why some subbasins experience slower rates of groundwater decline than others. From a management perspective, this underscores the importance of protecting recharge corridors and/or maximizing infiltration potential in basins where recharge is favored, while recognizing that atmospheric losses and groundwater extraction are likely to continue constraining resilience in hotter, drier, and more intensively managed subbasins. Conceptual models of Basin and Range aquifers describe groundwater storage as the balance between spatially focused recharge along mountain fronts and channels and more spatially extensive losses driven by atmospheric demand78–80. The PCA results reflect this flux partitioning by separating recharge-associated processes (precipitation and subsurface runoff) from loss-associated processes (total and vegetation evapotranspiration).

Discussion

Subbasin clusters in Central and Southern Arizona

Clustering of subbasin PC scores further classified the study area into four distinct clusters (Fig. 4). Cluster 4 dominates the south-central and southern subbasins, including portions of the central agricultural valley, and represents a loss-dominated, climate-sensitive regime characterized by low PC3 and PC2 values. These basins exhibit weak natural recharge signals and a strong influence from atmospheric uptake. Cluster 3 includes subbasins with mixed controls, where both elevated evaporative demand and limited natural recharge contribute to groundwater storage variability, and where management signals are likely to play a substantial role. Cluster 2 is concentrated along a north-central, higher-relief corridor and likely reflects more recharge/storage-responsive behavior (i.e., higher PC2/PC3 scores). Finally, cluster 1 appears in a small number of central basins and represents weaker or transitional coupling, with neither recharge nor loss processes clearly dominating the system.

These cluster-specific patterns have direct implications for the sustainability and management of groundwater in the study area (Table 1). Loss-dominated basins are highly sensitive to climate variability, with low natural recharge and high evaporative demand, underscoring the need for long-term conservation strategies and, where feasible, MAR support. Mixed-stress basins experience moderate recharge potential combined with intense pumping pressure, highlighting the importance of tighter integration among surface water availability, climate variability, and groundwater management. Recharge-responsive basins show favorable conditions for infiltration and storage, offering strategic opportunities to expand or sustain recharge programs. In contrast, compound-risk basins such as Gila Bend are more vulnerable, as they are both climatically stressed and largely unregulated. Gila Bend exemplifies the risks associated with unmanaged groundwater pumping outside AMA boundaries. Collectively, these results underscore the need for adaptive, risk-based groundwater policies that explicitly account for both natural vulnerability and institutional capacities.

Stricter overdraft measures could be necessary

In situ groundwater measurements from the Arizona Department of Water Resources (ADWR) index wells, mainly located in unconfined aquifers, show significant spatial and management-related differences in depth-to-water trends across Central and Southern Arizona from 2004 to 2021 (Fig. S2). The south and western subbasins exhibit steep and widespread declines (red shading in Fig. S2; red wells in Fig. 5), reflecting intensive groundwater use combined with limited natural recharge. In contrast, some northern and eastern subbasins display relatively stable or rising groundwater levels (blue shading in Figure S2; blue wells in Fig. 5), often associated with managed recharge activities and lower withdrawal pressures. Time-series analyses confirm statistically significant groundwater recovery in several AMAs and continued declines in non-AMA subbasins, demonstrating that groundwater governance plays an important role in moderating long-term trends (Fig. 6). However, continued declines in portions of the AMAs suggest that existing regulatory protections may be insufficient unless they explicitly address climate-driven stressors and cumulative groundwater extraction. Refer to Text S2 in the Supplementary Information for a detailed methodology on in situ data analysis.

Comparison of our findings with the most recent AMA Management Plans (4MPs) (available online at: https://www.azwater.gov/fourth-management-plan) indicates only partial alignment between current management strategies and the hydroclimatic patterns identified using our approach. Recharge-resilient basins in northern and central regions generally benefit from established recharge and Assured Water Supply programs; however, these programs are largely static and do not explicitly incorporate evolving hydroclimatic variables. Some basins, such as those in the Santa Cruz and Pinal AMAs, rely on conservation measures that often employ fixed evapotranspiration assumptions and therefore may not fully account for increasing climate stress. Intermediate basins remain relatively stable but lack robust, adaptive protection of recharge corridors or proactive recovery operations. As mentioned earlier, the Gila Bend subbasin exemplifies the compound risks resulting from the interaction of natural and anthropogenic pressures. It exhibits low PCA scores and strong correlations with evaporative demand, indicating a persistent natural hydroclimatic deficit. Located outside AMA boundaries, it is largely exempt from pumping regulations, and long-term monitoring data confirm steep groundwater declines (Fig. 5). The absence of large-scale recharge projects or formal management plans leaves this subbasin particularly exposed to ongoing climate stress and unregulated groundwater pumping. Overall, while the 4MPs emphasize recharge and conservation, they do not fully account for climate variability and risks associated with longer dry periods.

The diagnostic framework developed in this study provides a practical basis for prioritizing subbasins under post-2026 water management scenarios. Loss-dominated clusters may benefit from stricter pumping reduction, expanded recharge infrastructure, and enhanced monitoring. In contrast, recharge-responsive clusters may benefit most from protecting infiltration zones and sustaining artificial recharge operations.

GRACE/FO and WLDAS mismatch in subbasins with human modifications

As mentioned in Sect. 2.2, it is essential to note that the GRACE/FO-derived GWSA trend represents an observational estimate of the integrated change in groundwater storage, incorporating both natural and anthropogenic influences. In contrast, the WLDAS groundwater storage variable is a model-based representation of shallow groundwater dynamics driven solely by natural land–surface processes. As a result, differences between these two fields reflect both conceptual and process-level distinctions rather than inconsistencies.

Disentangling natural hydroclimatic variability from anthropogenic influences such as pumping and artificial recharge remains a central challenge when interpreting GRACE/FO-based groundwater storage anomalies. The PCA results presented here are derived from statistical correlations between GRACE/FO (which includes both human and natural signals) and WLDAS (which models only natural processes). Pronounced mismatches occur in certain AMAs (e.g., Phoenix and Pinal AMAs), highlighting the limitations of relying solely on hydroclimatic data or models that exclude human processes, such as pumping or water transfers/exchanges. These discrepancies likely reflect intensive water management activities (e.g., CAP imports, recharge basins, and regulatory frameworks), which can mask or offset underlying natural processes73.

The differences between GRACE/FO and WLDAS variables in managed basins highlight the need for dual-attribution frameworks that can separately quantify natural and human influences on groundwater storage changes. Without explicit consideration of managed recharge, land surface models may underestimate the buffering capacity of recharge infrastructure, while the omission of pumping data obscures the severity of groundwater overdraft. Future studies could address these limitations by incorporating observed or modeled groundwater extraction datasets or by applying inversion techniques that estimate anthropogenic contributions as residuals from the differences in GRACE/FO and WLDAS signals.

Independent paleo-hydrologic evidence supports the conclusion that natural hydroclimatic variability plays a secondary role relative to pumping-induced groundwater decline. For example, noble gas and multi-tracer reconstructions in the Tucson Basin indicate that Holocene-scale fluctuations in recharge were modest compared to water table declines caused by modern groundwater extraction, even during periods of enhanced aridity74. This long-term perspective reinforces the interpretation that the relatively small fraction of GRACE/FO groundwater trend variance explained by hydroclimatic modes (~ 16%) reflects a fundamental dominance of anthropogenic influence in regional groundwater depletion.

Broader impact, uncertainties, and limitations

The diagnostic approach presented here is directly relevant to both physical process-based and data-driven modeling approaches. In physically based groundwater models, understanding the balance between natural hydroclimatic and anthropogenic influences is crucial for accurately representing the mechanisms of recharge, evapotranspiration, and changes in storage. In Machine Learning (ML) and Artificial Intelligence (AI) models, the PCA structure provides an interpretable feature space that captures the dominant patterns of groundwater–climate coupling, thereby reducing the risk of overfitting to noise. Without explicit knowledge of the natural and human processes shaping groundwater storage variability, physical models risk misrepresentation, and without identifying the leading principal components, ML models risk misattribution of main drivers.

Several sources of uncertainty should be acknowledged. GRACE/FO satellite observations, while invaluable for detecting regional groundwater storage changes, are spatially coarse and represent total Terrestrial Water Storage (TWS), which includes anthropogenic influences such as pumping and managed aquifer recharge. This aggregation complicates the isolation of purely natural signals without the use of additional attribution methods. Similarly, WLDAS provides physically consistent, high-resolution estimates of natural surface processes but omits human water use, infrastructure, and policy interventions—factors that increasingly dominate groundwater dynamics in Central and Southern Arizona. These model design and scope differences can introduce signal mismatches in correlation and PCA, particularly in AMAs where human intervention is substantial. Moreover, the land surface model’s performance depends on the choice of meteorological forcing and calibration datasets, which can further influence simulated surface fluxes, such as evapotranspiration or runoff. To partially address these uncertainties, this study employed GRACE/FO products that assimilate observations and WLDAS outputs, which are validated against streamflow and evapotranspiration.

Conclusions

This study presents a diagnostic assessment of hydroclimatic influences governing groundwater storage variability across Central and Southern Arizona by combining NASA’s GRACE/FO satellite gravimetry, hydrometeorological products from NASA’s WLDAS, and multivariate statistical analyses. By linking satellite-derived groundwater storage anomalies with surface climate and land-surface processes, we provide a regionally consistent diagnostic framework for evaluating groundwater sensitivity to climate variability.

Results show pronounced spatial heterogeneity across the study area, characterized by recharge-responsive northern and central subbasins and loss-dominated southern subbasins. Loss-dominated subbasins exhibit persistently negative groundwater storage trends and strong coupling with evaporative fluxes, as reflected by their low PCA scores. These basins generally have limited natural recharge potential, high atmospheric water demand, and heightened sensitivity to warming and drying conditions, making them particularly vulnerable to climate-driven groundwater depletion. In contrast, recharge-responsive subbasins exhibit strong associations between groundwater storage and natural recharge-related variables, including precipitation, deep soil moisture, and runoff, and show higher PCA scores, forming coherent spatial clusters indicative of greater resilience. In such basins, expanded or sustained artificial recharge operations may further enhance favorable hydroclimatic conditions.

The resulting regime classification is diagnostic rather than causal. It identifies where climate–groundwater coupling is strong or weak, while targeted management data such as pumping rates and managed aquifer recharge volumes are required to complete attribution. Accordingly, the hydroclimatic modes identified here should be interpreted as indicators of relative groundwater sensitivity to climate variability, rather than as quantitative predictors of volumetric groundwater storage change.

As Arizona and LCRB confront prolonged drought, rising temperatures, and increasingly constrained surface water allocations, groundwater sustainability will depend on management strategies that explicitly account for both natural hydroclimatic variability and anthropogenic factors. The results presented here show that spatial differences in groundwater vulnerability are shaped by the balance between recharge potential and atmospheric demand, with management interventions playing a statistically significant moderating role. By distinguishing between recharge-responsive and loss-dominated groundwater regimes, this diagnostic framework provides a transferable basis for climate-informed groundwater assessment in arid and semi-arid regions, as well as other data-scarce areas. Incorporating human water-use metrics, artificial recharge volumes, and dynamic groundwater modeling into future analyses would enable more robust causal attribution of observed groundwater changes. Applying PCA in moving temporal windows could also show shifts in subbasin sensitivity as hydroclimatic conditions evolve and management interventions are implemented.

Beyond its technical contributions, this study has direct relevance for groundwater policy and management. By identifying where groundwater systems are primarily constrained by climate-driven recharge limitations versus atmospheric demand and loss processes, the results provide a basis for prioritizing management interventions, such as recharge protection, conservation measures, or enhanced monitoring. The four hydroclimatic regimes identified here (Table 1) provide a practical framework for prioritizing groundwater management actions under increasing climate stress. In the context of the lower Colorado River basin and post-2026 water allocation uncertainty, this diagnostic framework can potentially inform risk-based groundwater planning by helping policymakers distinguish basins where climate adaptation strategies may be effective from those where long-term sustainability will require stricter demand management.

Methods

Study area

Central and Southern Arizona (Fig. 7) provides a suitable setting to evaluate groundwater vulnerability and sustainability within the LCRB22. The region encompasses the Phoenix, Pinal, and Tucson AMAs, which are legally defined jurisdictions established to prevent groundwater overdraft under the Arizona Groundwater Management Act of 198024,25. The AMAs are located within the arid to semi-arid Basin and Range Province. They are characterized by large, fault-contained, alluvial basins with accumulations of unconsolidated material (Fig. S8) that have high potential for large-capacity aquifer systems and are more prone to significant aquifer drawdown, subsidence, and degradation in response to an extended duration of natural and anthropogenic stressors54–56.

Central Arizona can be viewed as a microcosm of compound water stress, where rapid urban development on finite supplies converges with historical irrigated agriculture, inter-basin surface water transfers diminished by prolonged droughts, and institutional constraints on supply and groundwater pumping29,57. The surface water supply in the study area comes primarily from the CAP infrastructure, which is a 336-mile canal that delivers the State of Arizona’s share of Colorado River water, and, to a lesser extent, local runoff from the Salt and Verde Rivers. However, CAP allocations are becoming more uncertain due to the prolonged megadrought, basin-wide over-allocation, and declining snowpack and runoff in the UCRB2,58. Agricultural users are already increasingly dependent on groundwater to fill supply gaps, especially in areas where CAP reductions have led to crop fallowing and necessitate drilling wells to ever greater depths59.

Recharge in these basins is variable, typically low, and driven by precipitation, streamflow, and mountain-front conditions60. Natural recharge rates are mostly low and spatially variable. However, managed recharge through retention basins and direct injections, coordinated by the AWBA, has become an essential means of groundwater recovery in AMAs where water may be available from the CAP61,62. Not all subbasins benefit equally from such institutionally structured interventions; for example, regions of central Arizona have experienced over 100 m of groundwater level decline and six meters of subsidence63. Therefore, focusing on Central and Southern Arizona presents an interesting testbed for scalable groundwater governance mechanisms, particularly considering the uncertain surface water demand and the increasing frequency and intensity of climate extremes.

Analytical workflow

This study employs a multi-step analytical workflow to quantify the influence of the main hydroclimatic controls on groundwater storage variability across groundwater subbasins in Central and Southern Arizona. The analysis consists of three main steps: (1) anomaly calculation, standardization, and linear trend estimation, (2) correlation analysis, and (3) dimensionality reduction and clustering to identify dominant hydroclimatic modes (Fig. 8).

All time series, including GRACE/FO-derived groundwater storage anomalies (GWSA) and land surface variables from WLDAS, were converted to standardized anomalies in the first step. Monthly climatology was computed over the full study period (January 2004–December 2021) for each variable, and standardized anomalies were calculated by subtracting the monthly climatology and dividing by the interannual standard deviation at each grid cell. Linear trends were estimated using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression64 applied to the standardized monthly time series at each grid cell, with time as the independent variable and anomaly magnitude as the dependent variable. The resulting slope coefficients represent the direction and relative magnitude of long-term change over the 2004–2021 period and were subsequently aggregated to the subbasin scale using spatial averaging. Statistical significance of trends was assessed using two-sided t-tests, with effective degrees of freedom adjusted to account for serial autocorrelation (see Supplementary Text S1). Next, the Pearson correlation coefficients were computed between standardized GRACE/FO anomalies and each WLDAS variable at the grid level65. These correlations were aggregated to the subbasin scale using zonal means, producing a subbasin-by-variable matrix that summarizes the strength and direction of hydroclimatic linkages across the study area.

A schematic overview of the analytical workflow

PCA was applied to extract the dominant modes of spatial variability66. The first three principal components were retained based on the cumulative variance explained. Components beyond PC3 contributed marginal variance and lacked clear physical interpretability. The retained components together explain 94.3% of the variance in the correlation structure and approximately 16% of the spatial variance in long-term trends of groundwater storage. PCA provides a linear decomposition of dominant variability patterns and does not explicitly capture nonlinear or lagged interactions between groundwater and climate. In this study, PCA is used as a diagnostic dimensionality-reduction tool to identify coherent hydroclimatic modes and spatial contrasts across subbasins, rather than to infer causality.

Subbasins were then embedded in a two-dimensional feature space defined by their PC1 and PC2 scores to identify spatial patterns in hydroclimatic control. K-means clustering67 was then applied to classify subbasins into four groups with similar hydroclimatic signatures. The scikit-learn implementation of the K-Means algorithm was used with a fixed random seed to ensure reproducibility68.

It is important to note that WLDAS outputs are not used to derive GRACE/FO groundwater storage anomalies in this study. Groundwater storage anomalies are obtained directly from the bias-corrected, enhanced-resolution GRACE/FO product49. WLDAS variables are employed solely for diagnostic purposes, to characterize natural hydroclimatic variability and to assess statistical coupling between surface–atmosphere processes and observed groundwater storage changes. Surface and subsurface runoff are interpreted as indicators of hydrologic partitioning and recharge potential rather than as representations of surface water storage. All analyses are conducted using standardized anomalies to minimize sensitivity to model structural assumptions and systematic bias. For full methodological details, including equations and notation, see Supplementary Text S1.

Data

This study integrates satellite observations and model-derived datasets to examine the hydroclimatic controls on groundwater storage variability across Central and Southern Arizona. Specifically, we utilize (1) groundwater storage anomalies from the GRACE/FO missions, which are extracted from observations of Total Water Storage (TWS)49, (2) land surface hydrometeorological variables from the Western Land Data Assimilation System (WLDAS)45. These datasets collectively enable a multiscale assessment of groundwater and climate interactions from 2004 to 2021. Table 2 describes the datasets used in this study. The analysis period (2004–2021) was selected to ensure temporal consistency across GRACE/FO groundwater storage anomalies and WLDAS hydrometeorological variables, while maximizing the length of overlapping observations. This period also captures the prolonged drought and warming conditions that characterize much of the recent hydroclimatic stress in the Lower Colorado River Basin, making it well-suited for diagnosing groundwater responses to sustained climate forcing.

WLDAS provides high-resolution, observation-driven estimates of key land surface hydrological variables, including precipitation, evapotranspiration, soil moisture, and runoff. WLDAS simulates physical processes at the land-atmosphere interface46 using the Noah-MP model framework47, which integrates satellite and in-situ data to simulate surface fluxes (e.g., evapotranspiration, runoff, and snowmelt) and storage components (e.g., soil moisture) at high spatial and temporal resolutions48. WLDAS integrates multiple data sources with advanced land surface models to produce gridded outputs at 0.01° spatial resolution. While WLDAS offers valuable insights into land-atmosphere processes and simulates hydroclimatic variability at fine spatial scales, uncertainties remain due to model structural limitations, errors in meteorological forcing inputs, and challenges in representing subsurface hydrology and anthropogenic water use67. Despite these limitations, WLDAS datasets have been widely used in regional water balance studies and serve as a critical complement to satellite-based storage observations in this study68,69.

This study utilizes global enhanced-resolution, bias-corrected GWSA data as described in49. These data support the investigation of groundwater storage variability across Central and Southern Arizona by providing spatially consistent, satellite-informed estimates of regional groundwater change, which, despite their coarse native spatial resolution (~ 0.25° × ~0.25°), are well suited for assessing basin-scale and subbasin scale groundwater–climate interactions over the 2004–2021 period. The GRACE/FO TWS data are based on the Landerer Mascon34,77 Release 6 Version 3 solution, which offers improved spatial resolution (~ 0.25° × ~0.25°) through post-processing that integrates Global Land Data Assimilation System (GLDAS) models70 and corrects for biases at the mascon scale49. This enhancement enables meaningful sub-mascon regional analysis while retaining consistency with the original GRACE/FO observations71. The processed dataset accounts for significant sources of uncertainty by incorporating JPL-provided formal errors and removing climatology from anomaly estimation72. The GRACE/FO mission gap (2017–2018) was excluded from the analysis, following49. Shorter and isolated gaps were interpolated after removal of the seasonal climatology.
