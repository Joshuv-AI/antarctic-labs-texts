First documented case of imported human babesiosis in Honduras: Diagnostic challenges with malaria falciparum
Authors: Lesly Chaver, Heydi Flores, Wendy Padilla, Jenny Rodríguez, Concepción Zúniga, Francisco Medina, Gabriela Matamoros, María Esther Araujo, Bryan Ortiz, Gustavo Fontecha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•First confirmed case of imported human babesiosis documented in Honduras.•Initial misdiagnosis as Plasmodium falciparum malaria led to inappropriate antimalarial therapy.•Persistent hyperparasitemia despite chloroquine–primaquine and artemether–lumefantrine treatment.•Molecular assays excluded malaria and confirmed Babesia 18S rRNA, with retrospective detection of the Maltese cross.•Advanced age, cardiac comorbidity, and delayed diagnosis contributed to a fatal outcome.•Case underscores the need for molecular diagnostic capacity in malaria-endemic regions to avoid misdiagnosis.

Introduction

Human babesiosis is an emerging zoonotic disease caused by intra-erythrocytic protozoa of the genus Babesia. Clinically and morphologically, it may resemble P. falciparum malaria [1]. The first human case was described in 1957 in Croatia, followed by the first in an immunocompetent host in Nantucket, USA, in 1969 [2]. Since then, cases have been documented in North America, Europe, Asia, and South America [3,4]. In North America, Babesia microti is the predominant species, transmitted by Ixodes scapularis ticks.

Symptoms are nonspecific, fever, chills, headache, and hemolytic anemia, closely paralleling malaria. A key morphological feature, occasionally observed in cases of high parasitemia, is the tetrad ‘Maltese cross’ formation within erythrocytes, along with the absence of pigment and gametocytes [2]; however, this finding is uncommon and should not be solely relied upon for diagnosis. In malaria-endemic settings, babesiosis may be mistaken for malaria due to P. falciparum unless molecular or serological methods are employed.

We report the first confirmed case of imported babesiosis in Honduras, initially diagnosed as P. falciparum malaria. This case highlights the diagnostic pitfalls of malaria-like febrile illness in returning travelers and the importance of molecular confirmation.

Case presentation

A 72-year-old Honduran man with chronic cardiac arrhythmia returned from Massachusetts on July 13, 2025. No information was available regarding potential risk factors for babesiosis during his stay in Massachusetts, such as outdoor or rural activities, or known tick exposure. He traveled the same day to Jutiquile, Olancho, Honduras (251 km from the airport). By July 22 (day 10), he developed fever, myalgia, arthralgia, and headache. On July 24, he experienced sudden disorientation and inability to recognize relatives. On July 26, he fell from a horse after experiencing dizziness, resulting in cranial trauma and a posterior circulation ischemic stroke. At a local clinic, the patient remained febrile, incoherent, and disoriented. Dengue fever without warning signs (as classified by the World Health Organization, 2009) [5] was suspected and later confirmed by NS1 antigen detection. He was subsequently referred to a tertiary hospital in Tegucigalpa.

At admission on July 27, he was fully conscious (Glasgow 15/15). Laboratory results showed leukocytes 4.2 × 103/µl (reference: 4.0-10.0 × 10³/µl), hematocrit 36.1% (reference: 40-50%), thrombocytopenia 48 × 103/µl (reference: 150-400 × 10³/µl), and monocytes 12.3% (reference: 2-10%). On August 1, laboratory tests revealed leukocytes 4.2 × 10³/µl, hemoglobin 10.4 g/dl, and hematocrit 30.1%. Dengue fever remained the working diagnosis, and supportive treatment was provided, including paracetamol, metoclopramide, bisoprolol, atorvastatin, and oral rehydration. In addition, a positive SARS-CoV-2 antigen test was detected.

On August 5, thick blood smear microscopy showed abundant asexual forms interpreted as P. falciparum. National protocol treatment was initiated with chloroquine (25 mg/kg over 3 days) and a single dose of primaquine (0.75 mg/kg). Despite this, by August 8 his fever persisted (39.2°C). Repeat smear revealed hyperparasitemia (238,000 parasites/µl; 4.8%; 556 asexual stages, no gametocytes) (Figure 1a). By August 9, parasitemia persisted and the patient developed multiorgan failure, anemia, acute kidney injury, and electrolyte imbalances. Suspecting drug resistance, artemether–lumefantrine (20/120 mg, six doses over 3 days) was prescribed. On August 10, labs showed persistent parasitemia and blood urea nitrogen 107 mg/dl (reference: 7-20 mg/dl), creatinine 6.25 mg/dl (reference: 0.6-1.3 mg/dl), hyponatremia (Na⁺ 128 mmol/l; reference: 135-145 mmol/l), hyperkalemia (K⁺ 5.6 mmol/l; reference: 3.5-5.1 mmol/l), hypocalcemia (Ca²⁺ 8.1 mg/dl; reference: 8.5-10.2 mg/dl), and hyperphosphatemia (P 7.1 mg/dl; reference: 2.5-4.5 mg/dl).

On August 14 (day 33 after return), microscopy revealed persistent parasitemia (107 parasites/µl; nine asexual forms per 14 leukocytes). The patient died later that day from an acute myocardial infarction type III. As a contributing factor, the use of chloroquine may have played a role in the fatal outcome, since it is contraindicated in patients with cardiac arrhythmias. At that time, severe malaria with suspected drug resistance was considered.

To clarify, dried blood spot samples were sent to the Universidad Nacional Autónoma de Honduras (UNAH) Center for Genetic Research. Sequencing of pfcrt [6], and pfs47 [7] failed, and photo-induced electron transfer-polymerase chain reaction (PET-PCR) pan-Plasmodium [8,9] was negative. Given the absence of gametocytes, lack of epidemiological link, and failure of therapy, malaria was reconsidered. PCR targeting the Babesia 18S rRNA gene was performed using genus-specific primers and conditions previously described by Amoah et al. [10], yielding a ∼500 bp amplicon (Figure 1b) that confirmed babesiosis. The ∼454 bp PCR products were subsequently sequenced by the Sanger method using primers PiroA1 and PiroB, as described by Földvári et al. [11], showing 100% identity with Babesia microti, supporting this as the likely species involved. The sequence was deposited in GenBank under accession number PX393150. Retrospective smear review demonstrated the pathognomonic “Maltese cross” (Figure 1c).

Discussion

This case represents the first confirmed imported human babesiosis in Honduras, initially misdiagnosed as falciparum malaria. The clinical overlap between Babesia and Plasmodium poses significant diagnostic challenges in malaria-endemic settings. Persistent fever, despite standard antimalarial regimens and the absence of gametocytes should have prompted alternative diagnoses. Misdiagnosis of babesiosis as malaria has been reported elsewhere, often delaying therapy and worsening prognosis [1]. In this case, both chloroquine–primaquine and artemether–lumefantrine were ineffective, reinforcing the need for molecular confirmation.

The inability to amplify Plasmodium markers (pfcrt, pfs47), combined with negative pan-Plasmodium PET-PCR, excluded malaria. PCR targeting Babesia confirmed infection, consistent with literature emphasizing the necessity of molecular tools for differentiating Babesia spp. from morphologically similar protozoa [12]. Exposure likely occurred in Massachusetts, which is a recognized as being endemic for Babesia microti [13]. No local transmission has been reported in Honduras, supporting the imported nature of this case.

This report also illustrates host susceptibility factors. Elderly patients with comorbidities are at greater risk of severe babesiosis. Age, chronic cardiac disease, and possible splenic dysfunction likely contributed to the patient’s multiorgan failure. Fatal babesiosis, while rare, is documented in immunocompromised and elderly populations [14,15]. Delayed diagnosis prevented initiation of appropriate therapy, atovaquone plus azithromycin for mild-moderate disease or clindamycin plus quinine for severe cases [16].

From a public health standpoint, this case broadens the regional epidemiology of babesiosis in Central America and underscores the need for diagnostic vigilance. In malaria-endemic countries, laboratory staff and clinicians should include babesiosis in the differential diagnosis of febrile hemolytic syndromes, especially in travelers unresponsive to antimalarials. Establishing confirmatory molecular capacity in reference laboratories is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment.

In conclusion, imported babesiosis should be considered in febrile patients with malaria-like illness returning from endemic regions. With increasing travel and expanding tick vectors [17,18], more imported cases are likely. Improving diagnostic capacity and clinical awareness is crucial to reduce morbidity and mortality.
