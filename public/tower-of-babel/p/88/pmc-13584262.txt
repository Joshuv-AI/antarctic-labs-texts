Environmental moderators of neighbourhood socioeconomic inequalities in mental health: a systematic review protocol
Authors: Rebecca A Reid, Suzanne Mavoa, Julia Gilmartin-Thomas, Sarah Foster, Jerome N Rachele
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMental health inequalities exist across levels of neighbourhood socioeconomic disadvantage. Numerous reviews have examined the relationship between neighbourhood social, economic, and physical environmental attributes and mental health. However, reviews investigating whether environmental attributes present an opportunity to reduce neighbourhood socioeconomic inequalities in mental health are scarce. Therefore, the aim of the systematic review proposed in this protocol is to identify potential environmental moderators to neighbourhood socioeconomic inequalities in mental health.MethodsThe following electronic databases will be searched: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, PsycINFO, CINAHL Complete, GreenFILE and ProQuest. In addition, grey literature will be searched. Quantitative studies will be included if they include community-dwelling adults, with neighbourhood socioeconomic disadvantage as an exposure, mental health as an outcome and an objective measure of an environmental attribute as a potential moderator. Two reviewers will independently determine the eligibility of studies based on the inclusion/exclusion criteria. Data extraction and quality appraisal will be conducted. Data synthesis will be carried out and a meta-analysis will be considered if sufficient studies are available. If a meta-analysis is not feasible a descriptive summary of the study characteristics will be presented.DiscussionExisting research illustrates the diversity of environmental attributes and the potential for them to moderate the association between neighbourhood socioeconomic disadvantage and mental health. Background

Mental health plays an essential role in all aspects of an individual’s life. Experiencing poor mental health not only diminishes the ability to cope with daily challenges but can lead to disability and premature mortality [1]. At a societal level, it presents a substantial economic burden [1, 2]. Millions globally experience poor mental health and little headway has been made to improve mental health outcomes [1, 3].

The public health challenge posed by poor mental health is further exacerbated by inequalities across socially and economically disadvantaged populations [1, 4]. Numerous studies have indicated that the socioeconomic characteristics of disadvantaged neighbourhoods can increase vulnerability to stress-inducing factors, elevating the risk of experiencing poor mental health [4, 5].

Additionally, the various environmental attributes of the neighbourhood can play a pivotal role in shaping mental health. The neighbourhood environment encompasses urban infrastructure and built environment attributes (roads, transport, pedestrian paths, retail outlets, services) and the natural environment (greenspace, blue space) as well as exposure to potential by-products of these features such as traffic and pollution [6–10].

Considering the broad range of environmental attributes, it is plausible that some may offer greater mental health benefits for residents of socioeconomically disadvantaged neighbourhoods compared to those in more advantaged neighbourhoods. The relationship between these two constructs, socioeconomic disadvantage and the neighbourhood environment, is complex [5, 11]. Residing in a lower socioeconomic neighbourhood does not necessarily imply poor quality environmental attributes. For example, several studies have found that socioeconomically disadvantaged neighbourhoods are more walkable [12–16]. Walkability can improve mental health through physical activity [17, 18] and the development of social connections [19, 20] which may modify the association between neighbourhood socioeconomic disadvantage and mental health [21, 22].

Nonetheless, there is evidence to suggest that socioeconomically disadvantaged neighbourhoods may be exposed to environments that adversely affect mental health or lack the necessary resources to promote good mental health. Disadvantaged neighbourhoods have been shown to have less greenspace [23, 24], an attribute that has the potential to reduce inequalities in mental health [25–27]. While those from more advantaged neighbourhoods may have access to a range of health-related resources or a wider array of recreational choices, those who are more disadvantaged may face limitations [25]. Neighbourhood greenspace can provide a health promoting resource that is accessible to all, regardless of socioeconomic position [25, 27]. Additionally, greenspace and tree density can improve air quality and provide a physical barrier to reduce noise pollution [28–30]. Residents of disadvantaged neighbourhoods are often disproportionately exposed to pollutants, due to proximity to major roadways or industrial areas [31, 32]. Considering traffic-related air and noise pollution have been linked to depression, anxiety, and psychological distress [7, 33–39], greenspace may play a pivotal role in moderating these harmful effects.

Transport disadvantage may be another pathway to increased risk of poor mental health for those residing in socioeconomically disadvantaged neighbourhoods through greater car dependence and increased exposure to traffic-related pollution [26, 40]. In addition to the mental health effects created by traffic-related pollution, owning and maintaining a vehicle can be a financial burden, especially for those who may already be experiencing financial stress [41, 42]. Financial stress can greatly contribute to psychological distress and depression, often more so than other socioeconomic factors like unemployment [43, 44]. Therefore, neighbourhoods with reliable and accessible public transport or greater walkability offer alternatives to private vehicle use, which may be particularly advantageous to residents of socioeconomically disadvantaged neighbourhoods [15, 26, 45].

Numerous studies have found a greater concentration of ‘environmental bads’ such as takeaway liquor, tobacco and gambling outlets in socioeconomically disadvantaged neighbourhoods [46–50]. The accessibility of these outlets in disadvantaged neighbourhoods may have substantially greater harmful effects on the mental health of those residents [51]. Alcohol consumption, tobacco smoking and problem gambling have been consistently linked with poor mental health [1, 52–55]. It is suggested that these behaviours may function as coping mechanisms for stressors experienced in disadvantaged neighbourhoods [56–58]. A greater availability of these outlets may reinforce these behaviours, leading to further inequalities in mental health.

This existing research illustrates the diversity of environmental attributes and the potential for them to moderate the association between neighbourhood socioeconomic disadvantage and mental health. Therefore, a synthesis of these studies is needed. Understanding the interplay between neighbourhood socioeconomic conditions and environmental attributes has the potential to inform urban planning policies aimed at reducing mental health inequalities. Existing systematic reviews examine a range of environmental attributes, such as greenspace, walkability, and transport, but often use differing methods to measure these attributes. Some systematic reviews focused on studies using only objective measures of environmental attributes such as Geographic Information Systems (GIS) [6, 7, 38] while others include both objective measures and participant perceptions [39, 59–62]. Self-report measures for both mental health and the environmental attributes may provide less consistent findings. Those experiencing poor mental health may be more inclined to report negative perceptions of the neighbourhood [56]. This can create a challenge when comparing results across studies and formulating a conclusion [7, 56]. Reviewing studies only using objectively measured environmental attributes may yield more meaningful results. Additionally, there is a knowledge gap in previous reviews, as they have not examined the relationship between mental health and the physical environment with respect to neighbourhood socioeconomic disadvantage [7, 9, 38, 39, 63]. A systematic review by Schüle & Bolte [6] investigated the interactive effects of neighbourhood and individual socioeconomic factors and the built environment on various health outcomes. The authors highlighted the lack of studies specifically addressing mental health outcomes [6]. Notably, the authors adopted a different strategy to our proposed strategy as they did not restrict their search exclusively to disadvantaged neighbourhoods. Therefore, a review of evidence is warranted to identify environmental attributes that have the potential to promote positive mental wellbeing and reduce inequalities in mental health across neighbourhoods.

Objective

This systematic review aims to identify objectively measured environmental attributes that may moderate (i.e. alter the strength or direction of) associations between neighbourhood socioeconomic disadvantage and mental health outcomes. The review synthesises evidence from observational exposure studies rather than evaluating intervention effectiveness.

Methods

Design and registration

This systematic review protocol will follow the reporting recommendations in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols (PRISMA-P) statement (Additional file 1) [64]. The protocol is registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database (registration ID: CRD42024519354).

Information sources and search strategy

The searches for relevant peer-reviewed literature will be performed using the following electronic bibliographic databases: PubMed (via National Library of Medicine), Scopus (via Elsevier), Web of Science (via Clarivate), PsycINFO (via EBSCOhost), CINAHL Complete (via EBSCOhost), GreenFILE (via EBSCOhost) and ProQuest (via ProQuest). These databases were selected to ensure coverage of multidisciplinary research and because they have been searched in similar reviews [6, 7, 60, 74].

Relevant records will be identified by searching the electronic databases using the search terms provided (see Additional file 2). The search terms will be organised into five categories: neighbourhood/area, socioeconomic disadvantage, mental health, environmental attributes and moderation terms based on the search strategy used in other applicable systematic reviews [7, 75]. The search terms will be modified according to the indexing systems of the databases and not limited to MeSH terms due to the limited applicable terms. The five categories will be combined using Boolean operator (AND) and searched in titles and abstracts. A fully developed search for PubMed is provided in Additional file 3.

To reduce the risk of publication bias, as recommended by Gebel et al. [76], a comprehensive grey literature search will be undertaken using OpenGrey and Overton. The search will target sources such as conference papers, dissertations, and government reports to identify non-peer-reviewed publications meeting the inclusion criteria. The search terms will be adapted to suit the specific requirements of grey literature databases [77].

Finally, a hand search will scan the reference lists of relevant records to ensure we have completed a comprehensive and exhaustive search of all available literature.

Data management and selection

Records will be imported into Covidence systematic review software [78] where duplicates will be removed. Firstly, two screeners will independently screen all records based on title and abstract to identify potential for inclusion. Secondly, two screeners will independently screen the full text of eligible records. During both stages of screening, any discrepancies will be resolved by a third screener.

Data extraction

Two reviewers will extract data from the final list of eligible reports. Information extracted will include report details (author, year); study design; location; timeframe of study/data collection; sample details (size and demographics); definition and measure of neighbourhood socioeconomic disadvantage (including methods); measure of mental health (including methods); type and measure of environmental moderators; methodology (moderation analysis); confounders; and the main findings (statistical significance and effect size, moderation) (see Additional file 4).

Data synthesis

The results of all eligible reports will then be analysed and synthesised to provide evidence to address our research aim. This will include all extracted data as outlined above. The environmental moderators will be categorised following the Neighbourhood Environment Walkability Scale (NEWS) and adapted from the method used by Cerin et al. [45]. Categories will include (a) walkability, determined by incorporating information on access to services/land use mix, residential density and/or street connectivity (b) residential density/urbanisation; (c) street connectivity; (d) access to and availability/diversity of services, land use mix, shops/commercial destinations, food outlets, health care services, public transport, greenspace/blue space/open space/recreational, and other destinations; (e) streetscape and pedestrian and cycling infrastructure such as benches/sitting facilities and streetlights, (f) aesthetics and cleanliness/order such as littering/vandalism/decay, pollution (air/noise/light/odour); and (g) safety and traffic including traffic/pedestrian safety, traffic volume, and crime/personal safety.

A meta-analysis to synthesise findings and quantify the strength of evidence will be considered if there are at least five articles examining the same environmental attribute to meet the methodological standards for meta-analysis [79, 80]. The meta-analysis will be conducted using Stata software.

Due to the expected heterogeneity in the environmental moderators (e.g. greenspace, transport, walkability), a weighted z-test meta-analysis will combine the results from multiple studies. This method standardises study findings by assigning z-values [81]. This approach is commonly used in systematic reviews of the built environment, rather than estimations of pooled effect sizes and their 95% confidence intervals [45, 60, 82–84]. Positive associations will be assigned a z-value of 1.96 (p = 0.05), negative associations will be assigned a z-value of − 1.96, and non-significant findings will be assigned a z-value of 0. Study weights will incorporate both sample size and quality assessment scores to provide greater weight to larger and methodologically stronger studies. Weighted z-values, associated p-values and the number and methodological quality of contributing studies will be reported to provide transparent interpretation of findings. This approach enables evidence to be synthesised while also accounting for differences in study quality. Furthermore, it provides a more robust assessment of evidence than simple counting of statistically significant findings. The strength of evidence will be determined using two-tailed p-values: p < 0.05 for weak evidence, p < 0.01 for strong evidence, and p < 0.001 for very strong evidence [85].

Risk of bias within individual studies (methodological quality assessment)

The quality assessment will be conducted by two reviewers working independently using the criteria developed by Cerin et al. [45] and adapted in line with the methods used in comparable reviews on the built environment [75, 83, 86]. Any conflicts will be resolved by a third reviewer. Score allocations are summarised in additional file (see Additional file 5). A score of 1 will be assigned to each of the following criteria if the criteria is met, or 0 if the criteria is not met (1) sample representativeness, (2) study design (longitudinal = 1, cross-sectional = 0), (3) exposure variability, (4) moderator variability, (5) exposure measure (measure of neighbourhood disadvantage), (6) outcome measure (measure of mental health), (7) moderator measure (objective measure of environmental attribute), (8) adjustment for individual socio-demographic covariates, (9) adjustment for residential self-selection, and (10) did not inappropriately categorise continuous environmental exposure. For the following three criteria, a score of 1/3 will be assigned if the criteria are met, or 0 if the criteria are not met (11) accounting for area-level clustering (if appropriate), (12) accounting for distributional assumptions, (13) analyses conducted and presented correctly. Finally, a score ranging from 0.25 to 1.75 will be assigned for the sample size. Articles will not be excluded solely based on quality assessment scores. Instead, quality assessment scores will be reported to provide transparency and context for interpreting the strength and consistency of findings across studies. Findings from studies assessed as having lower methodological quality will be interpreted with greater caution where methodological limitations may affect the validity of reported associations or moderation effects. If a meta-analysis is conducted, quality assessment scores will contribute to the weighted z-values.

Discussion

Inequalities in mental health persist across levels of neighbourhood socioeconomic disadvantage. With increasing evidence indicating a link between environmental attributes and mental health, as well as the significant contribution of mental health conditions to the global burden of disease, the need for further research into this association is essential to reduce inequalities and improve population health. This proposed systematic review seeks to identify environmental moderators of the association between neighbourhood socioeconomic disadvantage and mental health. By understanding how environmental attributes influence mental health, this review may provide valuable insights to advance mental health research and inform urban planning policy. The findings are expected to emphasise the importance of interdisciplinary approaches that integrate public health and urban planning.

Identifying environmental attributes, such as walkability and access to greenspace, that may mitigate the risk of poor mental health has significant potential to reduce inequities. Findings from this review can guide urban planning by highlighting opportunities for environmental interventions that promote positive mental health. Disadvantaged neighbourhoods, in particular, may benefit from tailored design and planning considerations as well as the development of mental health programmes to effectively address and narrow inequalities. Additionally, the review may uncover gaps in knowledge for future research and provide a basis for evaluating the long-term impacts of interventions to strengthen evidence-based policy recommendations.
