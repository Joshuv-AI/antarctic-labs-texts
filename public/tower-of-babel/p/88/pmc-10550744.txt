Computed Tomography and Magnetic Resonance Imaging Findings of Bicuspid Aortic Valve and Related Abnormalities of the Heart and Thoracic Aorta
Authors: You Jin You, Sung Min Ko
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) is the most common congenital cardiovascular malformation. Patients with BAV are at higher risk of other congenital cardiovascular malformations and valvular dysfunction, including aortic stenosis/regurgitation and infective endocarditis. BAV may also be related to aortic wall abnormalities such as aortic dilatation, aneurysm, and dissection. The morphology of the BAV varies with the presence and position of the raphe and is associated with the type of valvular dysfunction and aortopathy. Therefore, accurate diagnosis and effective treatment at an early stage are essential to prevent complications in patients with BAV. This pictorial essay highlights the characteristics of BAV and its related congenital cardiovascular malformations, valvular dysfunction, aortopathy, and other rare cardiac complications using multimodal imaging.

INTRODUCTION

The bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) is the most common congenital cardiovascular malformation (CCM), affecting 0.5%–2% of the general population [1]. Although BAV can present as an isolated disorder, it is often associated with other CCMs, valvular dysfunction, and aortic wall abnormalities. The BAV can vary anatomically in terms of the number and orientation of the commissures, cusps, and raphes. Accurate assessment of BAV morphology is clinically important for selecting the appropriate treatment and minimizing complications because of the association between the BAV phenotype and the presence of valvulopathy and aortopathy [2].

Transthoracic or transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is the primary diagnostic modality for BAV. However, echocardiography findings often cannot be used to differentiate BAV from tricuspid aortic valves (TAVs) because of severely calcified leaflets, diminutive raphe, or suboptimal operator skill. Cardiac computed tomography (CCT) has a high diagnostic performance for the diagnosis and classification of BAV and allows for simultaneous assessment of BAV-related CCMs, aortopathy, valvulopathy, and coronary artery disease (CAD). Cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) allows for the morphological and functional assessment of the BAV and its related left ventricular (LV) myocardium, such as flow quantification, to determine the severity of aortic stenosis (AS)/aortic regurgitation (AR), myocardial perfusion and viability, and myocardial fibrosis (MF) and strain.

BAV Classification

The typical BAV morphology involves two unequal leaflets with a raphe. The Sievers and Schmidtke classification is widely used, in which the number of raphes is the main characteristic used to determine the BAV type [3]. Subclassification is based on the spatial position of the cusps and the functional status of the valve (Fig. 1).

According to the 2021 international consensus [4], BAV are classified as fused BAV, two-sinus BAV, and partial-fusion BAV (Fig. 2, Supplementary Video 1). A fused BAV is identified when two of the three cusps appeared to have fused within the three aortic sinuses. Right-left (RL) cusp fusion is the most common phenotype (70%–80%), is associated with a significantly higher prevalence of AS, and is strongly related to coarctation of the aorta (COA). The right-non (RN) cusp fusion phenotype (20%–30%) has a higher prevalence of AS and independently predicts AR progression in adults [4]. Assessing the asymmetry of the fused BAV is important for planning BAV repair in AR. Asymmetry is classified into symmetrical and asymmetrical/very asymmetrical angles (Figs. 2, 3). Partial-fusion BAV resembles a typical TAV with three symmetrical cusps but forms a small raphe with < 50% cusp fusion. Two-sinus BAV is characterized by two cusps of nearly identical size and shape and is formed only within two aortic sinuses with commissural angles of 180°. International consensus has overcome the limitation that type 0 does not differentiate between two-sinus BAV and fused BAV with no raphe. The partial fusion type is now classified separately; furthermore, unicuspid AV is not included. Raphes were present in nearly 90% of patients with BAV in Western studies [5] and in only 74.5% of Asian patients [6]. Patients with BAV without raphe had more frequent AS and mid-ascending aorta dilatation [7].

BAV-related Congenital Cardiovascular Malformations

An increased prevalence of a left-dominant coronary system, separate left coronary ostia, high take-off coronary arteries, and short left main artery length (< 5 mm) has been found in patients with BAV (Fig. 4) [8]. Patients with BAV are three times more likely to have an anomalous coronary artery originating from the opposite sinus than those with TAV (Fig. 5) [9].

BAV often coexists with other left-sided CCMs, such as the COA and Shone complex [10]. Approximately 85% of COAs are associated with BAV [11]. Patients with both COA and BAV reportedly experience severe complications, including AS, AR, and aortic aneurysm (Fig. 6) and have a higher risk of acute aortic syndrome. Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) and ventricular septal defects (VSD) are common CCMs in patients with BAV (Fig. 7).

CCT and CMR play important roles in the diagnosis of concomitant diseases by accurately describing the cardiovascular anatomy of the CCMs. CMR can also be used for quantitative evaluation of cardiac function. Four-dimensional flow CMR can be an alternative to invasive catheterization of the COA, as it provides information about blood flow velocity and is used to visualize blood flow in the VSD and PDA. Additionally, it can aid in the detection of shunt fractions in VSD [12].

BAV-related Valvulopathy

Aortic Stenosis

AS is the most common valvular complication of BAV [4]. AS progresses at a younger age in patients with BAV than in those with TAV. The BAV phenotype has been suggested as a predictor of the AS rate or degree of AS [13].

The AV orifice area and calcium score (AVCS) are calculated using the CCT (Fig. 8). When severe AS is suspected and the grading on echocardiography is inconclusive, AVCS can complement AS severity assessment. The sex-specific thresholds for severe AS are > 1300 AU for women and 2000 AU for men [14]. Severe AV calcification may increase the risk of systemic calcification embolization such as calcium deposits in the coronary arteries (Fig. 9), cerebral arteries, and peripheral circulation. Severe AV calcification is a contraindication to transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) because of the risk of annular rupture, inadequate prosthetic anchorage, and calcific embolism [15]. CCT plays an essential role in planning surgical/transcatheter aortic valve replacement (AVR) as it allows for the measurement of the dimensions of the virtual basal ring of the aortic annulus and helps delineate the vascular access routes [16]. CCT can be used as an alternative to invasive angiography before AVR is performed because of the high prevalence of CAD in patients with severe AS.

Patients with AS exhibit concentric remodeling or concentric LV hypertrophy (LVH). LVH is usually symmetrical; however, asymmetric mid-basal septal LVH can occur, resembling hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [17]. CMR and CCT provide more accurate and reproducible assessments of LV mass (LVM) than echocardiography (Supplementary Fig. 1) [18]. An inappropriately high LVM is common in patients with asymptomatic severe AS and is an independent factor for poor prognosis. CMR can be used to delineate turbulent blood flow through the stenotic AV, quantify blood flow and peak velocity, and assess preoperative MF and strain [18]. Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) and T1 mapping allow the detection of focal or diffuse MF (Fig. 10) [18]. In severe AS, LGE patterns primarily affect the mid-basal interventricular septum, exhibiting a combination of mid-wall and junctional patterns [19]. Myocardial strain imaging is correlated with the extent of MF and enables the quantification of LV function [18].

The severity of MF is associated with poor long-term prognosis. LV dysfunction and heart failure are likely to develop after AVR. Therefore, LGE, T1 mapping, and myocardial strain can help optimize AVR timing by identifying high-risk patients through early screening for MF [18].

Patients with severe AS without CAD exhibit an impaired myocardial perfusion reserve on adenosine-stress CMR (Fig. 10). Low myocardial perfusion reserve predicts symptomatic development in asymptomatic individuals.

Aortic Regurgitation

AR is more common in young patients with BAV than in those with AS [1]. AR is usually caused by prolapsing cusps, valvular destruction secondary to infective endocarditis (IE), or post-valve surgery in children, while adult patients may functionally develop AR when the ascending aorta is dilated [20]. BAV and degenerative AR are the most common causes of primary AR [21]. Moderate or severe AS/AR is a clinically important and independent predictor of cardiac adverse events.

CCT and CMR can be used to evaluate the AR mechanism and measure the direct plane regurgitant aortic orifice. Velocity-encoded CMR can evaluate the severity of AR as determined by regurgitant flow measurements (Fig. 11) [18]. In the presence of AR only, cine CMR can quantify AR by comparing the difference between the left and right ventricular stroke volumes [18].

Infective Endocarditis

Patients with BAV have an approximately 30-fold higher incidence of IE than the general population [22]. Endocarditis can cause AR, heart failure, or structural destruction such as valve perforation, abscess, or fistula formation. Endocarditis accounts for up to 60% of the BAV cases with severe AR, most commonly due to cusp perforation. CCT is superior in detecting periannular complications, whereas TEE is more suitable for detecting vegetation (Fig. 12). A vegetation size of > 10 mm is an indication for surgical AVR owing to a higher incidence of embolism [23].

Valve Thrombus

Native valvular thrombosis is uncommon, particularly in the absence of cardiac catheterization, infection, or a hypercoagulable state. When these conditions are not present, the underlying AV condition must be considered because the restricted valvular orifice of the BAV or calcified stenotic AV may promote thrombus formation (Fig. 13) [24]. Vegetation can be distinguished based on the clinical signs of infection and ancillary findings, such as the presence of a perivalvular abscess. However, differentiating a valve thrombus from a valve tumor such as a papillary fibroelastoma or myxoma is difficult.

BAV-related Aortic Wall Abnormalities

Aortopathy is a common abnormality associated with BAV [4]. Genetic components and altered hemodynamic flow due to valve malformation are the two main causes of BAV aortopathy. Aortopathy can lead to aortic aneurysm or dissection (AD)/rupture.

BAV-associated aortic dilatation can be classified into three types [25]. Type 1, the most common type, involves the dilatation of the aortic root, tubular ascending aorta, and proximal arch. This type is associated with AS and RL fusion patterns (Fig. 14). Type 2 involves dilatation of the tubular ascending aorta, which may extend to the arch and spare the aortic root. This type is related to RN-cusp fusion. Type 3 is isolated aortic root dilatation. This rare type is associated with AR, younger age at diagnosis, and a higher prevalence in men than in women. Four-dimensional flow CMR demonstrates a correlation between the aortic dilation type and BAV phenotype, highlighting the influence of altered hemodynamics on the development of aortopathy (Supplementary Video 2) [18].

The most serious complications of BAV were intramural hematoma (Fig. 15) and AD (Fig. 16). The phenotype involving the aortic root demonstrated a higher incidence of aortic aneurysms and acute AD [26]. The risk of acute AD increases with aortic diameter.

An aorta ≥ 4.0 cm in patients with BAV is considered dilated and periodic imaging with TEE, CMR, or CCT should be performed for timely aortic intervention [27]. CCT or CMR imaging is recommended at least annually when the aortic diameter reaches 4.5 cm [27]. Ascending aortic replacement is considered appropriate when the aortic diameter is ≥ 5.5 cm and is recommended when the aortic dimension is 5.0–5.4 cm for patients with AD risk factors and ≥ 4.5 cm in those with AVR indications [27].

Treatments for Valvular Dysfunction

AVR is required for severe AS or AR with symptoms, or LV dysfunction [28]. Surgical AVR is the primary curative treatment for BAV. TAVR in patients with BAV has improved dramatically with the use of newer-generation valves (Fig. 17). Severely calcified BAV increase the rate of paravalvular leak, and an asymmetric annulus may lead to malposition, malfunction, and thrombosis of the transcatheter heart valve [29]. Aortic annular sizing using CCT increases procedural success. TAVR may be a good alternative to surgery in high-risk patients if careful imaging evaluation confirms favorable AV anatomy and the absence of significant aortic dilatation requiring aortic surgery.

BAV repair in patients with severe AR avoids prosthesis-related complications and long-term anticoagulant use. However, the durability of BAV repair is limited by degenerative calcification and fibrosis of the valve cusps [30].

CONCLUSION

BAV is a common CCM with various morphological features and causes many diseases. Multimodal imaging approaches are important for the comprehensive evaluation of the morphology and function of BAV. CCT is especially advantageous for evaluating the anatomical morphologies of the valve, coronary artery, and aorta. CMR is useful for quantifying the valve function and MF. Radiologists should have a good understanding of the diverse characteristics, imaging findings, and treatment options of BAV and its associated abnormalities.
