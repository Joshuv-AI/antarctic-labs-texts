Frailty-stratified effective dose of nalbuphine for postoperative analgesia in elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery: a modified Dixon-based dose-finding protocol
Authors: Weiwei Wang, Chuansong Wei, Songsong Chen, Guanghan Wu, Lingling Xu, Xiaojun Gao, Yuehan Du, Mengjie Liu, Min Zhang, Yongtao Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubstantial interindividual variability exists in postoperative analgesic requirements among frail elderly patients. Nalbuphine has the advantages of effective analgesia and a lower risk of respiratory depression, but its dose‒response relationship in patients with varying degrees of frailty remains unclear. This study will group patients based on the preoperative Modified Frailty Index-11 (mFI-11) and utilize the modified Dixon sequential method to explore, for the first time, the median effective dose (ED50) of a nalbuphine and dexmedetomidine combination administered via patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) in elderly individuals receiving laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery.MethodsThis will be a prospective, double-blind, interventional, dose-exploration study. The study participants will be selected from elderly patients (aged 65 years and above) undergoing elective laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery. Based on their preoperative mFI-11 scores, patients will be assigned to the healthy (mFI = 0), pre-frailty (0 < mFI < 0.27), or frailty (mFI ≥ 0.27) groups. Postoperatively, all patients will receive a combination of nalbuphine and dexmedetomidine via PCIA. Dose titration will be conducted using a modified Dixon’s up-and-down sequential method. All groups will receive a uniform initial dose of 1 mg/kg nalbuphine, with subsequent doses subject to ± 0.1 mg/kg adjustments. Titration for each group will be terminated once seven sequential crossover events are recorded, defined as the point where analgesia transitions from effective to ineffective. Probit regression analysis will be used to estimate the ED₅₀ for each group. The primary endpoint is the ED50 of nalbuphine required to achieve satisfactory analgesia (Numeric Rating Scale at rest [NRS-R] score ≤ 3) within the first 24 postoperative hours. Secondary endpoints include the 95% effective dose (ED₉₅) of nalbuphine, sedation scores, the incidence of adverse events (including respiratory depression, nausea, vomiting, pruritus, and dizziness), hemodynamic parameters (pulse oxygen saturation [SpO₂], systolic blood pressure [SBP], mean arterial pressure [MAP], diastolic blood pressure [DBP], and heart rate [HR]), and and postoperative recovery indicators (time to ambulation, time to oral diet tolerance, time to flatus, and time to urinary catheter removal).DiscussionA novel contribution of this work is the systematic investigation into how the dose-response relationship of nalbuphine varies with preoperative frailty levels, with the aim of determining effective postoperative PCIA strategies and more precise nalbuphine analgesic dosage guidance for patients undergoing laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery, particularly frail elderly patients. Background

With the acceleration of population ageing in China, the proportion of elderly patients in the surgical population continues to increase, and frail patients account for as many as 25–50% [1, 2]. Frailty is an age-related clinical syndrome defined as a multisystem impairment in physiological reserve. This condition heightens vulnerability to perioperative stressors like surgical trauma, resulting in increased rates of adverse postoperative outcomes and long-term mortality [3–6]. Postoperative pain, as a key source of stress, is more likely to induce cardiovascular events, respiratory dysfunction, and the development of chronic pain in frail patients, thereby delaying the recovery process and increasing the medical burden [7, 8].

In accordance with the enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) concept, regional nerve block combined with patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) is considered the preferred strategy for postoperative pain management [9]. The transversus abdominis plane block (TAPB) is clinically recognized for its simplicity and safety, as it effectively alleviates somatic pain at the surgical incision site; however, its analgesic effect on visceral pain is limited [10]. Currently, sufentanil—a commonly used medication for PCIA—primarily exerts its analgesic effects through µ-opioid receptor agonism. While it demonstrates notable efficacy in terms of controlling somatic pain, its effectiveness against visceral pain remains suboptimal. Furthermore, it is linked to adverse effects including respiratory depression, nausea, and vomiting [11, 12]. Nalbuphine is a dual-activity opioid, exhibiting agonist and antagonist activity at the κ- and µ-opioid receptors, respectively. It offers analgesic potency comparable to morphine while significantly mitigating the risk of respiratory depression and other associated side effects [13, 14]. Studies have shown that compared with sufentanil, nalbuphine has superior postoperative analgesic effects in gynaecological laparoscopic surgery, caesarean sections, and gastrointestinal surgeries, particularly in the management of visceral pain, where it offers advantages over single µ-receptor agonists. However, the precise dosage required to achieve effective analgesia in laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgeries remains to be determined [14, 15].

Current postoperative analgesia strategies are largely derived from studies on relatively healthy or young populations, often failing to adequately consider the specific needs of frail elderly patients [16, 17]. Frailty is often accompanied by changes in pain perception thresholds and significant pharmacokinetic differences (such as decreased hepatic and renal clearance and changes in body composition) [18–20], leading to considerable individual variability in response to traditional analgesic regimens [21, 22]. This can result in inadequate analgesia or an increased risk of drug accumulation and toxicity. Therefore, when analgesic regimens are formulated for frail patients, it is necessary to delicately balance efficacy and safety [17, 23–25]. The conventional “low-starting-dose, slow-titration” approach may result in either inadequate analgesia or drug overdose in this patient population. Crucially, the dose‒response relationship of nalbuphine in frail elderly patients remains poorly characterized to date, leaving clinical practice without quantitative guidance [16, 26]. The modified Dixon sequential method is an efficient dose-finding design used to accurately estimate the median effective dose (ED50) and the 95% effective dose (ED95) of a drug with a relatively small sample size. It is successfully applied in anesthetic and analgesic drug research [27]. Therefore, this study will employ the modified Dixon sequential method to, for the first time, ​​characterize the analgesic effectiveness​​ of nalbuphine in frail individuals, aiming to determine the dose‒response relationship and optimal dosage window of nalbuphine in patients with varying degrees of frailty who are receiving postoperative PCIA after laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery.

Methods and analysis

The First Affiliated Hospital of Shandong First Medical University in China will undertake this prospective, double-blind, dose-exploration study. The study protocol, designed in accordance with the SPIRIT guidelines [28] and the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, has received approval (YXLL-KY-2022(016)) from the hospital’s Ethics Committee as of March 31, 2022. Participants

The study population will consist of elderly patients (≥ 65 years) scheduled for elective laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery under general anaesthesia. This primarily includes laparoscopic gastric cancer resections (radical gastrectomy) and laparoscopic colorectal resections (right hemicolectomy, left hemicolectomy, sigmoidectomy, and anterior resection). More complex upper gastrointestinal and hepatopancreatobiliary procedures (esophagectomy, hepatectomy, pancreaticoduodenectomy) are explicitly excluded (see Exclusion Criteria). Patients will be enrolled sequentially and divided into three distinct groups based on their preoperative Modified Frailty Index-11 (mFI − 11) score: healthy group (mFI = 0), pre-frailty group (0 < mFI < 0.27), and frailty group (mFI ≥ 0.27). The recruitment period is anticipated to extend from October 2025 to December 2026.

Study implementation

To guarantee data quality and consistency, all study personnel must demonstrate competency in carrying out the designated assessments, namely the mFI-11 and NRS, through mandatory pre-study training. Only those who pass the assessment will be allowed to act as investigators in the study.

Preoperative frailty assessment

Preoperative frailty in elderly patients scheduled for laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery will be evaluated using the mFI-11. This instrument is well-established in research associated with the National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) for surgical risk stratification [1]. A recognized limitation of the mFI-11 is its predominant reliance on comorbidities and physical function parameters, with comparatively less emphasis on psychological or social dimensions of frailty. The mFI score will be computed as the proportion of positive factors present out of the total number of applicable factors with complete data. Clinically relevant positive factors will include non-independent functional status, specific comorbidities, and impaired nutritional/metabolic parameters. Specifically, “non-independent functional status” will be defined as a moderate-to-severe functional impairment corresponding to a Barthel Index (BI) score of ≤ 60. Based on their mFI scores, patients will be categorized into one of three groups: healthy (mFI = 0), pre-frailty (0 < mFI < 0.27), and frailty (mFI ≥ 0.27). This stratification threshold has been specifically calibrated for abdominal surgery populations and is distinct from the cut-off values used in non-cardiac surgery settings.

Evaluation of postoperative pain

Postoperative pain monitoring will utilize the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS, 0–10 points). Pain severity will be categorized as: no pain (0), mild tolerable pain (1–3, representing analgesic success), moderate sleep-disturbing pain (4–6), and severe pain causing significant nutritional and functional impairment (7–10). Two distinct types of pain—resting pain and pain during movement—will be documented. Following the cessation of the PCIA pump 48 h post-surgery, data on cumulative sufentanil consumption, administration of rescue analgesics, and incidence of nausea and vomiting will be recorded. Additionally, during hospitalization, if the NRS score remains ≥ 4 after three consecutive PCA doses, 50 mg of intravenous flurbiprofen axetil will be administered as rescue analgesia. A persistent NRS score of ≥ 7 for more than 24 h will be defined as an adverse event and documented in the CRF.

Randomization and blinding

This is a dose-finding study involving a single intervention (PCIA with a nalbuphinedexmedetomidine combination). Consequently, no randomization is performed for group assignment; patients are allocated to the healthy, prefrail, or frail group based solely on their preoperative mFI-11 score.

The nalbuphine dose for each patient is not randomized but is strictly determined by the response of the previous patient in the same frailty stratum according to the pre-specified modified Dixon up-and-down sequential method. This rule-based approach minimizes investigator bias in dose selection.

A triple-blind design will be implemented and maintained throughout the study. The blinding pertains specifically to the nalbuphine dose being administered. The patients, the anesthesiologists responsible for postoperative pain assessment and management, and the researchers collecting outcome data will all be unaware of the patient’s assigned dose. To ensure successful blinding, an independent healthcare professional who is not involved in patient care or data collection will prepare all analgesic pumps according to the dose determined by the sequential allocation schedule. To prevent unblinding, data collectors will be assigned to patients without access to the dose allocation log.

Anaesthesia protocol

All patients will comply with standard preoperative fasting protocols, which involve an 8-hour fast for solid foods and a 2-hour fast for clear liquids. Additionally, no premedication will be given. Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring will be established through electrocardiography (ECG), non-invasive blood pressure measurement, pulse oximetry (SpO₂), and bispectral index (BIS). After 10 and 15 min of rest, non-invasive blood pressure and heart rate (HR) will be recorded, with their average defining the baseline. Following this, anaesthesia induction will be performed using intravenous injections of midazolam (0.05 mg/kg), etomidate (0.2–0.3 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.3–0.5 µg/kg), and rocuronium (0.6–1.0 mg/kg). When the bispectral index (BIS) reaches 40–60, an endotracheal tube or laryngeal mask will be inserted, and mechanical ventilation will be initiated. Mechanical ventilation parameters are set as follows: a tidal volume of 6–8 mL/kg, a respiratory rate of 10–14 breaths/minute, an inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio (I: E) of 1:1.5–2, with airway pressure kept under 30 mmHg and end-tidal carbon dioxide (PETCO₂) maintained between 35 and 45 cmH₂O. Preoperative analgesia will be achieved via an ultrasound-guided bilateral transversus abdominis plane block (TAPB), injecting 20 mL of 0.5% ropivacaine hydrochloride per side [32, 33]. Maintenance of anesthetic depth will be supported by a continuous intravenous infusion of ciprofol (0.5-1 mg/kg/h), remifentanil (0.1–0.3 µg/kg/min), and rocuronium (5–6 µg/kg/min), keeping the BIS between 40 and 60. Thirty minutes before the end of the surgery, the infusion of rocuronium will be discontinued, and 8 mg of ondansetron and 0.15 µg/kg of sufentanil will be administered intravenously. If hemodynamic instability occurs, vasoactive drugs should be used as appropriate. At the end of the surgery, all anesthetic infusions will be discontinued. Neuromuscular blockade will be reversed using sugammadex (2–4 mg/kg intravenously based on the depth of blockade as assessed by train-of-four [TOF] monitoring). To ensure procedural consistency, a single, fixed surgical team will be responsible for performing all operations.

Upon arrival in the post-anaesthesia care unit (PACU), all patients will be connected to a PCIA analgesia pump, which will be initiated at that time. Once the patient’s consciousness, muscle strength, and spontaneous breathing satisfactorily recover, the endotracheal tube (or laryngeal mask) will be removed. Depending on the level of pain, an intravenous bolus of sufentanil at 0.1–0.15 µg/kg will be administered, with the goal of maintaining a resting-state Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) score of ≤ 3. Patients will remain under monitoring in the PACU for a minimum of 30 min. Transfer to the general ward will be authorized only after stable vital signs and the absence of complications are confirmed. Patients and their families will be instructed and encouraged to correctly use the analgesia pump, with the aim of maintaining the NRS score ≤ 3 as much as possible.

Postoperative analgesia protocol

The analgesic pump will be configured according to the modified Dixon sequential method, and the trial will be conducted simultaneously in three surgical groups [34, 35]. The initial PCIA regimen will comprise 1 mg/kg nalbuphine, 2 µg/kg dexmedetomidine, and 16 mg ondansetron in 100 mL of normal saline; the pump settings will include a 2 mL/h background infusion, a 0.5 mL bolus dose, and a 15-minute lockout period.

Dose determination and individual patient management

The modified Dixon up-and-down method will be applied separately within three frailty strata: healthy (mFI = 0), pre-frailty (0 < mFI < 0.27), and frailty (mFI ≥ 0.27). Each stratum will operate as an independent dose-finding cohort with its own titration sequence and stopping rule.

Patients will receive a fixed dose of nalbuphine via PCIA, determined before initiation based on the analgesic outcome of the previous patient in the same stratum. No dose adjustments will be made during the 48-hour PCIA period.

Pain intensity will be assessed using the NRS at rest and during movement at seven time points: PACU discharge (T1), 6 h (T2), 12 h (T3), 18 h (T4), 24 h (T5), 36 h (T6), and 48 h (T7) postoperatively.

If a patient reports NRS ≥ 4 at any assessment despite using PCIA bolus, rescue analgesia (flurbiprofen axetil 50 mg IV) will be given. This will be documented and considered a negative analgesic response for dose adjustment of the subsequent patient.

The starting dose is 1 mg/kg. The dose will be:

Increased by 0.1 mg/kg if the prior patient had analgesic failure (NRS > 3 at any of the four key time points: 6, 12, 18, or 24 h);

Increased by 0.1 mg/kg if the prior patient had analgesic failure (NRS > 3 at any of the four key time points: 6, 12, 18, or 24 h);

Decreased by 0.1 mg/kg if the prior patient had analgesic success (NRS ≤ 3 at all four time points);

Decreased by 0.1 mg/kg if the prior patient had analgesic success (NRS ≤ 3 at all four time points);

Unchanged if the effect was equivocal or rescue was given for reasons unrelated to background analgesia (e.g., procedural pain).

Unchanged if the effect was equivocal or rescue was given for reasons unrelated to background analgesia (e.g., procedural pain).

The first patient in each stratum will serve as the initial case. Enrollment in each group will continue until seven dose–response turning points (transition from effective to ineffective analgesia) are observed. If the prior patient’s response is equivocal, the next patient will receive the same dose.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is the ED₅₀ of nalbuphine for PCIA in patients undergoing laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery. ED₅₀ is defined as the dose predicted to achieve analgesic success in 50% of patients, where analgesic success is a binary patient‑level endpoint defined as the Numeric Rating Scale at rest (NRS-R) score ≤ 3 at all four postoperative assessments: 6, 12, 18, and 24 h after surgery. This binary endpoint serves as the response variable for both the modified Dixon up‑and‑down dose titration and the subsequent probit regression analysis.

Data collection and management

Data will be collected by independent researchers using CRFs. A unique identification number will be issued to each participant, with all data subsequently undergoing dual-entry verification by two separate researchers into the trial database. Logic checks and blinded verification will be applied to key variables. An independent statistician will conduct a safety assessment and sample size review at the midpoint of the trial, and the final database will be analysed after it has been locked. No patient or public involvement was incorporated in any aspect of the research, including conception, participant recruitment, implementation of interventions, reporting of outcomes, or dissemination of findings.

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation is based on the modified Dixon up‑and‑down method, applied independently to each of the three frailty strata. For each stratum, titration continues until seven crossover events (transitions between analgesic success and failure) are observed—a well‑established criterion that yields stable ED₅₀ estimates with acceptable precision [36]. Based on prior dose-finding studies using similar designs [36–38], we anticipate 20 to 40 participants per stratum (total 60–120) to achieve seven crossovers, depending on response variability within each frailty level. Simulation studies with a step size of 0.1 mg/kg and 20–40 participants per group indicate that the 95% confidence interval for the ED₅₀ is expected to have a half‑width of approximately 0.15–0.20 mg/kg, a clinically acceptable precision for guiding dose selection in frail elderly populations. Detailed simulation parameters and results are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Statistical Appendix). Importantly, the seven‑crossover criterion applies within each stratum individually, not to the combined sample; therefore, enrollment may finish at different times across strata, and the total sample size may vary accordingly.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses will be conducted using SPSS (version 25.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.1), the latter for generating dose‑response sequence plots and dose‑response curves. Normality of continuous data will be assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed data will be presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using Student’s t‑test or one‑way ANOVA, as appropriate. Non‑normally distributed data will be expressed as median (interquartile range) and analysed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data will be presented as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test.

Dose‑response analysis

Probit regression will be fitted separately for each frailty stratum (healthy, prefrail, frail) with nalbuphine dose (mg/kg) as the predictor and binary analgesic success (resting NRS ≤ 3 at 6, 12, 18 and 24 h postoperatively) as the outcome. From each model, ED₅₀ and ED₉₅ and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) will be estimated. Although dose assignment follows a sequential up‑down design, the final set of independent observations provides unbiased dose‑response estimates. To account for the sequential structure and obtain robust standard errors, bootstrap resampling (1,000 replicates) that preserves the allocation sequence will be used to calculate the 95% CIs for both ED₅₀ and ED₉₅.

Comparison between strata

Dose‑response curves will be compared across strata using likelihood ratio tests to evaluate differences in lateral shift (reflecting ED₅₀) and slope (reflecting sensitivity variability). Because the modified Dixon design is optimized for ED₅₀ estimation, ED₉₅ estimates may be less precise; therefore, between‑strata comparisons of ED₉₅ will be considered exploratory, and the widths of their CIs will be reported. Formal testing of slope differences will be interpreted cautiously, and any significant findings will be subjected to sensitivity analyses (e.g., logistic regression with dose as a continuous predictor). A two‑sided α = 0.05 will define statistical significance for all primary analyses.

Missing data management plan

All instances of missing data will be documented, including the reasons and extent. Given the sequential dose-finding design of this study, missing data will be handled according to the following plan:

For the primary outcome (NRS scores determining analgesic success/failure at 6, 12, 18, and 24 h postoperatively): Complete-case analysis will be used for dose-finding decisions, as the modified Dixon sequential method requires observed responses to determine the next patient’s dose. If the primary outcome is missing for a given patient, that patient will be replaced to maintain the integrity of the dose-escalation/de-escalation sequence.

For secondary outcomes and covariates in the final analysis: Should the proportion of missing data be less than 10% for any given variable, a complete-case analysis will be performed. If the missing proportion reaches or exceeds 10%, or if there is significant loss to follow-up, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) will be employed to handle missing values.

The imputation models will include all variables relevant to the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption and the subsequent probit regression analysis. Specifically, models will incorporate:

Discussion

The primary objective of this study is to determine how frailty influences the effective dose of nalbuphine required for satisfactory postoperative analgesia in elderly patients following laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery. Given that traditional opioids can easily cause respiratory depression in elderly patients (especially those who are frail) and that inadequate analgesia may lead to chronic pain, this study focuses on the individualized dosage requirements of nalbuphine combined with dexmedetomidine. Although nalbuphine has potential advantages in terms of providing analgesia for elderly individuals, research on the dose‒response relationship for different levels of frailty is lacking [39, 40].

Ageing leads to a decline in neurological function and physiological reserves (such as increased body fat and decreased water content), affecting pharmacokinetics (such as increased volume of distribution and slowed elimination of lipophilic drugs such as fentanyl) [41]. This study innovatively associates the preoperative mFI with PCIA, with the goal of determining the ED50/ED95 of nalbuphine based on different degrees of frailty [42]. Dexmedetomidine, as an important component of multimodal analgesia, offers advantages such as sedation, analgesia, anxiolysis, and a reduced risk of delirium. It can enhance analgesia and reduce opioid consumption [43–45]. However, research on the dose‒effect relationship of nalbuphine combined with dexmedetomidine in frail elderly patients is limited, and this study aims to provide preliminary data in this specific context.

If frailty is proven to be significantly related to the dosage of nalbuphine, this would demonstrate the applicability of the modified Dixon sequential method for dose-finding in this specific population and clinical context. Such a finding would suggest the potential for transitioning postoperative analgesia from empirical dosing toward a more stratified approach in similar settings. Quantifying the impact of frailty on opioid demand in this cohort may help clinicians tailor analgesic regimens for frail elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery, thereby potentially lowering complication risks and aligning with ERAS principles.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as a single-centre trial, the findings may have limited generalizability to other populations or healthcare settings. However, this design allows for rigorous standardization of surgical techniques, anesthetic management, and postoperative assessments. Second, and most pertinent to our primary objective, is the limitation of the frailty assessment tool itself. As noted in the Methods section, the mFI-11 predominantly captures comorbidities and functional impairments, with limited representation of psychological, cognitive, or social dimensions of frailty. This is an important consideration for our study, as cognitive dysfunction, depression, anxiety, and social vulnerability are known to influence pain perception, analgesic requirements, and postoperative recovery independently of physical frailty. The exclusive use of mFI-11 may therefore lead to misclassification of some patients—particularly those with predominant cognitive or psychosocial frailty but preserved functional status. If such misclassification occurs, it would likely be non-differential across groups and could attenuate the observed associations between frailty and nalbuphine dose requirements. Consequently, the true relationship between multidimensional frailty and opioid requirements may be stronger than what we are able to detect using mFI-11 alone. Third, the absence of a preoperative psychological assessment (e.g., anxiety, depression, pain catastrophizing) means we cannot adjust for these potential confounders or effect modifiers in our analysis of the frailty-dose relationship. Fourth, this protocol focuses on short-term postoperative outcomes (48 h) and does not assess long-term recovery, chronic pain, or functional outcomes beyond hospital discharge.

This study is expected to provide preliminary, context-specific evidence on the relationship between frailty and postoperative nalbuphine requirements in elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery. By generating frailty-stratified ED50/ED95 estimates for this specific procedure and analgesic regimen, we aim to offer initial guidance for dose selection in this vulnerable population. These findings may serve as a foundation for future multicentre trials that can validate and extend these observations to broader surgical populations, ultimately contributing to the development of more individualized, evidence-based postoperative pain management strategies for frail elderly patients.
