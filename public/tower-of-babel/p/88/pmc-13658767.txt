The expanding landscape of Deltex: signaling crosstalk and clinical implications
Authors: Vartika Sharma, Pratikshya Sahoo, Mousumi Mutsuddi, Ashim Mukherjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The deltex (DTX) gene family encodes RING-type E3 ubiquitin ligases that are central to the regulation of the Notch signaling pathway, a mechanism governing cell fate, differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis during development and adult tissue homeostasis. In Drosophila melanogaster, the Deltex (Dx) fine-tunes Notch signaling by modulating receptor endocytosis and degradation, acting as either an enhancer or a suppressor depending on the context. Insights from flies have revealed the evolutionary conservation of the Dx-Notch interaction across species, including humans. However, recent studies have expanded this view, identifying Dx as a regulator of multiple signaling pathways beyond Notch, specifically Wingless, Decapentaplegic, and Toll, underscoring its role as a hub for cellular communication. Through its broad regulatory network, Dx influences fundamental processes including cell growth, differentiation, apoptosis, and homeostasis. Dysregulation of Dx expression and function has been implicated in several diseases, ranging from cancer to immunological disorders and congenital abnormalities, positioning it as a promising therapeutic target. Despite these advances, important gaps remain in our understanding of Dx substrates, molecular mechanisms, and their full spectrum of physiological and pathological roles. Future research will be crucial to uncovering the broader biology of DTX family proteins and enabling their clinical application as diagnostic and prognostic markers. This review consolidates current knowledge and emerging insights on Dx, providing a framework to guide future research and therapeutic exploration.

Introduction

From its first description in Drosophila melanogaster over a century ago, the deltex (dx) gene has intrigued geneticists. Thomas Hunt Morgan originally identified dx in 1922 through flies with distinct Delta-like wing phenotypes [1, 2]. Decades later, in the early 1990 s, Dx gained significant attention when Tian Xu and the group demonstrated that Dx interacts with Notch, placing it within the “Notch group” of genes. Notably, mutations in dx were able to suppress the pupal lethality associated with Abruptex (gain-of-function) Notch alleles, firmly establishing Dx as a positive regulator of Notch signaling in Drosophila [1, 2]. Moreover, phenotypic defects resulting from Dx inhibition could be partially rescued by the introduction of an additional copy of Notch, further supporting this functional relationship [1, 3]. Subsequent studies extended its function to endocytosis of Notch receptors, operating through both canonical and non-canonical routes.

What began as a Notch-specific modulator has since expanded into a much broader paradigm. Research in the past decade has established DTX family of RING-type E3 ubiquitin ligases as a versatile regulator of cellular signaling, orchestrating processes as diverse as cell growth, differentiation, apoptosis, and immune function. Their dysregulation is increasingly associated with cancer, immunological disorders, and other diseases, positioning them at the intersection of basic biology and clinical relevance.

Despite this progress, much about Dx remains enigmatic. Key questions persist regarding its substrates, the precise molecular mechanisms by which it acts, and the full spectrum of physiological and pathological functions. In this review, we synthesize current knowledge on the structure and function of DTX family proteins, highlighting their expanding roles beyond Notch, and discuss how unraveling their biology could open new avenues for therapeutic intervention.

Deltex: Biochemical and molecular architecture

Early molecular analysis of the dx locus, aimed at deciphering its genetic interaction with Notch, revealed Dx as a cytoplasmic component of the Notch signaling pathway. Subsequent studies demonstrated that Dx colocalizes with Notch in the cytoplasm and binds directly to the Ankyrin repeats of Notch Intra-Cellular Domain (NICD) [4, 5].

The Drosophila dx gene resides on the X chromosome and comprises four exons and 3 introns. The 82 kDa Dx is a 737 amino acid long basic protein rich in glutamine (11.26%), histidine (5.97%), and serine (11.94%) residues with an estimated isoelectric point (pI) of 9.8 [6]. The murine homolog (MDTXs) contains four additional exons and introns [7]. In humans, the DTX family consists of five members: DTX1, DTX2, DTX3, DTX3L (DTX3-like), and DTX4. Human DTX1, located on chromosome 12 (12q24.13), encodes a 67.4 kDa protein with 620 amino acids and shares 26% identity and 40% similarity to Drosophila Dx. DTX2, on chromosome 7 (7q11.23), encodes a 67.2 kDa protein of 622 amino acids, with ~ 26% identity and 38% similarity to Dx [8]. DTX3, the least conserved member, maps to chromosome 12 (12q13.3) and encodes a 38 kDa, 347 amino acid protein that is predominantly nuclear [9]. DTX3L, located on chromosome 3 (3q21.1), encodes an 83.6 kDa protein of 740 amino acids with 21% identity and 36% similarity to Dx. The most recently identified member, DTX4, is on chromosome 11 (11q12.1) and encodes a 67.4 kDa, 619 amino acid protein with 27% identity and 39% similarity to Drosophila Dx.

The Drosophila Dx is arbitrarily divided into three regions (Domains I, II, and III) separated by OPA repeats (glutamine-rich stretches) [10]. Each domain contains conserved functional motifs that are highly homologous to those of human DTX proteins. The Domain I (N-terminal domain) harbors two WWE motifs that mediate binding to the CDC10/Ankyrin repeats of NICD [11]. Homology is retained in the N-terminus region of human DTX1, DTX2, and DTX4, whereas DTX3 has a truncated sequence in the N-terminus, preventing NICD interaction [12]. Further, the WWE domain recognizes and binds to ADPr (ADP-ribose) and iso ADPr moieties, which are indispensable for the recruitment of DTX E3 ligase to substrates in a PARylation-dependent manner [13–15]. Notably, putative nuclear localization signals (NLSs) has been identified in the N-terminus domain of DTX1 (aa 161–172 and 379–390) [16] and DTX3L (aa 20–26 and 462 − 478) [12]. DTX3L also carries a predicted nuclear export signal (NES) (aa 325–334) [12], though the functional significance of these motifs remains debated [17].

Domain II contains a proline-rich motif (PRM), serving as a putative SH3 binding site that regulates the interaction of Dx with other proteins such as Grb2 (growth factor receptor-binding protein 2) [10]. Functional studies using deletion and point mutations have demonstrated that loss of either the Proline-rich motif or Notch-binding domain can generate dominant-negative forms of Dx in a specific developmental context [18, 19]. Likewise, the human DTX homologs also contain proline-rich region (PRR), except for DTX3L. However, the functional contribution of these domains is yet to be explored.

The Domain III (C-terminal domain) is the most conserved across the phyla as assessed through their amino acid sequence and crystal structure alignment studies [12, 20, 21]. The region harbors both the RING and DTC domains. RING-H2 finger motif is essential for E3 ubiquitin ligase activity. Dx self-associates through this motif, which is essential for Dx function in Notch signaling [19]. Structural studies revealed that DTX-RING adopts a unique fold stabilized by eight conserved cysteine and histidine residues [22]. In contrast, DTX3 and DTX3L contain a RING-HC structure with a single histidine [12]. The DTC domain, on the other hand, is the least explored domain, and recent discoveries have added to its functions notably. The DTC domain contains a NAD+ binding pocket and a PAR binding site, which enable it to bind and modify its substrate in a PAR-dependent manner [14, 20]. Further investigations have shown that the RING and DTC domains are crucial for hybrid ADPr-Ub formation, though the biological significance of this modification is yet to be elucidated [23].

Together, these features underscore both the evolutionary conservation and functional diversification of the DTX family. The overall domain organization of Drosophila Dx and its human homologs is summarized in Fig. 1.

Deltex as a positive regulator of Notch signaling

The Notch pathway is an evolutionarily conserved cell-cell communication system that governs diverse biological processes, including cell fate determination, differentiation, proliferation, apoptosis, and stem cell maintenance, in a context-dependent manner [24–27]. Such functional versatility is possible because Notch signaling is subject to intricate regulation at multiple levels [28].

Among its regulators, Dx has been of particular interest. In 1994, Dx was first reported to physically interact with the CDC10/Ankyrin repeats of the Notch receptor. Yeast-two-hybrid assays and Drosophila S2 cell culture-based studies suggested that this interaction may occur independent of ligand binding, raising the possibility that Dx contributes to vesicular trafficking of Notch [4]. Shortly thereafter, Matsuno and the group provided further evidence that the Dx-Notch interaction is crucial for Dx function [5]. Over-expression studies revealed that both full-length Dx and its N-terminal domain (Domain I) recapitulate phenotypes characteristic of NICD over-expression, affecting eye, wing, and bristle morphology. In contrast, over-expression of Domain II or Domain III failed to elicit such effects. Consistently, dx mutant phenotype could be rescued by overexpression of full-length Dx or Domain I, but not by Domain II or III. Intriguingly, NICD overexpression itself was sufficient to rescue dx mutant phenotypes, reinforcing the model that Dx promotes Notch activity through its N-terminal domain.

Mechanistically, it was proposed that Dx binding to Notch facilitates the release and nuclear translocation of Suppressor of Hairless [Su(H)], which is normally retained in the cytoplasm via its association with Notch ankyrin repeats. Following ligand binding, Su(H) is released and shuttled into the nucleus to mediate transcriptional activation [5]. Complimenting these findings, studies in the late 1990 s showed that Suppressor of Dx [Su(dx)] negatively regulates Notch signaling, highlighting the presence of both negative and positive modulators within the pathway [29, 30].

Genetic and biochemical analyses further demonstrated that this positive role of Dx is conserved across the phyla. In mammals, DTX1, the closest homolog of Drosophila Dx, also interacts with the ankyrin repeats of human Notch1 and Notch2, and loss-of-function mutations result in attenuated Notch signaling [10]. Supporting this evolutionary conservation, misexpression of mouse Dx homologs (MDTXs) perturbs normal neurogenesis, underscoring their requirement for proper Notch signaling during development [21]. Collectively, these studies firmly establish Dx as a positive regulator of Notch signaling, with its function conserved from flies to mammals.

Deltex as a negative regulator of Notch signaling

Although initially characterized as a positive regulator, the developmental role of Dx soon proved to be more complex. The first indication that Dx might also function as a negative regulator of Notch signaling came from studies in mammalian neural cells, which suggested that Dx attenuates Notch activity [31]. Direct experimental evidence followed from the studies of Dx interaction with Kurtz (Krz), the mammalian homolog of non-visual ß-arrestin. Yeast two-hybrid analyses identified a region spanning amino acids 10–251 in Krz that interacts with Dx. This interaction leads to polyubiquitination and degradation of Notch, thereby suppressing Notch signaling [32]. Importantly, this study highlighted the central role of the Dx RING finger domain, whose E3 ubiquitin ligase activity is essential for targeting Notch for proteasomal degradation. These findings represented the first demonstration of a contradictory role for Dx, elevating it from a simple Notch activator to a context-dependent regulator of the pathway.

Subsequent work extended this paradigm to other family members. For example, DTX3 has been shown to interact with NICD through its RING finger motif, facilitating NICD’s ubiquitination and degradation, and thereby suppressing Notch signaling in human bladder cancer cells [33]. Similarly, studies in zebrafish neurodegeneration revealed that dtx2 mutants exhibit enhanced regenerative capacity due to increased activation of Notch-Rbpj signaling, which drives ependymo-radial glial proliferation [34]. Together, these findings underscore the inhibitory potential of Dx proteins under specific developmental and disease contexts.

The role of Suppressor of Deltex [Su(dx)], a member of the Nedd4 family E3 ubiquitin ligase, provides further insight into the negative regulation of Notch. Nedd4 ligases are generally maintained in an autoinhibited state through intramolecular interactions between their WW domain and a linker region [35]. When overexpressed, however, Su(dx) effectively blocks both endogenous activity of Notch and ectopic signaling driven by Dx overexpression [36–38].

Beyond direct ubiquitin-mediated regulation, additional layers of negative control have been revealed through genetic interaction studies. Ramain et al. reported an antagonistic interaction between Dx and Dishevelled (Dsh), a component of the Wingless signaling pathway. In this report, it was postulated that Dsh antagonizes the cytoplasmic activity of Dx to repress the anti-neural Dx-dependent function of Notch [39].

Our own studies further expanded this regulatory landscape. We demonstrated that Drosophila Dx physically interacts with TRAF6 (TNF Receptor-Associated Factor 6), a key adaptor in Eiger-JNK signaling. Co-expression of TRAF6 with Dx down-regulated Notch signaling, consistent with TRAF6 being a direct interactor of Notch [40]. In parallel, Dx was shown to genetically interact with Maheshvara (mahe), a novel RNA-binding protein of the DEAD-box helicase family, leading to suppression of the Notch pathway activity [41]. More recently, we identified a synergistic interaction between Dx and another RNA-binding protein, Hrp48 (Heterogeneous nuclear riboprotein 48), where their combined activity inhibits Notch signaling by inhibiting the transport of Notch receptor from the cell membrane to the cytoplasm [42].

Taken together, these studies establish that Dx is not a unidirectional regulator but rather exerts context-dependent effects on Notch signaling. By engaging distinct partners, including E3 ligases, signaling adaptors, and RNA-binding proteins, Dx can shift from promoting Notch activity to suppressing it, highlighting the pathway’s complexity and its fine-tuned regulation during development and disease.

Role of Deltex in other signaling cascades – Notch beyond!

Our earlier studies provided the first evidence of Dx function outside the Notch pathway. We showed that Dx synergizes with the RNA-binding protein Hrp48 to induce apoptosis through the activation of the JNK pathway [42]. Dx also facilitates the intracellular transport of TNF/Eiger, the ligand of the JNK cascade, from the cell membrane into the cytoplasm, thereby modulating its activity to trigger JNK-dependent cell death [43]. In addition, Dx cooperates with TRAF6 to activate JNK signaling through an Eiger-independent mechanism, implicating the role of Dx in apoptosis and cell migration [44].

Dx has also been involved in innate immune regulation in Drosophila. Specifically, Dx promotes Toll pathway activation by facilitating the nuclear localization of the transcription factors, Dorsal and Dif (Dorsal related immunity factor). Notably, this regulation was independent of JNK signaling [45]. Beyond immune signaling, Dx is well known to regulate Notch through endocytic trafficking. By depleting Notch from the apical cell surface, Dx promotes its accumulation in late endosomal vesicles [36, 46], and disruption of this trafficking step leads to reduced Notch signaling activity [46].

Building on this established role in Notch trafficking, our recent studies have uncovered additional dimensions of Dx-mediated trafficking. We reported that Dx interacts with Decapentaplegic (Dpp) and, together with Rab7, facilitates its gradient formation, thereby regulating Dpp signaling activity [47]. Similarly, Dx contributes to Wingless (Wg) spreading, directly influencing the expression of both short- and long-range Wg target genes. In parallel, Dx was observed to promote the degradation of Armadillo/β-catenin, revealing another layer of Dx-mediated Wg pathway regulation [48]. These findings not only expand the spectrum of Dx function but also suggest potential therapeutic avenues in diseases driven by aberrant Wg signaling.

Beyond Dx, its functional antagonist, Su(dx), also integrates into other signaling contexts. Su(dx) mediates degradation of Pez, the Drosophila homologue of non-receptor type protein tyrosine phosphatase14 (PTPN14), a regulator of the TGF-ß pathway and upstream inhibitor of Yorkie. By ubiquitinating Pez and targeting it for degradation, Su(dx) modulates Yorkie activity and thereby regulates intestinal stem cell proliferation and midgut homeostasis [49].

Most mechanistic insights into Dx and Su(dx) activity have been derived from Drosophila. Nevertheless, these studies provide a robust framework for extending investigations into the mammalian DTX family. Understanding how Dx integrates multiple signaling cascades in flies offers a valuable blueprint for uncovering conserved mechanisms in higher organisms, with important implications for development, immunity, and disease.

Deltex in ubiquitination and endocytosis

Signaling and endocytosis are intricately linked processes. Classically, endocytosis has been viewed as a mechanism to attenuate signaling by reducing the receptor availability, either through sequestration in the endosomal vesicles or degradation in lysosomes. However, mounting evidence indicates that endocytosis can also act as a positive regulator, promoting the initiation and propagation of signaling cascades [50, 51]. Within this context, Dx has emerged as a critical player, linking ubiquitination with endocytic trafficking to fine-tune signaling outcomes. Indirect evidence suggests that alone Dx promotes the mono-ubiquitinated state of NICD [46, 52], whereas in cooperation with Kurtz, Dx facilitates poly-ubiquitination and degradation of Notch [32].

Notch itself undergoes constant endocytosis. Following internalization into early endosomes, Notch is sorted into distinct trafficking routes, including recycling endosomes, multivesicular bodies, late endosomes, and lysosomes [25, 53]. These trafficking decisions maintain both a quantitative and qualitative pool of Notch receptors. In the canonical pathway, ligand binding drives receptor internalization, whereas in non-canonical contexts, Notch can be endocytosed in the absence of ligand interaction. In either case, once internalized, the Notch traverses the vesicular pathway, which ultimately determine its signaling competence [25, 38, 53–55].

Dx plays a pivotal role in these processes. It promotes the trafficking of Notch receptors from the cell membrane into early and subsequently late endosomal vesicles, where Notch is stabilized and its half-life extended [36]. Blocking this progression into the late endosome impairs Dx-mediated Notch activation, underscoring the importance of vesicular transport [36]. Genetic interaction studies further showed that Dx cooperates with the components of HOPS and AP-3 endocytic complexes, both of which strongly influence Notch signaling outcomes [38]. More recently, inhibition of Rab11 in stellate cells was shown to trap Notch in early and late endosomes, resulting in hyperactivation of Dx-mediated canonical Notch signaling [56].

The regulatory scope of Dx extends beyond Notch. We recently demonstrated that Dx contributes to morphogen trafficking, in Drosophila, including Decapentaplegic and Wingless [47, 48]. Dx interacts with Dpp and, together with Rab7, facilitates its endocytosis and gradient formation, thereby shaping Dpp signaling activity [47]. Similarly, Dx regulates Wg by promoting its spreading across tissues and by modulating Armadillo/β-catenin stability. Through its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, Dx is hypothesized to directly target Arm for proteasomal degradation, attenuating Wg signaling [48]. These findings highlight Dx as a central regulator of vesicular trafficking and morphogen distribution, with implications for gradient-dependent developmental patterning.

Evidence from mammalian systems reinforces the conserved role of DTX family proteins in ubiquitination and trafficking. DTX1 for instance, regulates the ubiquitination status of Notch. The lipid kinase PI5P4Kγ (Phosphatidyl Inositol-5-phosphate 4-kinase γ), a substrate of DTX1, facilitates the internalization of the Notch receptor through a Rab4 dependent mechanism. Subsequently, this prevents the recycling of Notch receptor to the membrane, consequently downregulating the signaling [57]. DTX3L can facilitate CXCR4, a chemokine receptor, trafficking from the early endosome to the lysosome, thereby accelerating receptor ubiquitination and degradation [58].

Other family members participate in broader cellular functions, including DNA damage response mechanisms. For instance, DTX2 is PARylated and subjected to DNA damage sites [59, 60], where it recognizes and presumably ubiquinates many proteins of the DNA damage response pathway in a PAR (Poly-ADP ribose) dependent manner via its WWE and DTC domains, respectively [14]. A recent study by Djerir et al., also revealed that DTX2 is actively recruited to DSBs in a PARylation-dependent manner, and it modulates DSB repair, mainly via HR (Homologous Recombination). It was also observed that DTX2-depleted cells are more sensitive to PARP-inhibitor treatment, in accordance with findings from Tsujino et al., [61] and Olivieri et al., [62, 63]. Further, DTX3L mediates histone ubiquitination during the DNA damage response, catalyzing H4K91 monoubiquitination and promoting 53BP1 recruitment through recognition of methylated H4K20. Depleting DTX3L, increases sensitivity to DNA damage, apparently as a consequence of NHEJ (Non-Homologous End Joining) deficiency [64]. DTX3L is recruited to DNA damage sites in a PARylation-dependent manner and mediates polyubiquitination and degradation of p53, thereby enhancing chemotherapy and radiation-induced apoptosis [65]. Again, overexpression of DTX3L ubiquitinates and mediates nuclear export and degradation of TIRR (a negative regulator of 53BP1), thereby increasing 53BP1 binding to DSBs and directing cells to prefer NHEJ over HR. Increasing DTX3L also leads to HR deficiency, thereby sensitizing cells to PARPi treatment [66]. DTX3L also contributes to BRCA1 recruitment by ubiquitinating RAP80 (Receptor-Associated Protein 80) in a PARP-1 and BAL-1 dependent manner [67]. These findings suggest potential involvement of DTX family members, especially DTX2 and DTX3L, in the DDR pathway and DSB repair mechanism, and understanding the detailed molecular mechanisms, substrate specificity, and specific contexts in which their modulation increases sensitivity to DNA-damaging agents and PARPi treatment could be useful for clinical applications.

Beyond canonical protein substrates, recent discoveries have expanded the substrate repertoire of Dx family ligases. Studies from Zhu et al. showed that DTX enzymes catalyze ubiquitination of ADP-ribose moieties that generate a hybrid ADP-ribose–ubiquitin modification [23, 68], which is previously believed to be the opposite, i.e. ADP-ribosylation of Ub [20, 69]. DTX family members perform this action through their C-terminal RING and DTC domain, where the DTC domain recognizes and binds to NAD+/ADP-ribose [14, 20, 23] and the RING domain recruits E2 ~ Ub to transfer Ub onto the 3′-hydroxyl of the terminal ribose [23, 70]. Consequent findings have confirmed that this modification is exclusively conserved among the DTX family E3 ligases [71], which positions them uniquely in the cross-talk of ADP ribosylation and ubiquitination. Though a handful of recent discoveries have shed light on how cells may read [72] and respond to these modifications [73, 74], their detailed cellular functions and regulatory processes are yet to be determined. Again, continued studies have revealed that DTX3L can directly ubiquitylate DNA and RNA in vitro by forming an ester bond at the 3′-terminal adenosine ribose, thereby protecting nucleic acids from 3′→5′ exonuclease digestion. This nucleic acid ubiquitylation activity is conserved in DTX3 but absent in the WWE domain-containing family members DTX1, DTX2, and DTX4, and can be reversed by deubiquitinases such as USP2, JOSD1, and SARS-CoV-2 PLpro [75]. Biochemical and structural studies further revealed that DTX3L binds single-stranded DNA and RNA and catalyses ubiquitination specifically at the 3′-end of these substrates, as well as on dsDNA bearing a 3′ overhang of at least two nucleotides. NMR and fluorescence‐based assays confirmed that the DTC domain mediates ssNA binding, while the RING domain recruits E2 ~ Ub to transfer Ub onto the 3′‐hydroxyl of the terminal ribose [76].

These findings extend the role of DTX E3 ligases beyond classical protein ubiquitination and endocytosis, implicating them in genome stability, RNA turnover, and nucleic acid–dependent signaling. The ability of DTX family proteins to integrate ubiquitination with trafficking thus positions them as versatile regulators at the crossroads of developmental signaling and cellular homeostasis. The emerging role of DTX family proteins in linking ubiquitination with ADP ribosylation reinforces their involvement in broader cellular processes as well as pathological responses.

Deltex and cancer

The role of Drosophila Dx in tumor biology has primarily been linked to its regulation in cell death and migration. Early studies showed that Dx enhances Eiger-induced cell death [43]. Dx also synergizes with TRAF6 to promote apoptosis and cell migration through JNK activation, even in the absence of Eiger [77]. Interestingly, Dx-driven JNK activation can also give rise to metastatic-like phenotypes independent of TRAF6 [77]. Human DTX1, a close structural homolog of Drosophila Dx, plays a key role in tumorigenesis, invasion, and metastasis of several cancers (Table 1). Its expression is detected in follicular lymphomas, a subset of Diffuse Large B-cell Lymphoma (DLBCL), and Burkitt’s lymphoma cell line [110]. Recurrent DTX1 mutations are now recognized as driver events in lymphoma pathogenesis [83]. DTX1 also contributes to osteosarcoma invasiveness through Notch-dependent signaling [79] and governs glioblastoma aggressiveness by activating non-canonical Notch pathways [81]. Mechanistically, DTX1 triggers the RTK/PI3K/PKB and MAPK/ERK mitotic pathways while inducing anti-apoptotic factor Mcl-1. Consistently, glioblastoma patients with low DTX1 expression show improved survival [81]. Missense DTX1 mutations have been reported in splenic marginal zone lymphomas and in patients with primary and recurrent diffuse large B-cell lymphomas [80, 82, 111]. Mutations in the WWE domain of DTX1 impair its function as a negative regulator of Notch and are recurrently found in DLBCLs, reinforcing its tumor-promoting potential [83]. Beyond lymphoma, DTX1 mutations influence patient outcomes in lung cancer. Overview of studies elucidating the role of Dx in different types of cancer

Other family members also reveal context-specific roles in cancer progression (Table 1). DTX2 is expressed in muscle cells, where it regulates myoblast proliferation through Pax7 while inhibiting differentiation through MyoD ubiquitination [88]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, DTX2 promotes tumor progression by facilitating the infiltration and polarization of immunosuppressive Tumor-Associated Neutrophils (TAN) via upregulating CXCL2 and CXCL6 [89]. More importantly, DTX2 confers therapeutic resistance. In Lenvatinib-resistant HCC, high JAK2-STAT3 signaling drives DTX2 expression, which degrades HSD17B4 to alter fatty acid metabolism, ultimately reducing Lenvatinib-induced ferroptosis and cell death and enabling resistance [90]. Elevated DTX2 also drives glioma proliferation and invasion by modulating levels of Helicase-Like Transcription Factor (HLTF) [91].

DTX3 has been studied in diverse tumor contexts and displays both oncogenic and suppressive functions. High expression correlates with tumor cell proliferation and poor prognosis in ductal breast carcinoma [9, 92]. In esophageal carcinoma, however, DTX3 expression is reduced, and functional studies suggest it may suppress tumor progression through NOTCH2 [94]. Similarly, in triple-negative breast cancer, low DTX3 levels impair NOTCH4 degradation, promoting tumor growth [93], while in bladder cancer, its downregulation enhances cell invasion and metastasis through NOTCH1 [33]. Conversely, in ovarian cancer, DTX3 acquires an oncogenic role by interacting with mutant p53 to enhance its tumor-promoting activity [95]. Its involvement in colorectal cancer has also been reported, where DTX3 regulates tumor growth by regulating E2F-1 mediated cell cycle progression [96].

DTX3L, also called B-lymphoma and BAL Associated Protein (BBAP), represents a newer member of the family with strong oncogenic associations. First identified as a binding partner of B Aggressive Lymphoma1 (BAL-1), and ARTD9 (diptheria-toxin-like ADP-rRbosyltransferase-9) [12, 112], DTX3L is highly expressed in gliomas, where its levels correlate with malignancy grade and poor prognosis [101]. Mechanistically, it promotes invasion and metastasis through the FAK/PI3K/AKT signaling and confers resistance to radiation in prostate cancer [99, 104], and to growth suppression in cervical cancer through PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling [105]. In myeloma, DTX3L promotes cell cycle arrest and stromal adhesion [100], while its inhibition increases caspase-3 and PARP1 activity, enhancing apoptosis [100]. In prostate cancer, DTX3L represses the transcription of IFN regulatory factor-1 (IRF-1) and promotes STAT1 phosphorylation, driving metastasis [98]. Further study in prostate cancer revealed that high levels of DTX3L promote homologous recombination deficiency, apparently by ubiquitinating and degrading TIRR, and sensitizing cancer cells to PARP inhibitors [66]. While in breast cancer, it is part of a feedback loop with IRF1 that regulates ATRA (All-Trans Retinoic Acid) dependent growth inhibition of breast cancer cells [103]. Collectively, DTX3L is increasingly recognized as a key oncogenic driver and potential therapeutic target.

DTX4, the most recently identified member, has been implicated in hepatocellular carcinoma, colorectal cancer, and melanomas, and sarcoma [97, 106, 107]. Intriguingly, DTX4 expression increases upon inhibition of IDOL1 and PD-L1in sarcomas, suggesting that DTX4 may modulate tumor immune evasion and influence immunotherapy outcomes [109].

The mammalian ortholog of Su(dx), ITCH(AIP4), belongs to the NEDD4 family of HECT-type E3 ligases and further illustrates the duality of the pathway. ITCH exerts both oncogenic and tumor-suppressive effects depending on context, targeting substrates across signaling pathways, including NOTCH1 [113], SMAD2 [114], Wnt/ß-catenin [115], p53 family members p63 and p73 [116, 117], the tumor suppressor RASSF5/NORE1 [118], and the Lysosomal-Associated Protein Transmembrane 5 (LAPTM5) [119]. Over-expression of ITCH has been documented in breast and ovarian cancer, sarcomas [120], and anaplastic thyroid sarcoma [121], while its depletion enhances the sensitivity of tumors to chemotherapy [122].

These findings highlight the multifaceted and context-dependent roles of the DTX family in cancer. While certain members promote tumor progression by enhancing proliferation, invasion, and resistance, others act as suppressors by promoting apoptosis or restraining oncogenic signaling. This duality underscores both the challenges and the therapeutic opportunities that come with targeting DTX family proteins. A deeper understanding of their mechanisms will be essential for harnessing their potential as diagnostic biomarkers, prognostic indicators, and therapeutic targets in oncology.

Deltex and immunity

Members of the DTX family have emerged as important regulators of immune function, influencing T cell development and maturation, cytokine production, and host response to infections (Table 2). Early reports revealed that DTX1 is ubiquitously expressed, with particularly high levels in blood vessels, consistent with the pathological consequences of impaired Notch signaling in humans. Mechanistically, DTX1 was shown to modulate Notch-dependent transcriptional activity by enhancing or suppressing basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factors, such as E47 [10]. By inhibiting the formation of E47 homodimers, DTX1 prevents B-cell lineage commitment and cell death. Thus, facilitating the formation of E47/HEB heterodimer promotes T-cell lineage specification. At later stages, DTX1 also contributes to T-Cell Receptor (TCR) αβ versus γδ lineage choice [135]. Further evidence has linked DTX1 to B-cell-mediated immunity. Elevated expression of DTX1 has been reported in tonsillar germinal center B-cells (CD19+, CD38+), spleen, and mesenteric lymph nodes, with lower expression in non-lymphoid tissue such as brain, heart, pancreas, liver, kidney, and testis [110]. In lymphoid cells, DTX1 forms a part of a negative feedback loop that downregulates Notch signaling. Over-expression of DTX1 in mouse hematopoietic stem cells redirected lymphoid progenitors to the B cell lineage by inhibiting ICN-1 (Intra-Cellular-Notch) mediated transcriptional activation [123]. In contrast, however, other studies demonstrated that DTX1-deficient mice develop a normal immune system without any defect in T-Cell differentiation and maturation [137]. Beyond lineage commitment, DTX1 exerts regulatory control over T-cell activation. Specifically, DTX1 promotes ubiquitin-mediated degradation of MEKK1, thereby dampening T cell activation [126]. A pivotal role has also been covered in T-cell anergy. DTX1 is a transcriptional target of the Nuclear Factor of Activated T-cells (NFAT) which is induced during in T-cell anergy to suppress T-cell activation [128]. DTX1 also promotes the ubiquitination-mediated degradation of HIF-1 α (hypoxia-inducible factor 1 α), which maintains the expression of Foxp3 (Forkhead box protein P3), a key regulator of regulatory T (T-reg) cell function [130]. Additionally, DTX1 also regulates the expression of GADD45ß (Growth Arrest and DNA-Damage-inducible 45 ß) during T-cell anergy [128]. Independent of Notch, DTX1 also targets PKC θ (Protein Kinase C θ) for ubiquitination and degradation, thereby stabilizing Cbl-b, another E3 ligase that suppresses immune activation [129, 138].

The immunomodulatory roles of DTX1 extend to helper T cell subsets. For example, DTX1 has been implicated in Th17 differentiation. Treatment of CD4 + T cells with IL-6 and TGF-β induces IL-17 production, an effect that is reversed by DTX1 knockdown. Mechanistically, DTX1 enhances the DNA-binding ability of RORγ in the nucleus, thereby promoting IL-17 cytokine production [132]. In addition, DTX1 has been shown to influence the maturation of CD4 + and CD8 + double-positive thymocytes, in part by competing with p300 for E2A/HEB binding site, which increases resistance to apoptosis [127].

Other DTX family members also participate in immune signaling. DTX4 has been linked to the type 1 interferon signaling pathway (IFN-1), a critical arm of anti-viral immunity. In response to viral infection, IFN-1 signaling is activated when TANK-Binding Kinase 1(TBK1) phosphorylates IRF3. DTX4, together with other E3 ligases, promotes polyubiquitination and degradation of TBK1, thereby restraining the IFN-1 signaling (Fig. 4) [139]. In contrast, DTX3L was shown to be increased upon RSV infection and enhance IFN signaling by ubiquitination and phosphorylation of TBK1 [140]. Further, DTX3L/PARP9 has been shown to enhance STAT1-mediated ISG expression. In parallel, DTX3L also interacts with host histones and viral 3 C protease and remodels chromatin accessibility to drive selective ISG expression and promotes immunoproteasome-dependent restriction of viral replication, respectively [141]. In addition, recent findings have established a potential role for DTX3L in modulating the IFN-mediated antiviral response. Studies by Kar et al. have suggested a regulatory role of DTX3L/PARP9 in PARP14-mediated ADPr upon IFN induction. It is also observed that ADPr and Ub modifications are in close proximity, which depicts the possibility of ADPr-Ub dual modification on the substrates [142]. However, further studies are required to confirm these results and establish a potential role of DTX3L in antiviral response.

These findings highlight the multifaceted roles of DTX proteins as context-dependent regulators of immune development and homeostasis, balancing lineage commitment, immune tolerance, and antiviral defense.

Deltex family proteins in human pathologies beyond cancer and immunity

Accumulating evidence implicates members of the DTX family in diverse human diseases, ranging from inflammatory and autoimmune disorders to neurodegeneration and cardiovascular pathologies (Table 3). Inflammatory conditions, in particular, reveal a strong link between DTX3L and immune regulation. During viral infection, poly ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP9), also known as BAL1 or ARTD9, forms a complex with DTX3L, functioning as an E3 ubiquitin ligase to target both host histones and viral proteases. This enhances interferon-dependent immunity in a STAT1-dependent manner, mimicking an antiviral defense mechanism that may protect against chronic inflammatory disease and acute illness [141]. Consistently, DTX3L is upregulated in patients with Primary Sjogren’s syndrome (SS), a systemic autoimmune disease characterized by xerostomia and xerophthalmia (oral and ocular dryness), and lymphocyte infiltration of exocrine glands. Here, the DTX3L/PARP9 complex enhances STAT1 phosphorylation, upregulating the expression of CXCL10 (C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10) and IFIT-1 (IFN-Induced protein with Tetratricopeptide repeats 1), thereby promoting mononuclear cell infiltration [145]. Similarly, in rheumatoid arthritis, another chronic autoimmune disorder characterized by synovitis and joint destruction, DTX3L/ARTD9 drives fibroblast-like synoviocyte invasion and cytokine production in a STAT1-dependent manner, thereby accelerating inflammatory progression [146]. Likewise, Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) is a prototypical autoimmune disease characterized by autoantibody production. SLE has been linked to reduced DTX1 expression, which is associated with elevated IFN-γ production in response to aberrant T-cell activation, suggesting its utility as a diagnostic biomarker [131]. In addition, DTX1 modulates microglial polarization and neuro-inflammation following traumatic brain injury by enhancing NF-κB/IRF5 signaling, thereby modulating IL-6 and TNF- α expression [143]. Beyond autoimmunity, DTX proteins have been implicated in cardiovascular and metabolic disease. In cerebral arteriovenous malformation, a congenital cardiovascular disorder, reduced expression of METTL3 (RNA N6-methyladenosine (m6A) Methyltransferase) was found to decrease DTX1 and DTX3L expression, resulting in aberrant activation of Notch signaling [148]. More recently, DTX3L was shown to alleviate cachexia-related skeletal muscle fibrosis in an L-carnitine-dependent manner, functioning through the ubiquitination and degradation of Runx2 to reduce Collagen type I α 1 chain (COL1A1) expression [147]. Neurodegenerative contexts also highlight the pathological significance of DTX. DTX3L expression is elevated in LPS-induced microglia cells, where it modulates STAT1 activity to promote cytokine production and microglial activation, a hallmark of Parkinson’s disease pathology [149]. This finding represents the first direct association between a DTX family ligase and PD, positioning DTX3L as a potential therapeutic target in neurodegeneration.

These findings establish DTX proteins as critical regulators at the interface of immunity, inflammation, and tissue homeostasis. While DTX3L appears to dominate in inflammatory and autoimmune processes, DTX1 exerts both protective and pathogenic functions depending on cellular and disease context. These insights not only highlight the clinical relevance of DTX family ligases but also underscore their potential as therapeutic targets across a spectrum of inflammatory, autoimmune, and neurodegenerative diseases.

Future perspectives

Beyond its well-established role in Notch signaling, Dx has emerged as a multifaceted regulator of cellular signaling and homeostasis. Recent studies highlight its involvement in oncogenesis, immune regulation, and disease progression, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target. Future research should focus on delineating the broader signaling networks governed by Dx and their implications for human health and disease.

Dx is a cytoplasmic modulator of Notch signaling that can either enhance or suppress the pathway, depending on cellular context [5, 32, 42]. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying Dx-mediated Notch regulation could not only deepen insights into developmental biology but also reveal new opportunities for therapeutic interventions in Notch-related disorders. Pharmacological modulation of Dx, using small molecules or biologics designed to enhance or inhibit its function, could restore signaling homeostasis in cancers and congenital syndromes linked to aberrant Notch signaling. Advances in Genome-editing technologies, particularly CRISPR/Cas9, further offer the possibility of correcting pathogenic mutations in Dx genes. In parallel, Dx expression levels and activity may serve as a biomarker for disease diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment stratification, especially in cancers driven by dysregulated Notch signaling.

Our own studies have revealed that Dx also plays a significant role in the gradient formation and trafficking of morphogens such as Decapentaplegic and Wingless [47, 48]. By facilitating morphogen trafficking, Dx ensures proper activation of both short-range and long-range target genes. Moreover, through its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, Dx regulates Wg signaling at the level of Armadillo/β-catenin stability, incorporating an additional layer of control. Because Wnt/Wg and TGFβ/Dpp signaling pathways are central to metazoan development and frequently disrupted in hereditary disorders and cancers, a detailed mechanistic understanding of Dx-mediated regulation of Wnt and TGFβ pathways will be invaluable in advancing therapeutic strategies for diseases involving aberrant Wnt or Dpp signaling.

The role of Dx in cancer progression also warrants deeper exploration. Increased DTX expression and its involvement in cell migration, invasion, and metastasis have been reported in follicular lymphomas, osteosarcoma, glioblastoma, and melanoma [79, 81, 98, 99, 110]. Dissecting the molecular mechanisms of DTX function in cancer progression will be crucial for the development of innovative therapies that target tumor progression and resistance.

Another largely unexplored frontier is the contribution of DTX to neurodegeneration. The role of E3 ubiquitin ligases in proteostasis, including protein aggregation and clearance of misfolded proteins, is well-recognized in neurodegenerative conditions [150], yet the involvement of the Dx family remains poorly understood. Given its E3 ligase activity, Dx may influence protein quality control pathways relevant to neurodegenerative diseases, and further studies should investigate this possibility. Such insights could open new avenues for target proteostasis in conditions such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease.

Emerging evidence further suggests that Dx E3 ligases, particularly DTX3L, extend their activity beyond proteins to non-proteinaceous substrates, including nucleic acids and aADPr moieties. In vitro studies show that DTX3L can ubiquitinate DNA and RNA [75, 76]. However, the physiological relevance of nucleic acid ubiquitination by DTX3L in vivo remains to be elucidated. Future investigations should focus on characterizing the biological consequences of nucleic acid ubiquitination and its potential roles in gene regulation, immune response, and genomic stability. Further, ubiquitination of ADPr moieties alone or on PARylated substrates (including both proteins and nucleic acids), which is exclusively conserved across the DTX family E3 ligases, positions them as a key modulator of the interplay between ADP ribosylation and ubiquitination process [23, 71]. Given that both ADP-ribosylation and ubiquitination are important modulators of diverse cellular and pathological processes [151, 152], and considering the increasing evidence of interactions between DTX family proteins and PARPs, further investigation into their targets and the biological significance of ADPr-Ub modification in vivo may provide additional insights into their potential physiological and pathological relevance.
