Genetic resistance to vector-borne hemoprotozoa in livestock: molecular markers, host-parasite interactions, and implications for breeding and control
Authors: Bashir Salim, Saeed Alasmari, Mohammed A Bakheit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportanceVector-borne hemoparasitic diseases, such as Theileria, Babesia, Trypanosoma, Leishmania, and Anaplasma, pose significant constraints to livestock production, particularly in Africa and other tropical regions. These infections cause considerable economic losses from mortality, decreased productivity, and the high costs of treatment and control efforts.ObservationsResistance to hemoparasitic infections in livestock is strongly influenced by the genetic factors of the host. The key host genes involved in immune responses (e.g., BoLA-DRB3 and TLR4), oxidative stress defense (SOD2 and GPX1), drug metabolism (ABCB1 and CYP3A4), and ectoparasite resistance (MC1R and MHC) have been identified as contributors to resistance phenotypes. On the parasite side, the genes responsible for immune evasion (VSG and AP2), drug resistance (MDR1 and CYTB), and host cell invasion (AMA1 and HSP90) play pivotal roles in infection persistence and treatment failure. The advances in genomic and transcriptomic tools, including genome-wide association studies, CRISPR, and multi-omics profiling, have enhanced the understanding of these host-parasite interactions and enabled identification of the molecular markers for resistance traits.Conclusions and RelevanceAdvanced genetic resistance offers a sustainable, long-term solution to managing vector-borne parasitic infections in livestock. The integration of resistance-associated markers into selective breeding programs, coupled with genome editing and real-time surveillance, can improve livestock resilience. Aligning these efforts with One Health strategies and collaborative genomic initiatives will be essential for achieving effective, regionally adapted disease control.Keywords: Host-parasite interactions, parasite resistance, immune response, disease control, selective breeding

INTRODUCTION

Vector-borne blood parasites significantly threaten global livestock health, causing diseases such as theileriosis, babesiosis, anaplasmosis, trypanosomiasis, and other malaria-like infections. These diseases are responsible for significant economic losses, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions, where their vectors (ticks, tsetse flies, mosquitoes, and biting midges) are abundant [1,2,3]. Livestock productivity is severely affected by these infections, leading to high morbidity and mortality rates, reproductive failure, weight loss, reduced milk production, and immunosuppression, which predisposes these animals to secondary infections. The need for sustainable disease control measures has driven research efforts to uncover the genetic determinants of host resistance and parasite adaptation.

The host resistance to blood parasites is a polygenic trait influenced by several factors, including the innate and adaptive immune responses, oxidative stress regulation, metabolic adaptation, and genetic polymorphisms associated with disease resilience [4,5]. Certain breeds of cattle, small ruminants, equines, and camelids exhibit remarkable resistance to these infections, not merely due to the presence of specific gene variants, but to their functional expression and regulatory dynamics that enhance immune responsiveness, stress tolerance, and vector avoidance. Understanding the genetic basis of the host resistance assists in selective breeding programs and paves the way for novel therapeutic and vaccine development strategies [6].

On the parasite side, survival and pathogenicity are largely dependent on their ability to evade host immune responses, establish chronic infections, and resist drug treatments. Major vector-borne hemoparasites show specific parasitic features that define their pathogenicity and target cell preferences in livestock. Trypanosoma species are extracellular flagellates that primarily inhabit the bloodstream and lymphatics, targeting erythrocytes indirectly via antigenic variation and immune modulation. Theileria parasites are unique in their ability to invade and transform leukocytes, particularly lymphocytes and macrophages, enabling them to proliferate within the immune system of the host before moving to erythrocytes. Babesia parasites are intraerythrocytic protozoans that multiply within red blood cells, leading to hemolysis and anemia. Leishmania species typically infect macrophages and other phagocytic cells in the reticuloendothelial system, but their presence in blood is often linked to vector transmission. Understanding these target cell tropisms is essential for unraveling host-pathogen interactions and designing effective control strategies.

Parasite-specific genes, such as variant surface glycoproteins (VSGs) in Trypanosoma spp., apical membrane antigen 1 (AMA1) in Theileria spp., and cytochrome b (CYTB) mutations in Babesia and Plasmodium spp., illustrate the adaptive strategies these organisms use [7,8]. By modulating their gene expression, blood parasites can escape host immune recognition, resist oxidative stress, and manipulate the host cellular pathways to ensure survival and proliferation [9].

Recent advances in whole-genome sequencing (WGS), transcriptomics, and CRISPR-based genome editing have facilitated the identification of key resistance-associated genes in the hosts and parasites [10]. Genes such as BoLA-DRB3 and Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) in cattle, MC1R in small ruminants, and CYTB in multiple species have been linked to resistance against vector-borne hemoparasites [1]. Similarly, parasite genes, such as ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, heat shock proteins (HSP90), and superoxide dismutases (SODs), contribute to the ability of a pathogen to counteract the host defenses and environmental stressors.

This review synthesizes current knowledge on host and parasite genetic factors underlying the resistance to blood-borne parasites in livestock. This paper highlights the key candidate genes involved in resistance and vector-host interactions. The implications of these findings for disease control are discussed further, focusing on selective breeding, vaccine design, and targeted therapeutics. Understanding these genetic interactions is crucial for the development of sustainable, genome-informed strategies to mitigate the burden of hemoparasitic infections in livestock.

METHODS

This narrative review summarizes current knowledge on the genetic basis of resistance in livestock to vector-borne hemoprotozoan parasites, with particular emphasis on host–parasite molecular interactions and their implications for disease control and selective breeding.

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive search of scientific literature was performed using the following electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. The search covered publications from January 2000 to March 2024, using a combination of keywords: “hemoparasites,” “livestock resistance genes,” “Trypanosoma,” “Theileria,” “Babesia,” “Anaplasma,” “Leishmania,” “genomic selection,” “vector-borne parasites,” “drug resistance,” “host-parasite interaction,” and “molecular markers.” Reference lists of the key articles were also screened manually to identify additional relevant studies.

Host-specific genes and their roles in resistance to blood parasites

The ability of livestock to resist infections caused by blood parasites, such as Theileria, Babesia, Anaplasma, and Trypanosoma, is mainly dependent on their genetic makeup. Table 1 lists the host-specific genes known to regulate immune response, oxidative stress defense, drug metabolism, and tick resistance, and they are illustrated in the graphical abstract (Fig. 1).

Immune response genes

Host immune responses play a crucial role in recognizing and eliminating parasites. The bovine leukocyte antigen class II (BoLA class II) genes in cattle are essential for antigen presentation and adaptive immunity, influencing the resistance to Trypanosoma, Theileria parva, Anaplasma marginale, Babesia bovis, and Babesia bigemina [11,12,13,14,15]. Variants of TLR4 are associated with the enhanced innate immune recognition of parasite-derived molecules, improving the host defense against Trypanosoma infections [16,17]. In addition, interleukins (IL-4 and IL-13), which regulate the Th2 immune response, contribute to the resistance against blood parasites by promoting antibody production and macrophage activation [18].

Oxidative stress defense genes

During infection, blood parasites induce oxidative stress in the host, necessitating robust antioxidant defenses. Cytochrome b-245 beta chain is a critical component of the NADPH oxidase complex, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) to kill intracellular parasites such as Theileria annulata [4]. Similarly, SOD2 converts superoxide radicals into less harmful molecules, preventing oxidative damage to host immune cells and improving parasite clearance [19]. The glutathione peroxidase 1 (GPX1) gene enhances resistance by detoxifying peroxides, protecting immune cells from oxidative stress induced by Babesia and Theileria, Anaplasma, and other apicomplexan parasite infections [20,21,22,23].

Drug metabolism and detoxification genes

Host genetics influence drug metabolism and treatment outcomes against blood parasites. ATP-binding cassette transporter B1 (ABCB1), also known as P-glycoprotein, plays a vital role in drug transport and resistance by regulating the uptake and excretion of antiparasitic drugs such as buparvaquone and diminazene aceturate [24,25]. Variants of cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4) affect drug metabolism, influencing the efficacy of trypanocidal and antiprotozoal drugs in livestock [26,27]. These genetic factors highlight the importance of precision medicine in veterinary parasitology.

Genes associated with tick resistance

Ticks are the primary vectors of many blood parasites, making host resistance to tick infestation a key factor in controlling parasite transmission. Melanocortin 1 receptor (MC1R) regulates coat pigmentation and skin thickness, which can influence the tick attachment rates and feeding success [5]. Cattle with specific MC1R variants often exhibit lighter coat colors, which are correlated with lower tick burdens caused by changes in surface temperature and hair density, making it less favorable for tick survival [28]. In addition, major histocompatibility complex (MHC) genes contribute to immune-mediated resistance by enhancing host recognition of tick salivary proteins and triggering inflammatory responses that limit tick feeding [29,30].

Remarks

The host genetic architecture plays a pivotal role in determining the resistance to blood parasites. Selection for favorable alleles and variants in immune response, oxidative stress defense, drug metabolism, and tick resistance genes offers potential strategies for improving livestock health and productivity. Future genomics research should integrate multi-gene resistance mechanisms to develop more resilient livestock populations against evolving parasitic threats.

Immune evasion genes

Parasites have developed intricate mechanisms to evade the immune system of a host, ensuring prolonged survival and transmission. VSG in Trypanosoma brucei is a prime example, allowing the parasite to constantly change its antigenic profile and avoid immune detection [31]. Similarly, apicomplexan AP2 transcription factors regulate gene expression in Theileria and Babesia, influencing immune-evasion strategies and metabolic adaptations [32,33]. Theileria parasites use a range of strategies to evade the host immune system and promote their survival. One such mechanism involves constitutive activation of the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway within the infected leukocytes, enabling Theileria to maintain its intracellular niche by preventing apoptosis of the host cell it transforms. This activation is crucial for maintaining the transformed state of the infected cells [34,35].

Drug resistance genes

Mutations in the key metabolic and transport genes often facilitate drug resistance in blood parasites. CYTB mutations in T. annulata and Babesia confer resistance to buparvaquone and atovaquone by altering the mitochondrial electron transport chain, reducing drug binding efficiency [8]. In addition, the multidrug resistance protein 1 (MDR1) and ABC transporters in Trypanosoma and Plasmodium function as drug efflux pumps, actively removing the therapeutic compounds from parasite cells, lowering drug efficacy [36,37,38]. These resistance mechanisms pose significant challenges for controlling parasitic infections, necessitating the development of alternative therapies.

Host cell invasion and survival genes

Successful invasion and survival within host cells are crucial for blood parasites. AMA1 in Theileria and Plasmodium facilitates red blood cell invasion by interacting with the host cell receptors, making it a key drug target [39]. HSP90 helps parasites survive within host cells by stabilizing proteins under stress conditions, such as fever or drug treatment [40,41]. Moreover, transferrin receptor in Trypanosoma ensures efficient iron uptake from the host, a critical factor for parasite growth and replication, especially in iron-limited environments [42,43].

Oxidative stress defense

The host immune responses often generate ROS as a defense mechanism against infections. Parasites counteract this through the expression of antioxidant genes such as SOD and glutathione S-transferase (GST). These substances neutralize ROS, which prevents oxidative damage and enables parasite survival within immune-reactive environments [20,44]. This adaptation is crucial in chronic infections, where parasites must endure repeated immune assaults over time.

Remarks

The genetic adaptations of blood parasites are central to their ability to persist in host populations. Understanding these genes provides insights into potential drug targets and vaccine development for effective parasite control. Addressing challenges such as antigenic variations, drug resistance, and immune suppression is essential for developing next-generation therapeutics against these resilient pathogens. Further studies should focus on targeting multi-gene networks rather than individual genes to overcome the complexity of parasite survival strategies.

Implications for selective breeding and disease control

Identification of the genetic factors associated with resistance to blood parasites in livestock has profound implications for selective breeding, disease control, and sustainable livestock management. Conventional control strategies for vector-borne parasitic infections, such as chemical treatment with antiparasitic drugs, acaricides, and insecticides, have faced increasing challenges, including the development of drug resistance in parasites and acaricide resistance in vectors [1,4]. Furthermore, vaccination efforts against hemoparasites have had variable success because of the ability of many parasites to evade the host immunity through antigenic variations [9]. These limitations underscore the necessity of integrating genetically based approaches, particularly selective breeding and genomic selection, to enhance the resilience of a host to infection.

Selective breeding for disease-resistant livestock

Selective breeding programs aim to improve livestock productivity by enhancing desirable traits such as milk yield, growth rate, fertility, and disease resistance. The identification of resistance-associated genes in livestock has opened avenues for marker-assisted selection and genomic selection strategies to incorporate disease resistance into breeding programs [6]. For example, specific alleles of BoLA-DRB3, a key gene in the bovine MHC, have been linked to resistance against Theileria, Anaplasma, and Babesia spp., making it a prime target for selective breeding in cattle [10]. Similarly, polymorphisms in TLR4, a gene encoding a TLR involved in innate immune responses, have been associated with resistance to trypanosomiasis and babesiosis in multiple breeds [10].

For small ruminants, resistance to Theileria lestoquardi and Trypanosoma spp. has been correlated with specific haplotypes of the MHC class II genes, suggesting that selective breeding programs could prioritize individuals with advantageous MHC alleles to enhance resistance [5]. In equines, resistance to Theileria equi and Babesia caballi has been linked to polymorphisms in the interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and IL-10 genes, highlighting potential genetic markers for breeding resistant horses and donkeys [4].

Genomic selection and precision breeding

The advances in WGS and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have allowed livestock breeding programs to use genomic selection to identify and propagate animals with superior resistance to blood parasites. Genomic selection relies on high-density single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers to predict the genetic merit of an animal for disease resistance traits [10]. This approach has been successfully implemented in dairy cattle to enhance their resistance to mastitis and other infectious diseases, highlighting its potential application for vector-borne parasitic infections [45,46].

Precision breeding using gene-editing technologies, such as CRISPR-Cas9, presents a promising avenue for directly modifying resistance-associated genes in livestock [47,48,49]. Recent research has shown that the CRISPR-mediated knock-in of advantageous alleles in BoLA-DRB3 and TLR4 enhances the immune responses in bovine cells, suggesting that targeted genome editing could be a viable strategy for improving the disease resilience in cattle [50].

Implications for disease control strategies

Beyond selective breeding, understanding host and parasite genetics can enhance disease control efforts through targeted interventions. The emergence of drug-resistant parasite strains has been a major challenge to controlling hemoparasitic diseases. Genetic studies on T. annulata, B. bovis, and Trypanosoma evansi have identified mutations in the CYTB gene that confer resistance to commonly used antiparasitic drugs such as buparvaquone and diminazene aceturate [51,52]. Thus, incorporating the genetic surveillance of resistance markers into disease monitoring programs can allow veterinarians and policymakers to make informed decisions regarding treatment protocols and drug rotation strategies to mitigate resistance development.

Parasite genetic studies have revealed potential vaccine targets that could improve the immunization efficacy. For example, AMA1 in Theileria spp. is a crucial gene involved in host-cell invasion and has been identified as a promising vaccine candidate [53]. Similarly, VSGs in T. brucei and T. parva are critical for immune evasion, and targeting conserved regions of these genes could lead to broad-spectrum vaccine formulations [4].

Vector control and integrated management

Genetic resistance in hosts should be complemented with vector control measures because many blood parasites rely on arthropod vectors for transmission. Recent studies suggest that host coat color and skin thickness, regulated by genes such as MC1R, influence tick attachment and feeding success [5]. Breeds with darker pigmentation and thicker skin exhibit lower tick burdens, suggesting that MC1R polymorphisms could be used in breeding programs aimed at reducing vector-host interactions.

Integrated control strategies combining host genetic resistance, parasite surveillance, vaccination, and vector management are essential for sustainable disease control. For example, tick-resistant cattle breeds such as the N’Dama and East African Shorthorn Zebu, which possess favorable alleles in BoLA and MC1R, could be introduced strategically into endemic regions to reduce the reliance on acaricides [54,55,56].

Remarks

Genetic-based approaches to disease resistance in livestock offer sustainable solutions to the growing challenges of vector-borne parasitic infections. Selective breeding, genomic selection, and gene editing have promise for developing resilient livestock populations while reducing the dependence on chemical treatments and pharmaceuticals. In parallel, understanding the genetic mechanisms of parasite resistance to drugs and host immune responses can assist in the development of next-generation vaccines and therapeutics. Future research should focus on integrating genomic tools with traditional breeding programs, enhancing genetic surveillance of the hosts and parasites, and implementing comprehensive disease management strategies to improve livestock health and productivity in endemic regions.

DISCUSSION

The integration of host and parasite genomics has advanced the understanding of genetic resistance to vector-borne hemoparasitic infections in livestock. Host genetic markers, such as BoLA class II, TLR4, SOD2, GPX1, and MC1R, have been identified as critical components in modulating the immune responses, oxidative stress, drug metabolism, and vector resistance (Table 1, Fig. 1). These findings provide insights into the biological basis of disease resilience and provide tangible targets for selective breeding and veterinary interventions.

BoLA-DRB3, a key component of the bovine MHC region, is strongly associated with the resistance to Theileria, Anaplasma, and Babesia spp., and is a major candidate for selection in breeding programs [10,11,12,13,14,15]. Polymorphisms in TLR4 improve innate immune recognition, particularly against Trypanosoma infections [16,17]. Antioxidant genes, such as SOD2 and GPX1, mitigate oxidative stress during infection, enhancing host cell survival and immune efficacy [19,20,21,22,23]. Variants in ABCB1 and CYP3A4 influence antiparasitic drug metabolism, making them potential markers for precision treatment strategies [24,25,26,27]. MC1R variants linked to the coat color and skin thickness are associated with reduced tick attachment, indirectly supporting the control of tick-borne parasite transmission [5,28].

On the parasite side, immune evasion and drug resistance mechanisms remain key challenges. T. brucei uses VSG switching to avoid immune detection, whereas apicomplexan parasites deploy AP2 transcription factors and activate the host NF-κB pathways to suppress apoptosis and persist intracellularly [31,32,33,34,35]. Resistance to antiparasitic drugs has been linked to mutations in CYTB and the overexpression of MDR1 and ABC transporters, necessitating vigilant molecular surveillance and development of new therapeutics [8,36,37,38].

Marker-assisted and genomic selection strategies based on the identified resistance alleles have shown promise in cattle, small ruminants, and equines. In cattle, BoLA-DRB3 and TLR4 polymorphisms have been associated with the resistance to Theileria, Anaplasma, Babesia, and Trypanosoma [10]. In small ruminants, resistance to T. lestoquardi and Trypanosoma spp. correlates with MHC class II haplotypes, while in equines, polymorphisms in IFN-γ and IL-10 are linked to resistance to T. equi and B. caballi [4,5].

Genomic selection using SNP arrays and machine learning might accelerate breeding for disease resistance, which is supported by the successful use of genomic tools in dairy cattle for traits such as mastitis resistance [45,46]. Gene-editing tools like CRISPR-Cas9 also have transformative potential, and studies showing targeted editing of BoLA-DRB3 and TLR4 confirm the feasibility of precision breeding for improved disease resilience [47,48,49,50].

Beyond breeding, understanding parasite genetics is essential for disease control. Genetic surveillance of resistance-conferring mutations, such as those in CYTB in T. annulata, B. bovis, and T. evansi, is essential for guiding therapeutic strategies and mitigating drug resistance [51,52]. Vaccine targets like AMA1 and invariant regions of VSG in Trypanosoma and T. parva also provide promising avenues for immunization efforts [4,39,53].

Integrated disease control strategies combining host genetic resistance, parasite surveillance, targeted vaccination, and vector management are vital. MC1R-based resistance to ticks, which is supported by coat color and skin traits, complements chemical control approaches. The strategic introduction of tick-resistant breeds, such as the N’Dama and East African Shorthorn Zebu, into endemic areas can substantially reduce acaricide dependence [54,55,56].

Ultimately, the sustainable control of vector-borne parasitic infections in livestock requires the integration of genomic tools, traditional breeding programs, and comprehensive disease management strategies. Innovations in molecular genetics must be aligned with on-the-ground practices to improve livestock health and productivity in parasite-endemic regions.

FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

The growing advances in genomics and molecular biology present unprecedented opportunities to enhance the livestock disease resistance against blood parasites. Nevertheless, several challenges and knowledge gaps remain. Future research should focus on refining genetic selection strategies, improving vaccine development, and integrating multi-omics approaches to enhance disease resistance.
