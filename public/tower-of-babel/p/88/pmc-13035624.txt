Propensity score matched analysis of short-term outcomes in rectal cancer robotic surgery: Hugo RAS versus da Vinci systems
Authors: Shigeo Toda, Kosuke Hiramatsu, Shuichiro Matoba, Yusuke Maeda, Naoto Okazaki, Yudai Fukui, Yutaka Hanaoka, Masashi Ueno, Hiroya Kuroyanagi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Hugo RAS system (Hugo), comprising an open surgical console and independent arm carts was launched into the surgical robotics market following the da Vinci surgical system (da Vinci). This study was designed to disclose the feasibility of rectal surgery using Hugo vs. da Vinci systems by assessing short-term outcomes. Between September 2019 and October 2025, 211 patients underwent robotic surgery for non-metastatic rectal cancer. The outcomes of 81 Hugo-assisted and 130 da Vinci-assisted robotic surgeries were compared. The incidence of postoperative complications classified as Clavien–Dindo (CD) grade III or higher was the primary outcome. Secondary outcomes were blood loss, operative time, console time, positive circumferential resection margin (CRM), postoperative hospital stay, and mortality. Hugo and da Vinci cases were compared using 1:1 propensity score matching, balancing for age, sex, BMI, ASA-PS, tumor location, clinical T and N stage, preoperative therapy, surgeon experience, console experience and sphincter preservation. After matching, each group included 56 patients with comparable pretreatment characteristics. There were no significant differences between the Hugo and da Vinci groups in CD grade ≥ III complications (7.1% vs. 8.9%, p = 1.000), blood loss, operative time, console time, CRM positivity, postoperative hospital stay, or mortality. No open conversions occurred in either group. The Hugo system enables feasible rectal surgery with no significant differences in short-term outcomes compared with those achieved using the da Vinci system.Keywords: Rectal neoplasms, Robotic surgery, da Vinci surgical system, Hugo RAS system, Short-term outcomes, Propensity score matching

Introduction

The surgical management of colorectal cancer has undergone outstanding improvement in recent years from open procedures to laparoscopic and robotic surgical approaches. These minimally invasive methods offer reduced post-operative pain, faster recovery and shorter hospital stays, without compromising oncological outcomes, according to multiple randomized trials and systematic reviews [1–4]. However, laparoscopic rectal surgery remains technically demanding, as it necessitates precise dissection using rigid instruments within the confined pelvic cavity, which is closely bordered by critical structures including autonomic nerves and urogenital organs. Indeed, randomized trials have reported open conversion rates of 9% to 17% [5–7] in patients undergoing laparoscopic rectal cancer surgery. Some studies suggested potential deterioration in pathological quality when treated laparoscopically compared to open surgery [8, 9]; albeit, no studies have demonstrated a statistical reduction in rates of disease-free survival from a laparoscopic approach.

More recently, robotic-assisted surgery has emerged providing enhanced dexterity, three-dimensional visualization, and ergonomic and technical advantages for the surgeon, including precise surgical dissection, even in confined spaces like the pelvic cavity. Robotic rectal surgery reduces the risk of open conversion [10, 11] and may improve postoperative urogenital function [12]. The da Vinci Surgical System (Intuitive Surgical, CA, USA), developed in 1999, was the first widely available commercial robotic system, marking a significant milestone in the mainstream of robotic-assisted minimally invasive surgery. The refined da Vinci Xi series was later launched in 2014. Since 2020, several new robotic surgical platforms have emerged, among which the Hugo RAS (Hugo) System (Medtronic, MN, USA) features a modular design with an open-type console and independent arm. The Hugo system, initially introduced in Chile, Panama, and the European Union, became available in Japan in 2022, and was approved for colorectal surgery in 2023, with the first report of its use for colorectal cancer surgery published in 2023 by an Italian surgical team [13]. In July 2023, Japan’s national health insurance approved the use of Hugo for the treatment of colorectal surgery for malignancy, with the first surgical case reported shortly thereafter [14].

The open console of Hugo enables the surgeon to operate with a more natural posture compared with traditional cockpit-type consoles (e.g., da Vinci and Hinotori [Medicaloid, Kobe, Japan]) and provides a three-dimensional view that allows simultaneous observation by nearby trainees and supervising surgeons. Each independent robotic arm can be positioned at an optimal docking angle and tilt, allowing a configuration tailored to both the patient’s anatomy and the specific surgical procedure. The system is equipped with ergonomic hand controls designed to reduce wrist strain. Key functions include “Flex Wrist,” which enables the manipulation of curved forceps while maintaining a straight wrist position to minimize surgeon fatigue, and “Rotation Multiplier,” which amplifies the instrument’s rotational motion up to two-fold, facilitating smooth suturing and reducing stress on the surgeon’s wrist [15].

Given these clear technical advantages, we sought to determine whether the Hugo system was as efficient and safe as the gold-standard da Vinic system for the treatment of rectal cancer patients. We report the short-term outcomes of rectal cancer surgery using Hugo compared with da Vinci, using a propensity score–matched analysis.

Materials and methods

Patients

Patients who underwent robotic surgery for rectal cancer between September 2019 and October 2025 at Toranomon Hospital and Toranomon Hospital Kajigaya and who were followed for more than 30 days were included. Exclusion criteria included patients with distant metastases.

Institutional Review Board approval was given from our institution (approval no. 2731), and participants were informed that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time.

Data Collection

We retrospectively collected data, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status (ASA-PS) classification, tumor location (above or below the peritoneal reflection), tumor distance from the anal verge, pretreatment cT, pretreatment cN, preoperative radiotherapy, type of operation, lateral lymph node dissection (LLND), surgeon experience (year), console experience (case), pathological tumor depth, pathological lymph node metastasis, circumferential resection margin (CRM) status, morbidity or mortality within 30 postoperative days and postoperative hospital stay.

Preoperative treatment strategy

Patients with cT3/cT4 or node-positive rectal adenocarcinoma located distal to the peritoneal reflection received neoadjuvant radiotherapy. Patients with tumors threatening the resection margin received preoperative chemotherapy with an oxaliplatin-based regimen.

Patients who met the indications for both neoadjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy, as described above, received total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT), consisting of preoperative chemotherapy and radiotherapy. The sequence of chemotherapy and radiotherapy was determined according to the physician’s discretion or the patient’s participation in other clinical trials.

Robotic surgical technique

Patient Positioning, Docking, Port Placement, Instrumentation.

The da Vinci Xi system was introduced into Toranomon Hospital in September 2019 and the Hugo RAS system to both Toranomon Hospital and Toranomon Hospital Kajigaya in February 2024. The surgical procedures, including trocar placement and operative techniques, are identical for the two systems, except for the handling of vessel clips, vessel sealing devices, and staplers, which were not yet available for Hugo during the study period. These instruments were therefore manipulated laparoscopically by a bedside assistant during Hugo-assisted surgery.

The arrangement of devices and staff in the operating room is shown in Fig. 1. Two arm carts were positioned bilaterally, caudal to the patient, and docked at defined angles and tilt to optimize instrument access: 130° docking with − 10° tilt for the first arm, 155° with − 45° tilt for the second arm, 190° with − 30° tilt for the third arm, and 225° with 0° tilt for the fourth arm. Each docking angle was adjustable by ± 10° to accommodate patient anatomy and procedural requirements.

The arrangement of devices and staff in the operating room. Two arm carts were positioned bilaterally caudal to the patient, each docked with a defined angle and tilt. Angles could be adjusted by ± 10° depending on patient anatomy and procedure

Trocar placement is illustrated in Fig. 2. A trocar for the second arm was inserted at the umbilicus. The fourth-arm trocar was positioned medial (about 2 cm) to the right anterior superior iliac spine. The first and third robotic arm trocars were aligned in a straight configuration, with the first trocar placed slightly caudal (about 2 cm) relative to the third; this is particularly important in Hugo procedures because the forceps length is shorter. Two 5-mm assistant trocars were inserted at an appropriate distance from the robotic trocars to ensure safe instrument exchange and assistance.

Trocar placement. A trocar for the 2nd arm was inserted at the umbilicus. A trocar for the 4th arm was placed in the right lower quadrant. The 1st and 3rd arm trocars were positioned in a straight alignment, with the 1st port placed slightly caudally. Two assistant trocars were inserted cranially laterally from the 2nd and 3rd robotic trocars

The following robotic instruments were used: a bipolar grasper for the first arm (left-hand instrument), a 30° rigid endoscope for the second arm, monopolar shears for the third arm (right-hand instrument), and a grasper for the fourth arm (right-hand reserve instrument).

In procedures performed using the Hugo system, the root of the IMA was ligated with clips and divided distal to the clips using a laparoscopic ultrasonic or vessel-sealing device by the bedside assistant. Division of the distal mesorectum was performed primarily using robotic bipolar coagulation and monopolar shears, and partially using laparoscopic ultrasonic or vessel-sealing devices. After mobilization and dissection of the rectum, the fourth robotic trocar (8 mm) was undocked from the robotic arm and replaced with a 12-mm laparoscopic trocar to allow for laparoscopic rectal transection with a stapler by the bedside assistant.

All surgical procedures were conducted with the participation of surgeons qualified under the Endoscopic Surgical Skill Qualification System (ESSQS) of the Japan Society for Endoscopic Surgery.

Pathological Assessment

Pathological evaluation of resected specimens was performed according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer staging criteria. Specimens were sectioned axially at 3–5 mm intervals, particularly for those resected from patients with cT3/4 tumors or who were treated with preoperative therapy. The dissected surface was inked to ensure accurate assessment of the CRM, which was regarded positive if tumor was identified within 1 mm of the resection margin.

Statistical Analyses

The primary outcome measure was the rate of complications, defined as Clavien–Dindo (CD) grade ≥ III. Secondary endpoints were blood loss, operative time, console time, CRM positivity, postoperative hospital stay, and mortality.

Propensity scores were calculated using logistic regression with age, sex, BMI, ASA-PS, preoperative radiotherapy, cT stage, cN stage, tumor location, surgeon experience, console experience, and sphincter preservation. Matching was performed using a caliper width of 0.2 times the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. A standardized mean difference (SMD) > 0.1 was considered indicative of a meaningful imbalance.

Associations between robot type and continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

All statistical analyses were conducted using EZR [16] (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a modified version of R Commander (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) that incorporates functions commonly used in biostatistical analyses.

Results

Overall study population

A total of 846 patients with non-metastatic rectal cancer underwent surgery in this study period. Patients treated with laparoscopic (n = 620) or open surgery (n = 15) were excluded. The study cohort consisted of 211 patients who underwent robotic surgery, comprising 81 cases performed with Hugo and 130 cases with da Vinci.

ASA-PS distribution was class 1 for 64 patients, class 2 for 134 patients, class 3 for 12 patients, and class 4 for 1 patient. Tumors were located distal to the peritoneal reflection in 94 patients (44.5%), with a median distance of 8 cm. Clinical tumor depth was cT1 in 53 patients, cT2 in 56 patients, cT3 in 84 patients, and cT4 in 18 patients. Clinical N+ disease was observed in 63 patients (29.9%). A total of 50 patients (23.7%) received neoadjuvant radiotherapy, including TNT in 25 patients.

In the overall cohort, postoperative complications of CD grade ≥ III were observed in 6 patients (7.4%) in the Hugo group and 11 patients (8.5%) in the da Vinci group. Complications in the Hugo group included anastomotic leakage in 2 patients, ileal long-tube decompression requirement for ileus in 3 patients, and percutaneous drainage requirement for pelvic fluid collection in 1 patient. In the da Vinci group, complications included anastomotic leakage in 3 patients, ileal long-tube decompression requirement for ileus in 3 patients, percutaneous drainage requirement for pelvic fluid collection in 2 patients, epidural blood patch for post-dural puncture headache in 1 patient, fistula between the ileal conduit and the colon in 1 patient, and duodenal ulcer perforation in 1 patient. All complications were classified as CD grade III, except for the duodenal ulcer perforation (CD grade IV). There were no complications specific to the robotic systems for either group.

Other short-term outcomes, including postoperative hospital stay (11 vs. 12 days, p = 0.569), 30-day readmission (0% vs. 5.4%, p = 0.243), and 30-day mortality (0% vs. 0%), were comparable.

Discussion

This study encompasses the largest retrospective cohort to date to compare rectal cancer surgery performed with Hugo and da Vinci by applying propensity score matching. The rate of postoperative complications (CD grade ≥ III) was comparable for the two systems. In addition, after matching for patient, tumor, treatment, and surgeon-related factors, we found similar postoperative outcomes between the two groups, including blood loss, operative time, console time, CRM status, postoperative hospital stay, and mortality.

Our short-term outcomes were also favorable compared with data from the National Clinical Database (NCD) [17], a nationwide registry of surgical outcomes in Japan. The NCD reports short-term results for laparoscopic and robotic low anterior resection performed between 2018 and 2019, during which time da Vinci was the single robotic system available for rectal surgery in Japan. Of note, the postoperative complication rate of CD grade ≥ III in our study (7.1% for Hugo and 8.9% for da Vinci) was similar to that reported in the NCD study (9.4%).

Other perioperative outcomes in the Hugo group were similarly favorable when compared with the NCD data, including median postoperative hospital stay (11 vs. 13 days), 30-day readmission rates (0% vs. 2.4%), and 30-day mortality (0% vs. 0.1%). Notably, our cohort included a substantial number of patients undergoing high-risk procedures such as ISR, APR, TPE, and LLND, as well as a higher proportion of patients who received preoperative radiotherapy (23.7% vs. 5.0%). Despite having a more complex case mix, the short-term outcomes remained satisfactory, which reinforces the feasibility of Hugo-assisted rectal surgery as an alternative platform to da Vinci.

The operative outcomes, including blood loss, operative time, and console time, were comparable. Hugo has four independent arm carts, each requiring docking at a specific angle and tilt, which raises the possibility of a prolonged setup and operative duration. However, our results did not show a significant increase in operative time, suggesting that Hugo provides functionality similar to that of da Vinci. Furthermore, each independent robotic arm can be positioned around the patient at an optimal docking angle and tilt, enabling a configuration tailored to both the patient and the specific surgical procedure. Itatani et al. [15] reported excellent outcomes for rectal surgery using the Hugo system with a unique setting, which differs substantially from the setup employed in our study.

Surgeon experience was one of the two background factors that differed significantly between the groups before PSM. This imbalance arose because surgeons with greater experience began performing robotic surgery with da Vinci and sought to be competent before training junior surgeons. In addition, because our institution operates with two Hugo systems and one da Vinci system, trainees had more opportunities to gain console experience using Hugo than with da Vinci. Given that surgeon-related factors can substantially influence postoperative outcomes, both surgeon experience and console experience were incorporated into the propensity score model. Yet, despite the relatively limited console experience in both groups (median 20–30 cases), operative and short-term outcomes remained satisfactory, likely due in part to the presence and supervision of ESSQS-qualified surgeons. In support of this, Sawada et al. [18] reported improved postoperative outcomes in laparoscopic colorectal resections when ESSQS-qualified surgeons were involved.

Other robotic systems have been adopted by clinicians for the treatment of colorectal surgery. The Senhance Surgical System is reported to be safe for colectomy, with outcomes comparable to those achieved with laparoscopic surgery; albeit, the operative time was reported to be longer with the Senhance Surgical System [19]. Similarly, the Hinotori Surgical Robot System is reported to be as safe as surgery performed with da Vinci [20], with not only comparable postoperative outcomes but also lower costs. Together with our findings, these results suggest that emerging platforms, such as Hugo, may represent viable alternatives to da Vinci in colorectal surgery.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, it was a retrospective analysis at two hospitals with a limited sample size. The relatively small cohort raises the possibility that the study was underpowered to detect subtle differences between the Hugo and da Vinci groups. For example, the standardized mean differences (SMDs) for sex and clinical N stage exceeded the conventional threshold, even after PSM. This imbalance is most likely attributable to the limited sample size. A possible explanation for the higher SMD for sex is the limited availability of the robotic system during the early study period. When we initiated robotic rectal surgery using the da Vinci system, access to the robotic platform was restricted. Therefore, based on previous reports, we preferentially selected patients who were considered more likely to benefit from robotic surgery, such as male patients with low rectal tumors. After the introduction of two Hugo systems, we were able to perform robotic surgery more freely with Hugo, regardless of tumor location. Despite our best efforts to balance baseline characteristics by maximizing patient inclusion, complete balance was not achieved, even after matching. However, Hugo, as a new system, is accessible in only a few countries, and thus the global experience with Hugo for colorectal surgery remains modest. This study is the largest retrospective cohort study employing PSM to compare rectal cancer surgery performed with Hugo versus da Vinci to date. Therefore, although derived from a relatively small cohort, our findings provide valuable early clinical evidence regarding the applicability of this system.

Second, there were discrepancies in the timing of the introduction of these two robotic platforms at our institutions. We intentionally included cases from the early adoption phase for both platforms to capture outcomes during their initial learning curves. Excluding the early da Vinci cases to shorten the study period would have made it difficult to adequately adjust for surgeon-related factors—specifically surgeon experience and console experience—in the propensity score model. Importantly, treatment strategies and surgical techniques at our institutions remained consistent throughout the study period, mitigating the impact of temporal variability.

Third, because certain instrumentation (clips, vessel sealing system and stapler) were not yet available robotically for Hugo RAS platform during the study period, several procedural steps required a laparoscopic approach; the assistant must be sufficiently skilled to perform these laparoscopic steps. In contrast, procedures performed with the da Vinci system are likely less technically demanding for the assistant.

Fourth, the patient population included individuals with relatively low BMI. The cohort had a median BMI of 22.7 kg/m², consistent with the Japanese population and lower than that observed in many Western populations. This lower BMI may have contributed to our favorable perioperative results, including minimal blood loss and a low conversion rate. Nevertheless, our cohort also included a patient with a BMI of 40.5 who successfully underwent robotic rectal surgery without conversion. We believe that our extensive institutional experience with laparoscopic rectal surgery, the involvement of ESSQS-qualified surgeons, and a strong educational environment contributed to these outcomes. Additionally, the open console design and hand controls of the Hugo system—features that resemble laparoscopic instruments—facilitated intraoperative teaching and communication. With ESSQS-qualified supervision in all cases, short-term outcomes remained satisfactory even when trainee surgeons were the primary operators.

Conclusion

The Hugo RAS system demonstrates short-term safety outcomes and operative feasibility similar to those achieved using the da Vinci surgical system. Our results suggest that emerging platforms, such as Hugo, represent viable alternative platforms for rectal cancer surgery.
