Prevalence and health checkup-based detection of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease in Japanese young adults
Authors: Satoko Tajirika, Takao Miwa, Tatsunori Hanai, Masahito Shimizu, Mayumi Yamamoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe aimed to elucidate the prevalence of steatotic liver disease (SLD) in Japanese young adults and to identify health checkup-based markers for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). We recruited participants (18–29 years) attending a health checkup. Abdominal ultrasonography and health checkup data were used to diagnose and classify SLD. The diagnostic ability of serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT), fatty liver index (FLI), and hepatic steatosis index (HSI) were evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis. Among the 1632 participants in 2023, 1004 (62%) were male, and SLD was observed in 271 (17%). In the SLD subclass, 143 (9%), 1 (< 1%), 0 (0%), and 127 (8%) were diagnosed with MASLD, MASLD with moderate alcohol intake, alcohol-associated/related liver disease, and cryptogenic SLD, respectively. The area under the curve of ALT, FLI, and HSI to identify MASLD were 0.76, 0.85, and 0.84, respectively. The cutoff value of ALT to rule in (specificity 0.90) MASLD was 31 IU/L. Similar results were observed in 1743 participants in 2024. In conclusion, the prevalence of SLD and MASLD in Japanese young adults was 17% and 9%, respectively. ALT > 30 IU/L was identified as a reasonable cutoff value to identify MASLD in Japanese young adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26346-w.Keywords: Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, MetALD, Steatotic liver disease, Young adult, Nara DeclarationSubject terms: Health care, Medical research, Liver, Gastroenterology, Hepatology, Liver diseases, Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease

Introduction

Due to the increase in metabolic risk factors, alcohol consumption, and aging, the global prevalence and disease burden of steatotic liver disease (SLD) have dramatically increased1. Although the antiviral treatment of viral hepatitis has been established, the prevalence of SLD is on the rise. A recent survey revealed that SLD is now the leading cause of cirrhosis and a major health concern that needs to be addressed in the Japanese population2. SLD due to metabolic abnormalities, namely nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), has traditionally been recognized as a disease that affects middle-aged and older adults with increased metabolic risk factors. However, recent studies have revealed that the prevalence and mortality of NAFLD are increasing, even in adolescents and young adults3. A reasonable explanation for these trends includes lifestyle changes in the younger generation and that NAFLD contributes to cardiovascular events, leading to a shorter life span4. The presence of NAFLD in children and young adults increases the risk of cardiovascular diseases and worsens the long-term mortality rates of these patients5. Therefore, early detection and intervention, including lifestyle modification, are essential for young adults with NAFLD to maximize healthy life expectancy.

In June 2023, a global panel of experts renamed NAFLD as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) to accurately explain the disease pathophysiology and eliminate the stigma6. Previous studies showed that evidence of NAFLD can be applied to that of MASLD because the characteristics of NAFLD and MASLD are mostly identical7,8; however, very few reports are available on the prevalence of MASLD, especially in young adults. We hypothesized that the concordance rate between NAFLD and MASLD could be low in younger adults at low risk of cardiometabolic criteria.

In Japan, an annual health checkup for university students is provided based on the School Health and Safety Act. The health checkup at school is an important opportunity for the early detection of MASLD at an early stage of life. Although medical resources, including time, cost, and manpower, are limited for population-based health checkups, it is necessary to establish simple and accurate screening methods to identify MASLD. The simple measurement of serum alanine aminotransaminase (ALT), fatty liver index (FLI), and hepatic steatosis index (HSI) are considered reliable methods to identify MASLD9–11; however, it remains unclear whether these screening tools are applicable in young adults.

This study aimed to clarify the prevalence of SLD according to the new definition, especially focusing on MASLD, in Japanese young adults. In addition, we validated the ability of ALT, FLI, and HSI to identify MASLD in Japanese young adults to establish a simple screening method that can be applied in a routine health checkup setting.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study included undergraduate and graduate students at Gifu University (Gifu, Japan) who underwent an annual health checkup in April 2023 or April 2024. The following inclusion criteria were applied: incoming students at Gifu University Undergraduate or Graduate Program of 2023 or 2024, aged 18–29 years, mother language of Japanese, and completion of the annual health checkup. International students and participants with missing data were excluded.

All study procedures complied with the ethical requirements of the national and institutional committees that oversee human studies, in addition to the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later revisions. The Ethical Review Committee of the Graduate School of Medicine, Gifu University, Japan has reviewed and approved the protocol of this study (approval no. 2021-B167). We obtained written informed consent from all participants.

Diagnosis of SLD

Liver steatosis was evaluated using abdominal ultrasonography (Aplio i800®; CANON Medical Systems Corporation, Ohtawara city, Tochigi, Japan or LOGIQ P10®; GE HealthCare Japan Corporation, Hino city, Tokyo, Japan) by a hepatologist certified by the Japanese Society of Hepatology or a clinical laboratory technologist with more than 10 years of specialized training, both of whom were blinded to the results of this study. After the initial screening, two hepatologists independently reviewed the ultrasonography results. The diagnosis of SLD, based on established image findings (bright liver, liver-to-kidney contrast, vascular blurring, and deep beam attenuation on B-mode ultrasonography)12, was confirmed only when both hepatologists reached a consensus, thereby minimizing operator-dependent variability.

Diagnosis of MASLD and other subcategories of SLD

Participants with SLD were divided into four subgroups: MASLD, MASLD with moderate alcohol intake (MetALD), alcohol-associated/related liver disease (ALD), and cryptogenic SLD, based on the new nomenclature6,13. Cardiometabolic criteria were evaluated as follows: overweight (body mass index [BMI] ≥ 23 kg/m2 or waist circumference >94 cm in males and >80 cm in females), impaired glucose tolerance (fasting serum glucose levels ≥ 100 mg/dl, or occasional glucose levels ≥ 140 mg/dl, or hemoglobin A1c ≥ 5.7%, or type 2 diabetes), hypertension (blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg), hypertriglyceridemia (serum triglycerides [TG] ≥ 150 mg/dl), low high-density lipoprotein (HDL)-cholesterol (serum HDL-cholesterol ≤ 40 mg/dL in males and ≤ 50 mg/dL in females), or any specific treatment related to these conditions. Participants with SLD and more than one of the cardiometabolic criteria were divided into MASLD or MetALD based on the amount of alcohol consumption: ≤140 g/week for females and ≤ 210 g/week for males in MASLD and 140–350 g/week for females and 210–420 g/week for males in MetALD. Participants with SLD and alcohol consumption of more than the allowed amount for MetALD or those without cardiometabolic criteria and alcohol consumption of more than the allowed amount for MASLD were diagnosed with ALD. Drinking within the MASLD limit was defined as mild alcohol drinking. Participants with any other cause of SLD were diagnosed with cryptogenic SLD13.

Assessment of biochemical parameters

Biochemical assessments included aspartate aminotransferase (AST), ALT, gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (γ-GTP), TG, HDL-cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol, HbA1c, fasting or occasional glucose levels, uric acid, albumin, and platelet count at the time of the health checkup.

Assessment of FLI and HSI

FLI was evaluated according to the following formula: (e 0.953*loge (TG) + 0.139*BMI + 0.718*loge (γ-GTP) + 0.053*waist circumference - 15.745)/(1 + e 0.953*loge (TG) + 0.139*BMI + 0.718*loge (γ-GTP) + 0.053*waist circumference - 15.745) * 10010. Originally, the rule-out and rule-in cutoff values of FLI were 30 and 60, respectively. HSI was evaluated using the formula: 8*(ALT/AST ratio) + BMI (+ 2, if female; +2, if diabetes mellitus)11. Originally, the rule-out and rule-in cutoff values of HSI were 30 and 36, respectively. Due to the lack of γ-GTP in the 2024 cohort, FLI was only assessed in the 2023 cohort.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages (%). The groups were compared using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables. Post-hoc analysis was performed using the Bonferroni’s method. The discriminative ability of each variable to identify MASLD was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and the results were presented as the area under the curve (AUC) with a 95% confidence interval. The optimal cutoff value to identify MASLD was estimated using the Youden index. In addition, the cutoff values for 90% sensitivity and 90% specificity were the rule-out and rule-in cutoff values, respectively. The discriminative ability was shown by the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value. DeLong’s test was used to compare ROC curves. The results were first assessed in the 2023 cohort and validated in the 2024 cohort. All tests were two-sided, and the significance threshold was p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro® version 17.0.0 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.4.1 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Prevalence of SLD and subgroups based on the new nomenclature

Of the incoming students in 2023 and 2024, 1632 students in 2023 and 1743 students in 2024 were included in the study (Supplementary Fig. S1). Among the 2023 cohort, 271 (17%) were diagnosed with SLD, and MASLD, MetALD, ALD, and cryptogenic SLD were diagnosed in 143 (9%), 1 (< 1%), 0 (0%), and 127 (8%), respectively (Fig. 1a). A sex difference in the SLD subtypes was observed. The prevalence of SLD were 17% and 16% in males and females and 11% of males and 6% of females met the diagnostic criteria of MASLD (Fig. 1b and c). Similar results were obtained in the validation cohort of 2024 (Fig. 1d to f). To avoid the effects of excessive drinking, 13 participants who took alcohol above the MASLD limit (over 20 g/day for females or 30 g/day for males) were excluded from subsequent analyses.

Clinical characteristics of the participants

The characteristics of the remaining 1622 participants in the 2023 cohort are presented in Table 1. The median age was 18 years, and 62% were male. The median waist circumference and BMI were 69 cm and 20.4 kg/m2, respectively. Regarding the cardiometabolic criteria, the prevalence of overweight, impaired glucose tolerance, hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, and low HDL-cholesterol was 19%, 2%, 15%, 11%, and 7%, respectively. The median values of ALT, FLI, and HSI were 16, 4, and 27, respectively. Similar results were observed in the remaining 1739 participants in the 2024 cohort (Table 2).

Comparison between each SLD status

A comparison between each SLD status in the 2023 cohort is also presented in Table 1. Between those without SLD, cryptogenic SLD, and MASLD, there was a significant difference in sex, BMI, and waist circumference. In addition, the prevalence of overweight, impaired glucose tolerance, hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, and lower HDL-cholesterol differed according to disease definition. Furthermore, participants with MASLD had significantly higher ALT, FLI, and HSI than those without SLD or those with cryptogenic SLD. Although similar associations were found in the sex-stratified analyses, there were no significant differences in impaired glucose tolerance, AST, γ-GTP, uric acid, low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol, and albumin levels in females (Supplementary Table S1). Similar results were shown in the 2024 cohort (Table 2 and Supplementary Table S2).

Discriminative ability of ALT to identify MASLD

In the 2023 cohort, the ROC analyses indicated that the AUC for ALT to identify MASLD was 0.76 for the total population, 0.81 for males, and 0.63 for females, respectively (Fig. 2a to c). Similar results were obtained in the validation cohort of 2024 (Fig. 2d to f).

Discriminative ability of ALT to identify MASLD in (a) the total cohort, (b) males, and (c) females of 2023 and in (d) total cohort, (e) males, and (f) females of 2024. Abbreviations: ALT, alanine aminotransferase; AUC, area under the curve; CI, confidence interval; MASLD, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease.

The discriminative ability of ALT to identify MASLD is shown in Table 3. In the 2023 cohort, the optimal cutoff value of ALT for identifying MASLD was 27 IU/L for the total population, 34 IU/L for males, and 16 IU/L for females. In addition, the cutoff value of ALT to rule-in (specificity 0.90) MASLD was 31 IU/L for the total population, 34 IU/L for males, and 23 IU/L for females. The rule-in cutoff values in the 2024 cohort showed similar results. In contrast, the rule-out (sensitivity 0.90) cutoff value in the 2023 cohort was 12 IU/L for the total population, 14 IU/L for males, and 9 IU/L for females. The diagnostic performance of ALT, assessed by AUC, was relatively lower for SLD than for MASLD (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Supplementary Table S3).

Similarly, the AUC for HSI to identify MASLD was 0.84 for the total population, 0.86 for males, and 0.79 for females, respectively (Fig. 3a to c). There was no significant difference between the AUCs of FLI and HSI. In addition, similar results for HSI were observed in the 2024 cohort (Fig. 3d to f).

Discussion

There is little information available about the prevalence and clinical characteristics of SLD in young adults. Our study demonstrated the prevalence of SLD to be 17% in Japanese young adults with a median age of 18 years. In addition, 9% of them were diagnosed with MASLD, whereas very few participants had MetALD or ALD. Furthermore, ALT, FLI, and HSI were valuable markers for identifying MASLD in a routine health checkup setting. This study contains important epidemiological data in the context of SLD in the young population. Moreover, the study suggests easy-to-use tools to identify MASLD in a health checkup setting.

In our study, which included a young Japanese cohort, the prevalence of SLD was 17%. In addition, MASLD, MetALD, ALD, and cryptogenic SLD were diagnosed with 9%, < 1%, 0%, and 8%, respectively, based on the new nomenclature. In the general population in the United States, the prevalence of MASLD, MetALD, ALD, and cryptogenic SLD was reported to be 31.3%, 2%, 0.7%, and 0.03%, respectively14. Although a similar prevalence of SLD subclasses was reported in the Japanese general population4,15,16, very few reports have the prevalence in young adults. In the United States, the prevalence of NAFLD was reported to be 13.2% in early and middle adolescents (aged 12–17 years), 18.7% in late adolescents (aged 18–24 years), and 24.0% in young adults (aged 25–29 years)17, all of which are higher than that of MASLD shown in our study. In addition, almost no participants had MetALD or ALD in our study. This seems reasonable because the legal drinking age in Japan is 20 years, and most of the participants were underage in our study.

The prevalence of MASLD in Japanese young adults was lower than that of NAFLD reported in previous studies17–19. Compared to the study in the United States, differences in race and lifestyle, regarding food culture or health education can be reasonable explanations for the distinct prevalence observed in our study17. Another important factor is the low concordance rate between NAFLD and MASLD in the younger generation. In a previous study including patients with NAFLD with a median age of 57 years, almost all patients fulfilled the criteria of MASLD (96.7%)8. In contrast, 270 participants were diagnosed with NAFLD, but only 143 (53%) of them met the criteria of MASLD in this study. The low concordance between NAFLD and MASLD seems reasonable because previous studies show that many individuals with NAFLD do not fulfill the cardiometabolic criteria in the young generation18–20. In fact, only 67% of children with NAFLD met the diagnostic criteria for MASLD in a study in Italy20. Our study suggests that the evidence for NAFLD may not apply to MASLD, particularly in the younger population. Sex difference of hormonal effect may also affect the distinct concordance between SLD subtypes in each sex21. In addition, the natural course of participants diagnosed with cryptogenic SLD in the absence of cardiometabolic criteria remains of great interest, as their long-term outcomes are largely unknown. Therefore, future studies should confirm our study results and further elucidate the characteristics of MASLD in the younger population.

Another important finding in our study is that we validated the usefulness of health checkup indexes, including ALT, FLI, and HSI, to identify MASLD in young adults. The health checkup is an excellent opportunity for the early detection of SLD in young adults, providing an opportunity for lifestyle improvement. Ultrasonography is minimally invasive and widely used for screening SLD; however, it is impractical to provide liver ultrasonography to all individuals undergoing a medical checkup in the viewpoint of cost effectiveness, time consuming, and staff shortage. Therefore, an easy-to-use marker to effectively identify MASLD during health checkups is an unmet clinical need. We found that the serum ALT levels have a favorable ability to identify MASLD in Japanese young adults, with a rule-in cutoff value of 31 IU/L. The sex-stratified analysis identified 34 IU/L in males and 23 IU/L in females as rule-in cutoff values, which are similar to the guideline recommendation22. This value corresponds with the recommendation by the Japan Society of Hepatology, known as the Nara Declaration, suggesting that ALT >30 IU/L can be used to identify chronic liver disease23. Therefore, our results support the existing recommendation and further refine them in terms of MASLD in younger generation. Similarly, FLI and HSI were also useful with high AUCs for identifying MASLD, which have already been reported as useful indexes to identify NAFLD24,25. Our study showed that FLI and HSI have a favorable ability to identify MASLD in Japanese young adults. Although both FLI and HSI have similar ability in identifying MASLD, a sex-related difference in the cutoff values was found, especially in FLI26,27. In addition, since the HSI does not require γ-GTP, it may be a more convenient index for use in health checkups compared to the FLI. Interestingly, these indexes demonstrated lower ability to identify SLD, including cases without high-risk factors, than MASLD, which is defined by cardiometabolic criteria28,29. Therefore, focusing on MASLD using these indexes alleviates concerns about overdiagnosis of SLD and about issues of cost-effectiveness. Moreover, because younger individuals tend to be more receptive to lifestyle modifications, early identification of MASLD in this population may be particularly valuable in reducing the risk of progression to MASH over time.

This study had some limitations. First, hepatic steatosis was diagnosed only by B-mode ultrasonography; therefore, objective evaluation methods should be applied in future studies. In addition, since liver biopsy was not performed in this study, the assessment of MASH was not feasible. Second, this was a single-center study that included university students in Japan and excluded those with alcohol consumption beyond the MASLD limit. Therefore, the results may not apply to other age groups, races, regions, and those with more alcohol consumption. A multicenter study, including an international cohort with the assessment of fibrosis, is required to validate the results of our study. Despite these limitations, our study has several strengths, including a large sample size, the assessment of laboratory data and liver ultrasound, which are generally not evaluated in this generation, and the evaluation of a validation cohort, all of which strengthen the robustness of the study.

In conclusion, the prevalence of SLD and MASLD in Japanese young adults was 17% and 9%, respectively, whereas very few participants with MetALD and ALD were identified. Our study provides important epidemiological data on the prevalence of SLD based on the new nomenclature in Japanese young adults. In addition, measuring ALT, FLI, and HSI at the health checkup is valuable in identifying MASLD in Japanese young adults; ALT > 30 IU/L was determined to be a reasonable cutoff value.
