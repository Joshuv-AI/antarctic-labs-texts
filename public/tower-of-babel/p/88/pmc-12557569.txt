Influence of a diaphragmatic fatigue protocol in healthy male population on muscle strength, respiratory function and exercise capacity: A randomized clinical trial
Authors: Marta de-la-Plaza-San-Frutos, Alberto Sánchez Sierra, Ángel González de la Flor, Diego Domínguez-Balmaseda, María Bravo Aguilar, José Ángel Del Blanco Múñiz, Beatriz Ruiz Ruiz, Arturo Ladriñán Maestro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInspiratory muscle fatigue has been shown to impair physical performance. This study investigated the impact of a specific inspiratory muscle fatigue protocol on lower limb muscle strength and cardiorespiratory outcomes in a cohort of physically active individuals.MethodA randomized controlled trial, adhering to CONSORT guidelines to ensure methodological rigor. Healthy male participants were randomly assigned to an inspiratory muscle fatigue group (EG) or control load group (CG). The EG performed a diaphragmatic fatigue protocol involving inspiratory exercises at 60 % of their maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP), while the CG performed two sets of 30 repetitions at 15 % of MIP. Measurements, including countermovement jump (CMJ), forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), FEV1/FVC ratio, half squat (HS), peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) and MIP, were collected before and immediately after the intervention.ResultsForty participants completed the study. Significant group-by-time interactions were found for CMJ (p < 0.001; η2p = 0.405), FEV1 (p = 0.030; η2p = 0.118), FEV1/FVC (p < 0.001; η2p = 0.279) and HS (p < 0.001; η2p = 0.332), all with moderate to large effect sizes. Post-hoc analysis revealed a significant decrease in CMJ in the EG (p < 0.001, −8.4 %), while the CG showed no significant change (p = 0.092). FEV1/FVC decreased significantly in the EG (p = 0.007, −5.3 %), while the CG showed a significant increase (p = 0.015, +4.7 %). Similarly, HS decreased significantly in the EG (p = 0.002, −6.3 %), while the CG showed a significant increase (p = 0.007, +5.4 %). No significant interactions were observed for SpO2, heart rate, MIP or FVC.ConclusionsDiaphragmatic fatigue significantly impairs lower limb strength in physically active individuals, potentially compromising athletic performance and increasing injury risk. These findings enhance understanding of the interplay between respiratory and muscular strength in athletes.

Introduction

During high-intensity exercise, the ability of the respiratory system to deliver oxygen to active muscles becomes a primary determinant of physical performance. The respiratory system (comprising the diaphragm, intercostal muscles and accessory muscles) plays a fundamental role in regulating thoracic pressure, ventilation and oxygen transport. The diaphragm, in particular, is the principal inspiratory muscle, accounting for approximately 75 % of the ventilatory effort.1

In recent years, inspiratory muscle training (IMT) has emerged as a key strategy to enhance respiratory function in diverse populations, including patients with respiratory diseases,2 individuals with neuromuscular conditions3 and high-performance athletes.4 IMT has been shown to improve pulmonary function, increase maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max) and promote diaphragmatic hypertrophy and neuromuscular control.5

High-intensity exercise may induce diaphragmatic fatigue, thereby triggering the respiratory metaboreflex. This physiological mechanism, mediated by the sympathetic nervous system, induces vasoconstriction in peripheral muscles to prioritize blood flow to the respiratory muscles and maintain ventilatory function.6 This redistribution of blood flow may compromise lower-limb force generation and impair overall physical performance.7 While previous studies have reported that IMT can delay the onset of the metaboreflex and enhance exercise tolerance,8 most research has focused on its effects in aerobic endurance sports, leaving a notable gap regarding its impact on explosive strength and neuromuscular performance in the lower limbs.

Despite increasing evidence linking respiratory fatigue to athletic performance, no study has specifically examined the acute effects of diaphragmatic fatigue on lower-limb strength and respiratory function in a controlled, randomized setting. Although previous research suggests that respiratory muscle fatigue may impair strength and power performance, the exact physiological mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. Therefore, this study aims to analyze the acute effects of inspiratory muscle fatigue on lower-limb performance, assessed through the countermovement jump (CMJ), in physically active individuals. Additional objectives include evaluating its impact on lower-limb strength (Half Squat), blood oxygen saturation (SpO2), pulmonary function and maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP). The findings of this study may yield valuable insights for sports physiotherapy and performance optimization, underscoring the importance of incorporating respiratory training into comprehensive strength and injury-prevention programs for athletes.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a randomized clinical trial design, conducted at the Physiotherapy Laboratory of the European University of Madrid (Madrid, Spain), following the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for methodological rigor9 and was prospectively registered on the ANZCTR on July 11, 2023 (Registration number: ACTRN12623000754640).

Ethical approval

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of University Rey Juan Carlos (Registry Number: 2501202306223). All participants provided written informed consent prior to data collection. The procedures complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human participants.10

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using G*Power Software (3.1.9.2), based on MIP scores from previous study (12) with an alpha error of 0.05, a beta error of 0.2 and a medium effect size (f = 0.25 or partial Eta squared, η2p = 0.06). Accounting for an anticipated 10 % dropout rate, the final sample size was 40 participants, randomized into two groups (n = 20 per group).

Participants

A total of 40 male participants were recruited from Universidad Europea de Madrid via social media announcements. Inclusion criteria were: age 18–45 years, recreationally active (≥150 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity for ≥1 year, per WHO guidelines) and non-smokers. Exclusion criteria included: musculoskeletal or neurological disorders limiting physical activity, cognitive impairment, cardiorespiratory disease or arterial hypertension, lower-limb surgery within the previous 12 months and active episodes of lower-limb pain.

Participants were randomly assigned to either inspiratory muscle fatigue group (EG) or an active control group, the control load group (CG), using the randomization.com program. An independent researcher conducted the randomization, ensuring allocation concealment. Both the outcome assessor and the data analyst were blinded to group allocation.

Intervention

The EG underwent a diaphragmatic fatigue protocol using an inspiratory threshold-loading device (Big Breathe®, GH Innotek, South Korea). The inspiratory load was set at 60 % MIP and maintained until fatigue, defined as the inability to sustain airflow for three consecutive maximal inspiratory efforts (9).

The 60 % MIP intensity was selected based on prior evidence demonstrating its capacity to induce diaphragmatic fatigue while preserving ventilatory function. Inspiratory loads between 55 and 60 % of MIP effectively induce inspiratory muscle fatigue over extended efforts (13). Additionally, progressive training protocols at 60–80 % MIP have led to significant improvements in inspiratory muscle endurance (14). Furthermore, inspiratory warm-up studies further support this load as effective for enhancing subsequent respiratory muscle performance (15). Therefore, this protocol was deemed suitable for eliciting controlled diaphragmatic fatigue in healthy individuals.

The CG performed an inspiratory low-load protocol consisting of two sets of 30 repetitions at 15 % MIP using the same device as the EG, as described in previous studies (16).

In summary, the EG performed inspiratory efforts at 60 % MIP until they were unable to complete three consecutive repetitions, while the CG completed 30 repetitions at 15 % MIP. Both groups completed the intervention in a single session, followed immediately by post-intervention outcome assessments.

Outcome measures

Measurements were collected at baseline and immediately post-intervention in the following order: age, height, weight, CMJ, HS, SpO2, pulmonary function and MIP.

Primary outcomes

Countermovement jump

The CMJ test began from a standing position, hands placed on hips, followed by a rapid upward jump initiated through knee flexion and extension. This plyometric movement followed an eccentric-isometric-concentric sequence.

CMJ measurements were obtained through the MyJump2 smartphone application, recorded by an iPhone 14 Pro camera (Apple Inc., USA) at 240 Hz and processed via MyJump2 app. Jump power (watts) and height (centimeters) were recorded. Each participant completed two trials separated by 2 min of rest, and the best result was analyzed.11

Secondary outcomes

Half squat

HS was performed with a load equivalent to 80 % of body weight. Two attempts were completed, with the best performance recorded. A 3-min rest interval was provided between trials. The Smith multipower machine (Technogym, Gambettola, Italy) was used and movement velocity (m/s) was estimated via a Vitruve linear position transducer (Vitruve, Madrid, Spain).12

Pulmonary function tests

Pulmonary function tests were conducted using the Air Smart Spirometer (Pond Healthcare Innovation, Sweden) following American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) standards. Variables included forced vital capacity (FVC) and forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), expressed in milliliters, as well as the FEV1/FVC ratio.13

Maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP)

MIP was objectively assessed utilizing the MicroRPM® respiratory pressure measurement device (MicroMedical, UK) in a seated position. In order to facilitate airflow through the mouth, nasal occlusion was applied. Participants performed up to six maneuvers, with 1-min rest intervals. The highest value within 5 % variation across three consecutive attempts was recorded.14

Peripheral oxygen saturation (SPO2)

SpO2 was measured using the Nonin® 3230 device (Nonin Medical, Inc., Plymouth, MN, USA), a validated pulse oximeter that utilizes non-invasive photoplethysmography technology to estimate SpO2. The device was placed on the index finger of the dominant hand, ensuring proper sensor positioning and minimizing external interferences such as excessive movement or poor perfusion. Measurements were taken in a seated position, with participants instructed to remain still and avoid unnecessary movements. The mean SpO2 value was calculated from continuous recordings obtained during the 10 min prior to the intervention and during the recovery phase of each session to ensure data consistency and reliability.15

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS v.29 software for Windows (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test for normality and histograms were examined. Data were reported as mean and standard deviation. Independent t-tests were performed to compare both groups at baseline. Homoscedasticity and sphericity were checked. When the assumptions were met, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) 2 × 2 was carried out. The effect size was estimated with partial eta squared (η2p), interpreting values of 0.01 (small), 0.06 (medium) and 0.14 (large). Percentage of change was calculated as ((baseline/post-intervention)/baseline) * 100. Effect sizes were interpreted using conventional thresholds, with d = 0.2 considered a small effect, d = 0.5 a medium effect, and d = 0.8 or greater a large effect. A 95 % confidence interval was set.

In the post hoc analysis for the primary outcome, CMJ showed a significant decrease in the EG (p < 0.001, d = 0.73), whereas no significant change was found in the CG (p = 0.092). For the secondary outcomes, no significant differences were detected in SPO2 for either the EG (p = 0.154) or the CG (p = 0.443). Similarly, HR showed no significant changes in the EG (p = 0.926) or the CG (p = 0.166).

A significant increase in MIP was found in both the EG (p = 0.004, d = −0.31) and the CG (p = 0.016). FVC improved significantly only in the EG (p = 0.042), but not in the CG (p = 0.391). FEV1 did not change significantly in the EG (p = 0.836) but showed a significant reduction in the CG (p = 0.002). The FEV1/FVC ratio decreased significantly in the EG (p = 0.007, d = 0.48), while the CG showed a significant increase (p = 0.015).

Finally, HS showed a significant decrease in the EG (p = 0.002, d = 0.48) but a significant increase in the CG (p = 0.007).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to explore the relationship between specific diaphragmatic fatigue and lower limb strength in healthy individuals. Our findings indicate that a protocol focusing on inducing diaphragmatic fatigue, targeting only the inspiratory musculature, negatively affects on lower limb muscle strength. Our results demonstrated significant changes in vertical jump height (p < 0.001) and squat execution speed (p < 0.001) in the EG compared to the CG. Conversely, the CG exhibited significant changes in FEV1 (p = 0.03) and FEV1/FVC ratio (p < 0.001) compared to the EG. These findings underscore that targeted and objective engagement of the inspiratory musculature can exert a significant influence not only on pulmonary function but also on peripheral muscle performance (in this case, the lower limbs) and, consequently, on overall physical performance. This is particularly evident in task-specific outcomes such as vertical jump height and squat execution speed, where inspiratory muscle fatigue was associated with performance deterioration, whereas its activation yielded measurable improvements. Accordingly, these results suggest that the inspiratory musculature should be regarded as a critical determinant in the domains of physical and sports performance, as well as in rehabilitation practice, given its multifaceted effects extending beyond respiratory function to encompass physical and functional capacities.

Current evidence on diaphragmatic fatigue protocols involving a control group remains scarce. An important methodological consideration of our study is the use of an active comparator (CG) instead of a passive control group. This approach has been previously adopted in inspiratory muscle training research, as applying a subthreshold load (e.g., 10–20 % of MIP) controls for nonspecific factors such as device familiarization, respiratory pattern practice and participant expectation. By using this comparator, the differences found between groups can be more confidently attributed to the physiological effects of the fatigue protocol rather than to the simple performance of repeated breathing maneuvers. However, we believe that the use of an active comparator enhances the robustness and clinical relevance of our findings. Several studies in the scientific literature have used threshold devices to induce fatigue in the inspiratory muscles in order to evaluate its systemic effects, particularly those affecting peripheral musculature.16,17 To induce respiratory muscle fatigue, the protocol used in this study is similar to that employed in previous research, in which the presence of fatigue was demonstrated through the assessment of intraoral pressures and the use of ultrasound-based markers.18,19 Previous literature often associates diaphragmatic muscle fatigue with lower limbs.7 This relationship has typically been evaluated in terms of reduced leg blood flow and decreased MIP.

The metaboreflex theory offers a possible explanation, suggesting that excessive contractile demand on the respiratory muscles leads to fatigue, triggering afferent signals to the brainstem respiratory centers. This response induces peripheral vasoconstriction and redistributes blood flow from limb muscles to the respiratory muscles.6 As a consequence, there is an increase in the perception of effort, a reduction in maximal oxygen consumption and limb blood flow, heightened sympathetic nervous system activation and a decrease in peripheral muscle oxygen saturation, resulting in greater muscle fatigue and a diminished capacity of these structures to generate force.19,20

In contrast to some previous studies21 our results did not show significant changes in MIP (p = 0.725) for both the EG and the CG. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the intervention protocol. Other studies combined maximal exercise tests with overall muscle fatigue and intense cardiorespiratory activity (using a threshold device). The sequence of post-intervention tests commenced with the battery of muscular strength assessments, concluding with the evaluation of MIP. This sequencing may influence the identification of results concerning MIP, considering the rapid recovery capability of the diaphragm previously demonstrated in other studies.22

Regarding lung function, our results demonstrate a statistically significant decrease in FEV1 (p = 0.03) in the CG compared to the EG. Additionally, there is a statistically significant improvement (p < 0.001) in the FEV1/FVC ratio in the CG compared to the EG, but no significant changes in FVC (p = 0.389). These findings align with previous studies16 that employed similar intensities for both groups (60 % MIP for EG and 10–15 % for CG). It is worth noting that these values fall within the normal range according to spirometric reference values.23

In terms of lower limb strength, significant reductions in vertical jump height and squat execution speed were observed. While previous research has investigated leg blood flow,7 sports performance24 and aerobic and anaerobic capacity,25 none have specifically investigated peripheral muscle strength in isolation.

CMJ is a widely used in sports and rehabilitation fields to assess lower limb strength and fatigue.26 The results obtained regarding the negative impact of inspiratory muscle fatigue on CMJ height are consistent with previous studies that conducted the same intervention.20 Additionally, strength training by velocity and execution speed has been shown to be valuable markers for fatigue and muscle strength assessment, as supported by various studies.25,27 Once again, the diaphragmatic fatigue induced in the EG of our study demonstrates its influence, with a statistically significant decrease in execution speed (p < 0.001) for the EG compared to the CG. These findings reinforce prior evidence on the relationship between inspiratory muscle fatigue and various aspects of physical performance. The observed reductions in peripheral muscle strength can be linked to previous studies reporting diminished limb blood flow, reduced maximal oxygen consumption7 and declines in sports performance.28,29 Thus, it can objectively and specifically highlight the interaction between respiratory musculature and peripheral musculature.

Regarding SpO2 and heart rate, no significant changes were observed (p = 0.634 and p = 0.357, respectively). This lack of significant changes may be due to the fact that such adaptations occur over a longer period and require a lengthier intervention, as suggested by available literature, typically around 6 weeks.30,31 Furthermore, no adverse effects were reported by any of the study participants, underscoring the safety of this type of intervention, as demonstrated in multiple previous studies (33).

Limitations

The primary limitation of this study is that the sample was composed exclusively of males. Additionally, only a single post-intervention measurement was collected, which limits the ability to determine the duration of diaphragmatic fatigue effects or the full recovery timeline. Furthermore, incorporating a specific passive test, such as cervical phrenic stimulation, alongside transdiaphragmatic pressure measurement and performing MIP assessment before other post-intervention tests, could have yielded more precise evidence regarding diaphragmatic fatigue. Finally, this study focused exclusively on lower-limb strength, without evaluating potential interactions with upper-limb and trunk musculature.

Clinical applications

The findings of this study highlight the importance of assessing and addressing respiratory musculature in physiotherapy interventions, given its direct interaction with the lower limb function. In this context, the negative impact of respiratory muscle fatigue warrants attention in rehabilitation, as the role of the respiratory musculature is often overlooked in managing peripheral musculoskeletal disorders across diverse clinical populations. Recognizing this influence could provide an additional therapeutic target for treatment. Regarding sports performance, the peripheral effects of respiratory muscle activity may offer a novel perspective for both the prevention and management of injuries (particularly those involving the lower limbs) and could serve as a key factor in optimizing athletic performance. Further research is necessary to strengthen the evidence in this field.

Conclusions

These findings suggest that diaphragmatic fatigue may impair muscular strength in non-smoking athletes. This could have important implications for athletic performance and injury prevention. Notably, studies like this contribute to the broader scientific understanding of the relationship between respiratory and muscular strength.
