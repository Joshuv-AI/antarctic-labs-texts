Sequence-based analysis of Theileria annulata reveals high Tams1 gene diversity and conservation of 18 S rRNA in Türkiye
Authors: Onur Ceylan, Muhammed Ahmed Selcuk, Ceylan Ceylan, Ferda Sevinc
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTheileria annulata, a tick-borne apicomplexan parasite, causes tropical theileriosis in cattle, leading to significant economic losses in endemic regions. In our previous study, T. annulata was detected in various bovine blood samples via sequencing, despite negative results from conventional species-specific PCR assays, suggesting potential sequence variation in diagnostic target regions. Building upon this observation, the present study aimed to investigate the genetic diversity and population structure of T. annulata isolates from Türkiye by analyzing two molecular markers with differing levels of variability: the conserved 18 S rRNA gene and the polymorphic Tams1 gene.ResultsA total of 36 bovine blood DNA samples previously confirmed as T. annulata-positive were analyzed using PCR and Sanger sequencing. All 36 sequences from the 18 S rRNA gene (717 bp) were identical, forming a single haplotype, and indicating strong conservation of this locus. In contrast, the Tams1 gene (584 bp) yielded 27 distinct haplotypes among 34 sequences, demonstrating high haplotype diversity (Hd = 0.988) and moderate nucleotide diversity (π = 0.0307). Neutrality tests revealed a significantly positive Tajima’s D (2.04089; P < 0.05) and a strongly negative Fu’s Fs (–5.685; P = 0.002) for the Tams1 region, indicating the presence of balancing selection and potential population expansion. Phylogenetic and haplotype network analyses further demonstrated significant genetic divergence within the Tams1 gene region, suggesting the presence of multiple divergent lineages and localized genetic structuring among isolates.ConclusionThis study represents one of the few sequence-based efforts to characterize the genetic structure of T. annulata in Türkiye using both conserved and polymorphic molecular markers. The findings reveal substantial genetic diversity within the Tams1 gene and underscore the limitations of relying solely on conserved targets for molecular diagnostics. These results suggest that regional variation in genetic diversity may influence diagnostic sensitivity, thereby supporting the potential value of incorporating polymorphic markers into surveillance strategies to improve epidemiological monitoring and control efforts.Keywords: Genetic diversity, Haplotype, Piroplasm, Theileria, Türkiye

Background

Tropical theileriosis is a tick-borne lymphoproliferative disease caused by the apicomplexan protozoan Theileria annulata, and is transmitted primarily by Hyalomma species, which are widely distributed in tropical and subtropical regions [1]. The disease imposes substantial economic losses in many parts of the Mediterranean Basin, the Middle East, Africa and Asia, particularly in countries like Egypt [2], India [3, 4], Iran [5], Pakistan [6, 7], and Türkiye [8, 9], where the pathogen is widely distributed and remains a significant constraint to livestock productivity [10]. Tropical theileriosis poses a significant economic burden on the livestock industry in Türkiye, with estimated losses reaching up to US$598,133 over a two-year period in the Cappadocia region alone [11], and additional reports indicating losses of US$130,000–598,000 in other endemic zones [12]. Control measures such as chemotherapy, tick management, and live attenuated vaccines have been used in endemic regions. However, limitations in vaccine production, strain-specific efficacy, and diagnostic sensitivity have prompted molecular approaches to better understand the parasite’s epidemiology and genetic variability [13, 14].

The 18 S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene is widely utilized as a molecular marker for the detection and identification of T. annulata due to its highly conserved nature across isolates and geographical regions. Although this genetic stability ensures reliable classification at the genus and species levels, its limited sequence variability restricts its utility in resolving intra-species genetic diversity and inferring population structure [15, 16]. In contrast, the T. annulata merozoite surface antigen 1 (Tams1) gene has emerged as a polymorphic and immunodominant molecular marker with wide-ranging applications in phylogenetic analysis, serological diagnosis, and vaccine development [17–19]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the Tams1 gene exhibits extensive polymorphisms linked to allelic variation, which may influence host–pathogen interactions and impact the effectiveness of immunization strategies [4, 14, 20]. Such genetic diversity has been documented in several endemic regions, including Egypt [21], India [22], and Tunisia [23], where multiple distinct genotypes have been shown to co-circulate and contribute to immune evasion and regional adaptation. Despite Türkiye being a well-recognized endemic region for bovine theileriosis [8, 9], comprehensive studies that simultaneously evaluate both conserved (e.g., 18 S rRNA) and polymorphic (e.g., Tams1) genetic markers within the same T. annulata populations are still lacking, limiting our understanding of local population structure. To date, the only molecular investigation focusing on Tams1 gene diversity in Türkiye was conducted by Altay et al. [24], who used PCR-RFLP to identify six restriction profiles among isolates from Elazığ and Bingöl provinces. However, no study to date has employed sequence-based approaches to assess the genetic diversity and phylogenetic structure of T. annulata in the country. This underscores the need for molecular epidemiological studies using both conserved and variable loci to clarify T. annulata’s genetic structure in Türkiye.

Interestingly, in our previous study, bovine genomic DNA samples that tested positive using universal primers (BTH) targeting the 18 S rRNA gene region of piroplasm species (Babesia/Theileria/Hepatozoon sp.) were subsequently screened with various species-specific primers, including those designed to amplify the Tams1 gene of T. annulata. While most samples yielded expected results, a subset failed to amplify with any species-specific PCR assays. To resolve these discrepancies, PCR amplification was repeated using RLB primers targeting a shorter, conserved fragment of the 18 S rRNA gene, followed by sequence analysis. This approach revealed that the majority of initially untypeable samples were indeed positive for T. annulata [9]. These findings highlight the potential limitations of conventional species-specific primer sets, particularly those targeting polymorphic loci such as Tams1, and raise important questions about primer-template mismatches, hidden genetic diversity, or underlying sequence variation within local T. annulata populations. Therefore, the present study was designed to comparatively assess the genetic structure of T. annulata isolates from cattle in Türkiye using two molecular markers with distinct evolutionary properties of the conserved 18 S rRNA and the highly polymorphic Tams1 genes. By evaluating haplotype diversity, nucleotide polymorphisms, and phylogenetic relationships, this study aims to elucidate the evolutionary dynamics of these gene regions and their implications for molecular diagnostics, genetic surveillance, and future control strategies.

Results

PCR amplification results of the 18 S rRNA and Tams1 genes

PCR analysis yielded amplification products of 812 bp for 18 S rRNA and 785 bp for Tams1 from all samples, and these products were subjected to sequence analysis. Following sequencing, erroneous read data were trimmed, resulting in final sequence lengths of 717 bp for 18 S rRNA and 584 bp for Tams1. According to BLAST analysis results, all isolates were identified as belonging to the T. annulata species. The obtained sequences were submitted to the NCBI database, and the sequence data of 34 Tams1 and 36 18 S rRNA genes were registered in GenBank. The accession numbers for these entries are provided in Table 1.

Haplotype analyses

A total of 36 sequences obtained from the 18 S rRNA gene region of T. annulata isolates formed a single haplotype, indicating that this gene region is genetically conserved. In contrast, the 34 sequences derived from the Tams1 gene revealed the presence of 27 distinct haplotypes, demonstrating a high level of genetic diversity in this region (Table 2). The haplotype networks resulting from the analyses are presented in Fig. 1. All sequences of the 18 S rRNA gene clustered around a single central haplotype, exhibiting a high degree of similarity. In contrast, a total of 27 different haplotypes were identified within the Tams1 gene region, separated by 43 distinct mutation sites. No dominant central haplotype was observed in the Tams1 region; instead, 25.93% (7/27) of the haplotypes were shared by two isolates, while the remaining 74.07% (20/27) were unique haplotypes represented by only one isolate.

In the analyses conducted on the 18 S rRNA gene region, no genetic variation was detected in terms of diversity or neutrality, indicating that this region is highly conserved. In contrast, a notable level of genetic diversity was observed in the Tams1 gene region (Table 3). The identification of 27 distinct haplotypes revealed significant genetic variation among the isolates. This finding is supported by the high haplotype diversity (Hd = 0.988 ± 0.010) and considerable nucleotide diversity (π = 0.03072 ± 0.00199). Tajima’s D value was positive (2.04089) and statistically significant (P < 0.05), indicating that the observed genetic variation exceeds neutral expectations. Additionally, Fu’s Fs test yielded a significantly negative value (−5.685; P = 0.002), suggesting a recent population expansion or the presence of rare haplotypes. Furthermore, Fu and Li’s D (1.69543) and F (2.14526) values were significantly positive (P < 0.02), implying a deficiency of low-frequency mutations and suggesting the possibility of population contraction or balancing selection. The G + C content of the 584 bp Tams1 gene fragment was calculated as 0.435, indicating moderate thermal stability of the gene region, which supports the reliability of PCR-based analyses.

Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analyses

During haplotype alignment, the reference sequence with accession number “NC_011129” was used for the 18 S rRNA gene region of T. annulata, and all samples were found to exhibit 100% similarity to this sequence. For the Tams1 gene region, the reference sequence “AJ276654” was used, and a total of 43 mutation sites were identified through comparative analysis. The nucleotide positions corresponding to these variations, based on the reference sequence, are presented in Table 4. In the analysis of 34 Tams1 gene sequences, a 584 bp segment was evaluated, and it was confirmed that the region contained no missing data or gaps. All 584 nucleotide positions were included in the comparison. Among these, 541 positions were identified as monomorphic, while 43 positions were found to be polymorphic. These findings indicate that the Tams1 gene region contains both a high level of genetic diversity and informative sites suitable for phylogenetic analysis.

The wide distribution of the obtained haplotypes on the phylogenetic tree indicates that the T. annulata population in Türkiye not only exhibits a marked genetic differentiation, but also possesses a pattern of variation that is organized independently of geographical distribution. The clustering of most haplotypes on closely related branches suggests that some genetic variants may have evolved from a common ancestor and potentially share a close geographical origin. On the other hand, the placement of isolates from the same country on different branches of the phylogenetic tree indicates a high level of intraspecific genetic diversity and suggests that animal movements within the country may play a role in enhancing this diversity. The fact that the newly identified haplotypes do not cluster under a single phylogenetic group indicates the presence of widespread polymorphism in the Tams1 gene region and suggests that this gene is evolutionarily highly variable.

Discussion

In recent years, molecular epidemiological approaches have been increasingly applied in parasitology to investigate intra- and inter-species genetic variations. Techniques such as point mutation analysis, haplotype identification, and phylogenetic methods have become essential tools for understanding the transmission dynamics, evolutionary history, and population structure of hemoparasitic infections [4, 14, 20]. Sequence-based analyses form the foundation of genetic characterization and, when complemented by haplotype diversity, phylogenetic clustering, and neutrality tests, provide valuable insights into parasite host adaptation, immune evasion strategies, and geographic distribution [14]. Within this framework, T. annulata is one the most extensively studied species regarding genetic diversity and evolutionary adaptation. Notably, the highly polymorphic Tams1 gene plays a critical role in phylogenetic classification and the assessment of population-level variation [14, 20]. The high haplotype diversity observed in the Tams1 gene sequences of T. annulata isolates from Türkiye underscores the utility of this molecular approach for regional surveillance and lays the groundwork for more comprehensive investigations into host-parasite interactions.

The high haplotype diversity (Hd = 0.988 ± 0.010) and significant nucleotide diversity (π = 0.03072 ± 0.00199) observed in this study suggest that the T. annulata population in Türkiye possesses a genetically heterogeneous structure. Comparable findings have been documented in the literature. Paliwal et al. [14] reported a haplotype diversity of Hd = 0.947 ± 0.005 and a notably high nucleotide diversity (π = 0.09562 ± 0.00357) among 337 mt-Tams1 gene sequences obtained globally, suggesting a high level of genetic variation at the global scale. Likewise, El-Alfy et al. [25] confirmed the highly variable nature of the Tams1 gene by reporting Hd = 0.981, π = 0.06331, and 63 polymorphic sites among 44 isolates. In contrast, Malik et al. [4] reported a relatively lower nucleotide diversity (π = 0.06069 ± 0.00238) but still detected high haplotype diversity (Hd = 0.83779 ± 0.011). Collectively, these findings indicate that although haplotype diversity consistently remains high, nucleotide diversity shows notable variation across studies, likely reflecting the influence of distinct selective pressures or demographic dynamics in different populations. In particular, the high haplotype diversity observed in our study supports the notion that T. annulata is undergoing active evolutionary processes, while the relatively low π value may indicate that the regional population consists of more closely related individuals or is currently undergoing a recent expansion event [26–28].

The 18 S rRNA gene analysis of T. annulata isolates revealed a single haplotype, reflecting a high degree of sequence conservation across geographically diverse samples. In contrast, Tams1 gene sequences yielded 27 distinct haplotypes, underscoring the remarkable genetic variability within this gene region. Other studies in the literature also confirm the high haplotype diversity of the Tams1 gene. For instance, El-Alfy et al. [25] analyzed 44 isolates from Egypt and identified 33 different haplotypes, emphasizing the highly variable nature of Tams1. Likewise, Paliwal et al. [14] reported 92 distinct haplotypes among 337 Tams1 sequences retrieved from GenBank, originating from 16 different countries, including India, Egypt, Türkiye, China, Tunisia, Iran, Pakistan, Portugal, Spain, Sudan, Italy, Iraq, Mauritania, Bahrain, Oman, and Morocco, highlighting the presence of strong genetic diversity on a global scale. Malik et al. [4] identified 35 haplotypes in a broader dataset comprising 389 sequences from 16 countries. When considered alongside our findings, these results suggest that the Tams1 gene region may exhibit even greater haplotype diversity as the number of analyzed isolates increases. Taken together, these findings indicate that the Tams1 gene region serves as a high-resolution molecular marker for assessing the genetic diversity of T. annulata at both regional and global scales, whereas the 18 S rRNA gene region is more appropriate for species-level identification and analyses of genetic conservation [14, 16, 29–31].

The Tajima’s D value for the 18 S rRNA gene region of T. annulata was found to be neutral (≈ 0) in the present study. This indicates that the region conforms to a neutral model of evolution, suggesting no significant selective pressure or demographic changes acting on this locus. In contrast, the Tams1 gene region yielded a significantly positive Tajima’s D value (D = 2.04089; P < 0.05), which may reflect the effect of balancing selection or an accumulation of intermediate-frequency alleles following a population bottleneck. This finding suggests that the Tams1 gene region is evolutionarily more dynamic and may be subject to selective or demographic processes shaping intra-species genetic diversity. Likewise, Paliwal et al. [14] documented a Tajima’s D value of −1.31211 for the Tams1 gene region in a global-scale study. This significantly negative deviation (P < 0.02) suggest that the population may be undergoing genetic expansion. Similarly, Malik et al. [4] also reported a significantly negative D value (D = −1.21941; P < 0.02) in their analysis of Indian isolates. Both studies provided evidence that the Tams1 locus is subject to the accumulation of new mutations and may be undergoing genetic expansion. In this context, the discrepancy between the positive Tajima’s D value observed in the present study and the negative values reported in global studies could be attributed to a range of environmental and epidemiological factors, including region-specific selective pressures, differences in host population structure, and variations in transmission dynamics. These findings underscore the likelihood of region-specific evolutionary trajectories in T. annulata, shaped by local ecological and epidemiological factors. Accordingly, as noted in previous reports [14, 32, 33], the Tams1 gene region can be considered a reliable molecular marker for tracking the evolutionary dynamics of T. annulata at both local and global scales.

A negative Fu’s Fs value calculated for the Tams1 gene region suggests a potential global expansion of T. annulata populations. Such expansion is typically characterized by an increase in low-frequency mutations, which often result in significantly negative Fu’s Fs test values. Indeed, several studies have reported negative Fu’s Fs values as indicators of population expansion [34, 35]. In contrast, the Fu’s Fs value calculated for the 18 S rRNA gene region from the same isolates was zero, highlighting the conserved and evolutionarily stable nature of this locus. Collectively, these findings not only reflect the local population structure but also support the hypothesis that T. annulata is undergoing genetic expansion and is subject to ongoing evolutionary processes. This expanding genetic diversity may provide a valuable foundation for epidemiological surveillance and for informing the development of targeted control strategies in the future [26, 36]. The observed genetic diversity in the Tams1 gene region also has direct implications for diagnostics and vaccine development. As a highly polymorphic and immunodominant surface antigen, Tams1 is considered a promising subunit vaccine candidate [17, 18, 20]. However, extensive antigenic variation may limit the sensitivity of diagnostic assays and reduce the effectiveness of Tams1-based immunizations. The identification of 27 distinct haplotypes in this study underscores the need to consider region-specific antigenic variants in both diagnostic tool development and vaccine design. Future studies should assess the immunogenic properties of locally circulating Tams1 variants to support more effective, regionally tailored control measures.

The evaluation of the phylogenetic trees revealed that the 18 S rRNA gene sequences exhibited high genetic similarity despite originating from various geographical regions. This finding suggests that the analyzed region is highly conserved or that intra-species genetic diversity is low. In contrast, the substantial genetic variation observed in the Tams1 gene region, along with the identification of 27 distinct haplotypes, indicates that T. annulata has undergone evolutionary diversification driven by regional differences. The observed similarity among Hap01–Hap24 variants and reference isolates suggests possible lineage relationships. The close clustering observed in the upper part of the tree further implies that these haplotypes may have originated from a recent common ancestor. Studies analyzing different gene regions within the same isolates have demonstrated that the degree of genetic differentiation can vary depending on the targeted gene region [37]. Additionally, the length of the analyzed gene region can also significantly influence the resolution of genetic differentiation. Therefore, both the choice and the length of the gene region are critical factors for accurately resolving phylogenetic relationships and understanding evolutionary dynamics at the population level, potentially explaining the haplotype discrepancies observed between studies targeting different regions.

While this study contributes valuable information regarding the genetic diversity and population structure of T. annulata isolates in Türkiye, certain limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the sampling was restricted to cattle from eight provinces, which may not fully represent the nationwide genetic landscape of the parasite. Secondly, the study utilized only two genetic markers, limiting the resolution of genetic analysis and potentially overlooking variations in other genomic regions. Finally, the absence of temporal sampling restricts our ability to infer evolutionary trends or population dynamics over time. Future studies should address these limitations by incorporating broader geographic coverage, additional molecular markers, and longitudinal sampling.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that T. annulata isolates circulating in eight provinces of Türkiye exhibit high haplotype diversity in the Tams1 gene, while the 18 S rRNA gene remains highly conserved. This underscores the presence of genetically diverse parasite populations within the country, which may have implications for diagnostic accuracy and vaccine efficacy. However, the findings are limited by the regional scope of sampling and the lack of host species diversity, which may not fully represent the nationwide genetic landscape of T. annulata. Despite these limitations, the observed genetic heterogeneity highlights the need for region-specific diagnostic tools and control strategies. Expanding molecular surveillance efforts to include a broader range of provinces and host species would provide more representative data, supporting Turkish veterinarians and researchers in developing more effective and locally adapted interventions.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and DNA isolation

In the present study, a total of 36 genomic DNA (gDNA) samples previously confirmed as T. annulata-positive by PCR or sequencing in our earlier study [9] were used. The gDNA samples included in the study, extracted from blood collected between 2019 and 2022, originated from asymptomatic cattle raised in small-scale family-owned farms in eight different provinces of Türkiye. Descriptive information of the sampled cattle and their sampling locations are presented in Fig. 4. The sampled provinces (Bitlis, Diyarbakır, Konya, Mardin, Muş, Siirt, Şanlıurfa, Şırnak) are located in different regions of Türkiye, ranging from Central Anatolia to Southeastern and Eastern Anatolia. The linear distances between sampling locations vary between approximately 100 km and 900 km. Notably, provinces such as Şırnak and Mardin share borders with neighboring countries, including Iraq and Syria. In Türkiye, internal animal movement is regulated through an official animal identification and registration system. However, enforcement and traceability may be limited in rural and border regions, potentially facilitating disease spread due to uncontrolled animal trade and seasonal transhumance practices. For samples with insufficient DNA quantity or quality, new gDNA was re-extracted from stored blood samples using the commercial High Pure PCR Template Preparation Kit (Roche, Germany). All genomic DNA extraction procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

PCR-based amplification of the 18 S rRNA and Tams1 genes

Each isolated gDNA sample was analyzed by PCR using specific primers targeting the T. annulata 18S rRNA gene region (F: 5’-AGCCATGCATGTCTAAGTATAAG-3’; R: 5’-CTAAGAATTTCACCTCTGACAGT-3’, 812 bp) [38, 39] and the Tams1 gene region (F: 5’-ATGAGGATGAAAAGAAAAAGGAGAAAAAAAAGATGT-3’; R: 5’-GCGAAGACTGCAAGGGGGGAGACT-3’, 785 bp) [13]. The PCR reaction mixture was prepared in a total volume of 25 µL, containing 10 µL of A.B.T.™ 2X PCR Master Mix (ABT Laboratory Industry, Ankara, Türkiye), 1 µL of each primer (20 pmol/µL), 2.5 µL of template DNA, and 10.5 µL of distilled water. The PCR conditions for the 18 S rRNA gene region consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 45 s, annealing at 58 °C for 45 s, extension at 72 °C for 45 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min.

For the Tams1 gene region, the PCR conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 45 s, annealing at 57 °C for 1 min, extension at 72 °C for 1 min, and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR products were subjected to electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel at 100 volts for 40 min and visualized under ultraviolet light using a gel documentation system (Erbiotek, Ankara, Türkiye). A 100 bp DNA ladder (ELK Biotechnology, USA) was used to determine the molecular weight of the bands.

Sequence, alignment and phylogenetic analysis of the 18 S rRNA and Tams1 genes

A total of 70 PCR products, including 34 Tams1 and 36 18S rRNA amplicons, were sent to a commercial company (ABT Laboratory Industry, Ankara, Türkiye) for unidirectional DNA sequencing. FinchTV 1.4.0 software (Geospiza Inc., Seattle, Washington, USA) (http://www.geospiza.com) was used to visualize the obtained sequence data. Prior to analysis, all sequences were compared against validated reference sequences in the GenBank database using BLAST (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST/), and then imported into the MEGA X software. In MEGA X, unreadable regions at the 5’ and 3’ ends were trimmed based on reference sequences, and alignment was performed. For phylogenetic analysis, both highly similar sequences and appropriate outgroup sequences were included in the alignment. The best-fitting phylogenetic model was selected using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method in MEGA X, and a phylogenetic tree was constructed using 1000 bootstrap replicates [40].

Nucleotide diversity, polymorphism, neutrality tests, and haplotype network analyses of the sequences

All sequence data were loaded into the DnaSP v6 software [41]. As part of the population genetic analyses, population diversity indices such as the number of haplotypes (h), haplotype diversity (Hd), and nucleotide diversity (π), along with neutrality tests including Tajima’s D [32], Fu’s Fs [42], and Fu and Li’s D and F statistics [43], were calculated using DnaSP v6. Subsequently, the data were exported in NEXUS format using the same software. Haplotype networks were then constructed using the Minimum Spanning Network (MSN) method in PopART v1.7, and the network topology was visualized to include all possible connections [44].

Authors’ contributions

O.C. and F.S. conceptualized the study. O.C., C.C., and F.S. suplied the study materials. O.C., C.C., and M.A.S. conducted laboratory analysis. M.A.S. performed haplotype and phylogenetic analysis. Data availability

The data generated and/or analyzed in this study have been published in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GenBank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). A total of 34 sequences of the T. annulata Tams1 gene are available under accession numbers PV750524–PV750557, and 36 sequences of the 18 S rRNA gene are available under accession numbers PV602755–PV602790.
