Evaluation of cutting speed and surface roughness in WEDM of functionally graded A356-10 wt% Si3N4 composite
Authors: S. Prathap Singh, D. Elil Raja, C. Ramesh Kumar, K. Gnanasekaran, Abhijit Bhowmik, Kedir Hussen, S. Sowndar, M. Sundara Cholan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Functionally graded composites (FGCs) have planned spatial changes in the distribution of the reinforcement and thus radial gradients in hardness, thermal conductivity, and microstructure. In this research, Al (Aluminium) A356 alloy reinforced with Silicon Nitride (Si₃N4) FGC was fabricated through a vertical centrifugal casting technique. The optical microscope, X-ray Diffraction (XRD), and Vicker’s microhardness evaluator examined the gradient distribution of ceramic particles. Such gradients pose great problems in the Wire Electrical Discharge Machining (WEDM), where the distribution of spark energies and the capability to remove materials become a zone-dependent process. The main aim of the research work is to investigate the effect of various machining control variables on the Cutting Speed (CS) and surface roughness during the machining in WEDM. An L27 experimental design was employed to systematically evaluate the influence of pulse on duration, wire tension, wire drum speed, and different zones of the FGC. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) revealed that pulse on duration had the dominant effect on CS (80.30%), while wire tension was the primary contributor to surface roughness (33.11%). The increase in CS is attributed to higher spark energy concentration and improved plasma stability, which enhance the rate of localized melting and material expulsion, whereas the uniform wire movement stabilized the spark discharge, thereby reducing surface roughness. These findings help optimize WEDM parameters for FGCs, improving engineering applications’ machining efficiency and surface quality.

Introduction

Many engineering and structural parts require location-specific service performance. Advanced materials like FGCs have multifunctional behaviors as well as efficiency inside a part. The function of that place changes for many reasons as the microstructure and composition evolve. In aerospace, defense, automotive, and biomedical engineering, FGCs are used1–4. Al Functionally Graded Metal Matrix Composites (Al-FGMMCs) have elevated temperature wear resistance on the exterior surface, improved fastening at the metal reinforcement element interface, the lowest thermal stresses, crack retardation, reduced interfacial stresses, and surface friction5,6. Fabricating components with such performance is a challenging task.

Centrifugal casting is a transport-aided method for making near-net structural components with improved characteristics. Centrifugal force moved secondary phase reinforcement particles here. Centrifugal casting uses centrifugal force to shape molten metal in a rotating mold. Thereafter, an intermediate gradient zone separates enhanced and depleted particle zones. These zones are defined by the mixture of molten liquid and reinforcement densities, centrifugal force, strengthening element size, melt liquid temperature, and cooling rate. Based on ceramic particle density, lighter particles flow toward the axis of rotation and heavier particles away7–9.

Radhika and Raghu optimized centrifugal speeds in Al-Silicon Carbide (SiC) FGC. The evaluation examined how fabrication and wear control variables affected Al FGC wear. They found the highest particle density and toughness in the exterior zone of the FGC, made at 1200 rpm. Additionally, the wear behavior testing confirmed minimal wear in the particle-wealthy exterior boundary at 1200 rpm centrifugal speed10. Jojith et al. evaluated mechanical and tribological properties of the thermally treated Al-strengthened TiC FGC with Al alloy. The horizontal centrifugal casting was used for fabrication. The increased hardness leads to improved surface characteristics in the outer zone, and this was owing to the accomplishment of transporting larger-density ceramic elements to the exterior in the course of centrifugal casting, utilizing centrifugal force11. Bassiouny and Mahmoud examined the mechanical and tribological effects of Al strengthened with 2.5, 5, and 10% Alumina (Al₂O₃) in horizontal centrifugal casting. They also examined how the mold’s rotating speed (800, 900, and 1000) affects gradient distribution. The gradient distribution of larger density Al₂O₃ strengthening components with the highest hardness at the outer boundary was observed in 10 wt% strengthened FGC12.

A secondary machining process is necessary to transform a product with a near-net shape after the fabrication of FGCs. Among the various non-traditional machining processes, WEDM is an acceptable machining approach to shape complex profiles into a required machine element. So it is necessary to improve the machining performance by the proper selection of input control variables13–16. Presently, industries preferred higher CS and minimum surface roughness to reduce the cost of the machine elements17,18. To attain this, an optimal combination of control variables for the machining process is to be chosen19–26.

Amresh Kumar et al. have looked over the optimization of cutting parameters for maximizing MRR and minimizing surface roughness during the machining of Al hybrid composites. The experimental outcome analysis demonstrates that the optimized results attained through the multiobjective optimization increased the MRR by 13.79% and reduced the surface roughness by 19.16%27. Dey and Pandey studied the implementation of WEDM process parameters that affect the performance of Al-reinforced cenosphere composite through a hybrid approach called grey-response surface methodology. They have detailed that the machining habits, such as cutting rate, kerf width, and surface roughness, were highly influenced by the pulse on and off times, wire feed, and weight% of reinforcements28. Vishnu et al. have audited the influence of WEDM machining process control variables on the MRR and surface roughness of an Al hybrid composite using Grey Relational Analysis (GRA). The resulting outcome shows that the comparison of the optimal composition of WEDM parameters is attained through Taguchi’s and GRA29. Sakthi and Ramanathan carried out the WEDM study on the stir-casted Al composite and analyzed the consequence of machining operation control variables on the MRR and surface roughness using a multi-criteria decision-making technique. The results indicate that the output parameters significantly influence the circumferential cutting parameters30. Vora et al. optimized the current, pulse off-duration, and pulse on duration in the WEDM of Ti6Al4V to accomplish the required MRR and surface roughness. ANOVA results showed that MRR was primarily influenced by pulse off duration, whereas SR was mainly affected by pulse on duration31. Rakesh et al. employed WEDM to machine pure titanium and optimize the pulse-on duration, discharge current, and pulse- off duration. GRA was utilized to optimize WEDM process parameters for titanium, the most popular material for aerospace and biomedical applications, to improve cutting rate and reduce surface roughness32. Uthayakumar et al. studied the performance of EDM drilling on aluminum reinforced with SiC FGC using GRA. They analyzed how the pulse current, on-period, and zone position affected the electrode wear rate, surface roughness, overcut, and power usage. The analysis of the percentage contribution of each variable to the outputs showed that pulse current is the most significant factor affecting the machined characteristics33. Jayavelu et al. indicated that WEDM is suitable to machine hybrid Al composites, and gap voltage and reinforcement percentage are of great importance to MRR and roughness of the surface. Taguchi and GRA optimization revealed the best parameter settings to achieve a compromise between increased MRR and controlled surface roughness34. Murali et al. maximized the nature of multi-response in the multi-EDM process of AlSi10Mg, Taguchi integrated GRA, and found the optimal pulse-on time, pulse-off time, servo voltage, and wire feed rate to optimize MRR and at the same time, minimize surface roughness. The confirmatory tests showed that the machining performance improved by 38.57%, which can be attributed to the fact that the proposed optimization methodology was effective in additive manufactured alloy35.

Although the research on WEDM of Al-based MMC has been conducted extensively, most of the past based research is on materials that have homogeneous distribution of reinforcements. Nonetheless, the FGCs are characterized by a gradual change in reinforcement concentration and hardness in a given direction. According to the literature, the centrifugal-cast FGCs have larger contents of ceramic particles in the outer part and a rich matrix in the inner part and might possess casting-induced porosity. The result of this radial gradient is that it creates significant differences in the thermal and electrical conductivity, hardness, melting behavior and nature of interfacial bonding. Moreover, the local material properties are very important to WEDM in generating spark, maintaining stability of the plasma channel, and dissipating heat. The mechanism of the material removal and surface integrity should, therefore, differ greatly in different areas of FGC. Nonetheless, there is a dearth of systematic experimental evidence relating reinforcement gradient and machining response.

The literature presents the optimization of the WEDM parameters of the homogeneous aluminum composites or hybrid MMCs as the primary result. Taguchi, GRA, RSM, and other methods are used to optimize the parameters. Although these studies have managed to determine optimum machining parameters, they fail to answer the effect of reinforcement gradients in determining the energy absorbed by sparks and crater formation and the influence of variation of hardness on the simultaneous cutting speed and surface roughness. This is the research gap identified, and novel work has been planned. In the present investigation, the WEDM is utilized for machining functionally graded A356-10 wt% Si₃N4 MMC to enhance CR and reduce surface roughness. Finally, the exterior morphology of the machined circumferential area is studied using HRSEM, EDS and an optical profilometer.

Experimental procedure

Fabrication FGC

A356 Al alloy is a popular matrix for automotive engine components due to its low density, easy fabricability, corrosion resistance, and excellent thermal conductivity. Si₃N4 particles are used as reinforcement because they are tougher and more wear resistant. The amount of reinforcement particles is kept at 10 wt% and their average size is kept at 50 μm. The density of the Al alloy is 2.67 g/cm3, and the density of Si₃N4 is 3.17 g/cm³. Materials for reinforcement and the base matrix were collected from the commercial vendor called Coimbatore Metal Mart, Coimbatore, Tamil Nadu, India.

Liquid metallurgy and vertical centrifugal casting are used to develop A356–10 wt% Si3N4 FGC. The bottom pouring type stir casting machine (make: Swam Equip Pvt. Ltd., India) is utilized to melt Al alloy under an inert gas atmosphere and is demonstrated in Fig. 1a. To prevent casting faults, the melting furnace is kept in an inert atmosphere. Once the melting temperature was reached (760 °C), 2 wt% magnesium and 10 wt% preheated (300 °C for 2 h) Si₃N4 reinforcement elements were mixed into the heated liquid metal and stirred at 250 rpm for 15 min. The addition of magnesium upgrades the wettability between the matrix and Si₃N4 particles. This stirring action creates a vortex, resulting in homogeneous dispersion of ceramic Si₃N4 particles in the molten metal. This mixture, along with reinforcement, is poured at 750 °C into the preheated rotating die (350 °C) of the vertical centrifugal casting machine (Fig. 1a) to make a hollow cylindrical component. Finally, functionally graded hollow composite with an external diameter of 150 mm, an internal diameter of 50 mm, and a thickness of 50 mm were obtained (Fig. 1b).

Fig. 1(a) Stir casting machine (Type: Bottom pouring) with vertical centrifugal rotating machine, (b) Developed A356–10 wt% Si₃N4 FGC.

(a) Stir casting machine (Type: Bottom pouring) with vertical centrifugal rotating machine, (b) Developed A356–10 wt% Si₃N4 FGC.

To improve the performance properties of the as-cast FGC, a typical T6 heat treatment was used in a muffle furnace. The solution treatment began at 550 °C for 10 h to ensure complete dissolution of alloying elements and uniform distribution of Si₃N4 particles, followed by quenching in warm water at 60 °C, artificial aging at 165 °C for another 10 h to achieve peak hardness, and then natural cooling at ambient temperature. The temperature during the treatment was carefully monitored throughout the process to guarantee consistent mechanical qualities11.

An upright optical microscope (Manufacturer: Olympus) was used to explore the functionally graded composite microstructure. Before the examination, specimens were cut from the composite at three different (outer, middle, and inner) regions and metallographically polished using a double disc polisher. The polished specimens were etched using Keller’s reagent to obtain a clear view of grains. XRD analysis was performed on the FGC sample sliced from the Al FGC’s exterior boundary to confirm reinforcement inside the Al matrix and characterize intermetallic reaction products formed during casting. The Davinci (BRUKER USA) D8 Advance was used to do XRD scrutiny from 0.101 to 117.813 degrees with a least step dimension of 0.029 degrees. Vicker’s hardness tester (Make: SHIMADZU) is executed to examine the microhardness of the FGC in accordance with the ASTM-E384 standard. Before testing, specimens were polished with fine emery paper, and a diamond indenter was used to apply 100 gf of load to the specimen surface for 15 s. The hardness is measured six times on the same surface to ensure uniformity, and the average is used as the surface hardness.

Machining study

Fig. 2(a) Sodick make Mark21 high quality WEDM machine, (b) FGC specimen fitted in the WEDM machine, (c) Machined FGC.

(a) Sodick make Mark21 high quality WEDM machine, (b) FGC specimen fitted in the WEDM machine, (c) Machined FGC.

The use of functionally graded Al materials is becoming more and more in demand day by day in various engineering and industrial applications. The machining of these FGCs, in particular WEDM, becomes imperative and cannot be dismissed. The mechanism behind the WEDM machining of FGCs varies from those of conventional materials because of their unique performance behaviors observed during the WEDM machining process. Figure 2a shows the high-quality WEDM machine (Model: Mark21, make: Sodick Co., Ltd., Japan) used to cut the pins (6 mm diameter and 50 mm height) from the different locations of the fabricated FGC. Figures 2b show the workspace with the specimen. To carry out the experiments, a 0.25 mm diameter brass wire with a composition of 60 wt% copper and 40 wt% zinc was employed as a tool electrode material having a positive polarity. The cutting circumference and depth were kept constant at 18.85 mm (π*6 mm) and 50 mm, respectively. Deionized water was used as a dielectric to carry out the cutting experiment. A 1.5 bar dielectric flushing pressure was maintained, with side flushing nozzles aligned with the machining zone, to guarantee stable spark removal and cooling. Figure 2c shows the specimen after complete machining and the machined samples, respectively.

The L27 orthogonal array table was adopted to complete the machining experiments36,37. To reduce time-dependent drift effects, each experiment was repeated three times in a random order. The choices for the input control variables include pulse on duration (Ton) (1, 2, 3 µs), wire tension (Wt) (4, 5, 6 gf), wire drum feed (Wdf) (1, 2, 3 mm/s), and different zones in the FGC (outer, middle, inner), and they are tabulated in Table 1.

The pulse off duration (14 µs), servo voltage (30 V), and pulse current (17 amp) were maintained at optimized values throughout the experiments to ensure stable discharge conditions and minimize variability, allowing the study to focus on the influence of selected machining control variables. The input and optimized control variables were selected based on the previous literature and preliminary trial runs conducted by considering the limitations of the WEDM machine38–40.

After the completion of each set of the control variable, the CS is the quantity of material cut per minute by length and was taken directly from the machine panel. The surface roughness was measured to investigate machined patterns with 3D surface plots and 2D depth graphs using an optical profilometer (Model: MicroXAM-800, Make: KLA Corporation, USA). The 2D depth graph was measured to a sliding length of approximately 200 μm. The measurements were made along the feed direction. To calculate surface roughness, four Ra values were obtained at various regions on the circumference of each WEDM sample, and then the average of the Ra values was tabulated. ANOVA was employed to examine the statistical significance of each control factor and their interaction effects on the output responses, such as CS and the surface roughness. ANOVA analysis was performed at a confidence level of 95% and a significance level of 5%.

A 3D analysis of the machined texture’s crests and valleys confirmed its unevenness. The Hi-Resolution Scanning Electron Microscope (HRSEM) (Make: Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. Model: Apreo 2 SEM) and Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) were used to investigate the machined surface morphology on the dissimilar areas of the heat-treated Al FGC. The analysis was conducted with a 20 kV accelerating voltage, an 8 nm spot size, and a 60 s acquisition period per point to achieve accurate element identification. The machined samples for analysis were washed with ethanol and dried to eliminate surface contamination.

Result and discussions

Microstructure examination

Figure 3 shows the optical microstructure of the heat-treated 10 wt% Si₃N4 strengthened Al A356 alloy FGC from the outside to the inside. It detected four major zones - the chilled region, the wealthy ceramic region, the transition region, the wealthy matrix region, and the porous region - and verified the gradient distribution of Si₃N4 ceramic particles. The gradient structure in the manufactured FGC is a result of the mixing of different solidification rates of the composite melt in the rotating mold and the centrifugal force. Figure 3a illustrates the microstructure of the chilled region, as assessed from the external edge extending to a depth of 5 mm; this data confirms that there is less strengthening element intensity since the Al and reinforcement liquid mixture solidifies immediately adjacent to the mold’s external surface. The microstructure of the wealthy ceramic region, located 6 mm to 20 mm after the external boundary, is shown in Fig. 3b–d. Because of the centrifugal force, it indicated that there was a higher concentration of Si₃N4 strengthening particles. The wealthy ceramic zone was formed as a result of centrifugal power throwing the high-density (3.17 g/cm³) Si₃N4 ceramic reinforcements to the outer periphery, in contrast to the Al A356 alloy (2.67 g/cm³). Similarly, Saleh et al. found a similar higher concentration of SiC reinforcement in the outside boundary of their Al reinforced with 10 wt% SiC FGC41.

The microstructure of the transition region, which is located 21 to 30 mm from the external boundary, is shown in Fig. 3e. In comparison to the nearby wealthy ceramic zone, it provided a lower concentration of Si₃N4 particles. Figure 3f shows the microstructure of the wealthy matrix region, located 31–40 mm from the outer periphery. This area, which is at the inner edge, displayed a reduction in reinforcing particles. Figure 3g shows that there are several gas porosities located within 40 mm of the composite’s inner edge. This effect occurs because centrifugal force concentrates contaminants and less dense gas foams towards the innermost perimeter. Premkumar John et al. demonstrated a similar gradient distribution of strengthening reinforcements, with a significant accumulation of these elements near the outer edge of their Al A356 alloy, which was reinforced with a 10 wt% SiC fiberglass composite produced by vertical centrifugal casting42.

Figure 3h shows heat-treated Al alloy supported with Si₃N4 FGC XRD peaks in the wealthy ceramic zone. XRD showed Si₃N4 in the Al alloy FGC. The analysis discovered the presence of Al, indicated by crests at 2θ = 38.65°, 44.82°, 65.47°, 78.68°, 82.76°, and 112.46° (JCPDS card number 89–4037), alongside Si₃N4 crests at 2θ = 28.61° and 47.60° (JCPDS card number 41–0360). The observation of silicon was noted through the peaks at 2θ = 88.48°, 95.37°, and 107.09° (JCPDS card number 27–1402). Furthermore, the Mg₂Si segment was detected in the FGC at 2θ = 56.44° and 69.57° (JCPDS card number 35–0773). No further reaction elements were found in the FGC.

Figure 4 exhibits the outcomes of a Vicker’s microhardness test and image analysis on a heat-treated Al FGC, which was conducted in a radial path from the outer to the inner at intervals of 5 mm. This trend is attributed to the lower reinforcement content and higher porosity in the inner region, which together reduce the load-bearing capability of the matrix. As one approached the inner periphery, a proportionately decreasing curvature was visible. Compared to the wealthy matrix region (30 to 45 mm), the wealthy ceramic region (5 to 20 mm) has a greater hardness of 182 HV, which is 73% higher. The improved wettability and ceramic particle dispersion of the alloy are responsible for this. The FGC’s outer boundary is lined with primary Si₃N4 particles due to the density differential and centrifugal force. Sajikumar found a similar hardness mechanism for the Al-10 wt% SiC ex situ FGC43. Furthermore, Fig. 4 depicts the Si₃N4 distribution profile of the Al-Si₃N4 FGC disc, which was analyzed using image analysis. Image analysis, like optical micrographs, reveals a reduced volume proportion of Si₃N4 particles near the outer periphery due to chill zone formation. There is an increase up to 15 mm, then a decrease until 45 mm, after which the zone is nearly particle depleted.

Effect of control variables on the cutting speed

During the WEDM machining process, many problems arise due to the anisotropic performance of the Al composites. CS is one of the vital response variables targeted in machining a machine element. It has a straightforward influence on final product quality and the economy of production. The lower CS affects the economy of the production process. This CS enhances the surface integrity of the material, resulting in a smooth surface on the machined material; however, when the CS increases again, it negatively impacts the quality of the surface formed on the material.

Table 3 presents the ANOVA results for CS, which were derived to analyze the individual effects of control variables on CS. In the ANOVA table, the maximum value of F denotes the highest influence of the control variable on the output responses. The ‘P’ value identifies the machining control variable with the highest significance. It is well known that a machining control variable with a ‘P’ value less than 0.05 makes a significant contribution to the response44,45. Among the four control variables, pulse-on duration is found to be a most influencing (80.30%) control variable having a significant impact on the CS, whereas the different zones had a percentage contribution of 4.97%. The other WEDM control variables, such as wire tension and wire drum feed, had a small contribution to the CS, which is 1.92% and 1.25%, respectively. Moreover, the other interaction of the control variable displayed a small amount of contribution to the CS, which is less than 4%.

Normally, the spark erosion process is the cause for the removal of materials through melting and vaporization in the WEDM operation. An increase in pulse on duration produces a steady, continuous electrical spark for a longer duration in the space between the brass wire electrode and material. This electrical spark is converted into heat energy, and the produced heat energy rapidly melts and vaporizes the materials and penetrates the cross-sectional areas of the FGC. The outcome obtained is the heavier CS from the huge amount of heat energy produced by the higher pulse on duration, creating a wider molten/vaporized zone per pulse and increasing MRR. Prathap Singh et al. observed similar observation in their Al reinforced with SiC and titanium dioxide hybrid composite46. This effect is more pronounced in the Al-Si₃N4 combination due to its low thermal conductivity and high hardness, which slows heat dissipation and causes localized melting and micro-fracturing over longer discharge periods. Thus, pulse on duration contributes the most to CS of the characteristics evaluated.

Further, the different zones of the FGC contributed more to the CS during WEDM operation, which is 4.97%. Figure 6 revealed that the maximum CS was achieved in the transition zone of the FGC. This effect could be due to the presence of a moderate amount of Si₃N4 ceramic reinforcement particles. The microstructure analysis clearly showed that the wealthy ceramic zone of the FGC had more hard ceramic reinforcement particles, while the inner wealthy matrix zone had more porosity and air bubbles due to the action of centrifugal force. Therefore, maximum CS was evaluated in the transition zone of the FGC. Suresh et al. observed a similar observation in their Al reinforced with SiC and boron carbide hybrid MMC. Their findings revealed that the moderate percentage of reinforcement gave enhanced output responses47.

The wire tension and wire drum feed had a small impact on the CS. Moreover, the increase in wire tension increased the CS by removing the slag in the wire. The CS decreased with an increase in wire drum speed, and it is due to the inadequate duration of the electrical spark between the electrode and the machining material. However, the higher wire drum speed does not allow the heat energy to distribute and melt the FGC. Hence, it reduces the CS at higher wire drum speeds.

Effect of control variables on the surface roughness

The upper CS is the source for the severe surface damage in the composites due to the electrical spark produced, leading to changes in the surface texture of the machined composite. As a result, cut marks, solidification layers, and thermally damaged craters and fissures develop on the machined surface, contributing to increased surface roughness. So it is essential to monitor the CS to achieve enhanced surface roughness.

While increasing pulse on duration enhances CS due to greater discharge energy, it also deteriorates surface roughness as larger craters are formed. Therefore, an optimal pulse on duration must be selected to balance improved CS with acceptable surface roughness. The interaction of pulse on duration with wire tension and wire drum speed showed a significant contribution of 11.18% and 16.37%, respectively. A higher pulse-on duration combined with increased wire tension causes transient fluctuations in the discharge gap, intensifying wire vibration and leading to uneven spark energy distribution. This increases localized melting and crater formation, resulting in higher surface roughness. Similarly, at higher wire drum feed, the longer pulse-on duration produces unstable sparks because the dielectric flushing and debris removal are not fully synchronized with the spark duration. This spark instability promotes irregular recast layers and microcracks on the machined surface.

The increase in wire tension decreased the surface roughness from 4 to 5 gf, and an increase in wire tension was observed after 5 gf. The decreasing surface roughness could be due to the movement of brass wire with uniform velocity. Therefore, the electrical spark created during the machining process is uniform; thereby, the surface roughness is small. When the wire tension was increased, the electrical spark generated during the machining process created an impact force on the wire and made the moving wire vibrate. When the wire tension is increased, it automatically increases the frequency of vibration and leads to the formation of higher surface roughness. Satishkumar et al. observed a similar observation in their Al reinforced with SiC composite48. In addition, a rigid wire enhances flushing efficiency by maintaining a stable inter-electrode gap, thereby reducing re-solidified debris and micro-pitting on the machined surface.

The minimum surface roughness was derived from pins machined from the transition zone of the FGC. The higher strength outer region shows the presence of high-hardness Si₃N4 particles, and the lower reinforced inner periphery demonstrates the presence of porosity and surface damage. These conditions caused wandering of the electric sparks over the surface. These defects will create poor surface roughness due to the uneven machining surface. In the case of the transition region, the existence of a balanced quantity of base and reinforcements reduces the machining defects and thereby decreases surface roughness. Variations in hardness and microstructure across the different zones play an important role in influencing the machining operation. Wealthy ceramic zones, which are tougher and have a larger particle content, resist spark energy during machining, resulting in smaller craters and reduced MRR. In contrast, wealthy matrix zones have easier MRR, resulting in larger craters and more surface roughness. This suggests that local microstructural features and hardness gradients significantly influence crater formation, MRR, and overall surface quality during WEDM.

Among the three levels of wire drum speeds, the 2 mm/s speed showed lower surface roughness. Additionally, the wire drum speed resulted in higher surface roughness at 1 mm/s because the continuous electrical spark concentrated on the same spot caused a heat-affected recast layer to form over the machined FGC’s surface. A similar model of higher surface roughness was observed with 3 mm/s. This feature could be due to the existence of a melt deposition layer over the material surface and the wire electrode. Rajender et al. observed a similar observation during the WEDM of ZE41A magnesium alloy with different levels of wire drum speed49.

The intensification in pulse-on duration yields a continuous spark in the WEDM process, enhances the CS, and thereby forms deep craters and skewness over the machining surface of the FGC50. Here, pulse-on duration showed no significant influence on the surface roughness.

Machined surface examination

The surface morphology of machined FGC was examined through HRSEM, 3D optical profilometer, and EDS analysis to gain an insight into the physical processes that occur in the machining and material removal mechanisms of the material as well as the surface integrity of the material at WEDM. As the composite has a radial gradient in reinforcement distribution and hardness, machine response is dramatically different between the wealthy ceramic, transition and wealthy matrix areas.

To remove material in the WEDM process, there is a series of discrete electrical discharges between the brass wire electrode and the workpiece that has been immersed in a dielectric medium. Once the applied voltage is greater than the dielectric breakdown strength, a plasma channel is created between the inter-electrode gap, which produces very high localised temperatures and pressures. This high thermal power leads to high melting and partial vaporization of work material. The Al MMC softens easily to spark energy in the current FGC, but the Si₃N4 reinforcement particles, which are very hard and have high thermal stability, do not melt. As a result of this thermal incompatibility between the matrix and reinforcement, there is a melting of the Al matrix around the particles of the ceramics locally, a partially debonded interface between the matrix and reinforcement, irregular crater geometry and unequal expulsion of materials. The deionized water dielectric is used to quickly quench the molten material after each discharge and quick solidification. It results in a solid recast layer with globules, splashes and developmental surface ridges.
