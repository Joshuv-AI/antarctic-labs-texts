Understanding data differences across the ENACT federated research network

Introduction

Clinical research data networks promise rapid discovery and feedback into the healthcare system. However, electronic health record (EHR) data is fraught with problems due to eccentricities in data collection, organization/structure, and errors in data processing, e.g, during Extract Transform Load (ETL).1,2 Federated data networks introduce further room for quality problems–non-uniformity of data across sites and the need for data harmonization across locations using different terminologies and coding practices.3–5 The quality of data in a research network can directly impact the quality of research that relies on it, even at early stages of study design and planning. To effectively convert data to knowledge, methods must be developed to help researchers understand how the data are arranged in these different settings.

The National Institute of Health’s (NIH) National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences (NCATS) program funds a large federated clinical research data network called Evolve to Next-Gen Accrual of patients to Clinical Trials (ENACT).6,7 The network consists of local EHR data repositories that use either the Informatics for Integrating Biology at the Bedside (i2b2) or the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) common data models.8,9 These are linked via the Shared Health Research Information Network (SHRINE) software platform.10,11 The ENACT network, the largest SHRINE network, consists of 52 participating sites across the United States, covering over 140 million patients. Queries are real-time in that once investigators specify their search criteria, sites immediately begin returning (obfuscated) counts of the number of matching patients. The patient counts enable investigators to assess feasibility of any planned study and to identify potential collaborating sites.

ENACT is a hub-and-spoke network (Supplemental Figure 1). All sites are connected through a central SHRINE Hub instead of site-to-site connections. The SHRINE Hub broadcasts network queries to all sites and relays back results. To connect to the SHRINE Hub, every ENACT site operates its own instance of i2b2 application. ENACT supports the i2b2 data model (default in any i2b2 application) and the OMOP data model, both of which are widely adopted.12–15,36,37 Sites using the i2b2 data model need to install the common ENACT ontologies. Sites using the OMOP model need to additionally run one-time initialization scripts to add ENACT ontologies and to direct query to the correct OMOP data elements. ENACT maintains and distributes both the common ontologies and the requisite scripts.

Sites map their local EHR data to common ENACT ontologies. This local-to-common ontology mapping is crucial as it enables the network sites to harmonize the types of data that are queried and shared in the network. However, mapping local data to common ontologies is nuanced and error-prone for several reasons: (1) Strategies for mapping data differ based on local infrastructure, policies, and processes. For example, some sites map to standard codes during ETL, whereas others utilize the ontology system to dynamically manage mappings.16 (2) Many codes captured during clinical practice are transformed by professional coders to satisfy reimbursement or reporting requirements. (3) Standard terminologies are often not assigned at the source, leaving informatics teams to encode facts using their best educated guess. (4) Modern ontologies contain hundreds of thousands of terms (ENACT ontology has over 800,000), and sites tend to use only a small portion, so a laboratory test like hemoglobin A1C could be encoded with any of several valid LOINC codes. (5) Implementation and maintenance of EHRs change over time, a factor that can have significant influence on the use of concepts.

Other clinical data networks have tried to surmount data quality problems with their own tools. OHDSI provides the data characterization tool Automated Characterization of Health Information at Large-scale Longitudinal Evidence Systems (ACHILLES).4 More recently, the Data Quality Dashboard (DQD) provides a snapshot view of common data quality issues through 24 data check types that check Plausibility, Conformance, and Completeness.17,18 National Patient-Centered Clinical Research Network (PCORnet) offers comprehensive data characterization reports with a similar combination of conformance checks and data characterization graphs.19 The Food and Drug Administration’s Sentinel Initiative developed a workflow whereby sites run data assurance software, distributed by Sentinel, to create data quality reports, then submit the reports back for review.20–22 Data Quality Workflow from PEDSnet works in similar ways, but site quality reports are automatically inserted into GitHub repository for tracking.20 These systems all run rules-based checks and produce static data quality reports. The National Clinical Cohort Collaborative (N3C) developed its Scorecards for site data quality.23,24 Each Scorecard includes rule-based checks and comparisons to a site’s peers for a selection of measures. Scorecards are most similar to our approach in that we also value peer-comparisons.

The ENACT network, built on i2b2, creates a unique opportunity for a flexible data quality methodology built around its unique star-schema data structure. Rather than delineating fact types through table definitions (e.g, CONDITION, DRUG, LAB), the i2b2 ontology system defines the possible facts in the data. Facts are stored as generic objects in a single fact table, with ancillary dimension tables providing additional context about the facts. For example, i2b2 uses the concept_dimension table to store information about the types of facts (e.g, diabetes or lymphoma). Rather than building hundreds of specific data quality checks, ENACT gives us the opportunity to engage in a measurement of quality through its common ontologies and relative to the network. For example, the absence of codes might not indicate a quality issue (perhaps the codes are esoteric and seldom used), but if every other site in the network has data with these codes, it may be an indicator that the missing data are a quality issue.

In ENACT, we could analyze the distribution and study similarities/differences in ontology usage at sites. This could be accomplished by counting the number of patients who have facts that are instances of a specific ontology concept, for all concepts. Analyzing such counts and comparing them across sites would enable us to answer questions about missingness, mapping variation, outliers, and averages. Visual analytic tools can be deployed to make the network-driven data quality analyses in large ENACT ontologies possible.25

Utilizing the entire network’s ontology usage for the purpose of data quality is novel, and our approach offers several benefits. It enables sites to compare its usage with the network in a mutually privacy-preserving way: site data remains private, and network statistics are aggregated. As the network grows, network statistics can become more reliable and robust by virtue of large sample sizes. Ontology usage across the network is immediately apparent to site users. ENACT can track ontology usage changes over time to find interesting trends or responses to major health events such as the COVID-19 pandemic.

Objective

We propose an approach for gathering, aggregating, and then analyzing patient-count data in an interactive tool for data quality. First, we develop and deploy high-performance patient counting tools across the network. Next, we introduce the Data Quality Explorer (DQE) to enable any site to compare its own data with the network statistics and detect potential data quality issues such as unexplained outliers or missing data.

Materials and methods

High performance patient counting

The core strategy for ENACT data quality relies on utilizing the total number of patients with data for each concept in the database. Patient counting is challenging because a typical i2b2 instance has an ontology of a half-million elements and a fact table with hundreds of millions of rows. We implemented this as SQL scripts that can interact directly with the database, leveraging the set operations and indexing that make relational databases so efficient. Careful attention was paid to optimizing temporary computation space and converting data types to more space-efficient versions. We describe this “totalnum” counting method below.

i2b2 1.8.0 (released 2023) introduced an optimized version of this “totalnum” script for SQL Server that uses a transitive closure table, a table that lists ancestor-descendent pairs. This precomputes the collection of child codes (step 1 above) for every node in the ontology. This allows the counting to be calculated with a simple SQL join operation, speeding up the process by an order of magnitude.

Site managers can run the “totalnum” scripts with obfuscation within their database and store the results in a “totalnum_report” table along with the item key, which is a consistent identifier for a concept across sites in ENACT. Site managers can optionally export these counts and contribute them to ENACT Hub for inclusion in the network statistics.

We developed a pipeline in the ENACT Hub to aggregate site-submitted totalnum reports and to calculate network statistics. The pipeline converts the obfuscated counts to percentages for each concept (i.e, divide patient count for concepts by the total number of patients at that site). We then use contributing sites’ percentages of the same concept to compute the concept’s network statistics: mean, standard deviation, quartiles, min, and max. We repeat that for all concepts. The network statistics are then organized by domain ontologies so data quality analysis tools can retrieve them on-demand.

Using network statistics for data quality

Many factors can affect a site’s percentage of patients with specific concepts, including the site’s geography, specialty, patient demographic, coding conventions, and other site-specific practices. Despite these factors, the ENACT network statistics exhibit remarkable centrality and consistency. As of this writing, of the thirteen sites that contribute data to the ENACT network, eleven have fewer than 10% (median = 2.6%) outlier concepts (Table 1). Outlier concepts are those with percentages of patients at least two-standard-deviations away from the network mean. This indicates a strong degree of agreement in percentage of patients among sites, making the network statistics valuable for data quality investigations.

Results

Data quality workflow

We implemented the workflow shown in Figure 1. The patient counting scripts for the three database platforms—SQL Server, PostgreSQL, and Oracle–are distributed with i2b2 1.8.0+ (a requirement for ENACT sites).26 We recommend that site managers run these scripts periodically (e.g, after each major data refresh).

Red arrows indicate the workflow taken by ENACT site administrator. Blue arrows indicate the ENACT Hub’s computation and dissemination of network statistics, which the Data Quality Explorer (DQE) consumes.

Sites are encouraged to export these distinct patient-per-concept counts to a file and contribute back to ENACT for inclusion in the network statistics. This is done through Secure File Transfer protocol (SFTP) to the ENACT Hub. Hub administrators then run the aggregation pipeline to update the network statistics.

ENACT data quality explorer (DQE)

The centerpiece of this workflow is the Data Quality Explorer (DQE),27 a self-service, interactive visualization web tool. It is hosted on the ENACT network website.28 DQE retrieves ENACT network statistics when a site’s data is loaded. Automatic comparison between site data and the network statistics is performed. An advantage of this design is that any site that has executed the patient-counting totalnum script can use DQE, even those not yet contributing data to the network. Additionally, site data remains private and is never exposed.

DQE design follows Shneiderman’s information-seeking mantra, where features are specifically designed to support data overview, detail on-demand, and filtering.29 DQE uses a traditional node-link representation for ontology overview. Unlike other representations, it can best convey ontological topology.30–33 DQE’s SumTree view enables efficient discovery of outlier clusters, and its data table view allows filtering for interesting concepts, akin to dynamic query.34 The comparison between site data with the network statistics occurs automatically and as soon as site data is loaded in DQE. Outliers and missingness are highlighted using consistent size and color-encodings, significantly reducing user effort in identifying their existence and location. Users can visually search for potential data quality issues and inspect the details by mouse hovering. A summary of the DQE interface and its features follows. We encourage readers to watch the DQE demonstration video, which provides a more immersive experience of the tool (Supplemental Video).35

Users first search for extreme outliers by setting the filters as shown in Figure 4, finding 1847 concepts. They are then ranked by percentage. Many of the top-ranked extreme outliers are related to malignant neoplasm of skin.

(a) shows the SumTree based on the root of the ontology ICD-10. Every rectangular node (SumNode) shows how many outliers there are that level of the subtree. For example, the 1st level has 0 outliers out of 23 total concepts. The 2nd level has 7 outliers out of 293 concepts. Clicking on the 2nd level SumNode reveals its outliers and their paths to the root in a gray inset. In this case, users have chosen to display the extreme outlier C43-C44 in the inset and display its details in a popup. C43-C44 turns out to be a lynch pin of many of other extreme outliers presented in Figure 4. (b) shows the users’ investigation of C44.52, an extreme outlier in the 5th SumNode, which has label “872/17432.” Its path up to the root indicates that all of its ancestors up to C43-C44 are extreme outliers indicated in Figure 4. The visualization can prompt users to investigate whether this cluster of outliers is an artifact of data mapping error or there is a reasonable explanation.

The SumTree in Figure 5a shows that the 2nd ranked extreme outlier C43-C44 is in the 3rd level of ICD-10 ontology. The Figure 5b inset shows that a long string of its descendants are all outliers: its child C44 (the 3rd ranked extreme outlier), its grandchild C44.5 (the 11th ranked extreme outlier), and its great-grandchildren C44.51 and C44.52. A cluster of outliers like this one might indicate data quality issues–some error was perhaps introduced to make this cluster outliers. However, considering this site’s warm climate and year-round sun, this result is perhaps unsurprising. While this example does not ultimately reveal any data quality problems because the outliers are explainable, it illustrates the usefulness of DQE’s outlier-detection features.

Dissemination and feedback

We engaged in a concerted collection of patient counts in 2023. Sites were requested to run the counting script, export that data to a file, and submit it to the ENACT Hub. At the end of 2023, 13 ENACT sites had contributed at least one set of counts. In May 2024, we made the DQE available to ENACT site managers.28

Accessing the ENACT-hosted DQE requires site managers to apply for a DQE account. Once vetted and approved, a credential is issued. DQE is prepackaged with an in-app interactive tutorial, sample data, video demonstrations, and a user guide. However, not all onboarding experiences went smoothly. Managers from two sites experienced trouble getting DQE to load their local data. This was because DQE’s parser was unreasonably picky, and its error messages were unhelpful, leaving users frustrated. After resolving users’ issues, we updated DQE parser and error reporting.

As of this writing, ten sites’ data managers have DQE accounts, and several have responded to our request for feedback. Site managers were enthusiastic about having a new tool for data quality. A manager from Site A wrote enthusiastically “I was able to review our site numbers, which was fantastic! [DQE] is very neat with the drill down functionality.” Other managers reflected on real cases and how DQE is useful to them:

Code-drift and missingness

Site C administrators recall a scenario where DQE would be helpful. A LIMS (Laboratory Information Management System) code for a common lab was changed upstream. However, the change was not communicated, and the ETL scripts had no LOINC codes to map it to, causing the lab count to drop to zero. We reconstructed the scenario to illustrate the complete missingness for the common lab Platelet by Automated count (Figure 6b).

Divergent outliers

Site D managers spotted a pair of outlier lab tests. The first was an orange high-outlier, and the second was a blue low-outlier (Figure 6c). Neither labs were outliers several data refreshes ago, but their patient counts began to diverge subsequently. The first’s count increased very quickly to 8% of total patients. The second’s reached below 2% of total patients due to growth of total patient counts. Using the divergence’s starting timestamp, the managers identified the specific source code commit that caused the problem. A mistake mapped two local lab codes to the first concept, and none mapped to the second. Because Site D performs incremental data updates, the second concept’s patient count did not immediately drop to zero. Instead, the symptoms were much subtler and developed over time. When the outliers eventually manifested the problem, it immediately prompted root cause analysis and remediation.

Discussion

Data quality in federated data networks is a complex issue. Identification of quality issues frequently requires local site-level knowledge and expertise. For this reason, many distributed research networks and data platforms have invested in their own tools to help local sites find the issues. ODHSI’s Achilles Heel reports and its more recent Data Quality Dashboard (DQD), FDA’s Sentinel Initiative, PCORnet’s Empirical Data Characterization reports, and PEDSnet’s DQ Workflow are examples of data quality tools that rely on sets of pre-defined queries and rules to measure quality. N3C’s Scorecards additionally lets sites compare with peers but only for specific areas. Our work is unique in that from its core it is about data quality relative to the network. Rather than specific conformance checks, it enables sites to compare its data to the entire network for all common ontology concepts. This shifts the focus from “what data types are missing” to “in what missing data types is my site unusual compared to other ENACT sites.” Missing codes relative to the network can be a strong indicator of a quality issue. In other words, it enables sites to distinguish unexpected missing data from expected missing data and focus efforts on true data quality problems.

Unlike the data quality workflows from Sentinel Initiative, PCORnet, PEDSnet, and N3C, our approach does not require site contribution of data prior to performing meaningful data quality analyses. Any sites that have run the publicly available patient-counting scripts can use DQE to compare its data with the current ENACT network statistics. For example, sites that are in the nascent stage of mapping local data to ENACT ontologies can utilize DQE as a tool for data quality regression test, continuously monitoring its data and informing its ETL development. i2b2’s support for OMOP can let OMOP sites more easily map data into ENACT and utilize DQE. These design details lower the barriers for adoption, and we hope to expand the ENACT network.

One of our work’s differentiators is DQE and its rich interactivity over static data quality reports. While DQD offers an RShiny interface that supports filtering data quality checks, it is still largely a table of quality checks and results. It does not let site administrators see the relevant data elements to start asking, for example, “why does more than 20% of my patients fall into the lowest categories of age,” or decide that there exists a perfectly fine explanation. DQE is a specialized case of an ontology browser applied to data quality. It gives users unprecedented interactivity. It puts the site managers in the driver’s seat, using their knowledge of their local data to drive the search for data quality issues. When spotting unexpected data, site managers can immediately use DQE’s to form hypotheses and investigate.

Our approach is designed around patient privacy protection at every level. The counting tool adds Gaussian noise and a low-patient threshold to the counts, following the same design as the SHRINE network tool. ENACT Hub ingestion pipeline folds individual site data into aggregate statistics that no longer represent any single site or patient. Finally, site data stays securely within the site’s boundary and is never transmitted anywhere when using DQE.

Our current collection of 13 participating sites in the ENACT network and fifteen registered DQE users from ten sites mark a significant milestone in ENACT’s broader data quality initiative. This early success highlights the potential for scaling data quality efforts across the network. We are working towards a second refresh of patient counts in 2025. Eight sites have already contributed their counts, including two new sites. We also plan to enhance the SHRINE user interface by incorporating network statistics into the ontology term annotation to convey network usage of the terms. We will start with the count of sites contributing patients to each concept, as it helps researchers identify potential collaboration opportunities. Furthermore, we have started identifying concepts that are not utilized by any sites. They represent opportunities for pruning and making leaner ontologies.

Our second refresh of patient counts will allow us to explore a related component of data quality: tracking temporal trends. We are building visualizations for site administrators to verify that their total data is increasing over time (decreases due to ETL errors are quite common). We are also planning a new feature in DQE, where suspected data quality issues based on network statistics can be pre-computed and organized as a step-by-step guide for users. As our network statistics become richer with more site contribution, we are also planning a way to let sites compare their data to only “sites like mine” instead of the whole network. For example, a children’s hospital in the Northwest can choose to compare it to the statistics of other children’s hospitals in the Northwest, making a more meaningful comparison. Finally, patient-count models do not account for the frequency of codes. Patients with one instance of a test are counted equally as patients with many. In future work, weighing by visits is one approach to highlight this difference.

Conclusion

The successful design and implementation of DQE across many sites within the ENACT network demonstrates the feasibility and value of an integrated data quality framework in large-scale health research networks. The ability to assess and improve data quality at both individual sites and across the network will enhance the reliability of research findings and foster greater confidence in the use of distributed data for clinical studies.
