Evaluating knowledge enhancement and attitudinal shift among physicians participating in capacity building workshops on expanded premarital screening in the United Arab Emirates: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Azhar T Rahma, Mushal Allam, Hajra Amoodi, Aminu S Abdullahi, Mohamed Salem Alameri, Wadha Mohammed Abdulrahman, Fatma Al Jasmi, Muna Al Saffar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPremarital screening is a preventive public health measure to identify genetic, infectious, and chronic conditions affecting the health of couples and their future offspring. It plays a crucial role in reducing the prevalence of common hereditary disorders, particularly in regions with high consanguinity rates, like the Middle East. In the United Arab Emirates, where approximately half of the marriages are consanguineous, premarital screening has become a cornerstone of genetic healthcare, helping to mitigate the increased risk of genetic disorders. Despite its importance, gaps remain in healthcare professionals’ awareness and training regarding the implementation of premarital genetic screening, highlighting the need for educational interventions to ensure its effective integration into routine practice. This study evaluates the knowledge enhancement and attitude shift among physicians participating in health education workshops on expanded premarital screening, addressing gaps in their awareness, training, and perspectives on its implementation and ethical considerations.ResultsA cross-sectional study was conducted between May 2023 and June 2024 among physicians participating in three premarital genetic screening workshops. Differences in knowledge scores before and after the training were assessed using paired t-tests. The study surveyed 60 physicians, predominantly females (85%) and Emiratis (67%). Only 25% received formal training or education on premarital genetic screening. The study observed a significant increase in knowledge scores after the workshop, with overall scores rising from a mean of 45% (SD = 15) to 77% (SD = 12), showing a mean difference of 32% (p < 0.001). This improvement was significant across different age groups, genders, and regardless of prior formal training. While the belief that expanded premarital screening should be obligatory decreased (90% to 76%), the proportion of physicians who strongly disagreed that it breaks personal privacy increased significantly (10% to 42%).ConclusionThese findings suggest that targeted educational interventions can significantly enhance healthcare professionals’ knowledge and attitudes regarding genetic screening practices. To maximize impact, these interventions should be sustainable and reinforced through regular refresher courses. Continuous education ensures that physicians remain updated on the latest guidelines, advancements in genetic screening, and best practices, ultimately improving the quality of patient care and counseling services.Keywords: Premarital screening, Health education workshop, Public health, Genetic counseling, Middle east, UAE

Background

Genetic diseases are conditions caused by identifiable genetic factors. There are three main types of genetic disorders: chromosomal abnormalities, multifactorial diseases, and single-gene disorders inherited as autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, or x-linked inheritance [1]. The incidence of genetic diseases worldwide is estimated at 5.3% of newborns when followed till the age of 25 years [2]. The Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMR) has a high burden of these conditions with 26% of annual total conceptions and 50% carrier rate for beta-thalassemia and a 30% carrier rate for sickle cell in some parts of the region [3]. There are around 350 genetic diseases identified in the Gulf region, 200 of which are hereditary diseases with a recessive character [4]. Some of these are rare globally but prevalent in the Arab world, like beta-thalassemia, which is also prevalent in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) along with Cystic Fibrosis and Sickle Cell Anemia [5]. In the UAE, 669 genetic diseases have been reported with 399 diseases being autosomal recessive [6]. Most of the genetic diseases prevalent in the UAE are rare, with 376 ultra-rare diseases having a prevalence of < 1 in 100,000 individuals, 93 rare diseases with a prevalence of 1–9 in 100,000 individuals, and 37 rare diseases with a prevalence of 10–50 in 100,000 individuals [5, 6]. The UAE also has the sixth-highest prevalence of birth defects per 1000 live births [7]. High prevalence of genetic disorders, especially autosomal recessive genetic disorders, are common in Arabs due to the high rates of consanguineous marriage in this population [8]. Around 50% of global rare single gene disorders are consanguinity-associated, consequently, the total baseline birth prevalence of single gene disorders is highest in EMR [9]. Around 20% of marriages around the world are consanguineous or endogamous marriages, but in the Arab world, approximately 50% of all marriages are consanguineous, reaching even 60% in certain countries, including the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Sudan, Mauritania, and Iraq [4]. In a study of 381 Emirati marriages, 64% were consanguineous and the majority were first-cousin marriages, and 71.6% of offspring having autosomal recessive disorder were from consanguineous marriage [8].

Premarital genetic screening (PMS) is a process of undergoing genetic testing for couples intending to get married. Its purpose is not only to discover carriers of shared autosomal recessive mutations that could be inherited by future offspring and cause preventable genetic diseases but also to test for infectious diseases [10]. This test has been implemented in several countries globally, especially those that have high rates of consanguineous marriages. It was first mandated in Cyprus in 1973, followed by other Mediterranean countries like Italy and Greece in 1975 or Muslim-majority countries like Turkey in 1995, Iran in 1997, Palestinian territories in 2000, Saudi Arabia and Jordan in 2004, Bahrain in 2005, Kuwait and Iraq Kurdistan in 2008, and Qatar in 2009. In the UAE, PMS started in 2005 with genetic expansion occurring in 2023 [11, 12]. Despite the launch of initiatives across the region, differences remain in the number of genes tested and the number of diseases selected for screening.

Genetic services are needed for those at risk because rare single-gene disorders are frequently underrecognized and premature mortality and morbidity create a public health burden [9]. Thus, premarital screening, genetic counseling, and genome projects like the one in the UAE can lower the burden of genetic disorders in future generations, decrease healthcare costs, and improve public health [13].

Genetic counseling process is a structured and dynamic interaction between a trained genetic counselor and an individual, couple, or family seeking information about genetic risks, testing, or inherited conditions. It focuses on educating them about the nature, consequences, and inheritance patterns of genetic diseases, while also helping them explore the available resources for prevention, and reproduction options. This process is non-directive and individual-centered, aiming to empower individuals with the knowledge to make informed choices aligned with their values, beliefs and circumstances. In the context of premarital genetic screening, the role of genetic counseling is pivotal in empowering couples to make informed decisions about their genetic risks and future reproduction options enabling early intervention to reduce burden of genetic diseases.

Globally, the number of genetic counselors in 2023 stood at around 10,250, spread across 50 countries, with approximately 220 genetic counselors in the Middle East. While the region has seen a rise in the number of genetic counselors due to national initiatives such as carrier screening, newborn screening, biobanks, and sequencing projects, the UAE still has a limited workforce, with only 10 genetic counselors as of 2023. Further, the UAE has announced a groundbreaking initiative to mandate genetic testing for all Emiratis as part of PMS. The government has expanded its premarital screening and genetic counseling efforts through the Emirati Genome Programme. The expanded PMS currently includes screenings for approximately 570 genes for over 840 common autosomal recessive genetic conditions. The initial pilot study indicated that approximately 11% of couples were at risk of carrying pathogenic variants. Among these couples, the most common conditions identified include adrenal hyperplasia (15%), familial Mediterranean fever (13%), Usher syndrome type 2 A (12%), early-onset severe retinal dystrophy (10%), cystic fibrosis (6%), anterior segment dysgenesis (5%), and retinitis pigmentosa 25 (5%) [13].

To address the immediate need for genetic counseling services, World Health Organization (WHO) recommends implementing short-term training courses for primary health care providers to build genetic counseling capacity as an interim solution. These courses aim to enhance knowledge about genetic disorders, the principles of genetic counseling, and the ethical, legal, and social considerations of medical genetics services [10, 14].

The Department of Genetics and Genomics, College of Medicine and Health Sciences at United Arab Emirates University (UAEU) in collaboration with the Department of Health (DoH) designed a five-day workshop (one day weekly) to upscale physicians’ expertise in premarital genetic counseling and screening. The DoH mandated expanded premarital genetic screening in 2024; prior to that, the expanded genetic screening was offered optionally. The genetic refresher course was needed before the mandate to ensure that, once implemented, there would be trained and upskilled physicians in place. The course was divided into 5 main Modules: (1) Basic genetic 101. (2) Genetic Pedigree Utility, Emerging Genetic Counseling & relevance to Primary Health Care. (3) Diagnosis of genetic diseases, Prenatal diagnosis and In Vitro Fertilization. (4) Premarital screening: benefit and limitation, example genetic conditions, treatable condition. (5) Genetic Counseling Workshop: Pre and post genetic testing cases.

Each Module was given in full-day series of lectures (one full day/ week over five weeks duration). Some topics were offered as practical interactive workshops on week 2 and week 5 included variety of pedigrees and pre and post testing counseling cases supervised by geneticist or certified genetic counselors. Upon completion of the course and passing the evaluation exam which required a high passing score of 75% and above, the candidates developed the competency to offer premarital screening, pre-test genetic counseling and posttest counseling for the negative cases.

Thus, the aim of this study is to assess the impact of such capacity building workshop about expanded premarital genetic screening on primary care physician’s knowledge and attitude.

Methods

Between May 2023 and June 2024, a cross-sectional study was conducted among physicians participating in three courses funded by the DoH and UAEU, and organized by the Department of Genetics and Genomics, College of Medicine and Health Sciences at UAEU. The recruitment process was facilitated by the health authorities within the different Emirates of UAE, including the Department of Health (Abu-Dhabi, UAE), Dubai Health Authority (Dubai, UAE), and Emirates Health Services (UAE), who identified primary healthcare physicians eligible for the program. Eligibility criteria included being a currently practicing primary healthcare physician, having a role in premarital screening or counseling services, and expressing willingness to attend all workshop sessions. The Department of Health (Abu Dhabi), Dubai Health Authority, and Emirates Health Services identified and invited physicians who met these criteria to participate in the workshops Formal invitations outlined the workshop’s objectives and emphasized its relevance to clinical practice. Attendance was voluntary, with participants required to confirm their interest and register via email. A purposive sampling strategy was used. Primary healthcare physicians actively involved in premarital screening and counseling were identified and nominated by the Department of Health (Abu Dhabi), Dubai Health Authority, and Emirates Health Services. This method was deliberately chosen to ensure that the participants were directly engaged in premarital screening practice, thereby enhancing the contextual relevance and applicability of the training and evaluation.

The course was designed to equip physicians with the genetic knowledge and competencies essential for clinical practice. Participating physicians were taught to clearly differentiate between genetic screening and genetic testing, including their utilities and limitations. Participants explored the challenges and benefits of genetic testing, including the ethical consideration and its impact on patient care. The course also provided in depth case studies of diseases relevant to premarital screening, examining their genetic etiology, pathophysiology, clinical manifestations, diagnostic evaluation and available treatment options. The training was designed to equip the physicians with the skills to do the pre-test genetic counseling and obtain consent for genetic testing. Certain classes introduced the basic principles of Mendelian inheritance of human traits with emphasis on genetic disorders and their exceptions, such as reduced penetrance, genetic anticipation, variable expression, haploinsufficiency, and genomic imprinting. The course format included lectures, practical workshops, case presentations and interactive assignments. All workshops were delivered face-to-face at UAE University. The language of instruction was English, consistent with the standard medium of medical education and clinical training in the UAE.”

Participants were continuously evaluated throughout the course in addition to practical evaluation and written final examination.

Upon positive feedback from participants in June 2023, the course was re-offered for second and 3rd batches between October 2023 and June 2024. Each course was offered to 25 physicians. Sixty (60) participants who expressed interest in providing feedback on refresher courses were invited to participate in pre- and post-course survey, and hence, were enrolled in this study. All participants provided verbal informed consent before completing the surveys. At the start of each workshop, the study objectives, voluntary nature of participation, and confidentiality assurances were explained. The study utilized a pre- and post-survey approach to measure changes in participants’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices before and after the training. The pre-survey was conducted on the workshop’s opening day and gathered baseline demographic data and prior formal training or education in premarital genetic screening, and assessed their knowledge of genetic concepts, attitudes towards premarital screening, and current practices in genetic counseling. The post-survey was administered on the final day and focused on capturing improvements in knowledge, confidence, and attitudes. Additional questions evaluated participants’ perceptions of the workshop and their readiness to apply premarital genetic counseling in clinical settings. Both surveys were distributed as printed hard copies during the training.

The surveys were developed based on existing literature about the attitude domain [15–20], while the knowledge-based questions were developed by specialists and faculty members involved in the workshop. To ensure clarity, content validity, and relevance, the surveys were reviewed by experts in genomics and public health, and minor edits were incorporated based on their feedback. A formal pilot test with an independent sample was not conducted due to time limitations.

The survey included multiple sections, demographic information collected details on participants’ age, gender, nationality, years of experience (both in general practice and genetics), and clinical setting (e.g., rural or urban practice). Participants were asked to indicate whether their facility was located in a rural or urban area. Urban practice referred to healthcare facilities in major cities (e.g., Abu Dhabi, Dubai, Al Ain), characterized by higher population density and advanced infrastructure. Rural practice referred to facilities in smaller towns or remote areas with lower population density and more limited access to specialized services.

In the attitudes section, participants were asked about their perspectives on premarital screening, including its significance, potential ethical and cultural challenges, barriers to implementation, and preferred approaches for handling genetic results. The knowledge section evaluated participants’ understanding of genetic conditions, screening methodologies, and counseling principles. It incorporated both direct questions and scenario-based assessments to evaluate comprehension. The practice section included questions to explore prior experiences in premarital screening, such as the number of couples counseled and the use of genetic resources in clinical practice. The post-survey included questions about perceived improvement in confidence and competence following the workshop. The survey was reviewed by experts in the field of genomics and public health and edited accordingly.

The surveys were initially administered as printed hard copies during the training sessions. The completed forms were later digitized using SurveyMonkey for data entry and analysis. The data were analyzed using R (version 4.4.1) [21]. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, while numeric variables were summarized using mean and standard deviation. Paired t-tests were used to compare differences in knowledge scores before and after the training. Knowledge score was computed by assigning a score of 1 for each correct answer and 0 for wrong answers. Total scores were then calculated for each participant and converted into percentages.

Results

A total of 75 physicians attended three workshops. 60 participants completed both survey ( pre and post workshop), the majority being females (85%), were included in this evaluation study (Table 1). The majority (44%) were between 35 and 44 years, followed by those aged 25 to 34 years (33%), and the rest (23%) were 45 years and above. Most of the participants were Emiratis (67%), working in urban areas (76%) with at least 5 years of working experience (91%), and met at least a couple daily for marital screening (88%). Regarding region of work, 28% of the participants worked in facilities in Abu-Dhabi, 20% in Dubai, and 15% in Al Ain, while the rest worked in other locations in the country. Finally, only 25% of the participants reported receiving formal training or education on premarital genetic screening.

Discussion

This study provides critical insights into the effectiveness of targeted educational interventions for healthcare professionals in the UAE. This study demonstrated that structured workshops on expanded premarital screening substantially improved physicians’ knowledge and attitudes in the UAE. Knowledge scores increased significantly from a mean of 45% before training to 77% after training (p < 0.001), with improvements observed across all age groups, genders, and regardless of prior formal training. Physicians also reported greater confidence in discussing premarital genetic counseling, with the proportion who felt “very confident” rising from 27% to 56%. Attitudinal shifts were also noted, including a decline in the belief that expanded premarital screening should be obligatory (90% to 76%) and a marked reduction in concerns about privacy, as those strongly disagreeing that PMS infringes on personal privacy increased from 10% to 42%. Additionally, more physicians recognized the value of providing genetic information to couples (22% to 43%), highlighting a transition toward patient empowerment and informed decision-making.

Despite the significant expansion of premarital screening in the UAE, our study revealed that three quarters of the participants were inadequately knowledgeable about basic genetics concepts related to the PMS. This may be attributed to several reasons, such as the lack of genetics education in medical curricula during undergraduate studies, and the conspicuous gap in continuous training and qualification for the primary care practitioner in the field of genetic screening and testing. This deficiency in knowledge among physicians practicing different genetic testing has been highlighted by several local, regional, and international studies [22–27]. Such a lack may pose challenges for the effective integration of genomic medicine into clinical practice.

Remarkably, a quarter of the physicians with personal experience in genetic testing did not demonstrate better knowledge than those without prior experience, suggesting that experience alone does not necessarily enhance understanding. This highlights the need for a foundational understanding of carrier screening, which is essential for educating patients about their testing options and obtaining informed consent. Such knowledge is directly correlated with the successful implementation of PMS programs.

Multiple studies demonstrate that physicians’ genetic knowledge scores improved after targeted educational interventions. A study by Edward et al., 2022 reported that knowledge scores of doctors participating in genetics seminars doubled from 40.4% pre-seminar to 88.1% post-seminar, and a majority who previously had poor or fair knowledge attained good knowledge after the training [28]. Other studies mirrored this trends and are statistically significant (with p-values consistently less than 0.001), showing that such educational workshops and seminars reliably elevate core genetic knowledge among healthcare providers regardless of age, sex, or prior training [29, 30]. The program’s success in improving short-term knowledge aligns with WHO recommendations for capacity-building in the EMR [14], yet long-term sustainability requires addressing gaps in workforce development, ethical frameworks, and interdisciplinary collaboration.

The workshops have a positive impact on the attitudes of the participants towards genetic testing, counseling, and clinical application. This is aligned with a similar attitude of physicians in Saudi Arabia, as many physicians had a positive perception toward the importance of premarital screening [31]. As a result of the workshop, participants felt more confident in discussing genetic issues with patients after participating in the training. Comparing our findings with other studies. Several studies have investigated the attitudes of primary care physicians toward genetic testing and have shown that, with proper education and support, physicians can develop positive attitudes toward integrating genetic testing into their practice [32–39]. This shift is crucial as genetic counseling and explaining complex genetic concepts are often perceived as daunting tasks for medical professionals without a strong genetic background.

Before the training sessions, some of the participant doctors thought that premarital screening may invade personal privacy. This was expected as some regional studies reflected that as well, for example, a study in Saudi Arabia found that 53.19% of respondents, including healthcare professionals, reported that premarital screening for mental health disorders is a violation of privacy [40]. In Oman, a survey on premarital screening attitudes revealed that some participants had concerns about confidentiality [41]. All this because the cultural beliefs significantly impact their recommendations on premarital screening in several ways, the most important of which are: religious considerations [42], consanguinity traditions [43], social stigma concerns [44], cultural sensitivity [45], education and awareness [46], and ethical considerations [47]. These factors often require doctors to adapt their approach to premarital screening recommendations, balancing the best medical practices with cultural sensitivity and respect for local traditions.

Regarding barriers to incorporating genetic services into primary health care, the majority of participants (both before and after the training) identified the lack of genetic training as the most significant obstacle, followed by time constraints during patient consultations. Other barriers, though less frequently emphasized, included perceptions that genetics may not be directly relevant to daily practice, limited resources, and insufficient institutional support. These findings are consistent with regional and international literature, which has repeatedly highlighted gaps in training, limited consultation time, and lack of institutional frameworks as major obstacles to implementing genetic services in primary care [22, 24, 25, 33, 37]. Collectively, this evidence underscores that without adequate education and system-level support, physicians may struggle to effectively integrate genetic counseling and premarital screening into routine care.

In the UAE, there is also a pressing need for primary care physicians to have access to evidence-based guidelines for counseling consanguineous couples, as consanguinity rates are high, and genetic disorders are consequently prevalent. This study further emphasizes the need for continued education for healthcare providers, especially regarding the importance of genetic screening and counseling. Although physicians exhibit positive attitudes toward expanded screening programs, there are still misconceptions about the confidentiality of test results and concepts related to autosomal recessive inheritance and transmission risks. Educational programs should prioritize strengthening general genetics knowledge and available resources rather than focusing too heavily on specific disease carrier rates, ensuring that healthcare providers are better equipped to administer successful genetic screening programs. As a long-term solution, a master’s level genetic counselors training program was established at the UAE University in 2024, further strengthening the country’s capacity to provide specialized genetic counseling services.

Case-based discussions and role play in the workshop emphasized non-directive counseling approaches, ensuring that patient autonomy and confidentiality were maintained while addressing sensitive issues such as consanguinity and reproductive decision-making. Participants reported that this training enhanced their confidence in balancing scientific knowledge with socio-cultural considerations in real-world practice. Similar findings have been reported across the region, where physicians highlighted the importance of cultural competence in facilitating acceptance of premarital screening programs. Studies in Saudi Arabia and Oman have also shown that concerns around privacy, stigma, and religious beliefs influence both healthcare providers’ and the public’s attitudes toward premarital screening, underscoring the need for culturally sensitive communication strategies. By explicitly addressing these challenges, our workshop model helped physicians develop the skills needed to provide respectful, contextually appropriate counseling, which is essential for strengthening public trust and improving the effectiveness of premarital screening initiatives [48–51].

These findings gain deeper significance when contextualized within global and regional efforts to mitigate genetic disease burdens through carrier screening programs. While the UAE’s expanded screening panel (570 genes for 840 conditions) [13] represents one of the most comprehensive initiatives globally, the study underscores systemic challenges, including a shortage of genetic counselors and uneven baseline knowledge among primary care providers.

This study provides valuable insights into the impact of health education and capacity building workshops on physicians’ knowledge and attitudes toward PMS. Its key strengths include a pre- and post-intervention design, allowing direct assessment of knowledge gains, and a diverse sample enhancing generalizability. The study also fills a critical gap in PMS education among primary care physicians in the UAE.

This study has several limitations. First, the evaluation was limited to short-term outcomes; we did not assess long-term retention of knowledge or its translation into clinical practice, which future studies should address. Second, although the sample size of 60 physicians may appear modest, it is comparable to other educational interventions and represents a diverse group from different regions and practice settings, which supports the external validity of our findings. Third, the absence of a control group limited our ability to draw causal inferences; however, the training was mandated nationally, making such a design infeasible. Finally, while the workshops emphasized cultural sensitivity—including issues of consanguinity, privacy, and religious beliefs—the study did not formally measure how physicians applied these strategies in practice, which future research should explore.

Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and clinical practice concerning expanded premarital genetic testing and counseling among primary care physicians in the UAE. The study demonstrates that targeted education can enhance UAE physicians’ preparedness for expanded PMS, contributing to global discourse on genetic capacity-building. However, the program’s long-term success hinges on addressing workforce shortages, ethical complexities, and cultural dynamics. By adopting a lifecycle approach—from premarital screening to prenatal diagnostics—the UAE can model a comprehensive strategy for high-consanguinity populations worldwide.
