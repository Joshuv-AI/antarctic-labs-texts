An enhanced neural network model for predicting the remaining useful life of proton exchange membrane fuel cells
Authors: Honghua Pan, Yujin Zou, Xiao Sun, Jun Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, a GMA based approach to predict proton exchange membrane fuel cell (PEMFC) stack voltage and remaining useful life (RUL) was proposed, and how different combinations of input and output sizes affect model performance was analyzed. The results show that the GMA model effectively captures the voltage degradation trend of the PEMFC, accurately reproducing the early rapid voltage drop and the later smooth degradation. Model performance is strongly influenced by the input and output configurations. Smaller input sizes lead to larger fluctuations in performance metrics (e.g., RMSE and score), whereas larger input sizes provide more informative features and improve predictive accuracy. In particular, with an input size of 300 and an output size of 40, the model achieves its best performance, yielding the lowest RMSE and a near optimal Score. Overall, the GMA model offers clear advantages for improving the accuracy and reliability of PEMFC prediction, and its predictive effectiveness and stability can be further enhanced through careful selection of input and output sizes. This study provides a practical reference for PEMFC RUL prediction and supports maintenance planning, performance evaluation and life cycle management of fuel cells.

Introduction

As a clean, efficient and sustainable energy source, hydrogen is becoming a key driver of the global transition to a green energy system1,2. Among hydrogen conversion technologies, proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) have attracted considerable attention because of their high energy density, excellent conversion efficiency, zero pollutant emissions, and ability to operate under near-ambient conditions3. Nevertheless, PEMFC performance inevitably degrades over time, primarily due to complex architectures, evolving material properties, and fluctuations in operating conditions. At present, the principal barriers to the widespread deployment of fuel cell vehicles are limited durability and high cost4,5. Accurate prediction of PEMFC degradation and estimation of remaining useful life (RUL) are therefore essential for reducing maintenance expenses, preventing failures, and extending system lifetime6.

PEMFC degradation prediction methods can be broadly categorized into three groups: model-based approaches, data-driven approaches, and hybrid approaches7. Model-based approaches simulate the degradation process by constructing mathematical models and applying filtering algorithms to adapt parameters over time, thereby predicting the degradation trajectory and RUL8,9. These methods include physics-based, empirical, and semi-empirical models, each describing aging from a different perspective. In addition, algorithms such as Particle filters and Kalman filters are commonly employed for state estimation10. However, model-based prediction is often constrained by model fidelity and may lack robustness across operating conditions, which limits practical deployment11,12.

In contrast, data-driven approaches do not rely on predefined mechanistic models or detailed knowledge of the degradation pathway13. They leverage statistical modeling, machine learning, and artificial intelligence to extract patterns from large datasets and to model nonlinear system behavior14–16. Representative techniques include backpropagation neural networks (BPNN)17, kernel extreme learning machines (KELM)18, recurrent neural networks (RNN)19, long short-term memory networks (LSTM)20, and gated recurrent units (GRU)21. Lee et al.22 developed a PEMFC degradation prediction model using a single-layer BPNN that matched experimental data well, although issues such as local minima and overfitting were observed. To address these limitations, Liu et al.23 proposed a KELM model enhanced with locally weighted regression smoothing, which improved RUL accuracy while reducing computational burden. Although RNNs are effective for time-series modeling, they are susceptible to the vanishing-gradient problem and often struggle to capture long-term dependencies24. To mitigate this, Zheng et al.25 introduced an LSTM-based model that effectively captured long-range dependencies and was validated using polarization and voltage decay curves. While LSTM models can offer superior performance, they typically entail higher complexity and computational cost, and GRU provides a simpler alternative with fewer parameters while retaining strong generalization capability26.

Hybrid approaches combine model-driven and data-driven techniques, or integrate multiple data-driven models, by fusing physical theory with the function-approximation capacity of machine learning to improve prediction accuracy27. Despite their promise, these methods still face challenges related to model fidelity, complex degradation mechanisms, environmental variability, and measurement noise, all of which can reduce robustness28. They also tend to require substantial labeled data, greater computational resources, and longer development cycles, complicating implementation and interpretability in real-world settings, which can limit effectiveness29.

In summary, model-based approaches elucidate degradation mechanisms by constructing theoretical representations of the PEMFC degradation process30. Despite their strong interpretability, the development of such methods is typically complex and depends on carefully specified parameters, which restricts their generalization to practical applications31. By contrast, data-based methods analyze operational data with statistical and learning tools to construct predictive models, offering greater flexibility and adaptability and making them well suited to real time monitoring and online prediction32. Nevertheless, the predictive accuracy of these methods is strongly conditioned by data quality and coverage and is often unsupported by a firm theoretical foundation. Hybrid approaches seek to improve predictive performance by combining physical theory with the adaptability of machine learning models33. Although such hybrids frequently deliver better results, they usually require more advanced data analytic capability together with deeper insight into the governing physical processes, which in turn entails higher development costs. To address these limitations, this study proposes a GMA model that integrates a GRU with attention mechanisms, with the objective of achieving accurate RUL prediction for PEMFC.

Data preprocessing

The FC1 endurance test ran for 1154 h and produced 143,862 raw data records, with stack voltage ranging from 3.19 V to 3.31 V. By comparison, the FC2 test ran for 1020 h and produced 127,370 raw data records, with stack voltage spanning 3.15 V to 3.33 V. For downstream analysis, the raw measurements were consolidated by averaging the stack voltage over 30 min intervals, which yielded 2308 data points for FC1 and 2041 data points for FC2. Because the raw signals contained appreciable noise and high frequency fluctuations that may be associated with regenerative phenomena, a robust locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (rloess) procedure was applied to enhance signal clarity. Figure 4 presents a comparative visualization of the raw, reconstructed, and smoothed time series for FC1 and FC2, which demonstrates that the preprocessing effectively reduces noise and reveals the underlying degradation trends.

Degradation

During PEMFC operation, hydrogen is supplied to the anode, where the hydrogen oxidation reaction occurs, releasing electrons and generating protons. The electrons travel through the external circuit to produce electric current and ultimately reach the cathode, while the protons migrate through the proton exchange membrane toward the cathode. At the cathode, the protons participate in the oxygen reduction reaction with oxygen from the air, forming water as the final product. Degradation of PEMFCs is unavoidable and progresses with time in service. Multiple factors contribute to this process, including inadequate water and thermal management, insufficient reactant supply, and variability in operating conditions. Poor water management can lead to liquid water accumulation at the anode, causing flooding of the electrode surface and catalyst layer, which diminishes the effective contact between the reactant and the active sites, thereby reducing the reaction rate. Conversely, inadequate humidification within the stack can cause membrane dehydration and accelerate membrane aging. Thermal management is equally critical: lower temperatures intensify polarization losses within the stack and depress the cell voltage, whereas elevated temperatures can damage the membrane material. The supply of reactant gases has a direct influence on the durability of the membrane electrode assembly (MEA), and even a modest shortfall in hydrogen can produce nonuniform current distribution and, in severe cases, cell reversal. Operating conditions, particularly dynamic load fluctuations, exert a pronounced effect on PEMFC lifetime and can hasten the degradation trajectory. Environmental factors, including ambient temperature and impurities in the reactant streams, further aggravate the degradation process. Taken together, these influences cause progressive performance loss and underscore the importance of optimizing operating conditions and rigorously managing water, heat, and reactant supply to maximize PEMFC service life.

The GMA model

GRU

The GRU is an advanced variant of RNN designed to enhance sequence learning by addressing common challenges such as vanishing gradients, preservation of long-range dependencies, and computational efficiency35. Compared with LSTM networks, GRUs adopt a more compact architecture while still implementing effective memory management and temporal modeling. This streamlined design has made GRUs a popular choice for many sequence-driven tasks, since they reduce architectural complexity while maintaining strong predictive performance.

The core mechanism of the GRU consists of two gates, the update gate and the reset gate. These gating controls regulate the flow of information and balance the retention of salient historical context with the incorporation of new inputs. The update gate plays a role analogous to the combined effect of the LSTM forget gate and input gate, determining how much past information should be retained when updating the hidden state during sequence processing. Through this operation, the GRU efficiently captures long term dependencies and preserves critical historical information as new observations arrive. The Sigmoid activation used in the update gate constrains its output to the interval from 0 to 1, which effectively modulates the influence of the previous hidden state. In parallel, the reset gate controls the relevance of prior hidden states when computing the current candidate activation. This architecture facilitates smooth information flow across time steps, preserving essential features from the past while integrating new data in a computationally efficient manner. The combined action of the update and reset gates mitigates the vanishing gradient problem and enhances the network’s capacity to learn dependencies over long sequences without incurring excessive computational burden.

Overall, the GRU architecture achieves an effective balance between complexity and performance. With fewer parameters than LSTM networks, GRUs typically converge more rapidly and impose lower computational requirements, which makes them well suited to tasks with constrained resources or large-scale datasets. Their effectiveness has been demonstrated across diverse application areas, including natural language processing, time series forecasting, and speech recognition, where maintaining long term context while adapting to swiftly varying inputs is essential.

Attention

The attention mechanism represents a substantive advance in neural network design and plays a central role in sequence modeling36. It enhances a model’s capacity to capture complex dependencies by dynamically allocating computational focus to the most relevant portions of an input sequence, independent of sequence length. Originally introduced to mitigate the difficulty recurrent architectures face in retaining long range dependencies, attention has become a cornerstone of modern architectures such as the Transformer and has reshaped fields including natural language processing. Conceptually, attention assigns distinct weights to different elements of the input, thereby modulating their relative importance. This selective focus enables the model to highlight contextually salient information and is particularly effective for sequences in which key elements are dispersed or not strictly local. In contrast to traditional recurrent models that process information strictly in order and are often constrained by vanishing gradients, attention supports parallel computation and direct access to any position in the sequence. As a result, it improves computational efficiency while providing a principled mechanism for modeling global dependencies.

The attention operation is built on three learned projections of the input: the query vector Q, the key vector K, and the value vector V. These vectors arise from multiplication by trainable weight matrices that map inputs into a shared representation space. The query encodes the element currently under consideration and specifies the direction of the model’s focus. The keys serve as reference anchors for all elements in the sequence and are used to assess their relevance to the query. The values carry the content that forms the basis of the output representation. By computing similarity scores between queries and keys and normalizing them into attention weights, the mechanism forms a weighted combination of the values that emphasizes the most informative components. In this way, attention adaptively concentrates representational capacity where it is most needed, thereby strengthening the model’s ability to capture salient information across the entire sequence. The Softmax function normalizes the attention scores into a probability distribution that assigns nonnegative weights summing to one, thereby determining the contribution of each value vector to the output representation.

The adaptability of the attention mechanism is reflected in its capacity to focus selectively on salient segments of a sequence, even in the presence of complex or nonlinear relationships. This property is especially important for tasks such as machine translation, where faithfully capturing contextual relationships between words across languages is critical. By leveraging attention scores, the model can dynamically align tokens in a source sentence with their counterparts in a target sentence, thereby capturing both syntactic structure and semantic nuance with greater fidelity. An important refinement is multi-head attention, which increases representational power by computing attention in multiple learned subspaces in parallel. Each head operates independently, allowing the model to capture distinct relationships within the sequence at the same time. The outputs of these independent heads are then concatenated and passed through a learned linear projection to form a richer overall representation that integrates diverse contextual information. The applicability of attention mechanisms extends well beyond natural language processing and now encompasses image analysis, time series forecasting, and reinforcement learning. Its breadth of use makes it a valuable tool for tasks that require modeling contextual dependencies. In essence, the attention mechanism offers a flexible, interpretable, and scalable approach to sequence modeling and has transformed how neural networks handle complex multidimensional data. By selectively emphasizing relevant information, attention enables models to capture intricate relationships across contexts, thereby solidifying its role as a foundational component of modern artificial intelligence architectures.

The GMA model

This study proposes the GMA model, which combines a gated recurrent unit with a multi-head attention mechanism. The architecture is specifically designed to capture both short term and long term dependencies in device operational data. By incorporating multi-head attention, the model strengthens its capacity to focus on the most informative portions of the sequence, thereby improving the accuracy and robustness of remaining useful life prediction.

The GMA model comprises two principal components, the GRU network layer and the multi-head attention layer. The GRU layer extracts time series features from the operation data and effectively identifies short term and long term dependencies. The multi-head attention layer then assigns data driven importance weights to different time steps conditioned on the GRU features, concentrating representational capacity on key points in the input sequence. This combination enables the model to learn both global trends and salient local features, which in turn enhances the fidelity of remaining useful life prediction. Results and discussions

In the experimental protocol, data from FC1 and FC2 are used as the training and test sets, respectively, and the first 550 h of FC2 are reserved for model validation. RUL is defined as the time required for the stack voltage loss to reach a specified threshold. For FC2, the fault threshold (FT) is set to 4.0%, 4.5%, 5.0% and 5.5% of the initial voltage of 3.33 V. To assess predictive effectiveness, two evaluation metrics are employed. First, the root mean square error (RMSE) measures the discrepancy between the actual and predicted voltages and thus reflects pointwise predictive accuracy. Second, the Score provides a comprehensive assessment of all RUL predictions and reflects the overall performance of the model across the degradation trajectory. The Score is a holistic measure of model performance. A lower RMSE, approaching zero, indicates a smaller gap between the predicted stack voltage and the measured value and therefore higher predictive accuracy. During training, the model employs a sliding window strategy whose window length equals the sum of the GMA model’s input and output sizes. This design enables dynamic learning by retaining relevant historical information while continuously incorporating new observations. To improve predictive accuracy, the effects of different input and output sizes are examined systematically. By tuning these parameters, the model’s capacity to capture historical trends and to forecast remaining useful life with fidelity can be optimized. The objective is to identify the combination of input and output sizes that maximizes accuracy and reliability, thereby strengthening overall forecasting performance.

Figure 6a,b report the RMSE and the Score for a grid of input–output configurations, highlighting model behavior under multidimensional parameter settings. In Fig. 6a, RMSE spans 0.012 to 0.028. Because RMSE quantifies pointwise prediction error, smaller values indicate better fit. A closer inspection shows that small input sizes, such as 100 and 200, produce pronounced RMSE variability across output sizes, which indicates that the output dimension exerts a strong influence on model fit when contextual information is limited. The smallest RMSE values occur when the output dimension is 30 or 50, suggesting that medium to large output horizons can regularize the learning target and improve accuracy when the input window is short. As the input size increases to 400 and 500, RMSE generally declines, which implies that broader temporal context provides richer information about degradation trajectories and improves predictive fidelity. The decline is not strictly monotonic. When the input size increases from 200 to 400, RMSE rises for certain output sizes, such as 10 and 20, a pattern that is plausibly explained by redundancy in the enlarged input or by sensitivity to overfitting when the effective signal to noise ratio does not scale with window length. Notably, the minimum RMSE is achieved at an input dimension of 300 and an output dimension of 40, which indicates an advantageous balance between the amount of historical context presented to the GRU with attention and the length of the multi step forecast to be produced.

Figure 6b displays the Score for the same configurations, with values ranging from 0.95 to 1.00, where values closer to 1.00 reflect better overall performance across the degradation trajectory. For small input sizes, particularly 100 and 200, the Score exhibits clear fluctuations, most evident at output sizes of 20 and 30. This behavior suggests that some output settings can induce instability when the contextual window is short, likely because the model lacks sufficient temporal cues to disambiguate short term noise from persistent trends. With an input size of 500, the Score is generally near 1.00, indicating that wider input windows help the encoder extract more discriminative features and yield more stable aggregate performance. In contrast, at an input size of 200, the Score drops markedly for output sizes of 20 and 30, a result consistent with reduced generalization capacity that manifests as either overfitting to local fluctuations or underfitting to the underlying degradation law. In agreement with the RMSE analysis, the Score improves substantially at an input size of 300 and an output size of 40, reinforcing the advantage of this pairing for faithful model fitting and reliable RUL estimation. Taken together, Fig. 6 shows that input and output dimensions exert nonlinear and interacting effects on accuracy and stability. Enlarging the input window often aids feature extraction, reduces RMSE, and raises the Score, yet excessively long windows may introduce redundant or weakly informative history that degrades generalization and increases computation without commensurate gains. The output dimension, which determines the prediction horizon, must be calibrated with equal care. Too short an output may encourage myopic fitting to immediate fluctuations, whereas too long an output may blur informative local structure and amplify error accumulation across steps. In this study, an input dimension of 300 and an output dimension of 40 are selected as the optimal configuration because they deliver the lowest observed RMSE (0.01272) together with a high and stable Score (0.98737), achieving a pragmatic balance between accuracy, robustness, and computational efficiency. Conclusions

In this study, a PEMFC stack voltage prediction method based on a GMA model is proposed, with a particular focus on how different combinations of input and output sizes affect model performance and RUL prediction. The results indicate that both accuracy and stability are highly sensitive to these design choices. Smaller input sizes lead to larger fluctuations in RMSE and Score across output configurations, which shows that the influence of output size on model fitting cannot be disregarded. As the input size increases, RMSE generally decreases, suggesting that broader historical context provides richer information and thus improves predictive fidelity. This improvement is not strictly monotonic, since excessively large inputs may introduce redundancy, elevate the risk of overfitting, and impair generalization. After a comprehensive evaluation, the configuration with an input size of 300 and an output size of 40 performs best, achieving the lowest RMSE together with a Score close to 1.0, which supports the superiority of this pairing in terms of both accuracy and stability.

For time series prediction, the GMA model captures the dominant trend of PEMFC voltage decay with high fidelity. For most intervals, the predicted trajectory closely follows the measured voltage, especially during phases characterized by small fluctuations, which demonstrates strong tracking of the underlying degradation process. Larger discrepancies appear during intervals of pronounced volatility, most notably between 800 and 900 h. These deviations suggest limitations in representing abrupt regime changes and complex nonlinear dynamics. To address these cases, the framework could benefit from enhancements such as nonlinear regression refinements, change point or regime switching models, and more expressive temporal feature extractors that better accommodate rapid transients.

Taken together, the findings validate the effectiveness of the GMA model for predicting the RUL of PEMFC stacks. By optimizing the pairing of input and output dimensions, both predictive accuracy and reliability are significantly improved. At the same time, challenges remain in handling nonlinear fluctuations and sudden shifts. Future work should prioritize architectural refinements and advanced dynamic feature extraction to strengthen robustness and generalization, thereby further improving RUL estimation. Such improvements would contribute meaningfully to performance evaluation, maintenance optimization strategies, and the life cycle management of PEMFC systems.
