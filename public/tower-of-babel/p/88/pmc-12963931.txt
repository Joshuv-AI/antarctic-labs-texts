Effects of Engaging Fathers and Bundling Nutrition and Parenting Interventions on Child Nutrition Outcomes and Related Parent Behaviors in Rural Tanzania: Results From EFFECTS, a Five‐Arm Cluster‐Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Nilupa S Gunaratna, Susmita Ghosh, Lauren Galvin, Joshua Jeong, Savannah Froese O'Malley, Dominic Mosha, Aisha K Yousafzai, Cristiana K Verissimo, George PrayGod, Frank Mapendo, Elfrida Kumalija, Evidence S Matangi, Cara Endyke‐Doran, Mary Mwanyika Sando, Mary Pat Kieffer, Ramya Ambikapathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSuboptimal diets contribute to child malnutrition globally. The Engaging Fathers for Effective Child Nutrition and Development in Tanzania (EFFECTS) study developed interventions that engaged fathers and delivered bundled nutrition and parenting content and tested their individual and combined effects on child dietary diversity and upstream parental behaviors. In a cluster‐randomized controlled trial in Mara, Tanzania, 80 villages were randomly assigned (1:1:1:1:1) to standard care, a nutrition intervention engaging either mothers or both parents, or a bundled nutrition and parenting intervention engaging either mothers or both parents. Eligible households (n = 960) had a child under 18 months at enrollment (October 2018–May 2019). Community health workers (CHWs) delivered gender‐transformative social and behavior change interventions through parental peer group sessions before March 2020 and subsequently through home visits. The primary outcome, child dietary diversity, and secondary outcomes on child feeding practices, morbidity, growth, and parental behaviors were assessed prior to, during, and after intervention. Intention‐to‐treat analyses used generalized linear mixed models. Engaging fathers while providing bundled content increased children's dietary diversity by 0.60 food groups/day (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.18–1.02) and 0.96 food groups/week (CI: 0.51–1.41) more than standard care. Significant interaction between father engagement and bundled content was detected for children's consumption of dairy (p = 0.04). Positive intervention effects were detected on several maternal and paternal behaviors related to household food access via home production or local food markets, complementary feeding, and hygiene practices. Gender‐transformative interventions, delivered by CHWs engaging both parents with bundled nutrition and parenting content, can synergistically improve children's diets. Introduction

Child undernutrition affects over 20% of the world's children under 5 years, increasing their risk of morbidity, mortality, and adverse outcomes in adulthood (R. E. Black et al. 2013; FAO, IFAD, UNICEF, WFP, & WHO 2023). Poor dietary quality, suboptimal child feeding practices, and high infection rates are major causes, driven by underlying determinants including food insecurity, limited caregiving resources, inadequate access to clean water and sanitation, and gender inequities (R. E. Black et al. 2013). In Tanzania, 30% of children under five are chronic undernourished, and dietary quality in particular remains poor, with only 19% of children aged 6–23 months meeting dietary diversity recommendations, a proxy for adequate micronutrient intake (Ministry of Health MoH Tanzania Mainland, Ministry of Health MoH Zanzibar, National Bureau of Statistics NBS, Office of the Chief Government Statistician OCGS, & ICF 2023).

Social and behavior change (SBC) interventions have improved young children's diets and nutrition, and in the past decade, there has been considerable research on how to design and implement such interventions sustainably and at scale (Fabrizio et al. 2014; Mahumud et al. 2022; Menon et al. 2020). Interventions increasingly address underlying determinants by integrating components of water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH), gender equality and women's empowerment, and agriculture (Ahmed et al. 2023; Freeman et al. 2020; Pickering et al. 2019; Ruel et al. 2018). However, many interventions combine SBC with provision of commodities such as agricultural inputs, foods, or nutritional supplements (Bird et al. 2019; Dewey et al. 2021; Muema et al. 2023), which complicate evaluation of SBC effects and may not be sustainable at scale.

Despite fathers' important roles in decisions and actions that determine children's diets, most interventions target only mothers (Martin et al. 2021; Quisumbing 2003). Engaging fathers can improve maternal outcomes during pregnancy and the perinatal period; however, few studies have evaluated interventions that engage fathers to improve complementary feeding (Martin et al. 2021; Nguyen et al. 2018; So et al. 2024c; Tokhi et al. 2018). Emerging research indicates that fathers have diverse information needs and could benefit from support and tailored interventions that provide accessible strategies, especially in the context of food insecurity, financial and mental strain, and other adversities (So et al. 2024a, 2024b, 2024c). Interventions that engage fathers can have a greater impact on the well‐being of children and families if they are gender transformative, directly addressing gender norms, women's empowerment, and communication and relationship dynamics between men and women (Doyle et al. 2018; Martin et al. 2021; Singla et al. 2015). These interventions may also increase fathers' engagement, though data collection on fathers is often limited (Martin et al. 2021; Rothstein et al. 2023).

The Nurturing Care Framework promotes multisectoral approaches to improve child outcomes, particularly bundling parenting with nutrition interventions to improve both child diets and early childhood development (ECD) (M. M. Black et al. 2017; Jeong et al. 2021). Such bundled interventions may also improve resource efficiency through a shared theory of change and joint implementation (Hurley et al. 2016). However, few evaluations have compared bundled nutrition and parenting interventions with nutrition interventions alone, limiting insight on any additive or synergistic effects of bundling nutrition and parenting intervention components (Dulal et al. 2021; Galasso et al. 2019; Vazir et al. 2013; Yousafzai et al. 2014). Although a few bundled interventions have intentionally engaged fathers as well as mothers, fathers' participation has been challenging, suggesting that intervention design should include specific strategies to increase father engagement (Luoto et al. 2021; Rempel et al. 2017; Singla et al. 2015).

To address these gaps, the Engaging Fathers for Effective Child Nutrition and Development in Tanzania (EFFECTS) trial tested the effects of (1) bundled nutrition and parenting interventions versus nutrition‐only interventions, and (2) engaging fathers as well as mothers versus engaging only mothers on child nutrition and development outcomes (EFFECTS Study Investigators 2024). Community health workers (CHWs) delivered these interventions to families with young children in rural Tanzania. This paper reports intervention effects on the primary outcome of child dietary diversity and secondary outcomes related to complementary feeding, child growth, and mothers' and fathers' knowledge and practices around complementary feeding and WASH. Additionally, we report intervention effects on children's consumption of specific food groups, child morbidity, fathers' engagement in complementary feeding, and mothers' and fathers' behaviors to increase food access from home food production or local markets (EFFECTS Study Investigators 2024).

Methods

Study Design and Setting

The EFFECTS study has been previously described (EFFECTS Study Investigators 2024). The study was approved by institutional review boards (IRBs) at the National Institute of Medical Research, Tanzania (reference number: NIMR/HQ/R.8a/Vol.IX/2852), Project Concern International (reference number: 31), and the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health (reference number: IRB18‐1020), with a reliance agreement between the Purdue University and Harvard IRBs. EFFECTS was a cluster‐randomized controlled trial with a 2 × 2 factorial design plus a standard care control arm, conducted in the Butiama and Musoma Rural districts of Mara, a predominantly rural region in northwestern Tanzania (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03759821). The study's sample size, 192 households across 16 villages per study arm, totaling 960 households across 80 villages, was based on power calculations for cross‐sectional, pairwise comparison of any two arms for the primary outcome of child dietary diversity; however, as described below, the study's research questions pool across arms in a longitudinal model, increasing statistical power (EFFECTS Study Investigators 2024).

Randomization and Blinding

Randomization and recruitment have been previously detailed (EFFECTS Study Investigators 2024). In brief, stratifying by district, 80 villages were randomly selected and randomly allocated to one of five study arms (1:1:1:1:1) through a community‐participatory event with village leaders. Specifically, Village Executive Officers from each of the 80 randomly selected villages were invited to the event and actively participated in the random allocation by drawing from an opaque bag a piece of paper, upon which was written a letter that corresponded to one of the five study arms. The arms were control (standard care), nutrition content delivered to mothers only, nutrition content delivered to both mothers and fathers (couples), nutrition and parenting (i.e., bundled) content delivered to mothers only, and bundled content delivered to couples. Given the nature of the interventions, only outcome evaluators could be blinded. In each village, 12 households were randomly selected and enrolled. A household was eligible if it had a child under 18 months at enrollment, the father was in residence at least 10 months per year, the household planned to remain in the study area, and both parents provided informed consent.

Interventions

CHWs delivered SBC interventions to 96 peer groups across the four intervention arms. Female CHWs led mothers' peer groups, and male CHWs led fathers' peer groups, except for 6 cases in which male CHWs led mothers' groups due to a shortage of female CHWs. CHWs were selected by the District Medical Office, had basic education and previous voluntary community work experience, and received a monthly stipend during intervention delivery.

Four gender‐transformative SBC packages were developed after extensive formative research and piloting and addressed both proximal and distal determinants of child nutrition and development (see Supporting Information Table S1 by EFFECTS Study Investigators (2024) for details on each session in each package). In brief, the packages were (1) a mothers' nutrition package, delivered to the two study arms receiving nutrition content; (2) a mothers' nutrition and parenting package, delivered to the two study arms receiving bundled content; (3) a fathers' nutrition package, delivered to the study arm that engaged couples with nutrition content; and (4) a fathers' nutrition and parenting package, delivered to the study arm that engaged couples with bundled content. Each package included a training manual for CHWs, a facilitators' guide for conducting sessions with peer groups, a flipchart to use during sessions, and a recipe book for infant and young child feeding (IYCF) designed for the local context. Packages delivering bundled content also included a play and communication guide to promote ECD.

The interventions targeted upstream factors influencing optimal child nutrition and development. The mothers' nutrition package covered IYCF, including responsive feeding; increasing access to diverse and nutritious foods through home production, market purchases, budgeting, and planning; WASH; stress management; and communication, decision‐making, and conflict resolution with one's partner. The bundled package for mothers included the same messages but with less intensity to allow the inclusion of parenting content, including responsive caregiving, play and communication, and positive discipline. Specifically, the bundled package's nutrition and WASH content was more streamlined, with less repetition of nutrition and WASH activities and key messages (see Supporting Information Table S1 by EFFECTS Study Investigators (2024) for details on each session in each package). However, while nutrition content was reduced by about 30%, the bundled package maintained the same key messages as the nutrition only package. These packages were adapted for fathers to provide gender‐transformative techniques and messaging and included sessions on gender norms and roles, positive masculinity, healthy relationships, and fatherhood. All packages were highly participatory, utilizing critical reflection, discussion, problem‐solving, skill‐building, commitment setting, and coaching. Following the onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic, all packages added content on preventing infection.

Sessions lasting approximately 2 h were conducted once every 2 weeks. The 12‐month interventions started in July 2019 but were interrupted by the COVID‐19 pandemic in March 2020 after 18 sessions. The interventions were halted for 4 months and then pivoted to CHWs delivering content one‐on‐one at participants' homes, with the remaining 6 sessions conducted twice per month through September 2020.

CHWs were trained in the fundamentals of behavior change, communication, facilitation, and negotiation skills, and their respective intervention packages, with follow‐up training every 3 months. Field supervisors mentored and oversaw CHWs' activities, conducting routine observations to provide feedback on delivery. Tools and checklists, based on the study's process evaluation framework, routinely capture program delivery and quality at all levels of intervention, including training and supportive supervision, group session delivery, reach, quality, and participant feedback and satisfaction (EFFECTS Study Investigators 2024). Data were summarized and visualized on an electronic dashboard with key indicators on reach, participation, timeliness, and quality, which were reviewed weekly by the project manager and field supervisors and used to develop tailored capacity‐building plans for each CHW.

Families in the control arm received only standard care from CHWs. Standard care services were delivered by CHWs at primary healthcare facilities and at the household level and focused on basic messages regarding hygiene and child immunizations. CHWs in the Mara Region did not receive any standardized curriculum on child nutrition or early development, and families therefore received minimal information, counseling, or support on IYCF or care. CHWs were an informal cadre, and home visits were not routine.

Outcome Evaluation

Outcome data were collected at three times, each during the short rainy season: baseline December 2018–May 2019, midline November–December 2019, and endline November 2020–January 2021. At baseline, 89% of participants (with similar numbers across study arms) had data collected December 2018–February 2019, while remaining data collection was delayed until May 2019 due to administrative challenges in the field. At each time point, questionnaires were administered to both parents to capture their knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to child feeding, nutrition, and development and to assess underlying family and household factors as hypothesized in the interventions' theory of change (EFFECTS Study Investigators 2024). Child anthropometry was conducted at a central location in each community, with heights (or recumbent lengths) and weights measured in duplicate and averaged before Z‐scores were calculated based on WHO child growth standards (WHO 2006). Tools for data collection with mothers, with fathers, and at central locations at baseline are provided in Supporting Information Files 1–3; the same questions were used at subsequent time points. Trained enumerators conducted all data collection in Kiswahili, with electronic data entry using Open Data Kit.

Outcomes

This paper presents results from all pre‐registered primary and secondary outcomes, except those already reported (Galvin et al. 2023; Jeong et al. 2024). The primary outcome here is child dietary diversity over 24 h, defined in our trial registration as the number out of the following eight food groups consumed by a child over 24 h: starchy staples, legumes and nuts, dairy products, flesh foods, eggs, vitamin A‐rich produce, other produce, and fats and oils, the last of these as they were a significant source of energy in this population (WHO 2010). Registered secondary outcomes included child dietary diversity over 7 days; children's, mothers', and fathers' consumption of animal source foods (ASF); child height‐for‐age Z‐score (HAZ) and weight‐for‐height Z‐score (WHZ); and mothers' and fathers' IYCF and WASH knowledge and practices.

To understand changes to children's dietary diversity and to compare this primary outcome to more recent World Health Organization (WHO) IYCF indicators, we analyzed intervention effects on children's consumption of individual food groups over 24 h and WHO IYCF indicators of minimum dietary diversity and minimum meal frequency (WHO 2021). We also report on intervention effects on intermediate outcomes including fathers' engagement in food preparation and complementary feeding; parents' behaviors to increase food access from home production or local markets; mothers' report that their partner gave them money to purchase healthy foods; mothers' report of allocating or saving money for the purchase of healthy foods; maternal and paternal purchase of healthy foods; parental report of growing a new food crop in the last 6 months; child morbidity in the preceding 14 days; and child weight‐for‐age Z‐score (WAZ).

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were intention‐to‐treat and tested for differences‐in‐differences over the intervention duration. For each outcome, we fit a generalized linear mixed model with district, study arm, time of evaluation (baseline, midline, or endline), and interaction between study arm and time as fixed effects. Logistic regression was used for binary outcomes. All models adjusted for clustering within villages and repeated measurements on individuals or households. All hypothesis tests were two‐sided with a 0.05 significance level. Analyses used Stata 17.0 (mixed, lincom, and contrast commands).

For each outcome, we asked four research questions, using contrasts to test for significant differences‐in‐differences (EFFECTS Study Investigators 2024). The first research question (RQ1) tested whether engaging families through any intervention affected the specified outcome beyond changes observed among families receiving standard care. The second research question (RQ2) tested whether engaging both parents affected an outcome differently than engaging mothers alone. The third research question (RQ3) tested whether delivering bundled nutrition and parenting content affected an outcome differently than delivering nutrition content alone. Finally, the fourth research question (RQ4) tested whether the effect of father engagement depended on which content was delivered or, similarly, whether the effect of bundling nutrition and parenting content depended on whether or not fathers were engaged. Statistically, this tests for interaction between father engagement and bundling of content.

Adjusted analyses were conducted using the mixed models specified above, along with additional outcome‐specific predictors. These included household size, household wealth quintile, age and education of both parents, parents' relationship (monogamous or polygamous), survey group (a time‐varying indicator of delayed data collection, which only occurred at baseline), child age (time‐varying), and child sex. Wealth quintiles were based on an index constructed using the first component from a principal component analysis of household assets: improved toilet, improved roof, improved floor, improved walls, tropical livestock units, and amount of land owned. Additional covariates varied by outcome. Specifically, the adjusted models for outcomes related to child diets, feeding practices, morbidity, and anthropometric outcomes included breastfeeding in the preceding 24 h, with the models for anthropometric outcomes additionally including morbidity in the past 14 days. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis for the anthropometric outcomes by adding household food security (Coates et al. 2007). Diagnostic plots were used to evaluate whether model assumptions (e.g., normality and constant variance of error terms) were satisfied. We used the missing indicator method to handle any missing values of predictor variables in regression models; missing values of continuous predictors were replaced with their sample mean (Little and Rubin 2020). We did not analyze children's consumption of staples or eggs because almost all children consumed staples (particularly at the endline), and few children consumed eggs at any time. We did not correct for multiple comparisons to allow exploratory analysis of outcomes beyond dietary diversity (Rothman 1990), though it should be noted that this increases the overall risk of type I errors.

Results

Initially, 960 households from 80 randomized villages were consented and enrolled per the study design (Figure 1). However, prior to baseline data collection, 30 households no longer meeting eligibility criteria were replaced. During the trial, 28 households (3%) were withdrawn. At each time point, additional households were unreachable or had partial data due to temporary absence of a mother, father, or child, resulting in data on 815 mothers (85%), 733 fathers (76%), and 838 children (87%) at endline.

No deaths of children or caregivers were determined to be associated with the interventions.

Characteristics of participating households, mothers, fathers, and children were similar across arms at baseline (Supporting Information Table S1). Among children, 49% were female, and the mean age was 11.5 (±5.6 SD) months at baseline. Given the interruption in intervention delivery due to the onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic, the mean age was 33.3 (±5.7 SD) months at endline. Parents had low but similar levels of education, with 82% of mothers and 77% of fathers discontinuing education after completing primary school (Supporting Information Table S1). Most parents (82%) were in a monogamous (vs. polygamous) marriage or cohabitation. Farming was the primary livelihood, with households owning a mean of 3.8 (±6.2) acres of land (86% landowning).

Interventions were delivered with high fidelity (Supporting Information Table S2). Field supervisors provided supervision and mentorship and monitored intervention quality by attending an average of 10.9 (±3.0) sessions per CHW. Quality of delivery was measured by the number of positive practices that a supervisor observed a CHW perform out of the total assessed positive practices. Average quality scores for group sessions were 91% (±11%). Participants were randomly surveyed to provide feedback on session quality; overall scores averaged 4.4 (±0.5) in women's groups and 4.5 (±0.6) in men's groups on a five‐point Likert scale from 1 = “very bad” to 5 = “extremely good/excellent.” Out of 12 participants, average attendance in peer groups was 9.0 (±1.6) for women, and 8.4 (±1.9) for men. After moving to home‐based delivery following the onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic, attendance and quality metrics remained high and comparable across study arms and with pre‐pandemic values, with CHWs visiting an average of 10.2 (±1.9) women and 9.2 (±2.4) men per session at their homes.

Child‐level (Table 1) and caregiver‐level (Table 2) outcomes were comparable across study arms at baseline. Dietary diversity increased as children aged. There was significant interaction between the father engagement and bundled content interventions on children's 1‐day dietary diversity (p = 0.004), and dietary diversity among children whose parents received both interventions increased by 0.60 food groups/day (95% CI: 0.18, 1.02) more than standard care (Table 3). Intervention effects were additive for 7‐day dietary diversity; among children whose parents received both interventions, 7‐day dietary diversity increased by 0.96 food groups/week (95% CI: 0.51, 1.41) more than standard care. Consequently, there was a concordant interaction effect between father engagement and bundled interventions on minimum dietary diversity (p = 0.007, Table 3). When parents received both interventions, the percentage of children achieving minimum dietary diversity increased from 26% to 45%, compared with an increase from 27% to 31% when parents received standard care (Table 1).

Specifically, there were significant interactions between the father engagement and bundled content interventions on children's 1‐day consumption of dairy (p = 0.04) and vitamin A‐rich produce (p = 0.004, Table 3). When parents received both interventions, dairy consumption in the previous day increased from 26% of children at baseline to 44% at endline, while consumption increased from 35% to only 38% when parents received standard care (Table 1). Consumption of vitamin A‐rich foods was common across all study arms at endline (Table 1), and increases in intervention arms mirrored the increase in the control arm (each pairwise comparison p > 0.05, Table 3). Consumption of other fruits and vegetables (not rich in vitamin A) was high even at baseline and increased over time (Table 1), particularly in the intervention arms (p = 0.04, Table 3), indicating that delivering only nutrition content to mothers alone can increase children's consumption of these foods. Consumption of staples was near universal (99%) at endline, while egg consumption remained low (4%, Table 1). No intervention effects were detected on children's consumption of legumes, nuts, flesh foods, fats, or oils (Table 3).

Child morbidity was high but decreased as children aged (Table 1). Delivery of bundled content, with or without father engagement, led to a greater reduction in the odds of a child experiencing symptoms (p = 0.04, Table 3), likely due to a greater reduction in the odds of cough (p = 0.05). Intervention effects were not detected for child anthropometric outcomes (Table 3), and height‐for‐age in particular decreased over time (Table 1).

Intervention with either parent increased that parent's knowledge of recommended IYCF and WASH practices and the number of situations in which they washed their or their child's hands (all p < 0.05, Tables 2 and 4). The positive effect of any intervention on the odds of the household consuming clean water was slightly but significantly mitigated (p = 0.04) when parenting was bundled with nutrition content. Intervention with fathers led to a greater increase in the odds of fathers contributing to food preparation, and joint intervention, in which both parents were engaged with bundled content, led to a greater increase in fathers contributing to food preparation specifically for the child (from 17% at baseline to 40% at endline, p = 0.02). However, no interventions affected fathers' engagement in child feeding. Any intervention led to a greater increase in the odds of a mother putting aside money for purchasing healthy foods (p < 0.001), while father engagement led to a greater increase in the odds of a household growing a new crop to improve the family's diet (p = 0.01). The interventions did not significantly affect other maternal, paternal, and household behaviors to increase access to nutritious foods. Small animal husbandry was already common prior to intervention (> 85% of households, Table 2). Overall, adjusted (Tables 3, 4) and unadjusted (Supporting Information Tables S3–S4) analyses yielded similar results for child and caregiver outcomes.

Discussion

To our knowledge, EFFECTS is the first published trial to test the effects of (1) engaging both mothers and fathers, rather than mothers alone, in gender‐transformative nutrition and parenting SBC interventions and (2) bundling nutrition and parenting interventions, rather than providing nutrition alone, using a gender‐transformative approach to improve child outcomes. This study found that engaging fathers as well as mothers and bundling parenting with nutrition content in SBC interventions can improve parental knowledge and behaviors and ultimately children's diets. When jointly implemented, these interventions led to a substantial increase in children's dietary diversity, an effect size not realized when only one of these interventions was delivered. The magnitude of this increase is practically significant, particularly as the study was implemented in a context where the majority of children do not meet the minimum recommendation for a diverse diet and consume inadequate amounts of multiple micronutrients (O'Malley et al. 2024). Moreover, children whose parents both received bundled content had a greater increase in the odds of consuming nutrient‐dense foods, particularly dairy products. Meanwhile, only engaging mothers with nutrition content, as many nutritional interventions do, was sufficient to increase children's odds of consuming other, potentially less nutrient‐rich produce. While this can also increase children's dietary diversity, this change alone may not translate into increased nutrient intake. These findings are surprising because bundling nutrition and parenting content reduced the delivered dose of nutrition content but nevertheless improved child dietary diversity and quality.

The joint father engagement and bundled content intervention may have improved children's diets through either synergy between the two component interventions or each intervention modifying upstream factors that are jointly needed to improve children's diets. A child's possible diet is expanded or limited by the household's access to food, and household decisions and behaviors that affect food access, whether related to home food production or to purchase from local markets, are highly gendered and context‐specific (Quisumbing and Doss 2021; Ruel et al. 2018). In this study, we found positive intervention effects on maternal and paternal behaviors that directly relate to household food access via both home production and purchase from local markets. Meanwhile, these interventions also improved interactions and strengthened relationships between parents as a couple and between both mothers and fathers and their child, with the greatest improvements occurring with joint intervention (Galvin et al. 2023; Jeong et al. 2024). By increasing access to nutritious foods while improving the quality of family relationships and the responsive care that is afforded to the child, joint intervention, in which both parents are engaged with both nutrition and parenting content, may realize improved child diets. Intervention effects on the quality of the relationship between mother and father, including their communication, decision‐making, and gender attitudes, may also explain why similar interventions effects were observed for both child and maternal dietary diversity (Galvin et al. 2023). Other studies have also demonstrated that gender‐transformative interventions can improve multiple outcomes for both adults and children (Doyle et al. 2018; Jensen et al. 2021; Singla et al. 2015).

The EFFECTS interventions had positive effects on mothers' and fathers' knowledge and behaviors around complementary feeding. Increased knowledge of complementary feeding and child nutrition, including among fathers, has been positively associated with child dietary diversity and quality (Ambikapathi et al. 2021; Bilal et al. 2016). However, increasing parental knowledge does not necessarily lead to improved child diets. A recent evaluation of nutrition behavior change communication interventions in rural Ethiopia concluded that interventions should include gender‐transformative elements that addressed household gender dynamics (Han et al. 2023). In our study, father engagement also increased the odds of a father helping to prepare food for the household, and joint intervention increased his odds of doing so specifically for the child. We also found that father engagement increased the time spent by fathers in domestic activities (Galvin et al. 2023), and joint intervention had positive effects on responsive feeding by mothers (Jeong et al. 2024).

Engagement of each parent had positive effects on that parent's WASH knowledge and behaviors with respect to themselves and their child. While following recommended WASH practices is often hypothesized to reduce children's risk of infectious diseases, intervention effects on child morbidity did not align with intervention effects on maternal and paternal WASH practices. Specifically, the interventions providing bundled content, regardless of father engagement, reduced the odds of children experiencing any symptoms of illness, an effect perhaps driven by a reduction in the incidence of cough.

Despite the positive effects on children's diets and morbidities, these interventions did not affect growth, and linear growth faltering continued during the interventions. Other studies also found that nutrition SBC interventions can improve IYCF practices and child dietary diversity but can have limited or no effects on linear growth or stunting except perhaps in very young children (Kennedy et al. 2018; Menon et al. 2020; Waswa et al. 2015), younger than our study participants. Other interventions that improved child dietary diversity, women's empowerment, or ECD have also not significantly affected child growth (Dulal et al. 2021; Prado et al. 2019; Santoso et al. 2021).

CHWs face challenges in intervention delivery. For example, dispersed households posed a challenge for a study conducting nutritional supplementation and home visiting interventions in Madagascar, due to increased travel times and difficulty in providing supervision, and participants' limited time and financial resources deterred behavior change (Galasso et al. 2019). New intervention activities in addition to their standard care can also overload CHWs, and resources, systems, and supervision are needed to support effective implementation (Kirkwood et al. 2023). The EFFECTS interventions were delivered with high fidelity by local female and male CHWs who were trained and provided ongoing supervision and mentorship. They implemented interventions in a rural area with rigid gender norms, where villages and households were dispersed, families had limited resources, and parents, especially mothers, had significant time demands. CHWs can be effective in delivering interventions to improve maternal and child outcomes (Gilmore and McAuliffe 2013; Yousafzai et al. 2014), and delivery through an existing cadre, along with reliance on SBC without provision of food or other commodities, can increase the sustainability and scalability of interventions.

Low attendance challenged some studies that sought to engage fathers, hindering effects on child outcomes (Luoto et al. 2021; Singla et al. 2015). Engaging fathers requires additional resources to develop gender‐responsive and transformative intervention materials and to provide additional human resources with adequate training and supervision (Jeong et al. 2023b). This study's findings indicate that investment in engaging fathers can be effective in improving child outcomes. A recent evaluation of interventions to engage men in maternal and child nutrition in rural Tanzania also concluded that interventions should have specific strategies to increase male engagement, and gender‐transformative approaches could increase their engagement by countering existing gender norms that may otherwise raise barriers (Rothstein et al. 2023).

This study had several strengths. Formative research guided intervention design, which helped to identify context‐specific barriers and enablers and to formulate intervention messages and strategies (EFFECTS Study Investigators 2024). This use of formative research can increase intervention effectiveness (Fabrizio et al. 2014). The study's detailed theory of change shaped the design of both the interventions and their evaluation, and the 2 × 2 factorial study design plus a standard care control arm allowed testing of the individual and combined effects of the father engagement and bundling interventions. These interventions were intentionally designed to be gender‐transformative, to provide no material inputs, and to be delivered by local CHWs, with the goal of increasing intervention effectiveness, sustainability, and scalability. Also to increase effectiveness, these interventions employed multiple behavior change techniques (Aboud and Yousafzai 2015; Jeong et al. 2023b). The interventions addressed behaviors and decisions of mothers, fathers, and couples that were hypothesized to lie upstream of optimal child feeding and nutrition per the theory of change, and in this paper, we present intervention effects on these intermediate behaviors alongside the registered primary and secondary outcomes to provide a comprehensive picture of what worked (Fabrizio et al. 2014). Data collection from both parents provides a more detailed understanding of how interventions interact with intrahousehold gender dynamics to influence child outcomes, filling an important gap in the literature (Jeong et al. 2023a, 2023b; Martin et al. 2021). Finally, the interventions were delivered with high fidelity and good supportive supervision, and they achieved regular attendance by both mothers and fathers, despite disruptions from the COVID‐19 pandemic.

This study, nevertheless, had several limitations. Each intervention contained multiple behavior change messages (EFFECTS Study Investigators 2024), and the study design does not allow us to unpack the effects of different intervention components on outcomes. The study excluded families with a father absent more than 2 months per year. Further work is needed to develop interventions to meet the needs of families in which parents, particularly fathers, migrate for work or are otherwise absent. Other family members, including older siblings and grandparents, also play important roles in child care, and research and intervention development could benefit from a family systems perspective (Aubel et al. 2021).

Because villages were allocated to study arms at a community participatory event prior to any recruitment, both CHWs and participants knew to which study arm they were being recruited. While these individuals could therefore not influence treatment allocation, this could potentially have led to some selection bias as CHWs and participants decided whether or not to participate in the study. Each CHW was assigned to only one study arm; therefore, differential delivery of interventions from a single CHW was not possible. While CHWs did report on delivery, which could be a source of bias, these data were triangulated with supervisors' direct observations and feedback from participants that was obtained without the CHW present. Participants' lack of blinding could have also introduced bias by changing their behavior or reporting. Participants became aware of upcoming data collection as appointments were made in advance, and this could, for example, have altered patterns of child feeding immediately prior to assessment of 1‐day and 7‐day dietary diversity. However, opportunities to change child feeding practices on short notice may have been limited, as families relied on home food production or travel to markets to access alternative foods. Each intervention also promoted multiple such behaviors, and it was likely not feasible to effect multiple behavioral changes on short notice. Alternatively, participants could have altered their reporting. Evaluation relied on maternal and paternal self‐reporting of several behaviors and outcomes, which can be vulnerable to social desirability bias. To mitigate bias, the study engaged enumerators who were not involved in intervention delivery and employed enumerator training to standardize data collection, ensure consistent delivery of questions, and minimize interviewer influence. Enumerators were trained to use non‐leading, culturally appropriate language to reduce both social desirability and recall bias.

The onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic interrupted the interventions and necessitated a switch from delivery via peer groups to individual delivery at participants' homes. These changes can affect generalizability of results. Since all study arms were similarly and simultaneously affected, and participant attendance, program fidelity, and quality remained high and comparable across study arms despite the change in delivery modality, internal validity was maintained, though conclusions cannot be made about the effectiveness of peer groups versus individual home delivery. We solicited CHW and participant feedback on this modality shift as part of our process evaluation and on‐going mentorship meetings with CHWs. Overall, CHWs reported that home visits were easier to organize and to reach the majority of participants, as they were being met in their own homes. One possible disadvantage of individual home visits was that participants no longer had their peer group as a form of regular support and social capital, and the opportunity for discussion, debate, and joint problem‐solving was lost. In the mother‐only study arms, it was feasible to conduct home visits without engaging the father, as fathers did not typically participate in CHW home visits. Additionally, during the pandemic, there were no strict lockdowns, and daily life in the region proceeded largely unchanged, with men and women alike continuing with their typical daily activities. Standard care, therefore, continued as such, largely at local health facilities, as home visits from CHWs were not routine and ECD services and centers for children under 3 years of age were non‐existent.

Symptoms of illness were reported by the mother and based on her recall of the preceding 14 days; symptom data are therefore subject to social desirability and recall bias. However, use of maternal reports of infectious illness symptoms and health‐seeking behavior is common and has been shown to agree well with physician diagnoses and medical records (Carter et al. 2018; Orts et al. 1995). Social desirability could also affect reporting of respiratory and other symptoms in the context of the COVID‐19 pandemic. However, this may have been unlikely as community behavior and activities were largely unchanged in the absence of strict lockdowns, and a lack of testing meant a lack of COVID‐19 diagnoses in these rural communities. Social desirability bias, if present, would likely affect all intervention arms similarly and therefore would not explain differences detected between bundled and nutrition‐only interventions.

The interruption in baseline data collection due to administrative challenges meant that 11% of participating households were assessed at least 3 months later than the rest of the sample, during which time changes could occur among participants, their families, or their local context. This could affect reported baseline summary statistics but was accounted for in models to quantify intervention effects. Power calculations assumed 10% loss to follow‐up, though a greater loss to follow‐up was observed at endline, which could lead to loss of statistical power. However, power calculations were based on cross‐sectional, pairwise comparison of study arms, while the study's analysis plan involved pooling across arms longitudinally to answer the four identified research questions, and this can increase statistical power. For future research, long‐term evaluations of interventions would be useful to assess retention of messages, continued adoption or disadoption of promoted behaviors, and spillover effects within families and communities. Finally, recognizing that food environments vary significantly by urbanization, interventions will need to be adapted to more urban contexts.

This study provides novel evidence that jointly engaging fathers and bundling nutrition and parenting content using a gender‐transformative approach can synergistically increase the effect of SBC interventions on young children's diets in resource‐poor settings without provision of additional inputs. CHWs can effectively deliver such interventions with ongoing training and mentorship. Engaging fathers while addressing context‐specific barriers and expanding the scope of nutrition SBC interventions to address underlying determinants of optimal child nutrition and development including household food access, intra‐household gender dynamics, and parenting can enable better outcomes for children.
