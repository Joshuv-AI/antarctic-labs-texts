Isolated Unilateral Pleural Effusion Associated With Hypothyroidism: A Case Report and Review of the Literature
Authors: Hesam Amini, Fereshteh Attaran, Sara Soltanmohammadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 77‐year‐old man with COPD and hypertension presented with progressive exertional dyspnea over 3 months. Physical examination revealed decreased breath sounds in the right lung base and signs suggestive of hypothyroidism, including dry skin and lateral eyebrow thinning. CT imaging showed emphysematous changes with moderate right pleural effusion. Pleural fluid analysis consistently showed borderline transudate/exudate characteristics. Comprehensive evaluation excluded heart failure, malignancy, pulmonary embolism and abdominal pathology. Subsequent thyroid function tests confirmed profound hypothyroidism (TSH: 49.6 μIU/mL). Levothyroxine therapy was initiated and titrated. Remarkably, follow‐up imaging after 4 months demonstrated complete resolution of the pleural effusion, with normalised thyroid function and significant symptomatic improvement. Isolated pleural effusion represents an uncommon manifestation of hypothyroidism. The pathogenic mechanism may involve reduced albumin synthesis and increased vascular permeability, potentially mediated by TSH‐stimulated VEGF production. This case is notable for the absence of classic myxedema symptoms while presenting significant pleural effusion. The diagnosis was established only after methodical exclusion of more common etiologies. Levothyroxine replacement therapy proved effective, resulting in complete effusion resolution.Keywords: case report, hypothyroidism, levothyroxine, myxedema, pleural effusion

Introduction

Inadequate thyroid hormone production or function in target tissues leads to hypothyroidism [1]. This condition can present with life‐threatening symptoms or no symptoms at all, affecting almost any organ and resulting in variable clinical presentations [2, 3]. Hypothyroidism can lead to serous effusions, including pericardial effusion, ascites and pleural effusion (PE) [4]. An isolated PE in the setting of hypothyroidism is rare and is usually related to comorbidities such as malignancy, heart failure, or infectious processes [5]. When there is no evident reason for pleural fluid accumulation other than hypothyroidism, the effusions are typically small and asymptomatic [6] and can be either transudative or exudative [7]. Here, we present a case of an isolated borderline transudative PE attributed to hypothyroidism after excluding all other differential diagnoses. This case report is prepared in accordance with the CARE guidelines.

Case Report

A 77‐year‐old man with underlying chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and hypertension presented with insidiously progressive exertional dyspnea for 3 months. He was recently admitted with the same complaint without clinical improvement. He also complained of chronic productive cough with no change in pattern or quality. He denied night sweats or significant weight fluctuations. The patient was a heavy smoker with a history of COPD and hypertension, taking atenolol 50 mg daily and tiotropium bromide inhaler irregularly.

Physical examination revealed a blood pressure of 170/100 mmHg, a pulse rate of 54/min, a respiratory rate of 14/min, an ambient air SpO2 of 85% and a temperature of 37°C. Pulmonary auscultation revealed decreased breath sounds in the lower half of the right hemithorax and decreased tactile fremitus in the same area. He had dry skin and thinning in the outer third of his eyebrows. Other observations were unremarkable. A non‐contrast chest CT scan showed extensive bullous and emphysematous changes in both lungs, with an upper lobe predominance and a right‐sided PE of moderate size.

Thoracentesis was performed, and pleural fluid analysis was consistent with a borderline transudate/exudate (pleural protein level of 3.6 g/dL and pleural lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) of 211 U/L). Application of Light's criteria suggested an exudative process, given a pleural fluid/serum protein ratio of 0.53 and an LDH ratio of 0.34. However, this classification was contradicted by an elevated serum‐pleural protein gradient (3.1 g/dL), a pleural fluid cholesterol level of 18.5 mg/dL, and a pleural LDH level less than 0.60 times the serum upper limit of normal, all of which strongly support a transudative aetiology. Repeat ultrasound‐guided therapeutic thoracentesis showed the same pattern and about 700 cc of straw‐colored pleural fluid was drained. The fluid was notably lymphocytic‐predominant, with a white cell count of 400/μL, of which 96% were lymphocytes. Critical ruling‐out tests for tuberculosis were negative, as the adenosine deaminase (ADA) level was within normal limits at 11 U/L, and the smear and culture were negative and there was no evidence of malignant cells in cytologic examination. The remainder of the serum workup, including renal function, inflammatory markers (CRP and ESR), liver function tests and NT‐proBNP, was within normal limits.

After therapeutic thoracentesis the oxygen saturation raised to 89% in ambient air.

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 50%–55%, no diastolic dysfunction, normal size and function of the right ventricle, systolic pulmonary artery pressure 20 mmHg, and no signs of pericardial effusion. Abdominal ultrasound was normal. CT pulmonary angiography showed no evidence of pulmonary thromboembolism (Figure 1). Although hypothyroidism was not initially considered, after excluding all other possible diagnoses and based on physical examination, we suspected hypothyroidism. A serum TSH of 49.6 μIU/mL and low total T4 confirmed this diagnosis. Given the negative cytology and normal ADA level, the decision was made to monitor the patient, and a pleural biopsy was not performed. Levothyroxine started at 50 μg for 2 weeks and then increased to 100 μg daily. Umeclidinium/vilanterol 62.5/25 μg inhaler 1 puff daily and losartan 25 mg twice daily were started, and the patient was discharged after 5 days. A chest CT scan revealed complete resolution of the PE after 4 months (Figures 2 and 3); thyroid function tests were within normal limits, and dyspnea improved significantly. Spirometry revealed FEV1/FVC: 60%, FEV1: 45%, FVC 74% without bronchodilator response.

Discussion

Serous effusions are a rare presentation of hypothyroidism, and isolated PEs are even rarer [8]. PEs that result exclusively from hypothyroidism typically fall in the intermediate spectrum between exudates and transudates and generally lack significant inflammatory features [6]. The precise frequency of PEs caused by hypothyroidism is still unknown. Most PEs in the setting of hypothyroidism are due to other etiologies such as congestive heart failure, but it can rarely cause PE without any other underlying condition [9].

The pathogenesis of hypothyroidism‐related effusions remains unknown to date. Hypothyroidism reduces albumin synthesis [10]. Increased capillary permeability causes albumin leakage from capillaries into the intravascular compartment, which decreases plasma volume [11]. This results in an increase in interstitial fluid volume, and lymphatic drainage is compromised due to the elevated albumin concentration in the extravascular compartment [9]. Another contributing factor could be inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion [12]. Various normal adult tissues, including the kidney, lung, uterus, ovary, brain, heart, skin, pituitary gland and macrophages, have been shown to express VEGF [13]. Research has demonstrated that thyroid follicular epithelial cells produce VEGF in vitro when the TSH receptor is stimulated [14, 15, 16, 17]. The stimulation of VEGF receptors on endothelial cells by secreted VEGF may increase vascular permeability, leading to PEs and peripheral edema [18]. In individuals with Hashimoto's thyroiditis, serum VEGF levels positively correlate with TSH levels [19].

To the best of our knowledge, only a few reports have described isolated PE as a presenting symptom of hypothyroidism (Table 1). Isolated PE associated with hypothyroidism was first described by Schneierson and Katz in 1958 [27].

Ansari et al. presented a 75‐year‐old man with a history of hypothyroidism, chronic renal failure and cirrhosis, who had worsening dyspnea and altered mental status. He had bradycardia, hypoxemia, non‐pitting edema and decreased breath sounds in the right hemithorax. Further investigations revealed right‐sided exudative PE and a TSH of 323.5 μIU/mL. Intravenous methylprednisolone followed by intravenous levothyroxine was started and then changed to oral; the patient fully recovered after 1 month [20].

Treatment typically starts with 1.5–1.8 μg per kg of levothyroxine and 12.5–25.0 μg per day of levothyroxine in patients with cardiac symptoms and elderly patients with multiple comorbidities [1]. PE typically responds to levothyroxine replacement but sometimes therapeutic drainage is needed for symptom relief [6]. TSH measurement is recommended after 4–12 weeks, then every 6 months when stable, and annually when treatment targets are reached [1].

The presented case shares fundamental characteristics with the established clinical picture of hypothyroidism‐related effusions. Primarily, the complete resolution of the PE following levothyroxine replacement therapy provides strong evidence for a causal relationship, which is a consistent finding in the literature. Furthermore, the borderline biochemical nature of the effusion, which did not clearly fit the classic exudative or transudative profile, aligns with previous reports describing such effusions as often falling into an intermediate category. This presentation is unique for three main reasons: first, unlike many reported cases that involve multiple serous effusions (e.g., pericardial, ascites) or altered mental status, this patient had a symptomatic, isolated PE as the primary manifestation. Second, the patient lacked classic signs of advanced hypothyroidism (e.g., non‐pitting edema, pericardial effusion), demonstrating that a significant effusion can occur without other stigmata of myxedema and third, the report provides a thorough biochemical characterisation of the “borderline” effusion, using conflicting Light's criteria and supplementary markers, which is less common in prior case reports.

The main limitation of this report was the short follow‐up period of only 4 months, which may not be sufficient to assess the long‐term outcomes or recurrence of PE after treatment. Longer follow‐up periods would provide insights into the sustainability of the treatment response and potential recurrence.

In conclusion, hypothyroidism can rarely cause isolated PE. Reduced albumin synthesis and increased vascular permeability are contributing factors. In the evaluation of PEs of uncertain origin that exhibit mixed exudative and transudative characteristics, hypothyroidism should be considered a potential underlying aetiology. Recognising this atypical presentation is crucial for timely diagnosis and treatment with levothyroxine.
