Prognostic value of tertiary lymphoid structures in high-risk esophageal squamous cell carcinoma following neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy
Authors: Dongdong Huang, Yuejun Han, Hui Wu, Qiyue Wang, Xiaodong Teng, Huifang Zhang, Runzhou Zhuang, Xianghua Ye, Yingqi Chen, Tongshuo Wu, Peng Ye, Haiping Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) are implicated in anti-tumor immunity. However, their prognostic value in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) following neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (NCI) remains unestablished, particularly in high-risk subgroups.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed a cohort of 186 ESCC patients who underwent curative-intent surgery after NCI. TLS density, maturity, and spatial distribution were evaluated. Prognostic models were developed using LASSO and multivariable Cox regression. Spatial proteomic profiling (GeoMx DSP) and immunohistochemistry were performed to characterize TLS-associated immune landscapes in high-risk stage III patients.ResultsHigh overall TLS (oTLS) density and the presence of secondary follicle-like TLSs (SFTs) were independently associated with superior overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS). Notably, in patients with poor pathological response, lymph node metastasis, or advanced-stage disease, those with robust TLS signatures had survival outcomes that were not significantly different from those of lower-risk patients who lacked favorable TLS features. Furthermore, TLSs in the peritumoral compartment demonstrated significant prognostic value. A TLS-incorporated nomogram and a simplified TLS-based risk-scoring system were developed, which demonstrated superior predictive accuracy over models based on traditional clinicopathological factors alone. Spatial proteomic and immunohistochemical analyses revealed that TLS-high tumors exhibited enrichment of CD38⁺ plasma cells surrounding TLSs.ConclusionTLS characteristics are potent, independent prognostic determinants in ESCC after NCI, capable of mitigating risks associated with adverse clinicopathological features. The developed models provide practical tools for personalized risk stratification, with mechanistic insights linking TLSs to plasma cell-mediated immunity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16518-w.Keywords: Tertiary lymphoid structure, Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, Neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, High-risk subgroups, Prognosis

Introduction

Esophageal cancer (EC) is a major gastrointestinal malignancy and ranks as the seventh leading cause of global cancer mortality [1]. The most common subtype, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage due to a lack of early symptoms [2, 3], posing a significant therapeutic challenge. For patients with locally advanced or metastatic disease, systemic therapy plays a crucial role in palliating symptoms, prolonging survival, and improving quality of life. The integration of biomarker testing into clinical practice, which includes the assessment of HER2 status, microsatellite instability, and PD-L1 expression, has further refined therapeutic strategies. This refinement enables the application of targeted agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors such as trastuzumab, nivolumab, and pembrolizumab, all of which have demonstrated promising efficacy in clinical trials [4]. Building upon this progress, the management of locally advanced ESCC has shifted toward a multimodal approach centered on curative-intent surgery preceded by neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (NCI) [5–8].

Even with the advent of multimodal therapy, the overall prognosis for ESCC remains unsatisfactory, a challenge that is particularly pronounced in certain high-risk clinical scenarios [9, 10]. Following neoadjuvant therapy, patients with poor tumor regression, lymph node metastasis, or advanced clinical stages consistently exhibit poorer outcomes, characterized by higher recurrence rates and shorter overall survival (OS). Intriguingly, however, clinical observations indicate that a subset of even these high-risk patients still achieves a relatively favorable prognosis [11]. The mechanisms underlying these heterogeneous outcomes remain largely elusive. Growing evidence, however, suggests that the complex and dynamic tumor microenvironment (TME) may be a critical determinant of such differential treatment responses and survival [12]. The TME constitutes a complex ecosystem comprising not only malignant cells but also a diverse stromal compartment such as cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), multiple immune cell populations, endothelial cells, and a dynamically remodeled extracellular matrix (ECM) [13–16].

Importantly, among the various elements of the TME, tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) have recently attracted considerable attention. These ectopic lymphoid tissues, which develop in chronic inflammatory settings like tumors, are considered analogs of secondary lymphoid organs [17, 18]. By potentially facilitating immune cell recruitment to the tumor site, TLSs have emerged as crucial organizers of adaptive anti-tumor immunity. Indeed, previous studies have established that the presence and maturity of TLSs are significant predictors of improved immunotherapy response and longer OS in a range of solid tumors, including melanoma [19], gastric cancer [20], hepatocellular carcinoma [21], and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [22]. A limited but growing body of work has begun to examine TLSs in ESCC. However, the majority of these investigations have been conducted in patients who underwent primary surgery without prior systemic treatment, thereby reflecting the baseline tumor immune landscape [23–25]. Notably, NCI is known to profoundly remodel the TME, potentially altering TLS density, maturity, and functional status [26]. Therefore, findings from treatment-naïve cohorts may not directly translate to the post-NCI setting. Although a few recent studies have extended these observations to ESCC patients after neoadjuvant therapy [27, 28], the specific impact of TLSs on clinical outcomes in ESCC patients after NCI remains poorly characterized and scarcely reported, particularly for high-risk patients.

Consequently, to address this gap, this study aims to characterize the presence, distribution, and maturity of TLSs in post-NCI ESCC tissues and to determine whether these TLS features hold prognostic value that complements or even surpasses that of conventional clinicopathological factors, especially within high-risk subgroups. Ultimately, this work seeks to provide a novel, TLS-based framework for risk stratification that could inform more personalized post-NCI management strategies for these patients.

Methods

Patients and pathological evaluation of samples

The research was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. 2024 − 1147). We retrospectively enrolled 186 consecutive ESCC patients who received curative-intent surgery after NCI with an anti-PD-1 antibody plus nab-paclitaxel and carboplatin between December 2020 and December 2024 at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine. From the surgical specimens of all patients, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) blocks were prepared. Tissue blocks containing the deepest tumor invasion front were selected, and 5-µm-thick serial sections were obtained from these for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis.

Clinicopathological data were obtained from hospital and pathology archives for the entire cohort. Specifically, the tumor stage was classified according to the 8th edition of the UICC TNM system. Additionally, pathological response in the surgical specimens was assessed using the tumor regression grade (TRG) criteria recommended by the College of American Pathologists (CAP) and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN).

Examination and evaluation of TLSs

TLSs were assessed on serial sections using H&E staining and IHC for CD20, CD3, CD21, and CD23. Following established criteria [29–31], as shown in Fig. 1A, TLSs were identified and stratified into three progressive maturation stages: (1) lymphoid aggregates (LAs), defined as dense but unstructured lymphocyte clusters lacking CD21 and CD23 expression; (2) primary follicle-like TLSs (PFTs), characterized by well-circumscribed, round-to-oval aggregates of small lymphocytes that were CD21-positive but CD23-negative; and (3) secondary follicle-like TLSs (SFTs), distinguished by the presence of a structured germinal center and co-expression of CD21 and CD23. The proportional distribution of each maturation category was systematically documented for every sample.

In addition, all TLSs meeting the above criteria were manually enumerated on whole-slide images, and the total number per case was recorded as overall TLSs (oTLSs). High- and low-TLS groups were defined based on the median oTLS count of the entire cohort, and this cutoff was applied consistently across the oTLS-related survival analyses, including TRG subgroups, lymph node metastasis subgroups, and tumor stage subgroups. In patients with residual viable tumor (TRG 2 or TRG 3), the preserved tumor morphology enabled further spatial subtyping. TLSs were classified based on anatomical location: intratumoral TLSs (iTLSs), entirely within the tumor mass; marginal TLSs (mTLSs, Figure S1), located within 1000 μm of the tumor boundary [32]; and extratumoral TLSs (eTLSs), encompassing all TLSs outside the regions defined for iTLSs and mTLSs. For these spatial subtypes, high- and low-density groups were defined using the median count of each respective subtype. In contrast, for patients with a major pathological response (TRG 0 or TRG 1), extensive tumor regression precluded reliable spatial delineation, yielding only oTLSs for analysis without spatial subclassification.

Predictive model development and validation

To mitigate potential overfitting and multicollinearity, variable selection was performed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. Subsequently, two multivariate Cox regression models were constructed to predict OS and disease-free survival (DFS), along with their corresponding nomograms (using the “rms” and “regplot” R packages) for 12‑, 24‑, and 36‑month probability estimation, followed by internal validation with 1,000 bootstrap repetitions. Time‑dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted to compare the predictive performance of our nomogram against established models based on tumor regression grade (TRG), tumor stage, and a combined TRG‑tumor stage model. Additionally, model calibration was evaluated by comparing predicted probabilities with observed event rates across 10 risk strata derived from the multivariate Cox model.

Furthermore, to improve clinical applicability, a practical risk-scoring system was developed based on the coefficients of multivariable Cox regression. Points for each variable were assigned by dividing its coefficient by the smallest absolute coefficient in the model and rounding to the nearest integer. Reference categories received 0 points, with positive and negative scores indicating increased risk and protective effects, respectively. Each patient’s total risk score was calculated by summing individual points, and patients were stratified into low- and high-risk groups based on the median score. The discriminative ability of the scoring system was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index), with 95% confidence intervals calculated via bootstrap resampling (1000 repetitions). The linear dose-response relationship between the continuous risk score and survival was further assessed, reporting the hazard ratio (HR) per 1-point increase with its 95% confidence interval and p-value for linear trend.

Construction of tissue microarrays and GeoMx DSP protein profiling

Patients for tissue microarray (TMA) construction were randomly and evenly selected from the entire cohort, with balanced representation of high- and low-TLS groups across all stages, based on the availability of sufficient FFPE tissue with adequate tumor cellularity, yielding 77 selected patients. TMA blocks were constructed using a tissue arrayer (Beecher Instruments, USA). Cores (1.5 mm in diameter) were punched from selected FFPE donor blocks and embedded into new recipient blocks, which were then serially sectioned at 5 μm thickness. The resulting TMA sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated before antigen retrieval in 1× Citrate Buffer (pH 6.0). After retrieval, a hydrophobic barrier was drawn around the tissue. Blocking was carried out using Buffer W (NanoString). The slides were then stained with fluorescently labeled morphology markers, including Syto13 nuclear stain (AF488; 1:10; Thermo Fisher Scientific), PanCK (AF532; 1:20; Novus Biologicals) and CD45 (AF594; 1:100; Cell Signaling Technology). These three morphology markers allowed delineation of the nuclear, epithelial and immune compartment. Slides were loaded into the GeoMx DSP instrument (NanoString) for immunofluorescence imaging, region of interest (ROI) selection, and spatially indexed barcode cleavage and collection following the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were prepared using supplied PCR reagents, purified with AMPure XP beads, assessed on a Bioanalyzer, and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

FASTQ files were aggregated into count matrices as previously described [33]. Deduplicated sequencing counts were calculated based on UMI and molecular target tag sequences. Single-probe targets were reported as the deduplicated count value. The limit of quantitation was estimated as the geometric mean of the negative control probes plus 2 geometric standard deviations of the negative control probes. Targets that consistently fell below the limit of quantitation were removed, and the datasets were normalized using upper quartile (Q3) normalization. Proteins with a false discovery rate-adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 0.5 were considered significant. Pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed targets was performed using the ClusterProfiler package in R, with P-values adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction.

Immunohistochemical validation and evaluation criteria

From each TMA block, the first section was stained with H&E for pathological evaluation, and the remaining sections were reserved for IHC staining. IHC staining was performed on the Roche BenchMark XT automated platform using the EnVision two-step method. The following primary antibodies were used: CD20 (rabbit anti-human monoclonal, 1:50, Abcam), CD38 (rabbit anti-human monoclonal, 1:50, Abcam). The positive staining of CD20 and CD38 proteins were localized in the cell membrane. All of them showed brown-yellow fine granularity.

In the semi-quantitative scoring system, the immunoreactive score (IRS) was calculated by multiplying the percentage score by the intensity score, yielding a final value ranging from 0 to 12 [34]. Staining intensity was categorized as follows: negative (0), light-yellow (1), brown-yellow (2), and brown (3). The proportion of positively stained cells was determined as the percentage of mononuclear cell infiltration area relative to the total stromal area, and then classified into five categories: negative, < 25%, 25–50%, 51–75%, and > 75%, with corresponding scores of 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD and were compared using two‑tailed Student’s t‑tests, whereas categorical variables were compared using the chi‑square test. All statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism (v 10), SPSS (v 22.0), and R (v 4.5.1). Survival curves were generated by the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups were compared with the log‑rank test. For survival analyses involving more than two groups, pairwise log-rank comparisons were performed with Bonferroni correction for multiple testing. In addition, Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST) analysis was performed at a 36-month landmark to further quantify between-group differences in survival, with RMST differences reported alongside 95% confidence intervals. Both OS and DFS were measured from the date of first treatment, with OS defined as the interval to death or last follow-up, and DFS defined as the interval to disease progression or any-cause death. Statistical significance was set at p-value < 0.05.

Results

Baseline clinicopathological characteristics

Detailed baseline clinicopathological characteristics of the 186 enrolled ESCC patients are presented in Table S1, with a summary provided in Table S2. The cohort comprised 163 males (87.6%) and 23 females (12.4%), with a mean age of 64.7 years (range, 39–82 years). Based on the post-NCI pathological stage, 98 patients (52.7%) were stage I, 34 (18.3%) stage II, and 54 (29.0%) stage III. Lymph node metastasis was present in 54 patients (29.0%). Assessment of pathological response revealed that 75 patients (40.3%) achieved a major pathological response (TRG 0/1), while 111 patients (59.7%) had a partial or poor response (TRG 2/3). Following surgery, 137 patients (73.7%) received adjuvant therapy, while 49 (26.3%) underwent observation only.

Prognostic stratification by TLS features in the overall cohort

From the most representative tumor sections of all 186 patients, a total of 5,116 TLS-like structures were manually identified and enumerated, comprising 3,379 LAs, 1,327 PFTs, and 410 SFTs (Fig. 1B). Based on the median number of oTLSs, patients were stratified into high- and low-TLS groups (each n = 93). After a median follow-up of 24.7 months, during which 32 deaths and 44 disease progression events were recorded, survival analysis confirmed that high abundance of oTLSs was significantly associated with superior OS (p = 0.0006, Fig. 1C) and DFS (p = 0.0273, Fig. 1D). We next compared clinicopathological characteristics between the high- and low-TLS groups. As detailed in Table 1, the high-TLS group had a significantly higher proportion of patients with poor pathological response (TRG 2/3) (p = 0.007) and a higher frequency of mature TLSs (p < 0.001).

Clinicopathological characteristics between the high- and low-TLS groups

We next evaluated the prognostic impact of TLS maturation. Patients were first dichotomized into immature (no SFT present) and mature (≥ 1 SFT present) TLS groups. This analysis revealed that mature TLSs conferred significantly better OS (p = 0.0008, Fig. 1E) and DFS (p = 0.0315, Fig. 1F). To achieve a finer granularity in risk assessment, patients were further stratified into three tiers based on the highest TLS maturation stage present, revealing a clear stepwise improvement in clinical outcomes. As evidenced in Fig. 1G and H and Table S3, patients with only LAs (n = 13) had the poorest prognosis (median OS: 15.3 months; median DFS: 15.3 months), those with PFTs (n = 76) had intermediate outcomes (median OS: 23.6 months; median DFS: 23.3 months), and patients harboring SFTs (n = 97) experienced the most favorable survival (median OS: 25.9 months; median DFS: 24.5 months). Collectively, these results indicate that both the abundance and maturation stage of oTLSs are positively associated with prognosis in ESCC patients receiving NCI.

TLS-associated risk mitigation across high-risk subgroups

To evaluate TLS features in the high-risk TRG 2/3 cohort, we identified a total of 3,447 oTLSs from 111 patients, which comprised 2,302 LAs, 881 PFTs, and 264 SFTs (Fig. 2A). Consistent with the overall cohort, both TLS abundance and maturity provided powerful prognostic stratification, with high abundance or the presence of SFTs being associated with markedly superior survival (Fig. 2B, C, D and E; pairwise comparisons and RMST analyses are summarized in Table S3). Notably, the presence of SFTs was associated with a 30-month DFS of approximately 70% and a remarkable 50-month OS exceeding 90%. Most strikingly, the survival curves of TRG 2/3 patients with favorable TLS features converged with those of TRG 0/1 patients with poor TLS characteristics. For example, patients of TRG2/3 with high oTLSs showed OS similar to that of patients of TRG0/1 with low oTLSs (p = 1.000; RMST difference: -0.2 months; 95% CI: -2.8 to 2.4 months). Likewise, patients of TRG2/3 with SFTs had OS similar to that of patients of TRG0/1 without SFTs (p = 1.000; RMST difference: -1.3 months; 95% CI: -3.5 to 0.9 months). This convergence unequivocally demonstrates that, in specific clinical contexts, the quality and quantity of TLSs can outweigh the degree of pathological tumor regression as a determinant of long-term survival.

Furthermore, spatial analysis of the 3,447 oTLSs revealed a distinct distribution (Fig. 2F): 33.1% iTLSs (n = 1141), 28.5% mTLSs (n = 984), and 38.4% eTLSs (n = 1322). High abundance of both mTLSs and eTLSs were significantly associated with improved OS and DFS, whereas iTLS abundance demonstrated no significant prognostic value (Fig. 2G, H, I, J, K and L). These findings pinpoint the peritumoral compartment as the critical anatomical niche where TLSs exert their dominant protective effect in patients with poor pathological response.

A parallel and equally potent prognostic role for TLSs was observed in the context of lymph node metastasis (N+), another established high-risk factor. Among N+ patients, both high oTLS abundance and the presence of SFTs were linked to markedly improved OS and DFS (Fig. 3A, B, C and D; detailed pairwise comparison and RMST results are provided in Table S3). For example, N+ patients with high oTLSs maintained a 30-month OS exceeding 90%, while those with low oTLSs experienced a steep decline to near 75% over the same period. Crucially, a similar convergence was again observed, effectively blurring the prognostic distinction between node-negative (N0) patients with poor TLS features and N+ patients with robust TLSs. For instance, N+ patients with high oTLSs had OS similar to that of N0 patients with low oTLSs (p = 1.000; RMST difference: 0.1 months; 95% CI: -3.4 to 3.6 months).

Finally, we assessed the association between TLS characteristics and tumor stage (Fig. 3E, F, G and H; Table S3). The prognostic influence of TLSs was most pronounced in advanced-stage disease. Within stage II and III patients, both high oTLS density and the presence of SFTs produced exceptionally sharp risk stratification. The most critical insight emerged from cross-stage comparisons. Specifically, stage III patients with high oTLS levels achieved DFS rates similar to those of stage II patients with high oTLS levels (p = 1.000; RMST difference: -1.3 months; 95% CI: -7.6 to 5.0 months). More remarkably, in OS, Stage III patients with high TLS levels attained outcomes that approached those of stage I patients with low TLS (p = 1.000; RMST difference: -1.4 months; 95% CI: -4.8 to 2.0 months). Collectively, these findings indicate that a potent TLS microenvironment can confer a survival benefit analogous to a functional down-staging effect, thereby challenging the deterministic prognosis traditionally assigned to advanced TNM stage.

Development and simplification of TLS-based models for individualized prognosis

Individualized nomograms were developed to quantify the probability of 1‑, 2‑, and 3‑year OS and DFS. These models incorporated five predictors selected by LASSO regression from the entire cohort, comprising abundance of oTLSs, presence of mature TLSs, TRG, tumor stage, and vascular invasion (Fig. 4A and B, Figure S2A and B). Of note, both oTLSs abundance and tumor stage retained independent prognostic significance in the nomogram analysis. Each level of these variables was assigned a specific point on a scale, and an individualized total risk score was calculated by summing the points across all variables (Fig. 4C and Figure S2C).

Time‑dependent ROC analysis confirmed the strong discriminatory power of both nomograms, with AUCs for 1‑, 2‑, and 3‑year predictions reaching 0.913, 0.874, and 0.853 for OS (Fig. 4D and F), and 0.856, 0.846, and 0.832 for DFS (Figure S2D and F). Furthermore, comparative analysis demonstrated the superior predictive accuracy of our five‑variable model over benchmark models reliant solely on TRG, tumor stage, or their combination across all time points for both OS and DFS. Calibration curves indicated close alignment between predicted and observed outcomes (Fig. 4G and Figure S2G). Although a slight deviation was noted in the intermediate‑risk range for DFS, overall calibration was maintained at a satisfactory level.

To facilitate clinical translation, we further derived a simplified risk-scoring system from the multivariable Cox model. Consistent with the nomogram, oTLS abundance received the strongest protective weighting, while tumor stage carried the highest risk weighting (Table S4 and Table S5). This simplified system demonstrated excellent discriminatory performance. For OS (Figure S3A), using a natural cutoff of 0 points (low-risk: ≤0, n = 111; high-risk: ≥1, n = 75), the C-index was 0.856 (95% CI: 0.783–0.923) for continuous scores and 0.764 (0.697–0.823) for the dichotomized stratification. For DFS (Figure S3B), with thresholds of ≤ 3 (low-risk, n = 93) and ≥ 4 (high-risk, n = 93), the C-index was 0.817 (0.753–0.878) and 0.723 (0.667–0.780), respectively. This TLS-based stratification resulted in significantly divergent survival outcomes (log-rank p < 0.0001 for both OS and DFS; Figure S3C and D). Notably, a strong linear dose-response relationship was observed (Figure S3E and F). Specifically, to illustrate this relationship at a key clinical timepoint, at 36 months, each additional point corresponded to a 4.0% decrease in OS probability (slope = − 0.040, R² = 0.704; P < 0.001) and a 2.91% decrease in DFS probability (slope = − 0.0291, R² = 0.772; P < 0.001), confirming the graded prognostic impact of TLS characteristics.

Collectively, these results underscore the independent prognostic significance of TLS features in ESCC following NCI. The proposed nomograms, which incorporate TLS metrics, offer a clinically applicable tool for stratifying patients according to their risk of mortality and disease recurrence.

Spatial proteomic and immunohistochemical characterization of TLS-associated immune landscapes in high-risk patients

To characterize the TLS-related tumor microenvironment, we performed GeoMx DSP protein profiling on TMA cores from 77 post-NCI ESCC patients (Fig. 5A and Figure S4), with subsequent spatial and IHC analyses focused on the 37 stage III patients among them (detailed clinical information is provided in Table S6). Representative images from two of the stage III patients are shown in Fig. 5A (a and b), where ROIs were selected and segmented into tumor (PanCK⁺) and immune (CD45⁺) compartments, with the latter being the primary focus for evaluating TLS-associated features. Within this high-risk stage III subgroup, a heatmap of selected differentially expressed proteins clearly distinguished between patients with high and low TLS abundance (Fig. 5B, Table S7). Consistent with this clustering, the volcano plot identified a distinct protein signature distinguishing the two groups (Fig. 5C). Reflecting their known cellular composition, TLS-high samples showed elevated expression of T-cell (CD3 family) and B-cell (CD20) markers. More notably, CD38, a marker of plasma cells, was significantly enriched as well in the TLS-high group, suggesting an active humoral immune response within these structures. In contrast, the TLS-low group exhibited higher expression of immunosuppressive factors such as monoamine oxidase A [35, 36]. To validate the spatial proteomic findings, we performed IHC staining for CD20 and CD38 on serial sections from the same TMA cohort. Representative images from six stage III patients are shown in Fig. 5D. Consistent with the DSP results, IRS for CD38 was significantly higher in the high-oTLS group compared to the low-oTLS group (p < 0.0001, Fig. 5E). Strikingly, CD38⁺ plasma cells were observed surrounding CD20⁺ TLSs and extending into the surrounding stroma, suggesting that TLSs may serve as local hubs for plasma cell differentiation and activation, contributing to the humoral immune response within the tumor microenvironment. Pathway enrichment analysis further revealed that the proteins upregulated in the high-TLS group were primarily enriched in immune-related pathways, including hematopoietic cell lineage, PD-L1/PD-1 checkpoint pathway in cancer, Th1/Th2/Th17 cell differentiation, and T cell receptor signaling (Fig. 5F). Protein-protein interaction analysis supported these findings, showing a highly interconnected network among key immune markers (Fig. 5G).

Discussion

Despite advances in multimodal therapy, the prognosis for ESCC patients, especially those in high-risk subgroups, remains poor. However, significant outcome heterogeneity exists even among those with traditionally high-risk features, suggesting that conventional clinicopathological factors alone are insufficient to capture the underlying biological complexity. This prognostic variability highlights the critical need for novel biomarkers that can more accurately stratify individual risk and inform personalized management strategies. To address this need, we investigated TLS as potential candidates. In this study, we systematically characterized TLS density, distribution, and maturity in post-NCI ESCC, validated their independent prognostic value across high-risk subgroups, and constructed a clinically integrated prognostic model.

Previous studies have predominantly used a binary TLS presence/absence paradigm, linking TLS positivity to better survival in various cancers [30, 31, 37]. In our NCI-treated ESCC cohort, stratification by median oTLS count confirmed higher TLS abundance as a strong predictor of superior OS and DFS. Moreover, a three-tiered maturation staging revealed a stepwise prognostic improvement, with the presence of SFTs emerging as a key favorable determinant. Our findings corroborate and extend the prognostic relevance of TLS maturation, specifically SFTs, from the earlier neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NCT) context to the current NCI setting [38]. This is further strengthened by comparative analyses showing that NCI is associated with the highest SFT frequency among different neoadjuvant regimens, confirming its independent prognostic value [27, 39].

It has been established that TLS distribution is spatially heterogeneous and carries prognostic significance [40–42]. In ESCC patients with poor pathological regression, our spatial subtyping revealed that high densities of mTLSs and eTLSs were significantly associated with improved survival, whereas iTLSs showed no prognostic value. This finding pinpointed the peritumoral compartment as the critical anatomical niche for TLS-mediated protection, a conclusion consistent with prior works in ESCC [43] and colorectal cancer (CRC) [30, 44] positioning peritumoral TLSs as the frontline of anti-tumor immunity. Collectively, these data suggest that peritumoral TLSs appear to orchestrate anti-tumor immunity by activating and deploying tumor-specific lymphocytes, thereby bolstering immunosurveillance against disease progression [45–47].

Beyond their general protective role, our study systematically demonstrates the capacity of TLSs to stratify prognosis across all major high-risk subgroups. In patients with poor pathological response, lymph node metastasis, or advanced stage, favorable TLS profiles consistently identified a subset with survival outcomes matching or even surpassing those of lower-risk patients with poor TLSs. This prognostic convergence, most evident in cross-stage comparisons, illustrates that a robust TLS microenvironment can functionally offset the impact of adverse clinicopathological factors, conferring an immune-mediated “functional down-staging” effect.

Importantly, this specific protective effect of TLSs within high-risk subgroups remains a relatively underreported phenomenon. To our knowledge, only isolated reports have hinted at this possibility. For instance, one study in ESCC noted that the prognosis of patients with stages III and IV experienced significantly improved prognosis when accompanied by a high density of mature TLSs after NCT [38]. Similarly, in lung adenocarcinoma, the presence of mature TLSs was associated with a lower frequency of lymph node metastasis, though its prognostic impact within the node-positive population was not further analyzed [48]. To further understand the biological basis of TLS-mediated protection, we performed spatial proteomic and immunohistochemical analyses. Within the high-risk stage III subgroup, TLS-high tumors exhibited a distinct immune-active protein signature, characterized by enrichment of T- and B-cell markers along with CD38⁺ plasma cells. Notably, CD38⁺ plasma cells were observed surrounding CD20⁺ TLSs and extending into the surrounding stroma, suggesting that TLSs may serve as local hubs for plasma cell differentiation and activation, contributing to an active humoral immune response. These findings align with converging evidence from multiple cancer types. In triple-negative breast cancer, high densities of plasma cells were significantly associated with higher numbers of TLS [49]. In rectal cancer and high-grade serous ovarian cancer, plasma cells surrounding TLS were associated with the highest levels of CD8⁺ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), which conferred prognostic benefit in the presence of plasma cells [50, 51]. Even in immunologically “cold” gliomas, the presence of TLS with dynamic immune responses, including the generation of IgA⁺ and IgG⁺ plasma cells, was associated with improved survival [52]. Collectively, our findings provide molecular evidence that TLS-high tumors harbor an immune-active microenvironment centered on TLSs and enriched with peritumoral plasma cells, extending the prognostic value of TLSs to a mechanistic understanding of TLS-associated anti-tumor immunity in ESCC following NCI.

Collectively, the development of our TLS-integrated prognostic models demonstrates that TLS parameters are essential complements to traditional factors, and the superior accuracy of the integrated model underscores their synergistic predictive power. Crucially, our scoring system quantitatively captures the biological insight that high TLS abundance can offset the risk of advanced disease, thereby identifying high-risk patients with a more favorable immune profile. Thus, rather than replacing conventional staging, our model incorporates it into a more holistic, immune-aware paradigm for individualized prognosis and postoperative management. For instance, patients with low TLS abundance who are at high risk of recurrence might be candidates for adjuvant therapy intensification or more intensive surveillance, whereas those with high TLS abundance and favorable pathological response might be considered for de-escalation of adjuvant treatment to avoid unnecessary toxicity. These hypotheses, however, require prospective validation in appropriately designed clinical trials.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, TLS assessment was performed on surgical specimens obtained after NCI without pre-treatment baseline controls. For patients with poor pathological response, we cannot determine whether the observed TLSs represent pre-existing structures that remained after treatment or newly formed structures in response to residual tumor. Future studies with pre-treatment biopsies are needed to clarify the dynamic evolution of TLSs during neoadjuvant therapy. Second, our system does not apply to stage IV patients with distant metastasis, leaving the role of TLSs in this advanced setting an open question for future study. Third, while internally validated, our models were derived from a single-center cohort. Consequently, future prospective, multi-center studies are needed to confirm the utility of our TLS-based scoring system.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that TLS characteristics are strongly associated with favorable prognosis in ESCC following NCI, and may help stratify outcomes even among patients with traditionally high-risk features. The developed TLS-integrated nomogram and simplified risk score offer a practical framework for personalized prognosis, potentially guiding adjuvant therapy decisions and enriching patient stratification in future clinical trials.
