Nudges in A Learning Health System
Authors: Diana De Oliveira-Gomes, Muthiah Vaduganathan, Tor Biering-Sørensen, Larry A. Allen, Clara K. Chow, Nihar R. Desai, Adam J. Nelson, Cass R. Sunstein, Ankeet S. Bhatt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nudges are behavioral interventions that influence decision-making by subtly altering the choice environment without restricting freedom. Rooted in behavioral economics, they have shown promise in health care by improving adherence to guidelines and promoting preventive behaviors. Learning Health Systems offer infrastructure for implementing nudges at scale through tools like electronic health records and decision support systems. In cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic care, nudges targeting both patients and providers, ranging from defaults and reminders to gamification, have improved prescribing, vaccination rates, and physical activity. Frameworks such as EAST and MINDSPACE guide effective design, emphasizing timing, audience, and framing. Looking ahead, artificial intelligence–powered nudges promise personalized, adaptive interventions that respond to real-time behavior and performance, enhancing scalability and sustainability. By aligning behavioral science with health technology, nudges can help optimize care delivery, reduce variation, and improve outcomes across complex health systems.

“Nudges” are interventions designed to influence, while still preserving freedom of choice.1 Nudges have been applied across diverse domains, but their relevance to clinical medicine, particularly promoting effective cardiovascular care, has expanded considerably in recent years. In this review, we 1) define nudges and place them within the framework of Learning Health Systems (LHSs), 2) outline key principles of nudge design, 3) synthesize the evidence for nudges in cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic care, and 4) examine the emerging role of artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled nudges.

What is a nudge?

The concept of nudges was broadly introduced in 2008 in the book “Nudge: Improving Decisions about Health, Wealth, and Happiness,” suggesting that private and public policymakers can encourage better decision-making by modifying the environment in which choices are made.1 The basic idea is that consumers, investors, patients, professionals, and countless others make choices within a “choice architecture” and that changes to the choice architecture can significantly affect outcomes. For example, a GPS device is a nudge, which alters the choice architecture for drivers; fuel economy labels are nudges, which alter the choice architecture for motor vehicle purchasers.1

Nudges can be a highly effective means of promoting desired behaviors without imposing mandates or prohibitions.1 Some nudges are educative, as in the case of fuel economy or energy efficiency labels; some nudges are architectural, as in the case of default settings on cell phones and laptops, or uses of product placement in grocery stores or in online displays. Nudges may work by leveraging existing cognitive biases and heuristics, such as “present bias” (a focus on the short term), limited attention, unrealistic optimism, and inertia (the tendency to stick with the status quo).1 Nudges often make certain choices easier to make, thereby increasing the probability of their selection or other (less desirable) choices harder to make. Choice architecture, designed by reference to empirical findings in behavioral science, is often meant to promote healthier and safer choices.2

The use of nudges extends across multiple domains. For example, in the area of finance, studies have shown that automatically enrolling employees in retirement savings plans, with the option to opt out, significantly increases participation rates and long-term savings compared to a voluntary opt in approach.2 This shift capitalizes on cognitive phenomena including inertia and loss aversion, ultimately encouraging individuals to save for the future. Similarly, changing the default position for organ donation from “opt-in” to “opt-out” can increase the number of people who are characterized as donors, though actual donation rates depend on multiple features of the underlying choice architecture.3

Nudges in learning health systems

A LHS is a system in which “science, informatics, incentives, and culture are aligned for continuous improvement and innovation, with best practices seamlessly embedded in the delivery process and new knowledge captured as an integral by-product of the delivery experience.”4 LHSs represent dynamic ecosystems where data, technology, and collaborative partnerships converge to drive continuous improvement.5 These systems rely on the integration of scientific evidence, real-world clinical experience, and patient preferences into clinical workflows. This infrastructure offers a unique opportunity to implement and iteratively refine behavioral interventions, such as nudges, in a systematic, scalable, and sustainable manner.

Within an LHS, nudges can be deployed using existing operational tools such as electronic health records (EHRs), clinical decision support systems, and routine performance feedback mechanisms.6 Unlike standalone research interventions, nudges embedded in LHS are by design integrated into routine care, allowing for naturalistic testing and continuous adaptation. Common examples include default options in order sets to promote evidence-based prescribing, active choice prompts to encourage cancer screening or vaccination, and peer comparison feedback to influence provider behavior without mandates or incentives.7, 8, 9 In LHS, the implementation of nudges offers a potentially powerful mechanism to standardize and optimize health care delivery by enhancing clinical decision-making.6

At scale, nudges can play a critical role in democratizing access to high-quality care by reducing unwarranted variation and promoting adherence to clinical guidelines across different health care systems. For example, system-wide EHR-based prompts can encourage consistent prescribing of statins or heart failure therapies across all practices, or promoting their adherence irrespective of location or available local expertise. By embedding nudges into these centralized infrastructures, large LHSs can align day-to-day clinical practice with the latest evidence, advancing their mission to translate knowledge into action and deliver measurable improvements in population health.

Designing a nudge

This section outlines the key principles for designing and implementing nudges in health care, with a focus on how specific features of the intervention influence their effectiveness. The effectiveness of any nudge is contingent upon several key factors, with the most important being: 1) intended audience, 2) timing, and 3) framing. Identifying the target audience is essential, as different populations may respond differently to the same nudge due to varying cognitive biases, cultural norms, and socioeconomic factors. In health care settings, nudges can be directed toward patients, providers, other members of the health care team, or even the health care system itself. Understanding the targeted audience is key for a successful design. For example, nudges directed at patients might focus on promoting preventive care, medication adherence, or healthy lifestyle choices. If the targeted audience are older patients, it must take into account factors such as cognitive decline and complex medication regimens. Meanwhile, nudges targeting providers could aim to improve adherence to clinical guidelines, reduce unnecessary testing, or enhance communication with patients. Moreover, system-level nudges could be designed to optimize resource allocation, streamline administrative processes, or promote the adoption of evidence-based practices.

Another important element is the timing of a nudge, since its impact can vary significantly based on when it is delivered in relation to the decision-making process. A well-timed nudge can capitalize on moments when individuals are most receptive to change or actively deciding. Finally, the framing refers to how information is presented and can significantly influence decision-making. The same choice can yield different responses depending on whether it is framed positively or negatively. For example, presenting a health intervention as having a “90% success rate” vs a “10% failure rate” can lead to different perceptions and choices.

In addition to these 3 elements, several other factors are fundamental to understanding and developing effective nudges. One valuable tool for this purpose is the EAST framework, developed by the Behavioral Insights Team in the United Kingdom: Easy, Attractive, Social, and Timely.10 In brief, useful nudges often make it easy for people to engage in the relevant behavior—as, for example, by simplifying the process by which patients take prescribed medicines. Useful nudges also tend to make desired choices salient and, in that sense, attractive; they are based on an understanding that people have limited attention and may ignore information or options that are not highly visible. Information, or educative nudges, about current or coming social norms can also be effective, as, for example, when patients or doctors are informed that most patients, or most doctors, make a particular decision. We have also seen that timing can greatly matter.

Another valuable tool is the MINDSPACE framework, which provides a structured approach to examining the psychological influences on behavior and designing impactful nudges within health care contexts (Figure 1).11 MINDSPACE is an acronym that stands for: Messenger: The source of the information can significantly affect its reception. Nudge communications in health care should be delivered by trusted sources, such as official guidelines, to maximize impact. Incentives: Although nudges generally do not alter economic incentives, they can complement existing systems by utilizing small rewards to promote positive behavior. Norms: As noted, highlighting prevailing social norms can effectively motivate individuals to change behavior. Defaults: Setting favorable default options can guide individuals toward healthier decisions while preserving their freedom of choice. Salience: As noted, making a nudge noticeable and attention-grabbing is essential for its effectiveness. For example, by using bright colors or bold fonts in reminders about preventive care, health care systems can capture individuals’ attention more effectively. Priming: Subtle changes in the environment can influence behavior. For instance, exposure to health-related information, such as images of fruits and vegetables, can prime individuals to make healthier food choices subsequently. Affect: Emotional responses play a significant role in decision-making. Messages that evoke positive emotions, such as hope or encouragement toward preventive interventions, can foster a more favorable disposition toward healthy behaviors. Commitments: Encouraging individuals to make commitments can increase accountability. Ego: Individuals have a natural desire to feel good about themselves. User perspective

User perspectives are a critical determinant of the success of nudging strategies. Studies show that while many people are initially unfamiliar with the concept of nudges, once explained, most express moderate to high approval, particularly for health-related behaviors when the interventions are transparent and perceived as beneficial.12,13 Moreover, nudges implemented by expert users received more approval than those by policymakers.14 For providers, studies consistently show acceptance of nudges such as default options, peer comparison, and feedback, especially when these interventions are transparent, designed with clinicians “in the loop” and facilitate evidence-based practice without being overly intrusive or disruptive.15,16 Future work should systematically use a user-centered design approach to optimize patient and provider perspectives to increase user acceptability and satisfaction.

Provider-directed nudges

By targeting providers, nudges have demonstrated their potential to address critical gaps in adherence to clinical guidelines and improve patient outcomes. For example, the PROMPT-HF (PRagmatic trial Of Messaging to Providers about Treatment of Heart Failure) clinical trial investigated the impact of targeted EHR alerts on the use of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) among patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. Results showed a 40% increase in GDMT class prescription within 30 days.17 Another example is the BETTER CARE-HF (Building Electronic Tools To Enhance and Reinforce CArdiovascular REcommendations–Heart Failure) trial, an EHR-embedded alert shown during patient encounters more than doubled mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist prescribing for patients with heart failure compared to usual care, outperforming both asynchronous EHR messages and controls.18 Similarly, in the NOTIFY-1 (Incidental Coronary Calcification Quality Improvement Project) project, notifying both clinicians and patients about incidental coronary artery calcium findings on prior computed tomography scans led to a more than sevenfold increase in statin prescriptions within 6 months.19

The effectiveness of nudges in cardiovascular disease care is shaped not only by the target population but also by the design and delivery of the intervention. Evidence from an RCT highlights this nuance: a visit-based, interruptive EHR alert did not significantly increase statin prescribing, whereas a semiautomated, asynchronous referral to pharmacists led to a 16.4% absolute improvement.20 These findings underscore the importance of optimizing the timing and format of nudges, particularly through scalable and team-based approaches. This issue is reinforced by other studies showing that combining different strategies or targeting multiple audiences can further enhance impact. For example, in an RCT conducted in a primary care setting, clinician-targeted nudges utilizing EHR active choice prompts and monthly peer comparison feedback led to a 5.5 percentage point increase in statin prescribing compared to usual care.8 When these clinician nudges were combined with patient-focused nudges, such as text messages sent prior to visits, the increase in statin initiation rose to 7.2 percentage points.8 Together, these findings highlight that careful attention to design and context is essential for maximizing the effectiveness of nudge interventions.

Patient-directed nudges

The NUDGE-FLU (Nationwide Utilization of Danish Government Electronic Letter System for Increasing Influenza Vaccine Uptake) trial demonstrated that electronically delivered letter nudges significantly increased influenza vaccination rates among young and middle-aged patients with chronic diseases compared to usual care. Participants receiving any intervention letter showed an increase in vaccination rates of approximately 11.7 percentage points, with the largest effects observed from a repeated reminder and a letter highlighting cardiovascular benefits.21 Similarly, the EPIC-HF (Electronic Health Record-leveraged, Patient-centered, Intensification of Chronic Care for HF) trial demonstrated the impact of electronically delivered patient activation tools, which included a brief video and checklist, on the initiation and dose uptitration of GDMT for patients with heart failure. Results showed that 49% of participants using the activation tools experienced initiation or intensification of their GDMT within 30 days, compared to 29.7% in the control group.22

For patient-targeted nudges, gamification is an emerging behavioral strategy that incorporates game design elements, such as goal setting, feedback, and rewards, to promote a desired outcome. For example, in the BE ACTIVE (Behavioral Economic Approaches to Increase Physical Activity Among Patients With Elevated Risk for Cardiovascular Disease) randomized controlled trial, gamification delivered via text messages significantly increased physical activity over 12 months, both alone and in combination with financial incentives, among patients with or at high risk for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, with the combined approach demonstrating the most durable effect during 6-month follow-up.23 Similarly, the ENGAGE (Evaluation of the Novel Use of Gamification With Alternative Goal-setting Experiences) trial showed that among economically disadvantaged adults at elevated cardiovascular risk, gamification paired with self-chosen, immediately implemented goals led to sustained increases in physical activity.24 Similarly, in the STEP UP (Social Incentives To Encourage Physical Activity and Understand Predictors) trial, which enrolled over 600 adults with overweight/obesity, gamification interventions incorporating social incentives such as support, collaboration, or competition all increased physical activity.25

Negative and null trials

Despite appeal and logical validity, not all nudges have been successful and it remains critically important to rigorously evaluate their impact like any other health care intervention. For instance, the PROMPT-HF (PRagmatic Trial Of Messaging to Providers About Treatment of Heart Failure in the Inpatient Setting) trial tested a tailored, real-time EHR alert during acute heart failure hospitalizations but found no increase in GDMT prescriptions at discharge among 1,012 patients compared with usual care.26 This contrasts with the outpatient PROMPT-HF trial, which did improve GDMT use, highlighting how context may influence the effectiveness of such interventions.17,26 Similarly, the large nationwide NUDGE-CKD (Nationwide Utilization of Danish Government Electronic Letter System for Increasing Guideline-directed Medical Therapy in Chronic Kidney Disease) trial in Denmark tested electronic letters to patients and general practitioners but did not increase uptake of renin–angiotensin system inhibitors or sodium glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors compared with usual care.27 In addition, the U.S. cluster-randomized CONNECT-HF (Care Optimization Through Patient and Hospital Engagement Clinical Trial for Heart Failure) trial applied a multifactorial intervention including audit and, feedback, in addition to clinician and practice education, but showed no improvement in outcomes or quality of care among patients hospitalized with heart failure.28 Likewise, the REVeAL-HF (Risk EValuation And Its Impact on ClinicAL Decision Making and Outcomes in Heart Failure) trial tested provision of individualized 1-year mortality risk estimates to clinicians during HF hospitalization but found no effect on readmissions, mortality, prescribing, device implantation, or palliative care referrals, perhaps due to the lack of actionable steps linked to the nudge themselves.29 Finally, in a large pragmatic RCT involving over 9,500 patients, text message interventions, including generic reminders, behavioral nudges, and chatbot-enhanced messages, did not improve medication adherence based on pharmacy refill data or reduce clinical events over 12 months.30 Similar results were seen in other global contexts.31 Together, these findings underscore that not all nudges translate into meaningful improvements in care or outcomes, and the reasons for these failures need to be further explored.

Key gaps and future directions

While the evidence for nudges in cardiovascular care is promising, important gaps remain, particularly regarding their long-term effectiveness and impact on hard clinical outcomes. Many existing studies focus on intermediate or surrogate endpoints, such as medication initiation or physical activity levels, with limited data on whether these behavioral changes translate into sustained risk reduction or improved morbidity and mortality. This reliance on surrogate measures may constrain clinical and policy adoption, as guideline updates have traditionally preferred evidence of patient-centered and clinical outcomes. Implementation research can build upon well-established surrogates, but for even broader impact, future trials should consider designs that capture hard clinical endpoints, including mortality, rehospitalization, and quality of life when feasible. Innovative analytic approaches, such as the win ratio, which integrates clinical outcomes with implementation and patient-reported measures, may offer a path forward to more comprehensive evaluation.32

Furthermore, the durability of nudging interventions is rarely assessed beyond 6 to 12 months. Given their typically low cost and ease of implementation, even modest, short-term effects may be acceptable in certain contexts. Future research should therefore aim not only to optimize the design and delivery of nudges but also to rigorously evaluate their sustained impact over time and their ability to influence meaningful clinical endpoints.

Looking ahead, pivotal progress in the field will depend on studies that are larger in size, broader in scale, and more diverse in populations to establish the generalizability of nudge interventions across health care systems. For example, the NUDGE-FLU trial in Denmark randomized nearly 300,000 adults aged 18 to 64 years with chronic diseases and demonstrated that behaviorally informed electronic letters increased influenza vaccination uptake compared with usual care.21 While NUDGE-FLU established proof of principle in Denmark using a national electronic governmental letter system, the recently completed KP-VACCINATE (Kaiser Permanente VACCination Improvement with Nudge-based CardiovAscular Targeted Engagement) megatrial tested the generalizability of this approach in a more diverse multicenter health system in the United States randomizing more than 3.6 million adults.7,33 KP-VACCINATE, which found that a cardiovascular-focused messaging strategy did not improve vaccination update when compared to usual care messaging evaluated scalability, optimal timing, and generalizability. These types of large-scale validations are essential to determine the conditions under which nudges are effective and to advance a more generalizable understanding of their role in health care. The alternative is broader, multisystem, and global implementation studies, few of which have been conducted to date.

Limitations of nudges

Despite their promise, nudge interventions face significant implementation barriers that can limit real-world effectiveness.34 Electronic nudges are particularly vulnerable to alert fatigue and workflow disruption, which may overwhelm clinicians and reduce engagement. Resistance can also arise from concerns about professional autonomy or skepticism toward algorithm-driven prompts. To what extent alert fatigue may limit initial or long-term uptake of intended behaviors needs to be studied. On the patient side, disparities in digital literacy and access pose additional challenges.34

While often perceived as low-cost, successful implementation may require substantial investment in infrastructure, training, and maintenance, therefore, cost assessments should be quantified wherever possible.

A Future vision

Artificial Intelligence Powered Care Delivery

As technology continues to advance within health care, the potential for integrated, AI-enabled systems that effectively employ nudges to enhance patient outcomes and provider behaviors is vast. Integrating AI to Advance Learning Health Systems: Real-Time Personalization and Continuous Evaluation of Behavioral Nudges

AI has the potential to enable real-time personalization and continuous evaluation of nudges in LHSs, supporting adaptive feedback, synchronized patient–provider prompts, and tailored strategies for health care teams. AI = artificial intelligence; LHS = Learning Health System; RCTs = randomized clinical trials.

One of the possibilities that AI offers is having dynamic personalization and continuous improvement, or “precision” nudges. More personalized nudges, based on an understanding of (for example) the particular patient’s values and concerns, might turn out to be especially effective; AI can help on that count. Recent applications of machine learning and AI in digital behavior change interventions have shown promising results, particularly in the cardiometabolic domain.35 For instance, reinforcement learning algorithms have been used to tailor physical activity prompts via SMS in patients with diabetes, increasing step counts, and improving glycemic control.36 Similarly, AI-powered recommender systems have supported personalized dietary and physical activity suggestions based on user behavior logs, while natural language understanding has enabled chatbots to deliver therapeutic support for substance use and smoking cessation.35,37 Many of these interventions, such as Lark for chronic disease prevention,38 and mPulse Mobile for medication adherence,39 among many others, have demonstrated positive outcomes in randomized controlled trials and real-world settings, highlighting the scalability and impact of AI-driven nudges.35

Building on this foundation, AI offers the potential to revolutionize how we design, test, and optimize behavior change strategies in health care. Future systems could leverage AI to design and execute continuous RCTs to evaluate the effectiveness of various nudge strategies. These trials could involve altering elements such as timing, color, font, and the location of nudges, as well as the triggers for these interventions. Successful nudges would not only continue to be deployed but also serve as benchmarks for developing new strategies, while new, evidence-based approaches could be explored simultaneously. Furthermore, AI could facilitate randomized deimplementation of successful nudges to determine how long they should continue in practice, assessing whether their removal is necessary. This deimplementation process is crucial to ensure that health care providers and patients are not burdened by outdated nudges, allowing for more effective and relevant interventions.

Real-time AI feedback mechanisms could also tailor nudges to the evolving needs of the individual health care providers and patients based on their historical performance. For instance, if a provider consistently prescribes statins appropriately, the system would refrain from nudging them until their performance deviates from the expected standard. This targeted approach minimizes unnecessary interventions and reduces the risk of nudge fatigue. By fostering an adaptive system that responds to user needs, health care providers can maintain the effectiveness of nudges while minimizing any negative consequences associated with overexposure.

Another strategy would be paired “closed loop” synchronized nudges. This represents an innovative approach to enhancing communication and collaboration between health care providers and patients. This strategy involves simultaneously sending prompts to both a health care system partner, such as a physician or a nurse, and the patient, thereby creating a coordinated effort to facilitate health care interactions. For instance, when a patient is due for a follow-up appointment or a recommended screening, a nudge can be sent to both the patient’s mobile device and the provider’s system, prompting them to reach out and confirm the appointment. By establishing a feedback loop, these synchronized nudges can promote proactive engagement from both parties, ultimately leading to improved adherence to care plans and better health outcomes.

Finally, advanced intelligence strategies could be used to deliver tailored nudges to different parts of the care team. Nudges directed at other parts of the care team can play a critical role in ensuring cohesive and coordinated patient care. For instance, a nudge might alert pharmacists to review a patient’s medication adherence prior to a scheduled appointment, prompting them to discuss potential barriers with the patient upon their arrival. These nudges not only facilitate timely communication among team members but also ensure that every contributor to patient care is empowered to take proactive steps.

While these opportunities are compelling, AI-powered nudges also raise important risks that warrant ongoing evaluation. Concerns include algorithmic bias (eg, reinforcing inequities if models are trained on nonrepresentative data), digital divide and inequities in access or usability, and privacy and data security issues when continuously monitoring sensitive health information. These challenges highlight the need for transparent design, regulatory oversight, and continuous monitoring to ensure that AI-enabled nudges enhance cardiovascular care without unintentionally worsening disparities.

Moreover, although AI-driven nudges have shown early promise in limited cardiometabolic contexts, such as diabetes management, physical activity promotion, and medication adherence, evidence from large-scale randomized trials remains scarce.35 Embedding these tools within existing cardiovascular registries or pragmatic trials could provide a practical path to generate the robust, context-specific evidence needed to inform clinical practice and policy adoption.

In short, nudges now serve, and should increasingly serve, as a pervasive tool to influence behavior to overcome the effects of cognitive biases, reduce undue variation or “noise” in care, improve outcomes, and unburden clinicians.40 When grounded in data and implemented through collaborative systems, nudges can enhance decision-making and ensure that care is more consistent, more effective, less costly, and more responsive to the needs of both clinicians and patients.
