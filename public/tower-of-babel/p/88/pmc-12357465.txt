CD4/CD8 ratio trajectories and their impact on prognosis: a 15-year retrospective longitudinal cohort study of people living with HIV
Authors: Lihui Feng, Yanhui Gao, Xin Zhou, Shufang Rao, Junqi Ou, Jing Lin, Yanqiu Li, Hongbo Jiang, Yuting Li, Yueping Li, Lixia Li, Weidong Jia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe aim of this study was to identify the heterogeneous classes of CD4/CD8 ratio trajectory and their impacts on prognosis in people living with HIV (PLHIV) during long-term antiretroviral therapy.MethodsA retrospective cohort study of PLHIV receiving ART treatment was conducted in the Eighth Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University, and the latent growth mixture model (LGMM) was used to identify the trajectories of longitudinal changes in the CD4/CD8 ratio between February 10, 2004, and January 31, 2019. Cox proportional hazard model and proportional subdistribution hazard model were conducted to explore the impact of CD4/CD8 ratio trajectory on prognosis.ResultsThree heterogeneous trajectories were identified: the baseline-low-level slow increase class (Class 1: 57.45%), the baseline-moderate-level rapid increase class (Class 2: 34.29%), and the baseline-high-level sharp increase class (Class 3: 8.26%). Cox proportional hazard model showed that CD4/CD8 ratio trajectory was associated with all-cause mortality among PLHIV, with adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) (95% confidence interval [CI]) for Class 2, and Class 3 being 0.53 (0.38–0.75), and 0.35 (0.14–0.86) respectively, compared with Class 1. The result of the proportional subdistribution hazard model showed that the CD4/CD8 ratio trajectory was associated with the risk of AIDS-related and non-AIDS-related mortality.ConclusionsOur study demonstrated that there were three different trajectories of CD4/CD8 ratio during long-term ART. Personalized interventions and treatment plans can be developed based on individual changes in CD4/CD8 ratio, which is important for improving the survival of the PLHIV and reducing disease burden.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24055-7.

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection remains a global public health challenge, with antiretroviral therapy (ART) significantly improving survival by suppressing viral replication and restoring CD4+ T cell counts [1]. However, CD4+ T cell counts alone inadequately reflect immune dysfunction in treated individuals, as persistent immune activation, inflammation, and senescence contribute to non-AIDS morbidity and mortality [2]. The CD4/CD8 ratio has emerged as a critical biomarker of immune reconstitution, linked to T-cell activation, viral reservoir size, and both AIDS-related and non-AIDS-related outcomes [3–7]. While baseline CD4/CD8 ratio is associated with prognosis, longitudinal trajectories of this ratio during long-term ART and their impact on mortality remain underexplored.

This study aimed to characterize heterogeneous CD4/CD8 ratio trajectories in people living with HIV (PLHIV) using latent growth mixture modeling (LGMM) and evaluate their associations with all-cause, AIDS-related, and non-AIDS-related mortality. Identifying distinct trajectory patterns may inform personalized interventions to improve outcomes in this population.

Methods

Study design and participants

A retrospective cohort study of PLHIV receiving ART treatment was conducted at the Eighth Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University, the largest infectious disease hospital in Guangdong Province, China. Participants were selected using the following inclusion criteria: inclusion of PLHIV aged ≥ 15 years who initiated ART between February 10, 2004, and January 31, 2019 with at least 3 follow-up visits exclusion of individuals with prior ART exposure, pregnancy, or missing baseline CD4/CD8 cell counts. A total of 14,293 participants met these criteria. This sample size is sufficiently large to mitigate the risk of overfitting, particularly for the LGMM which requires adequate statistical power to reliably identify heterogeneous trajectories.

Outcomes and study covariates

The outcomes of interest were AIDS events and non-AIDS events. Death information was obtained through physician reports or active follow-up of PLHIV. The death data were coded according to the 10th revision of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems. Causes of death included AIDS-related deaths and non-AIDS-related deaths, with AIDS-related deaths being those due to opportunistic infections, AIDS-related neoplasms, and AIDS-related specific diseases and syndromes. Non-AIDS-related deaths involve a broad spectrum of disease, including respiratory diseases, cardiovascular diseases, digestive system diseases, endocrine nutritional and metabolic diseases, unnatural causes (suicide, accident or other violent death, euthanasia, and substance abuse), malignancies not caused by AIDS, and other non-AIDS-related diseases. All other deaths (excluding “unknown” deaths which were not included in analyses of cause-specific mortality) were assumed to be non-AIDS related.

According to the recommendations of the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of HIV/AIDS (2021 Edition) [8], CD4+ T cell counts should be measured once before treatment, once 3 months after treatment initiation, and every 3 to 6 months during the first 2 years after treatment. The measurement of CD8+ T cell counts is the same as the CD4+ T cell counts.

Potential confounders for adjustment were age, gender, marital status, body-mass index (BMI), mode of transmission, WHO clinical stage, interval from diagnosis to ART, use of cotrimoxazole, number of clinical symptoms, opportunistic infections, initial ART regimen, blood indicators (leukocyte count, platelet count, hemoglobin), liver and kidney function-related indicators (total bilirubin, ALT and AST), hepatitis virus infection indicators (hepatitis B surface antigen and hepatitis C antibody) and CD4+ T cell count.

Follow-up was calculated from the date of ART initiation, until death or the last follow-up visit. PLHIV who were lost to follow-up, transferred treatment, or were alive at their last clinic visit were censored.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described as frequency and percentage (%) quantitative variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and non-normally distributed continuous variables as median and interquartile range (IQR). LGMM was used to identify trajectory heterogeneities of CD4/CD8 ratio in PLHIV after ART. LGMM is a method for identifying multiple unobserved subpopulations by describing longitudinal changes within each unobserved subpopulation [9]. The model contains both continuous and categorical latent variables, with the continuous latent variable describing the growth characteristic parameters of intercept and slope, reflecting the developmental trajectories of different subclasses, and the categorical latent variable describing class heterogeneity and classifying study individuals into different subclasses based on individual response patterns. Models with 1–4 classes were estimated, and three unconditional models were fitted: linear LGMM, quadratic LGMM, and free LGMM. Model-fit indices were used to evaluate the goodness of the LGMM: Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), sample size–adjusted BIC (aBIC) and entropy values. Models with lower AIC, BIC, and aBIC suggest a better model fit [10, 11]. The entropy value ranges between 0 and 1; the closer it is to 1, the more accurate the classification [12]. Bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT) and Vuong-Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio test (VLRT) are statistical methods that compare the improvement of two adjacent models (k and k-1). If the p value is statistically significant, the current model (k) is selected. The models were estimated using the robust maximum likelihood approach. We hypothesized that data were missing at random (MAR). To evaluate this assumption, we compared baseline characteristics (age, gender, BMI, CD4+ T count at ART initiation) between participants with complete data and those with missing data. No statistically significant differences were found in these key variables (all p>0.05,Table S1), indicating that missingness was not systematically associated with the measured baseline factors. This pattern is consistent with the core assumption of MAR.

Characteristics across different subclasses were compared using the Chi-square test for categorical variables, and the Mann-Whitney U test and the Kruskal-Wallis test were used for quantitative variables. Kaplan-Meier graph was used for the accumulative mortality curve and the log-rank test was used to compare the all-cause mortality among different subclasses. AIDS-related deaths and non-AIDS-related deaths were competing events, and the cumulative incidence function was utilized to estimate the cumulative incidence of AIDS-related deaths and non-AIDS-related deaths. The Gray test was used to compare the cumulative incidence curves [13, 14]. Cox proportional hazard model and proportional subdistribution hazard models were employed to examine the impact of the CD4/CD8 ratio trajectory on all-cause mortality, AIDS-related mortality, and non-AIDS-related mortality. Unadjusted and adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were reported. We used multiple imputations in the SAS MI procedure to account for all missing covariates.

The descriptive statistics were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute) and figures were generated using Stata Statistical Software, version 16.0 (StataCorp LP College Station, TX, USA). LGMM analyses were performed using Mplus, version 7.4 (Muthe´n & Muthe´n).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 17,537 PLHIV initiated ART between February 10, 2004 and January 31,2019. Among this cohort, 14,879 had complete baseline and follow-up records, 586 were excluded due to prior ART exposure, pregnancy, or missing baseline CD4/CD8 cell counts, resulting in 14,293 participants with at least three follow-up visits (Fig. 1). Table 1 showed the baseline characteristics of the study population, with the majority (n = 11,570, 80.95%) being male. The mean age was 36.64 (11.97) years, with over half of the cohort aged 25–44 years. After an HIV positive diagnosis, 74.53% of participants started ART within 6 months, and 53.42% had a normal BMI at ART initiation. Homosexual and heterosexual transmission accounted for 43.06% and 45.23%, respectively. Notably, 13,785 (96.44%) started ART at WHO clinical stage III or IV; 37.77% had a history of cotrimoxazole use, and 37.21% were using cotrimoxazole. At baseline, 1,289 (9.02%) had opportunistic infections, including 0.95% with two or more concurrent infections, and 32.06% exhibited clinical symptoms. Approximately 86% of PLHIV were on an initial ART regimen consisting of two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) plus one non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs).

Patterns of CD4/CD8 ratio change trajectories over time based on LGMM

To identify and predict unobserved subpopulations, we used the LGMM model to fit to repeated measures of the CD4/CD8 ratio using linear, quadratic, and free estimation methods, respectively. Models with 1–4 classes were estimated, beginning with the simplest model. Table 2 showed that AIC, BIC, and aBIC decreased from 1-class to 4-class solutions. The entropy values increased from 2-class to 3-class and decreased from 3-class to 4-class, where the information indicator of free estimation was the smallest, the Entropy value was the largest (0.839), and classification accuracy was optimal. Both the VLRT p-value and the BLRT p-value were significant in the 3-class model, but the VLRT p-value became non-significant in the 4-class model. Therefore, the 3-class model was initially adopted as the best-fitting solution.

Baseline characteristics of trajectory profiles clusters

Table 3 presented the distribution of participants’ characteristics across trajectory classes. Males constituted 81.72%, 79.51%, and 81.53% of Class1, Class2, and Class 3, respectively, with WHO clinical stages III/IV accounting for 96.15%, 97.24%, and 97.03% in each class. Significant differences were observed in gender, marital status, age at ART initiation, mode of transmission, WHO clinical stage, tuberculosis history, current cotrimoxazole use, opportunistic infection, number of opportunistic infection types, clinical symptom count, and initial ART regimen (p < 0.01) across the three classes. In Class 1, a higher proportion of participants initiated ART at 35–55 years of age, while only 12.81% were aged 15–24; the proportion with more than 6 months between HIV diagnosis and ART initiation was the highest (28.03%). Cotrimoxazole use was reported in 44.30% of Class 1 participants, who also had the highest rates of ≥ 2 opportunistic infections (1.38%) and clinical symptoms (18.53%). Married individuals (51.90%) and heterosexual transmission (45.84%) were significantly higher in Class 1 than in Class 2 or Class 3.

Association between CD4/CD8 ratio trajectory classes and prognosis

The cohort was followed up for 56615.41 person-years, and a total of 229 participants died during this period, including 98 AIDS-related deaths and 131 non-AIDS-related deaths, with mortality rates of 1.73 and 2.31 per 1000 person-years, respectively. Class 1, Class 2, and Class 3 had 177, 47, and 5 deaths, corresponding to mortality rates of 5.26 per 1000 person-years, 2.46 per 1000 person-years, and 1.28 per 1000 person-years. A log-rank test revealed statistically significant differences in all-cause mortality across the three trajectories (p < 0.001).

AIDS-related deaths in Class 1, Class 2, and Class 3 were 83, 13, and 2 participants, with mortality densities of 2.47, 0.68, and 0.51 per 1000 person-years, respectively. Non-AIDS-related deaths were 94, 34, and 3 participants in each class, with mortality densities of 2.79, 1.78, and 0.77 per 1000 person-years. A Gray test confirmed significant differences in cumulative incidence between trajectories (p < 0.05). Furthermore, because of the presence of AIDS-related deaths (or non-AIDS-related deaths) as a competing event, we also analyzed HRs using the proportional subdistribution hazard model. The adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) for AIDS-related deaths for Class 2 (vs. Class 1) was 0.27 (95% CI: 0.15–0.49), and Class 3 (vs. Class 1) was 0.21 (95% CI: 0.05–0.87). Following the same strategy, Class 2 (aHR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.46–0.99), and Class 3 (aHR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.10–0.98) had a lower risk of non-AIDS-related death compared with Class 1. All differences were statistically significant (p < 0.05).

Discussion

Our study identified three distinct CD4/CD8 ratio trajectories in PLHIV during long-term ART, each with unique baseline characteristics and mortality risks. Class 1 comprised 57.45% of participants, exhibiting severe immune impairment at ART initiation (median CD4/CD8 ratio < 0.2), higher rates of opportunistic infections (11.45%), and the highest all-cause mortality (5.26 per 1000 person-years). In contrast, Class 3 achieved rapid immune reconstitution (ratio normalized to ≥ 0.8 within 1 year) and had the lowest mortality (1.28 per 1000 person-years), highlighting the protective effect of early immune recovery [15]. These findings align with prior studies linking low CD4/CD8 ratios to T-cell exhaustion and non-AIDS complications, such as cardiovascular disease and cancer [16–18].

Notably, non-AIDS-related deaths accounted for 57.2% of all deaths, surpassing AIDS-related mortality. Multivariate analysis showed that Class 2 and 3 had significantly lower risks of both AIDS-related (aHR = 0.27 and 0.21, respectively) and non-AIDS-related deaths (aHR = 0.67 and 0.32, respectively) compared to Class 1 (Table 4). This supports the CD4/CD8 ratio as a prognostic biomarker for chronic disease risk, consistent with European cohorts demonstrating that persistent low ratios correlate with T-cell activation and non-AIDS mortality [19–21]. However, our results contrast with a large collaborative study by Adam et al., which found weak associations between CD4/CD8 ratio and mortality [22], possibly due to differences in follow-up duration or population demographics.

Clinical implications arise from the demographic and therapeutic heterogeneity across trajectories. For example, Class 1 participants were more likely to be older, married, and infected via heterosexual transmission, with delayed ART initiation (> 6 months post-diagnosis in 28.03%) and higher rates of cotrimoxazole use (44.30%). These factors suggest a need for targeted interventions, such as intensified screening for comorbidities (e.g., hepatitis B/C, anemia) and earlier ART initiation to mitigate immune decline.

The major strengths include the large sample size, the long follow-up, and the analytical strategy using LGMM to explore CD4/CD8 ratio trajectories in PLHIV during ART.

The study has several limitations, including the absence of viral load data, potential underestimation of mortality due to reliance on outpatient records, and lack of socioeconomic variables (e.g., literacy, income). Future studies integrating viral dynamics and longitudinal socioeconomic data could further validate the clinical utility of CD4/CD8 trajectories. Additionally, although the 3-class model showed strong internal validity, external validation in independent cohorts is required to confirm the generalizability of these trajectory classes.

In summary, our findings emphasize the importance of monitoring CD4/CD8 ratio dynamics alongside CD4+ T counts to inform personalized ART strategies, particularly for high-risk subclasses with delayed immune recovery.
