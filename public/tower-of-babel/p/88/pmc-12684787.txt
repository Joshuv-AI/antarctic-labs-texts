Cardiac Troponin Is Infrequently Tested at Emergency Department Visits With Suspected Acute Ischemic Stroke
Authors: Kleona Kolludra, Alexander Merkler, Babak B. Navi, Hooman Kamel, Anokhi Pawar, Lillie Lanphier, Ava L. Liberman
Published: 2025

Stroke is a major cause of morbidity and mortality. For patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS), a concomitant myocardial infarction (MI) is associated with an increased risk of mortality. 1 A recent prospective study found that 1 in 5 patients with AIS with elevated high‐sensitivity cardiac troponin have evidence of concurrent type 1 MI. 2 American Heart Association/American Stroke Association guidelines encourage cardiac troponin testing in all patients with stroke. However, there are limited data on the real‐world frequency of its testing, which could affect both acute cardiac treatment and secondary stroke prevention strategies. 3 We therefore sought to use nationally representative data to ascertain the frequency of cardiac troponin laboratory testing among emergency department (ED) visits with suspected AIS with a cross‐sectional design.

We used data from the NHAMCS (National Hospital Ambulatory Medical Care Survey) to identify all ED visits where AIS was suspected. 4 This project was deemed exempt by the Weill Cornell institutional review board because NHAMCS data are publicly available and deidentified. All visits for patients aged >17 years between January 1, 2016 and December 31, 2022 with an initial primary ED diagnosis of AIS, defined as having International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM) code I63, were included. The main study outcome was frequency of cardiac troponin testing.

We calculated point estimates using the NHAMCS survey methodology and report 95% CIs with variance scaled to handle strata with a single sampling unit. Survey‐weighted chi‐square testing was used to compare demographics, medical history, and clinical features between those with or without troponin testing. Logistic regression was used to analyze the relationship between troponin testing and study year accounting for survey design. The threshold of statistical significance was α=0.05. Analyses were performed with Stata MP (version 15).

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Based on 394 observations, an estimated 3.5 million (95% CI, 2.9–4.3) visits or 0.35% (95% CI, 0.29–0.43) of all ED visits during the study period were determined to have an ED diagnosis of AIS. Mean patient age was 70 years (95% CI, 69–72), 53.5% (95% CI, 48.1–57.9%) were female, and many had vascular risk factors, including 73.9% (95% CI, 66.5–80.0%) with history of hypertension (Table). Head computed tomography was obtained in 80.5% (95% CI, 73.2–86.2%) of visits.

A total of 28.9% (95% CI, 19.5–40.5%) of identified visits had at least 1 cardiac troponin tested in the ED. Conversely, 85.6% (95% CI, 77.5–91.2%) of visits had an ECG obtained in the ED. Proportion of troponin testing among AIS visits was not associated with study year (odds ratio [OR], 1.0 [95% CI, 0.8–1.3]). In the subgroup of AIS visits with head computed tomography, 30.9% (95% CI, 20.6–43.6%) had troponin tested. In unadjusted analyses, the only factor out of 19 significantly associated with troponin testing was history of alcoholism.

Using nationwide survey data, we found that slightly more than a quarter of ED visits with initial diagnosis of AIS had at least 1 cardiac troponin level obtained. We are not aware of any other population‐level data assessing rates of troponin testing in AIS. 1 Though registries have found relatively low rates of concurrent MI in patients with stroke (1%–2%), recent literature suggests a nontrivial rate of MI when troponin values are elevated. 2

Although the precise mechanism of elevated troponin in AIS remains somewhat unclear, our findings suggest a lack of real‐world guideline adherence regarding troponin testing in AIS. Given the results of a recent prospective study where 20% of patients with AIS with elevated troponin values had a type 1 MI, determined by advanced evaluations, developing strategies to address current practice gaps in ED troponin testing are likely warranted considering the time‐sensitive nature of addressing ongoing ischemia. Reasons for the discrepancy between the frequency of ED laboratory versus electrocardiographic testing for cardiac disease in AIS that we observed requires further study. It is possible ED environmental issues, provider knowledge gaps, or patient preferences are driving this inconsistency. Although ECG can evaluate for both cardiac injury and arrhythmias, including atrial fibrillation, cardiac troponin testing is essential for guideline‐based MI detection. 5

Our study has several limitations. The NHAMCS data set lacks detail on cardiac troponin values, type of troponin testing (eg, high sensitivity), ECG results, and other cardiac testing performed that could affect timely and accurate MI diagnosis. We also do not know how often troponin was tested after patients left the ED (eg, during admission). Additionally, we have no information on stroke severity, presenting clinical syndrome beyond main reasons for visits, and neuroimaging. Finally, our finding of a significant association between alcoholism and troponin testing with an α=0.05 is of uncertain clinical relevance; this result is consistent with what would be expected by chance alone.

Using population‐level data, we found that only slightly more than a quarter of all included AIS visits had cardiac troponin testing completed in the ED across the United States.
