Sequential drug-release microsphere system for scarless wound healing
Authors: Dong Luo, Linxue Huang, Boxiong Bai, Huiqi Zhang, Wenshang Liu, Beibei Wen, Zhengmao Lu, Xiaohui Pu, Qian Chen, Zhengyu Shen, Meng Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe dynamic imbalance in skin wound healing is a key factor of hypertrophic scar (HS) formation. Traditional treatments often target only a certain stage of the wound healing process and fail to comprehensively address the dynamic imbalance, thus making it difficult to achieve scarless wound healing. This study developed a chronologically adaptive methacrylylated gelatin (GelMA)-based microsphere delivery system (G/E/T) that achieves comprehensive intervention in wound healing process through precisely orchestrated sequential release of epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) and triamcinolone acetonide (TA). In vitro studies have confirmed that the G/E/T microsphere system, utilizing this sequential release mechanism, not only alleviates oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in the early stages but also persistently inhibits the excessive proliferation and migration of fibroblasts in the later phases, demonstrating stage-specific therapeutic precision. Moreover, validated through mouse full-thickness wound model and rabbit ear HS model, the system exhibited remarkable scarless healing outcomes. The "sequential release and dual-agent synergy" design paradigm provides an innovative strategy for scarless wound healing while establishing a foundational framework for developing sequential multidrug delivery systems targeting complex pathological processes.Keywords: Scarless wound healing, Sequential release, GelMA microsphere, EGCG, TA

Introduction

The improper or delayed wound healing can lead to hypertrophic scar (HS) [1]. Early oxidative stress and inflammatory responses during wound healing process can delay tissue repair, thereby increasing the risk of HS formation [2,3]. Furthermore, the excessive proliferation and migration of fibroblasts in the late stage of wound healing can lead to excessive deposition of collagen, ultimately resulting in HS [4,5]. Most current research strategies for scarless wound healing primarily focus on intervening in early oxidative stress and inflammatory responses [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Although these approaches can partially prevent the formation of HS, they often fail to effectively inhibit the excessive proliferation and migration of fibroblasts in the late stage of wound healing, thereby triggering tissue fibrosis and the formation of HS [6]. Thus, there is an urgent need for an innovative integrated therapeutic strategy that can regulate early inflammation while inhibiting fibroblast activity in later stages to achieve more ideal wound healing outcomes.

To address the aforementioned challenge, advanced drug delivery systems exhibit significant advantages in that they can meet the intervention requirements of different stages of scarless wound healing [11]. At present, common drug delivery systems include nanoparticles, liposomes, and polymeric carriers [12]. While these systems offer advantages in drug targeting, controlled release, and enhanced absorption, they also have limitations. For instance, nanoparticles have short drug release times and poor long-term controlled release [13]. In contrast, microsphere-based systems are ideal controlled-release drug delivery systems [14,15], as they achieve sustained and stable drug release through polymer degradation and diffusion and can accommodate various types of drug molecules [16]. More importantly, microsphere-based delivery systems can achieve sequential release of multiple drugs by regulating the encapsulation positions and binding modes of different drugs within the carrier, thereby precisely meeting the intervention requirements of each stage [17].

To achieve precise intervention in the two stages of wound healing and HS formation, the selected drugs must target early inflammation and late fibrosis respectively. Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), a major water-soluble polyphenol in green tea [[18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23]], reduces pro-inflammatory factors via the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway, exerting antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects to promote wound healing process [24]. Triamcinolone acetonide (TA), a water-insoluble, long-acting glucocorticoid with marked antifibrotic properties, inhibits the transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1) pathway to down-regulate α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) expression, thereby suppressing fibroblast activity, reducing collagen deposition, and decreasing scar volume and hardness [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. Meanwhile, the differences in physicochemical properties between EGCG and TA precisely provide an ideal material basis for achieving sequential drug release based on the microsphere system.

Leveraging these properties of EGCG and TA, we developed a sequential drug-release microsphere system (G/E/T) based on methacrylylated gelatin (GelMA), which releases EGCG and TA successively, providing an innovative strategy for scarless wound healing (Scheme 1). As a water-soluble antioxidant and anti-inflammatory small molecule, EGCG forms weak hydrogen bonds with outer GelMA due to rich hydroxyls, adsorbs evenly on the surface and shallow layer of microspheres, and is rapidly released during wound healing to accelerate the process. In contrast, fat-soluble antifibrotic TA is stably embedded in the microsphere core via hydrophobic interactions. Its release depends on GelMA degradation, and it is continuously released during scar formation as the scaffold disintegrates, inhibiting scarring. In vitro and in vivo experiments showed the G/E/T system precisely intervenes in both stages, achieving scar-free healing. This strategy exploits their synergy, avoids adverse effects from premature release via precise timing, and has large-scale production potential due to biocompatible materials and simple preparation, meeting clinical needs. The study offers new insights for scarless healing and lays a foundation for therapeutic systems requiring sequential release of multiple drugs for other diseases.

Materials and methods

Materials

EGCG and TA were purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China); GelMA was obtained from Engineering for Life (Jiangsu, China); HUVECs and NIH/3T3 were bought from Gaining Biological (Shanghai, China); the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was obtained from Beyotime (Shanghai, China); the superoxide anion detection kit was purchased from BOXBIO (Beijing, China); and the ELISA kit was sourced from Elabscience (Wuhan, China).

Healthy C57BL/6 mice and New Zealand White rabbits were used in the animal experiments. All experimental and care protocols were approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Subcommittee of Henan University and strictly adhered to the Guiding Principles of Laboratory Animal Ethics (HUSOM2025-582). Throughout the experiment, all procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant ethical regulations to ensure scientific rigor and ethical compliance.

Synthesis of microspheres

The mobile phase consisted of GelMA (50 mg/mL), TA (2.5 mg/mL), and the photoinitiator (2.5 mg/mL), while the dispersed phase was composed of isopropyl palmitate and the surfactant EM180. Microdroplets of varying sizes were prepared via a microtube dispenser with a flow rate of 4 μL/min for the mobile phase and 85 μL/min for the dispersed phase. After collection, the droplets were subjected to photopolymerization under ultraviolet (UV) light (5 mW/cm2, 15 s). Following polymerization, the samples were washed alternately three times with petroleum ether and hexane, followed by five washes with PBS, and then freeze-dried. An appropriate amount of freeze-dried microspheres was subsequently mixed with a preprepared EGCG aqueous solution (2.5 mg/mL) and redispersed at 37 °C for 24 h to form G/E/T composite microspheres. The preparation method for the G/T microspheres was identical to that for the G/E/T microspheres, except that no EGCG aqueous solution was used for redispersion. G group microspheres were prepared by photopolymerizing GelMA (50 mg/mL) alone under UV light (5 mW/cm2, 15 s). G/E group microspheres were obtained by redispersing freeze-dried G group microspheres in an EGCG aqueous solution. The key differences among the groups were as follows: the G/E/T microspheres contained GelMA, TA, and EGCG; the G/T microspheres contained only GelMA and TA; the G group microspheres contained only GelMA; and the G/E group microspheres contained GelMA and EGCG.

Release of EGCG and TA from G/E/T

Twenty milligrams of freeze-dried G/T microspheres were added to an appropriate amount of EGCG aqueous solution. After full resuspension, the mixture was centrifuged to pellet the microspheres. The pelleted microspheres were carefully transferred into a dialysis bag, ensuring complete transfer without loss. The dialysis bag containing the microspheres was placed into a 50 mL centrifuge tube with 30 mL of PBS containing sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS). The centrifuge tube was placed on a shaker. At predetermined time points, 2 mL of the release medium was accurately collected, and the absorbance was measured at 274 nm and 238 nm via a UV spectrophotometer.

Cell compatibility test

HUVECs and NIH/3T3 cells were seeded in 96-well plates. After the cells adhered to the wall, different concentrations of G microsphere leachate (0, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, 320, 640, and 1280 μg/mL) were added, and the cells were cocultured for 72 h. The culture medium was then aspirated, and the cells were washed with PBS. CCK-8-containing medium was added, and the cells were incubated for 1 h. The cytotoxicity of the extract of G microspheres was evaluated by measuring the absorbance values at 450 nm.

Cycle experiment

Microspheres from different groups were soaked in DMEM culture medium. After 72 h, the supernatant was taken out for CCK-8 assay using NIH/3T3 cells. Then, fresh DMEM medium was added to the culture dishes for another 72-h incubation. This process was repeated three times in total.

In vitro wound scratch assay

HUVECs and HSFb were added to a 12-well plate at a concentration of 3 × 105 cells per well. A sterile 10 μL pipette tip was subsequently used to create scratches on the formed monolayer of cells, and any resulting cellular debris was removed through a gentle washing step with PBS. Next, the cells were cultured for 24 h with different formulations. Images were captured at the beginning of the culture and after 24 h, and the migration distance of the cells was measured. The rate of wound closure was determined via the following formula:

W0h: initial wound width; W24h: wound width at 24 h.

ROS scavenging assay

The antioxidant effectiveness of samples is determined by evaluating their capacity to neutralize DPPH radicals, ⋅O2−, and H2O2. For the DPPH radical scavenging assay, microsphere samples from each group were placed in a DPPH ethanol solution and incubated at 37 °C in the dark, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 517 nm after the reaction to calculate the scavenging rate. For the ⋅O2− inhibition experiment, a xanthine oxidase system was used, and the inhibition efficiency was assessed by monitoring the formation rate of reduced nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) according to the kit instructions. For the H2O2 scavenging experiment, microsphere samples were immersed in H2O2 solution and incubated at 37 °C, and the resulting supernatant was collected via centrifugation. After mixing the supernatant with KI solution and allowing the reaction to proceed at room temperature in the dark, the characteristic absorption of iodine was measured at 350 nm to quantitatively analyze the scavenging capacity of H2O2.

Polarization of macrophages

RAW264.7 cells were seeded into a 24-well plate at a density of 7.5 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. After the old medium was aspirated and discarded, LPS at a concentration of 1 μg/mL was mixed with various leachates, and these mixtures were added to the wells of the 24-well plate for another 24-h incubation. The old culture medium was subsequently aspirated again, after which the cells were washed, collected, stained, and finally analyzed via flow cytometry.

ELISA test

The cells were cultured following the established protocol for flow cytometry, after which the cell culture supernatant was collected. A commercial ELISA kit was subsequently used to quantify the expression levels of IL-6 and TNF-α in the culture medium.

Wound healing assessment in mice

Seven-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were obtained and acclimated to a temperature-controlled breeding environment for one week prior to the experiment. The mice were subsequently randomized into six experimental groups. Anesthetize and depilate the mice. Two circular wounds (6 mm each) were created on both sides of the back of each mouse. Then, different microspheres were applied to the wounds, and the area was secured with sterile adhesive film. The wound morphology was recorded on days 0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 postinjury. The mice were euthanized on the 6th and 12th days after microsphere treatment, and the wound sites were excised along with the surrounding healthy skin tissue for subsequent experiments. The percentage of wound closure can be determined via the following formula:

Wound closure (%) = (Initial wound area − Final wound area)∕Initial wound area × 100.

Wound healing and scar assessment

First, New Zealand White rabbits were securely fixed in dedicated fixing boxes. Subsequently, 1 mL sterile syringes were used to draw up pentobarbital sodium solution, which were then injected via the marginal ear veins at a dose of 30 mg/kg to anesthetize the New Zealand rabbits. After the rabbits were fully anesthetized, a punch biopsy instrument was used to create 4 circular wounds on the ventral side of each ear. Each wound had a diameter of 6 mm, and the distance between adjacent wounds was >10 mm. The positions had been marked in advance with a marker pen to ensure accuracy. Then, the perichondrium at the wound was carefully removed using ophthalmic scissors and forceps, with care taken not to damage the cartilage during the operation. Finally, the microspheres of different groups were applied to the corresponding wounds respectively, and immediately sealed with a sterile transparent dressing film with good air permeability and sealing performance.

During the experimental period, photographs of the wound surfaces were captured using a high-definition camera at weeks 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 to document the healing process and scar formation of the rabbit ear wounds. At the end of the 4th week, the New Zealand rabbits were euthanized. Subsequently, the tissue samples containing the wound surfaces were precisely excised using a scalpel and tissue scissors, and immediately placed into appropriate containers filled with 10 % formalin fixative for preservation, so as to provide qualified tissue materials for subsequent experimental analysis.

Immunohistochemical assessment

HE, Masson, and Sirius Red staining are commonly used in histological studies to assess wound healing and scar formation. HE staining involved dewaxing and hydrating tissue sections, followed by staining the cell nuclei with hematoxylin, the cytoplasm and ECM with eosin, and finally dehydration, clearing, and mounting. Masson staining included dewaxing, Weigert iron hematoxylin staining, acid ethanol differentiation, Masson blueing, Ponceau Brilliant Red staining, phosphotungstic acid and aniline blue staining, which revealed the collagen and muscle fibers in tissues. Sirius Red staining involved dewaxing and hydrating the sections, staining them with Sirius Red solution, counterstaining them with hematoxylin, dehydrating them, clearing them, and mounting them, which were primarily used for observing the distribution and arrangement of collagen fibers in tissues.

Immunofluorescence assessment

Paraffin-embedded tissue samples were sectioned at 4 μm, placed on slides, and heated at 60 °C for 30 min. Sections were dewaxed in xylene, hydrated through ethanol solutions, and blocked before incubation with primary antibodies against CD86, CD206, and α-SMA overnight at 4 °C; nuclei were stained with DAPI (1 μg/mL) for 10 min. After being mounted with antifade medium and covered with a coverslip, the samples were imaged under a fluorescence microscope for analysis.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the means ± SDs. Statistical analyses were carried out with GraphPad Prism version 9.0. Group comparisons were made via unpaired Student's t tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as applicable. A P value of <0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Significance levels are indicated as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

Formulation and characterization of G/E/T

In this study, microfluidic chip technology was used to fabricate four microsphere systems, namely GelMA (G), GelMA-EGCG (G/E), GelMA-TA (G/T), and their ternary composite (G/E/T). To comprehensively evaluate the drug-loading performance of the G/E/T, we determined the drug loading (DL) and encapsulation efficiency (EE) capacity of EGCG and TA. The results showed that the EE of EGCG and TA was 80.05 ± 0.50 % and 93.83 ± 1.84 %, respectively, with their DL capacities being 8.89 ± 0.06 % and 10.43 ± 0.20 % (Fig. S1 and Table S1). These excellent drug-loading indices verify that the G/E/T has high-efficiency loading capacity for both drugs with significantly different physicochemical properties. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that the four microsphere types exhibited uniform spherical morphologies with narrow size distributions and were devoid of noticeable aggregation or structural defects, which could be attributed to the precise control of droplet generation by microfluidic technology (Fig. 1A). Notably, the G/E and G/E/T microspheres containing EGCG exhibited smoother surfaces. This may have resulted from incomplete incorporation of EGCG from the aqueous solution into the microspheres during redispersion. As the freeze–drying process removed moisture, the EGCG molecules on the microsphere surfaces formed a more uniform layer, thereby enhancing the surface smoothness. ImageJ analysis of the G/E/T microspheres revealed a normal distribution of particle sizes, with an average diameter of 95.58 ± 10.12 μm, and 97.73 % of the particles were concentrated within the 80–120 μm range (Fig. 1B and C). This optimized size range ensures an optimal balance between drug loading capacity and tissue permeability, thereby establishing a solid foundation for subsequent research on sequential drug release.

In the G/E/T microsphere system, the two drugs exhibit distinctly different distribution and release characteristics due to differences in their physicochemical properties. EGCG, being water-soluble, can bind to the surface and shallow pores of microspheres through hydrogen bonds. This binding mode allows it to quickly detach from the carrier via surface diffusion, thereby achieving early release. In contrast, the fat-soluble TA is stably encapsulated in the core of the three-dimensional GelMA scaffold through hydrophobic interactions, and its release process strictly depends on the degradation rate of the carrier material, thus forming a slow release in the middle and late stages. We systematically evaluated their sequential release characteristics of EGCG and TA in an in vitro environment. The experimental data clearly show that EGCG has a rapid release behavior, with a cumulative release rate of 83.27 % within two days. This rapid release is crucial in the early stages of wound healing, providing timely and sufficient drug action to support the initial healing process and effectively promote key physiological responses such as inflammation resolution and tissue repair. Starting from day 4, the release of TA followed a distinctly different pattern, gradually releasing at a slow and steady pace over a full 30 days, with the cumulative release rate reaching 86.47 % by day 30. The slow release of TA in the middle and late stages of healing continuously exerts its anti-scarring effects, promoting further tissue repair and remodeling (Fig. 1D). Moreover, the detection of the presence of EGCG and TA further confirms that the G/E/T microsphere system can successfully encapsulate these two drugs. This sequential drug release strategy is scientifically sound and rationally designed, matching the complex needs of different stages in the wound healing process.

The biocompatibility and blood compatibility of microspheres are prerequisites for their application in the field of biomedicine [29]. As a fibroblast model, mouse embryonic fibroblasts (NIH/3T3) cells play a key role in regulating collagen deposition during the tissue remodeling phase of wound healing [30], while human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), as a vascular endothelial cell model, promote the healing process by participating in angiogenesis in the early stage of repair, and together they cover the core links of wound healing [31]. In this study, the CCK-8 assay was used to assess the viability of HUVECs and NIH/3T3 after co-incubation with G microspheres. Even at a high concentration of 1280 μg/mL, the G microsphere system had extremely low toxicity to both cell types, indicating good biocompatibility (Fig. 1E). The hemolysis rate (HR) test further confirmed the blood compatibility of all the tested microsphere composites. The HR of all tested microsphere composites was below 1.0 %, indicating that no significant red blood cell rupture occurred (Fig. 1F). In brief, the microsphere materials developed in this study showed excellent biocompatibility and blood compatibility in vitro, laying a solid foundation for future in vivo applications and clinical translation.

In the later stage of wound healing, the excessive proliferation of fibroblasts is a core factor in scar formation [5]. Their hyperactivity leads to excessive deposition of collagen, which in turn causes tissue disorganization and scar formation [32]. To simulate the dynamic process of in vivo drug release over time, this study employed a multi-cycle method to detect the sustained inhibitory capacity of different microsphere composites on the proliferation of NIH/3T3 fibroblasts. The results showed that in the first cycle, the G/T group inhibited NIH/3T3 cell viability similarly to TA alone. However, in the 2nd and 3rd cycles, TA alone gradually lost its activity due to metabolism and degradation processes, failing to sustainably suppress fibroblast proliferation. In contrast, the G/T and G/E/T groups, benefiting from the protective and sustained release effects of the microsphere carrier, continuously released TA, effectively inhibiting NIH/3T3 cell proliferation over an extended period. Notably, the inhibitory effect of the G/E/T group on fibroblasts only became evident from the 2nd cycle, which is directly related to the characteristic of TA that achieves sustained release through carrier degradation (Fig. 1G and H). The G/E/T group only rapidly releases EGCG without releasing TA in the early stage, and it is not until the microsphere carrier gradually degrades and releases TA that the effect of inhibiting fibroblast proliferation can be exerted. This sustained release of TA enables G/E/T to provide stable therapeutic effects in the late stages of wound healing, aiding in scar prevention.

Antioxidant and anti-inflammation of G/E/T in vitro

The overaccumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) can trigger strong oxidative stress, causing severe cell damage and slowing healing. Conversely, effective ROS elimination can significantly reduce oxidative stress, providing a protective barrier for wound cells, accelerating tissue repair, and greatly increasing the healing speed [33,34]. Thus, neutralizing excess ROS in time is key to reducing unnecessary inflammation, promoting secondary inflammatory responses, and facilitating the wound-healing cascade. We used DPPH as a stable free radical to assess the antioxidant activity of the different microsphere groups. The results showed that the G/E and G/E/T microspheres with EGCG could eliminate 80 % of the DPPH radical scavenging ability, which was equivalent to that of EGCG alone, indicating that the antioxidant effect of EGCG was effectively maintained in the microspheres (Fig. 2A). In contrast, the G microspheres had negligible DPPH-scavenging activity, further confirming the key role of EGCG in antioxidant processes. The main intracellular ROS are superoxide anions (·O2−) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). For these two types of ROS, the G/E and G/E/T microspheres effectively eliminated them (Fig. 2B and C). These findings indicate that the G/E and G/E/T microspheres not only exhibit good antioxidant properties in vitro experiments, but can also effectively scavenge ·O2− and H2O2 under conditions that mimic the in vivo environment, thus protecting cells from oxidative damage and facilitating wound healing.

To further validate the antioxidant effects of G/E/T, a typical in vitro oxidative stress model was established by coincubating HUVECs with Rosup reagent. The intracellular ROS levels in HUVECs were subsequently assessed via a 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) assay. As shown in Fig. 2D and E, the fluorescence intensity of dichlorofluorescein (DCF) in HUVECs significantly increased after incubation with Rosup reagent. In contrast, in both the G/E and G/E/T groups treated with EGCG, ROS generation was markedly lower in the Rosup-induced HUVECs than in the cells treated with DCFH-DA alone. Additionally, we evaluated the antioxidant capacity of G/E/T by examining the migration of HUVECs induced by H2O2. As shown in Fig. 2F–H, compared with that of untreated cells, the migration area of HUVECs treated with G/E and G/E/T significantly increased after exposure to H2O2. This study further demonstrated that G/E and G/E/T microspheres exhibit significant antioxidant properties because of the ability of EGCG to scavenge reactive oxygen species. These characteristics not only protect cells from oxidative damage but also promote cell migration and tissue repair.

Oxidative stress and inflammation are intricately connected. The ROS generated during oxidative stress can directly injure cells and also induce inflammatory reactions. This results in the production of proinflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) and interleukin 6 (IL-6). These cytokines further exacerbate oxidative stress, forming a detrimental feedback loop that impairs the wound healing process [35,36]. Hence, developing therapeutic strategies that can simultaneously reduce oxidative stress and inflammation is crucial for promoting wound healing. We stimulated RAW264.7 cells with LPS to differentiate them into proinflammatory M1 macrophages, simulating the in vivo inflammatory environment. After 24 h of incubation, we collected the supernatant and used ELISA to measure the TNF-α and IL-6 levels. The results revealed a significant reduction in these cytokines in the G/E and G/E/T groups (Fig. 2I and J). These findings indicate that the G/E and G/E/T microspheres can break the vicious cycle of oxidative stress and inflammation, accelerating wound healing. This is because EGCG can block the activation of inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB, thereby inhibiting the excessive secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and thus exerting a significant anti-inflammatory effect [37].

Macrophages are categorized into proinflammatory M1 (marked by CD86) and anti-inflammatory M2 (marked by CD206) types on the basis of their functions and surface markers, and their balance is vital for wound healing [38]. M1 macrophages aid in early inflammatory pathogen and apoptotic cell clearance via cytokine secretion, whereas M2 macrophages suppress inflammation and foster tissue repair later [39]. However, in chronic wounds or pathological states, excessive M1 macrophage activation can cause prolonged inflammation and tissue damage [40]. Therefore, shifting M1 macrophages to the M2 phenotype helps restore immune balance, promotes inflammation resolution, and accelerates wound healing. As shown in Fig. 3A–C, after LPS stimulation, the percentage of CD86-positive RAW264.7 cells increased significantly, indicating M1 differentiation. Following incubation with G/E and G/E/T, the proportion of CD206-positive cells markedly increased, suggesting that the polarization of RAW264.7 cells from the M1 to the M2 phenotype shifted from a proinflammatory to an anti-inflammatory state. These findings demonstrate that G/E/T, with its encapsulated EGCG, can reprogram macrophages from the M1 to the M2 phenotype. This transition helps initiate subsequent repair and regeneration phases, laying a solid foundation for successful wound healing.

In skin wound healing, cell migration is crucial for repair and skin structure restoration [41]. However, excessive fibroblast migration in the late healing stages can lead to scar formation, compromising healing quality [42]. Controlling cell migration during this period can prevent excessive scarring, promoting a near-normal skin structure and function. Human scar fibroblasts (HSFb), key players in scar formation, synthesize collagen [43,44]. To assess the ability of G/E/T microspheres to inhibit late-stage cell migration, we performed in vitro wound scratch assays. We cocultured HSFb cells with microsphere leachates from different groups for 24h. The results revealed that, compared with the untreated group, the G/T and G/E/T groups had significantly reduced cell migration areas, with effects similar to those of TA alone, indicating that the microspheres have the potential to inhibit cell migration (Fig. 3D and E). This inhibition is mainly due to the TA in the microspheres, which has anti-proliferative and anti-migration properties. The microsphere carrier ensures sustained TA release, maintaining an effective inhibitory concentration in the wound area. This process controls collagen fiber deposition and alignment, preventing disordered scar formation.

In summary, in vitro studies indicate that G/E/T microspheres are pivotal for scar-free wound healing. EGCG is released early to harness its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, thereby accelerating the healing process. TA is subsequently released from the microspheres in a sustained manner to suppress cell proliferation and migration, preventing scar formation. By precisely targeting different stages of wound healing, this approach offers a promising strategy for rapid and scar-free healing.

G/E/T achieves scarless wound healing in C57BL/6 mice

In order to assess the therapeutic efficacy of G/E/T microspheres in vivo, we developed a C57BL/6 mouse model with 6 mm full-thickness skin defects (Fig. 4A). Analysis of healing kinetics revealed that both G/E and G/E/T groups exhibited clean and dry wound beds by postoperative day 3, which was closely associated with the rapid release of EGCG. By day 10, the G/E/T group demonstrated optimal wound closure with near-complete re-epithelialization, whereas the G/T group showed the poorest healing outcome (Fig. 4B and C). This discrepancy stemmed from the premature release of TA in the G/T group, which inhibited fibroblast proliferation and migration. Quantitative analysis of wound healing further corroborated these findings, showing that the G/E/T group achieved a wound closure rate of 99.62 % on day 10, which was significantly greater than that of the Ctrl and G/T groups (87.63 % and 76.46 %, respectively) (Fig. 4D). Notably, despite containing the same amounts of EGCG and TA as the G/E/T group, the blank cream group (BC) exhibited a healing efficacy that was lower than that of the Ctrl group. This is because the BC group lacks a sequential release mechanism, resulting in the synchronous release of the two drugs. TA exerted its inhibitory effect in the early stage, interfering with the normal process of wound healing, whereas the Ctrl group is not subjected to such inhibitory effect. This phenomenon highlights the critical importance of temporal regulation of drug release in achieving optimal therapeutic outcomes.

Re-epithelialization is a crucial early stage in skin wound healing that occurs before dermal repair. It provides a temporary functional barrier to damaged tissue, preventing excessive water loss and infection [45]. To confirm the regeneration of the epithelium and the associated formation of granulation tissue, we systematically performed hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining and Masson's trichrome staining on the wound samples from all the experimental groups on days 6 and 10 after injury. HE staining results revealed that on day 6, more pronounced wound openings and extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells were observed in all the groups except the G/E and G/E/T groups, indicating that the G/E/T microspheres played a significant role in reducing wound inflammation (Fig. S2 and S3). By day 10, wounds in the G/E/T group exhibited nearly complete healing, featuring a more intact epithelial layer and a greater number of hair follicle appendages than those in the other experimental groups (Fig. 5A and S4). As a pivotal structural element of the dermis, collagen deposition plays an indispensable role in expediting wound healing, as elevated collagen levels within the wound area accelerate tissue regeneration [46]. By using Masson staining, we further quantified the collagen volume fraction (CVF) to evaluate the collagen deposition in each group. Compared with the other groups, the G/E and G/E/T groups exhibited significantly denser and more extensive collagen deposition, which aligns with their accelerated healing progression (Fig. 5B and E).

To clarify the G/E/T's wound healing mechanism, the anti-inflammatory and angiogenic potential of G/E/T at wound sites was assessed. As shown in Fig. 5C and Fig. S5, compared with the other groups, the G/E and G/E/T groups had weakened CD86 signals but enhanced CD206 signals, indicating M1-to-M2 macrophage polarization. The results showed that the G/E/T group effectively exhibited anti-inflammatory effects by releasing EGCG to modulate macrophage polarization and regulate inflammatory cytokine expression (Fig. 5F and G). Given the crucial role of angiogenesis in wound healing [47], we evaluated new blood vessel formation by detecting α-SMA expression. Fig. 5D and H reveal that G/E/T treatment markedly elevated α-SMA expression relative to that in the Ctrl group, indicating that G/E/T treatment facilitates 3D vascular network formation and restoration. This provided nutrients for epidermal cell regeneration, accelerates re-epithelialization, and significantly promotes wound healing. Moreover, the α-SMA level in the G/T group was lower than that in the Ctrl group because of the premature release of TA, which was likely linked to TA-mediated regulation of angiogenesis-related factors during its release. Notably, the G/E/T group presented lower α-SMA expression than the G/E group did, likely due to the release of TA. However, this reduction had a minimal impact on rapid early-stage wound healing, as the early healing process relies primarily on the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of EGCG rather than on α-SMA expression. These findings indicate that despite the TA-induced reduction in α-SMA expression, its effect on overall healing outcomes was limited, and the G/E/T system remained effective in promoting wound healing.

Finally, the mice were humanely euthanized, and their major organs were subjected to HE staining. Histological examination of the G/E/T-treated group revealed preserved tissue architecture with normal cellular morphology and no inflammatory infiltration, necrosis, or hyperplasia. These findings suggest that at the tested dosage, the material did not induce notable toxicity in vital organs (Fig. S6).

The G/E/T microsphere system showed remarkable efficacy in multiple aspects of skin wound recovery. It effectively reduces inflammation, accelerates wound closure, promotes collagen deposition, and enhances angiogenesis. These combined effects provide comprehensive support for skin wound recovery, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic option for skin injuries.

G/E/T achieves scarless wound healing in rabbits

Since full-thickness skin defect models in mice are incapable of developing HS that are comparable to human pathology [48], we successfully developed a rabbit ear model of HS (Fig. 6A). Using this model, we comprehensively evaluated the capacity of the G/E/T microsphere system to enhance wound repair and suppress HS formation. During the first 2 weeks of the experiment, which corresponded to the wound healing phase, the results from the rabbit ear model were consistent with those from the mouse model. At week 2, the wound areas in the G/E and G/E/T groups were significantly smaller than those in the Ctrl group by 85.3 % and 86.1 %, respectively. By week 3, the wounds in the G/E and G/E/T groups had completely closed, indicating that the G/E and G/E/T groups, which contained EGCG, significantly accelerated early wound healing. HS formation mainly occurs in the late stage of wound healing. At week 4, the Ctrl, BC, G, and G/E groups all developed obvious red HS due to the lack of targeted anti-fibrotic intervention in the late stage. Among them, although the G/E group accelerated wound healing in the early stage due to the effect of EGCG, it still could not avoid the formation of HS because of the absence of TA's inhibition on the excessive proliferation of fibroblasts and collagen deposition in the late stage. The G/T group, while being able to inhibit excessive scar hyperplasia to a certain extent through the continuous release of TA, showed a significant delay in wound healing process due to the lack of early anti-inflammatory and repair-promoting effects of EGCG, and the wound was still not completely closed by week 4. In contrast, the G/E/T group, relying on the synergistic effect of EGCG, which exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects rapidly in the early stage to accelerate wound healing, and TA, which is continuously released in the late stage to inhibit fibrosis, not only achieved complete wound healing, but also had scar morphology closest to normal tissues with no obvious HS formation, fully demonstrating the advantages of the sequential drug release strategy (Fig. 6B–D).

Subsequently, the Vancouver Scar Scale (VSS), a validated clinical tool, was used for comprehensive evaluation of scar tissues in week 4, covering 4 dimensions: pigmentation, thickness, vascularity, and pliability. Compared with other groups, the G/E/T group had significantly lower scores in all four dimensions, further confirming that it had better scar healing outcomes (Fig. 6E).

With HE staining, we can intuitively observe the changes in the thickness of scar tissue. The data from this study indicate that the average thickness of scar tissue in the Ctrl group animals reached 1674.27 μm, which was 3.99 times the baseline value of normal skin (419.30 μm), intuitively reflecting the abnormal hyperplasia of scar tissue. The G experimental group showed no significant therapeutic effect on scar thickness regulation, with measurement data remaining statistically similar to the Ctrl group. In stark contrast, the G/T and G/E/T combination treatment groups achieved scar thickness values of 1089.47 μm and 785.00 μm, respectively, representing reductions of 34.93 % and 53.11 % compared to the Ctrl group, and demonstrating significant scar inhibitory effects (Fig. 7A and S7). Because physiological differences among individual experimental animals can cause natural variations in normal skin thickness, comparing only absolute thickness values might impact the conclusion's accuracy. To address this, we introduced the scar elevation index (SEI) as a standardized assessment parameter, quantifying the analysis by calculating the ratio of the scar area to the adjacent normal skin area. The SEI analysis results were highly consistent with the thickness measurement trends. The G/E/T combination treatment group achieved the lowest SEI value (1.39), which not only confirmed that this group had the most minimal scar elevation but also indicated that its histological features were closest to the physiological state of normal skin (Fig. 7B).

Abnormal deposition of collagen fibers is a core pathological feature in the formation of pathological scars. During the skin wound healing process, the excessive activation of fibroblasts leads to an imbalance between the synthesis and degradation of the collagen, ultimately resulting in HS [49]. In this study, we used Masson's trichrome staining combined with digital image analysis technology to quantitatively evaluate the changes in CVF among different treatment groups. The results showed that the CVF value of the Ctrl group was as high as 65.73 %, which significantly exceeded the baseline value of normal skin (46.43 %). The G group, containing only GelMA microspheres without any loaded drugs, had a CVF value showing no significant difference from that of the Ctrl group, indicating that the GelMA carrier had no regulatory effect on collagen deposition. Although the BC group contained EGCG and TA in the same amounts as the G/E/T group, due to the lack of a sequential release mechanism, the synchronous release of drugs led to only a slight decrease in its CVF value compared with the Ctrl group, resulting in limited antifibrotic effects. The G/E group, loaded only with EGCG, could accelerate healing in the early stage through antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, but it lacked the inhibition of TA on late-stage fibrosis. Its CVF value decreased by 11.2 % compared with the Ctrl group, which was better than that of the BC group and G group, but still significantly higher than the baseline of normal skin. The G/T group, with sustained release of TA, could inhibit fibroblast activity, and its CVF value decreased by 20.7 % compared with the Ctrl group, showing better antifibrotic effects than the G/E group. However, due to the lack of early inflammation control by EGCG, the CVF remained higher than the normal level. In contrast, the experimental group treated with the G/E/T microsphere system exhibited a significant regulatory effect on collagen, with the CVF value reduced to 49.77 %, representing a 24.3 % decrease compared to the Ctrl group (Fig. 7A and C). This significant antifibrotic outcome is likely attributable to the G/E/T microsphere system facilitating the long-term release of TA in the later treatment stages. Its slow-release characteristics can maintain local drug concentrations, prolonging the duration of TA's inhibitory effects on fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis, thereby achieving dynamic balance regulation of collagen metabolism.

In addition, the type of collagen fibers plays a crucial role in skin morphology and function. As the primary structural support of the skin, thick and rigid Type I collagen is overexpressed in HS tissue, leading to skin stiffness and functional limitations. In contrast, the smaller Type III collagen, which provides flexibility and elasticity to the skin, is significantly reduced in the context of HS [50]. To further analyze the type and distribution of collagen fibers, we employed Sirius Red staining combined with polarized light microscopy. The findings revealed that Type I collagen was predominant in the Ctrl group. However, following intervention with the G/E/T system, there was a notable rise in the proportion of Type III collagen (Fig. 7A and D). This suggests that the G/E/T microsphere system can help shift the collagen composition toward that of normal skin, thus enhancing the mechanical properties and appearance of scar tissue.

Excessive collagen deposition and angiogenesis are key driving factors for scar formation and contracture [51,52]. Myofibroblasts promote angiogenesis, cell migration, and collagen deposition through α-SMA expression [53,54]. Therefore, α-SMA is a key marker for distinguishing normal tissue from HS. We detected the expression level of α-SMA in different groups through immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 7A and E and Fig. S8). The results showed that the expression of α-SMA in the Ctrl group was significantly increased, indicating enhanced myofibroblast activity and abnormal angiogenesis; whereas the G/E/T group in the experimental groups had the lowest α-SMA expression, which was closest to the state of normal skin. These results demonstrate that the G/E/T system markedly down-regulates α-SMA expression, thereby blocking the excessive activation of myofibroblasts and their subsequent promotion of collagen deposition and angiogenesis, and consequently exerts regulatory control at the critical stages of scar formation.

In summary, the G/E/T microsphere system has been instrumental in expediting wound healing and impeding the development of HS. These results further underscore the system's potential to foster scar-free wound healing and offer a vital theoretical foundation for clinical intervention.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed a microsphere-based sequential delivery system, G/E/T, which promotes scarless wound healing by sequentially releasing EGCG and TA. By leveraging the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of EGCG in the early stages to reduce oxidative stress and inflammation and the antifibrotic effects of TA in the later stages to inhibit fibroblast over-proliferation and collagen deposition, the system effectively accelerates wound closure and prevents HS formation. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated the system's efficacy, showing accelerated wound healing, reduced inflammation, and enhanced collagen deposition in mouse and rabbit ear models, closely resembling normal skin. The system also exhibited excellent biocompatibility and hemocompatibility, making it a promising candidate for clinical applications. By maximizing the synergistic effects of EGCG and TA while minimizing side effects through precise timing, this innovative approach provides a robust foundation for scarless wound healing and advances the development of drug delivery systems for other biomedical applications requiring sequential multidrug release.
