CD30 CAR-T cells in combination with anti-PD-1 camrelizumab in relapsed/refractory CD30+ lymphomas
Authors: Min Yu, Longzi Liu, Xiaoxi Zhou, Yulan Zhou, Shan Xu, Fancong Kong, Ling Qi, Jing Wu, Tongcun Zhang, Fei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlthough CD30-directed chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy has demonstrated antitumor activity in CD30+ lymphomas, its therapeutic efficacy remains suboptimal. We therefore conducted a prospective, phase II, single-arm, multicenter clinical trial (ChiCTR-2100046763) to evaluate the efficacy and safety of CD30 CAR-T cell therapy in combination with camrelizumab, an immune checkpoint inhibitor, in patients with relapsed/refractory (r/r) CD30+ lymphomas.MethodsAll participants received a lymphodepleting regimen followed by infusion of CD30 CAR-T cells at a dose of 1 × 107 cells/kg. Camrelizumab was subsequently administered on a scheduled basis starting 15 days after CAR-T infusion and continued until unacceptable toxicity or disease progression.ResultsA total of 18 patients were enrolled, of whom 12 (66.7%) completed the CD30 CAR-T infusion, including eight with classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL) and four with T-cell lymphomas. Among 11 efficacy-evaluable patients, the best objective response rate (ORR) was 63.6%, including four complete responses (CR, 36.3%). After a median follow-up of 30.8 months, the median overall survival (OS) was not reached, and the median progression-free survival (PFS) was 11.1 months (95% CI, 0–26.2). In the subset of seven cHL patients, the ORR and CR rates were 100.0% and 57.1%, with 2-year PFS and OS of 57.1% and 100.0%, respectively. Remarkably, five cHL patients who had previously failed PD-1 blockade still achieved an ORR of 100.0% and a CR rate of 40.0%; median OS was not reached, and median PFS was 15.0 months. In contrast, none of the four T-cell lymphoma patients achieved an objective response. Cytokine release syndrome occurred in eight patients (66.7%), all grade 1–2. The most frequent grade 3–4 toxicities were lymphopenia (58.3%) and neutropenia (41.7%).ConclusionsThe combination of CD30 CAR T-cell therapy with camrelizumab demonstrated a favorable safety profile and elicited durable, clinically meaningful responses in patients with r/r cHL, including those who had failed prior PD-1 blockade. In contrast, although this regimen remained therapeutically well tolerated, its antitumor activity was limited in T-cell lymphomas, underscoring the need for alternative approaches or more effective combinatorial strategies in this disease context.Keywords: Chimeric antigen receptor, CD30, PD-1, Efficacy, Relapsed/refractory CD30+ lymphoma

Introduction

CD30 is a receptor expressed on several hematologic malignancies, most notably classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL) and anaplastic large-cell lymphoma (ALCL). Because its expression in normal tissues is limited, thereby minimizing “on-target, off-tumor” toxicity, CD30 has become an appealing therapeutic target [1]. Among the CD30-targeted strategies, the antibody–drug conjugate brentuximab vedotin (BV) has achieved the most striking clinical success, demonstrating response rates exceeding 75% with acceptable toxicity in relapsed/refractory (r/r) cHL and ALCL, leading to accelerated regulatory approval for both indications [2]. Nonetheless, durable remission remains uncommon, as most patients ultimately experience relapse. Moreover, the clinical benefit of BV appears less pronounced in non-ALCL CD30+ T-cell lymphomas [3]. These limitations highlight an urgent need for alternative, more effective therapeutic approaches.

Over the past decade, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy has revolutionized the treatment landscape of hematologic malignancies, achieving remarkable efficacy in CD19+ and BCMA+ lymphoproliferative diseases [4, 5]. This success has spurred efforts to extend CAR-T cell therapy to additional hematologic and even solid tumors. CD30-directed CAR-T cells have thus emerged as a promising option for r/r CD30+ lymphomas [6–9]. However, while generally well tolerated, their efficacy has lagged behind that of CD19-targeted CAR-T cells [10, 11]. In a phase I dose escalation study, 7 patients with r/r cHL and two with ALCL have received CD30 CAR-T infusion, achieving an objective response rate (ORR) of 33.3% [6]. Consistent with these findings, a separate trial involving 18 evaluable patients has reported an ORR of 39% following CD30 CAR-T infusion [9]. In a subsequent phase I/II study by Ramos et al., optimization of the lymphodepletion regimen leads to an improved response rate of approximately 60%; however, the 1-year progression-free survival (PFS) remains modest at 36.0% [8]. Taken together, these results indicate that, while CD30 CAR-T therapy demonstrates measurable antitumor activity, its overall efficacy remains suboptimal and warrants further therapeutic refinement.

PD-1/PD-L1 pathway activation is frequently observed in cHL and several T-cell lymphomas, and immune checkpoint blockade targeting this axis has shown impressive therapeutic activity [12–14]. PD-1/PD-L1 expression within the tumor microenvironment may contribute to CAR-T cell exhaustion, hypofunction, and treatment failure [15]. Importantly, inhibition of this pathway can reinvigorate exhausted CAR-T cells and augment their anti-tumor activity [16, 17]. Recent clinical studies exploring CAR-T therapy in combination with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade have reported encouraging results [18–20]. Moreover, elevated PD-1 expression has been detected in CD30 CAR-T cells at relapse following infusion [6]. These findings provide a strong biological rationale for investigating the combination of CD30 CAR-T cells with PD-1 blockade in appropriately selected patients. We conducted the present study to assess the efficacy and safety of CD30-directed CAR T-cell therapy in combination with camrelizumab, a commercially available humanized, high-affinity IgG4 monoclonal antibody targeting PD-1, in patients with r/r CD30+ lymphomas.

Materials and methods

Patients

This prospective, phase II, multi-center, single-arm, clinical trial (registration number: ChiCTR-2100046763) was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University and Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. Eligible participants had r/r disease after at least two prior lines of therapy. The diagnosis of lymphoma was confirmed according to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of tumors of hematopoietic and lymphoid tissues. CD30 positivity was required to be confirmed by immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, as independently assessed by at least two pathologists.

Additional inclusion criteria included: age ≥ 14 years; an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status ≤ 2; presence of measurable disease; and adequate hematologic, hepatic, renal, and cardiac function. Key exclusion criteria were as follows: autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) within 100 days prior to enrollment; active autoimmune disease; uncontrolled infection; or significant organ dysfunction.

All patients provided written informed consent in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committees of both participating centers.

CAR-T cell manufacturing

The construct used in this trial was a third-generation CD30-directed CAR lentiviral vector incorporating a single-chain variable fragment derived from a murine monoclonal antibody targeting human CD30 (patent number: CN106589139B) [21]. This CAR design contained dual co-stimulatory domains (CD28 and 4-1BB) linked to the CD3ζ signaling domain. Manufacturing and quality control procedures for CAR-T products were performed by Wuhan Bio-Raid Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Briefly, T cells were isolated from leukapheresis products using anti-CD3 magnetic beads (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) and subsequently activated with monoclonal anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). Two days following initial activation, the T cells were transduced with a lentiviral vector encoding the CD30–CD28-4-1BB-CD3ζ chimeric antigen receptor transgene. Forty-eight hours after transduction, excess lentivirus was removed by centrifugation, after which the cells were resuspended in fresh culture medium to support further expansion. The expanded cells were harvested and cryopreserved between days 10 and 12. Prior to infusion, the transduced CD3+ T cells underwent comprehensive quality control assessments, including evaluations of cell number, purity, viability, and sterility. Only CAR T-cell products that met all predefined release criteria were deemed eligible for clinical administration.

Clinical procedures

Patients received lymphodepleting chemotherapy with fludarabine (30 mg/m2/day) and cyclophosphamide (300 mg/m2/day) for three consecutive days (days −5 to −3) prior to CAR-T cell infusion. On day 0, CD30 CAR-T cells were infused at a target dose of 1 × 107 cells/kg. Beginning on day 15 after CAR-T infusion, camrelizumab (200 mg) was administered intravenously every 2 weeks. Treatment continued until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, death, or withdrawal for any other reason.

Study objectives and endpoints

The primary endpoint was ORR, whereas secondary endpoints included complete response (CR), duration of response (DOR), PFS, overall survival (OS), and safety. Disease burden and treatment response were evaluated by positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) or CT in accordance with the Lugano 2014 classification criteria [22]. Tumor response evaluations were performed 4 weeks after CAR-T cell infusion and every 3 months thereafter. Any discrepancies between assessments were resolved through consensus discussion.

Adverse events (AEs) were recorded from the initiation of lymphodepletion chemotherapy until 1 year post-CAR-T infusion. For each patient, only the highest grade of each AE type was reported. Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) were graded according to the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (ASTCT) criteria. In contrast, all other AEs were graded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0. AEs were categorized as immune-related (irAEs) if they (1) belonged to a recognized irAE category and (2) were unlikely to be attributable to CD30 CAR-T cells.

DOR was calculated as the time from initial response (CR or PR) to the first documentation of disease progression or death. PFS was defined as the interval from CAR-T cell infusion to disease progression, death, or last follow-up, whereas OS was defined as the time from the first CAR-T infusion to death or last follow-up.

Cellular kinetics and cytokine assessment

Droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) was employed to quantify CAR-expressing cells in peripheral blood samples collected at baseline and at multiple time points post-infusion, as previously described [7]. Briefly, DNA was extracted from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit, and amplification was conducted using primers and probes specific to the CAR single-chain antibody fragment (Wuhan Bio-Raid). Real-time PCR was performed using a Roche LightCycler 480 system. Flow cytometric analysis of CAR-expressing cells was performed on peripheral blood specimens following standard procedures [6]. Cytokines, C-reactive protein (CRP), and ferritin levels were measured according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

IHC staining for CD30 and PD-L1

IHC staining was performed on 4-μm-thick sections of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor specimens obtained from diagnostic or relapsed biopsy samples. Slides were incubated with an anti-CD30 antibody (clone Ber-H2; pre-diluted mouse monoclonal antibody, Dako) and an anti-PD-L1 antibody (clone 22C3; pre-diluted mouse monoclonal antibody, Dako) according to standard protocols, primarily using the automated Leica BOND-III immunostaining platform (Leica Biosystems). Staining intensity was semi-quantitatively scored as 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), or 3 (strong), while the proportion of tumor cells exhibiting positive staining (0–100%) was independently evaluated by two pathologists. An H-score, ranging from 0 to 300, was calculated by multiplying the staining intensity score by the percentage of positively stained tumor cells.

Statistical analyses

Sample size determination was performed using PASS software (version 15.0; NCSS) based on a single-stage phase II trial design. The study was powered to detect an improvement in ORR from 33.3% to 60% following CD30 CAR T-cell combination therapy. Under these assumptions, 25 evaluable patients were required to reject the null hypothesis that the post–combination regimen ORR was ≤ 33.3%, assuming a true ORR of at least 60%, with a two-sided α of 0.05 and 80% statistical power. To account for an anticipated dropout rate of approximately 15%, a total enrollment target of 30 patients was set. Continuous variables were summarized as medians and ranges, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons of continuous data were analyzed using independent two-sample t-tests or non-parametric tests, as appropriate. Categorical data were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. The probabilities of PFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and survival differences were compared with the log-rank test. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics

Between January 1, 2022, and January 30, 2025, a total of 18 patients were screened for eligibility in this trial. Six patients were excluded from the study. During the screening phase, two patients were excluded; one withdrew consent, and the other presented with a serious comorbidity while awaiting hospitalization. Subsequently, one patient was ineligible due to failed CAR-Tcell production. In addition, three patients were unable to proceed to infusion owing to disease progression prior to treatment initiation (Fig. 1). Ultimately, 12 patients (66.7%) successfully received the CD30 CAR-T cell infusion and were included in the study cohort. This group comprised eight patients with cHL and four with T-cell lymphomas, including two cases each of peripheral T-cell lymphoma–not otherwise specified (PTCL-NOS) and ALCL. Patients with cHL and ALCL exhibited strong CD30 expression, with a median H-score exceeding 150, whereas peripheral T-cell lymphoma, not otherwise specified (PTCL-NOS) cases showed only partial positivity. The median PD-L1 H-scores were 84 (range, 36–136) for cHL and 15 (range, 5–40) for PTCL.

Study CONSORT diagram

The median age of the enrolled patients was 30 years (range, 14–67), with eight males and four females. Prior to lymphodepletion, ten patients (83.3%) had stage III–IV disease, and six (50.0%) showed extranodal involvement, including lesions in the lung, bone, or liver. Patients had received a median of 3 prior lines of therapy (range, 2–5). The majority of patients had previously received chemotherapy, targeted therapies. Among the eight cHL cases, six (75.0%) had ongoing PD-1 blockade therapy or had received their most recent PD-1–based regimen within 3 months before enrollment. Only one cHL patient had previously received BV. Of the four patients with T-cell lymphoma, one had prior exposure to PD-1 inhibitors, and none had been treated with BV. Four patients had primary refractory disease, while eight patients experienced disease relapse and were refractory to their most recent prior therapy. All 12 patients exhibited disease progression prior to CD30 CAR-T cell infusion, and bridging therapy was administered in three cases.

The manufacturing process for CD30 CAR-T cells required a median of 16 days (range, 9–24), yielding anti-CD30 CAR expression rates of 86.5–99.8%, with comparable quality between products derived from cHL and T-cell lymphoma patients. Camrelizumab was administered for a median of nine cycles (range, 1–30), over a median duration of 19.2 months (range, 2–24). Treatment was discontinued due to disease progression in seven patients (7/11, 63.6%). As of April 30, 2025, the median follow-up duration was 30.8 months (range, 1–40.2), and no patients were lost to follow-up. A detailed summary of clinical characteristics is presented in Table 1.

Efficacy

In the overall study population, 11 patients were evaluable for disease response (Table 2; Fig. 2A). The best ORR was 63.6%, comprising four patients (36.4%) with CR, three (27.3%) with PR, three (27.3%) with stable disease (SD), and one (9.0%) with PD. The median time to initial response was 1 month (range, 1–3 months). In the cHL cohort (n = 7), the ORR at 1 month post-infusion was 100.0%, comprising two patients (28.5%) achieving CR and five patients (71.5%) achieving PR. By month three, two patients who initially had a PR converted to CR following anti–PD-1 therapy, resulting in a best ORR of 100.0% and a CR rate of 57.1%. Four patients maintained ongoing CR at the last follow-up, whereas all three patients with PR experienced PD after a median of 10.0 months. The median DOR in cHL was 26.4 months (95% CI, 15.7–37.0). Among those who progressed, two achieved PR after BV–based salvage therapy, and one attained PR following bendamustine treatment. All cHL patients remained alive at data cutoff. Conversely, none of the four patients with T-cell lymphomas achieved objective clinical responses. Among the T-cell lymphoma patients, three (75.0%) achieved SD, while one (25.0%) experienced progressive disease (PD) after 2–5 cycles of camrelizumab. Three patients subsequently died due to disease progression, whereas one patient with ALK+ ALCL remained alive after receiving multiple lines of therapy, including crizotinib, BV, mitoxantrone liposome, and lenalidomide. Representative PET-CT images of two cHL patients, obtained before CAR-T therapy and at the time of best response post-infusion, are shown in Fig. 2B.

Importantly, patients previously exposed to anti-PD-1 therapy retained favorable responsiveness to the CD30 CAR-T plus camrelizumab regimen. Among seven evaluable cHL patients, five had prior PD-1 blockade, achieving three PRs (60%) and two CRs (40%), with median OS not reached and median PFS of 15.0 months. In contrast, both anti-PD-1-naïve patients achieved CRs (ORR and CR 100.0%), and median PFS/OS were not reached (p = 0.227; Fig. 3G). Patients who achieved CR demonstrated substantially improved outcomes, with median PFS not reached, compared with 10.0 months in non-CR responders (p = 0.01; Fig. 3H). Due to slow patient recruitment and limited efficacy in T-cell subgroup, the study was prematurely terminated by the steering committee.

Safety

AEs of any grade were recorded in all enrolled patients (n = 12). The most frequently observed AEs included cytopenia (100.0%), CRS (66.7%), and rash (66.7%) (Table 3). A total of eight patients (66.7%) experienced CRS, all graded as mild to moderate (grade 1–2). Two patients required tocilizumab and corticosteroids for CRS management. The median onset of CRS was 4 days post-infusion (range, 3–9), with a median duration of 5 days (range, 3–6) under supportive care. Figure 4 illustrates the dynamics of serum cytokines, CRP, and ferritin levels in all patients during the first month following CAR-T cell infusion. Among the measured cytokines, interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) were markedly elevated in patients experiencing CRS, whereas IL-1β and IL-10 levels remained largely unchanged (Fig. 4A). In addition, CRP and ferritin levels increased post-infusion, with the elevation most pronounced in patients with CRS (Fig. 4B–C). These markers generally returned to baseline within 2 weeks. No cases of ICANS were reported.

Cytopenia was nearly universal (100.0%), with leukopenia (100.0%) being most common, followed by neutropenia (66.7%), thrombocytopenia (58.3%), and anemia (58.3%) following the lymphodepleting regimen. These cytopenias largely resolved within 1 month without ongoing growth factor support. Late-onset grade ≥ 3 neutropenia (41.4%) or thrombocytopenia (16.7%) occurred in three patients at a median of 100 days (range, 70–365) after infusion. Two of these patients showed hematologic recovery, while one experienced persistent grade 3 neutropenia, which improved to grade 2 after dose-interval adjustment of camrelizumab over the subsequent year.

Eight patients (66.7%) developed new-onset maculopapular, pustular, or eczematous rashes within 2 weeks after CD30 CAR-T infusion. These eruptions were predominantly asymptomatic, transient, and self-resolving without specific intervention. Reactive cutaneous capillary endothelial proliferation (RCCEP), a characteristic camrelizumab-related immune AE, was observed in five patients (41.7%), all mild (grade 1–2). Within the first month post-infusion, four infectious episodes occurred in three patients (25.0%), including one fatal COVID-19 infection. Subsequently, eight additional infectious episodes were recorded in four patients (33.3%), predominantly grade 1–2. Notably, the fatal AE occurred in patient 5 during the first month, who developed COVID-19–related acute respiratory distress syndrome despite treatment with nirmatrelvir/ritonavir, glucocorticoids, and intensive supportive care; this event might be considered treatment-related. Three additional deaths occurred during follow-up in the T-cell lymphoma subgroup, all attributable to disease progression. Other laboratory abnormalities, such as transient prolongation of activated partial thromboplastin time, hypocalcemia, and mild creatinine elevation, were clinically manageable and reversible.

CD30 CAR-T cell expansion and persistence

The kinetics and persistence of peripheral CD30 CAR-T cells were monitored at multiple time points using flow cytometry and PCR. In vivo expansion of CAR-T cells was observed, with peak concentrations generally occurring around day 14 post-infusion. Although significant CAR-T cell expansion was not observed following camrelizumab administration (p > 0.05; Fig. 5C), the overall expansion levels appeared higher compared with previously published data. Blood CAR-T cell counts did not differ significantly between cHL patients achieving CR versus PR (p > 0.05; Fig. 5D), between patients with or without CRS (p > 0.05; Fig. 5E), or between cHL and T-cell lymphoma cohorts (p > 0.05; Fig. 5F).

Antigen expression in PD patients post–CD30 CAR-T therapy

We sought to investigate CD30 expression in patients who experienced relapse or progression following CD30 CAR-T therapy. Repeated biopsies and immunophenotyping were performed in two of the three cHL patients with PD (Fig. 6). Interestingly, neither patient exhibited significant loss of CD30; instead, IHC analysis confirmed that neoplastic Hodgkin–Reed–Sternberg (HRS) cells retained CD30 expression. Therefore, the relapses in these two cases were more likely attributable to insufficient infiltration of the infused CD30 CAR-T cells rather than antigen escape.

Discussion

CD30 CAR-T therapy represents a highly promising therapeutic modality for r/r CD30+ lymphomas. A recent meta-analysis has reported that CD30 CAR-T monotherapy in r/r cHL achieves an ORR of 57%, with a CR rate of 34% [23]. Comparable efficacy estimates have been observed in real-world experience and recent meta-analyses evaluating single-agent BV therapy in r/r cHL [24]. Notably, a single infusion of CD30 CAR-T cells appears to offer efficacy similar to multiple cycles of BV while potentially improving patient adherence, a critical consideration for adolescent and young adult patients with cHL [23]. Nevertheless, despite favorable tolerability, there remains substantial room for improvement relative to the striking success observed with CD19-targeted CAR-T therapy. Accumulating evidence indicates that the anti-tumor activity of adoptive T-cell therapy can be further enhanced through PD-1 blockade [25]. In this context, we reported the clinical outcomes of a prospective cohort of patients with r/r CD30+ lymphomas who received camrelizumab following CD30 CAR-T infusion. Our findings demonstrated notable clinical efficacy, particularly in cHL, with manageable safety profiles, thereby supporting the rationale for combining PD-1 inhibition with CD30 CAR-T therapy.

In this study, the combination approach yielded a best ORR of 100.0% and a CR rate of 57.1%, outcomes that compare favorably with those reported in CD30 CAR-T monotherapy trials and meta-analyses [23]. Furthermore, survival outcomes were encouraging, with an estimated 2-year PFS of 57.1% and 2-year OS of 100.0%, trends that surpassed those observed with CD30 CAR-T monotherapy, which have reported a median PFS of 6 months, a 1-year PFS of 39%, and a 1-year OS of 89% within follow-ups ranging from 9.5 to 71.5 months [6, 8, 9]. These results aligned with prior studies evaluating anti-PD-1 antibody consolidation post–CD30 CAR-T therapy, which have demonstrated ORRs approaching 100%, CR rates up to 80%, and low toxicity [7, 26]. Notably, among seven evaluable cHL patients in our cohort, five had received prior anti–PD-1 therapy, of whom three achieved PR (60%) and two achieved CR (40%), suggesting that the synergistic effect of PD-1 blockade might be preserved even in the context of prior PD-1 resistance. In the remaining two cHL patients without prior PD-1 exposure, both ORR and CR reached 100.0%. Importantly, all seven r/r cHL patients remained alive at last follow-up. Collectively, these findings indicated that the addition of PD-1 blockade could substantially enhance the efficacy of CD30 CAR-T therapy in r/r cHL patients, supporting its potential as a highly effective combinatorial approach.

The efficacy of CD30 CAR-T cell therapy in r/r cHL patients who have previously undergone CD30-targeted therapy remains a significant clinical challenge [27]. Notably, the approval of BV for medical insurance coverage in China is relatively delayed, resulting in less than 10% of patients in this cohort having prior exposure to BV. Nevertheless, prior studies of CD30 CAR-T therapy have included patients who had received BV or experienced progression following BV treatment, and prior BV exposure does not appear to compromise the therapeutic activity of CD30 CAR-T cells [6, 8]. In our cohort, three patients with cHL ultimately experienced relapse. No significant down-regulation or loss of CD30 protein expression was observed in tumor samples following CD30 CAR-T therapy, suggesting that, in these cases, the CAR-T cells might not have persisted long enough to sustain durable immune surveillance. However, as decreased CD30 expression after CAR-T treatment has been reported in other studies, and because the Ber-H2 clone (which does not recognize the CAR-targeted epitope) was used, potential misinterpretation of the targeting epitope cannot be excluded. Therefore, we cannot rule out the possibility of CD30 reduction under treatment pressure [28]. Encouragingly, two of the three relapsed patients were successfully salvaged with BV, with one achieving a CR. In support of this observation, the BELIEVE study has demonstrated that BV re-treatment is highly effective, achieving an ORR of 76.7% and a high proportion of CRs as best response (68.8%) [29]. Collectively, these results indicate that CD30 expression is generally preserved in cHL, and antigen loss does not appear to represent a major mechanism of resistance to anti-CD30 therapies. Our findings further strengthened the evidence for the therapeutic relevance of CD30 targeting and provided a foundation for future studies aimed at optimizing treatment strategies in this patient population.

CD30 is uniformly expressed by systemic ALCL cells and is also detected across other subtypes of T-cell lymphomas, including 32–64% in PTCL-NOS, 50–100% in enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma (EATL), and 46–80% in angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma (AITL) [30]. According to NCCN Guidelines, BV is recommended as the preferred second-line therapy for r/r ALCL and other CD30-positive T-cell malignancies, including PTCL-NOS, highlighting the potential of CD30 CAR-T cells as a therapeutic option in this setting [31, 32]. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that CD30 CAR-T cells can suppress tumor growth and promote tumor lysis in PTCL models [33]. Clinical trials have also enrolled patients with CD30+ T-cell lymphomas [6, 9, 34]; however, the overall efficacy has been limited. Published studies report that among four ALCL patients, two have achieved at least a PR, and one patient with EATL has experienced a durable remission lasting 24 months following CD30 CAR-T therapy [35]. In a recent consolidation study post-ASCT, five of six patients with T-cell lymphoma have relapsed despite CAR-T therapy [36]. In the present study, we attempted to enhance CAR-T efficacy through subsequent PD-1 blockade; however, therapeutic outcomes remained suboptimal despite anti-PD-1 administration. One possible explanation was that CD30 expression levels on T-cell lymphomas might fall below the threshold required for effective CAR-T activation. Nonetheless, our patients exhibited moderate to high CD30 expression and significant in vivo CAR-T expansion, suggesting intrinsic resistance to CAR-T–mediated cytotoxicity. Additional factors, such as inadequate tumor penetration, intra-tumoral heterogeneity, and immunosuppressive elements within the tumor microenvironment, may also contribute [37]. Despite a favorable safety profile, these disappointing results underscored the current limitations of anti-CD30 CAR-T therapy in T-cell lymphomas and highlighted the need for alternative or combinatorial therapeutic strategies in this setting.

In our study, the combination of CD30 CAR-T cell therapy and camrelizumab did not induce high-grade CRS or ICANS, consistent with previous CD30 CAR-T monotherapy studies, in which only low-grade CRS and no neurotoxicity are observed [6, 34]. Acute CRS primarily occurred within the first 2 weeks following CAR-T cell infusion, whereas camrelizumab was administered starting 2 weeks post-infusion. Hematologic events were observed in all enrolled patients but were largely self-limiting or reversible with appropriate management. In most cases, these cytopenias could be attributed to lymphodepletion. Some patients experienced delayed cytopenias, particularly grade 3–4 neutropenia, which was most likely an irAE associated with camrelizumab, in line with findings from other CAR-T cell trials combined with checkpoint inhibitors [20, 38]. Nonetheless, potential contributions from lymphodepletion, CAR-T infusion, and limited hematopoietic reserve to these late cytopenias could not be completely excluded. Rashes were reported at frequencies comparable to previous CD30 CAR-T clinical trials (40–48%) [6, 9]. Biopsies of affected skin revealed CAR-T cell infiltration, suggesting a possible mechanistic link to the observed dermatitis [8]. RCCEP, a camrelizumab-specific irAE, occurred in 70% of patients but was generally mild and did not require special intervention [39]. Other AEs were largely transient and clinically manageable throughout the study. Notably, based on collaborative experiences, a total dose of 1 × 107/kg CD30 CAR-T cells was infused without any attributable AEs [7]. This dose is considerably higher than that typically used in CD19- or BCMA-targeted CAR-T therapy, where 1 × 106/kg is often sufficient to achieve robust clinical responses [40, 41]. A likely explanation is that the relatively low abundance of CD30+ cells within the tumor mass necessitates higher numbers of CAR-T cells to achieve adequate antigen stimulation and therapeutic efficacy.

In vivo expansion of CD30 CAR-T cells was observed, reaching peak concentrations approximately 14 days after infusion. Evidence of CAR-T cell activity was apparent from early tumor regression and CAR-T cell expansion prior to the administration of camrelizumab. We hypothesized that camrelizumab could further enhance both the efficacy and in vivo expansion of CAR-T cells. However, administration of camrelizumab did not induce a secondary expansion of CD30 CAR-T cells. Furthermore, no significant differences in blood CAR-T cell levels were observed among patients regardless of CRS occurrence, clinical response (CR or PR), or lymphoma subtype (cHL or T-cell lymphoma). A plausible explanation was that CAR-T cells were gradually depleted from the circulation, yet detectable CAR-T cells persisted at 3 months post-infusion, indicating improved persistence compared to previously published data [6, 42]. These findings underscore the potential role of the camrelizumab-based regimen in sustaining CAR-T cell persistence, which might contribute to enhanced therapeutic efficacy.

Additional mechanisms underlying the clinical benefit of this combination therapy might include: (1) camrelizumab had activity independent of CD30 CAR-T cells; (2) checkpoint blockade immunotherapy often requires pre-existing tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes for optimal activity; adoptive transfer of CD30 CAR-T cells provides tumor-specific T cells, thereby increasing the efficiency of checkpoint blockade; and (3) CD30 CAR-T cells may eliminate allo-reactive or regulatory T cells (Tregs), which could contribute to the observed durable responses [43].

In summary, this study has several limitations, including a small sample size, a heterogeneous patient population, and a relatively short median follow-up, which introduces uncertainty regarding long-term efficacy, durability, and potential late-onset adverse effects of the combination therapy. In addition, correlative biomarker analyses were limited by sample availability. Future studies involving larger biomarker cohorts are warranted to validate predictive signatures of response, including baseline PD-1 expression, plasma PD-L1 levels, and markers of T-cell exhaustion. Moreover, factors potentially influencing CAR-T cell proliferation and persistence were not fully assessed in this study. These included the phenotypic characteristics of the infused T cells, features of the tumor microenvironment, as well as intrinsic properties of the patients’ own T cells and tumor cells. Nevertheless, our results indicated that the combination of CD30 CAR-T cells and camrelizumab elicited a safe and durable response in r/r cHL, including in patients previously refractory to PD-1 blockade. This novel combination therapy warrants further validation in larger prospective trials to determine optimal dosing and treatment protocols. Importantly, while this regimen demonstrated safety in patients with T-cell lymphomas, efficacy was limited, highlighting the need for alternative therapeutic strategies in this population.
