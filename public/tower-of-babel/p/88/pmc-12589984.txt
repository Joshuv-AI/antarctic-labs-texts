Isobutyric Acid Regulates Gelatinase Secretion by Human Periodontal Ligament Stem Cells Via NF-κB Signalling
Authors: Ying Tu, Xiaoyao Fu, Xinjie Ning, Huiling Zheng, Li Zhang, Qiang Guo, Minglei Huang, Hanxi Cao, Haoran Chen, Yonglu Jia, Mengmeng Duan, Yang Liu, Caixia Pi, Biao Ren, Chengcheng Liu, Jing Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriodontitis is primarily an inflammatory condition initiated by bacterial dysbiosis. Isobutyric acid, a metabolite derived mainly from subgingival pathogenic bacteria, induces apoptosis and cancer cell metastasis. However, its role in periodontitis remains elusive. The aim of this study was to investigate the effects of isobutyric acid on the destruction of periodontal tissue and the underlying mechanisms involved.MethodsThe concentration of isobutyric acid in the saliva of clinical subjects was detected via liquid chromatography‒mass spectrometry. Isobutyric acid local injection models in 5-week-old male Sprague‒Dawley rats and an isobutyric acid treatment model of human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs) were established to explore the effect of isobutyric acid on periodontal tissue destruction. Alveolar bone resorption in the rats was evaluated via microcomputed tomography. The activity and expression of gelatinases (MMP-2/MMP-9) and signalling proteins in hPDLSCs and periodontal tissues were detected via zymography, western blotting, immunofluorescence, and immunohistochemistry.ResultsA higher concentration of isobutyric acid was detected in the saliva of individuals with gingivitis and periodontitis than in that of healthy individuals. Local injection of isobutyric acid induced damage to periodontal soft tissues and increased resorption of alveolar bone in rats. Under in vitro conditions, isobutyric acid increased the activity of gelatinases in hPDLSCs, and isobutyric acid-regulated gelatinase secretion occurred mainly via the activation of NF-κB (p-p65) signalling. In rat periodontal tissues, the effects of isobutyric acid on the expression of gelatinases and NF-κB (p-p65) were verified.ConclusionThis study revealed a direct interaction between the metabolite isobutyric acid and hPDLSCs through the regulation of the activity of gelatinases, which partially explains the degradation of periodontal tissue and resorption of alveolar bone.Key words: Isobutyric acid, Microbial metabolite, Periodontitis, Gelatinases, NF-κB signalling

Introduction

Periodontitis, a chronic inflammatory disease that leads to periodontal tissue destruction, is initiated by bacterial dysbiosis. This disease affects more than 10% of adults worldwide.1,2 The primary characteristics of periodontitis include periodontal pocket formation, alveolar bone resorption, and tooth loosening.3 Periodontal tissue destruction is attributed to an imbalance between subgingival bacteria and the host immune response.2,4 The subgingival region harbours a complex ecosystem that comprises diverse microorganisms and their microenvironments. To maintain oral health, the host immune response must not only resist pathogenic bacterial invasion but also sustain homeostasis with the commensal microbiota.5 In most cases, virulence factors derived from pathogenic bacteria stimulate host cells to release inflammatory mediators, which leads to degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM) in periodontal tissues and resorption of alveolar bone.6

The accumulation of subgingival plaques is a fundamental prerequisite for the destruction of periodontal homeostasis. Subgingival plaque is predominantly composed of periodontal pathogens and their metabolic byproducts.7 Pathogenic microbial molecules that interact with host cells can be broadly classified into three categories: whole pathogens, outer membrane vesicles released by pathogens, and metabolites secreted by pathogens.7,8 The metabolites released by subgingival bacteria include short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), bacteriocins, and amino acid derivatives.9,10 These metabolites bind to transmembrane receptors on host cells, triggering the secretion of various cytokines, including interleukin-6 and interleukin-10, ultimately promoting an acute proinflammatory response.11

Among microbial metabolites, SCFAs have been shown to significantly contribute to periodontal destruction.12 SCFAs are generated in the oral cavity by bacterial fermentation of amino acids,13 which can exert cytotoxic effects and induce tissue inflammation and damage,12,14 independently influencing cell behaviour. Butyrate, for example, has been shown to impair the integrity of the gingival epithelial barrier and promote apoptosis via the upregulation of caspase-1 expression.15,16 Furthermore, butyrate can disrupt the equilibrium between matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and their inhibitors, tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), thereby decreasing fibroblast viability.17 Isobutyric acid, a branched-chain fatty acid structurally related to butyric acid, is derived from valine fermentation.12 Leonov et al18 reported that isobutyric acid was secreted in the supernatant of a range of subgingival pathogens, such as Porphyromonas gingivalis, Porphyromonas asaccharolytica, and Prevotella intermedia. Isobutyric acid upregulates the expression of the G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) GPR41 and GPR43 in lung cancer cells, leading to increased apoptosis and cell cycle arrest.19 Oral administration of isobutyric acid in mice has also been shown to modulate antitumour immunity.20 Moreover, this metabolite is associated with rheumatoid arthritis and may contribute to muscle catabolism via inflammatory responses.21 These studies underscore that isobutyric acid might play a potent role in the pathogenesis of inflammatory diseases.

Connective tissue destruction in periodontitis is typically initiated by the overexpression of inflammatory factors, followed by the excessive secretion of tissue hydrolases.22, 23, 24 MMPs represent a major class of such hydrolases and are classified into 6 subtypes on the basis of their substrate specificities, with 23 distinct members identified in humans.25 They directly facilitate the degradation of ECM components and can cleave procytokines and prochemokines to convert them into active forms, thus further accelerating the inflammatory process and tissue destruction.26,27 Gelatinases, also called MMP-2 and MMP-9, are integral subtypes of the MMP family and are instrumental in ECM degradation.28 In particular, MMP-2, which is derived predominantly from fibroblasts, degrades gelatine as well as type I and III collagens in gingival tissue.29 MMP-9, a potential biomarker for periodontal diagnosis, degrades various collagen and fibronectin substrates,30 stimulates host immune cells, and modulates IL-6 and IL-8 production.31 Moreover, both MMP-2 and MMP-9 can activate other MMPs, such as MMP-1, MMP-7, and pro-MMP-13, thereby indirectly promoting collagen degradation.32 This interactive action loop eventually results in connective tissue attachment loss, collagen bundle damage, and connective tissue texture degradation.31,33

Given the potential involvement of isobutyric acid in inflammatory pathogenesis, we hypothesized that isobutyric acid influences gelatinase activity and contributes significantly to the initiation and progression of periodontitis. In this study, we aimed to provide direct evidence of the impact of isobutyric acid on tissue hydrolases, underscoring the importance of this bacterial metabolite in disease occurrence in oral tissues.

Materials and methods

Salivary sample collection

Salivary sample collection from human patients was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Stomatology (WCHSIRB-D-2023-370). All the subjects were categorized into three groups, each comprising 15 individuals: the healthy group, the gingivitis group, and the periodontitis group. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for the clinical participants are provided in Text S1. The participants avoided spicy foods, chewing gum, smoking, and any oral cleaning activities for 2 hours prior to the collection process to ensure that the samples were not compromised. Unstimulated saliva was collected in a sterile centrifuge tube, centrifuged at 4°C for 15 minutes at 1000 × g to ensure clear supernatant, and stored at –80°C for evaluation analysis.

Mass spectrometry

The extracted metabolites were stored at low temperature. Standard solutions of different concentrations were prepared, and these standard solutions and test samples were subjected to liquid chromatography‒mass spectrometry (LC‒MS) detection under the same conditions. The specific LC‒MS parameters are provided in Text S2. The concentration of isobutyric acid was determined according to the standard curve.

Cell culture

The use of the human periodontium was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University (WCHSIRB-D-2023-398). Human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs) were isolated from periodontal ligament (PDL) tissues. To summarize, we gathered healthy adult premolars that were free from caries and periodontal disease, with patients aged 12 to 24 years as previously described.34 The PDL tissues located in the middle third of the tooth root were carefully scraped into 1 mm3 pieces. The pieces were then treated with 3 mg/mL collagenase I solution (17100017, Gibco) at 37°C. These pieces were subsequently incubated in culture flasks with alpha-minimum essential medium (α-MEM, SH30265.01B, HyClone) containing 1% penicillin‒streptomycin and 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, SH30406.06, HyClone) and cultured in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37°C. The identification of hPDLSCs is provided in Text S3 and Figure S1. In this study, hPDLSCs between passages 3 and 5 were utilized.35

Cell counting kit-8 assay

To investigate the cytotoxicity (cell viability) of isobutyric acid on hPDLSCs,17,36 a cell counting kit-8 (K04-500T, Dongren) solution was used in Text S4 to determine the appropriate experimental concentrations (Figure S2).

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed according to a previously described protocol.37 After being washed with phosphate buffer solution (PBS), the hPDLSCs treated with isobutyric acid in Text S5 were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (68117726, Biosharp). Protein samples were separated by electrophoresis and then transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore). The membranes were subsequently blocked with 5% skim milk and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The primary antibodies used were as follows: anti-β-actin (1:1000, T200068-8F10, Zen-Bio), anti-MMP2 (1:1000, 10373-2-AP, Sanying), anti-MMP9 (1:1000, 10375-2-AP, Sanying), anti-NF-κB p65 (1:1000, 250060, Zen-Bio), anti-phospho-NF-κB p65 (1:1000, 310013, Zen-Bio), anti-JNK (1:1000, 380556, Zen-Bio), anti-phospho-JNK (1:1000, 381100, Zen-Bio), anti-p38 (1:1000, N340697, Zen-Bio), anti-phospho-p38-MAPK (1:1000, 310091, Zen-Bio), anti-ERK (1:1000, 343830, Zen-Bio), and anti-phospho-ERK (1:1000, 301245, Zen-Bio). Afterwards, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (1:5000, sc-516102, Cruz Marker; 1:5000, sc-2357, Cruz Marker) before visualization via a ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (ChemiDoc Touch, Bio-Rad). ImageJ (FIJI, NIH, USA) was used to quantify the band intensities.

Animal experiments

All the animal experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (WCHSIRB-D-2023-589) and strictly followed the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 for reporting. Five-week-old male Sprague–Dawley rats were obtained from Chengdu Dashuo Animal Center. The sample sizes (6-11/group, total N = 12-22) for the study were calculated via the resource equation method, and we selected the minimum sample size per group (n = 6). The rats were housed in a controlled environment with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and a temperature of 23 ± 2°C and maintained at a humidity of 50% to 60% with adequate ventilation. Three rats were kept in each cage, which was lined with nontoxic bedding and changed twice a week. Animal care included daily monitoring for health and well-being, and environmental enrichment included the use of nesting materials. After 7 days per treatment (Text S6), the study was designed as a randomized controlled trial with a computer-generated random number table, with 12 rats randomly assigned to two experimental groups (PBS, isobutyric acid groups, n = 6). After anesthetization with inhaled isoflurane, the rats were separately injected with PBS or isobutyric acid (5 mM, selected on the basis of both previous clinical data and experimental studies)38, 39, 40 into the palatal mesial and distal alveolar bone crest (ABC) of the maxillary second molar three times per week. After 4 weeks, all the rats were first fully anesthetized with isoflurane and then euthanized by cervical dislocation to ensure rapid and humane termination. The maxillae were subsequently obtained and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, BL539A, Biosharp). These samples were used for microcomputed tomography scanning (μ-CT50, Scanco) and histological staining analysis. Rats were excluded if rapid weight loss and mental or behavioural burnout occurred. The investigator, who was blinded to the group assignments used in the experiments, evaluated the outcomes and analysed the data via random cage units. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the ‘3R’ principles.

Gelatin zymography

Gelatin zymography was performed according to a previously described protocol.34 Equivalent protein samples were electrophoresed on gels with 0.05% gelatin (V900863, Sigma‒Aldrich) for 135 minutes. The gels were washed with 2.5% Triton X-100 (P0096, Beyotime) to reactivate the enzymes. The gels were then incubated in proteolysis buffer (50 mM CaCl2, 0.5 M NaCl, 50 mM Tris, pH 7.8) at 37°C for 12 to 16 hours. The gels were stained with Coomassie blue for 2 hours and destained with destaining buffer. Enzymatic activity was visualized as white bands against a blue background, and band densities were quantified via ImageJ software.

Microcomputed tomography (μ-CT)

The rat maxilla samples were scanned with a micro-CT scanner (μ-CT50, Scanco). The scanning parameters were set as follows: tube voltage at 70 kVp, current at 200 μA, exposure time at 300 ms, and resolution at 10 μm. Three-dimensional reconstruction of the tissues was accomplished via SCANCO Medical Evaluation and Visualizer software. The region extending from the cementoenamel junction to the ABC of the maxillary second molar was subjected to analysis. The bone volume/tissue volume ratio and degree of trabecular separation were also analysed.

Immunofluorescence analysis and confocal laser scanning microscopy

Immunofluorescence was performed according to a previously described protocol.41 The hPDLSCs were fixed and then incubated with primary antibodies. The samples were subsequently incubated with secondary antibodies. Afterwards, the cells were treated with FITC-conjugated phalloidin and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) to visualize the cytoskeleton and the nuclei (Text S8). Fluorescence imaging was performed via a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM FV3000; Olympus), and the quantification of fluorescence intensity was conducted via ImageJ software. The fluorescence intensity was quantified via integral densitometry, and the total fluorescence intensity within a defined region of interest and the average fluorescence intensity per cell were calculated. The results obtained were compared with those of the control group.

Immunohistochemistry

The maxillary sections were initially dewaxed with xylene, followed by rehydration and antigen retrieval. The sections were treated with the relevant primary antibody. The next day, the sections were incubated with an IgG secondary antibody. Immediately after, they were treated with a 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution kit (SK-4100, Vectorlabs) (Text S9). To observe changes in periodontal tissue, the sections were imaged via a microslide scanner (Olympus SLIDEVIEW VS200, Olympus) for initial assessment.

Statistical analysis

All the data are expressed as the means ± standard deviations and were derived from a minimum of three independent experiments (n ≥ 3). The Shapiro‒Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the data distribution. When the normality test resulted in a P value less than .05, nonparametric tests were conducted. In contrast, parametric tests were employed. For comparing two groups, either an unpaired Student’s t test or a Mann‒Whitney test was selected based on the normality of the data. For analyses involving three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted, with Tukey’s HSD test used for multiple comparisons if the ANOVA indicated significance (P < .05).

Results

Isobutyric acid induces periodontal tissue damage and alveolar bone resorption in rats

To investigate the function of isobutyric acid in the progression of periodontal disease, isobutyric acid was first detected in the saliva of individuals with periodontitis and healthy individuals via LC‒MS. The results revealed that the concentration of isobutyric acid in the saliva of individuals with gingivitis and periodontitis was much greater than that in healthy individuals (Figure 1A), indicating its important potential role during the occurrence and development of periodontitis. Hence, to investigate the direct role of isobutyric acid in periodontitis, we established a rat model via direct injection of isobutyric acid as previously described42 to clarify whether isobutyric acid is involved in tissue destruction in periodontitis (Figure 1B). Four weeks after isobutyric acid injection, samples were collected and analysed via μ-CT to assess alterations in alveolar bone (Figure 1C-F). Using 3D reconstructed images from μ-CT, we found that compared with the control treatment, isobutyric acid treatment resulted in more severe bone resorption. The distance between the cementoenamel junction and the ABC in the isobutyric acid-treated group was significantly greater than that in the control group (Figure 1C, D). With respect to the μ-CT parameters, we confirmed the loss of alveolar bone via analyses of bone volume/tissue volume (Figure 1E) and trabecular separation (Figure 1F). At the histological level, we performed H&E and Masson staining to detect changes in periodontal soft tissue and alveolar bone (Figure 1G, H). H&E staining revealed periodontal tissue damage caused by isovaleric acid (Figure 1G), and Masson’s trichrome staining revealed disordered collagen fibre tissue in the gingival matrix of the rats in the isovaleric acid treatment group (Figure 1H). Collectively, these results indicate that isobutyric acid treatment can cause damage to periodontal tissue and the loss of alveolar bone.

Isobutyric acid enhances MMP-2 and MMP-9 activity in hPDLSCs

Given the damage caused by isobutyric acid to periodontal tissue, we investigated the effects of isobutyric acid on hPDLSCs, which are responsible for the repair and regeneration of periodontal tissue.43 We first investigated the activity of gelatinases, a core subtype of the MMP family that plays an important role in the degradation of the ECM.28, 29, 30 Zymography revealed that isobutyric acid increased the activities of MMP-2 and MMP-9 in hPDLSCs in a dose- and time-dependent manner (Figures 2A and 3A). As the active and proenzyme bands of MMP-2 are 62 and 72 kDa, respectively, protein dimers with the ability to digest gelatine substrates are formed above the proenzyme band (>72 kDa). Similarly, MMP-9 dimers with the ability to digest gelatine substrates also formed above the proenzyme band (>92 kDa), and the formation of these protein dimers was very large, even approaching the enzyme activity at the proenzyme band (92 kDa). Quantitative analysis revealed that the activities of MMP-2 and MMP-9 increased in a time-dependent manner in hPDLSCs treated with isobutyric acid at different concentrations (Figures 2B and 3B). We then quantified the activities of MMP-2 and MMP-9 at 96 hours, and the results revealed that significant differences were distinctly observed at concentrations of 100 and 200 μM relative to the control group (Figures 2C and 3C). We next detected changes in the total protein content of MMP-2 and MMP-9 via western blotting, and the results revealed that the protein expression of MMP-2 and MMP-9 increased with increasing isobutyric acid concentration (Figures 2D and 3D). Quantitative analysis of the optical density confirmed changes in the total amounts of the MMP-2 and MMP-9 proteins (Figure 2E and 3E). In rat periodontal tissue, we detected greater expression of MMP-2 and MMP-9 in the isobutyric acid-treated group than in the PBS control group (Figures 2F and 3F). Quantitative analysis of optical density further confirmed the changes in MMP-2 and MMP-9 expression in the periodontal tissue of the rats (Figures 2G and 3G). Taken together, these results indicate the significant effect of isobutyric acid on gelatinases in hPDLSCs.

Isobutyric acid activates NF-κB signalling

To elucidate the signalling involved in the regulation of gelatinase secretion in hPDLSCs, we performed western blotting to determine activated signalling. The results indicated that isobutyric acid increased the upregulation of NF-κB (p-p65) in hPDLSCs (Figure 4A, red box). In contrast, other signalling pathways such as MAPK signalling (p-ERK, p-JNK, and p-p38), which involves in maintaining basal cellular physiological activity, was not substantially altered. Quantitative analysis of p-p65 confirmed that the expression of p-p65 was significantly increased by isobutyric acid, especially at 100 µM isobutyric acid (Figure 4B). We then assessed the changes in the protein distribution of p-p65 in hPDLSCs induced by isobutyric acid. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that p-p65 was distributed throughout the cytoplasmic and nuclear regions, and that isobutyric acid increased the expression of p-p65 in both the cytoplasmic and nuclear regions (Figure 4C). Quantitative analysis of the mean fluorescence density confirmed these results (Figure 4D). More importantly, the accumulation of p-p65 in hPDLSCs induced by isobutyric acid in the nuclear region was much greater than that in the cytoplasm (as indicated by the white boxes), and quantification of the linear fluorescence density confirmed the increase in p-p65 in the nuclear region (Figure 4E). In rat periodontal tissue, we also verified that isobutyric acid treatment significantly increased the expression of p-p65 in periodontal tissue (Figure 4F), and that the high expression of p-p65 induced by isobutyric acid occurred mainly in the nuclear region (the expression of p-p65 coincided with that in the nucleus, as indicated by the purple boxes). Quantification of the mean optical density further confirmed these results (Figure 4G).

Isobutyric acid increases gelatinase secretion by activating NF-κB signalling

To further confirm the role of p-p65 in the regulation of gelatinases, we applied SC75741, a specific inhibitor of NF-κB (p65).44 The results of western blotting revealed that SC75741 effectively inhibited the expression of p65 and p-p65 in hPDLSCs in the presence of isobutyric acid (Figure 5A). Quantitative analysis confirmed the changes in the p65 and p-p65 levels (Figure 5B). To investigate the change in the distribution of p-p65 in hPDLSCs induced by SC75741 in the presence of isobutyric acid, we performed immunofluorescence staining (Figure 5C), which revealed that SC75741 impaired the increase in p-p65 in hPDLSCs induced by isobutyric acid and, importantly, that the nuclear accumulation of p-p65 was eliminated. Quantitative analysis of the mean fluorescence density confirmed these results (Figure 5D). Zymography revealed that the activities of MMP-2 and MMP-9 were impaired in hPDLSCs by SC75741 in the presence of isobutyric acid (Figure 5E). The quantification of MMP-2 (Figure 5F) and MMP-9 (Figure 5G) activity further confirmed these changes. These data indicate that isobutyric acid regulates the expression of MMP-2 and MMP-9 mainly through the activation of NF-κB p-p65.

Discussion

Alveolar bone resorption is widely recognized as a significant clinical hallmark of periodontitis deterioration and is modulated by bacterial metabolites, inflammatory factors, and chemokines.1,28,45 For example, tryptophan-derived metabolites in the oral cavity can activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, leading to upregulation of the cytokine IL-17 and mediating inflammation.46,47 Notably, the increase in 5-hydroxytryptophan in rat models of periodontitis results in the activation of osteoclast formation through the NF-κB pathway and contributes to alveolar bone resorption.48 Similarly, derivative metabolites of glycerophospholipids produced by pathogenic bacteria can increase RANKL expression and increase the levels of IL-1 and IL-6, further inducing bone absorption.49,50 Moreover, the secondary metabolite bacteriocin has been demonstrated to increase IL-22 levels in macrophages and modulate cytokine levels through the regulation of multiple signalling cascades.51 In the present study, isobutyric acid stimulation in rats induced distinct pathological changes in periodontal tissues, such as disruption of the PDL and marked alveolar bone resorption. These observations indicate that isobutyric acid is a crucial bacterial metabolite involved in the pathogenesis of periodontitis.

MMPs play a pivotal role in ECM degradation. The subtype gelatinases MMP-2 and MMP-9 are essential enzymes for ECM breakdown because they target type I and III collagens, as well as gelatine.28,30 These enzymes are predominantly secreted by neutrophils and macrophages and can activate downstream signalling through the TGF-β pathway.52 Chen et al53 demonstrated that isobutyric acid can interact with RACK1, activating downstream pathways and enhancing the transcriptional activity of MMP-2/MMP-9, which in turn promotes colorectal cancer metastasis. In our study, the in vitro results demonstrated that isobutyric acid stimulation significantly increased both the activity and protein expression levels of MMP-2/MMP-9 (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). Consistent with these findings, tissue-level analysis revealed greater expression of MMP-2/MMP-9 in the alveolar crest of the isobutyric acid-treated group than in that of the control group (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). These results collectively imply that isobutyric acid contributes to periodontitis pathogenesis through the modulation of gelatinase activity, with a subsequent influence on disease onset and progression. The dynamic equilibrium of MMPs/TIMPs is also crucial. Typically, under inflammatory conditions, the production of TIMPs is suppressed, and the expression of MMPs is overactivated, disrupting the MMP/TIMP equilibrium and facilitating tissue degradation.54 A comprehensive understanding of gelatinase function in periodontitis must therefore incorporate the regulatory role of TIMPs. Moreover, isobutyric acid is one of the downstream products of branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs).55 BCAAs can reduce the levels of IL-6 by regulating signalling pathways such as NF-κB and MAPK, thereby affecting the inflammatory state of skeletal muscle and causing inflammatory bone loss.56, 57, 58 We propose that the effects of isobutyric acid on alveolar bone resorption and gelatinase secretion observed in this study may be partially mediated through BCAA-related pathways.

Multiple signalling pathways, including the NF-κB, MAPK, and Wnt pathways, are involved in the occurrence of periodontitis.59 SCFAs regulate the activation of NF-κB signalling by activating the receptors FFA2 and FFA3.60 Butyrate, for example, modulates the phosphorylation of ERK, p38, JNK, and NF-κB via the TLR4 pathway, ultimately inducing apoptosis in colorectal cancer cells.61 Zhang et al28 reported that the activity of MMP-2/MMP-9 is predominantly regulated by NF-κB signalling. Similarly, in this study, the findings revealed that NF-κB signalling was also the central mechanism involved in isobutyric acid-induced periodontal destruction (Figure 4). Isobutyric acid treatment resulted in notable upregulation of NF-κB (p-p65) signalling in vitro and in vivo. Blockade of NF-κB (p65) signalling by SC75741 partially suppressed the activity of MMP-2/MMP-9 (Figure 5). These findings indicate that isobutyric acid modulates the activity of MMP-2/MMP-9 via NF-κB (p-p65) signalling. However, the phosphorylation levels of key components in the MAPK pathway (ERK, p38, JNK) did not exhibit consistent or statistically significant changes in hPDLSCs induced by isobutyric acid. This selective activation of the NF-κB signalling compared to the MAPK signalling highlights a distinct mechanism of action of isobutyric acid, which differs from that of butyric acid, a known metabolite in regulating cytoplasmic signalling.61

Several unresolved issues remain that merit further investigation. Although the NF-κB-dependent axis in hPDLSCs in the context of periodontitis was the focus of this study, intercellular interactions within the periodontal microenvironment should not be overlooked. Zhan et al62 revealed that pexidartinib-mediated inhibition of CSF-1R expression restrains macrophage senescence through the reprogramming of glycolysis in the periodontitis microenvironment. Concurrently, evidence indicates that SCFAs can indirectly influence blood glucose levels by regulating glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) secretion and thereby modulating glycolysis.63 These findings indicate that the potential crosstalk between glycolysis and isobutyric acid could form a synergistic axis that amplifies immune senescence and oxidative stress, ultimately exacerbating periodontitis. Moreover, virulence factors such as LPS play a dominant role in periodontitis.12,64 LPS triggers HIF-1α expression through coactivation of the p38/MAPK and NF-κB pathways, reducing hPDLSC proliferation.65,66 Both virulence factors and microbial metabolites, such as isobutyric acid, likely work together to promote the occurrence and deterioration of periodontitis. Finally, the cell receptors corresponding to isobutyric acid were not investigated. We believe that the selective activation of NF-κB pathway is the key signalling in isobutyric acid-regulated gelatinase secretion. This finding suggests that isobutyric acid, as an important bacterial metabolite, may not trigger a generalized inflammatory signalling response but rather operates through a more specific mechanism that predominantly involves the NF-κB pathway. This specificity might be attributed to the potential receptor mechanism of isobutyric acid.67 As a SCFA, it is likely sensed by specific GPCRs on host cells. The engagement of these receptors can preferentially activate NF-κB phosphorylation without robustly activating MAPK pathways (ERK, p38, and JNK). Although SCFAs are known to signal primarily through GPCRs, which can modulate host immune responses via histone deacetylase regulation,67,68 the exact receptors for isobutyric acid and their role in NF-κB activation require further elucidation. These findings indicate that further investigations are needed to elucidate the isobutyric acid receptor-mediated activation of NF-κB signalling.

Conclusion

This study revealed that the metabolite isobutyric acid directly interacts with hPDLSCs and regulates the activity of gelatinases via NF-κB (p-p65) signalling, which partially explains the degradation of the periodontium and absorption of alveolar bone in periodontitis (Figure 6). This discovery may offer a potential novel therapeutic strategy for managing and controlling periodontitis progression.

Schematic showing that isobutyric acid regulates gelatinase activity in hPDLSCs via NF-κB signalling. Isobutyric acid (IBA) induces canonical NF-κB activation in human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs), resulting in the nuclear translocation of the p65 subunit. NF-κB (p-p65) signalling subsequently upregulates matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) and MMP-9 expression, thereby promoting periodontal tissue degradation and alveolar bone resorption.
