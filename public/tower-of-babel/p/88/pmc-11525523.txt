Effect of Waste Glass Powder and Bamboo Fiber on the Physico-Mechanical Properties and High-Temperature Behavior of a Fly Ash-Based Geopolymer
Authors: Tonggui Cheng, Chengfeng Xiong, Caihong Zhou, Zhenyang Li, Xinli Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the properties of fly ash-based geopolymers containing waste glass powder (WGP) and bamboo fiber (BF) after exposure to ambient and high temperatures. Precursor replacement of fly ash by WGP at 2.5, 5, 7.5, and 10% levels coupled with the addition of BF at 1, 1.5, 2, and 2.5% levels was used to enhance the reactivity and toughness of the geopolymer matrix. The impact of WGP and BF on the fluidity, setting time, density, water absorption, and compressive and flexural strengths of the geopolymer pastes was examined. The experimental results showed that the physical properties of the geopolymer were altered by adding WGP and BF. The optimal percentages of WGP replacement and BF addition were 5 and 2%, respectively. Additionally, tests such as thermogravimetric analysis, mass loss, thermal shrinkage, compressive and flexural tests, visual observation, optical microscope, scanning electron microscopy, and X-ray diffraction analysis were conducted to assess the behavior of the geopolymers after exposure to high temperatures (200–800 °C). Addition of WGP and BF led to higher residual strengths, demonstrating that the geopolymer composites tended to gradually densify with increasing temperature due to the pore-filling effects of WGP and the channel effects of BF, while the pure geopolymer continuously developed more cracks and wider pores. The study provides a viable approach for using solid waste materials and renewable resources in the production of high-performance geopolymer composites as sustainable building materials.

Introduction

Geopolymers have emerged as a prominent subject of discussion within the field of materials science owing to their substantial potential as alternative to conventional cement. The appeal of geopolymers extends beyond their performance capabilities; they also offer a viable means of repurposing industrial byproducts and waste materials, thereby contributing to a more circular economy. The production of fly ash (FA), a byproduct of coal combustion in thermal power plants, is a significant global concern. Annually, millions of tons of FA are generated, posing substantial risks to human health and exacerbating a range of environmental issues, as highlighted by Nguyen et al. in 2023.1 Despite its status as a waste material, FA is increasingly recognized for its potential as a valuable resource, primarily due to its high silica and alumina content.

The utilization of FA in the synthesis of geopolymers presents a dual advantage: it addresses the challenge of FA disposal while simultaneously providing a sustainable solution to the demand for construction materials. By transforming FA into geopolymers, we can effectively mitigate the surplus of FA and harness its latent benefits, paving the way for a more environmentally conscious approach to materials science and construction.

The use of FA as a key ingredient in the synthesis of geopolymers has been extensively documented by a multitude of scholarly investigations.2−4 Majority of the research on FA-based geopolymers focuses on product development, roles of alkali and its concentration on the mechanical performance, as well as durability assessments.5−8 However, FA-based geopolymers are not without their challenges, as they often suffer from poor toughness and susceptibility to cracking. In particular, microcracks are prone to form under harsh environmental conditions, which can be triggered by fluctuations in load and temperature. These microcracks may eventually merge to form one or more macrocracks that can propagate and cause structural damage. Therefore, new technologies should be adopted to enhance the mechanical properties of FA-based geopolymers to promote their broader adoption in construction projects. Various strategies aimed at improving the mechanical performance of FA-based geopolymers have been proposed. Notably, these enhancements are often achieved through the incorporation of diverse types of fibers,9,10 aggregates,11,12 and alternative silica–alumina source material,13,14 which collectively contribute to the overall performance of the geopolymers.

In recent years, there has been a growing recognition of the viability of incorporating waste glass powder (WGP) or cullet into geopolymers, either as source material or aggregate. This is largely due to the high amorphous silica content and uniform composition of waste glass (WG), which can serve as a viable source of silica akin to FA.15,16 When WG is employed as a partial precursor in geopolymer formulations, studies such as those by Wang et al.17,18 have showed that the compressive strength of alkali-activated slag mortar can be enhanced by incorporating 10% and 20% WG as a replacement. Similarly, Novais et al.,19 El-Naggar and El-Dessouky,20 Catauro et al.,21 and Si et al.22 explored the use of WG as a supplementary Si source in metakaolin-based geopolymers, noting that the highest compressive strength was achieved at specific WG concentrations, while further additions led to decreased strength. Researchers such as Tho-In et al.,23 Jiang et al.,24 and Ziejewska et al.25 delved into the impact of WGP substitution levels for FA on the physical characteristics of geopolymer pastes, with a consensus emerging that optimal compressive strength was achieved at 20% WGP content. Additionally, Jiang et al.24 and Ziejewska et al.25 have observed that the incorporation of WGP can also enhance the fire resistance of geopolymers.

Bamboo fiber (BF) is renowned for its renewability and degradability, and it possesses exceptional qualities, including high tensile strength, a high modulus, and excellent abrasion resistance. Recent studies have explored the use of BF to toughen geopolymers, with notable contributions from Sá Ribeiro et al.26−28 and Zhang et al.29−31 However, the impact of WG on the performance of geopolymers, as reported in the literature, has not been uniformly positive and sometimes presents contradictory findings.32,33 To date, there has been a noticeable absence of a comprehensive study that examines the use of both WGP and BF as reinforcing materials in FA-based geopolymers. Furthermore, several critical aspects need in-depth exploration, particularly the high-temperature behavior of these composite materials.

The primary aim of this investigation was to enhance the strength properties of FA-based geopolymers by partially replacing the precursor with WGP and incorporating BF as a reinforcing material. The present study focuses on the synergistic effect of incorporated WGP and BF on the physical behavior and mechanical characteristics of FA-based geopolymers, both before and after being subjected to high temperatures. The findings presented herein can serve as a valuable reference for designing sustainable geopolymer composites that incorporate WGP, BF, and FA, thereby facilitating the broader adoption of these materials in the construction of eco-friendly building materials.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Fly ash (FA) of Class F, sourced from the Huabang Mineral Products Processing Factory in Shijiazhuang, served as the aluminosilicate precursor to prepare of geopolymer samples. Additionally, waste glass powder (WGP), collected from the dust collector at an alkali-free glass fiber cloth processing workshop in Hengyang, China, was employed to partially replace FA. The chemical compositions of the FA and WGP, as determined through X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analysis and detailed in Table 1, revealed a notably high silica content in each. Specifically, the WGP exhibited a substantial 67.92% SiO2 content, along with a significant presence of CaO. These findings underscore the substantial potential of WGP in geopolymer synthesis. The FA used in the study contained a mere 3.16% CaO, while the WGP contributed higher amounts of CaO (10.98%) and Na2O (11.06%). These additional components are expected to influence the Si/Al ratio, thereby potentially altering other properties of the resulting geopolymer.

The natural bamboo fiber (BF) employed in this study was sourced from Ningbo Rengui Bamboo Fiber Science and Technology Co., Ltd., China. With a density of 0.68 g/cm3, BF’s primary chemical constituents include 41% by weight of cellulose, 28% of lignin, and 10% of hemicellulose. Before use, the BF was meticulously cut into segments approximately 20 mm in length, followed by three rounds of washing with deionized water and subsequent drying in a 60 °C oven until it reached a consistent weight. This meticulous preparation ensures the purity and consistency required for subsequent experimental procedures.

Geopolymer specimens were fabricated through the alkaline activation of FA and WGP with an activator modulus of 1.2. The FA and WGP were accurately weighed and thoroughly blended before being combined with BF. Subsequently, this mixture was combined with the alkaline activator and stirred until a homogeneous consistency was achieved. The resulting fresh geopolymer paste was then poured into cubic silicone molds and subjected to vibration on a vibrating table for 2–3 min to remove any remaining air bubbles. Following this, all specimens within the molds were sealed using a thin plastic film to maintain moisture, and were cured at 60 °C and a relative humidity of 45–65% for a duration of 24 h. Afterward, they were demolded and subjected to a further curing process at a temperature of 20 ± 2 °C with a relative humidity of at least 80% for a period of 28 days. A comprehensive depiction of the geopolymer composite preparation process is illustrated in Figure 3.

Preparation process of bamboo fiber-reinforced geopolymer composites.

Temperature Exposure Regime

The prepared geopolymer specimens were subjected to a fire test by being placed into a Muffle furnace (model SX2–2.5–12, manufactured by Shanghai Lichen Bangxi Instrument Technology Co., Ltd.). The specimens were exposed to progressively elevated temperatures of 200 °C, 400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C for a period of 1 h each. After the heat treatment, the specimens were permitted to cool naturally to ambient temperature within the furnace, resulting in the acquisition of the heat-treated geopolymer specimens for further analysis.

Test Methods

Fluidity and Setting Time

The fluidity of the fresh geopolymer pastes was assessed using a standard flow table test (model NLD-3, Beijing Zhongjiao Jianyi Technology Co., Ltd.) following the guidelines outlined in GB/T 2419–2005. The setting time was determined with a Vicat apparatus (Shanghai Luda Experimental Instrument Co., Ltd.) in compliance with the Chinese standard GB/T 1346–2011. The reported values represent the mean of three independent measurements to ensure accuracy and reliability.

Density and Water Absorption

The density was determined by measuring both mass and volume, with the reported values being the average of six measurements. Water absorption was evaluated in accordance with the Chinese standard GB/T 5486–2008 and calculated using eq 1. For this test, specimens measuring 25 × 25 × 25 mm3 were first saturated in water at a temperature of 20 ± 5 °C for a period of 24 h. Postsoaking, the specimens were removed, excess water was gently wiped off using a clean, dry cloth, and the specimens were then weighed to obtain their wet mass, denoted as M. The initial dry mass of the specimens, referred to as M0, was also recorded to facilitate the calculation of water absorption.

Thermal Shrinkage

The dimensions of the prisms were measured using a vernier caliper both before and after exposure to high temperatures. The thermal shrinkage value was then computed using eq 2, where L0 and L represent the lengths of the samples before and after exposure to high temperatures, respectively.

Compressive Strength and Flexural Strength

The compressive strength and three-point flexural strength were assessed using an universal testing machine, strictly in compliance with the Chinese standard GB/T 17671–1999. The specimen dimensions for the compressive strength and flexural strength tests were 25 × 25 × 25 mm3 and 40 × 40 × 160 mm3, respectively. Six and three specimens were measured separately for compression and bending tests, and the average values were calculated. Details regarding the equipment and testing procedures are graphically depicted in Figure 4.

Universal mechanical testing machine (a), compressive strength test (b), and flexural strength test (c).

Microstructure Analysis

After completing the flexural strength tests, the fracture surfaces of the samples were examined using an optical microscope (AO-FD600E, Shenzhen Aoswei Optical Instrument Co., Ltd., China). Small block-shaped samples were taken from the center region of the fracture section and prepared for Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM, model S4800, Hitachi, Japan) observation by applying a thin layer of gold to enhance conductivity and image quality. Concurrently, additional block samples were collected from areas adjacent to fracture center, ground into powder and passed through a 200-mesh sieve, and subjected to Thermogravimetric (TG) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) tests. TG analysis was carried out using a NETZSCH TG209F3 thermogravimetric analyzer, with the temperature range spanning from 25 to 800 °C under flowing nitrogen (20 mL/min) at a consistent temperature ramp of 10 °C/min. The XRD analysis was performed using an Empyren powder diffractometer (PANalytical, Netherlands), employing Cu–Kα radiation (36 kV, 20 mA) at a scanning rate of 0.02°/s.

Results and Discussion

Effect of WGP

Fluidity and setting time represent pivotal considerations in the investigation of geopolymer materials. Typically, examinations regarding the impact of low glass constituents (<10%) on fluidity and setting time are scarce. Effect of WGP on the Properties of Geopolymers

Evidently, it was observed that an increase in the WGP replacement level led to a decrease in fluidity. This reduction is likely due to the irregular shape and larger particle size of WGP particles compared to FA, which was also noted by Ziejewska et al.25 in their study of FA-based geopolymers. They reported that the incorporation of 10% WGP with a particle size finer than 250 μm resulted in diminished flow diameter and Novikov’s cone immersion depth in fresh geopolymer mortars. Conversely, other research, such as that conducted by Jiang et al.24 and Manikandan and Vasugi,35 suggests that WGP can enhance the fluidity of fresh geopolymer slurries due to the smooth surface and low water absorption characteristics of WGP.

Despite these conflicting results, the influence of WGP on fluidity appears insignificant, particularly at lower concentrations. The use of WGP as a precursor in FA-based geopolymers is deemed viable owing to the consistency and homogeneity it imparts to the paste. In fact, the fluidity of WGP-incorporated geopolymers is predominantly contingent upon various factors, encompassing the nature, functionality, dimensions, and surface roughness of WGP, alongside the specific type of geopolymer used. Further research is necessary to fully understand these complex interactions, as highlighted by Dong et al.36

The data presented in Table 3 illustrates that substituting FA with WGP notably reduced both the initial and final setting times of the pastes. Specifically, incorporating WGP at levels of 2.5%, 5%, 7.5%, and 10% led to reductions of 0.87%, 4.78%, 9.13%, and 12.17% in the initial setting time, and 1.27%, 3.48%, 5.06%, and 8.54% in the final setting time, respectively. Corresponding outcomes were corroborated by Jiang et al.24 and Ziejewska et al.,25 who observed similar trends in FA-based geopolymers. However, this is in contrast to earlier studies, such as those by Wang et al.17,18 and Özkılıç et al.,37 which suggested that the water-repellent characteristics of glass could potentially extend the setting time of alkali-activated materials. Understanding and guidance are still insufficient due to conflicting insights and limited data within existing literature. Further investigations are imperative to clarify the impact of glass constituents on the setting time of cementitious pastes.38

Research on the impact of WGP on the density of geopolymers is limited and presents varying results, which can be attributed to differences in the source of WGP and the type of geopolymer matrix.19,25,39 In this study, the addition of WGP had a negligible effect on the density of the geopolymer. Furthermore, a marginal correlation was observed between the WGP content in geopolymers and their water absorption capacity. As expected, the water absorption diminished with increasing WGP content in geopolymers, aligning with the findings of Ziejewska et al.25

The impact of WGP doses exceeding 10 wt % on the compressive and flexural strengths of geopolymer composites has been extensively documented. This study, however, delves into the effects of a lower WGP dosage on the strengths of FA-based geopolymers. As depicted in Figure 5, the compressive strength of the geopolymers with WGP are slightly higher than that of geopolymer without WGP. The highest compressive strength value (45.15 MPa) was achieved with a 5% WGP substitution, surpassing that of the pure geopolymer by 3.44%. This enhancement in compressive strength is linked to the increased reactivity and accelerated dissolution rate of the silica present in WGP. These properties are helpful in accelerating the geopolymerization reaction and fostering a greater degree of gel formation, thereby enhancing the overall structural integrity of the composite.15,34,35

Effect of WGP on the strengths of geopolymers.

Regarding flexural strength, its development differs from that of compressive strength. Specimens incorporating 0%, 2.5%, 5%, 7.5%, and 10% WGP exhibited flexural strengths ranging from 3.94 to 4.27 MPa. The flexural strength initially increased and then decreased with rising WGP substitution, peaking at 5% WGP content. This indicates that 5% WGP within the geopolymer matrix represented the optimal proportion for achieving enhanced mechanical performance when the WGP substitution remained below 10%. As illustrated in Figure 2, FA particles were spherical, whereas WGP displayed an irregular shape and a higher aspect ratio compared to FA. This morphological disparity can contribute to the increased flexural strength observed in composites containing WGP. However, the smooth surface and the presence of microcracks in WGP can lead to a weakening effect on the flexural strength of the composites.40 Consequently, a decline in flexural strength was observed at higher WGP substitution levels.

Effect of BF

On the basis of utilizing FA with a 5% WGP substitution to prepare geopolymers, this investigation delves into the influence of BF on both the slurry and the resulting hardened composites to explore their synergistic effect. As indicated in Table 4, the incorporation of BF resulted in decreased fluidity and setting time of the slurry, attributed to its porous network structure and notable hygroscopic properties.41 These characteristics of BF are also the primary contributors to the observed reduction in density and the corresponding increase in water absorption of the geopolymers. Similar effects of BF on the density and water absorption of geopolymers have been documented in our prior studies.29,30

Effect of BF Content on the Basic Physical Properties of the Geopolymers

The addition of BF introduces a new dimension to the geopolymer’s properties, with implications for both processing and performance. The decrease in fluidity and setting time during the slurry stage suggests a need for careful consideration of mixing casting procedures and curing schedules. These changes in processing parameters are critical to ensure the production of high-quality geopolymer composites with the desired characteristics. Moreover, the altered physical properties, such as reduced density and increased water absorption, indicate potential trade-offs in the material’s performance. While these properties may be beneficial in certain applications where lightweight and high-water retention capabilities are advantageous, they may also suggest a compromise in mechanical strength and durability.

The influence of BF content on the compressive strength and flexural strength of the geopolymers is depicted in Figure 6. In comparison to the composite without BF (FW5), the compressive strength of geopolymer composites with BF contents of 1.0%, 1.5%, 2.0%, and 2.5% decreased by 3.74%, 10.79%, 13.20%, and 17.98%, respectively. Despite the declining trend in compressive strength with increasing BF content, the strengths remain within the requisite range for construction purposes. The reduction in compressive strength may be linked to the defect density resulting from the presence of BF. Similar trends in compressive strength variations have been observed in prior studies concerning metakaolin-based geopolymers.26,30

Effect of BF on the strengths of geopolymers.

In contrast to the behavior observed in compressive strength, the flexural strength of geopolymers with BF exhibited an initial increase followed by a subsequent decrease, reaching its peak at a content of 2.0%. Specimens with BF contents of 1.0%, 1.5%, 2.0%, and 2.5% showed respective improvements in flexural strength of 7.49%, 10.30%, 22.48%, and 11.71% when compared to the control sample FW5. The flexural performance reflects the intricate interplay between BF and the geopolymer matrix. Throughout the degradation of reinforced composites, the fibers enhanced toughness, restrained crack development, and assumed the tensile stresses in the tensile zone.42 This mechanism facilitates a more effective stress transfer between the geopolymer matrix and the fibers, thereby improving the flexural strength.43

The study provides valuable insights into the effects of BF on the formulation and properties of FA-based geopolymer composites with WGP. Understanding these relationships is crucial for the development of sustainable construction materials that combine the environmental benefits of waste utilization with the performance requirements of modern building applications.

Effect of High Temperature

Thermal Characterization

The thermal characteristics of the three geopolymer samples (F100, FW5, and FW5B2) were examined through TG analysis conducted under a Nitrogen atmosphere. FW5B2 was chosen due to its superior flexural strength. As depicted in Figure 7, the TG and derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) curves were represented by solid and dotted lines, respectively. The TG curves reflected the continuous decrease in weight percentage during the heating process, and the curve of FW5 sample followed a similar pattern with the benchmark curve of pure geopolymer (F100), indicating that no relevant oxidation processes occurred in the samples of FW5 and F100. As expected, the FW5B2 sample showed a more significant mass loss than FW5 and F100 once the temperature surpassed 270 °C, followed by two steps that took place at high temperature, which was attributed to decomposition of the main chemical components of bamboo, similar to what has been detected in our previous study for other geopolymer containing bamboo.30

TG and DTG curves of the geopolymers.

All specimens experienced a multistage degradation during the heating process. Particularly, prior to 200 °C, rapid mass loss in all samples was attributed to the release of evaporable water within the geopolymer matrix.21,44 At 800 °C, both F100 and FW5 samples displayed identical weight retention rates of 89.17%, while the weight retention rate of FW5B2 was 85.67%. This discrepancy is related to the two distinct weight losses observed in FW5B2 after the temperature surpassed 270 °C, as indicated by the DTG curve. Thermal degradation of biomass components and residues in the FW5B2 sample occurred at approximately 290 and 547 °C, respectively.

Mass Loss and Thermal Shrinkage

The mass loss and thermal shrinkage of F100, FW5, and FW5B2 subjected to various temperatures were investigated, as shown in Figure 8. The mass of the cubic specimens was measured both before and after heating process, with the resultant average mass loss values graphically represented in Figure 8a. A clear increasing mass loss trend was observed as the temperature increased. A considerable mass loss occurred within the temperature range of 200 to 400 °C, attributable to the evaporation of absorbed and weakly bound water molecules. Beyond 400 °C, a marginal increase in mass loss was noted, a phenomenon ascribed to the near-total depletion of moisture content, aligning with findings from prior investigations.45,46 Additionally, a comparative analysis of the mass loss tracks for F100, FW5, and FW5B2 revealed no distinct divergences, suggesting a uniformity in their thermal response. The influence of WGP and BF on the mass loss was virtually negligible because it is difficult for them to limit the loss of moisture, consistent with earlier findings where moisture loss approximated mass loss.11

Mass loss (a) and thermal shrinkage (b) of the geopolymers.

As presented in Figure 8b, the thermal shrinkage of all the samples increased then decreased with the rising temperature, and the values of FW5 and FW5B2 were proximate to each other, and all of them were lower than that of F100. In the range of 200–600 °C, the shrinkage rate increased with the increase of temperature. This phenomenon can be attributed to the heightened evaporation of moisture at elevated temperatures, leading to pore closure and internal volume alterations of the materials. When the temperature exceeds 600 °C, the thermal shrinkage of all samples suddenly decreased. At 800 °C, the shrinkage rate of F100 was merely 0.85%, while FW5 and FW5B2 recorded values of −3.24% and −2.23%, respectively, which means they have undergone volume expansion. This inversion to volume expansion likely resulted from the phase transition reactions of WGP at such elevated temperatures, inducing internal stresses inside the matrix. Similar observations regarding the volumetric expansion of geopolymer composites postexposure to high temperatures have been reported by Tahwia et al.47

Postheating Mechanical Properties

Figure 9 illustrates the variations in compressive strength and the corresponding percentage changes for F100, FW5, and FW5B2 when exposed to different temperatures. From 25 to 200 °C, there was a notable increase in the residual compressive strengths for all samples, as depicted in Figure 9a. Specifically, enhancements of 5.15%, 8.46%, and 14.54% were achieved in the compressive strength of F100, FW5, and FW5B2, respectively, upon exposure to 200 °C, attributed to the dissolution and poly condensation of reactions of any unreacted precursors.48 Analogous phenomena were also documented in prior investigations.47,49,50 Subsequently, significant strength loss was observed from 400 to 800 °C, with the exception of FW5B2, which exhibited a minimal reduction of only 0.48% at 400 °C. This loss in strength at elevated temperatures has been documented by previous researchers,51,52 who noted a similar decline when materials were subjected to temperatures exceeding 200 °C. The decline in compressive strength at high temperatures is predominantly attributed to dehydration, an increase in pore size, the formation of cracks, and potential phase transitions within the geopolymer matrix, as reported by Zhang et al.46

Compressive strength (a) and percentage changes (b) of geopolymers subjected to high temperatures.

Within the range of high temperatures examined in this study, the compressive strength of FW5 consistently surpassed that of F100, suggesting that the incorporation of WGP could enhance the residual compressive strength of the FA-based geopolymer. Furthermore, the FW5B2 specimen demonstrated only minor deterioration in compressive strength compared to FW5. Notably, it retained strength slightly above that of F100 even after exposure to temperatures of 600 and 800 °C. This suggests that the introduction of BF not notably impact the residual strength of FW5, despite the known decomposition of BF at high temperatures.

Figure 10 depicts a comparative analysis of the flexural strength and its percentage change for F100, FW5, and FW5B2 when subjected to various temperatures. As shown in Figure 10a, the flexural strength of all three samples decreased with increasing temperature, and the residual flexural strength of FW5 and FW5B2 consistently remained superior to that of F100 throughout the entire temperature range. This observation suggests that the incorporation of appropriate amount of WGP and BF have no adverse effect on flexural strength of the geopolymer, even at high temperatures. Consistent with prior studies, the presence of fibers demonstrated negligible influence on the flexural strength of geopolymer when exposed to temperatures that exceed the decomposition threshold of the fibers.46,51

Thermo-Physical Appearance

Figure 11a presents the visual characteristics of the prisms following exposure to high temperatures, including changes in color, the development of cracks, and alterations in volume. As the temperature increased from 25 to 800 °C, the color of all the samples changed from dark gray to light gray, and then to slight brick red at 800 °C, although these color changes were not obvious. Notably, certain black carbonization layers became visible on the surface of the FW5B2 specimens after being subjected to 600 and 800 °C, attributable to the carbonization of BF at elevated temperatures.

Visual appearance of the prisms (a) and cubes (b) before and after exposure to different temperatures.

It is a well-established fact that concrete can develop cracks during a fire event, primarily due to the internal pressure exerted by evaporable water. A key factor contributing to this cracking is the low porosity of the material,51 which retards the migration of water vapor and provides fewer escape channels, consequently increasing internal pressures, as discussed by Khaliq and Kodur.53 In this investigation, macroscopic cracks are gradually begun to appear with the rising temperature. For the F100 sample, fine hairline cracks were observed at 400 and 600 °C, evolving into conspicuous surface fractures at 800 °C. The initial formation of these cracks can be attributed to localized changes in the microstructure, a result of normal thermal shrinkage and the dehydroxylation process of the geopolymer paste. As water is lost at higher temperatures, these hairline cracks extend deeper into the matrix. Consequently, more pronounced and wider cracks form, leading to a looser structure.

For the FW5 sample, a network of fine hairline cracks was observed at temperatures of 200 and 400 °C, and wider and denser cracks occurred on the surface at 600 °C. The observed cracking is likely a result of the vapor pressure effects, the reduced porosity due to the incorporation of WGP, and the differential in thermal expansion between the WGP and the geopolymer paste. However, in comparison to the F100 sample, the FW5 sample exhibited finer and less pronounced surface cracks after exposure to 800 °C, possibly attributed to the phase transition of WGP from 600 to 800 °C. The melting of WGP could potentially fill the pore system of the matrix, thereby enhancing the structural integrity of the geopolymer.24

For the FW5B2 sample, only minor hairline cracks were observed at 400 and 600 °C, suggesting that the incorporation of BF aids in mitigating the cracking of the FW5 matrix caused by high temperatures. The decomposition of BF within this temperature range creates small voids and channels inside the geopolymer matrix, increasing the porosity of the composites and facilitating the escape of water vapor without substantial damage to the microstructure.31 Nonetheless, more obvious and denser cracks appeared at 800 °C, and its structure became noticeably looser. The reason for this phenomenon may result from the volumetric expansion following dehydration, which accounts for the significant loss of strength at 800 °C, as depicted in Figures 9 and 10.

Observations regarding the changes in volume of the prisms with increasing exposure temperature have been documented. Within the temperature range of 200 to 600 °C, there is a gradual reduction in volume, likely attributable to the collapse of pores induced by the evaporation of bound water. At 800 °C, the volumes of all three samples experienced a minor increase due to the thermal expansion effect. This expansion could be attributed to the formation of new crystalline phases and alterations in the pore volume within the matrix, influenced by the presence of WGP and BF, or potentially owing to the alkali-silica reaction between the alkali components in the geopolymer paste and the silica in the WGP. Compared with prisms, no significant volume change was detected in the cubic specimens (Figure 11b) which may be related to the smaller size of the cubes. This is consistent with the results of most cubic geopolymer samples exposed to high temperatures.31,48,49,52

Microstructure Analysis

To investigate alterations in the microstructure of composites at the mesoscale, optical microscopy observations were performed, with the findings presented in Figure 12. At room temperature, the internal structure of F100 sample is uniform with a few of pores dispersed. However, cracks and more pores emerge progressively within the matrix as the temperature increases. Notably, some light-colored WGP distribute in the dense matrix of FW5 sample. As the temperature increases, the WGP components gradually melt and seep into the matrix. This infiltration not only repairs the microstructural defects but also contributes to the enhancement of the overall mechanical strength. In the FW5B2 sample, the observation focus is the distribution and variation of BF, which can be seen to be uniformly dispersed and well bonded with the matrix. When temperature increases, the BF gradually carbonizes and decomposes, leaving behind specially shaped pores.

Optical micrographs of the fracture surface of F100, FW5, and FW5B2 specimens after the three-point flexural strength test.

The FW5 and FW5B2 samples display fewer and smaller cracks, thereby demonstrating superior mechanical performance compared to the F100 sample. This suggests that the addition of WGP and BF is beneficial for the high-temperature resistance of the composites. The reason can be attributed to several factors: at elevated temperatures, WGP may undergo a phase transition that assists in filling the cracks, while the fiber-shaped traces left by BF degradation create channels that facilitate the escape of water vapor.

Figure 13 displays the SEM micrographs of F100, FW5, and FW5B2 specimens at ambient temperature and subsequent exposure to 600 and 800 °C. It is evident that the microstructure of F100 at room temperature contains a few quantities of unreacted FA balls and a distributed amount of voids. When FA is partially replaced by 5% WGP, more reactive silica participates and promotes the geopolymerization process, resulting in a denser microstructure and fewer voids in the FW5 sample. The FW5B2 sample exhibits a dense structure similar to that of FW5, with some voids introduced into the matrix by the addition of bamboo fibers. It is important to highlight that the SEM magnification levels for the FW5B2 sample after exposure to temperatures of 25 and 600 °C are intentionally distinct from those used for the F100 and FW5 samples. This adjustment in magnification serves to display more bamboo fibers and the distinctive traces left by the carbonization process of these fibers.

SEM images of the fracture surfaces of F100, FW5, and FW5B2 specimens after the three-point flexural strength test.

At 600 °C, the microstructure of the F100 sample remains predominantly dense with some cracks. When compared to the F100, the FW5 sample had better homogeneity, with many reacted and partially reacted glass particles dispersed in the matrix, as well as some small pores. The FW5B2 sample exhibits distinct shape traces from the thermal degradation of BF, which not only create pathways for the escape of water vapor but also suggest that the matrix helps to prevent or reduce the oxygen supply to the interior of the composites. However, as the specimens are further heated, many voids and honeycomb-shaped caverns form in the matrix, probably resulting from volumetric expansion, the release of water vapor, and the disintegration of gel phases.54 At 800 °C, the F100 sample has larger pores with a broken microstructure compared to the samples of FW5 and FW5B2, resulting in worse mechanical performance, which is consistent with the observed reduction in compressive and flexural strengths as depicted in Figures 9 and 10.

X-ray Diffraction Analysis

Figure 14 presents the XRD patterns for the F100, FW5, and FW5B2 samples at the ages of 28 days under ambient and elevated temperatures. For the sake of brevity, the XRD plots for samples heated at 200 and 400 °C have been omitted, as they exhibit a high degree of similarity to those at 600 and 25 °C. At ambient temperature (25 °C), the F100 sample displayed a broad hump at 2θ = 20° ∼ 40°, indicative of the presence of major crystalline phases such as quartz, mullite, and hematite, which are commonly identified in FA-based geopolymers.8,55 The FW5 and FW5B2 samples exhibited similar phase peaks to the F100, which can be attributed to the amorphous nature of the WGP within their structure. Upon exposure to 600 °C, the XRD patterns of the three samples remained nearly indistinguishable. This observation suggests that no new crystalline phase was precipitated within the matrix at this temperature. When the temperature was further increased to 800 °C, nepheline diffraction peaks emerged in all samples, contributing to microstructural weakness and strength loss. Furthermore, the presence of sillimanite peaks in the crystalline phase was evident in FW5 and FW5B2 samples, suggesting WGP involvement in geopolymerization.24 Importantly, throughout the entire process, the presence of BF exhibited no influence on the crystalline structure of the geopolymers, consistent with a finding from our previous research.31

Conclusions

This research investigated the feasibility of utilizing WGP and BF in FA-based geopolymers by analyzing their impacts on microstructure and physical properties. Based on the experimental findings, the following conclusions can be made:

1.At ambient conditions, the addition of WGP resulted in lowered flowability, reduced setting time and water absorption, and concurrently increased density of the geopolymer composites. An increase in WGP content from 2.5% to 10% had a slight effect on the compressive strength and an initial increase followed by a decrease on the flexural strength. Optimally, substituting 5% of FA with WGP was seen to provide the optimal compressive and flexural strengths.

2.For FA-based geopolymers containing 5% WGP, the addition of BF caused decreased fluidity, reduced setting time and density, and increased water absorption. With increasing content of BF from 1% to 2.5%, the compressive strength experienced a gradual decline, while the flexural strength first increased and then decreased. In this particular matrix, BF content of 2% was found to be optimal.

For FA-based geopolymers containing 5% WGP, the addition of BF caused decreased fluidity, reduced setting time and density, and increased water absorption. With increasing content of BF from 1% to 2.5%, the compressive strength experienced a gradual decline, while the flexural strength first increased and then decreased. In this particular matrix, BF content of 2% was found to be optimal.

3.Upon exposure to high temperatures, widened microcracks, larger voids, and increased crystal content were observed. The geopolymer matrix showed surface cracking, internal damage, and decreased strength due to mass loss, structural decomposition, phase transitions, and pore pressure effects. Nevertheless, all sample’s compressive strengths increased following heating to 200 °C, which thanks to the intensified geopolymerization reaction during heating process.

4.The superior residual compressive and flexural strengths, along with the more intact structural integrity of the geopolymer composites compared to the pure geopolymer, were ascribed to the pore-filling effect of WGP and the channeling effect of BF. This indicated that WGP and BF could serve as viable additives to enhance the fire resistance of FA-based geopolymer structures.
