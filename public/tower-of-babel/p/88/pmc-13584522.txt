CD44 expression associates with EBV LMP1, reduced CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, and lower PD-L1 combined positive score in nasopharyngeal carcinoma
Authors: Amani Sammoud, Nehla Mokni Baizig, Salma Kamoun, Alia Methnani, Yosr Zenzri, Alia Mousli, Maha Driss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is an Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)-associated malignancy characterized by a highly immune-infiltrated yet clinically heterogeneous tumor microenvironment. Iron metabolism has been proposed as a potential determinant of tumor progression and immune regulation. CD44 and transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) represent two distinct iron uptake pathways, but their relative contribution to EBV-driven NPC and immune modulation remains unclear.MethodsSixty-eight NPC biopsies from a Tunisian cohort were analyzed by immunohistochemistry for CD44, TfR1, EBV LMP1, PD-L1, CD8, FoxP3, and CD163. Associations with clinicopathological parameters and immune infiltrates were assessed using chi-square and Spearman correlation tests.ResultsHigh CD44 expression was observed in 48.5% of cases and was significantly associated with lymph node involvement (p = 0.012) and TNM stage (p = 0.042). CD44 expression positively correlated with LMP1 (r = 0.463, p = 0.001). In contrast, CD44 was inversely correlated with CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration (r = − 0.283, p = 0.019) and PD-L1 combined positive score (CPS) (r = − 0.247, p = 0.042). A significant positive correlation was observed between LMP1 and PD-L1 expression, both for tumor proportion score (TPS) (r = 0.341, p = 0.003) and CPS (r = 0.345, p = 0.002). In contrast, TfR1 expression was associated with advanced tumor stage (p = 0.022) but showed no significant relationship with immune parameters or LMP1. CD44 and TfR1 expression were inversely correlated (r = − 0.250, p = 0.040), suggesting alternative iron acquisition pathways.ConclusionOur findings suggest that CD44 may act as a key regulator linking iron metabolism and immune modulation in EBV-associated NPC. Although direct iron measurements were not performed, the observed associations with reduced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration are consistent with a potential role of CD44 in shaping an immune-restricted tumor microenvironment. These results provide integrative evidence of a CD44–iron–immune axis in a Tunisian NPC cohort and highlight CD44 as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13027-026-00776-1.

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a head and neck malignant tumor that develops from the epithelial cells of the nasopharynx. NPC displays a distinct geographic distribution, with a particularly high incidence in Southeast Asia and North Africa [1]. Tunisia is considered one of the top ten endemic countries for NPC, based on age-standardized incidence rates [2]. Due to its deep anatomical location and frequently misleading clinical symptoms, diagnosis is ordinarily made at an advanced stage. This, in turn, contributes to aggressive tumor progression, a high risk of metastasis and a high mortality rate, which seriously endangers public health. The pathogenesis of NPC is complicated and multifactorial, involving genetic, environmental and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection. Histologically, the most common subtype in endemic regions is undifferentiated non-keratinizing carcinoma (UCNT), which is strongly associated with EBV infection [3]. This cancer is characterized by a highly immune-infiltrated tumor microenvironment and relatively high radiosensitivity. Despite therapeutic advances, prognosis remains variable, particularly in advanced stages, and reliable prognostic biomarkers are still needed.

Iron metabolism is an essential metabolic pathway for tumor cells, which exploit the increased iron supply to support their proliferation, survival and resistance to oxidative stress [4]. Transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) plays a central role in iron endocytosis. Its expression is increased in many cancers and is often correlated with an aggressive phenotype and poor prognosis [5–7]. In parallel, CD44, a transmembrane glycoprotein involved in cell adhesion and signaling, has been extensively investigated in oncology. CD44 exists as a standard isoform (CD44s) and multiple variant isoforms (CD44v) generated by alternative splicing. As the principal receptor for hyaluronic acid, CD44 is involved in tumor progression processes such as proliferation, invasion, metastasis, treatment resistance, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) [8–10]. Recent evidence suggests that CD44 may also participate in iron uptake through hyaluronic acid (HA) dependent internalization mechanisms, indicating a possible link between CD44 and tumor iron handling [11]. This pathway could act as an alternative or complementary mechanism to TfR1-mediated endocytosis.

EBV infection plays a central role in NPC pathogenesis. Among its latent proteins, Latent Membrane Protein 1 (LMP1) is a major oncogenic driver that activates multiple signaling pathways involved in proliferation, survival, and immune modulation [12]. LMP1 has also been implicated in immune escape mechanisms within the tumor microenvironment. The existence of a potential link between TfR1 and CD44, and LMP1 expression could reflect a synergy between iron metabolism and viral oncogenesis.

The tumor immune microenvironment plays a crucial role in NPC progression. CD8⁺ T cells are associated with antitumor cytotoxic activity and favorable outcomes [13], whereas tumor-associated macrophages with an M2 phenotype (CD163⁺) and regulatory T cells (FoxP3⁺) contribute to immunosuppression and tumor progression [14, 15]. The balance between these immune populations reflects the state of the tumour microenvironment and may determine the response to treatments, particularly immunotherapy. However, the relationship between iron-related pathways (TfR1 and CD44), EBV oncogenic signaling, and immune infiltration remains incompletely understood in NPC.

This study aims to comparatively evaluate the clinical and biological impact of two major iron endocytosis markers, TfR1 and CD44, in NPC within a North African population. Specifically we investigated their associations with clinicopathological characteristics, their potential involvement in EBV-driven oncogenesis, and their impact on the tumor immune microenvironment, including CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, regulatory T cells, M2 macrophages, and the expression of the immune checkpoint molecule programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1). Through this integrative approach, we sought to better explore the interplay between iron metabolism, immune modulation, and tumor aggressiveness, thereby providing new insights into potential prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Materials and methods

Study population and selection criteria

This retrospective study analyzed 68 biopsies from patients diagnosed with UCNT. The samples, collected and archived in the Immuno-Histo-Cytology Department of the institute between 2017 and 2023, were selected based on strict inclusion criteria, including histologically confirmed UCNT diagnosis and the availability of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) biopsy material. Exclusion criteria comprised cases with unusable tissue blocks, insufficient material for immunohistochemical analysis, missing clinical data, or loss of tumor tissue during processing. Clinical variables, including age at diagnosis, primary tumor size (T), lymph node involvement (N), metastatic status (M), overall TNM stage (AJCC 8th edition), and treatment response, were systematically retrieved from patient medical records.

This retrospective study was conducted on archived biopsy specimens; therefore, the requirement for informed patient consent was waived. The study protocol adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Salah Azaiez Institute (Ref: ISA/2025/18).

Immunohistochemistry analysis

An immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis was performed on 68 FFPE tumor samples to evaluate the expression of TfR1, CD44, LMP1, FoxP3, CD163, CD8, and PD-L1 using the Novolink™ Polymer Detection System (Leica Biosystems, USA). Tissue sections were deparaffinized in toluene, rehydrated through graded ethanol concentrations (100%, 95%, and 80%; 10 min each), and rinsed in distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the slides at 98 °C for 30 min in Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 6 for CD44, CD163, PD-L1, and FoxP3; pH 9 for TfR1, CD8, and LMP1). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using Novocastra™ Peroxidase Block, followed by washes with Tris-buffered saline (TBS, pH 7.4).

Tissue sections were circled using a DakoPen to retain reagents and then incubated with primary antibodies under specific conditions: CD163 and CD8 (30 min at room temperature), and TfR1, CD44, FoxP3, PD-L1 and LMP1 (60 min at room temperature). After TBS washes, slides were sequentially incubated with Post Primary Block and NovoLink Polymer for 30 min each. Antigen–antibody complexes were visualized using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate (10 min), followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin (5 min), dehydration through ascending ethanol concentrations (80%, 95%, and 100%; 5 min each), clearing in toluene, and mounting with Eukitt.

The primary antibodies used were: TfR1 (clone EPR20584, Abcam; dilution 1:400), CD44 (clone IHC144, GenomeMe Lab; dilution 1:100), CD163 (clone 10D6, Leica Biosystems; dilution 1:200), CD8 (clone 4B11, Leica Biosystems; dilution 1:50), FoxP3 (clone 236 A/E7, Abcam; dilution 1:100), PD-L1 (clone 73 − 10, Leica Biosystems ; pre-diluted) and LMP1 (clone CS1-4, Cell Marque; pre-diluted).

Evaluation of immunostaining

Evaluation of immunostaining expression was independently performed by two pathologists blinded to clinical data, using hematoxylin-eosin (HE)-stained sections as morphological reference. Slides were first examined at low magnification to identify representative tumor areas, then evaluated at ×400 magnification. Positive cells were counted in five high-power fields, and the percentage of labeled cells was calculated as follows : (average number of positive cells / total number of cells) × 100.

Sections showing technical artifacts or tissue detachment were excluded from the analysis. Immunolabeling intensity and distribution were scored according to standardized criteria to ensure reproducibility and inter-observer concordance.

Scoring methods were adapted to each biomarker. For CD44, a combined score was calculated based on staining intensity (0 = absent, 1 = weak, 2 = moderate, 3 = strong) and the percentage of positive tumor cells (0 = 0%, 1 = 1–25%, 2 = 26–50%, 3 = 51–75%, 4 = 76–100%). The immunostaining score was then categorized based on the proportion of stained tumor cells: 0–10% as negative (−), 11–25% as slightly positive (+), 26–50% as moderately positive (++), and 51–100% as strongly positive (+++). Patients with (+) or (++) scores were considered to have low expression, while those with (+++) scores were categorized as high expression. The TfR1 score was obtained by multiplying the staining intensity (0 = absent, 1 = weak, 2 = strong, 3 = very strong) by the percentage of positive cells, categorized into five intervals: <1% = 0, 1–25% = 1, 26–50% = 2, 51–80% = 3, and > 80% = 4. The final score was interpreted as follows: low expression (0–3), moderate expression (4–7), and high expression (> 7). For PD-L1, expression was assessed using two scoring methods : the tumor proportion score (TPS) and the combined positive score (CPS). The TPS was defined as the percentage of viable tumor cells showing partial or complete membrane staining relative to all viable tumor cells in the sample. A TPS was considered positive if staining was detected in at least 1% of viable tumor cells.

The CPS was calculated as the number of PD-L1–positive cells (including tumor cells, lymphocytes, and macrophages) divided by the total number of viable tumor cells on the slide, multiplied by 100. A CPS was considered negative if < 1 and positive if ≥ 1.

These criteria enabled a standardized and comparative analysis across all biomarkers, with exclusion of compromised samples.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 23.0. Associations between tested markers and clinicopathological parameters were evaluated using Pearson’s chi-square (χ²) test for categorical variables and one-way ANOVA for continuous variables. Correlations between tissue markers (TfR1, CD44, CD163, CD8, LMP1, PD-L1 and FoxP3) were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (rho). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with a 95% confidence interval.

With regard to tumor stage (T), 58.82% of patients were classified as having large tumors (stages T3 and T4) at first consultation, while advanced lymph node involvement (stages N2 and N3) was observed in 61.77% of cases. Distant metastases (M1) were present in 16.18% of patients. According to the eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM staging system, 58 patients (85.3%) were classified as having stage III–IV disease.

Treatment response was evaluated in 35 patients, three months after therapy, which consisted of primary chemotherapy followed by concomitant chemo-radiotherapy. A complete response was observed in 18 patients (51.43%), while 17 patients (48.57%) achieved a partial response.

TfR1 expression and its association with clinicopathological features

Typical TfR1 staining in NPC tissues is shown in Fig. 1. TfR1 expression was low in 16.18%, moderate in 32.35%, and high in 51.47% of NPC samples. As presented in Table 2, TfR1 expression was not significantly associated with sex (χ² = 1.042 ; p = 0.594) or age (χ² = 3.127 ; p = 0.209). In contrast, a significant correlation was observed between TfR1 expression and tumor T stage, with advanced tumors (T3–T4) occurring more frequently in patients exhibiting high TfR1 expression compared with those showing low or moderate expression (74.29%; χ² = 7.181, p = 0.028). No statistically significant associations were identified between TfR1 expression and lymph node involvement (χ² = 3.725, p = 0.155) or distant metastasis (χ² = 0.889, p = 0.641). Notably, analysis of the overall TNM stage revealed a significant association with TfR1 expression (χ² = 7.668, p = 0.022), as high TfR1 levels were predominantly detected in patients with advanced-stage disease (TNM III–IV). Finally, among the 35 patients evaluable for treatment response, TfR1 expression was not significantly associated with therapeutic outcome (χ² = 0.283, p = 0.868).

Statistical analysis was performed using the Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests, with significance set at p < 0.05. CD44 expression was significantly correlated with lymph node involvement (p = 0.012) and TNM stage (p = 0.042). No significant associations were observed with sex (p = 0.782), age (p = 1), tumor T stage (p = 0.623), presence of distant metastases (M) (p = 0.514), or treatment response (p = 0.740).

Correlation of CD44 and TfR1 expression in NPC

The correlation between CD44 and TfR1 expression was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation test. A significant negative correlation was observed between TfR1 and CD44 expression (Rho = − 0.250, p = 0.04) (Fig. 3), indicating that higher TfR1 expression was associated with lower CD44 expression in NPC tissues.

Correlation of TfR1 and CD44 with tumor-infiltrating immune cells (CD8 + T cells, FoxP3 + tregs, and CD163 + M2 macrophages)

To investigate the relationship between tumor-infiltrating immune cells and the expression of CD44 and TfR1 in tumor cells, immunohistochemical analysis was performed to quantify the infiltration of CD8⁺ T lymphocytes, FoxP3⁺ regulatory T cells, and M2-polarized macrophages within the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 4).

Correlations between iron endocytosis markers (CD44, TfR1) and immune cells were analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation test. A significant negative correlation was observed between CD44 expression and tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T lymphocytes (Rho = − 0.283, p = 0.019), indicating that higher CD44 expression was associated with reduced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration. In contrast, TfR1 expression showed no significant correlation with CD8⁺ T cells (Rho = − 0.086, p = 0.484). No statistically significant associations were observed between the expression of CD44 or TfR1 and regulatory T cells (FoxP3⁺) (Rho = − 0.096, p = 0.474 and Rho = 0.039, p = 0.771, respectively). Similarly, neither CD44 nor TfR1 expression was significantly correlated with M2 macrophage (CD163⁺) infiltration (Rho = − 0.032, p = 0.803 and Rho = − 0.025, p = 0.848, respectively) (Table 4).

Conversely, LMP1 expression demonstrated a significant positive correlation with PD-L1, both for TPS (r = 0.341, p = 0.003) and CPS (r = 0.345, p = 0.002) (Table 5).

Discussion

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma remains a complex and heterogeneous disease, with early diagnosis continuing to pose a major challenge. In this context, the identification of reliable biomarkers is of particular importance. CD44, a transmembrane glycoprotein involved in cell adhesion and signaling, has been extensively investigated for its role in tumor progression and modulation of the tumor microenvironment across various malignancies. Certain alternatively spliced variants of CD44 are among the most common surface markers of cancer stem cells (CSCs), contributing to the maintenance of their phenotype and promoting metastasis. The CD44–HA interaction has been reported to induce EMT, thereby enhancing tumor invasiveness and dissemination. In addition, CD44 has recently been implicated in iron uptake processes, particularly through HA–dependent endocytosis, suggesting a potential link between iron metabolism and tumor plasticity [11]. However, in NPC, the respective roles of CD44 and TfR1, as well as their interactions with EBV-driven oncogenic pathways and the tumor immune microenvironment, remain incompletely understood.

Accordingly, our study aimed to evaluate CD44 expression in NPC, compare it with TfR1 expression, and analyze their associations with clinicopathological parameters, EBV LMP1 expression, and immune microenvironment features in a Tunisian cohort.

Our results showed high CD44 expression in 48.53% of patients, with a significant association between CD44 expression and lymph node involvement and TNM stage. Interestingly, high CD44 expression was more frequently observed in early-stage tumors and cases with limited nodal involvement. These findings suggest a tumor-associated expression pattern of CD44 compared with adjacent non-tumoral tissue. Although many studies report a positive correlation between high CD44 expression, advanced TNM stage, and poor prognosis across various cancer types [16–20], some reports describe a relative decrease in CD44 expression at advanced stages of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, esophageal cancer, colorectal cancer, and breast cancer compared with early-stage disease [19, 21, 22]. These discrepancies may reflect tumor-type specificity, intratumoral heterogeneity, and differences in antibody specificity or detection of CD44 isoforms. Most studies employ antibodies targeting the extracellular domain of CD44; thus, apparent decreases in signal intensity may reflect cleavage and shedding of the extracellular domain rather than reduced protein expression, a process frequently observed in tumors and implicated in tumorigenesis and cell migration [23].

To further investigate the relationship between CD44 and iron metabolism, we analyzed its association with TfR1, the major receptor mediating transferrin-dependent iron uptake. A significant negative correlation was observed between CD44 and TfR1 expression. This finding may suggest that distinct or partially compensatory iron uptake pathways coexist in NPC tumor cells. CD44-mediated endocytosis of the HA–iron complex has been shown to facilitate intracellular iron uptake, particularly in cancer cells [11, 24]. During EMT, increased iron demand may be associated with a preferential use of CD44-related uptake pathways over the classical TfR1 route, which could contribute to self-renewal, metabolic plasticity, and resistance to oxidative stress. Previous studies have reported that elevated intracellular iron can inhibit TfR1 membrane expression and may induce transcription of EMT-related genes including CD44 [11]. This iron-dependent epigenetic regulation may contribute to the maintenance of CSC properties and survival under iron-limited conditions, supporting a potential role of CD44 in tumor iron metabolism.

Given that EBV infection represents the principal etiological factor in NPC, we further explored the role of LMP1, the major EBV-encoded oncogenic protein, in relation to tumor stemness and immune escape markers. LMP1 acts as a constitutively active receptor-like molecule capable of activating several oncogenic pathways, including NF-κB, JAK/STAT, PI3K/Akt, and MAPK signaling, thereby promoting cell proliferation, survival, invasion, and immune evasion. In the present study, LMP1 expression was positively correlated with both PD-L1 and CD44 expression.

The observed association between LMP1 and PD-L1 is consistent with previous studies showing that LMP1 can induce PD-L1 expression through NF-κB-, STAT3-, and AP-1-dependent pathways [25, 26]. This mechanism may contribute to immune evasion by inhibiting cytotoxic T-cell responses in the tumor microenvironment. In parallel, the positive correlation observed between LMP1 and CD44 supports previous studies suggesting that LMP1 promotes cancer stem cell–like phenotypes in NPC, including enhanced self-renewal capacity, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, invasion, and resistance to therapy [27, 28].

Taken together, these correlation findings suggest that LMP1 expression may be associated with a biological profile linking CD44 expression and PD-L1 positivity in NPC. This pattern may reflect an interplay between EBV-related oncogenic activity, stemness-associated features, and immune evasion. Although no causal relationship can be inferred from the present study, the coexistence of these markers may be associated with a more aggressive tumor phenotype and potential resistance to conventional treatment.

Tumor cells can exploit tumor-infiltrating immune cells to meet their iron demands, thereby impairing immune surveillance [29, 30]. In our cohort, CD44 expression negatively correlated with intra-tumoral CD8⁺ T-cell density. This finding may be consistent with iron sequestration by tumor cells, which could contribute to reduced TIL proliferation and activation, in line with previous evidence that iron deficiency impairs CD8⁺ T-cell function [31–33]. Concordantly, CD44 expression was also inversely associated with PD-L1 measured by CPS. This observation may reflect an alternative immune evasion pattern, whereby tumors with high CD44 expression are associated with lower CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and potentially reduced IFN-γ signaling, resulting in less adaptive induction of PD-L1. Given that CPS incorporates PD-L1 expression on both tumor and immune cells, its reduction in CD44-high tumors may be consistent with an immunologically “cold” microenvironment potentially associated with metabolic and stem-like features. Collectively, these findings suggest that tumors with high CD44 expression may evade immune surveillance through mechanisms potentially involving metabolic reprogramming and immune exclusion rather than predominant PD-L1 overexpression alone. Further studies integrating intratumoral iron quantification, IFN-γ profiling, and spatial mapping of CD44-high/PD-L1-positive tumor cells and TILs are warranted to investigate these hypotheses and clarify the underlying mechanisms.

From a therapeutic perspective, tumors with a CD44-high/PD-L1-low phenotype could potentially show reduced responsiveness to anti-PD-1 therapies. However, they may represent candidates for future studies evaluating strategies targeting iron metabolism, strategies aimed at modulating local iron availability to potentially restore TIL activity, or directly inhibiting CD44 to limit tumor proliferation.

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) play a key role in iron metabolism and immune regulation. They control iron import, export, and storage, and their polarization affects cancer prognosis [34, 35]. Tumor cells can modulate macrophage polarization according to iron requirements, converting pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages (which sequester iron) into anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages (which recycle iron) to promote tumor progression [36–38]. In our study, infiltration of CD163⁺ M2 macrophages was assessed ; however, no correlation with TfR1 or CD44 expression was observed. Nevertheless, previous reports in other cancers suggest a positive correlation between TAM infiltration and CD44 expression [10], and high TfR1 expression has been associated with increased infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells and macrophages [39].

This study has several limitations. The relatively small size of the NPC cohort may limit the generalizability of the findings. The analyses were primarily correlational, lacking direct functional validation, and the role of CD44 in tumor iron endocytosis and in establishing an immunologically “cold” microenvironment remains hypothetical. The absence of measurements of intracellular iron levels or PD-L1–related signaling pathway activation further limits the interpretation of our results. In addition, immunohistochemistry is subject to methodological variability and non-standardized positivity thresholds. Future studies using multi-omics approaches and functional experiments, both in vitro and in vivo, are needed to investigate these mechanisms further and evaluate the therapeutic potential of CD44 in NPC.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study suggests that CD44 may have a relevant clinical and biological role in NPC, potentially exceeding that of TfR1 in the studied cohort. CD44 expression was associated with LMP1 positivity, nodal status, and reduced immune infiltration, suggesting a potential link with a less inflamed tumor immune microenvironment. These findings support CD44 as a potential prognostic biomarker in NPC. However, further functional studies are required to clarify its mechanistic role and therapeutic relevance.
