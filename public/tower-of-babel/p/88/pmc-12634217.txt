Bridging the gap: addressing NACC's evolving cognition batteries across UDS versions
Authors: Michael L Lee, Seo‐Eun Choi, Shubhabrata Mukherjee, Phoebe Scollard, Connie Nakano, Laura E Gibbons, Jessica Culhane, Kathryn Gauthreaux, Kwun C G Chan, Sarah Biber, Kari Stephens, Walter Kukull, Emily Trittschuh, Jesse Mez, Andrew J Saykin, Shannon Turner, Derek Archer, Alaina Durant, Logan Dumitrescu, Timothy Hohman, Paul K Crane

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe Uniform Data Set (UDS) version 3 neuropsychological battery replaced old tests used in UDS 1&2. BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers (ADRCs) collect standardized data and report them centrally to the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC). Cognitive data are collected using several versions of the Uniform Data Set (UDS), which have evolved to reflect changing measurement priorities and reduce intellectual property entanglements. Longitudinal ADRC participants may have cognitive assessments with different UDS versions. UDS versions 1 and 2 have nearly identical test batteries; however, many tests were replaced with the introduction of UDS version 3 in 2015. The changes made between UDS 2 and 3 make investigations of cognitive changes over time either impossible or dependent on ad hoc scoring rules based on assumptions such as test similarity, re‐test effects, and the generalizability of data generated from the sample chosen for classical test theory‐based equating efforts. 1 Similarly, analyses of cross‐sectional relationships between cognition and other factors, such as imaging findings or genetic variants from data across the UDS 1&2 and UDS 3 eras, would face similar challenges. A strategy is necessary to bridge the gap between the UDS 1&2 and UDS 3 eras.

In 2015, NACC conducted a crosswalk study on 955 participants to assess the correlation of UDS 2 and 3 neuropsychological tests. This crosswalk for specific pairs of tests – the UDS 2 test and the UDS 3 test that replaced it – was developed using equipercentile equating, 2 which has been used in multiple studies that spanned the UDS 2–UDS 3 transition. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 The equipercentile‐equating approach transforms a score for one test to an estimated score for a different test using distributions from a specific sample who were administered both tests (i.e., crosswalk sample). This approach is limited by the characteristics of the particular sample. For example, if the sample had few participants with late‐stage dementia, it may be problematic to use the resulting equipercentile‐derived crosswalks for other participants with late‐stage dementia. Similarly, equipercentile approaches are most appropriate for individual pairs of tests with the same or close to the same number of test points. If the number of points is not similar within a pair of tests, one of the tests will necessarily be faced with a many‐to‐one mapping situation, and vice versa. Equipercentile approaches are most useful for scientific questions about a particular test. In many situations, investigators may be interested in a particular test only as an indicator of a broader construct, such as functioning on a cognitive domain. A single test may have idiosyncratic characteristics and may only partially reflect the cognitive domain, an issue that can be minimized by using composite scores that optimize measurement precision by incorporating multiple indicators of the domain. 10 Finally, while equipercentile approaches may be appropriate for analyses of data from the equated test pair, investigators may wish to consider data across multiple studies, where not every study may have administered those tests. In these settings, broader approaches, such as the modern psychometric‐informed domain score approach we have adopted for the Alzheimer's Disease Sequencing Project Phenotype Harmonization Consortium (the ADSP‐PHC), can facilitate readily accessible analyses of data across multiple studies across distinct cognitive batteries. 11

In this paper, we demonstrate the use of co‐calibrated scores in data collected “across the gap” of the UDS 1&2 and UDS 3 eras. We consider data from the crosswalk sample where the distinct batteries were concurrently administered to 955 individuals. We compare correlations between individual pairs of tests using the equipercentile approach and between domain scores derived from non‐overlapping UDS 1&2 and UDS 3 items for each domain. We then demonstrate the validity of the co‐calibrated memory, executive functioning, and language domain scores by evaluating associations between domain scores and imaging and APOE genotype data. These analyses leverage the extensive data collected by NACC over many years, on either side of the shift between UDS 1&2 and UDS 3 and demonstrate the use of co‐calibrated scores for cognitive domain constructs independent of sample constraints for NACC investigators.

METHODS

Overview

We used data available from NACC through May 2024 covering the UDS 1&2 and UDS 3 eras. With data collected from the 2015 NACC crosswalk study, we used NACC's equipercentile‐equated tables to generate estimated equivalent UDS 1&2 test scores from the UDS 3 era tests. 12 We also used modern psychometric approaches to generate domain scores from non‐overlapping UDS 1&2 and UDS 3 cognitive items. We compared correlations of individual pairs of equipercentile‐equated test scores alongside harmonized and co‐calibrated cognitive domain scores.

We then performed analyses considering cognitive data together with imaging (linear regression) and APOE genotype data (two‐sample t test). We defined a priori several regions of interest (ROIs) for memory (as in Crane et al. 13 ), executive functioning (as in Gibbons et al. 14 ), and language (as in Choi et al. 15 ) and determined correlations of co‐calibrated cognitive domain scores with the volumes and cortical thicknesses of these ROIs. Linear regression analyses were performed, stratified by diagnostic group (e.g., normal cognition [NC], mild cognitive impairment [MCI], and Alzheimer's disease [AD] dementia), and we also evaluated the same analyses over the entire sample. In addition, we assessed associations between co‐calibrated cognitive domain scores and any APOE ε4 allele.

Finally, we compared co‐calibrated domain scores for NACC participants at their enrollment visit by diagnostic group. We assigned participants based on the UDS version from their enrollment and their last visit: both visits in UDS 1&2, first visit in UDS 1&2 and last visit in UDS 3, and both visits in UDS 3. To compare modern psychometric approaches with traditional methods, we created global cognitive z‐scores (details in “Global cognitive z‐scores”).

Cognitive data methods

We published detailed methods for our entire workflow in a special issue of Neuropsychology devoted to harmonization for cognitive tests. 11 Here we outline these steps briefly, highlighting issues of particular importance for analyses of data from NACC.

Based on content, an expert panel assigned each item or test to a single domain – memory, executive functioning, or language – or none of these. Co‐calibration entails identifying common items (anchors) across studies and applying psychometric models to statistically link scales to be on the same metric. This ensures that a score of 1 standard deviation (SD) in a cognitive domain means the same thing across different cohorts and test batteries. To date, the ADSP‐PHC 12 has co‐calibrated all of the cognitive tests included in the UDS cognitive batteries (Appendix List 1).

We applied item parameters derived for specific items from our test bank. We reviewed extensive amounts of materials from each study to ensure we understood the context in which each item was administered. For each new study, for items identical to items we estimated in previous studies, we used our previously generated item parameters. If they were not identical, we treated the new item as a distinct item and generated new item parameters for it. Notably, no items were dropped with these procedures, optimizing the number of indicators per cognitive domain. We then used confirmatory factor analysis models to generate co‐calibrated domain scores. Details of items for each domain for the NACC data are shown in the Appendix list 2–7 (A2‐A7), and all code is available from the authors upon request.

Crosswalk study and equipercentile estimated scores

We considered the crosswalk dataset in which a sample was administered cognitive items from the UDS 1&2 and UDS 3 batteries. Equipercentile methods were applied for specific pairs of tests as previously published. 12 We used tables from that publication to generate estimated UDS 1&2 test scores (e.g., Logical Memory Immediate) from the UDS 3 test score (e.g., Craft Story Immediate). We determined correlations between observed UDS 1&2 test scores and those estimated from the crosswalk from the UDS 3 test scores.

For this crosswalk sample, we also generated cognitive domain scores with no overlapping items across UDS eras (Appendix). Trail Making Test Parts A and B (TMT‐A and TMT‐B and Category Fluency Animals and Vegetables were administered in both UDS eras (Appendix lists 4 and 6). Since the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) is only administered in UDS 3 and contains Trail Making Test items, TMT‐A and TMT‐B were assigned to UDS 1&2 for this analysis (A4). MoCA in UDS 3 has a letter F‐fluency item, so the Category Fluency Animals and Vegetables were assigned to UDS 1 and 2 (A7).

Global cognitive z‐scores

We created two sets of global cognitive z‐scores by averaging test z‐scores from the UDS 1&2 era and UDS 3 era. To create global cognitive z‐scores, we considered the first visit during UDS 1&2 (one dataset) and first visit during UDS 3 (second dataset). Within each of these data sets, we focused on participants with NC and evaluated each test item independently to determine mean and SD at that first NACC visit from participants with NC. We used those means and SD to determine z‐scores for each cognitive test item for each person at every NACC visit. We used an average of those test z‐scores to determine global cognition z‐score for each NACC visit. We did this separately and independently for the UDS 1&2 data set and for the UDS 3 data set.

Imaging methods

We evaluated the association between imaging data and cognitive scores from the visit closest in time to the imaging data acquisition. Imaging data collection and processing are described on the NACC website: https://naccdata.org/data‐collection/forms‐documentation/biomarker‐imaging. We used quantitative data, including predefined ROIs generated by the IDeA lab (http://idealab.ucdavis.edu/), for which detailed methods are available at the following website: https://files.alz.washington.edu/documentation/updated‐mri‐methods.pdf. We summed left‐ and right‐sided measurements for ROIs for the associations with memory and executive functioning domains but considered only the left‐sided ROIs when the language domain was the outcome of interest. For association testing with memory, we considered hippocampal volume and cortical thickness of the parahippocampus, entorhinal cortex, and fusiform gyrus. For executive functioning, we considered caudal middle frontal, rostral middle frontal, superior middle frontal, lateral orbitofrontal, and pars triangularis. For language, we considered superior temporal gyrus, inferior temporal, fusiform, lingual gyrus, lateral occipital, and pericalcarine.

We implemented linear regression analysis between each of the domain scores and corresponding ROIs, adjusting for age at closest visit, sex, years of education, and APOE genotype (0 vs ≥1 ε4 allele).

APOE genotype

APOE genotype is available for many NACC participants. Each ADRC site uses appropriate measures for genotyping and reports these data to NACC. We relied on APOE data from the NACC database for these analyses.

Bridging the gap between UDS eras

Scores were assessed for each group: (1) both enrollment visit and last visit in UDS 1&2, (2) both visits in UDS 3, and (3) enrollment visit in UDS 1&2 and last visit in UDS 3. We used descriptive statistics, box plots, and unadjusted t tests to demonstrate similarities and differences at the enrollment visit across these groups.

Statistical approaches

We used Stata 18 for data management and to perform t tests, Spearman correlations, and linear regression, as appropriate. Mplus 8.11 was used for all confirmatory factor analysis models, as detailed in Crane et al. 11

Calculation of domain scores in the crosswalk sample (n = 955)

For each individual in the NACC crosswalk sample (n = 955), we determined domain scores based on UDS 1&2 specific tests and separate domain scores based on UDS 3 tests, with no overlapping content. We determined correlations between UDS 1&2 domain scores and UDS 3 domain scores. We then compared these to observed scores for UDS 1&2 tests and the estimated scores for those same tests derived from the equipercentile‐equating approach (Table 5). Co‐calibrated domain scores from non‐overlapping items had similar correlations (ranging from 0.68 to 0.79) for the UDS 1&2 and UDS 3 batteries as those found for individual test pairs with the equipercentile‐equating approach. We compared global cognitive z‐scores derived from means and SD from the participants with NC in the UDS 1&2 era to z‐scores derived from means and SD from the participants with NC in UDS 3 era (correlation coefficient = 0.82).

For executive functioning, associations of a priori identified ROIs with executive functioning domain scores were somewhat weaker than those for memory ROIs and memory scores, as shown in Table 6. For language, associations of a priori identified ROIs with language domain scores were also somewhat weaker than those for memory ROIs and memory scores, as shown in the right column of the third group of results in Table 6. One ROI – the left superior temporal gyrus – was associated with language scores in each diagnostic group, including participants with NC (coefficient = 0.09), MCI (coefficient = 0.17), and dementia (coefficient = 0.23; all p values survived Bonferroni‐corrected p value). Among participants with NC, associations between volumes of the other language ROIs and the language score were close to 0, except for the left pericalcarine ROI (coefficient = 0.10, survived Bonferroni‐corrected p value). We made full‐sample comparisons between ROIs and domain scores or global z‐scores, and nearly all comparisons had significant associations (Table S1).

DISCUSSION

In this paper we demonstrate analyses of cognitive data from two distinct cognitive batteries – UDS 1&2 and UDS 3. Evaluating longitudinal cognitive data from NACC requires bridging the gap introduced by the changing cognitive batteries. We performed four sets of analyses. First, we considered the crosswalk sample, where correlations between cognitive domain scores were similar to equipercentile‐equating pairs of tests. Second, we considered imaging data. Associations between cognitive domain scores and a priori selected ROIs were in the expected direction within each diagnostic group, with stronger associations for memory than executive functioning or language domains. Third, we considered APOE genotype data. Associations were strongest for memory, with much smaller differences for language or executive functioning. Finally, we considered baseline scores across groups defined by which UDS cognitive battery was used at their first and last visit. For each diagnostic group, we found significant differences between these cohorts for each cognitive domain.

Full sample comparisons of co‐calibrated domain scores were also analyzed with APOE and imaging data. Results from the entire sample combine the different diagnostic, heterogeneous groups together. Participants with MCI may not go on to develop dementia or AD dementia. The AD dementia group is homogeneous – everyone in that group developed AD dementia – while the MCI and NC groups includes a substantial proportion who will go on to develop AD dementia, but also others who will never develop dementia, and others who will develop some other form of dementia.

In large datasets, like NACC, small findings could be statistically significant. There were stronger significant statistical differences in co‐calibrated memory and executive functioning domain scores by APOE genotype than with global z‐scores. Differences within diagnostic group are small yet were in the expected direction (worse with ≥1 APOE ɛ4 allele), demonstrating the scores are working as intended confirmed by known‐group validity.

Equipercentile methods could potentially bridge the UDS era battery gap. 12 Although we obtained similar correlations between UDS 1&2 and UDS 3 scores with the equipercentile approach and our co‐calibrated domain scores, these scores differ fundamentally in their assumptions, implementation, and inferential scope. Equipercentile equating offers a flexible, non‐parametric method for aligning scores across instruments. 16 This approach does not rely on psychometric modeling assumptions and can be implemented with total score data, making it useful when item‐level data are unavailable or test content differs substantially. Nonetheless, equipercentile methods have important limitations. They do not address measurement error or differences in construct validity. Mappings are inherently sample‐dependent and may not generalize across populations. As shown in Table 1, the crosswalk sample has good representation of participants with NC (> 50%). The representation of participants with dementia was weaker: 17% had CDR ≥ 1. Representation of participants with moderate and severe dementia severity was weaker still: 6% had CDR ≥ 2, and only nine participants – under 1% of the sample – had CDR = 3. Investigators using equipercentile‐equating tables should be aware of the limitations of this sample.

Another choice to bridge the gap is to use modern psychometric approaches, as we did, with two important advantages. First, for studies with longitudinal data, we choose the most recent occasion for each person. Cognition declines over time for participants with AD, so we capitalize on each person's maximal dementia severity level, enhancing confidence in scores for participants with greater dementia severity. We leverage combined samples across multiple studies, including large numbers with higher CDR levels. Second, modern psychometric methods are much less affected by the specific characteristics of the co‐calibration sample, a “distribution‐free” feature. With a good spread of ability levels ranging from highly able to profound deficits, the specific distribution of ability levels is much less important for modern psychometric approaches. 17 , 18 , 19

With the imaging and APOE genotype data, we found that the cognitive domain scores worked as expected. Even among participants with the same dementia status, volumes of a priori selected ROIs were associated with cognitive domain scores, which corresponded with dementia status. Within each group, participants with ≥1 APOE ε4 allele had lower memory scores. These analyses further established the validity of the domain scores generated using modern psychometric approaches and should be reassuring to investigators considering analyzing NACC's rich cognitive data.

A third option for bridging the gap between UDS era cognitive batteries is averaging of z‐scores across batteries. For each test, mean and standard deviations are determined for participants with NC in each UDS era sample and used to standardize test z‐scores from each sample, which are then averaged together either within a cognitive domain or across the board to represent global cognition.

The z‐score approach is fraught with important assumptions that are likely violated at multiple levels. First, z‐scores using means and standard deviations are a linear transformation, which only makes sense if each score has linear scaling properties. The many constraints that must be satisfied for standard scores to be appropriate (and linear) have been described. 1 Our analysis of commonly used global cognitive tests found distinct curvilinear scaling and demonstrated that using standard scores biased estimates of decline compared to modern psychometric approaches that do not assume linearity. 17

Second, batteries often vary in their coverage. The battery for one sample could have only a few memory tests, all probing a single aspect of memory, such as orientation, while a second sample could have an extensive array of tests probing multiple aspects of memory. Average z‐scores for the two samples would look similar but represent different things. Similarly, for global cognition, batteries may vary substantially in domain content, which would not be accounted for in an average z‐score approach. The average z‐score approach also forces each test to have equal weight. This assumes that the measurement properties of each of the tests is interchangeable and equal. Modern psychometric analyses facilitate specific attention to measurement properties. To summarize findings from our work over the past decades, we have repeatedly found cognitive tests vary substantially in terms of measurement properties. 10

Domain score findings across samples from NACC illustrate the violation of a third set of assumptions made by the average z‐score approach to bridging the gap. In the z‐score approach, different samples of participants with NC are used to standardize scores. This assumes that the ability levels of different samples of participants with NC are equal. This was not the case across UDS eras in NACC. For example, the distribution of memory scores for participants with NC administered the UDS 1&2 battery only was somewhat lower than that for those administered the UDS 1&2 battery and the UDS 3 battery, which was in turn somewhat lower than those administered only the UDS 3 battery. As it happens, whether due to historical trends and cohort effects, evolving recruitment goals over time, or the makeup of which specific ADRCs contributed data over time, the distribution of memory scores differed across samples. The numerical similarity of average z‐scores obscures any differences in ability levels for the groups used for standardization.

Modern psychometric approaches do not rely on equal ability levels across samples to obtain co‐calibrated scores, whereas average z‐score approaches rely on distributional assumptions. As long as there is a good spread of ability levels in the sample, modern psychometric approaches are “distribution free.” 16 , 17 , 18 , 19

Investigators intending to use data from participants assessed with distinct cognitive batteries face important gaps that need to be bridged. It is not easy to make valid inferences in this setting. The average z‐score approach is widely used, but we cannot recommend it. The equipercentile approach makes fewer distributional assumptions, but it may be limited by the particular characteristics of the crosswalk sample. The sample used to equate pairs of tests from the UDS 1&2 and UDS 3 cognitive batteries had poor representation of participants with moderate or severe levels of dementia. Investigators need to consider whether their hypotheses involve participants with NC or MCI – groups well represented in the crosswalk sample – or whether they involve participants with dementia, including participants with moderate or severe levels of dementia.

Investigators should also consider whether their hypotheses involve specific individual test scores or whether they involve an underlying domain, such as memory, executive functioning, or language. In several of the examples of papers that have used the tables from the equipercentile approach with the crosswalk data set cited above, investigators took equipercentile‐equated scores, made z‐scores from those scores, and combined z‐scores to form composites that would face all of the challenges addressed above. This procedure, like the average z‐score approach in general, is not one we recommend.

Taken together, our findings suggest that co‐calibrated scores for memory, language, and executive functioning developed using modern psychometric approaches can be used for analyses across time, neatly bridging the UDS 1&2 and UDS 3 cognition battery gap. Co‐calibrated cognitive domain scores are available from NACC alongside other NACC‐derived data. Multiple approaches have been used to bridge the gap created by differing cognitive batteries. The approach we have developed has much to recommend it, as illustrated here with NACC's rich, well‐characterized data.
