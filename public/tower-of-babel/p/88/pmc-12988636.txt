Assessment of influenza burden, preventive behaviours, and vaccination uptake among multi-ethnic university students in the United Arab Emirates: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Benzil Binu, Haniel Karmel Jinu, Mohammed Isham Shiju, Sachin Syal, Nelofar Sami Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeasonal influenza remains a significant public health concern worldwide. Although young adults are generally at lower risk of severe outcomes, they play a key role in influenza transmission. Universities in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) are highly multi-ethnic settings where cultural, socioeconomic, and healthcare access factors may influence influenza burden, preventive behaviours, healthcare-seeking, and vaccination uptake. However, data among university students in the UAE remains limited.MethodsA cross-sectional, questionnaire-based survey was conducted among university students aged 18 years or older across multiple UAE campuses. The study assessed the burden of influenza-like illness, preventive behaviours, healthcare-seeking patterns, and influenza vaccination uptake, and examined sociodemographic, clinical, and healthcare access factors associated with these outcomes. Associations were assessed using chi-square tests. Binary and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of healthcare-seeking behaviour and influenza vaccination uptake.ResultsA total of 963 students participated (median age: 20 years; 51.4% male). Overall, 82.6% reported influenza-like illness in the past five years, with significant variation by ethnicity and gender. Nearly half (47.6%) reported attending university while symptomatic. Influenza vaccination coverage in the preceding year was 59.2%, while willingness to vaccinate increased to 88.2% if vaccination were free. Despite high awareness, 40.5% believed vaccination reduces natural immunity. In multivariable analysis, healthcare-seeking for influenza was independently associated with health insurance status, illness severity, and symptom duration, while gender, ethnicity, and economic status were not significant after adjustment. Influenza vaccination uptake was independently associated with having health insurance (AOR = 6.51), seeking medical consultation for influenza (AOR = 1.80), belief in individual protection from vaccination (AOR = 27.11), and belief in community-level protection (AOR = 2.95).ConclusionUniversity students in the UAE experience a substantial burden of influenza-like illness, with persistent gaps between awareness and preventive action. Insurance coverage and engagement with healthcare services play a central role in both healthcare-seeking and vaccination uptake, whereas economic status alone does not independently predict these behaviours. University-based public health strategies, including free on-campus vaccination, clinician-led recommendations, and targeted interventions to address misconceptions, may improve vaccine uptake and reduce influenza transmission in this highly mobile, multi-ethnic population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-12944-0.Keywords: Ethnicity, Influenza, Influenzafluenza-like illness, Healthcare-seeking behaviour, Public health prevention, United Arab Emirates, University students, Vaccination uptake

Introduction

Influenza (Flu) is a contagious disease that is caused by a range of influenza viruses. Some of these viruses infect humans, while others are specific to animal species. It affects the respiratory tract, and transmission occurs primarily via droplets released while coughing, sneezing, or speaking, and through contact with contaminated objects [1]. Influenza can be transmitted before symptom onset, and infected individuals may remain infectious for 5–7 days. While most healthy individuals recover within a few days, complications such as pneumonia and death are common among immunocompromised individuals, pregnant women, and other high-risk groups. Typical symptoms include rhinorrhea, fever, cough, and sore throat [2, 3].

Seasonal influenza is a highly contagious viral illness that occurs in annual epidemics and represents a significant global public-health burden. In temperate regions, epidemics typically occur during the fall and winter months, infecting a significant proportion of both adults and children [4]. Older adults (≥65 years), young children, pregnant women, people with chronic diseases, including asthma, cardiovascular disorders, diabetes, and immunosuppression, are at increased risk of getting severe infections [5].

Two major human influenza virus types—Influenza A and Influenza B—co-circulate annually and are responsible for most seasonal epidemics. Surveillance data from the Eastern Mediterranean and North African (EMNA) region, as defined in regional influenza surveillance literature, indicate that A(H1N1) and A(H3N2) strains accounted for 50.8% and 15.9% of influenza-positive cases, respectively, with notable regional variation. Altogether, Influenza A accounted for 67.9% and influenza B accounted for 23.8% of all positive cases. Predominance of this subtype has important implications for vaccine composition, epidemic forecasting, and public health responses [6].

The severity of influenza presentation varies, from self-limiting mild illness to life-threatening manifestations. Mild cases are generally managed with rest and adequate fluid intake, and in some instances with antiviral medications such as zanamivir [7]. In extremely severe cases, influenza may even prove life-threatening if the infection causes a severe inflammatory reaction leading to sepsis. [8].

Seasonal influenza imposes a substantial and persistent public health burden globally. Data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) indicate that, since the 2010–11 season (excluding the 2020–21 season), seasonal influenza in the United States has been associated with approximately 9.3–41 million infections annually, 120,000– 710,000 hospitalizations, and 6300– 52,000 deaths each year [9]. During the 2023–24 influenza season alone, an estimated 40 million infections, 18 million healthcare visits, 470,000 hospitalizations, and 28,000 deaths were reported [10].

Within the Eastern Mediterranean and North African (EMNA) region, available evidence also indicates ongoing influenza circulation. A systematic review of 112 studies from the region reported a large number of laboratory-confirmed influenza cases, although estimates of hospitalization rates, illness duration, and mortality varied across studies. These findings reflect the diversity of study designs and surveillance approaches used in the region and highlight the importance of continued research to better characterize influenza epidemiology in different settings [6].

Furthermore, emerging evidence indicates that the burden of influenza is not evenly distributed across populations. Certain racial and ethnic minority groups are reported to experience disproportionately severe outcomes of influenza infection. In the United States, the influenza-associated rate of hospitalization among Black population (non-Hispanic), American/Alaska Native, and Hispanic adults was approximately 1.8-, 1.3-, and 1.2-fold higher, respectively, than those among White adults across influenza seasons between 2009 and 10 and 2021–22 [11]. Delayed healthcare-seeking behavior—more common in some minority populations due to cultural norms, mistrust of healthcare systems, or limited access to preventive services—has been associated with increased disease severity [11]. These disparities are also reflected in varying rates of influenza vaccination among adults, with only 42% of Black and 37.9% of Hispanic adults vaccinated, compared with 53.9% of White adults [11]. Socio-cultural beliefs, perceived susceptibility, and attitudes toward preventive healthcare influence differences in vaccination uptake [11]. A U.S. systematic review reported that influenza vaccination uptake among university students is generally suboptimal and remains well below recommended targets. Some studies have identified racial and ethnic differences in vaccination uptake or intention, reporting significantly lower vaccination uptake among certain racial or ethnic minority student groups compared with White students. It highlights the need for further research to clarify these disparities [12].

Influenza vaccination protects individuals and communities against infection. Large-scale community-level vaccination reduces transmission, thus protecting the vulnerable populations. Evidence of “herd protection” indicates that higher population-level vaccination coverage can substantially reduce infection rates, thereby protecting unvaccinated individuals as well [13].

Universities in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) are highly multi-ethnic environments, where students often live, study, and socialize in close proximity. In such settings, influenza transmission and disease impact may be shaped not only by cultural and behavioural practices, but also by patterns of healthcare use, preventive behaviours, and access to healthcare services. Understanding how these factors interact across diverse student populations is essential for designing effective, context-specific influenza prevention and control strategies in higher education settings. Accordingly, this study aimed to assess the burden of influenza-like illness, preventive behaviours, healthcare-seeking patterns, and influenza vaccination uptake among multi-ethnic university students in the United Arab Emirates, and to examine how sociodemographic characteristics, clinical severity, and healthcare access factors influence these outcomes.

Materials and methods

Aim, study design, and sample population

Primary aim: To assess the burden of influenza-like illness, preventive behaviours, healthcare-seeking patterns, and influenza vaccination uptake among multi-ethnic university students in the United Arab Emirates.

Secondary aim: To examine sociodemographic, clinical, and healthcare access factors associated with healthcare-seeking behaviour and influenza vaccination uptake, with particular attention to the role of insurance coverage and illness severity.

Sample size calculation

The minimum required sample size was calculated using the single-proportion (prevalence) formula for cross-sectional surveys: n = z2 pq/d2, where z = 1.96 corresponds to a two-sided 95% confidence level, p represents the expected prevalence, q = 1 − p, and d is the desired absolute precision. To anchor the prevalence estimate in contextually relevant evidence, we used the proportion of respondents reporting seasonal influenza vaccination (54%) from a prior UAE-based cross-sectional survey (p = 0.54; q = 0.46) [14]. At the time of study planning, UAE-specific estimates of influenza vaccination uptake among university students were not available; therefore, this study provided the closest population-based estimate for parameterization. Assuming an absolute precision of 3.4% and an anticipated non-response rate of 10%, the target sample size was approximately 900 participants. The achieved sample exceeded this minimum requirement, supporting adequate precision for the planned analyses.

Questionnaire development and validation

The questionnaire was developed following a comprehensive literature review that covered influenza epidemiology, knowledge, attitudes, and vaccination behaviour [15–17]. Content validity was ensured through expert review by faculty with experience in epidemiology and infectious diseases. A pilot study with ten students assessed clarity, length, and feasibility. Minor linguistic and structural revisions were made in response to feedback. Data from the pilot phase were excluded from the final analysis. The questionnaire developed for this study is attached as a supplementary file.

Survey instrument and study measures

A structured, self-administered questionnaire was used to collect data. The final instrument comprised five main sections: sociodemographic characteristics; influenza-related history and healthcare use; knowledge and preventive behaviours; vaccination status; and perceptions regarding influenza vaccination.

Sampling technique and data collection

A convenience sampling approach was employed, whereby university students who were readily accessible and willing to participate were invited to complete an online, self-administered questionnaire. Data collection was conducted across multiple university campuses in the UAE, including Ajman, Sharjah, Dubai, Abu Dhabi, and Ras Al Khaimah. No predefined quotas were applied for individual universities, and participant numbers varied across institutions based on voluntary participation and response rates rather than equal allocation. Consequently, the sample was not evenly distributed across universities. The ethnic composition of the sample largely reflected the demographic structure of the UAE university student population, with South Asian and Arab students comprising the majority, alongside representation from other ethnic groups. While the use of an online questionnaire may have limited participation from some smaller ethnic groups, this approach was selected to facilitate wider reach, logistical feasibility, and participant anonymity.

Ethical considerations

The Institutional Review Board approved the study before commencement. All participants provided informed consent electronically before the survey began. Participation was voluntary, and no personal identifiers were collected; thus, confidentiality was maintained. All study procedures were conducted in conformity with the “Declaration of Helsinki.”

Statistical analysis

Data were downloaded as Microsoft Excel and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics, influenza prevalence, preventive behaviours, and vaccination patterns. For analytical purposes, perceived illness severity originally measured on a five-point self-rated scale was collapsed into three categories: mild (scores 1–2), moderate (scores 3–4), and severe (score 5).

The chi-square test was used for associations between demographic variables and key outcomes, including influenza-like illness prevalence, severity, healthcare-seeking behaviour, and vaccination status. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Binary logistic regression was conducted to calculate crude odds ratios (ORs) for predictors of healthcare-seeking and vaccination uptake. Variables with significance of p < 0.05 in bivariate analysis were included in multivariable logistic regression models, and adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated.

Experiencing flu-like symptoms in the past five years was reported by 82.6% of participants, with a significantly higher prevalence among Arabs (86.0%) and those categorized as Others (89.1%) compared with South Asians (78.2%) (p = 0.002). Males reported a higher prevalence of influenza-like illness than females (90.9% vs. 73.7%, p < 0.001). Overall, 59.9% of participants sought medical consultation for influenza symptoms. Consultation-seeking differed significantly by ethnicity, with a higher proportion among Arabs (66.2%), and by gender, with males more likely to consult than females (68.5% vs. 50.9%; both p < 0.001). While consultation rates did not differ significantly across economic groups, insured participants were substantially more likely to seek medical care compared with uninsured participants (75.7% vs. 46.5%, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 1).

Among those who consulted a physician, 84.5% reported laboratory-confirmed influenza. The duration and perceived severity of illness varied across sociodemographic groups. Prolonged illness lasting more than five days was more frequently reported by Arabs and by females (both p < 0.001). Across economic groups, shorter illness duration (1–2 days) was more commonly reported among lower-income participants, whereas longer illness duration was more prevalent among middle- and high-income participants (p = 0.004). Severity of illness also differed by ethnicity, with severe symptoms reported more frequently by Arabs (28.0%, p < 0.001). Differences in illness severity across economic or insurance categories were modest, with no statistically significant variation (Supplementary Table 1).

Use of medications perceived by participants as antiviral treatment was reported by 72.9% of those who developed influenza-like illness and differed significantly by ethnicity, with higher reported use among Arabs (84.6%, p < 0.001). Reported antiviral use also varied by economic status (p = 0.009) and was more common among insured participants compared with uninsured participants (76.1% vs. 46.5%, p < 0.001). Hospitalization for influenza was reported by 25.5% of participants, with higher rates among Arabs (42.9%, p < 0.001). Hospitalization did not differ significantly by economic status but was more frequent among insured participants than uninsured participants (26.8% vs. 14.6%, p = 0.017).

Influenza-related complications, including pneumonia, were reported by 20.6% of participants and occurred more frequently among South Asians (27.3%, p < 0.001). Complication rates did not differ significantly across economic groups but were higher among insured participants compared with those without insurance (21.9% vs. 9.3%, p = 0.006) (Supplementary Table 1).

Awareness and preventive behaviours

Table 2 shows overall awareness regarding influenza was high, with 95.0% of participants recognizing influenza as a viral infection and 88.0% being aware of peak transmission periods. Awareness that influenza is transmitted through coughs, sneezes, or contaminated hands differed significantly across ethnic groups, with lower awareness among participants categorized as “Others” (86.1%) compared with Arabs (99.5%) and South Asians (96.8%) (p < 0.001). Awareness of potentially fatal influenza-related complications was also significantly lower in the “Others” group. By gender, males demonstrated higher awareness of influenza-related complications than females (93.1% vs. 85.1%, p < 0.001).

When examined by socioeconomic factors (Supplementary Table 2), awareness indicators showed significant gradients. Participants in the high-income group and those with health insurance consistently demonstrated higher awareness in all assessed domains, including that influenza is a viral infection, knowledge of peak transmission periods, understanding of transmission routes, and potentially fatal complications.

Regarding preventive behaviours, nearly half of participants (47.6%) reported attending university while ill. This behaviour was significantly more common among the “Others” ethnic group (67.3%) and among males (51.3%) (both p < 0.05). Across economic groups, attendance at university while sick increased progressively from high-income to lower-income participants (42.9%, 55.1%, and 71.4%, respectively; p < 0.001), whereas no significant difference was observed by insurance status (p = 0.371) (Supplementary Table 2).

Most participants reported covering coughs or sneezes with their elbow or arm (92.2%), with only minor variation by ethnicity, gender, or economic status. Hand hygiene after coughing or sneezing was also commonly reported (90.5%) but varied significantly across ethnic and economic groups, with lower adherence observed in the middle-income group (79.6%) compared with high-income participants (95.1%, p < 0.001).

Factors associated with seeking medical consultation for influenza

In the bivariate analysis (Table 3), several factors were associated with an increased likelihood of seeking medical consultation for influenza-like symptoms. Male sex, “Others” ethnic group, having health insurance, greater illness severity, and longer symptom duration were all significantly associated with healthcare-seeking behaviour at the crude level. Economic status was not significantly associated with consultation-seeking in the unadjusted analysis.

In the multivariable logistic regression model, adjusting for sociodemographic and clinical factors, health insurance status, illness severity, and duration remained independently associated with seeking medical consultation. Participants with health insurance had more than 4-fold higher odds of consulting a physician for influenza than uninsured participants (AOR = 4.16, 95% CI: 2.45–7.08, p < 0.001). Regarding clinical severity, participants reporting moderate illness had approximately twice the odds of seeking medical consultation compared with those with mild symptoms (AOR = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.31–3.26, p = 0.002). Those reporting severe illness had markedly higher odds of healthcare utilization, with nearly fifteenfold increased odds relative to the reference group (AOR = 14.80, 95% CI: 6.26–34.96, p < 0.001). Illness duration was also independently associated with consultation-seeking. Participants with symptoms lasting 2–5 days had significantly higher odds of seeking medical care compared with those with symptoms lasting 1–2 days (AOR = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.29–3.31, p = 0.003). Gender and ethnicity were significantly associated with healthcare-seeking behaviour in the crude analysis; however, these associations were attenuated and no longer statistically significant after adjustment, suggesting that their effects were confounded by illness severity, duration, and insurance coverage.

Influenza vaccination status and perceptions

Table 4 shows that awareness of the influenza vaccine was high overall (92.5%), with no statistically significant differences across ethnic or gender groups. Influenza vaccination uptake in the preceding year was reported by 59.2% of participants and did not differ significantly by ethnicity or gender. The intention to receive influenza vaccination in the current year was generally high. While 41.2% of participants expressed willingness to be vaccinated “at all costs,” overall willingness increased substantially to 88.2% if the vaccine were offered free of charge. Intention to vaccinate differed by gender, with females more likely than males to report willingness to vaccinate “at all costs” (44.8% vs. 37.9%, p < 0.001), whereas ethnic differences were not statistically significant.

When socioeconomic factors were considered (Supplementary Table 3), marked disparities in vaccination uptake and access were observed. Vaccination in the previous year was significantly higher among participants in the high-income group compared with middle- and lower-income groups (64.6% vs. 48.1% and 54.5%, respectively; p < 0.001). Similarly, insured participants were substantially more likely to report vaccination in the previous year than uninsured participants (63.8% vs. 23.9%, p < 0.001).

Regarding the perceived benefits of vaccination, 79.9% believed it offers direct protection for individuals, and 80.3% perceived it as controlling community spread, with significant differences across ethnic groups, gender, economic status, and insurance groups; higher perceptions among South Asians, females, and high-income participants (all p < 0.001). Approximately 40.5% believed that vaccination reduces natural immunity, with a higher proportion among males (47.3% vs. 33.3%; p < 0.001) and lower-income participants. Perceptions of vaccination among individuals with chronic illness indicated that 86.0% considered vaccination preventive, with a significant ethnic difference (p = 0.029).

After multivariable adjustment, health insurance status emerged as a strong independent predictor, with insured participants having over 6-fold higher odds of vaccination compared with uninsured participants (AOR = 6.51; 95% CI: 3.44–12.31; p < 0.001). Participants who sought medical consultation for influenza were also more likely to be vaccinated (AOR = 1.80; 95% CI: 1.19–2.74; p = 0.006).

The belief that influenza vaccination provides individual protection remained the strongest independent predictor of uptake (AOR = 27.11; 95% CI: 11.63–63.15; p < 0.001), followed by the belief that vaccination helps control community spread (AOR = 2.95; 95% CI: 1.22–7.13; p = 0.016). In contrast, belief in vaccination as a public health necessity did not retain statistical significance after adjustment.

Gender, ethnicity, economic status, and the belief that vaccination reduces natural immunity were not included in the multivariable model because they were not statistically significant at the crude level.

Discussion

This study examined the burden of influenza-like illness, preventive behaviours, healthcare-seeking patterns, and influenza vaccination uptake among multi-ethnic university students in the UAE, with particular attention to factors influencing healthcare use and vaccination decisions. As this socially mobile young-adult population plays a central role in community transmission, understanding their cultural beliefs, attitudes, and behaviours is crucial for formulating targeted interventions in higher education settings.

Influenza prevalence and Healthcare-Seeking Behaviour

The prevalence of influenza-like illness was high, with more than 80% of university students reporting experiencing symptoms in the past 5 years (Table 1). Substantial exposure and transmission potential among young adults (18–24 years) has been supported by documented 80–90% H3N2 seroprevalence in Egypt over the past few years [18]. Due to this high burden, influenza accounts for approximately 21% of acute lower respiratory infection hospitalizations among young adults across the Middle East and North Africa [19]. Ethnic differences were apparent in our sample, with Arabs and students in the “Others” category reporting higher influenza prevalence than South Asians. Additionally, among those who got infected, a relatively smaller proportion of South Asians (56.2%) versus Arabs (66.2%) sought medical consultation. This hesitancy in seeking medical consultation may be the reason that the symptom duration for the majority of South Asians (68.4%) lasted for 3–5 days, compared to only 35.8% of the Arabs, and also for higher rates of complications such as pneumonia (Table 1). Ethnic differences in infection and severity have been observed in England, where South Asian, Black, and Mixed populations experienced higher clinical influenza incidence compared to White populations [20]. Gender-related differences were also observed in our study: fewer females reported influenza illness, whereas males were more likely to seek medical care. Additionally, females reported longer illness durations, which may be attributable to hesitancy to seek medical care (Table 1). Similar trends have been reported in Egypt, where being female was protective against H3N2 [18], suggesting that gender influences disease patterns. However, regression analysis in our cohort demonstrated that illness severity and symptom duration were the strongest predictors of healthcare-seeking behaviour, while gender and ethnicity were not independently associated after adjustment (Table 3). The lack of an independent association between illness duration exceeding five days and consultation-seeking after adjustment is likely due to confounding by clinical severity. Students with prolonged symptoms were more likely to seek medical care primarily because they experienced more severe illness, rather than because of symptom duration alone. Consistent with our findings, other studies on influenza-like illness report that greater symptom burden and longer illness duration are the main determinants of consulting a health professional, and socio-demographic characteristics demonstrate weak or inconsistent associations after adjusting for clinical severity [21, 22]. However, economic status was examined as a potential socioeconomic factor in bivariate analysis but did not reach statistical significance at the crude level. In contrast, insurance status remained an independent predictor of consultation-seeking after adjustment. This finding is supported by evidence from Saudi Arabia showing that health insurance independently facilitates healthcare utilization by reducing financial and access barriers, with insured individuals demonstrating higher odds of seeking medical care than uninsured individuals after adjustment for socioeconomic factors. These findings underscore the role of insurance coverage, rather than income alone, in enabling healthcare-seeking behaviour in comparable health system contexts [23].

Similar evidence from the United States indicates that insurance-related factors outweigh income in determining preventive healthcare utilization, with income losing independent significance once insurance coverage is accounted for, reinforcing our finding that insurance status is a key determinant of healthcare-seeking behaviour [24]. Although economic status was explored as a socioeconomic indicator, healthcare access—particularly insurance coverage—emerged as the more consistent determinant of healthcare-seeking and vaccination behaviour in this cohort.

Awareness and preventive behaviours

Our cohort exhibits high awareness, with over 90% of students identifying influenza as a viral disease and recognizing respiratory droplets as the primary route of transmission (Table 2). Additionally, more than 90% reported covering their sneeze or cough with their elbow or arm. Respiratory etiquette is identified by domestic and international students from Sydney, Australia, as a valuable strategy for controlling disease transmission [25]. However, a knowledge-behaviour gap persists among our student population, with nearly 50% attending university while symptomatic, which could facilitate viral transmission. Similar concepts have been documented internationally, with university students demonstrating high awareness but low adherence to preventive behaviours, such as staying home when symptomatic, highlighting a disconnect between knowledge and practice [25].

Perceptions and attitudes toward vaccination

Perceptions of our study participants toward influenza vaccination varied across demographic subgroups: awareness exceeded 90% across groups, whereas belief in its effectiveness differed significantly (Table 4). The majority of South Asians (86.5%) demonstrated confidence in vaccine effectiveness, agreeing that the vaccine prevents getting influenza, compared with 71.0% of Arabs. Similarly, 87.3% of South Asians, 71.9% of Arabs, and 82% of participants from Other ethnic groups endorsed the belief that community vaccination reduces influenza transmission. These results broadly align with regional evidence from Saudi Arabia, which reported that approximately 60% of respondents expressed confidence in the influenza vaccine, and nearly 70% were aware of its preventive role in reducing influenza transmission [26]. Gender-based differences were also noted; 84.9% of females versus 75.2% of males agreed that the vaccine prevents influenza, and 87.3% of females versus 77.3% of males believed it was necessary for public health. Greater vaccine acceptance and lower hesitancy by females is reported in other university student populations also [27].

A key misconception identified in 40.5% of our study participants is the belief that influenza vaccination weakens natural immunity. This concern was more frequently observed among males (47.3%) than among females (33.3%), highlighting gender-specific patterns of misinformation. Comparable findings are documented in other populations. For instance, a survey of a Saudi community found that 54.8% of respondents believed that the influenza vaccine weakens the immune system and increases susceptibility to infection [28]. A qualitative study among nurses documented concerns about the manipulation of the immune system by influenza vaccination, indicating that this belief is not confined to any region or demographic group [29]. Research on vaccination hesitancy also shows that concerns about adverse effects and perceptions of vaccine effectiveness remain common barriers to uptake [30]. Taken together, these data suggest that, beyond awareness of vaccine availability and effectiveness, misconceptions about immunity waning and side effects must be actively addressed in health-promotion efforts among university populations.

Determinants of vaccination uptake and access

Despite high awareness and generally positive attitudes, influenza vaccination uptake in this population remained moderate, with 59% of students reporting vaccination in the preceding year. Vaccination uptake observed in this study falls within the range reported in other settings. For example, studies from China have documented vaccination coverage of approximately 61.3% among youth and adult populations over three years [30] while a study from Saudi Arabia reported uptake of around 65% among adult respondents [26]. In contrast, lower uptake has been reported in university-based studies from Lebanon, where 47.4% of respondents reported never having been vaccinated, and only 10.2% reported regular annual vaccination [15], and among Israeli college students, where only 12% received the vaccine during the current season, despite easy access [27].

A persistent knowledge–behaviour gap observed in the present study, characterized by relatively high awareness but suboptimal influenza vaccination uptake among university students, has also been reported in other settings. For example, a population-based study from Yemen found that while 62.5% of respondents perceived influenza vaccination as protective, only 11.3% had ever been vaccinated [16]. Similarly, among healthcare workers in Kenya, 87.3% recognized that influenza could cause severe illness, yet only 17.6% reported prior vaccination [31]. In a study of Spanish adults, vaccination coverage remained low despite widespread awareness, with a lack of healthcare provider recommendation cited as a major barrier [32]. Although these studies were conducted in non-student populations, they provide contextual evidence that awareness alone may not be sufficient to drive vaccination uptake. Consistent with behavioural models of preventive health decision-making, engagement with trusted healthcare providers has been shown to facilitate the translation of vaccine awareness into preventive action [33].

Interestingly, 88.2% of the students in our study expressed willingness to receive the influenza vaccine if it were provided free, thus underscoring the importance of affordability as a key facilitator of vaccine uptake. Consistent patterns have been reported in Kenya, where the median willingness to pay was only USD 2, which is substantially lower than prevailing private-sector prices of USD 10–15 [31], highlighting the persistent influence of financial barriers on vaccination decisions, even among educated populations.

Beyond affordability, several access- and perception-related factors identified in this study may further explain the persistence of moderate vaccination uptake despite high awareness. Vaccination uptake was substantially higher among insured compared with uninsured students (63.8% vs. 23.9%), and seeking medical consultation for influenza was independently associated with higher vaccination uptake, suggesting that engagement with healthcare services facilitates vaccine acceptance. Regional and international evidence supports the findings that individuals with health insurance were more likely to seek preventive and clinical care even in the presence of similar economic constraints [23, 24]. Moreover, more than half of the participants did not perceive influenza as a serious illness, and 40.5% believed that vaccination could lower natural immunity, indicating that risk perception and residual misconceptions continue to influence vaccination behaviour. These factors may be particularly relevant in the UAE university context, where preventive healthcare utilization is often insurance-driven, and vaccination is commonly sought opportunistically rather than through structured campus-based programs.

Furthermore, regression analysis demonstrated that vaccination behaviour in this cohort was primarily driven by health-seeking behaviour and belief in vaccine benefits, rather than by demographic variables (Table 5). Consistent with healthcare-seeking patterns, insurance coverage facilitated vaccination access, whereas economic status alone did not independently predict uptake after adjustment. Students who consulted a physician for influenza-like illness were significantly more likely to be vaccinated. Additionally, strong belief in individual and community protection through vaccination emerged as the strongest predictor of vaccination uptake. These observations align with evidence that a physician’s recommendation strongly influences vaccination decisions [16, 33]. Prior vaccination is reported as one of the most consistent predictors of future vaccination [31, 32], also evident in our study, as regular annual influenza booster uptake is reported among 53.3% (Table 4). Thus, shaping attitudes early in adulthood and ensuring regular on-campus free vaccination opportunities may help establish long-term vaccination habits.

Public health implications

Overall, this study reveals high awareness but inconsistent translation into preventive action among university students, a pattern observed in several other settings. Tailored university-based interventions could meaningfully improve outcomes by offering free on-campus vaccination, integrating physician or nurse endorsement into health services, correcting common misconceptions, and incorporating structured reminder systems. Evidence from regional and international studies suggests that such strategies may effectively convert willingness into action and increase vaccination coverage [15, 32, 34].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including a large, ethnically diverse sample drawn from multiple emirates and a comprehensive assessment of influenza-related knowledge, preventive behaviours, healthcare-seeking patterns, and vaccination practices among university students.

Several limitations should also be considered. The use of a convenience sampling approach with voluntary participation may introduce selection bias, result in unequal representation across universities, and limit generalizability beyond the university setting. Although participants were recruited from multiple emirates and diverse ethnic backgrounds, smaller ethnic groups were less represented, reflecting the underlying demographic composition of the UAE university population. The reliance on an online, self-administered questionnaire may have further limited participation from some groups who might have been more readily engaged through face-to-face data collection.

Influenza history, healthcare utilization, treatment, and vaccination status were based on self-report over a five-year recall period, which may be subject to recall bias and misclassification. Influenza cases were not defined using a standardized clinical case definition, and laboratory confirmation and vaccination records were not independently verified; consequently, some participants may have misclassified other acute respiratory infections as influenza or misunderstood clinical diagnoses. Similarly, medication use during influenza episodes was self-reported and not verified, and some participants may have misclassified antibiotics or symptomatic treatments as antiviral therapy.

The relatively small number of participants in the low-income category may have limited statistical power to detect independent income-related effects after multivariable adjustment; therefore, findings related to economic status should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, the study’s cross-sectional design precludes causal inference.

Future studies incorporating population-based samples, objective vaccination records, and longitudinal designs would further strengthen the evidence and allow assessment of changes in influenza-related behaviours over time.

Conclusion

University students in the UAE experience a high burden of influenza-like illness, with clear gaps between awareness, preventive behaviours, and vaccination uptake. Despite high levels of knowledge regarding influenza transmission and vaccine availability, nearly half of the students reported attending university while symptomatic, and vaccination coverage remained moderate. Clinical factors, particularly illness severity and symptom duration, were the strongest predictors of healthcare-seeking behaviour. At the same time, insurance coverage emerged as a key facilitator of both medical consultation and vaccination uptake, independent of economic status. In contrast, demographic characteristics such as gender and ethnicity were not independently associated after adjustment. Persistent misconceptions, including beliefs that influenza is not serious and that vaccination reduces natural immunity, continue to influence preventive decision-making. These findings highlight the need for university-based public health strategies that combine free or insurance-facilitated vaccination, clinician endorsement, and targeted educational interventions to translate awareness into effective preventive action and reduce influenza transmission in multi-ethnic academic settings.
