An Antarctic toothfish eye lens protein resists thermal stress even when extensively deamidated
Authors: Collin Sroge, Vanessa Encinas, Zane G Long, Rayhan Fauzi Said Bawahab, Thomas Cao, Amanda Abiad, Rachel W Martin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCrystallins are highly stable, soluble proteins that refract light and maintain transparency in the vertebrate eye lens. They are not replaced after early development, making them an excellent system for studying protein stability and solubility in crowded environments. To better understand the effects of deamidation on these ubiquitous vertebrate crystallins, we investigated a particularly extreme example, a lens protein from the long-lived Antarctic toothfish (Dissostichus mawsoni), γS1 crystallin (DmγS1). This protein remains soluble in the crowded fish lens, maintaining its transparency even at −2°C and at concentrations more than twofold that of humans (nearly 1000 mg/mL) and over a comparable timescale. As the organism ages, crystallins accumulate oxidative damage such as deamidation of Asn and Gln side chains, leading to aggregation and cataract. Previous studies of human γS crystallin (HγS) have shown that extensive deamidation reduces stability and increases aggregation propensity. Here, we present the biophysical characterization of wild-type DmγS1 and variants with three, five, and seven deamidation sites. In sharp contrast to results for human γS-crystallin, increasing the number of deamidations does not significantly change the thermal stability of DmγS1. These proteins are startlingly resistant to thermal denaturation; despite their psychrophilic origin, they have midpoint unfolding temperatures between 56°C and 63°C. Extensive deamidation does make the protein more vulnerable to chemical denaturation as well as aggregation below the unfolding temperature; however, all the variants resist aggregation well above the fish’s physiological temperature. These proteins present a useful model system for aggregation resistance in extreme environments; most studies of protein solubility focus on unusually aggregation-prone proteins, but understanding the underlying biophysics also requires studying extremely soluble proteins.

Why it matters

The vertebrate eye lens uses highly specialized proteins called crystallins to refract light, forming an image on the retina. Unlike most proteins, which are constantly replaced, crystallins have very little turnover after early development. Maintaining healthy vision requires these proteins to remain stable for decades. During aging, crystallins accumulate damage, causing aggregation and eventually cataract. In order to understand this process and how it can be prevented, we study the extremes of protein solubility, in this case a lens protein from an Antarctic fish that remains transparent at low temperature and very high concentration. In contrast to the human protein, the fish crystallin resists thermal unfolding and aggregation even when subjected to multiple modifications.

Introduction

The vertebrate eye lens is a transparent, refractive tissue comprising layers of fiber cells that lose their nuclei and other organelles during development, leaving behind a crowded protein-rich phase characterized by only short-range order (1,2,3). Crystallin proteins are packed to form a dense hydrogel at concentrations greater than 400 mg/mL in humans (4) and approaching 1000 mg/mL in fish (5,6,7). This is remarkable given that these are some of the longest-lived proteins in the body. Due to the low protein turnover in the lens, healthy visual function requires crystallins to last for the lifespan of the organism. Here, we focus on the structural lens protein γS1-crystallin from the Antarctic toothfish, Dissostichus mawsonii (DmγS1). Most vertebrate lens proteins belong to two ubiquitous superfamilies: α (small heat-shock proteins) and βγ (related to calcium ion-binding proteins). The α-crystallins are small heat-shock proteins that act as holdase chaperones, binding to damaged structural proteins and keeping them in solution. Our protein of interest is a γ-crystallin; like other βγ-crystallins, it has a double-Greek-key architecture and is monomeric in its functional state. β- and γ-crystallins are structural proteins whose role is to refract light, focusing images on the retina while maintaining solubility.

In general, vertebrates have several γ-crystallin genes that have diverged after gene duplication, (8,9,10). In humans, γD is the most abundant in the central nucleus of the lens (11), whereas γS is more abundant in the outer cortex (12). Fish have an additional type, γM, which is more closely related to γD and is highly enriched in methionine relative to other crystallins. The high methionine content is thought to contribute to the protein’s refractivity (13,14,15), which is critical in aquatic lenses that must derive all of the optical power from the lens itself, without an air-water interface at the cornea. Like other teleost fish, D. mawsoni has many γ-crystallin paralogs, most of them γM-crystallins (16,17,18). The toothfish lens is resistant to cold cataract down to its freezing point of −12°C; however, the isolated γM-crystallins do undergo liquid phase separation (LLPS) below the physiological temperature (16). The onset temperature can be shifted up or down by about 10°C by adjusting the number of Arg and Lys residues on the surface (19).

Posttranslational modifications (PTMs) are a major cause of age-related cataract in humans. In general, the major deleterious PTMs found in cataractous lenses include C-terminal or N-terminal truncation (20), deamidation, disulfide bonds (21,22), and methionine oxidation (23). Clinically discovered cataract-associated variants are a source of natural experiments that dramatically highlight a few residues that are critical for solubility and lens transparency; however, engineered variants enable testing of specific hypotheses about the biophysical properties of these unique proteins. Because of their relatively high Cys and Met content, γS-crystallins are particularly susceptible to oxidative damage (24). A previous study on human γS-crystallin variants with three, five, seven, and nine specific deamidation sites based on observed deamidation in aged lenses found that although structure was maintained, these variants denature readily under heat stress. Furthermore, increased deamidation promotes first intra- and then intermolecular disulfide bonding in aged samples (25). We also saw evidence that the internal disulfide bond can exchange with the intermolecular one, releasing a soluble monomer, supporting the hypothesis of a chemical role for γS-crystallin as the last line of defense against oxidation in the lens, after the glutathione reservoir of the lens is exhausted. The relevant cysteine residues are strongly conserved in γS- (but not γD-) crystallins across a wide range of chordates (26). A different set of cysteines is strongly conserved in γD- and γC-crystallins, also suggesting the potential for disulfide shuffling and redox buffering functions (27,28).

Deamidation, the conversion of asparagine/glutamine to (iso-) aspartic acid and glutamic acid, is especially prevalent in cataract lenses, both on its own and in conjunction with disulfide cross-links (21,23,29,30,31). Structural changes have been reported for deamidated variants, even when the modified residues are exposed on the surface rather than in the hydrophobic core (32,33,34,35). Deamidation can also make the protein more susceptible to other types of damage, potentially by inducing conformational changes that expose normally buried residues to solvent (25,36,37). Deamidations of γD-crystallin near the interdomain interface are destabilizing (38,39). In contrast, in several other variants, deamidation of a single site did not significantly impact stability or structure (40). The situation for γS-crystallin is somewhat different, with deamidated variants being more susceptible to dimerization and further aggregation (33,34,35). Depending on the position of the mutations, variants with multiple deamidation sites can exhibit more attractive protein-protein interactions than wild-type or the corresponding single variants (25,33,37).

Studies on crystallins from animal models, such as mice (41,42) and zebrafish (43,44), are particularly valuable for understanding cataract formation, because the disease progression generally takes decades in humans. The F9S variant of murine γS is essentially correctly folded; however, it is prone to chemical denaturation via an intermediate wherein the N-terminal domain unfolds before the C-terminal domain (45). Solution-state NMR relaxation dispersion experiments showed that many residues in the N-terminal domain were in conformational exchange with the partially unfolded intermediate, the population of which was estimated to be 1%–2% (46). Although aquatic organisms are subjected to lower ambient oxygen concentrations than terrestrial ones, and water provides some protection from UV exposure, fish are also susceptible to oxidative damage and cataract. Cataract has been investigated in the context of veterinary care for captive aquarium specimens (47) as well as fish farmed for food (48,49). The lenses of wild-caught fish in the waters near Chernobyl and Fukushima showed negligible oxidative damage from ionizing radiation; (50) however, exposure UVB causes loss of transparency in rainbow trout lenses (51,52). The Antarctic toothfish has a complex life cycle where young fish live at the surface and then gradually move into deeper waters as they mature (53). This fish has a UV photoreceptor, suggesting that it is exposed to significant amounts of UV at least early in life (54). Zebrafish have been used as a model organism for investigating normal lens development (18,55) and hereditary cataract (56,57) as well as for screening anticataract compounds (58).

The Antarctic toothfish swims in the Southern Ocean, which is about −2°C year-round. D. mawsoni is an active predator that depends on its eyesight for survival, and it has a relatively long lifespan (≥ 35 years) (59,60). Its lens proteins are therefore resistant to age-related loss of solubility as well as cold cataract, a reversible LLPS that happens at around 19°C in mammalian lenses (61,62). LLPS can also be induced in mammalian crystallins by elevated pressure and the addition of an osmolyte that is abundant in deep-sea fish (63). The toothfish has two homologs of γS-crystallin, a ubiquitous lens protein that is conserved throughout the vertebrate family tree. We chose D. mawsoni γS1-crystallin (abbreviated DmγS1 throughout) because of its similarity to human γS-crystallin, its resistance to cold cataract (16,64,65), its remarkable aggregation resistance at almost 1 g/mL concentrations, and its high thermal stability (despite its psychrophilic origin, its midpoint unfolding temperature is 68.5°C) (66).

Materials and methods

Protein expression and purification

Plasmids containing the cDNA sequence of DmγS1 wild-type (WT) (GenBank, DQ143971.1) genes were purchased from Blue Heron Biotech (Bothell, WA). The subsequent gene products were cloned into pET-28a (+) vectors (Novagen, Darmstadt, Germany). DmγS1 3SV and 5SV were produced by site-directed mutagenesis utilizing protocols for New England Biolabs Q5 High-Fidelity 2X Master Mix. Plasmids for DmγS1 7SV genes were purchased from Twist Bioscience HQ (South San Francisco, CA). DmγS1 WT, 3SV, 5SV, and 7SV were overexpressed in Rosetta Escherichia coli using autoinduction (67). The pellets were then lysed and purified using EconoFit UNOsphere Q Anion Exchange Column (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA) and a HiLoad 16/60 Superdex 75 pg size exclusion chromatography column (Cytvia, MA, USA). The pure fractions were dialyzed against water overnight and lyophilized in 1-mL aliquots, which were then stored at −80°C until use.

Circular dichroism

Lyophilized protein was resuspended in 10 mM NaPO 4 and 150 mM NaCl (pH 7) and sterile-filtered through a 0.22-micron filter. Each sample was diluted to 0.1 mg/mL, and measurements were collected using a Jasco J-810 spectropolarimeter (JASCO, Easton, MD). Readings were performed from 260 nm to 190 nm with a 1-nm bandwidth, 1-s digital integration time, and 50-nm/min scanning speed with eight accumulations.

Differential scanning fluorimetry

Lyophilized protein was resuspended in 10 mM NaPO4, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.05% NaN3 (pH 7.0) to a concentration of 1 mg/mL. Samples were run in triplicate. Each well contained 2.5× SYPRO orange dye and 0.5 mg/mL protein and was sealed with transparent Microseal “B” seals. Samples in the presence of reducing agent contained 0.25 mM DTT. The thermal ramp (25°C–94°C in 0.5°C increments) was performed in a Stratagene Mx3005P PT-PCR (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The samples underwent 140 cycles of temperature increases, with each cycle lasting 30 s. Buffer baselines were subtracted from each value, and each curve was then fitted to a two-state unfolding curve (Eq. (1)) followed by a Boltzmann equation (Eq. 2) using Prism (Graphpad).

Dynamic light scattering

Lyophilized protein was resuspended into 10 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.05% NaN3 (pH 7.0) and sterile filtered through a 0.22-μm filter. Each protein was then diluted to 1 mg/mL. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements were performed using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK). Measurements were taken with a 2-min equilibration step at the beginning followed by triplicate measurements at each half degree from 25°C to 80°C.

Chemical denaturation

Lyophilized protein was resuspended in 20 mM NaPO4 and 300 mM NaCl (pH 6.9) and diluted to 0.075 mg/mL in the presence or absence of excess DTT. Eleven samples of increasing concentrations up to 4 M guanidine hydrochloride (Thomas Scientific, Mt Prospect, IL) in 20 mM NaPO4 and 300 mM NaCl (pH 6.9) were made in a black CELLSTAR 96-Well Polystyrene Cell Culture Microplate (Greiner Bio-One North America, Monroe, North Carolina). The samples were equilibrated for 24 h before acquiring the intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence spectra. The measurements were collected on a F4500 fluorescence spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) using an excitation wavelength of 280 nm and emission wavelengths of 360 nm and 320 nm. Analysis of the fraction unfolded versus concentration of guanidine hydrochloride was performed using the baseline-corrected ratio of 360 nm and 320 nm emission intensities. Each curve was then fitted to a to a two-state unfolding curve (Eq. (1)) followed by a Boltzmann equation (Eq. (2)) using Prism (Graphpad); for three-state unfolding curves, each unfolding transition was fitted separately (Eq. (1)).

Mass spectrometry

Exact mass measurements of intact proteins, as well as LC-MS/MS analysis of protein digests, were performed on a Waters Xevo G2-XS QTOF instrument. The instrument was calibrated using a sodium formate cluster series between 50 and 2000 Da, and active lockmass correction to the m/z of Leu-enkephalin was performed every 15–30 s.

Intact mass measurements of proteins were performed using an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Phenyl VanGuard Pre-column, 130Å, 1.7 μm, 2.1 mm × 5 mm (Waters corp) to desalt proteins before elution into the source. Samples were bound to the column using isocratic 0.1% FA and then eluted using an increasing gradient of acetonitrile. Data were collected in positive mode, within a range of 400–4000 Da, in continuum mode. Multiple scans across the peak were integrated, followed by deconvolution of the charge state series to yield the exact mass of the protein. MaxEnt1 (MassLynx) was used to perform the deconvolution, using a range of 15–25 kDa for the monomer and 35–50 kDa for the dimer, with a resolution of 0.1 Da/channel and uniform Gaussian width at half-height 0.05 Da.

LC-MS/MS analysis of protein digests was performed using an ACQUITY Premier CSH Phenyl-Hexyl 1.7 μ 2.1 × 50 mm (Waters Corp.). Each protein samples was brought to 100 μg, precipitated using 100 mM HCl, 25 mM ZnSO4, 95% acetonitrile, and 5% water, and pelleted. An additional wash step with the solvent was performed before decanting the solvent and resolubilizing the pellet in 250 mM MES, 50 mM EDTA (pH 6), and 250 μM of acrylamide and left at 80°C for 30 min. From there the variants were precipitated and washed two additional times with 100 mM HCl, 25 mM ZnSO4, 95% acetonitrile, and 5% water. The solvent was decanted, and the pellet was dried before adding 250 μg/mL of pepsin, which was left for ∼3 h at 37°C. From there the samples were split into two. MES buffer was added to the digested samples, and either 40 mM TCEP or water (as a control) was also added. Each sample was then heated at 80°C for 30 min. Samples were analyzed using a 30-min gradient of 0.1% FA/ACN (Table 1). Cycles of scans were collected throughout the retention time using fast data directed analysis: 1) a single 0.2-s MS1 scan to identify multiply charged peptide precursor adducts and then 2) two 0.4-s MS2 scans targeting the two most intense peaks of interest, applying a collision energy ramp of 20–40 V. Raw data files were exported and then converted to mzML using MSConvert (Proteowizard) while also removing the lockmass scans. mzML files were then loaded into PEAKS Studio 13 for peptide mapping.

NMR spectroscopy

Lyophilized 15N-DmγS1 was resuspended in 10 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.05% NaN3 (pH 7.0) to 0.5 mM with 10% D2 O and 0.5 mM sodium trimethylsilylpropanesulfonate (DSS) as an internal standard. Experiments were performed on a Bruker AVANCE NEO 800-MHz spectrometer. Proton spectra were collected using the pulse sequence zggpw5 followed by HSQCs acquired with the pulse sequence hsqcfpf3gpphwg. The F2 dimension was referenced directly using DSS, and the F1 dimension was referenced indirectly.

SEC-MALS

Protein was resuspended in 1× PBS buffer from lyophilized stocks at 1 mg/mL. Each protein was run with and without 1 mM DTT. Each sample was separated using a GPC-SEC column (Protein KW-802.5, Shodex), and data were collected using a SEC-MALS instrument (Viscotek SEC-MALS, Malvern) with a UV detector (SPD-20A, Shimadzu), and 100-μL samples were run through the open solvent system in 1× PBS buffer for 20 mins.

Results

Central to the experimental design of this study is the expression and characterization of three deamidated variants of DmγS1, with three (3SV), five (5SV), and seven (7SV) surface deamidations, respectively. The sequence of WT DmγS1, each deamidated variant, and human γS crystallin are shown in Fig. 1, along with a molecular model of WT DmγS1 produced using D-I-Tasser (68). Fig. 1 A shows the model overlaid with the experimental structure of HγS, illustrating the similarity of the folds. Fig. 1 B indicates the location of the mutation sites, which are labeled using the same colors as the arrows indicating them above the sequence (Fig. 1 C). The remaining Asn and Gln residues are shown in Fig. S1, and an SDS-PAGE gel demonstrating purity is shown in Fig. S2. These variants were chosen for comparison to an earlier study of deamidated HγS variants (25), which was itself based on observations of which specific residues of HγS were most prone to deamidation in cataractous lenses (21). In HγS, thermal stability decreased monotonically with increasing number of deamidation sites. Variants with three, five, and seven deamidation sites aggregated at a lower temperature than WT; however, the nine-site variant had approximately the same onset temperature of aggregation, possibly because of the tension between the structural disruption caused by increasing numbers of deamidations and the repulsion caused by adding negative charges. The objective of the current study is to investigate how multiple deamidations impact the homologous protein DmγS1, which shares about 60% sequence identity with HγS. This protein’s provenance from a psychrophilic, aquatic organism might suggest that it is less resistant to oxidative stress than HγS; on the other hand, the extremely crowded environment of the fish lens suggests the opposite hypothesis, that it is selected to be even more resistant to all kinds of disruption. In order to test these hypotheses, we designed variants with three, five, seven, and nine deamidations at the sites corresponding to those in HγS. Solvent accessibility of these sites was verified using VADAR (69). The nine-site variant was not successfully expressed in E. coli and was not pursued further. The others were biophysically characterized in order to measure structural stability and aggregation propensity.

Multiple deamidations do not cause large-scale unfolding of DmγS1-crystallin

In order to investigate the effects of deamidation on the overall protein fold, circular dichroism (CD) was performed at room temperature (Fig. S3). As expected, the spectra are consistent with a primarily β-strand structure, indicated by a minimum around 218 nm, similar to that of HγS. Each variant has a similar overall CD spectrum to WT, indicating that cumulative deamidation does not perturb the overall structure of the protein at room temperature, although subtle differences in the spectra do suggest minor structural differences among variants.

Integrity of the protein folds was also assessed using 1H-15N heteronuclear single quantum coherence nuclear magnetic resonance (HSQC-NMR) spectra of WT, 3SV, 5SV, and 7SV DmγS1, shown overlaid in Fig. 2 and plotted individually in Fig. S4. Comparing the spectra of WT and the variants, the 1H and 15N resonances are spread out over the typical chemical shift range for a folded protein, making it clear that extensive deamidation does not dramatically destabilize the structure. All the proteins are folded, and for WT, 3SV, and 5SV, the only major changes appear to be associated with the mutation sites. However, more widespread chemical shift differences indicate fairly extensive structural differences between 7SV and the others. Although full resonance assignments for these proteins are not yet complete, some side chains can be identified by their characteristic chemical shifts. The red box highlights the differences in the number of Asn and Gln side chains, indicating the deamidation of 3SV, 5SV, and 7SV DmγS1. The shift in 7SV DmγS1 Trp side-chain resonances (blue box) indicates subtle changes to the protein core, mostly likely in the N-terminal domain near the Cys loop. This chemical shift perturbation is not observed in the rest of the variants.

Unfolding was measured using the intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence, which is a sensitive probe of γ-crystallin structure (70). Dm γS1-crystallin and its variants form an intramolecular disulfide bond in the cysteine loop

Mass spectrometry was used to determine whether DmγS1 and its deamidated variants form disulfide bonds. Both intramolecular and intermolecular disulfide bonding can occur in HγS crystallin, and HγS variants with more deamidation sites have a higher propensity to undergo disulfide shuffling (25,36). HγS crystallin has a feature called the “cysteine loop” (labeled in Fig. 1) where there are three cysteine residues (C23, C25, and C27) in close proximity. In extensively deamidated HγS variants, the outer cysteines in the loop (C23 and C27) can undergo intramolecular disulfide bonding, whereas the middle reside (C25) can undergo intermolecular disulfide bonding. In DmγS1, only two of those cysteines (C21 and C25) are conserved, leading us to hypothesize that only the intramolecular disulfide bond forms. We used mass spectrometry to test this hypothesis.

We measured the intact mass of each protein in the presence and absence of reducing agent to identify the number of disulfide bonds. Addition of reducing agent resulted in a 2-Da shift in WT and 3SV and a 1-Da shift in 5SV and 7SV, as shown in Fig. S6, suggesting that one disulfide bond is formed in each variant. The 1-Da shift in 5SV and 7SV is within the practical tolerance of this measurement and is interpreted as the same intramolecular disulfide bond; however, other explanations including spontaneous deamidation at an additional site or a mixed population of oxidized and reduced proteins are not ruled out by this observation. The dimer mass is also observed in these data, indicating a mixed population (Fig. S6). Previous work on redox reactions of human hemoglobin variants has also seen this enigmatic 1-Da mass shift, which the investigators showed to be a disulfide linked dimer (71). For each variant, both monomers and dimers were also observed using SEC-MALS (Fig. S7). In each case, the relative population of dimer decreased upon addition of reducing agent. In order to detect which specific Cys residues are involved in intramolecular disulfide bonding, the free cysteines were capped using acrylamide, digested with pepsin, and finally reduced, meaning any uncapped cysteine was formerly in a disulfide bond. The results of this digest, shown in Fig. 4, Table S1, and Fig. S8, provide evidence that C21 and C25 (the outer residues of the cysteine loop) in each variant form an intramolecular disulfide bond, consistent with the intact mass data. This is consistent with previous work on HγS where the homologous C23 and C27 undergo intramolecular disulfide bonding, whereas HγS C25 (which is absent in DmγS1) forms an intermolecular disulfide bond.

Dm γS-crystallin and its deamidated variants resist thermal unfolding and aggregation

Thermal denaturation of proteins differs from chemical denaturation in the details of the process as well as the unfolded state produced. Thermal unfolding often results in the formation of insoluble aggregates and is therefore frequently irreversible, precluding the calculation of thermodynamic quantities. However, the midpoint of the unfolding transition (Tm) is a useful indicator of protein stability, particularly when thermal aggregation is also measured. Comparison of the two enables estimation of the onset temperature of local transient unfolding that leads to formation of multimers, which often occurs well below the actual unfolding temperature (72). Thermal stability of WT DmγS1 and its deamidated variants was measured in the presence and absence of a reducing agent (DTT), using differential scanning fluorometry (Fig. S9). The midpoint unfolding temperatures (Tm) are compiled in Table 3. In the absence of DTT, all of the variants have similar Tm values, between approximately 57°C and 60°C. Addition of DTT stabilizes all the proteins slightly, with an average increase of about 2°C in Tm for each of them. The small but consistent increase in the Tm of the reduced forms suggests that disulfide bonding may negatively impact stability by stabilizing a nonnative conformation, a phenomenon that has previously been observed in cataract-related variants of human γD-crystallin (73).

Thermal unfolding and thermal aggregation differ as many proteins form intermolecular contacts due to local, transient unfolding well below their unfolding temperature. We used DLS to measure the average particle size as a function of temperature. Fig. 5 shows the onset temperature of aggregation (Tagg) for each variant in the absence of reducing agent, Fig. 5 A, and with 1 mM DTT, Fig. 5 B. We define Tagg as the temperature where each protein forms large, strongly scattering aggregates of approximately 100-nm diameter. Whether reducing agent is added or not, differences in Tagg among the variants are small (the largest is the difference between WT and 7SV in the absence of reducing agent, which is 7°C) and do not vary monotonically with increasing deamidation. All proteins are more aggregation prone in the absence of DTT (with increases in Tagg in the range of 3°C–5°C), supporting the hypothesis that the disulfide bond locks the protein in an unfavorable conformation (Table 3). 7SV is the most aggregation resistant, followed by 5SV, WT, and 3SV. This pattern does not follow that of the thermal or chemical stability, consistent with previous observations that aggregation propensity and stability are not necessarily directly correlated (72,74). When exposed to reducing agent, the order of aggregation onset does not change, with 7SV still being the most aggregation resistant followed by 5SV, then WT, and finally 3SV. We hypothesize that the aggregation propensity is controlled by a balance of transiently unfolded states exposing otherwise buried parts of the protein and the increase of electrostatic repulsion upon addition of more negative surface charge.

The addition of 1 mM DTT enhances each variant’s aggregation resistance without changing their order. Error bars show standard deviation based on three replicates for each measurement.

Discussion

In general, γS-crystallins are highly resistant to cold cataract (75). For example, the theoretical onset of LLPS for bovine γS-crystallin is −28°C, which is experimentally inaccessible in practice because even concentrated protein solutions freeze at a higher temperature (76). For most folded proteins, cold denaturation, the lower point at which the protein stability curve crosses the zero point of free energy, causing protein unfolding, likewise occurs well below the freezing point of the solution in the absence of elevated pressure or added denaturants (77), and in our experience the toothfish γ-crystallins are no exception. We chose to investigate heat tolerance in this psychrophilic protein partly because of our previous observation that its Tm is 68.5°C (66), only 10°C below the value for human γS-crystallin despite the much larger difference in physiological temperatures (−2°C for the toothfish vs. 37°C for the human). This led to the hypothesis that thermal stability may provide insight into aggregation resistance more generally.

Deamidation is a deleterious posttranslational modification that is often found in cataractous lenses and has previously been shown to induce unfolding and aggregation of structural crystallins. In HγS crystallin, in particular, deamidation increased the propensity for the protein to undergo intra- and (in some cases) intermolecular disulfide bonding. Here, we investigated the effects of deamidation in a model system whose crystallins are able to maintain stability at concentrations near 1 g/mL and at temperatures near freezing. Initially, we hypothesized that increasing the number of deamidation sites would decrease the stability and increase aggregation propensity of DmγS1. Based on the experimental data presented here, that is not the case. Deamidation is not straightforwardly correlated with instability and aggregation propensity. However, deamidation does appear to have some subtle impacts on the structure and stability of these proteins.

All the proteins examined here undergo two-state unfolding when exposed to GdnHCl under reducing conditions (excess DTT). In contrast, 5SV and 7SV exhibit apparent three-state unfolding in the absence of reducing agent, whereas WT and 3SV undergo the same two-state unfolding. We hypothesize that under oxidizing conditions the 5SV and 7SV variants undergo a transiently stable structural rearrangement that allows for the proteins to stabilize before completely unfolding, consistent with results previously observed for other γ-crystallins. Previous studies of human γ-crystallins have shown that the N-terminal domain is less stable than the C-terminal domain (78), and that the interdomain interface is critical for maintaining stability. In human γD-crystallin, interactions among specific hydrophobic residues hold the interdomain interface together: mutating any of these to alanine leads to a destabilized protein that unfolds via an intermediate where the C-terminal domain is intact, but the N-terminal domain is unfolded (32). Several cataract-related variants of human γD can adopt a conformation where the N-terminal domain is partially unfolded, even under native conditions (79). The zebrafish γM7-crystallin (43) and the Opj variant of mouse γS-crystallin (46) likewise undergo at least partial unfolding of the N-terminal domain along the way to complete unfolding. Based on these results and the locations of the deamidation sites, we hypothesize that the primary reason for three-state unfolding in 5SV and 7SV but not WT or 3SV is the addition of a second negatively charged residue to the interdomain interface. Of the three deamidated sites in 3SV, one is in the N-terminal domain (N12), one is in the second Greek-key motif of the C-terminal domain (N118), and the third is in the C-terminal domain at the interdomain interface (N141). In the 5SV, two additional negative charges are added, one of which is also in the interdomain interface (N51), in close proximity to N141. Comparison with the existing results on other γ-crystallins suggests that this disruption to the interdomain interface may be sufficient to destabilize the N-terminal domain, causing it to unfold first. Alternatively, it may be that the apparent three-state unfolding is due to a mixture of protein states, where the oxidized protein fully unfolds at a lower concentration of GdnHCl than the reduced one. Full characterization of the temperature-dependent dynamics of these proteins will be a topic of future investigation via NMR spectroscopy.

As in aged HγS, an intramolecular disulfide bond is present in the cysteine loop of DmγS1, between C21 and C25. Unlike in the human protein, this disulfide bond does not appear to be correlated with increasing deamidation and is observed via mass spectrometry in all variants examined here. Formation of intermolecular disulfide bonds could potentially form with increased oxidation, but we do not see direct evidence of them here, and this protein lacks the cysteine residue most commonly involved in intermolecular disulfide bonds in HγS (residue 23 is a Thr). Deamidation does not dramatically change the overall structure of DmγS1, as measured by CD and solution-state NMR. The differences in thermal stability are very small and not monotonic with respect the number of deamidations. However, all variants are stabilized by the addition of DTT, consistent with the idea that the disulfide bond is an example of deleterious oxidative damage rather than a stabilizing feature. The details of this unfolding pathway will be a topic of future investigation via H-D exchange. Although all of these proteins are highly resistant to thermal aggregation, 7SV is the most aggregation-resistant variant, with a Tagg higher than WT. Each variant is more aggregation resistant when DTT is added. 7SV being the most aggregation resistant may be due to the overall charge state of the protein; the addition of seven negative charges on the surface appears to produce enough electrostatic repulsion to counteract any attractive interactions caused by the mild structural disruption induced by deamidation. Further studies of these proteins will focus on understanding the effects of deamidation and disulfide bond formation on DmγS1 deamidation variants using solution-state NMR and mass spectrometry.
