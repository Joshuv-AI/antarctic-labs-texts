Color Doppler ultrasound study: assessing orbital hemodynamics and vascular changes in mucosal lichen planus
Authors: Abbas Mohammadi, Nader Pazyar, Mohamad Ghasem Hanafi, Samaneh Azhineh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveLichen planus (LP) is a chronic inflammatory condition that can have systemic implications, including effects on vascular health. This exploratory study aimed to evaluate orbital hemodynamics and vascular changes in patients with mucosal LP using color Doppler ultrasound (CDU).ResultsThis retrospective case‑control study enrolled 25 mucosal LP patients along with 30 age‑ and gender‑matched subjects. CDU was used to evaluate the resistive index (RI), pulsatility index (PI), peak systolic velocity (PSV), and end‑diastolic velocity (EDV) in the ophthalmic artery (OA), short posterior ciliary arteries (PCAs), and central retinal artery (CRA). The severity of mucosal LP was assessed with the White Erosive Atrophic Modified Scoring System (WEA‑MOD). All patients underwent complete ophthalmic examination as well as determination of choroidal thickness (CT) using a high‑speed, high‑resolution spectral‑domain optical coherence tomography (SD‑OCT) device. Doppler ultrasonographic measurements revealed significant differences in multiple hemodynamic parameters between LP patients and controls associated with the PCA (p < 0.05). Notable differences were observed in parameters such as OA‑RI, OA‑PI, CRA‑EDV, CRA‑RI, CRA‑PI, and PCA‑PSV (all p < 0.05), with significant variations correlating with different LP severity scores (p = 0.021). Although CT measurements did not differ significantly between the two groups overall, notable variations were observed when assessing CT across different levels of LP severity. CT measurements were significantly lower in the LP group, and significant variations were observed when assessing CT across different levels of LP severity (p < 0.001). These associational findings suggest that mucosal LP severity is associated with altered orbital hemodynamics. These findings raise the possibility that color Doppler ultrasound might serve as a non‑invasive tool to identify patients at higher risk of subclinical ocular vascular changes. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether these hemodynamic alterations predict future ocular complications and whether they warrant monitoring or adjunctive vascular‑protective strategies. Due to the retrospective and cross‑sectional design, causality cannot be inferred. This hypothesis‑generating study underscores the need for larger, longitudinal investigations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13104-026-07942-z.Keywords: Lichen planus, Orbital hemodynamics, Color Doppler ultrasound, Systemic vascular implications, Choroidal thickness

Introduction

Lichen planus (LP) is a chronic inflammatory disease affecting approximately 1–2% of the population, with higher prevalence in adults aged 30–60 years [1]. While primarily involving skin and mucous membranes, it can also lead to ocular complications [2, 3]. Ocular involvement—including conjunctival LP, dry eye syndrome, and anterior segment inflammation—can impair comfort and vision, affecting quality of life [2–4].

LP activity correlates with systemic inflammatory markers that may influence vascular dynamics [5]. Inflammatory processes disrupt normal blood flow homeostasis, altering tissue perfusion and potentially leading to pathological edema [5, 6]. Retrobulbar hemodynamics—blood flow in retrobulbar vessels—may be particularly relevant in LP patients, given chronic inflammation’s effects on systemic and localized vascular responses [7].

Color Doppler ultrasound (CDU) is a non‑invasive tool for assessing critical hemodynamic parameters, including peak systolic velocity (PSV), end‑diastolic velocity (EDV), resistive index (RI), and pulsatility index (PI) in retrobulbar vessels [8]. CDU has been widely used to evaluate ocular blood flow abnormalities in retinal disorders and glaucoma [9–11]. Active inflammatory states significantly influence retrobulbar blood flow, with increased PSV and altered RI values reported in other inflammatory conditions, indicating heightened vascular resistance and blood flow adjustments [8, 12–15]. However, the specific influence of LP activity on retrobulbar hemodynamics remains largely unexplored.

This exploratory study aimed to elucidate the relationship between mucosal LP clinical activity and retrobulbar hemodynamic changes using CDU. By analyzing retrobulbar blood flow parameters, we sought to identify correlations that could clarify how systemic inflammatory processes affect ocular health, ultimately contributing to better management strategies for affected individuals.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective case‑control study complied with the Medical Ethics Committee of Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences (Approval Code: IR.AJUMS.HGOLESTAN.REC.1403.163) and adhered to the 2013 Declaration of Helsinki. The study included 25 mucosal LP patients and 30 age‑ and sex‑matched healthy controls recruited from the dermatology outpatient clinic at Imam Khomeini Hospital, Ahvaz, Iran, between April 2024 and June 2024.

Inclusion criteria for LP patients: age ≥ 18 years, clinical LP diagnosis confirmed by a dermatologist, and active mucosal lesions. Healthy controls had no history of skin, ocular, or systemic diseases and were not on medications. All participants had BCVA ≥ 20/20 and a refractive error within ± 1 D spherical equivalent.

Exclusion criteria for both groups: previous ultraviolet phototherapy; systemic corticosteroids, acitretin, cyclosporine, or biological agents; pregnancy; chronic endocrine, cardiovascular, hematological, hepatic, or renal diseases; history of retinal disease, smoking, or alcohol consumption; IOP > 21 mmHg or glaucomatous findings; previous intraocular surgery, laser therapy, contact lens use, or meibomian gland dysfunction; strabismus, ocular trauma, amblyopia, cataract, or ongoing ocular inflammation.

Assessment of mucosal LP activity

Mucosal LP activity was evaluated using the White Erosive Atrophic Modified Scoring System (WEA‑MOD) based on lesion characteristics: score 0 (normal), 1 (white striae only), 2 (striae + atrophic areas < 1 cm²), 3 (striae + atrophic areas > 1 cm²), 4 (striae + erosive areas < 1 cm²), and 5 (striae + erosive areas > 1 cm²) [16]. All evaluations were performed by a dermatologist specializing in LP.

Color Doppler ultrasound procedure

Examinations used a Samsung ultrasound system (Samsung Medison, Seoul, South Korea) with a 7–12 MHz linear probe. Participants were supine; conductive gel was applied to the closed eyelid. Retrobulbar vessels assessed: central retinal artery (CRA), short posterior ciliary arteries (PCAs), and ophthalmic artery (OA). Spectral Doppler imaging obtained PSV, EDV, RI (PSV – EDV/PSV), and PI (PSV – EDV/mean velocity) [8]. Each measurement was performed three times, and the mean value was recorded. A representative image of the short posterior ciliary artery (PCA) obtained with this system is shown in Fig. 1.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) protocol

Comprehensive ophthalmologic evaluation included BCVA, IOP, and choroidal thickness (CT) measurement using high‑resolution spectral‑domain OCT (Heidelberg Spectralis, Heidelberg Engineering, Germany) with enhanced depth imaging (EDI). CT was measured vertically from the retinal pigment epithelium to the chorioscleral interface at the foveal center. Images with signal strength ≥ 25 dB and clear chorioscleral interface were included; three independent measurements were averaged. Axial length was not measured, so CT values are presented unadjusted.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using independent-samples t tests or one-way ANOVA; non-normally distributed variables are reported as median (interquartile range) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis tests. Correlations with WEA-MOD scores used Pearson or Spearman coefficients as appropriate.

Because of the exploratory design and limited sample size, no multivariable adjustment was performed. P values were interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating; p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons used Welch’s t-test and are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Results

Study population characteristics

The study enrolled 25 patients with clinically active LP and 30 age‑ and gender‑matched healthy controls. Demographic and baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. No significant differences were observed between groups for age (p = 0.641), gender (p = 0.781), BCVA, or IOP (p = 0.759). Within the LP cohort, mean disease duration was 28.1 ± 36.2 months. Nail involvement was present in 9 patients (36%), genital involvement in 3 (12%, all male), and a family history of LP in 1 patient (4%). None of the 25 LP patients had clinical evidence of ocular involvement on comprehensive ophthalmic examination. No participant in either group had a history of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, or cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease per exclusion criteria.

Orbital hemodynamics and choroidal thickness: LP patients vs. controls

A comprehensive comparison is presented in Table 2. CT was lower in the LP group but did not reach statistical significance (256.43 ± 37.22 μm vs. 312.04 ± 22.51 μm, p = 0.122). CDU revealed significant retrobulbar blood flow alterations in LP patients. Compared to controls, LP patients demonstrated significantly higher RI and PI in the OA (OA‑RI: 0.768 vs. 0.714, p < 0.001; OA‑PI: 1.846 vs. 1.507, p < 0.001), CRA (CRA‑RI: 0.727 vs. 0.636, p < 0.001; CRA‑PI: 1.379 vs. 1.059, p < 0.001), and PCAs (PCA‑RI: 0.712 vs. 0.589, p < 0.001; PCA‑PI: 1.315 vs. 0.947, p < 0.001). PSV was significantly reduced in the CRA (8.45 vs. 12.12 cm/s, p < 0.001) and PCAs (10.46 vs. 18.71 cm/s, p < 0.001), and PCA‑EDV was also reduced (2.90 vs. 5.97 cm/s, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Association with mucosal disease severity

Patients were stratified by WEA‑MOD scores into severity groups: score 2 (n = 6), score 3 (n = 10), and score 4 (n = 9). The distribution of parameters across severity strata is shown in Table 3, with ANOVA results in Table 4. CT varied significantly across groups (p < 0.001), with the lowest mean values observed in higher severity groups. Hemodynamic indices of vascular resistance showed clear increasing trends with greater severity. Specifically, OA‑RI, OA‑PI, CRA‑RI, and CRA‑PI all demonstrated significant variation across severity groups (p = 0.005, p = 0.009, p = 0.011, and p = 0.005, respectively). CRA‑EDV decreased with increasing severity (p = 0.001), and PCA‑PSV also varied significantly (p = 0.012, Fig. 3).

Post‑hoc pairwise comparisons (Supplementary Table S1) revealed that for CT, score 2 differed significantly from both score 3 (mean difference + 64.96 μm, 95% CI 30.02–99.90, p = 0.002) and score 4 (mean difference + 62.54 μm, 95% CI 27.59–97.49, p = 0.003), while scores 3 and 4 were not significantly different (p = 0.80). For OA‑RI, significant differences were observed between score 2 and score 3 (mean difference − 0.077, 95% CI − 0.126 to − 0.028, p = 0.006) and between score 2 and score 4 (mean difference − 0.096, 95% CI − 0.151 to − 0.041, p = 0.003), but not between scores 3 and 4 (p = 0.44). Similar patterns were observed for OA‑PI, CRA‑RI, CRA‑PI, and CRA‑EDV, with score 2 consistently showing lower vascular resistance compared to both higher severity groups. For PCA‑PSV, score 2 was significantly higher than score 3 (mean difference + 4.25 cm/s, 95% CI 1.73–6.77, p = 0.005) and borderline higher than score 4 (mean difference + 2.33 cm/s, 95% CI 0.00–4.66, p = 0.050), with no difference between scores 3 and 4 (p = 0.12). A correlation heatmap (Fig. 2) illustrates relationships between all parameters, disease duration, and severity scores. Discussion

In this retrospective case‑control study, CDU revealed significant associations between mucosal LP activity and altered retrobulbar blood flow, specifically increased RI and PI in the OA, CRA, and short PCAs (Table 2) — established indicators of downstream vascular resistance [8]. These findings align with CDU studies in other systemic inflammatory diseases [17–19]. Patients with systemic lupus erythematosus and rheumatoid arthritis similarly demonstrate increased RIs in the CRA and PCAs, correlating with disease activity [13, 20–23]. This parallel suggests a common pathway where systemic inflammation is associated with elevated orbital vascular resistance, possibly via endothelial dysfunction and microvascular remodeling [18, 19].

A key finding is the correlation between these hemodynamic changes and clinical severity of mucosal LP graded by WEA‑MOD score. Parameters such as OA‑RI, OA‑PI, CRA‑EDV, CRA‑RI, CRA‑PI, and PCA‑PSV varied significantly across severity strata (Table 4). Higher severity scores were associated with greater vascular resistance, supporting the concept that the orbital vascular bed is associated with systemic inflammatory burden in LP. This is analogous to observations in Takayasu arteritis, where retrobulbar hemodynamic alterations correlate with disease duration and activity [24].

Our findings are partially consistent with El‑Sadek et al., who reported choroidal thinning and retinal vascular changes in LP patients using OCT angiography [25]; however, they did not assess retrobulbar Doppler parameters. Together, these studies support the concept that LP may be associated with ocular vascular changes. None of our LP patients exhibited clinical ocular involvement, suggesting that observed hemodynamic alterations likely reflect systemic inflammatory effects rather than local ocular inflammation.

The average LP duration was 28.1 ± 36.2 months, and our results revealed significant differences between LP and control groups, with strong associations between disease activity, hemodynamic parameters, and choroidal thickness. Patients with severity score 3 exhibited elevated PCA‑RI, while those with score 4 showed longer disease duration, suggesting possible chronic vascular alterations in advanced LP. Although between‑group CT did not differ significantly, within‑group severity analysis showed CT varied significantly with LP activity score, indicating choroidal changes may relate more to inflammatory activity than to disease presence alone.

Previous research on posterior segment structures in psoriasis has yielded inconsistent results [25–28]. Kılıç et al. reported a positive correlation between disease duration and CT, while Türkcü et al. observed significantly increased CT in psoriasis patients [26], and Ersan et al. documented choroidal changes without macular alterations [28]. Recent research demonstrates that LP patients exhibit notable retinal vascular and choroidal alterations, indicating systemic vascular involvement beyond skin manifestations [28].

Beyond CDU, emerging non‑invasive modalities such as Laser Speckle Flowgraphy (LSFG) offer the ability to assess relative blood flow velocity in the optic nerve head and choroid [29–31]. Future investigations incorporating LSFG alongside CDU could provide a more comprehensive picture of orbital and ocular hemodynamics and clarify whether vascular changes precede or follow disease flares.

While we attribute elevated RI and PI to LP‑related inflammation, we cannot exclude undiagnosed hypertension, subclinical atherosclerosis, or inter‑observer variability. Moreover, our control group was not screened for subclinical inflammatory conditions. At present, these findings are exploratory and do not support routine orbital Doppler ultrasound in all LP patients. However, they suggest that patients with high WEA‑MOD scores (≥ 3) might have subclinical ocular vascular changes. Future longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether such changes predict ocular complications.

Limitations

Sample size: The relatively small cohort (n = 25 LP patients) limits generalizability and statistical power, particularly for subgroup analyses based on WEA-MOD severity scores (scores 2, 3, and 4; group sizes 6–10). This increases the risk of both Type I (false positive) and Type II (false negative) errors. Therefore, subgroup correlations must be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Conclusion

This exploratory study reveals significant associations between mucosal LP activity and altered orbital hemodynamics, with increased resistive and pulsatility indices across retrobulbar vessels correlating with disease severity. These findings suggest an association between systemic inflammation and ocular vascular resistance. However, due to the retrospective cross‑sectional design, results should be interpreted as associational, not causal. Larger longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal relationships and mechanisms. Nevertheless, monitoring vascular parameters in severe LP may be considered to optimize patient care.
