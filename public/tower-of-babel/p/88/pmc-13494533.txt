Caution Against Uncritical Adoption of Neoadjuvant Chemoimmunotherapy Followed by Definitive Radiotherapy in Stages II to III NSCLC: In Response to Adib et al
Authors: Steven J. Feigenberg, Cole Friedes, Jeffrey D. Bradley
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

We read with interest the multicenter retrospective study by Adib et al.1 This is among the first published reports characterizing outcomes of definitive radiation therapy (RT) after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (nCIO) in patients with stages II and III NSCLC who did not proceed to resection. We commend the authors for addressing this clinically important and growing population; however, several points warrant careful consideration before broad adoption of this strategy.

First, the real-world rate of surgical dropout after nCIO substantially exceeds that reported in pivotal phase III trials. In our single-institution series, 42% of patients treated with nCIO did not proceed to surgery.2 From our experience, we learned the following two valuable points: (1) all patients considered a pneumonectomy candidate upfront did not proceed to surgery and (2) patients who were not evaluated by a thoracic surgeon before the start of nCIO were at a substantially higher risk of surgical abandonment. The SAKK pooled trial analysis found that 20% of patients who began neoadjuvant treatment did not undergo definitive surgery and that three-year overall survival (OS) was markedly inferior among nonoperative patients.3 These data underscore a critical gap: when surgery does not occur, patients are exposed to the toxicity and treatment delays of nCIO without the locoregional therapy for which the regimen was designed. Adib et al.1 describe exactly this population, and their results should not be interpreted as validation of a planned nCIO-followed-by-RT strategy. Notably, the true denominator—the total number of patients who initiated nCIO with surgical intent at these institutions—is not reported, precluding estimation of the institutional surgical dropout rate. Furthermore, patients who fail to proceed to surgery after nCIO represent a biologically adverse subgroup selected by disease progression, toxicity, or functional decline, and outcomes achieved with definitive RT in this population should not be expected to mirror those reported in surgical series. Comparing survival in this cohort with historical benchmarks from resected patients risks obscuring the inherent selection disadvantage these patients carry from the outset.

Second, it is often stated at multidisciplinary tumor boards, “Is there any harm in starting with a few cycles of chemoimmunotherapy?” The present study suggests that the answer is yes. Concurrent chemoradiotherapy could not be delivered in 30% of patients, raising concern that nCIO compromises and deconditions rather than preserves performance status. The median interval from the last dose of systemic therapy to definitive RT exceeded 7 weeks, and treatment delays of this magnitude have been associated with negative oncologic outcomes in LA-NSCLC.4

Third, the pneumonitis signal warrants explicit emphasis. When the PACIFIC regimen was adopted, the relative increase in grade 2 pneumonitis increased by 50%, yet the grade more than or equal to 3 rate remained low at 3.4%.5 Concerningly, the current series, despite receiving less intensive concurrent chemoradiotherapy and achieving favorable radiation dose volume metrics, demonstrated a grade more than or equal to 3 pneumonitis rate exceeding 10%. This is consistent with prior reports demonstrating increases in pneumonitis, including fatal cases, when immunotherapy was administered before radiation, an effect associated with worse progression-free survival and OS.6 Adopting nCIO before definitive RT without rigorous patient selection and pulmonary function monitoring risks converting a potentially curable patient into one with treatment-limiting toxicity before definitive local therapy can be delivered.

We commend the authors for adding this important paper to the literature. However, we caution against uncritical adoption of this approach across institutions. The parallel story to induction chemotherapy in head and neck cancer is instructive. In the mid-2000s, early phase data of induction docetaxel, cisplatin, and fluorouracil improved outcomes than cisplatin, and fluorouracil alone before concurrent chemoradiation, generating enthusiasm for the sequential approach. Two subsequent phase III trials, PARADIGM7 and DeCIDE,8 independently demonstrated no OS benefit to induction therapy, and many patients were exposed to excess toxicity without a survival advantage.

We face an analogous evidentiary gap here: there is no randomized comparison of nCIO followed by definitive RT versus upfront chemoradiotherapy and consolidation immunotherapy. Without this comparator, it is not possible to determine whether patients in the cohort of Adib et al.1 fared better, equivalently, or worse than they would have under the established standard of care. Moving forward, mandatory multidisciplinary surgical evaluation before initiating nCIO should be a minimum requirement. Prospective trials directly comparing definitive chemoradiotherapy with or without nCIO in stage III NSCLC are needed before this strategy is broadly adopted outside of a clinical trial.
