Progression-Free Survival Effect Sizes in Randomized Controlled Trials of Targeted Cancer Therapies with Positive Outcomes
Authors: Wei Zhang, Wen Yan, Mengyun Chen, Robert Peter Gale, Junren Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionImprovement in progression-free survival (PFS) is increasingly used by health authorities for cancer drug approvals.MethodWe analyzed 106 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with positive PFS outcomes published in JAMA Oncology, Lancet Oncology, Journal of Clinical Oncology, and Annals of Oncology during 2020–2023. RCTs were categorized as “targeted” (n = 83) or “conventional” (n = 23) by their interrogated treatment variables. Two measures of PFS effect sizes were analyzed: hazard ratio (HR) and PFS increment.ResultsAmong the RCTs of targeted therapies, the 25th-percentile value (Q1) of HR increased significantly (slope = 0.05 per year; p = 0.02) during the study period whilst Q1 of PFS increment decreased significantly (slope = −9.1 percentage points per year; p = 0.03). Similar trends were not observed among the RCTs of conventional therapies.ConclusionsOur analyses suggest a potential bias in accepting RCTs of targeted therapies despite decreased PFS effect sizes.

Introduction

Health authorities such as the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) increasingly approve new cancer drugs based on data from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with a progression-free survival (PFS) primary endpoint [1–3]. Whether PFS is an appropriate endpoint is controversial because longer PFS rarely translates to longer survival, especially during the postmarketing period, and, furthermore, PFS is subject to potential biases including control arm quality, discordant definitions of PFS, variability in assessment intervals, informative censoring, and mistakes in designation of events and nonevents [3–14].

Results of many RCTs are published in prominent cancer journals and influence guideline development and clinical practice. Increasingly, these publications have PFS as a primary endpoint. We noticed PFS effect sizes were small in several instances of RCTs of targeted therapies. We sought to test our observation.

Methods

We surveyed RCTs with positive outcomes that were published in JAMA Oncology, Lancet Oncology, Journal of Clinical Oncology, and Annals of Oncology during 2020–2023, had a PFS primary endpoint, and reported a hazard ratio (HR) <1 (i.e., subjects in the experimental arm had longer PFS compared with the control arm) with a p value <0.05. (Prespecified threshold for HR or p value was not analyzed in this study.) The PubMed query we used was: {(“Annals of oncology: official journal of the European Society for Medical Oncology”[Journal]) OR (“Journal of clinical oncology: official journal of the American Society of Clinical Oncology”[Journal]) OR (“JAMA Oncology”[Journal]) OR (“The Lancet. Oncology”[Journal])} AND ([cancer] OR [cancers] OR [leukemia] OR [leukaemia] OR [myeloma] OR [sarcoma] OR [lymphoma] OR [osteoma] OR [tumor] OR [tumors] OR [tumour] OR [tumours]) AND (survival[Title/Abstract]) AND (Randomized Controlled Trial[Filter]).

The RCTs were categorized into “targeted” or “conventional” based on the interrogated treatment variable. RCTs of targeted therapies tested the incremental effect of a disease-targeted small molecule and/or monoclonal antibody in the experimental arm relative to the control arm. (The US National Cancer Institute maintains a list of targeted therapy drugs [15]). For example, a trial comparing “abemaciclib plus fulvestrant” versus “fulvestrant only” was classified as “targeted” because the interrogated treatment variable was abemaciclib [16]. In contrast, RCTs of conventional therapies tested the incremental effect of conventional chemotherapy and/or radiation therapy in the experimental relative to control arm. For example, a trial comparing proton craniospinal irradiation versus photon involved-field radiotherapy was classified as “conventional,” as was a trial comparing “pegylated liposomal doxorubicin plus bevacizumab plus carboplatin” versus “gemcitabine plus bevacizumab plus carboplatin” because the interrogated treatment variables were radiotherapy protocol and “pegylated liposomal doxorubicin versus gemcitabine,” respectively [17, 18].

We compared study compositions between years with respect to the following dimensions using the chi-square test (for categorical co-variates) or Kruskal-Wallis test (for ordinal co-variates): (1) the interrogated treatment variable (targeted vs. conventional); (2) cancer type (common [lung, breast, prostate, or colorectal] vs. uncommon); (3) study phase (1/2, 2, 2/3, 3, or 4); (4) sample size; and (5) geographic region(s) (America, Asia, Europe, or ≥2 continents [e.g., including Africa and/or Australia]).

The term “effect size” encompassed 2 measures of PFS effect: HR and PFS increment. PFS increment, defined as the percentage increase in time interval to 50-percent PFS in the experimental arm relative to the control arm, could be calculated only when both therapy arms reached 50-percent PFS.

We quantified the evolution of effect sizes over time using quantile regression (QR). Rather than assuming a uniform mean effect like in least-squares regression, the quantile regression model (constructed using quantreg, version 5.97, in R, version 4.4.0) estimated the heterogeneous effect of publication year at different quantiles (Q1, median [Q2], and Q3) of effect sizes.

Results

A total of 106 studies were included in our analysis (Fig. 1a; online suppl. Table; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553384) [16–121]. Compositions of interrogated treatment variable (p = 0.24), cancer type (p = 0.99), study phase (p = 0.87), sample size (p = 0.74), and geographic region (p = 0.53) were stable during 2020–2023 (Table 1). Rank correlation between HR and p value was 0.63 (95-percent confidence interval, 0.50–0.73; Fig. 1b). Q1 of HR in the targeted therapy studies increased significantly during 2020–2023 (slope = 0.050 per year; p = 0.02; Fig. 1c), whereas there was no significant trend for Q2 (p = 0.27) and Q3 (p = 0.17). Simply put, the largest HR sizes of targeted therapies have become smaller during the study period. In contrast, there was no significant trend of HR in conventional therapy studies (Q1: p = 0.78; Q2: p = 0.80; Q3: p = 0.26; online suppl. Fig).

For 91 (86%) studies including 74 targeted and 17 conventional therapy studies, we were able to calculate PFS increment. Q1 of PFS increment in the targeted therapy studies decreased significantly during 2020–2023 (slope = −9.1 percentage points per year; p = 0.03; Fig. 1d), whereas there was no significant trend for Q2 (p = 0.10) and Q3 (p = 0.40). In other words, the lower bound for PFS increment sizes of published targeted therapies is approaching 0 during the study period. There was no significant trend of PFS increments in the conventional therapy studies (Q1: p = 0.83; Q2: 0.63; Q3: 0.28; online suppl. Fig).

Discussion

When we interrogated published data of RCTs of targeted therapy with a PFS primary endpoint, we found a significant trend of decreased effect sizes even though we only analyzed RCTs with positive outcomes. These data indicate a potential (at least temporary) willingness of oncology journals to publish RCTs with decreased PFS effect sizes, especially for studies of targeted therapies.

There are several potential explanations for our observations. One is that it is difficult to achieve a large effect size with new targeted therapies. Only 29 percent of clinical trials of targeted therapies supporting US FDA approvals during 2015–2022 reported “substantial clinical benefit” based on the European Society of Medical Oncology-Magnitude of Clinical Benefit Scale (ESMO-MCBS) criteria [122]. Moreover, greater than one-half of “preferred” genome targeted cancer drugs endorsed by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines in advanced settings were treatments with high or promising but unproven benefit [123]. A second, plausible explanation is that some reviewers of submitted typescripts and some journal editors are willing to recommend acceptance of a typescript describing a benefit of a targeted therapy even when the PFS effect size is small. Bias favoring new therapies is well documented [122, 124–126].

We acknowledge several limitations of our analyses. First, our study period was limited. Second, our analyses relied exclusively on PFS and did not consider more comprehensive measures of clinical benefit such as ESMO-MCBS [127]. Third, we did not assess the role of control arm quality [6, 13]. Fourth, we included only positive randomized trials, which were a subset of studies that tested better-performing new therapies. This selection criterion did not allow us to assess the temporal trend of negative trials, i.e., those testing worse-performing new therapies. Fifth, given the substantial biological and clinical heterogeneity among cancers, we acknowledge that the observed trends may not be generalizable to every disease setting. Sixth, our analyses combined all targeted therapy studies into one category. Our conclusion might not apply to every targeted therapy if analyzed individually. More nuanced analyses that consider co-variates such as differences in populations, disease stages, therapy goals, drug targets, and modes of action are needed.

In conclusion, publications based on PFS are increasing even though PFS effect sizes are sometimes small. Academic journals, regulatory agencies, and clinical guidelines should carefully consider the justification for using PFS as the clinical endpoint, risk of bias in trial design, conduct, or analysis, and results of confirmatory trials [127–130]. We suggest physicians pay attention not only to p values, which are commonly misunderstood and/or misused, but also to relative and absolute measures of PFS effect sizes when evaluating the value of new cancer therapy, especially a targeted therapy [131–133]. The way trial results are presented – relative versus absolute measures – shapes clinicians’ interpretation and may indirectly influence patient decision-making. It is also important that physicians consider whether clinical trial reports continue to be updated with additional follow-up. In this context, it may be wise to remember the advice of Alexander Pope in his An Essay on Criticism: “Be not the first by whom the new are tried, nor yet the last to lay the old aside.”
