Development and evaluation of a gelatin-integrated wool hydrogel dressing for enhanced wound healing using in vitro and in vivo models
Authors: Zahid Sarwar, Nouf Aleem, Muhammad Sohail Zafar, Faheem Ahmad, Sheraz Ahmad, Muhammad Jawad Aslam, Farooq Azam, Bushra Mushtaq, Arief Cahyanto, Muhammad Amber Fareed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A wool nonwoven reinforced composite hydrogel of gelatin was fabricated, exhibiting enhanced structural integrity for wound dressing applications.•Incorporation of wool reinforcement improved tensile strength, swelling percentage, and exudate absorption, while preserving biocompatibility.•Composite performance was primarily governed by gelatin concentration and wool areal density.•The developed composite hydrogel considerably outperformed controls by achieving 98% wound closure in an animal model in just 20 days.

Introduction

Wound healing is a complex biological phenomenon that consists of four interlinked phases: hemostasis, immune response, cell proliferation, and tissue remodeling, which collectively restore tissue integrity and prevent complications [1,2]. Damaged skin is more prone to bacterial invasion due to the loss of its protective barrier, increasing the risk of persistent infections and severe complications [3,4]. Additionally, excessive bleeding from wounds can lead to morbidity or mortality if not managed effectively [5]. Wound dressings play a vital role in mitigating these risks by protecting the wounds, absorbing exudate, and preventing microbial contamination [6,7]. While traditional dressings have been widely used in wound care, they often fail to provide an optimal healing environment [8]. Their tendency to adhere to the wound bed can cause pain during removal, while inadequate moisture retention may prolong the healing process [9,6].

An ideal wound dressing should not only protect wounds and absorb exudate but also address the limitations of conventional dressings by maintaining an optimal moist environment, being air-permeable, demonstrating sufficient mechanical strength, and being biocompatible [10]. Additionally, it should be non-allergenic, non-toxic, antimicrobial, and easily removable without causing pain [6]. Maintaining this optimal moist environment helps prevent tissue or cell death from dryness and promotes angiogenesis [11]. In this context, the adoption of hydrogels as wound dressings has garnered considerable acclaim as a preferred choice because of their unparalleled capability to emulate the body's natural extracellular matrix, exhibiting elastic behavior like human tissues [12], maintain a moist and nurturing environment [13], absorb exudate from wounds, and create a biological environment that is favorable for cell division, migration, and proliferation [14,15]. These three-dimensional hydrophilic polymeric chain networks can retain substantial amounts of water while preserving their structural integrity, creating a favorable microenvironment for wound healing. Additionally, hydrogels allow for painless scab removal through natural dissolution, enhancing patient comfort [14,9,6].

Hydrogels can be fabricated from both natural and synthetic materials, but natural materials are preferred for biomedical and tissue engineering applications due to their inherent biocompatibility and biodegradability [16]. Among naturally derived materials, gelatin is widely used in wound healing because of its unique intrinsic properties, such as biocompatibility, biodegradability, flexibility, nontoxicity, low antigenicity, and its ability to support cell attachment and proliferation [17,18]. Its high hydrophilicity, affordability, ease of availability, gel-forming and water-retention abilities, and ease of functional modification and crosslinking make it a preferred choice for biomedical applications [19]. Derived from collagen [20], gelatin exists in two forms: Type A (acid hydrolyzed) and Type B (alkali hydrolyzed), both of which support tissue regeneration [21]. Despite its many remarkable features, gelatin generally demonstrates low mechanical strength, is readily soluble in aqueous fluids, and is excessively brittle when fully dried or excessively soft when fully wet [22,23]. To address these limitations, low strength, cross-linking, blending with synthetic polymers, incorporation of nanomaterials, and reinforcement with nonwoven fabric can be used as effective countermeasures [24,25].

Among these techniques devised to fortify the mechanical strength of the gelatin hydrogel, reinforcement with nonwoven fabric is the most useful. The use of pure hydrogels is often inadequate in terms of mechanical strength and elasticity, leading to bursting or swelling at the injured site. Consequently, incorporating a supportive nonwoven textile material is vital to ensure stability and preservation of shape in hydrogel-based wound dressings, thereby enhancing their adhesion, resilience, and permeability throughout the healing process [26,27].

Nonwoven fabrics are engineered by bonding fibers directly into a web-like structure, bypassing the traditional yarn production process. This leads to the creation of lightweight, flexible, porous, and mechanically robust materials, making them highly suitable for biomedical applications. Among various nonwoven options, needle-punched nonwovens stand out for their outstanding mechanical properties and remarkable abrasion resistance, making them highly advantageous as reinforcement for wound dressings [25,26,28].

Wool, a naturally occurring protein-based animal-origin fiber, has gained interest as a reinforcement material in hydrogel-based wound dressings due to its unique properties, including notable elasticity, high fluid and odor absorption, recyclability, flame retardancy, softness, biocompatibility, biodegradability, ease of handling, and temperature-regulating capability. Wool fibers can stretch over 30% without permanent deformation, endure bending up to 20,000 times without breaking, and absorb up to 35% of their weight in water, making them highly suitable for breathable, flexible bandages [29,30]. These exceptional features make wool nonwovens an ideal reinforcement for gelatin hydrogels, particularly for exudating wound dressings.

This work involved developing a gelatin hydrogel-embedded wool fibrous nonwoven composite using the sol-gel technique. The GSM (g/m2) of the wool fiber nonwoven and the gelatin matrix concentrations were varied to investigate their impact on the resulting properties of the dressings. The inherent biocompatibility and biodegradability of both wool and gelatin make this composite an ideal candidate for developing efficient, pain-free, and antimicrobial wound dressings tailored for exudating wounds. The results of this study revealed that the gelatin hydrogel and wool fibrous nonwoven provide a unique combination of comfort and strength for advanced wound care applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

In this study, gelatin (CAS No. 9000–70–8) was purchased from Bio Basic, USA, and glutaraldehyde (CAS No. 111–30–8) from Daejung Chemicals, South Korea. A local textile industry provides wool fibers.

Development of nonwoven fabric

First, raw wool fibers were cleaned with 1.2 g/L washing soda and 0.6 g/L Lissapol solution at 60 °C to remove dust and other impurities. The fibers were then thoroughly rinsed with hot and cold water to eliminate residual chemicals before being air-dried at room temperature. To ensure proper fiber separation, the cleaned fibers were manually opened before nonwoven web formation. A needle-punching sampling machine from Dongwong Roll Co., Ltd., located in Incheon, South Korea, was used to develop the nonwoven web. In this process, the fibers were first fed into the opening preparatory line, then carded and cross-lapped to form the web. The final nonwoven web was produced with a needle-punch penetration of 12 mm. A trio of wool nonwovens with GSM values of 100, 200, and 300 was fabricated.

Development of a nonwoven incorporated composite hydrogel of gelatin

Three homogenized aqueous gelatin solutions (1, 3, and 5 w/v %) were prepared at 40 °C by dissolving the specified amount of gelatin in distilled water. The solutions were stirred on a hot-plate magnetic stirrer at 700 rpm for 4 h to ensure a clear, homogeneous solution. Then, the fabricated non-woven samples were placed in gelatin solution for 40 min at room temperature. The fabric side was flipped after half-time to ensure uniform solution penetration on both sides. After that, the samples were removed from the gelatin solution, allowed to air-dry to remove excess solution, and placed in the glutaraldehyde solution for 30 min to cross-link the gelatin molecular chains. Following that, these nonwoven fabric-reinforced composite hydrogel samples were removed from the crosslinking solution, and they were washed with tap water to remove uncrosslinked molecules from the surface of the composite hydrogel. Afterward, these washed samples were dried at 40 °C for 20 h. The primary mechanism by which aldehydes crosslink with gelatin involves the interaction between the amine groups of lysine or hydroxylysine residues in the gelatin and the terminal aldehyde groups of glutaraldehyde, resulting in the formation of an aldimine or a relatively labile Schiff base [31], as illustrated in Fig. S1. This reaction, known as Schiff base formation, produces an imine compound, a Schiff base [32]. This spontaneous reaction occurs at ambient temperature and pressure without additional catalysts or agents, yielding a fully crosslinked gelatin hydrogel. This crosslinking not only stabilizes the hydrogel's structure but also significantly enhances its mechanical strength. The design for this research was generated using a full factorial design in MINITAB 18 (statistical software). In this design of experiment, three levels of both factors, gelatin concentration and reinforcement GSM, were used. A total of 12 samples were developed as presented in Table S1.

Characterization

Surface morphology

The surface morphology of the neat wool nonwoven fabric, along with wool nonwoven/ gelatin composite hydrogel, was examined by scanning electron microscopy (FEI NOVA SEM). Before SEM analysis, these samples were gold-coated using a sputter coater. The visual analysis of the SEM micrographs revealed the morphological configuration and existence of the hydrogel.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The fabricated samples were analyzed using a Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (Spectrum, Perkin-Elmer, USA) in transmission mode, scanning wavenumbers from 4000 to 600 cm⁻¹. This enabled the identification of specific functional groups and the analysis of the chemical composition.

Differential scanning calorimeter (DSC)

The change in thermal behavior after applying a gelatin hydrogel to the nonwoven wool fabric was investigated using a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC 400, PerkinElmer). The samples were heated at 10 °C/min to 300 °C under an inert nitrogen atmosphere to assess their thermal characteristics.

Swelling behavior

The swelling behavior of the fabricated composite hydrogels was evaluated in phosphate buffer solution (PBS) at pH 7.4 (physiological conditions) by immersing the hydrogel samples in PBS until equilibrium was reached. Before immersion in the liquid, the samples were dried in an oven at 37 °C overnight and weighed on a digital balance to determine their initial weight.

At regular intervals, the immersed samples were removed from the liquid media, carefully wiped with paper tissue to remove excess liquid from their surfaces, weighed, and returned to the liquid media. The following equation [33] was used to compute the percentage of swelling:Swelling(%)=Wf−WiWi×100

Here, Wi is the initial weight, and Wf is the final weight.

Tensile strength

The elongation and tensile strength of the fabricated samples were evaluated using a universal tensile testing machine (LRX Plus). This test was performed in accordance with ASTM D5035–19. Rectangular strips of 152.4 mm by 50.8 mm were cut from hydrogel specimens for this test. At ambient temperature, the samples were uniformly stretched until they fractured. The force at which the specimen ultimately broke was noted as the breaking force (N). For each sample, five measurements were recorded, and the average value was calculated and reported.

Air permeability

The air permeability (AP) of the developed samples was measured using an AP tester (M-021A, SDL Atlas, UK) in accordance with ASTM D737. For this test, the sample was placed on a 20 cm² area, and air was passed through it at a pressure of 100 Pa to measure its air permeability.

Exudate absorbency

The exudate absorbency of composite hydrogel samples was determined by using the standard test method EN 13,726–1:2002. To prepare the exudate solution, 2.298 g of sodium chloride (NaCl) and 0.368 g of calcium chloride (CaCl2) were added to 1 L of water at 37 °C. Before immersion in the prepared aqueous media, the samples were dried in an oven at 40 °C overnight and weighed on a digital balance to determine their dry weight (Wd). Then, these dried samples were placed in the simulated exudate solution at 37 °C for 1 day. After 24 h, the immersed samples were removed from the aqueous medium and carefully hung to air-dry. These samples were then reweighted to determine the final wet weight. The following equation [34] was used to compute the exudate absorbency:Absorbanceofexudate(g/g)=Ww−WdWd×100

In vitro antibacterial efficacy

The agar disk diffusion method was used to assess the antibacterial efficacy of the fabricated composite hydrogel samples against a gram-positive bacterium (S. aureus), widely recognized as a trigger for the transmission of skin and soft tissue diseases. For this investigation, a fresh bacterial strain was prepared in a nutrient broth solution by continuous stirring at 140 rpm for 24 h at 37 °C. The samples were sliced into 15 mm disks, sterilized at a higher temperature, and irradiated with UV light for 40 min before testing. These sterilized samples were then cultured in an incubator for 18 h on a colonized agar culture medium. The agar discs were then used to examine the zone of inhibition, which was quantified using a scale to assess bacterial resistance. This antibacterial activity experiment was performed under complete darkness.

In vivo wound healing

An overview of the experimental design and timeline is presented in Fig. S2. An in vivo animal trial was conducted to assess the extent of wound healing. For the trial, 30 albino rats, 8 weeks old, with an average weight of 150 ± 10 g, were divided into six experimental groups (each group = 5 rats): negative control (healthy animals that received no wound induction or treatment and served as the normal control), positive control (animals with a full-thickness dorsal excisional wound that received no wound dressing or treatment and served as the untreated wound control), Treatment Group I (wounded animals treated with a wool-reinforced hydrogel containing 0% gelatin), Treatment Group II (wounded animals treated with a wool-reinforced hydrogel containing 1% gelatin), Treatment Group III (wounded animals treated with a wool-reinforced hydrogel containing 3% gelatin), and Treatment Group IV (wounded animals treated with a wool-reinforced hydrogel containing 5% gelatin). The experimental animals were acclimatized before the start of the trial; each experimental group was housed in separate cages under constant light-dark cycles at 25 ± 2 °C and a relative humidity of 42–58%. The rats are subjected to the same food and water. The negative control group received only food and water. Before wound induction, the rats were sedated using chloroform fumes in a desiccator and subsequently anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (90 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), in accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020). In the positive control group, a measured skin lesion (2 cm surgical wound) was created on the dorsal abdominal surface without any treatment, as shown in Fig. S3. Treatment group 1–4 received fabricated composite hydrogel samples applied to the wound site.

All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Biosafety and Bioethics Committee (IBC) at the Office of Research, Innovation, and Commercialization, University of Agriculture (D No. 1450/ORIC, October 8, 2024). The study was conducted in strict compliance with the National Biosafety Guidelines 2005, Punjab Biosafety Rules 2014, Punjab Animal Health Act 2019, and the ARRIVE 2.0 bioethics protocols and guidelines.

Hematological samples and digital images of the experimental animals in each group were collected on days 0, 7, 14, and 21. The animals were decapitated on day 21, and hematological samples were obtained to analyze the extent of wound healing through biochemical parameters. The percentage reduction in the size of the induced wound area was determined by using the following formula [35] to calculate wound closure over time.%reductioninthesizeofthewound=Ai−AdAi×100

Here, Ai is the initial area on day 0, and Ad is the area on the measuring day.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using MINITAB 22 software. Parametric statistical methods were employed throughout the study. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons among multiple groups, and Student’s unpaired t-test was used for two-group comparisons. Statistical significance was defined at a 95% confidence level (p ≤ 0.05).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The FTIR transmission spectra of pure nonwoven wool and its composite hydrogel are presented in Fig. 1C. The spectrum of pure wool showed a broad peak between 3392 and 3186 cm⁻¹, peaks at 2920 and 2851 cm⁻¹, a sharp peak at 1622 cm⁻¹, and additional peaks at 1537, 1418, 1253, and 1009 cm⁻¹. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was used to investigate the thermal behavior of the pure wool nonwoven fabric and the gelatin hydrogel composite. The analysis was performed from 0 °C to 300 °C, and the results are shown in Fig. 1D Pure wool shows a broad endothermic peak at approximately 111 °C and another peak at around 237 °C. In the composite sample, the main endothermic peak shifts to approximately 130 °C, and an additional peak is observed near 240 °C. Changes in peak intensity and position are also observed between the two samples.

Evaluation of antibacterial efficiency

The antibacterial activity of pure wool nonwoven fabric and wool-reinforced gelatin hydrogel composites was evaluated using the agar disk diffusion method in accordance with ISO 20,645. Fresh cultures of Staphylococcus aureus were used for the assessment, and the results are presented in Fig. S4. The pure wool nonwoven fabric showed no visible antibacterial activity, as evidenced by bacterial growth surrounding the sample and the absence of an inhibition zone. In contrast, the wool-reinforced gelatin hydrogel composites exhibited a distinct zone of inhibition of approximately 9 mm around the samples. Furthermore, the size of the inhibition zone increased with increasing gelatin matrix concentration.

To further assess the healing response, hematological, biochemical, and oxidative stress parameters were evaluated. Liver function analysis showed significant improvements (p < 0.05) in alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels in the treatment groups compared with the positive control group (Fig. S5).

Renal function analysis revealed significantly lower creatinine, uric acid, and urea levels (p < 0.05) in the treatment groups than in the positive control group (Fig. S6).

Oxidative stress biomarkers were also assessed. Serum catalase and superoxide dismutase (SOD) levels were significantly higher (p < 0.05) in the treatment groups compared with the positive control group (Fig. S7).

Hematological parameters, including red blood cell (RBC) count, hemoglobin (Hb), and white blood cell (WBC) count, are presented in Table S2. The treatment groups showed significantly higher RBC and Hb levels (p < 0.05) than the control groups, whereas WBCs were present in all experimental groups.

Histopathology evaluation

Cutaneous tissue samples were collected and processed for histopathological examination using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Representative histological images are presented in Fig. S8. The positive control group exhibited disruption of the epidermal layer, denatured glandular epithelium, and disruption of the subcutaneous fascia (Fig. S8A). Treatment Groups I and II showed reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, acanthosis in the stratum spinosum, mild inflammatory infiltrates in the upper dermis, and the presence of loose connective tissue within the dermal papilla (Fig. S8B). Treatment Groups III and IV exhibited reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, marked hyperkeratosis in the stratum corneum, restoration of the dermal–epidermal interface, and pronounced cutaneous cell proliferation (Fig. S8C).

Histomorphometric analysis of the H&E-stained wound sections further demonstrated progressive epidermal regeneration in the treated groups. The positive control group exhibited a thin and discontinuous epidermis with an average thickness of 23 ± 4 µm. In contrast, the treatment group with lower gelatin content showed an increased epidermal thickness of 37 ± 6 µm, while the optimized treatment group with the highest gelatin content exhibited the greatest epidermal thickness (58 ± 7 µm), which was significantly higher than that of the control group (p < 0.001). These findings indicate enhanced re-epithelialization and tissue regeneration in wounds treated with the wool–gelatin composite hydrogel. The arrows in Fig. S8 highlight representative regions of re-epithelialization, reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, and improved dermal organization.

Statistical analysis

The results were statistically analyzed using MINITAB 22 to evaluate the impact of varying gelatin concentrations and GSM of the reinforcement material through ANOVA. Table S3 presents the ANOVA results for each input variable and its corresponding influence on the responses. The analysis showed that gelatin concentration significantly affected tensile strength, elongation at break, and exudate absorbency (p < 0.05). However, its effect on air permeability was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). The GSM of the reinforcement significantly influenced air permeability (p < 0.05), whereas its effects on tensile strength, elongation at break, and exudate absorbency were not statistically significant. The coefficient of determination (R²) values obtained for tensile strength, elongation, and air permeability exceeded 90%. In contrast, the R² value for exudate absorbency was 74%.

Discussion

The present study successfully developed a wool-reinforced gelatin hydrogel composite as a potential wound dressing material. The integration of gelatin hydrogel into the wool nonwoven fabric was confirmed through various physicochemical characterizations.

The SEM results demonstrate a clear morphological transformation after incorporation of the gelatin hydrogel into the wool nonwoven structure. The presence of a continuous coating layer over the wool fibers suggests effective penetration and distribution of hydrogel within the fibrous network. The neat wool nonwoven fabric exhibited the typical scale structure of wool fibers and entanglement resulting from the needle punching technique [36]. In contrast, the composite sample showed a uniform and smooth gelatin matrix layer covering the wool fibers, effectively masking the characteristic scales and filling the inter-fiber spaces [25]. This consistent, smooth distribution of the gelatin matrix enhances the composite's structural integrity and indicates successful integration of the hydrogel into the nonwoven wool substrate.

The formation of a gelatin hydrogel on nonwoven wool fabric was confirmed by FTIR, which showed characteristic peak patterns of the associated functional groups. The broad peak observed in pure wool in the 3392–3186 cm⁻¹ region is attributed to overlapping N–H and O–H stretching vibrations [37]. Peaks at 2920 and 2851 cm⁻¹ correspond to C–H stretching vibrations of methylene groups. Additional peaks at 1537, 1418, 1253, and 1009 cm⁻¹ correspond to amide-related vibrations, carboxylate, and S–O functional groups in wool structure [39,40,38]. In the composite hydrogel, the broadening of the peak in the 3200–3550 cm⁻¹ region indicates increased N–H and O–H stretching vibrations. The shift of the carbonyl peak from 1622 cm⁻¹ to 1590 cm⁻¹, along with the appearance of a peak at 1120 cm⁻¹ (C–O stretching), suggests intermolecular interactions such as hydrogen bonding between gelatin and wool keratin [41,42]. The presence of characteristic peaks from both components, combined with reduced intensity of some wool peaks, confirms the successful integration and development of the gelatin hydrogel matrix on the wool nonwoven fabric [43].

The DSC thermograms provide insight into the thermal transitions and interactions within wool and the composite structure. The broad endothermic peak at 111 °C in pure wool is associated with the loss of bound water molecules present in keratin due to its hygroscopic nature. The second peak at 237 °C corresponds to the thermal transition of crystalline α-keratin [37]. In the composite sample, the shift of the main endothermic peak from 111 °C to 130 °C indicates increased thermal stability, which can be attributed to the interaction between wool fibers and the gelatin matrix. The crosslinked gelatin network likely restricts water mobility, requiring higher energy for evaporation. The additional peak observed at 240 °C suggests a gelatin phase transition in the composite system [44]. The increase in peak intensity in the composite further suggests enhanced interaction between wool fibers and the gelatin hydrogel. Overall, the DSC results indicate that incorporating wool nonwoven fabric into the gelatin hydrogel modifies the system's thermal behavior, reflecting intermolecular interactions between the two components.

Understanding the swelling behavior of composite hydrogels is crucial, as it affects their fluid-absorbing capacity, structural integrity, and ability to provide the moist environment essential for wound-healing applications. The increase in swelling with higher gelatin concentration can be attributed to the greater availability of hydrophilic functional groups, which enhance water uptake capacity through hydrogen bonding [45,46]. Conversely, the decrease in swelling as GSM increases is associated with higher fiber density, which reduces porosity and limits water penetration into the fabric structure. A denser and thicker fibrous network restricts fluid diffusion pathways, resulting in lower overall swelling capacity [47,48]. The significantly higher swelling capacity (264% increase) of the composite hydrogel compared to pure wool indicates a strong contribution of the gelatin matrix to fluid absorption. This improved swelling behavior is particularly relevant for wound-healing applications, as high fluid uptake supports hemostasis by absorbing blood and maintaining a moist wound environment. Since blood is predominantly aqueous, effective fluid absorption plays a critical role in controlling bleeding and promoting wound repair processes [49]. Consequently, optimizing GSM and the gelatin matrix concentration are essential to achieve maximum swelling, which helps stop bleeding and expedite wound healing.

Tensile strength measures a material's capacity to withstand the maximum applied force before rupture. In contrast, elongation measures the extent to which a material can stretch before breaking, which is important for composite hydrogels intended for wound-healing applications. So, sufficient strength is a fundamental requisite for it. The improvement in tensile strength with increasing GSM can be attributed to the higher fiber density per unit area, which enhances load-bearing capacity and promotes more uniform stress distribution within the composite structure [50]. A denser fibrous network provides greater mechanical interlocking, thereby improving resistance to applied tensile forces. The increase in tensile strength at higher gelatin concentrations is attributed to enhanced intermolecular interactions between the gelatin matrix and wool fibers. The presence of additional functional groups in gelatin facilitates hydrogen bonding with amino groups present in wool keratin, resulting in improved interfacial adhesion and stronger composite formation [51]. The improved tensile strength results also demonstrate a high degree of compatibility between the nonwoven fabric reinforcement and the hydrogel layer. The elongation at break decreased with increasing GSM and gelatin concentration. This reduction can be attributed to the increased rigidity of the structure caused by higher polymer concentration, which leads to greater crosslinking density and reduced chain mobility [52]. Additionally, higher GSM results in a denser fiber network with more fibers per square meter, limiting the ability of the material to stretch and deform [53].

A material's air permeability is a crucial factor in determining its airflow and, consequently, its applicability for applications that require controlling moisture and ventilation. The air permeability of the reinforcing nonwoven fabric depends on the raw material and the manufacturing technique. The openness or compactness of the fabric's structure, influenced by the fabrication technique, directly correlates with its air permeability, whereas the fabric's thickness is inversely related to it [51]. The pure wool nonwoven fabric showed higher air permeability than the composite hydrogels, primarily due to the formation of a hydrogel layer that acts as a physical barrier to airflow [25]. The decrease in air permeability with increasing GSM is due to higher fiber density per unit area, resulting in a more compact structure with reduced pore size. This densification limits the available pathways for air passage and reduces overall permeability [47]. Similarly, increasing gelatin concentration leads to the formation of a thicker and more compact hydrogel layer, which further obstructs airflow through the composite structure [25]. From a functional perspective, controlled air permeability is essential in wound dressing applications, as adequate oxygen transport supports tissue regeneration and wound healing processes [54]. Therefore, optimizing GSM and hydrogel concentration is critical to balancing moisture retention with sufficient oxygen permeability for effective healing.

Exudate absorbency is a vital feature of wound dressings, as it determines how effectively they manage and retain wound fluid, thereby establishing an optimal healing environment and minimizing the risk of infection. This exudating fluid of wounds can disrupt the cohesion of interconnected tissues, leading to prolonged healing times [55]. Consequently, the absorption of this fluid is essential for supporting wound healing by preventing epidermal dryness, facilitating neovascularization, and stimulating collagen formation. Thus, proper moisture management reduces discomfort, accelerates the healing process, and shields the wounds from infection [56]. The observed decrease in absorbency with increasing GSM can be attributed to the higher fiber density per unit area, which produces a more compact structure with reduced porosity and lower fluid uptake capacity [47]. Conversely, the increase in absorbency with increasing gelatin concentration is associated with the greater availability of hydrophilic functional groups within the hydrogel matrix. The presence of additional hydroxyl (OH) groups enhances the attraction and retention of liquid molecules, resulting in improved absorbency [45,46]. Therefore, the enhanced exudate absorbency observed in composites with higher gelatin content highlights their potential to provide effective moisture management and support favorable wound-healing conditions.

Antibacterial activity is a critical property of wound dressings because it helps prevent microbial colonization and reduces the risk of wound infection, thereby supporting the healing process [57]. Staphylococcus aureus is one of the most common pathogens associated with skin and soft-tissue infections and can significantly delay wound healing [58]. The absence of an inhibition zone around the pure wool nonwoven fabric indicates that wool alone does not possess significant antibacterial activity under the test conditions. In contrast, the clear inhibition zones observed around the composite hydrogel samples demonstrate their ability to suppress bacterial growth. Although the inhibition zone was smaller than those reported for nanoparticle-based antibacterial systems [59,60], the composite hydrogel exhibited measurable antibacterial activity without incorporating additional antimicrobial agents. This antibacterial effect is likely due to the inherent antibacterial characteristics of gelatin [61], further enhanced by crosslinking with glutaraldehyde. The biocidal activity of glutaraldehyde arises from the alkylation of hydroxyl, carbonyl, and amino groups in microbial cells, leading to disruption of DNA, RNA, and protein synthesis [62,63]. The increase in inhibition zone diameter with increasing gelatin concentration is consistent with previous findings reported by Ahmad et al. [25], suggesting that higher hydrogel content enhances antibacterial performance. Therefore, the observed antibacterial activity supports the potential of the wool-reinforced gelatin hydrogel composite as a wound dressing material that reduces bacterial contamination while providing a favorable environment for healing.

The in vivo wound-healing analysis aimed to assess how effectively the composite dressing promoted cellular regeneration. It accelerated the healing process by comparing wound closure rates and wound quality with those of untreated wounds. The accelerated wound closure observed in the treatment groups demonstrates the beneficial effect of the wool-reinforced gelatin hydrogel composite on tissue regeneration. The optimized composite dressing provided a favorable environment for wound repair, resulting in substantially faster healing than that observed in untreated wounds. This enhanced performance can be attributed to the ability of the composite hydrogel to maintain a moist wound environment, which promotes cellular proliferation, minimizes infection risk, and supports efficient tissue remodeling [64].

The gelatin-infused wool keratin hydrogel is also expected to facilitate fast hemostasis, an essential initial phase of wound healing. Gelatin facilitates the adhesion and activation of platelets due to its RGD sequences and superior ability to absorb fluids [65]. Additionally, wool keratin amplifies this effect through its hydrophilic amino acid residues and cell-binding motifs, such as RGD and LDV [66,67]. These characteristics enable rapid blood absorption, accumulation of clotting factors, and early clot formation at the wound site. The observed wound-healing performance is consistent with previous reports on gelatin-based hemostatic hydrogels, which demonstrated effective clot formation and reduced blood loss in both in vitro and in vivo studies [[68], [69], [70], [71], [72]]. Furthermore, recent biomaterial studies have highlighted the importance of surface characteristics and fluid management in promoting rapid hemostasis and wound healing [73].

The biochemical analyses further support the healing efficacy of the developed composite. Improvements in liver function biomarkers (ALT, AST, and ALP) suggest reduced systemic stress and enhanced recovery in treated animals. Similarly, reductions in creatinine, uric acid, and urea levels indicate improved physiological status and normal renal function during the healing process. The elevated catalase and superoxide dismutase levels observed in treated groups indicate enhanced antioxidant defense mechanisms, which may help mitigate oxidative stress associated with tissue injury. Increased RBC and hemoglobin levels further suggest improved oxygen delivery and nutrient transport to regenerating tissues, both of which are essential for effective wound repair. The presence of WBCs across all groups reflects the normal inflammatory and immune responses involved in wound healing.

Histopathological analysis provided clear evidence of improved tissue regeneration in the hydrogel-treated groups. The positive control group exhibited marked tissue disruption, including damage to the epidermis and subcutaneous fascia. In contrast, Treatment Groups III and IV, containing higher gelatin concentrations, demonstrated superior healing characteristics, including reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, acanthosis, hyperkeratosis, restoration of the dermal–epidermal junction, and enhanced tissue regeneration. These histological features were more pronounced than those observed in Treatment Groups I and II, indicating a gelatin concentration-dependent improvement in wound healing. Histomorphometric analysis further supported these observations, with the optimized treatment group exhibiting a significantly greater epidermal thickness (58 ± 7 µm) compared with the positive control (23 ± 4 µm, p < 0.001), demonstrating enhanced re-epithelialization and wound remodeling. These findings are consistent with previous reports on advanced hydrogel wound dressings, which have shown improved tissue regeneration and collagen remodeling following treatment [74]. Overall, the histopathological and histomorphometric findings confirm the beneficial effect of the wool-reinforced gelatin hydrogel in promoting cutaneous wound healing.

Statistical analysis using ANOVA revealed the influence of the independent variables (gelatin concentration and GSM) on the performance of the wool-reinforced gelatin hydrogel composites. An input parameter is deemed to have a significant effect on the response if the probability of the null hypothesis (P-value) is less than or equal to 0.05, indicating 95% confidence. Conversely, if the P-value is greater than 0.05, the effect of the input factor on the output response is considered statistically insignificant [75]. The ANOVA results indicate that gelatin concentration is the primary factor governing tensile strength, elongation at break, and exudate absorbency. This finding is consistent with gelatin's role in regulating hydrogel network formation, intermolecular interactions, and fluid absorption behavior. Conversely, its influence on air permeability was not statistically significant, suggesting that airflow through the composite is controlled predominantly by structural characteristics rather than by matrix concentration. The significant effect of GSM on air permeability highlights the importance of reinforcement density in determining pore structure and airflow resistance. The absence of a significant GSM effect on the remaining responses suggests that these properties are more strongly influenced by matrix composition than by variations in reinforcement density within the investigated range.

The high R² values (>90%) for tensile strength, elongation, and air permeability demonstrate that the selected input variables effectively explain the variability in these responses, indicating a strong predictive capability of the developed statistical models. In contrast, the lower R² value for exudate absorbency (74%) suggests that additional variables not included in the present experimental design may also contribute to the composite hydrogel's absorbency behavior.

Conclusion

This study developed a biocompatible wound dressing by incorporating durable wool nonwovens into a gelatin hydrogel matrix via a sol-gel technique, thereby advancing wound-healing applications. This research examined how the gelatin matrix concentration and wool reinforcement GSM affect the composite's mechanical properties, air permeability, and exudate absorption. Results showed that increasing the gelatin concentration reduced air permeability and elongation at break, while higher GSM (g/m2) of the wool reinforcement reduced both air permeability and elongation at break. Additionally, higher gelatin concentrations improved tensile strength and absorbance, whereas higher GSM (g/m2) increased tensile strength but reduced absorbance. The wool-reinforced gelatin composite achieved a peak tensile strength of 163.59 N and exudate absorption of 1468%. These findings underscore the suitability of this composite as a breathable, flexible wound dressing without compromising patient comfort. The composite also exhibited excellent antibacterial properties. In vivo studies showed that wounds treated with these composite dressings healed more quickly, achieving 98% closure by day 20, compared with the untreated control group, which healed more slowly. These findings suggest that wool-reinforced gelatin hydrogels create a favorable environment for wound healing, making them a promising solution for managing exudating wounds and accelerating healing.
