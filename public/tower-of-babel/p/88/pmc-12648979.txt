Piezo-type mechanosensitive ion channel component 1 (PIEZO1) is upregulated in peripheral arterial disease (PAD) and a novel murine PAD model
Authors: Fujie Zhao, Feifei Li, Farbod Sedaghati, Hai Dong, Sandeep Kumar, Dennis Gene Foster, Jim Otto, Dana Giangiacomo, Lucas H. Timmins, Maiko Sasaki, Gloriani Sanchez Marrero, Kyung In Baek, Michael Tu, Sandra Peprah, Xiangqin Cui, Jeffrey H. Lawson, Rudolph L. Gleason, Hanjoong Jo, Luke Brewster
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe objectives of this work are to: define murine femoral artery stiffening with age and the modifiability of this process by exercise; impose peripheral arterial disease (PAD) hemodynamics on murine femoral arteries and to deliver focal atherosclerotic plaque to femoral arteries; and test piezo-type mechanosensitive ion channel component 1 (PIEZO1) expression in human and murine femoral arteries of PAD.MethodsWe used a running wheel to exercise young and old S129 mice and biomechanical testing to quantify changes in arterial stiffness. We created a novel partial femoral artery ligation (PFL) model to impose PAD hemodynamics via low wall shear stress (WSS) to create a flow-mediated model of arterial aging in femoral arteries. In vivo mechanics were defined with ultrasound. Ex vivo arteries underwent biaxial tests. Atherogenic conditions were induced using PCSK9 infection and a high-fat diet. Arterial remodeling and PIEZO1 expression were quantified by histology.ResultsFemoral arteries are naturally stiffer than carotid arteries; both stiffen further with aging, but exercise improved compliance in old femoral arteries. PFL imposed low WSS and stiffening, similar to that seen in aging. Under atherogenic conditions, PFL delivered focal atherosclerotic plaques in femoral arteries. Low WSS increased PIEZO1 expression in femoral arteries (∼1.8× in endothelial cells, ∼2.4× in smooth muscle cells, and ∼2.8× in macrophages). Human PAD arteries with high-grade stenosis validated increased PIEZO1 mRNA (∼1.83×).ConclusionsFemoral artery mechanics differ significantly from the carotid artery but can be modified by exercise. This PFL model confers arterial stiffness, and under atherogenic conditions, delivers focal femoral atherosclerotic plaque. PIEZO1 expression increases in both PFL-treated mouse femoral arteries and human PAD arteries with severe stenosis, supporting this as a translational target for PAD.Clinical RelevancePeripheral artery disease (PAD) is the third most common atherosclerotic bed. PAD is associated with increased risk of limb loss and death, but the mechanisms driving site-specific arterial remodeling in PAD remain unclear. This work uniquely creates a model of PAD that incorporates arterial stiffening via aging and flow disturbances and inducing atherosclerotic plaque into the murine femoral artery. By comparing murine femoral arteries with PAD arteries, piezo-type mechanosensitive ion channel component 1 (PIEZO1) was discovered as key mediator linking PAD blood flow and stiffening to untoward changes in endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and macrophages within femoral arteries. Targeted modulation of PIEZO1 activity provide PAD-centric therapeutic strategies and help promote the vascular health, life, and limb outcomes in patients with PAD.

Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) is a significant age-related medical condition, frequently characterized by atherosclerotic blockages to arteries in the leg, such as the femoral artery.1 PAD ranks as the third leading cause of atherosclerotic disease morbidity, following coronary artery disease and stroke.1 PAD is on the rise and already affects 10 to 12 million people in the United States and >230 million worldwide.1 Although the role of atherosclerosis in PAD is well-established, the molecular mechanisms underlying plaque formation and progression in peripheral arteries are not well-understood.

Muscular arteries (eg, femoral artery) are structurally distinct and are naturally stiffer than elastic arteries (eg, carotid arteries).2 This inherently stiffer characteristic may impact flow-mediated arterial remodeling and the pathways involved in this process. Further, these mechanical differences likely contribute to the distinct fibrotic plaque characteristics observed in PAD compared with coronary and carotid artery disease.3 Because arterial stiffening is a hallmark of aging and PAD,4 it is known to exacerbate atherosclerotic progression and is a modifiable precursor to PAD.5 We used exercise to test the reversibility of arterial stiffening due to age. In both human and murine models, we and others have shown that disturbed flow and low and oscillatory wall shear stress (WSS), can activate fibrotic pathways and induce stiffening in otherwise healthy carotid arteries.6 Here, we used a similar flow-mediated model of arterial stiffening in the femoral artery, which we hope will help translate discovery using muscular femoral arteries to identify exciting pathways that can be validated in human PAD arteries.

Although the role of transient receptor potential (TRP) channels in cardiovascular disease is well-established, recent studies have highlighted piezo type mechanosensitive ion channel component 1 (PIEZO1) as an emerging player in vascular disease.7 PIEZO1 is a nonselective cationic channel that mediates the conversion of mechanical forces, such as fluid shear stress, stretch, and static pressure, into intracellular electrochemical signals.8 It is highly permeable to calcium ions (Ca2+) and broadly expressed across multiple organ systems.8 In the cardiovascular system, PIEZO1 is highly expressed in endothelial cells (ECs),9 vascular smooth muscle cells (SMCs),10 macrophages (Mϕs),11 cardiomyocytes,12 and red blood cells.13 Through its mechanosensory function, PIEZO1 contributes to several physiological and pathological processes, including cardiovascular and lymphatic development, vascular tone regulation, red blood cell volume control, and the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis, hypertension, cardiac arrhythmia, and cardiac hypertrophy.8 Moreover, pharmacologic inhibition of PIEZO1 has exhibited potent anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects in preclinical models.14 Excitingly, the role of PIEZO1 in PAD is not known, and this study may help investigators invest resources into this promising and femoral-centric pathway.

We developed a novel, flow-dependent, murine model of atherosclerotic remodeling in a mouse model to help discover PAD-centric pathways. This model uses a representative artery and mimics the hemodynamic features of human PAD, allowing for direct comparison to PAD arteries. Using this model, our objectives were to: (1) characterize the intrinsic mechanical stiffness of femoral arteries in comparison to carotid arteries in the context of aging, and the modifiability of stiffness from aging with exercise; (2) determine how this novel partial femoral ligation (PFL) induces low WSS and promotes arterial stiffening, both hallmarks of PAD arteries; (3) test whether under atherogenic conditions, PFL promotes focal plaque formation in the murine femoral artery; and (4) discover the expression profile of PIEZO1, a mechanosensitive ion channel, in relevant vascular cells from PFL-treated murine femoral arteries, and then validate this in a PAD artery cohort with similar hemodynamics. This model provides a clinically relevant platform to further study the role of PIEZO1 in PAD and to explore its potential as a therapeutic target for modulating arterial stiffness and plaque development.

Methods

Data availability

Please see the Major Resources Table and detailed method information in the Supplementary Material (online only). All supporting data and study materials are available upon request from the authors. The gene array data of human PAD samples have been submitted to the Gene Expression Omnibus. The Genomic Spatial Event accession number is GSE281841.

Mice

Younger mature (12- to 20-week-old) and old (80-week-old) mice15 (Jackson Laboratory, strain 101,043) were utilized in this study. Mice were maintained and housed according to the National Institutes of Health guidelines in our Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International-accredited experimental animal facility under controlled conditions (21 ± 2°C, 50% ± 10% relative humidity, and a 12-hour light:12-hour dark cycle). All animal studies were carried out by procedures approved by the Emory University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

PFL surgery

Our PFL model was designed as a modification of the partial carotid ligation model developed in Dr Hanjoong Jo’s lab operated on adult age (12- to 20-week-old) male and female mice.16 Briefly, anesthesia was induced by 3% isoflurane and subsequently maintained on 1.5% isoflurane. The epilated area was disinfected with betadine, and an incision (∼1 cm) was made from the knee towards the medial thigh. The left femoral artery was exposed by blunt dissection. Four branches of left femoral artery (saphenous artery, proximal caudal femoral artery, superficial caudal epigastric artery, and the medial proximal genicular artery) were ligated with 6–0 silk suture while the popliteal artery was left intact. The incision was then closed with a 4–0 suture. The right femoral artery system was not manipulated. Mice were monitored until recovery in a chamber on a heating pad following surgery. A single subcutaneous injection of buprenorphine (0.1 mg/kg) was given 12 hours after partial ligation for additional pain relief. The development of low WSS after ligation was confirmed by ultrasonography. Hindlimb perfusion was measured before and immediately after surgery, and then at 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days post-surgery by laser Doppler imaging (Moor Instruments). Image analysis software (PimSoft; Moor Instruments) was used to calculate the hindlimb mean flux units. The final blood flow values were represented as the ratio of ischemic to nonischemic hindlimb perfusion.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed by using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.1 software or MATLAB. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean. Specific replicate numbers represent biological replicates. The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to test normal (Ganssian) distribution. For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed paired or unpaired Student t-test was conducted for normally distributed data; a two-tailed Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test or unpaired Mann-Whitney test was conducted for non-normally distributed data. For comparisons between three or more groups, either one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or two-way ANOVA was conducted; for normally distributed data, Tukey or Sidak multiple-comparison post hoc tests were used for pairwise comparisons after ANOVA; For non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal-Wallis test was conducted, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test for post hoc pairwise comparisons. P < .05 was considered significant for all statistical tests. Dr Xiangqin Cui provided expertise in all the statistical analyses.

Results

Femoral arteries are naturally stiffer than carotid arteries, stiffen with aging, and are more compliant with exercise

Biomechanical forces play a profound role in vascular cell signaling and arterial remodeling.6,17 To quantify the biomechanical characteristics of younger and older femoral arteries compared with carotid arteries in mice, younger (12- to 20-week-old) and older (80-week-old) mice arteries underwent ex vivo cylindrical biaxial testing (Fig 1, A). Younger femoral arteries were stiffer than carotid arteries under physiologic pressures (60-80 mmHg) (Fig 1, B). Both femoral arteries and carotid arteries stiffened naturally with aging as evidenced by less arterial compliance in older femoral arteries compared with older carotid arteries (Fig 1, C). To test the modifiability of femoral artery stiffening by exercise, selected mice had a running wheel in their cage for spontaneous exercise. In average, younger mice ran 8.55 ± 0.58 kilometers per day, and old mice ran 2.06 ± 1.04 kilometers per day. We used published distances traveled by age-matched mice in cages for comparison, grouping younger mouse studies for comparison to younger running wheel mice and a 60-week-old mice study to compare with our 80-week-old running wheel mice (Supplementary Fig 1, A; Supplementary Table I, online only). Here, exercise recovered femoral artery compliance in the older mice; without exercise, arterial compliance in older femoral arteries was significantly stiffer than in younger femoral arteries (Fig 1, D and E). To better understand mechanical stiffening, we examined various stiffness parameters. Physiologic stretch, which reflects longitudinal arterial stiffness, was quantified by the ratio of the in vivo length to the unloaded (excised) length of an artery. We found that femoral arteries stiffened longitudinally by having decreased physiologic stretch compared with carotid arteries regardless of age. Physiologic stretch decreased with age in both femoral arteries and carotid arteries (Fig 1, F). Physiologic stretch in older femoral arteries was improved by exercise, but not in older carotid arteries (Fig 1, F). We further tested the constitutive material parameters k1, k2, ζ, and c based on the unified-fiber-distribution (UFD) model,18 where k1 represents the fiber initial stiffness, k2 represents the stiffening effect under stretch, ζ is a scalar characterizing the fiber distribution, and c is an arterial matrix stiffness. We found that older femoral arteries had an elevated arterial stiffening factor (k2), which was attenuated by exercise therapy (Fig 1, G). Exercise did not change circumferential fiber component (ζ), arterial matrix stiffness (c), and fiber initial stiffness (k1) in femoral arteries or carotid arteries (Supplementary Fig 1, B-D, online only). Although physiologic stretch mainly represents longitudinal stretch, the opening angle is regarded as an indicator of residual stress. Although older femoral arteries did not exhibit a change in residual stress with exercise, younger femoral arteries had less residual stress after exercise (Supplementary Fig 1, E, online only). Altogether, these results demonstrate that, in mice, femoral arteries are naturally stiffer than carotid arteries, stiffen with aging similar to carotid arteries, and exercise improves compliance in older femoral arteries but not in older carotid arteries. Thus, age-related femoral artery stiffness occurs by modifiable pathways that have not been discovered.

PFL induces low shear flow and stiffens femoral arteries

To test the role of pathologic flow conditions in the mouse, we developed a PFL model, mimicking human PAD flow,6 by ligating the saphenous artery, proximal caudal femoral artery, superficial caudal epigastric artery, medial proximal genicular artery, and leaving just the popliteal artery patent (Fig 2, A and B). The influence of PFL on blood flow was monitored by high-resolution ultrasonography and illustrated by WSS. WSS plays a crucial role in the onset and progression of atherosclerosis.19 A “physiologic” WSS, typically considered to be ∼15 dyn/cm2 and unidirectional, provides a stable flow (s-flow) environment that is athero-protective,20 whereas a “pathologic” WSS, characterized by low and/or oscillatory WSS, provides a pro-atherogenic environment.6 WSS was calculated as published.6 Therefore, WSS after PFL exhibited persistently low WSS (Fig 2, C). The contralateral right femoral artery was used as an internal control in this study. Additionally, the success of the ligation was confirmed via reduced blood flow downstream (Supplementary Fig 2, online only). To characterize the hindlimb perfusion defects from PFL, we used the laser Doppler perfusion index. We found that there was a transient decrease in the perfusion ratio of both nonatherogenic and atherogenic PFL hindlimbs at day 3, which was resolved by day 7, and then maintained at ∼0.9 at day 28 (Supplementary Fig 3, A, online only). To further examine the effect of low WSS on femoral artery stiffness, we used the constitutive equation to compute the material properties of the arteries. All parameters (c, k1, and k2) were calculated. We found that low WSS exposure reduced the femoral artery physiologic stretch to a level comparable to that observed in aging femoral arteries in both male and female mice (Fig 2, D). Upon calculating the stress using Equation 1 (Supplementary Material, online only), it becomes apparent that the Cauchy circumferential stress of the femoral artery is greater in the low WSS group at the in vivo axial stretch (Fig 2, E). As depicted in Fig 2, F, there was a statistically significant difference (P = .0029) in the average elastic modulus between the normal WSS (0.54 MPa) and low WSS (0.90 MPa) groups. The average squared value obtained from fitting a line to the stress-strain data was 0.89 for all artery samples. Next, we found a statistically significant difference in the femoral artery exposed to normal WSS vs low WSS in the product of k1 and k2 (k1 × k2), which denotes fiber stiffness at large deformations (Fig 2, G). Specifically, the mean stiffness value for the low WSS group (337.97 kPa) was substantially higher (∼3×) than the normal WSS group (102.76 kPa) (Fig 2, G). This suggests that at higher physiological pressures, femoral arteries exposed to low WSS undergo significantly greater stresses due to stiffer fiber properties, potentially elucidating certain aspects of arterial remodeling in these arteries. Although other parameters, including c, k1, and k2 were higher in the low WSS group, the difference was not statistically significant. Altogether, our results demonstrate that due to altered hemodynamic and physiological conditions, fibers in the low WSS exposed arteries experience higher stresses at in vivo stretch and transmural pressure caused by the blood flow.

Low WSS in atherogenic mice delivers focal atherosclerotic plaque in murine femoral artery

To induce atherogenic conditions, mice were injected with AAV-PCSK9 and fed with a high-fat diet as published21 (Fig 3, A). Serum lipid profiles for total cholesterol (low-density and high-density lipoprotein) and triglycerides were tested to ensure atherogenic conditions (Supplementary Fig 4, online only). The native vessel architecture and blood flow pattern play determined roles in the onset and progression of artery plaque.3 Arterial Green strain Eθθ is negatively correlated with the invasive measurements of arterial stiffness and a method of quantifying arterial stiffness.6 As depicted in Supplementary Fig 5, A and B (online only), the ultrasonography of vessel movement demonstrated a significant decrease in arterial Green strain Eθθ in femoral arteries compared with carotid arteries. Importantly, after 4 weeks of PFL, ultrasonography confirmed in affected arteries there were narrowed femoral artery lumen (Fig 3, B), sustained low-flow velocities, and increased arterial stiffness, characterized by decreased peak systolic velocity (PSV) (Fig 3, B and C), and reduced arterial Green strain Eθθ (Fig 3, B and D). Velocity time integral (VTI), also called stroke distance, represents how far the column of blood travels in one heartbeat cycle.22 As depicted in Fig 3, E, PFL significantly inhibited VTI. Taken together, these results suggest that PFL-induced low WSS renders stiffer femoral arteries. PIEZO1 is specifically upregulated in PAD and after PFL

Given the emerging role of PIEZO1 in mechanotransduction in atherosclerosis,23 we sought to explore its involvement in PAD. PIEZO1 is also known to be more highly expressed in small arteries and plays a critical role in their contraction.10 Because femoral arteries are classified as muscular arteries, we examined PIEZO1 expression in patients with PAD. To begin, we utilized a gene expression database generously provided by Jeff Lawson, MD (Duke University), which includes microarray data from 39 human PAD arteries collected from amputation procedures (Supplementary Table II, online only). This allowed us to analyze differential gene expression related to PIEZO1 in the context of PAD. Among the 24,000 genes tested, 1433 genes were differentially expressed using a fold-change threshold of 1.5. Of these, 701 genes were upregulated and 732 genes were downregulated in arteries with severe stenosis (grade IV and V) compared with grade I (Fig 5, A; Supplementary Table III, online only). Notably, PIEZO1 stood out as one of the most significantly upregulated mechanosensitive ion channels, showing a consistent ∼1.83-fold increase in both grade IV and grade V PAD arteries (Fig 5, A; Supplementary Table III, online only). Interestingly, KCNMA1, which encodes the alpha subunit of calcium-activated BK (big potassium) channel, was among the most downregulated genes, reduced by over 2-fold in high-grade PAD arteries (Fig 5, A; Supplementary Table III, online only). Discussion

PAD is a significant age-related medical condition that leads to limb-threatening amputation and even death, and imposes a huge economic burden on health care systems due to its associated morbidity, mortality, and resource utilization.1 Despite the high (and increasing) incidence of PAD, it is understudied and a point of emphasis for future discovery.

The native biomechanics of muscular arteries may influence the onset and progression of PAD.25 Our study shows the muscular artery (eg, femoral artery) is naturally stiffer than the elastic artery (eg, carotid artery) in mice with lower arterial compliance, arterial stiffening factor (k2), and physiologic stretch. This is consistent with our testing of primates.2 Importantly, whereas aging is a common issue for arterial stiffness, we found that age-related arterial stiffness is modified by exercise in femoral arteries but not carotid arteries. This is consistent with a clinical publication of lower extremity arteries and the current guidelines for PAD management.26 Currently, the mouse model of hind limb ischemia is the most commonly used preclinical model for human PAD.27 This model is useful to study the neovascularization of ischemic muscle, but it does not model diseased vessels or how PAD flow patterns affected vessels. It also does not model atherosclerotic plaques in limb arteries. Yet more than 90% of PAD is chronic and is highly associated with atherosclerosis.26 Clinically, relevant animal models of arterial remodeling, like the one we developed here, may assist in our understanding of this disease.

Here, we developed a novel model of femoral artery atherosclerosis in mice by combining the PFL surgery and atherogenic diet. The low shear forces identified in this model mimic that of patients with PAD,6 and have been rigorously characterized by ultrasound. It is important in the hindlimb to note that Lardenoye et al used a cuff model to induce femoral atherosclerotic plaque in the common femoral artery.28 Plaque developed quickly in their model, but the hemodynamics of the cuffed arteries were not tested. They found that in the high-cholesterol group, cuff placement resulted in a rapid, lipid-loaded foam cells- and smooth muscle cell actin-positive cells–rich intimal hyperplasia at day 3, and a near-total occlusion of the lumen ensued at day 14. It is likely that a similar hemodynamic environment was formed in their model by mimicking composed or oscillatory low WSS characteristics defined in our model.

Interestingly, our results have shown that femoral arteries appear to have unique mechanical characteristics and activity of PIEZO1. Why might PIEZO1 be especially relevant in the femoral artery? First, the femoral artery is a muscular conduit with a higher SMC content than elastic arteries such as the carotid. Retailleau et al reported a remarkably strong expression of PIEZO1 in small-diameter resistance arteries (caudal artery and cerebral arteries), and a much lower expression in large-diameter conduit arteries (aorta or the main renal artery).10 Moreover, they demonstrated that SMC PIEZO1 was involved in the structural remodeling of small arteries but not large arteries upon hypertension.10 Coincidentally, we found a substantially higher PIEZO1 expression in the femoral vs carotid artery. Excitingly, PIEZO1 has also been found to be involved in carotid artery disease in these recent publications.29 Second, the superficial femoral artery is exposed to an unusual combination of mechanical cues (chronically reduced or oscillatory shear stress, high cyclic strain, and external compression during locomotion),30 that are well suited to engage a polymodal mechanotransducer such as PIEZO1. Consistent with this, we found that PIEZO1 is markedly upregulated in both severely stenotic human PAD arteries and in murine femoral arteries following partial ligation. Third, ischemic conditions have been reported to involve PIEZO1: (1) endothelial PIEZO1 promotes angiogenic recovery after femoral ligation31; and (2) myeloid PIEZO1 impairs perfusion recovery after hindlimb ischemia in mice.7 Moreover, PIEZO1 has been reported to be expressed in human superficial femoral artery and contribute to the arterial medial calcification.32 Together, these data argue that the femoral artery’s muscular architecture and distinctive mechanical milieu make it particularly dependent on PIEZO1 signaling, helping to explain the vessel-bed selectivity we report and positioning PIEZO1 as an attractive, PAD-relevant therapeutic target.

In addition, we found increased expression of PIEZO1 in ECs, SMCs, and Mϕs of femoral arteries under low WSS, suggesting a multifaceted role of PIEZO1 in PAD vascular remodeling. PIEZO1 mediates distinct mechanosensitive processes in these cell types.9,23,29,33 In ECs, PIEZO1 is well recognized for its role in vascular development, flow sensing, and pathological vascular remodeling by regulating cell proliferation, migration, barrier function, and inflammation.9 In vascular SMCs, PIEZO1 contributes to mechanotransduction associated with vascular stiffness, calcification, and transdifferentiation32; it has been implicated in hypertensive remodeling of small arterial,10 pulmonary arterial hypertension,23 aging-related decline in mechanosensation,34 cytoskeletal stiffness in aortic aneurysm,34 and foam cell formation under mechanical stress.32 By contrast, in Mϕs, PIEZO1 primarily modulates inflammatory and immune functions, including as activation of NLRP3 inflammasome,11 polarization toward pro- or anti-inflammatory phenotypes,33 and efferocytosis.29 These functional differences suggest that PIEZO1 integrates unique mechanical cues to drive endothelial dysfunction, SMC phenotypic switching, and macrophage-driven inflammation in a cell type–specific manner. Although our study establishes PIEZO1 upregulation across all three vascular cell types in PAD, further work is required to dissect their individual contributions to ECM remodeling and lesion progression. Specifically, vascular SMCs experience pulsatile pressure-based stresses, such as hoop stress, about 100,000 times greater than the shear stresses sensed by ECs, making them highly sensitive to pressure changes.15 How PIEZO1 discriminates between different WSS patterns in ECs to orchestrate constrictive vs expansive remodeling, and how it responds to large pulsatile pressure stresses in SMCs under different pressure–time relationships to regulate phenotypic switching and extracellular matrix synthesis, warrant further investigation.

Emerging evidence supports a critical role of PIEZO1 in vascular fibrosis and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and we came to PIEZO1 during investigations of CCN2/connective tissue growth factor pathways.35 We have previously demonstrated a unique role for CCN2 in vascular stiffening and flow-induced atherosclerotic plaque formation.35 In the present study, we identify a involvement of PIEZO1 across all vascular cell types queried and within the vascular wall of patients with PAD. Mechanical stimuli, especially low or oscillatory WSS, likely regulate ECM remodeling via PIEZO1 through several pathways. PIEZO1 activation leads to Ca2+ influx, which in turn triggers downstream cascades such as p38 MAPK–YAP/TAZ36 and calcineurin–NFAT,37 which are known regulators of fibrotic gene expression including CCN2. In models of renal fibrosis, matrix stiffness activates PIEZO1–p38MAPK–YAP to drive ECM secretion, and inhibition of this axis attenuates fibrosis.36 PIEZO1 also influences CCN2 and CCN1 expressions directly via a YAP-dependent mechanism in keloid fibroblasts.38 Additionally, PIEZO1 engagement may enhance TGF-β signaling and modulate matrix metalloproteinase activity, two established mediators of ECM synthesis and degradation, through mechanosensitive activation and local protease release.39,40 Although these pathways remain untested in PAD-specific femoral artery disease, our findings and prior literature support PIEZO1 as an integrator of hemodynamic signals driving vascular remodeling via fibrotic pathways. Our novel murine femoral artery atherosclerosis model thus provides a powerful platform for mechanistic dissection of PIEZO1’s temporal regulation and downstream CCN2, TGF-β, and ECM remodeling signaling in future work.

Although our findings underscore PIEZO1 as a selectively upregulated mechanosensor in PAD arteries, age-related arterial stiffness is a multifactorial process that involves other mechanosensitive pathways beyond PIEZO1. Prominent among these are integrin-mediated adhesions: integrins such as β1 and αvβ3 sense extracellular matrix stiffness and transmit mechanical forces through focal adhesion kinase and cytoskeletal linkages, leading to vascular SMC stiffening and reduced contractility in aging vessels.41 Additionally, TRP channels, including TRPV4, TRPC6, and TRPM4, respond to mechanical stretch and shear, contributing to Ca2+ influx that induces endothelial dysfunction, SMC phenotypic switching, and fibrotic remodeling.42 Recent studies suggest that TRPV4 channels may serve as stiffness sensors in atherosclerosis by mediating macrophage and fibroblast activation and fibrosis in stiffened vascular tissue.43 Furthermore, mechanosensitive GPCRs such as angiotensin II type 1 receptor can transduce pressure and flow stimuli to promote inflammation and fibrosis in vascular aging.44 Although our study specifically highlights PIEZO1 dysregulation, these complementary mechanosensors likely act in concert or parallel to drive age-related arterial stiffness, especially in PAD. Investigating how these pathways intersect with PIEZO1 will be important for understanding the full mechanobiology of vascular remodeling.

There are some notable limitations of this work. First, although both aging and PFL induce arterial stiffening, we do not compare these models directly. Rather, we used exercise to determine if the mechanical stiffening of aged femoral arteries could be modified. It could. This led us to search for putative modifiable pathways related to stiffening. Second, although our data did not reveal sex-based differences in PIEZO1 expression in these mice, such differences may be elicited in gonadectomy models of vascular aging or in older mice (after menopause). The arteries are known to stiffen through fibrotic pathways after gonadectomy, and these models mimic menopause, which drives the uptick in vascular disease in women in later years.45 Third, our PAD cohort shows a high prevalence of diabetes and hypertension, both major drivers of vascular dysfunction and remodeling. Although our model does not directly replicate diabetes or hypertension, both conditions converge into stiffened arteries, which we modeled by PFL. In this study, we overlaid relevant comorbidities onto the stiffened arterial background b using AAV-PCSK9 and HFD. Together, these interventions in mice mimic the atherosclerotic remodeling observed in patients with PAD. Fourth, we do not perform in vivo or in vitro gain- and loss-of-function experiments for PIEZO1. Such studies may establish a causal relationship between PIEZO1 activity and PAD-associated vascular remodeling. Although our results demonstrate significant upregulation of PIEZO1 in both human PAD arteries and the murine PFL model, future studies using cell-specific PIEZO1 knockout or pharmacological modulation will be critical to define its mechanistic role and downstream signaling pathways. Additionally, the testing was performed in adult mice. Testing in older mice would be of great interest. Finally, the modification of these pathways with exercise has not yet been tested. Future work will evaluate these opportunities to clarify how PIEZO1 interacts with other mechanosensors and inflammatory mediators during PAD progression.

After a comprehensive evaluation of the hemodynamics, biomechanics, and histology of the femoral artery, we demonstrate that: (1) PFL induced sustained low WSS in femoral arteries; (2) low WSS promoted femoral artery stiffness in mature 12- to 20-week-old mice; (3) under atherogenic conditions, the low WSS imposed by PFL led to focal femoral artery plaque formation, which was associated with prominent PIEZO1 throughout the vascular wall. In summary, our established mouse chronic PAD model (PFL + atherogenic conditions) closely mimics key aspects of human chronic PAD, such as arterial stenosis, ischemia, and impaired blood flow in the peripheral arteries.3,6,24

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study shows a novel murine chronic atherosclerotic PAD model and identifies PIEZO1 as a potential therapeutic target in PAD. The interaction of PIEZO1 with flow-mediated stiffening is likely clinically important. A better understanding of the PIEZO1 pathway in arterial stiffness is needed.
