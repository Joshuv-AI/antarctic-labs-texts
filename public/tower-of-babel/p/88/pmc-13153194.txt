Cortical and basal ganglia beta oscillations and frequency-dependent DBS effects in the A53T Parkinson’s disease rat model
Authors: Laura Kondrataviciute, Minesh Kapadia, Ivan Skelin, Srdjan Sumarac, Luka Zivkovic, Luis Antonio Franco Vergara, Sabika Jafri, Cherisse Tan, Hien Chau, Yuhan Hou, Savanna Blade, Xilin Liu, Clement Hamani, Taufik Valiante, William D Hutchison, Luka Milosevic, Lorraine V Kalia, Suneil K Kalia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson’s disease (PD) is a movement disorder characterized by alpha-synuclein (a-Syn) aggregation, dopaminergic degeneration, and pathological beta oscillations (13–30 Hz) in the basal ganglia circuit. Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is an effective neurosurgical treatment for the motor symptoms of PD. However, the extent to which mitigation of beta oscillations mediates DBS therapeutic effects remains uncertain. Using an adeno-associated virus-mediated nigral A53T a-Syn overexpression rat model, we examined basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical electrophysiology and the model’s responsiveness to DBS. In vivo recordings revealed early beta emergence in the motor cortex (MCx), spreading to the subthalamic nucleus (STN) and entopeduncular nucleus (EP) with neurodegeneration. This was accompanied by alterations in STN and EP single-unit activity. Awake-state beta oscillations manifested as transient bursts. Low- and high-frequency DBS differentially modulated beta bursts and motor performance. Our results demonstrate that the A53T a-Syn model replicates key PD-like electrophysiological features, providing a platform to investigate DBS mechanisms and optimize therapies targeting aberrant beta activity.Subject terms: Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the fastest-growing neurodegenerative disorder worldwide, characterized by the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SN) and the accumulation and aggregation of alpha-synuclein (a-Syn), a primary component of Lewy bodies and neurites1,2. These pathological hallmarks underpin the dysregulation of basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical circuits critical for motor control, including the initiation of movement3. PD-related basal ganglia circuit dysfunction manifests across multiple spatial scales, from altered single-neuron firing patterns4 to synchronized population-level activity, most prominently in the beta frequency band (13–30 Hz)5,6.

The emergence of pathological beta oscillations is one of the key neurophysiological features of PD, with beta power correlating with motor symptom severity7–11. Beta oscillations are thought to reflect an “anti-kinetic” state that inhibits movement initiation12 and are dynamically modulated by behavior, desynchronizing during movement and resynchronizing upon its cessation—a phenomenon exaggerated in the Parkinsonian state13. These oscillations are also highly responsive to therapeutic interventions that relieve motor symptoms. Dopaminergic medication14, deep brain stimulation (DBS) of the subthalamic nucleus (STN), and the globus pallidus internus (GPi) each suppress beta power and modulate beta burst dynamics15–18. However, the precise mechanisms by which DBS alters circuit activity—ranging from the firing properties of individual neurons to large-scale oscillatory patterns—remain only partially defined16,19.

Animal models that recapitulate the progressive nature and key features of PD are essential for investigating these mechanisms. Targeted overexpression of human a-Syn with the A53T pathogenic variant within the rat SN results in progressive nigrostriatal degeneration and associated motor deficits20–24. Recent work has identified the emergence of STN beta oscillations in this model25 and shown that its motor impairments are responsive to high-frequency stimulation (HFS) of the STN26. Yet, a comprehensive electrophysiological mapping of this model, capturing how single-unit firing and beta-burst dynamics evolve across multiple basal ganglia nodes and respond to distinct DBS frequencies, has not yet been performed.

In this study, we examined whether an adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated A53T a-Syn rat model recapitulates the core electrophysiological signatures of PD and exhibits frequency-dependent responsiveness to DBS. Using electrophysiological recordings in anesthetized and freely behaving animals following nigral A53T a-Syn overexpression, we evaluated circuit- and cell-level changes across basal ganglia nuclei. We hypothesized that this model would show: (1) progressive development of pathological beta oscillations accompanied by nucleus-specific alterations in single-unit activity, and (2) differential DBS responses, in which HFS, but not low-frequency stimulation (LFS), suppresses aberrant beta activity and improves motor behavior. These studies are designed to establish both the face and predictive validity of this model for mechanistic investigations of DBS and for refining neuromodulation strategies aimed at correcting dysfunctional network dynamics in PD.

Results

Sequential emergence of beta oscillations due to a-Syn-mediated dopaminergic cell loss

We acquired in vivo extracellular electrophysiological recordings under isoflurane anesthesia (GA) at 3 and 6 weeks post-AAV injection, corresponding to mid-stage and late-stage neurodegeneration due to A53T a-Syn overexpression23,24,27. At these timepoints, we detected elevated local field potential (LFP) beta oscillations in the primary motor cortex (MCx), STN, and entopeduncular nucleus (EP), the rodent homolog of the GPi28 (Fig. 1A). Rats with mistargeted electrodes were excluded from the analysis (Fig. 1B). Immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 1C) revealed that at 3 weeks post-injection, A53T a-Syn overexpression resulted in a 12% loss of TH+ cells in the SN compared with the empty vector (EV) control group (Fig. 1D). By the 6-week timepoint, dopaminergic cell loss reached 43%, consistent with our previous work29–32 and that of others23,24,27. This progression confirms the model’s degenerative trajectory and validates its utility for studying the electrophysiological consequences of dopaminergic neurodegeneration.

Recordings from the MCx revealed that A53T a-Syn-overexpressing animals exhibited elevated beta oscillatory power in both low and high beta frequency bands compared with EV controls at both timepoints (Fig. 2C, E). In contrast, beta oscillatory power in the STN was comparable between groups at 3 weeks post-injection, but was elevated in A53T a-Syn-overexpressing animals at the 6-week timepoint (Fig. 2J). Taken together, these results demonstrate that nigrostriatal expression of A53T a-Syn in rats promotes the sequential emergence of beta oscillations, initially detectable in the MCx and subsequently in the STN and EP as the extent of neurodegeneration progresses.

a-Syn overexpression in the SN induces bursting activity in the STN and EP

In order to ascertain whether excessive beta LFP activity in A53T a-Syn-overexpressing animals is accompanied by changes at the single-neuron level, we acquired recordings from 196 STN neurons and 167 EP neurons under GA (Table 1). Average STN firing rates in the A53T a-Syn and EV groups were comparable at both timepoints (Fig. 3A, B). However, STN neurons from A53T a-Syn-overexpressing animals exhibited increased bursting activity 6 weeks post-injection compared with EV controls (Fig. 3C). Neuronal discharge irregularity, assessed using the interspike interval coefficient of variation (CV), was elevated in the STN of rats with nigrostriatal A53T a-Syn expression and increased in severity as neurodegeneration progressed relative to EV-injected controls (Fig. 3D). In contrast to the STN, EP neurons (Fig. 3E) in A53T a-Syn-overexpressing animals exhibited elevated firing rates 6 weeks post-injection compared with EV controls (Fig. 3F). Increased EP bursting activity was already evident 3 weeks after A53T a-Syn injection and persisted at the 6-week timepoint relative to EV-injected controls (Fig. 3G). This heightened bursting in EP neurons was accompanied by increased discharge irregularity, as measured by the interspike interval CV (Fig. 3H). Together, these findings demonstrate nucleus-specific alterations in neuronal firing patterns in A53T a-Syn-overexpressing rats. Specifically, EP neurons show early-onset bursting and later increases in firing rate as the disease progresses, whereas STN neurons maintain stable firing rates but develop increased bursting only at an advanced stage of neurodegeneration.

Longer and more prominent beta bursts in freely behaving A53T a-Syn-overexpressing animals

To assess beta oscillatory activity in a more physiologically relevant state, we compared LFP beta power in A53T a-Syn-overexpressing animals and EV controls during awake, freely behaving conditions 6 weeks post-AAV injection. Consistent with recordings under GA, elevated beta oscillations were observed in both the STN (Fig. 4A–C) and EP (Fig. 4H–J) of A53T a-Syn-overexpressing animals compared with EV controls. These findings indicate that isoflurane anesthesia does not fully abolish beta oscillations in A53T a-Syn-overexpressing animals and suggest that this electrophysiological signature is intrinsic to the Parkinsonian state.

As supported by previous studies25,33–36 and illustrated in representative spectrograms from recordings in the STN (Fig. 4A) and EP (Fig. 4H), beta oscillations in the awake state were not continuous but instead manifested as transient bursts. A53T a-Syn overexpressing animals exhibited longer beta bursts in both nuclei compared with EV controls (Fig. 4D, K). In addition, A53T a-Syn-overexpressing animals showed an increased incidence of beta bursts in both the STN and EP, reflected by a greater beta burst rate (Fig. 4E, L) and longer total beta burst duration (Fig. 4F, M). Our results replicate clinical findings linking longer-lasting and more frequent beta oscillations with the Parkinsonian state35,37,38.

We next examined whether beta oscillations desynchronize during movement, a phenomenon observed in both PD patients and animal models of the disease5,13,39. To assess this relationship, we compared the total distance traveled during 100 s recordings and correlated it with the mean beta power z-score. No differences in total distance traveled were observed between rats overexpressing A53T a-Syn and EV counterparts during STN or EP recordings (Supplementary Fig. S2). We detected a negative correlation between beta power z-score in the STN and distance moved in A53T a-Syn-expressing animals (Fig. 4G), consistent with movement-related beta desynchronization. A similar relationship was not observed in the EP of A53T a-Syn-overexpressing animals (Fig. 4N). No such correlations were detected in EV control animals for either nucleus (Fig. 4G, N), further supporting an association between aberrant beta dynamics and Parkinsonian pathophysiology.

Beta burst characteristics predict model responsiveness to DBS

To evaluate the responsiveness of select beta burst characteristics to DBS in the A53T a-Syn overexpression model, we applied low-frequency stimulation (LFS; 25 Hz) and high-frequency stimulation (HFS; 100 Hz) to the STN and EP under both GA and awake, grid-walking conditions 6 weeks post-AAV injection. These stimulation frequencies were chosen based on their differential effects in PD: LFS is known to have minimal or potentially adverse effects on motor symptoms, whereas HFS typically improves symptoms, particularly bradykinesia15. We therefore focused on beta power and beta burst parameters, since beta-band oscillations are strongly correlated with motor symptom severity and are suppressed during therapeutic DBS9,15,19,40,41.

In A53T a-Syn-overexpressing animals under GA, both LFS (Fig. 5A) and HFS (Fig. 5B) applied to the STN reduced beta power, independent of stimulation frequency (Fig. 5C). In contrast, no changes in beta power were observed in EV controls following STN stimulation (Fig. 5C). Similarly, DBS of the EP using either LFS (Fig. 5D) or HFS (Fig. 5E) led to a reduction in beta power 10 s after stimulation selectively in A53T a-Syn-overexpressing animals, with no detectable changes in the EV group (Fig. 5F). Together, these results suggest that under GA, DBS of both the STN and EP effectively suppresses beta power irrespective of stimulation frequency.

To assess DBS responsiveness in a more physiologically relevant state, we performed 2-minute LFS and HFS sessions in the STN and EP of awake, freely moving rats during a grid-walking task. We analyzed the mean beta burst duration and the overall time the beta signal exceeded the 75th percentile 10 s before and after stimulation. In the STN, LFS increased mean beta burst duration relative to baseline, whereas HFS produced a smaller effect (Fig. 5G). These effects on mean beta burst duration were more prominent in EV animals (Fig. 5G). In contrast, opposing effects of STN LFS and HFS were detected when assessing the total time the beta signal exceeded the 75th percentile 10 s after STN stimulation compared to the baseline levels in both EV and A53T a-Syn-expressing rats (Fig. 5H). Similarly, stimulation of the EP with LFS and HFS produced opposing effects on both mean beta burst duration (Fig. 5I) and the total time the beta signal exceeded the 75th percentile (Fig. 5J) relative to the baseline, irrespective of A53T a-Syn expression status. These frequency-dependent effects were further supported by behavioral outcomes, as HFS, but not LFS, of the EP reduced the slip ratio of A53T a-Syn-overexpressing animals during the grid-walking test (Fig. 5K–L). Collectively, our results demonstrate frequency-specific modulation of beta burst dynamics in both the EP and STN by DBS and suggest that such modulation may underlie its therapeutic efficacy.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that minimal dopaminergic neurodegeneration induced by nigrostriatal overexpression of A53T a-Syn is sufficient to elicit elevations in beta oscillations within the MCx, whereas more substantial neurodegeneration is required to promote excessive beta oscillations in the STN and EP. Contrary to several studies linking elevated beta power to motor symptom severity and dopaminergic neurodegeneration14,42,43, we did not observe a further increase in MCx beta power with advancing neurodegeneration. These findings align with recent data reported in parallel by Hofman and colleagues25, who also failed to detect a correlation between MCx beta power and nigrostriatal dopaminergic neuron loss. Emerging evidence points to a more complex relationship between beta oscillations and disease progression44,45. Although beta power was shown to increase in a MPTP-lesioned non-human primate model of PD, its dominant frequency shifted from higher to lower bands as the disease progressed, indicating dynamic changes in both the magnitude and spectral properties of beta oscillations45. Using the same primate model, Connolly et al. demonstrated increased coupling between the phase of beta and the amplitude of high-frequency oscillations (256–362 Hz) in early PD stages, which intensified with disease severity44. Together, these findings suggest that Parkinsonian motor signs arise not from aberrant activity within a single frequency band, but from progressive dysregulation of interactions across multiple oscillatory spectra.

We selected isoflurane anesthesia to isolate the intrinsic electrophysiological phenotype of the A53T a-Syn model from the behavioral state-dependent modulation of beta oscillations5,12,46 and to ensure the stability required for reliable single-unit detection. Although the effects of anesthetics on neurophysiology are dose-dependent and remain controversial—with some clinical studies advocating for alternatives such as ketamine or dexmedetomidine during microelectrode recording47,48—several practical and experimental considerations guided our choice. Isoflurane permits rapid, physiologically guided titration and is logistically simpler than injectable agents for prolonged stereotaxic procedures. While a meta-analysis suggested that isoflurane may have a relatively modest impact on STN firing compared with other agents49, its influence on microelectrode recordings is complex and likely contingent on dose, the extent of neurodegeneration, and the recording target. Moreover, we were mindful that ketamine, a commonly proposed alternative, acts primarily through NMDA receptor antagonism—a mechanism that could substantially alter cortical excitability and thus confound interpretation of our motor cortex recordings48. Acknowledging known limitations of volatile anesthetics, including broader sustainability concerns50 and evidence that isoflurane can dampen oscillatory power via enhanced GABAergic inhibition51, we confirmed that the pathological beta phenotype identified under anesthesia was preserved in awake, freely behaving animals. This cross-state validation confirms that the elevated beta power and altered burst dynamics reflect a stable network abnormality of the model, rather than an artifact of the anesthetic regimen. As expected, beta activity appeared more continuous under anesthesia and more transient, burst-like in the awake state, likely reflecting behavior-driven modulation of oscillatory dynamics5,12,46. Notably, these burst-dominated signatures detected in our awake recordings closely resemble those observed in PD patients52. Together, these findings support the use of isoflurane anesthesia as a reproducible approach for behavior-independent mechanistic electrophysiological studies, while our awake recordings confirm that the pathological beta phenotype generalizes across brain states in the A53T a-Syn model.

The differential effects of A53T a-Syn overexpression on beta oscillations in the MCx, STN, and EP likely arise from the different circuits that generate these rhythms. High beta frequencies (21 –30 Hz) are thought to originate from hyperdirect cortico-STN inputs, whereas low beta frequencies (13–20 Hz) are mediated by the indirect cortico-striatal-GPe-STN pathway53–55. This division could explain why EP recordings were characterized by low beta frequencies, while the MCx and STN exhibited both low and high beta bands. Importantly, these distributions align with those reported in 6-OHDA-treated rats, where MCx, STN, and EP display frequency ranges of ~18–25 Hz, ~20 Hz, and 4–18 Hz, respectively56. The later emergence of high-beta power within the EP at the 6-week timepoint may signal progressive changes in circuit engagement, potentially reflecting increased hyperdirect pathway recruitment or plasticity-driven shifts in network synchrony, as neurodegeneration worsens. Comparable increases in entrainment frequency over time have been described in the substantia nigra pars reticulata of 6-OHDA-lesioned rats, consistent with such plasticity-related reorganization57.

It is worth noting that Hofman and colleagues did not detect low beta oscillations in the STN and MCx in the same model with comparable levels of neurodegeneration25. The differences in our findings may stem from nuances in data analysis, with the previous study considering shorter 30 s epochs, which can be more prone to artefacts, pre-movement-related beta power desynchronization10, and normalization of beta oscillatory power by cumulative power of 5–45 Hz and 55–95 Hz frequencies, which are known to be altered in the Parkinsonian state58,59. The hypothesis that pathological oscillations may originate in the MCx could potentially explain our observation of STN single-unit bursting activity, which is reported to be driven by glutamatergic input from the cortex via the hyperdirect pathway3. Our findings align with other reports suggesting that cortical hyperactivity may play a key role in driving electrophysiological changes in downstream basal ganglia nuclei56,60–62.

Interestingly, single-unit bursting activity in the EP was elevated as early as 3 weeks post-injection of A53T a-Syn, while significant bursting in the STN was observed only at the 6-week timepoint. Additionally, firing rates were higher in the EP but remained unchanged in the STN of A53T a-Syn-overexpressing animals. The absence of large changes in STN firing rates in our model contrasts with the increases reported in acute, extensive 6-OHDA-lesion models63,64. We hypothesize that this discrepancy reflects fundamental differences in underlying pathophysiology. More specifically, the progressive and partial dopamine depletion in the A53T a-Syn model might permit compensatory mechanisms that preserve firing rates, whereas the abrupt, near-complete dopaminergic denervation in the 6-OHDA model results in immediate and pronounced STN disinhibition64–66. The temporal and functional divergence between electrophysiological readouts in the EP and STN in the a-Syn overexpression model may reflect differences in nuclei sensitivity to dopaminergic loss, as well as the effects of electrode insertion and anesthesia. Isoflurane, which primarily enhances GABAergic inhibition, may exert a greater suppressive effect on the EP, a nucleus dominated by inhibitory neurotransmission, than on the STN, which receives both inhibitory and excitatory inputs67–69. However, since both A53T and EV control groups were exposed to similar anesthetic conditions, the observed differences are unlikely to be attributable to GA. Instead, these differences may be explained by the underlying pathophysiology of the A53T a-Syn model. The progressive nature of the synucleinopathy may allow for compensatory mechanisms to initially stabilize STN activity, which receives inputs primarily from the hyperdirect and indirect pathways68,69. In contrast, the EP, as the convergent output nucleus integrating the direct, indirect, and hyperdirect pathways68,69, may be compelled to manifest network dysfunction earlier due to its position as the final integrator of a progressively destabilizing circuit. Moreover, electrode implantation has been shown to induce transient clinical improvements prior to stimulation onset, likely due to microlesion effects70. Given the smaller size of the STN relative to the EP71, electrode insertion may result in a greater degree of transient normalization of neuronal activity, thereby masking pathological changes. These factors could explain why significant bursting activity emerged later in the STN than in the EP.

To eliminate the potential confounding effects of anesthesia, we performed recordings in awake, freely behaving animals. Unlike the continuous beta rhythms observed under GA, beta oscillations in awake animals occurred as transient bursts, consistent with findings in PD patients and animal models5,10,34,39,41,42,52,72. These bursts were longer and more prominent in A53T a-Syn-overexpressing animals, potentially contributing to poorer motor performance in the grid-walking test. Indeed, Hofman and colleagues reported that high beta burst probability for long bursts in the STN correlated with motor deficits in the cylinder test in a similar model with comparable dopaminergic neurodegeneration25. Our findings are also aligned with reports linking longer beta bursts to greater motor impairment in PD patients34. We did not observe longer beta bursts in the EP compared to the STN in A53T a-Syn-overexpressing animals, in contrast to observations in human studies52. This discrepancy may reflect differences in patient populations across nuclei, as the STN recordings in that study were obtained from tremor-dominant patients, who typically exhibit reduced beta power73.

We further validated the model by examining beta power desynchronization during movement, a feature of PD pathophysiology13,34,74. In A53T a-Syn-overexpressing animals, beta power in the STN was negatively correlated with the distance traveled. This finding suggests that exaggerated beta bursts in the A53T a-Syn rat model may interfere with normal movement initiation, consistent with the hypothesis that pathological beta activity impairs motor function5,12,75. Although technical variability cannot be entirely ruled out, the absence of a similar correlation in the EP is more likely explained by its functional role in encoding specific movement kinematics and context, rather than the global locomotor state, as supported by evidence in rodents76.

Interestingly, beta oscillations are not detected in the MCx following AAV-mediated wild-type a-Syn overexpression, even at later timepoints characterized by comparable levels of neurodegeneration ( ~ 50%) and mild motor impairment77. This discrepancy may reflect differences in recording and analysis methodologies. While the Brys et al. study normalized cumulative beta power to “pink noise,” our analysis normalized beta power to the 46–56 Hz range and removed aperiodic 1/f “pink noise” activity. This approach is more sensitive to transient beta bursts, which may be obscured when cumulative power is analyzed with aperiodic components78. In addition, the use of contralateral hemispheres as internal controls in the previous study77 may have masked compensatory effects, whereas our study employed a separate cohort of EV-injected control animals.

A notable feature of our study was the variability in beta oscillatory power among A53T-expressing animals, with a subset displaying values comparable to EV controls despite significant nigral neurodegeneration. This heterogeneity, observed across both anesthetized and awake conditions, likely arises from multiple sources. One contributing factor is the precise anatomical location of the recording site, as even small differences in electrode placement within a nucleus can produce pronounced variability. For example, in patients with PD, the greatest amplitude of beta oscillations is often localized to the dorsal region of the STN, while recordings from neighboring regions often show weaker oscillations79. In addition, inter-animal differences in AAV transduction efficiency and the resulting gradients of synaptic dysfunction likely introduce biological variation in network pathology, compensatory responses, and the degree of emergent network-level synchrony.

Our investigation of the model’s predictive validity revealed that both EP and STN LFS and HFS produced comparable reductions in beta power under anesthesia. At first glance, these results may appear contradictory, as LFS is expected to exacerbate or have no effect on beta activity if beta oscillations directly reflect motor impairment7,80. However, local beta power may not fully capture the therapeutic mechanisms of DBS, which likely involve circuit-level modulation rather than purely local effects16,75,81. In awake animals, beta burst dynamics provided a more nuanced measure of DBS efficacy, with opposing effects of LFS and HFS emerging on both beta burst duration and the time the beta signal exceeded the 75th percentile threshold. It is worth noting that post-stimulation beta suppression remains debated since some studies report near-instantaneous reversion to baseline after stimulation cessation82,83, while others demonstrate prolonged attenuation11,84, possibly due to differences in stimulation parameters or neural circuit engagement81. In our study, we did not observe any drift or gradual return toward baseline in LFP beta power or beta-burst characteristics after DBS was switched off. The variability observed in our results–and the fact that some beta burst parameters did not reach statistical significance–may stem from subtle differences in electrode placement, resulting in the activation of distinct neural populations during stimulation. Nevertheless, the opposing effects of LFS and HFS on beta burst characteristics, together with the improvement in motor performance observed during EP HFS compared to EP LFS in the grid walking test, collectively support the predictive validity of the A53T a-Syn overexpression model for DBS research.

Despite these strengths, our study has limitations. Beta activity can be influenced by factors beyond movement, such as cognitive processing12,46. While we considered longer recording epochs and employed beta burst definitions comparable to those in human PD patients13,62,85, we cannot rule out the possibility that beta dynamics could have been affected by other behaviors, which are difficult to control in animals. Furthermore, our definition of movement, based on changes in head position, may not capture subtler movements in other body parts. While we identified a relationship between gross locomotion and beta power, the temporal resolution of the present setup did not allow for millisecond-level alignment between video tracking and electrophysiology recordings. As a result, the precise timing of beta bursts relative to movement initiation and termination could not be fully resolved. Future studies that integrate higher-resolution behavioral tracking with synchronized electrophysiology will enable more detailed dissection of the temporal dynamics linking beta oscillations, DBS, and motor function in this model, and will further enhance its utility for mechanistic and translational studies.

Methods

Animals

All procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines and regulations set by the Canadian Council on Animal Care (AUP #3818). Rats were purchased from commercial suppliers (Envigo, IN, USA and Charles River, MA, USA) and housed in the animal care facility at the Toronto Western Hospital under standard conditions (ad libitum access to food and water, 21 °C, 12 h light/dark cycle). A total of 80 adult female Sprague-Dawley rats ( ~ 250 g) were used for experiments under isoflurane anesthesia (n = 10 rats per group, timepoint, and recording site).

Electrophysiological recordings were obtained from the MCx, STN, and EP28. An additional cohort of 24 rats was used for awake-state recordings, comprising 15 rats implanted in the EP and 9 rats implanted in the STN. Rats were housed in pairs and individually after AAV injection and permanent electrode implantation, respectively. Recordings were performed prior to stimulation experiments. The study design is illustrated in Fig. S1.

Surgical procedure for AAV injection

The surgical procedure for AAV injection was adapted from our previous work with the AAV A53T a-Syn model29,30. Briefly, rats were anesthetized with isoflurane/oxygen and administered ketoprofen (5 mg/kg) for analgesia. Using stereotaxic coordinates from the rat brain atlas71, animals received a unilateral injection into the SN of either 2.0 µL of empty AAV1/2 vector (EV) or 2.0 µL of AAV1/2 expressing human mutant A53T a-Syn (A53T) at a concentration of 3.4 × 10¹² genomic particles (gp)/mL. Coordinates relative to bregma were anteroposterior (AP) –5.2 mm, mediolateral (ML) –2.0 mm, and dorsoventral (DV) –7.5 mm71. Body temperature was maintained at 37–38 °C throughout surgery using a heating pad. Post-operative monitoring was conducted daily for one week to ensure recovery and assess any adverse effects.

Surgical procedure for permanent electrode implantation

Procedures for implanting a 5-channel microelectrode array (BPS, Taiwan) into the EP and STN were adapted from a previously described protocol86. Surgeries were performed under isoflurane/oxygen anesthesia with ketoprofen (5 mg/kg) analgesia 5 weeks post-AAV injection. Briefly, two holes were drilled for stabilizing screws (0–80 ×1/16 Pan Head Phillips Screws, Protech), and another hole was drilled above the olfactory bulb to house reference and ground wires. Craniotomies were performed above the EP and STN using stereotaxic coordinates from the rat brain atlas71: EP (−2.5 mm AP; −2.3 to −3.3 mm ML; −7.6 to −8.0 mm DV) and STN (−3.6 mm AP; −2.25 to −3.25 mm ML; −7.4 to −8.2 mm DV). The electrode array was advanced using a stereotaxic frame (David Kopf, USA), with the longest electrode used as a reference for coordinates. The array was secured using dental cement (C&B Metabond, Parkell, Canada), and body temperature was maintained at 37–38 °C throughout the procedure. Post-operative monitoring was conducted daily for one week.

Electrophysiology recordings under isoflurane anesthesia

Terminal electrophysiological recordings were performed under 1.5–2% isoflurane anesthesia at 3 or 6 weeks post-AAV injection. Recordings were taken from the ipsilateral MCx, STN, and EP using the Blackrock CerePlex Direct system (Blackrock Neurotech, USA) at a sampling rate of 30 kS/s. Two platinum/iridium (Pt/Ir) microwires (125 µm diameter, FHC, USA) were inserted into a cannula and positioned using the following stereotaxic coordinates71: STN (−3.6 mm AP; −2.5 mm ML; −7.5 to −8.6 mm DV), EP (−2.5 mm AP; −2.8 mm ML; −5 to −7 mm DV), and MCx (−1.0 mm AP; −2.2 mm ML; −2.0 mm DV). The active signals were acquired from the Pt/Ir microwires, which were referenced to the guide cannula. The tip of the cannula was positioned 4 mm above the STN and EP targets, and 1.5 mm above the MCx. MCx recordings were acquired after STN recordings in the same animals, while a separate cohort was used for EP recordings. Neural activity was allowed to stabilize for 5 min before data acquisition. Body temperature was maintained at 37–38 °C throughout the experiment. Electrolytic lesions (100 µA for 15 s) were made at recording sites to confirm electrode placement post-mortem87.

Deep brain stimulation under isoflurane anesthesia

Low-frequency stimulation (LFS; 25 Hz) and high-frequency stimulation (HFS; 100 Hz) were applied to the STN and EP for 1 min, followed by a 3 min washout period88, under 1–1.5% isoflurane anesthesia at 6 weeks post-AAV injection (n = 10 per group). Stimulation was delivered using the Blackrock CereStim stimulator (Blackrock Neurotech, USA), and local field potentials (LFPs) were recorded 10 s before and after stimulation using the Blackrock CerePlex Direct system. Stimulation parameters were consistent across frequencies and targets and based on commonly used settings in rodent DBS studies89–91: biphasic stimulation cathodic pulse first, amplitude of 100 µA, pulse width of 120 µs, and interphase delay of 53 µs.

Analysis of electrophysiology recordings under isoflurane anesthesia

Raw data were high-pass filtered (cutoff 1 Hz), notch filtered (59–61 Hz), downsampled to 3000 Hz and wavelet transformed (n of wavelets = 7) for LFP analysis using code custom-written in Matlab (Mathworks, USA). 100 s recordings with minimal artefacts (5 min after reaching the target structure) were analyzed for low beta (13–20 Hz) and high beta (21–30 Hz) LFP powers. Analyzed frequency range powers were normalized by power in 46–56 Hz range which is not typically associated with known pathological or physiological oscillatory activity in the Parkinsonian brain59. Spike analyses were performed using Spooky Spikes toolbox (ReleasesToronto-TNBS/spooky-spikes (github.com)). 10–20 s stable single-neuron recordings were considered for analysis. Briefly, the raw signal was first bandpass filtered within 250–3000 Hz range using a fourth-order Butterworth filter, and spikes were detected using median absolute deviation with a signal-to-noise ratio threshold of ≥2.5. Spike sorting was performed using 2 principal component analysis followed by k-means clustering, with sorting efficiency confirmed visually and using a Silhouette score ( > 0.5). Firing rate, bursting index (BI) and interspike interval coefficient of variation (CV) were obtained and compared between groups. LFP analysis pre- and post-DBS was performed using Spike2 software (v. 8, CED, UK). Raw data were downsampled to 3000 Hz and Fast Fourier transformed (FFT) using a Hanning window and an FFT size of 8192 points, yielding a segment length of 2.73 s and a frequency resolution of 0.366 Hz. The power spectrum was generated by averaging (Welch’s method) consecutive windows with a 50% overlap. Cumulative powers in the 13–30 Hz and 46–56 Hz ranges were calculated, and normalized beta power (with respect to power in the 46–56 Hz band) was used to assess stimulation effects.

Electrophysiology recordings in awake animals

Electrophysiological recordings from the EP were acquired using the NeuLive wireless system (BPS, Taiwan) in 7 rats (n = 4/A53T vs n = 3/EV). The Blackrock CerePlex Direct system was used to acquire recordings from the EP in 8 rats (n = 4/A53T, n = 4/EV) and the STN in 9 rats (n = 5/A53T, n = 4/EV). For all chronic awake recordings, the active intracerebral contacts were referenced to a pair of wires implanted in the olfactory bulb. Animals were allowed to acclimate for 5 min after connecting to the data acquisition system. Behavior was videotaped and synchronized with electrophysiological recordings for offline analysis. Electrolytic lesions (100 µA for 60 s) were made at recording sites prior to sacrifice87.

Deep brain stimulation in awake grid walking animals

LFS (25 Hz) and HFS (100 Hz) were applied for 2 min, followed by a 2 min rest period during grid walking 1-week post-electrode implantation (6 weeks post-AAV injection). Stimulation parameters matched those used under GA. Motor performance was scored by counting the total number of steps and missed steps during 2-minute baseline and stimulation periods while grid walking92,93. The slip ratio (missed steps/total steps) was used to assess fine motor skills92,93.

Analysis of electrophysiology recordings in awake animals

For beta power analysis, 100-second recordings during free behavior were downsampled to 1250 Hz, high-pass filtered (1 Hz cutoff), and low-pass filtered (500 Hz cutoff). Short-time Fast Fourier transform (STFT) with a 50 ms Hamming window and 25 ms overlap (NFFT = 4096) was computed in Matlab. We employed STFT rather than wavelet analysis to better accommodate the non-stationary nature of awake signals. Aperiodic 1/f activity was removed using the Fitting Oscillations and One-Over-F (FOOOF) algorithm78. Beta power (13–30 Hz) was normalized to the 46–56 Hz range, and data were z-score normalized before merging results from Blackrock and NeuLive systems94. z-score for mean beta power in each animal was correlated with the total distance obtained from DeepLabCut output. Beta burst analysis was performed using Hilbert transformation for LFPs in beta frequency range (13–30 Hz)34. Bursts were defined as periods when the signal exceeded the 75th percentile with durations >100 ms to ensure inclusion of at least 3 waveform34,52. Beta burst rate (number of bursts per 100 s recording epoch), mean burst duration and total burst duration (time beta signal exceeded 75th percentile) were analysed25,34,37. For stimulation effects in the awake state, mean beta burst duration and overall time beta signal exceeded 75th percentile 10 s post LFS and HFS were compared.

Behavioral analysis using DeepLabCut

Animal behavior was videotaped and synchronized with electrophysiological recordings. The DeepLabCut toolbox (v 2.3.8)95 was used for body part tracking. Five videos (either wired Blackrock or wireless NeuLive systems) were used to extract frames for labeling. A total of 95 frames with two body parts (headstage and proximal tail) were used for training. The trained network generated body positions and likelihoods for all videos. Total distance traveled was calculated using Euclidean distance between consecutive frames. Frames with likelihood scores <0.9 were excluded to minimize noise.

Post-mortem analysis

Rats were sacrificed 3- and 6-weeks post injection. They were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane in oxygen at 5%, transcardially perfused with 100 mL heparinized saline solution, and processed for immuno-labeling as previously described29. Coronal sections were cut at a thickness of 40 μm on a sliding microtome (Leica Microsystems Inc., Richmond Hill, ON), and 6 series of sections were stored in cryoprotectant (30% glycerol, 30% ethoxyethanol, 40% PBS). Free-floating sections were washed with PBS-T, incubated in a blocking solution. This procedure was followed by incubation with primary antibodies, washing, and subsequently incubation with secondary antibodies. Immunofluorescence staining of the sections was performed on a single series of sections to visualize tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) (primary antibody chicken 1:500, Abcam, ab76442) and human a-Syn (primary antibody mouse 1:500, Invitrogen, 32-8200), and counterstained with DAPI (nuclear stain) prior to slide mounting and cover slipping. Stained sections were imaged with a Zeiss AxioObserver Widefield fluorescence microscope. Assessment of TH+ cells in the SN was done manually from microscope images (10x magnification). The number of TH+ cells (puncta) was quantified using ImageJ software (Version 1.54 g, NIH, USA). Nissl staining was used to confirm electrode location26. Briefly, Cresyl violet solution (0.1 g cresyl violet acetate (Sigma, C5042) in 100 mL ddH2O with 1 mL 100% acetic acid) was stirred overnight and filtered before use. Sections were washed in ddH2O (1 min), mounted on cover slides and dipped in solutions in the following order: ascending concentrations of ethanol (70%, 95%, 100% for 3 min each), xylene for 3 min, descending concentrations of ethanol (100%, 95%, 70% for 3 min), ddH2O for 3 min, cresyl violet solution for 5 min. Cover slides were rinsed with ddH2O (3x1min) and dipped in ethanol with ascending concentrations (70%—1 min, 95%—1 min, 100%—1 min), incubated in xylene solution (15 min) and cover slipped using Cytoseal mounting media (Epredia 831016). Anatomical recording location was extrapolated from the lesioned dot seen on brightfield images (20x magnification) with brain atlas71.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad, MA, USA). Normality of the data was tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (N > 50) or Shapiro-Wilk (N < 50) tests. Analysis of variance (ANOVA), ANOVA with repeated measures, Student’s T test, and Mann-Whitney test were used for group comparisons. Group (EV vs A53T) and Timepoint (Week 3 vs Week 6) were considered between-group factors. Stimulation (Pre vs Post) and Frequency (LFS vs HFS) were considered within-group factors, where applicable. If significant interactions were detected, Tukey’s HSD was used for post hoc comparisons. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated when examining bivariate linear relationships for normal variables. The criterion for statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. Significant differences of p ≤ 0.05, p ≤ 0.01, p ≤ 0.001 and p ≤ 0.0001 are depicted as *, **, ***, and ****, respectively.
