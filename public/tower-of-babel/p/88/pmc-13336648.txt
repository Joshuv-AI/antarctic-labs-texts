A Comparative Study: Can Large Language Models be a Supportive Tool in the Diagnosis and Treatment of Scabies?
Authors: İstem Ş. Çamur, Eren Çamur, Duru T. Onan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The advent of large language models (LLMs) has endowed artificial intelligence with remarkable capabilities to comprehend and generate human-like text by leveraging extensive datasets. Numerous studies in medicine increasingly focus on LLMs, particularly highlighting their substantial potential in diagnostic and therapeutic management. Scabies is a contagious skin disease that continues to affect over 200 million people globally each year and is particularly prevalent in developing countries. In recent years, there has been a marked increase in scabies cases both in Turkey and worldwide, elevating scabies to the status of a public health concern. Therefore, the Clinical Practice Guideline for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Scabies (CPGDT) was published in 2024.Aims and Objectives:This study evaluates the performance of LLMs on scabies-related questions created from CPGDT and compares it to a dermatologist.Materials and Methods:Sixty-four multiple-choice questions in English were administered to nine LLMs, including Claude 3 Opus, ChatGPT (versions 3.5, 4, and 4o), Google Gemini (1.0 and 1.5 Pro), Microsoft Copilot, Meta LLaMA 3 70B, and Perplexity, as well as to a senior dermatologist with 22 years of experience in dermatology. Responses were scored as correct or incorrect. Statistical analyses, including the McNemar test, were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0.Results:Claude 3 Opus achieved the highest accuracy (89%), significantly outperforming other LLMs and dermatologists (75%) (P < 0.05). ChatGPT 4o (79.6%) and ChatGPT 4 (78.1%) followed, with no significant differences observed among other LLMs or between LLMs and dermatologists (P > 0.05).Conclusion:This is the first study evaluating LLMs’ performance on scabies-related knowledge. Claude 3 Opus’ superior performance highlights its potential in dermatology, surpassing widely recognized LLMs like ChatGPT. Claude 3 Opus demonstrated exceptional accuracy in scabies-related queries, underscoring its value as adjunctive tool in dermatology. Enhancing LLMs’ visual diagnostic capabilities is vital for their integration into clinical practice.

Introductıon

The advent of large language models (LLMs) has endowed artificial intelligence with remarkable capabilities to comprehend and generate human-like text by leveraging extensive datasets. Consequently, LLMs have garnered significant attention and curiosity in the medical field, as in many other domains. Numerous studies in medicine increasingly focus on LLMs, particularly highlighting their substantial potential in diagnostic and therapeutic management.[12] Similarly, dermatology has witnessed a steady rise in research centred on LLMs. However, as these models predominantly depend on text-based interactions, they have not yet reached an adequate level of competency in dermatological diagnosis, where visual assessment is essential.[345] Addressing the question of whether LLMs could be developed for dermatological diagnosis in the future requires first demonstrating their knowledge and potential regarding dermatological examination findings, diseases, and treatment management algorithms.

Scabies is a contagious skin disease caused by the infestation of the Sarcoptes scabiei mite within the skin, leading to intense pruritus.[6] It continues to affect over 200 million people globally each year and is particularly prevalent in developing countries. In addition to posing a significant burden on healthcare services, scabies leads to considerable psychosocial and economic challenges for affected individuals.[7] In recent years, there has been a marked increase in scabies cases both in Turkey and worldwide, elevating scabies to the status of a public health concern.[8] The limited availability of evidence-based options for scabies treatment complicates the establishment of standardized clinical practice guidelines. Therefore, the Clinical Practice Guideline for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Scabies (CPGDT) was published in 2024, presenting clinical practices and approaches for the diagnosis and management of scabies cases through simple and practical algorithms.[9]

The aim of our study is to evaluate the performance of LLMs in responding to scabies-related questions derived from CPGDT, and to compare their performance both among themselves and against that of a dermatologist.

Materıals and Methods

Study design

CPGDT, published in January 2024, served as the primary reference for preparing 64 MCQs by dermatology resident (İ.Ş.Ç.). The reason why the questions were derived from the CPGDT is both because it is the most up-to-date guideline on scabies in the literature and because the senior dermatologist included in the study, who answered the same questions, manages his patients in clinical practice according to this guideline. Thus, it is aimed to make a more objective performance evaluation by preventing a possible bias between senior dermatologist and LLMs. The questions, comprising four options each, covered all sections of this guideline. An example of a chat with ChatGPT 4o

The MCQs did not utilize real patient data or clinical scenarios, thereby negating the need for ethics committee approval.

Design of input-output procedures for LLMs

The input prompt for LLMs was standardized as follows: “I want you to act like a Dermatology Professor with 30 years of experience who has lots of studies on scabies. I will ask you multiple-choice questions about scabies. Give me your single correct answer for each question below.” In December 2024, dermatology resident (İ.Ş.Ç.) gave this prompt across nine different LLMs (Anthropic’s Claude 3 Opus, OpenAI’s ChatGPT 3.5, ChatGPT 4, ChatGPT 4o, Google Gemini 1.0, Gemini 1.5 Pro, Microsoft Copilot, Meta LLaMA 3 70B, and Perplexity), maintaining each model’s web-interface default hyperparameter settings throughout.

Each LLM answered the 64 questions sequentially within a single chat session to ensure consistency. The authors did not subject the LLMs to pre-training or fine-tuning, and no specific criteria were provided that could influence the research objectives or outcomes.

The responses from LLMs and a senior dermatologist (D.O.) were assessed by a dermatology resident (İ.Ş.Ç.), who categorized them as either correct (1) or incorrect (0) [Figure 2]. To ensure transparency, the questions and data are included in Supplementary Materials 1 and 2 [http://links.lww.com/IJOD/A47, http://links.lww.com/IJOD/A48].

Workplace the study

Dermatologist performance evaluation

A senior dermatologist (D.O.) with 22 years of experience in dermatology participated in the study by answering the MCQs. The dermatologist provided responses in a blinded manner using their own computer.

Upon completion, the dermatology resident (İ.Ş.Ç.), with 2 years of experience, evaluated the responses provided by the dermatologist (D.O.). Each response was categorized as either correct (1) or incorrect (0).

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was applied to assess the distribution of variables within the dataset. Descriptive statistics, including minimum and maximum values, median, interquartile range, and percentages, were calculated to provide a detailed summary of the data.

Given the non-normal distribution of the data, non-parametric tests were utilized for the comparison of quantitative variables. To evaluate the proportions of correct responses across different prompts or language models, the McNemar test, a statistical method specifically designed for paired nominal data, was employed. Inter-model consistency comparison of responses between models was performed with Fleiss’ Kappa test.

Results

Among the LLMs, Claude 3 Opus achieved the highest accuracy rate at 89%. ChatGPT 4o followed with an accuracy rate of 79.6%, placing it second. These were followed by ChatGPT 4 at 78.1%, Gemini 1.5 Pro at 75%, and LLaMA 3 70B at 75%. ChatGPT 3.5 scored 73.4%, while both Gemini 1.0 and Perplexity scored 70.3%. Microsoft Copilot had the lowest accuracy rate among the models at 67.1%. The dermatologist (D.O.) demonstrated an accuracy rate of 75% [Figure 3].

Accuracy rates of LLMs and dermatologists

Claude 3 Opus (89%) demonstrated statistically significant superiority in accuracy compared to both the other LLMs and the dermatologist (D.O.) (P < 0.05) [Table 1]. However, no significant differences in accuracy rates were observed among the remaining LLMs (P > 0.05). Furthermore, with the exception of Claude 3 Opus, there was no statistically significant difference between the performance of the other LLMs and that of the dermatologist (D.O.) (P > 0.05).

Discussion

As LLMs are increasingly integrated across all fields of medicine, they have also begun to enter the field of dermatology. Although progress in dermatology has been somewhat slower due to its reliance on visual diagnosis, studies highlight the considerable potential of LLMs in this area.[101112] Recent research has focused on the performance of LLMs in text-based dermatological knowledge and visual diagnostics.[1314151617]

In a study by Passby et al.,[18] the performance of ChatGPT 3.5 and ChatGPT 4 on the “Dermatology Specialty Certificate Examination” questions was evaluated, revealing accuracy rates of 63.1% for ChatGPT 3.5 and 90.5% for ChatGPT 4. Lewandowski et al.[13] assessed the performance of ChatGPT 3.5 and 4 in three different “Specialty Certificate Examinations in Dermatology” consisting of 120 questions, ChatGPT-4 achieved an 80–90% accuracy rate in these examinations and exceeded the 60% pass rate in all dermatology certificate examinations. In another study, Rundle et al. presented the physical descriptors of 39 patients with cutaneous neoplasms (22 benign, 17 malignant) to ChatGPT for diagnosis and ChatGPT achieved an accuracy rate of 66.7% for malignant lesions and 69.6% for benign lesions.[16]

One of the most significant results of this study is that, despite certain variations, most LLMs exhibit notable competence in answering questions related to scabies. This result highlights that LLMs possess extensive knowledge of scabies and suggests their potential utility as a supportive tool for dermatologists. The impressive performance of Claude 3 Opus, in particular, raises the question of whether it could become a game-changer among LLMs, potentially surpassing ChatGPT in this specific field.

Performance discrepancies among LLMs can often be attributed to differences in their design and architecture. Models with internet access, such as Microsoft Copilot, Google’s Gemini Pro 1.5, Gemini 1.0, and Perplexity, may generate responses based on arbitrary or non-peer-reviewed sources. This dependency on non-validated information likely contributes to the relatively lower accuracy observed in internet-enabled LLMs compared to models trained on curated, reliable datasets that operate without internet access.

ChatGPT models and Claude 3 Opus are each trained on their respective proprietary datasets. One limitation of ChatGPT is that its training only included data up to September 2021. Despite this limitation, its performance on current queries appears minimally impacted.

Another crucial result of our study is that LLMs demonstrated the ability to provide answers as accurate as those of dermatologists, not only in specific sections but across all aspects of the scabies guideline. By employing questions covering every part of the guideline, our findings indicate that LLMs possess comprehensive knowledge extending beyond diagnosis to encompass the entire guideline.

To the best of our knowledge, no prior research has evaluated the performance of LLMs specifically on scabies. Our study is unique in assessing the performance of LLMs against the scabies guideline and comparing it with that of dermatologists. The inclusion of multiple well-known LLMs in our analysis enables a comprehensive comparison of their performance in this field. This approach helps identify the most effective LLM for assisting dermatologists with scabies-related guidelines and management. We believe our findings could serve as a starting point for future studies aiming to integrate LLMs into dermatology.

Limitations

Our study has a few limitations. First, we used only MCQs to evaluate the text-based knowledge of LLMs, without assessing their performance on open-ended questions. In clinical practice, dermatologists generate their own responses to patient issues, which resemble open-ended questions rather than predefined options. Therefore, MCQs may not fully reflect the depth of LLMs’ knowledge. Future studies comparing the performance of LLMs on open-ended questions with their MCQ performance would help address this limitation. Our study only assessed the text-based knowledge of LLMs, without evaluating their ability to perform visual assessments. For a comprehensive understanding of LLMs’ capabilities in visual analysis, broader studies incorporating multimodal LLMs are required. Also, the development of LLMs is a continuous process, with models progressively enhancing their performance through new models and updates. Consequently, the responses evaluated in this study represent the state of the models only at the current time. We did not evaluate how the response of LLMs changed during the interval (longitudinal) time. It should be noted that over time, the performance of LLMs can further improve and produce better results.

In conclusion, among the evaluated LLMs, Claude 3 Opus demonstrated the highest accuracy in interpreting scabies-related clinical queries. All other LLMs demonstrated performance levels comparable to those of the dermatologist. This finding underscores the significant potential of current LLMs as supportive tools in clinical decision-making for patients with scabies.
