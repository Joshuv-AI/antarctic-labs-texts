Cannabis as a source of novel therapeutics: A computational study combining LBDD and structure-based docking/dynamics to identify novel drug-like compounds for colorectal cancer
Authors: Khadija Khaddam Allah, Khaoula Mkhayar, Rachid Haloui, Amine Ballari, Ossama Daoui, Kaouakeb El Khattabi, Abdelmoula El Abbouchi, Samir Chtita, Souad El Khattabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCannabis represents a valuable source of bioactive compounds with significant therapeutic potential, thanks to its rich phytochemical profile, making it a prime candidate for cancer research. Our study investigates the potential of cannabis-derived compounds to inhibit cyclin-dependent kinase 2 (CDK2), a crucial regulator of cell-cycle progression and colorectal cancer cell proliferation. A comprehensive computational workflow integrating 3D-QSAR modeling, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, drug-likeness assessment, and ADMET prediction was employed to investigate a dataset of 33 cannabis-derived compounds. The robustness and predictive performance of the generated CoMFA and CoMSIA models were confirmed through internal and external validation, leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV), and Y-randomization tests, yielding excellent statistical parameters for CoMFA (Q2=0.651, R2=0.986, SEE=0.069) and CoMSIA (Q2=0.665, R2=0.985, SEE=0.071). Using contour map analysis, twenty new molecules (V1–V20) with enhanced antiproliferative activity against the HCT-116 colorectal cancer cell line were developed, and outperformed the original dataset’s most active one. Following drug-likeness screening and ADMET profiling, compounds V2, V5, V7, and V8 emerged as the most promising candidates, exhibiting favorable pharmacokinetic properties and drug-like characteristics. Molecular docking studies revealed that V7 and V8 exhibit high stability within the CDK2 active site and possess stronger binding affinity than the reference compound. Furthermore, 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations demonstrated that both protein–ligand complexes reached stable conformational states, as confirmed by converged backbone RMSD profiles, low residue fluctuations (RMSF), persistent protein–ligand interactions, and complementary structural descriptors including radius of gyration (Rg), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), and molecular surface area (MolSA). Collectively, these analyses confirmed the structural stability and compactness of the investigated complexes throughout the simulation period, with V8 exhibiting the most favorable dynamic behavior.Keywords: Cannabis, Colorectal cancer, 3D-QSAR, ADMET, Molecular docking, Molecular Dynamics

Introduction

Cannabis is one of the most extensively cultivated and historically utilized medicinal plants, recognized for its wide range of therapeutic properties and bioactive constituents. 1,2 From a chemical standpoint, cannabis is very complex plant type for reasons related to the nature of its constituents. 3 Numerous studies over the years have explored its chemical composition, identifying more than 500 compounds, including 125 classified cannabinoids. The main psychoactive ingredient, Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ9-THC), has been identified as one of the main cannabinoids in Cannabis sativa, along with cannabidiol (CBD), cannabinol (CBN), cannabigerol (CBG), and cannabichromene (CBC). Cannabis also contains non-cannabinoid components like alkaloids, terpenes, flavonoids, phenolic compounds, and others. 3 Because of their affinity for various other pharmacological targets, including non-cannabinoid receptors, phytocannabinoids have pharmacological effects in addition to their interaction with cannabinoid receptors. 4,5 This broad range of activity explains the diverse therapeutic potential of cannabis, which has been investigated for its potential to treat a wide range of medical conditions, including autoimmune disorders like multiple sclerosis, [6], [7], [8] asthma, 9 rheumatoid arthritis, 10 colitis, 11 dermatological conditions, 12 Alzheimer’s disease, 10 and different types of cancer. 13,14 According to the World Health Organization, one of the main causes of death worldwide is still cancer. 15 Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer and the primary cause of cancer-related illness and death, with rising incidence rates among young adults under 50 worldwide. 16,17 A number of factors, including male gender, advanced age, and lifestyle choices like poor diet and lack of physical activity, increase the risk of colorectal cancer (CRC), which accounts for about 5% of cases. 17,18 Several therapeutic strategies are currently available for the management of colorectal cancer, with treatment selection primarily depending on the tumor stage, disease progression, and the patient’s clinical condition. For advanced rectal cancer, radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy is recommended before surgery to reduce the chance of a local recurrence. 19 Postoperative chemotherapy is frequently used for stage III colon cancer cases and infrequently for high-risk stage II cases. Drugs such as 5-fluorouracil and oxaliplatin are used in these cases. Targeted therapies, including small-molecule multikinase inhibitors like regorafenib, fusion proteins that target multiple pro-angiogenic growth factors like aflibercept, and monoclonal antibodies that fight against EGFR and VEGF-A, have significantly improved the treatment of metastatic colorectal cancer. Survival rates have increased as a result of these treatments, especially for patients without certain genetic mutations. 19 However, there are still a lot of problems in spite of these treatments. Current medications are ineffective for many patients, and long-term use is frequently restricted by toxicity and side effects. Furthermore, it is common for resistance to targeted therapies and chemotherapy to emerge. These problems show that new, safer, and more potent therapeutic agents that can specifically target cancer cells and get past resistance mechanisms are still needed. Treatment resistance is a complicated issue with many underlying causes, which highlights the need for sophisticated models to improve therapeutic response prediction and create more focused interventions. 18,20 The need to create efficient treatments for colorectal cancer is underscored by early research confirming that hemp-derived compounds have anti-proliferative activity against the colorectal cancer cell line HCT-116. 20 HCT-116 is a human cell line derived from colorectal carcinoma. It is widely used in research to evaluate cell proliferation, the cytotoxicity of new compounds, and molecular pathways involved in cancer. A mutation in the TP53 gene, which codes for the tumor suppressor protein p53, is what distinguishes these cells. Because of this mutation, cells are less sensitive to stress signals and are more likely to proliferate uncontrollably. 21 HCT-116 cells’ resistance to apoptosis and capacity to accumulate genetic mutations two traits of colorectal cancer are facilitated by the loss of p53 function. More aggressive tumor progression and a decreased response to chemotherapeutic treatments are linked to this alteration. 22 These features make HCT-116 a useful cell model for researching the molecular causes of colorectal cancer and evaluating the effectiveness of novel treatment approaches that target particular pathways, like CDK2 inhibition. A kinase called CDK2 (Cyclin-Dependent Kinase 2) controls the cell cycle, specifically the G1 to S phase transition. Cyclins and checkpoints like p53 tightly regulate CDK2 activity in healthy cells. Together with cyclins A, B, and E, CDK2 is essential for controlling the cell cycle, especially during the G1 to S phase transition. 23 While CDK2 is less critical in healthy cells, it is essential for growth and progression in cancer cells. Overexpression of CDK2 and cyclins A and E has been noted in several cancers, including colorectal, ovarian, and breast cancers. Several CDK2 inhibitors, such as flavopiridol, roscovitine, and kenpaullone, have been explored as potential therapies. However, their lack of specificity and toxicity highlight the need for more selective and safer alternatives. In cancer cells, including HCT-116, CDK2 is frequently overexpressed or hyperactive, allowing uncontrolled cell division even in the presence of defective regulatory pathway. HCT-116 cells rely heavily on CDK2 for progression through the cell cycle and proliferation. Inhibiting CDK2 in HCT-116 can: Block the G1 à S transition, halting cell division, Cause accumulation of cells at a specific cell-cycle stage and induce apoptosis if checkpoints (like p53) are defective. 20,22,23

In this study, we aimed to investigate the potential of a series of cannabis-derived molecules to inhibit the proliferation of HCT-116 colorectal cancer cells. To achieve this, a robust 3D-QSAR model was developed using an experimental dataset of 33 cannabis-based compounds to predict their cytotoxicity against HCT-116. ADMETox analyses were performed to evaluate pharmacokinetic properties and potential toxicity, providing an initial assessment of drug-likeness. Molecular docking studies were then conducted to examine the interactions between the selected compounds and the human CDK2 enzyme (PDB ID: 6GUE), followed by 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations to evaluate the conformational stability, structural convergence, and dynamic behavior of the selected protein–ligand complexes. Overall, this integrated in silico approach provides a comprehensive evaluation of the investigated compounds and facilitates the identification of promising lead candidates for further experimental validation and colorectal cancer drug development.

Methodology

QSAR

To investigate the therapeutic potential of cannabis-derived compounds, a 3D-QSAR modeling approach was employed to identify the key structural and physicochemical features governing antiproliferative activity. The workflow involved several essential steps, including dataset preparation, molecular optimization, structural alignment, model construction, and rigorous validation to ensure the reliability and predictive capability of the developed models.

Dataset

Thirty-three (33) compounds with experimentally proven antiproliferative activity against the HCT-116 colorectal cancer cell line were selected from the literature. 18,19,24

The initial dataset was collected from published studies reporting the antiproliferative activity of cannabis-derived compounds against HCT-116 cells. To ensure dataset consistency and suitability for 3D-QSAR modeling, a two-step filtering strategy was applied. First, compounds exhibiting weak activity (IC50 > 100 μM) were excluded. Second, structural inspection was performed to identify a common scaffold suitable for molecular alignment. Since molecular alignment is a critical step in CoMFA and CoMSIA modeling, one compound lacking the common benzene core shared by the remaining molecules was removed. No additional compounds were excluded as statistical outliers during model development. The final dataset consisted of 33 structurally related compounds.

The antiproliferative activities of these cannabis-derived compounds against HCT-116 cells were measured by the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values, which were expressed in μM. These values were then transformed into their negative logarithmic form (pIC50=−log(IC50)) to be used as a dependent variable in the 3D-QSAR analysis. The 33-compound set was then divided into two groups: a test set with 6 compounds to validate the predictive accuracy of the model from low active to high active, and a training set with 27 compounds to develop the 3D-QSAR models (Table 1).

Geometry optimization, calculation of molecular descriptors, and data division

SYBYL 2.0 was used for the Comparative Molecular Field Analysis CoMFA 25 and Comparative Molecular Similarity Indices Analysis CoMSIA 26 studies on a MEDION laptop running Windows 11 with an Intel i7 processor and 16 GB of RAM. 33 compounds’ molecular structures were built using the Sketcher module in order to get ready for 3D-QSAR modeling, and they were then refined in the SYBYL-X 2.0 program. 27,28 Up to 5000 iterations, a convergence criterion of 0.01 kcal/mol, and the Tripos standard force field with Gasteiger–Hückel partial atomic charge computation were all used in the optimization process. 29 A precise alignment of the dataset was carried out because molecular alignment is essential for the development of pharmacophore models. 29 The alignment template was molecule 4, which was chosen due to its high activity and low conformational flexibility. The DATABASE ALIGNMENT method in SYBYL was used to align all molecules to molecule 4 according to their benzene ring (Fig. 1). Based on structural diversity and pIC50 values, a dataset of 33 molecules was split into training (80%) and test (20%) sets for the creation of 3D-QSAR pharmacophore models (Table 1). The division was performed to ensure a balanced distribution of biological activities ranging from low to highly active compounds while preserving structural diversity in both subsets. The training set was used for model construction, whereas the external test set was reserved exclusively for evaluating predictive performance. 30 The 3D-QSAR models were constructed using the training set, which included 27 molecules with pIC50 values between 4.25 and 5.92. The performance and predictive accuracy of the developed 3D-QSAR pharmacophore models against HCT-116 were assessed using the test set, which included six molecules with pIC50 values ranging from 4.32 to 5.67. 31,32

Model building and validation

We created 3D-QSAR models using Comparative Molecular Field Analysis (CoMFA) and Comparative Molecular Similarity Indices Analysis (CoMSIA) techniques, the most recent and widely used statistical methods in 3D-QSAR modeling in drug design, in order to identify important structural features that affect the biological activity of the compounds under study. [33], [34], [35] These techniques make it possible to investigate how different molecular characteristics, such as steric, hydrophobic, electrostatic, hydrogen bond donor, and hydrogen bond acceptor properties, affect biological activity. 36 In particular, the partial least squares (PLS) algorithm in the Tripos force field was used to correlate these descriptors with anti-proliferative activity (pIC50). An sp3 hybrid carbon atom was used as the probe for calculating steric and electrostatic energy, and the input grid was defined with a spacing of 2 Å in all Cartesian directions. The correction factor, which controls the Gaussian function’s gradient and has a default cutoff energy of 30 kcal/mol, was kept at its default value of 0.3. 37,38 A number of statistical criteria, including the coefficient of determination (R2), the standard error of estimate (SEE), the cross-validated determination coefficient (Q2), and the predictive determination for the test set To minimize the risk of overfitting associated with the relatively small dataset size, the optimal number of PLS components was selected based on leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV). The robustness of the developed 3D-QSAR models was further evaluated using Y-randomization tests, which ensures that any observed correlation between the model’s dependent variable (pIC50 values) and independent variables (molecular descriptors) is not due to random chance. In this test, the pIC50 values were randomly permuted multiple times while maintaining the original descriptors matrix unchanged. With each permutation, a new QSAR model was generated. If the correlation coefficients R2 and Q2 of these randomized models remain consistently lower than those of the original model, it indicates that the model’s predictive performance is not the result of random correlations. Drug likeness and in silico pharmacokinetics ADMET prediction

Understanding the structure–activity relationship and identifying the molecular features responsible for enhanced biological activity are fundamental steps in the drug discovery process. However, beyond biological potency, a promising drug candidate must also exhibit favorable physicochemical and pharmacokinetic properties to ensure adequate efficacy, safety, and bioavailability. These assessments represent critical stages in the drug discovery and development process, as many compounds are eliminated due to unfavorable drug-like properties, limited bioavailability, or potential toxicity concerns. Furthermore, experimental validation at this stage is often costly and time-consuming, requiring substantial financial resources, specialized methodologies, and extensive. [45], [46], [47], [48] To mitigate these limitations, in silico predictive approaches have emerged as valuable tools for the early assessment of pharmacokinetic and toxicological profiles, enabling more efficient compound prioritization and substantially reducing the cost and time associated with experimental screening. Among the most widely used predictive tools, SwissADME and pkCSM offer reliable assessments of drug-likeness, ADMET (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion) properties, and toxicity risks. In this study, the molecules designed based on the validated 3D-QSAR model were subjected to these evaluations. To determine if the twenty molecules we created in this work are appropriate for use as novel anticancer inhibitors that could be developed further into future drug designs. Their oral bioavailability and drug-like qualities were first evaluated using SwissADME. The ADME-Tox properties of molecules that satisfied the drug-like criteria were then predicted through pkCSM analysis. In order to ascertain their practical applicability for chemical synthesis, their synthetic feasibility was also assessed using the synthesis score. The most promising drug candidates with the best pharmacokinetic profiles and the fewest toxicity risks were found thanks to the integration of these computational models. [49], [50], [51]

Molecular docking

To examine their interactions with the target protein and determine their affinity for the active site of human cyclin-dependent kinase 2 (CDK2) chain A, the molecules with the best pharmacokinetic and drug-like characteristics were chosen for molecular docking studies. AutoDock 4.2 was used for these studies. 52 The RCSB Protein Data Bank provided the crystal structure of CDK2 (PDB ID: 6GUE, resolution: 1.99 Å). Protein preparation was performed using Discovery Studio 2024. The co-crystallized ligand and crystallographic water molecules were first removed from the protein structure. Subsequently, polar hydrogen atoms and Kollman charges were added to ensure proper protein parameterization prior to molecular docking studies. A docking grid box with dimensions of 126 × 126 × 126 Å 3 and a point spacing of 0.375 Å was created, with the grid center defined at coordinates x=−16.415, y=−7.262, z=18.182, covering the binding site of CDK2 based on the ligand sizes. The PDBQT file for the prepared protein was then generated. A Lamarckian genetic algorithm (LGA) run was set with 50 iterations and a maximum of 2.5×106 energy evaluations for each ligand. [52], [53], [54], [55], [56] The ligand–enzyme interaction patterns were analyzed using the protein–ligand interaction profiler (PLIP), a fully automated computational server 57 and Discovery Studio 2024. 58 To verify the accuracy of the molecular docking protocol, the crystallized ligand 4-(2-methyl-3-propan-2-yl-imidazol-4-yl)- N-(4-methylsulfonylphenyl)pyrimidin-2-amine was re-docked into the CDK2 receptor and considered as positive control binders. The docking protocol was considered valid, as the re-docking process produced an RMSD value below 2.0 Å, which is widely accepted as evidence of accurate reproduction of the experimental binding conformation. 59

Molecular dynamics (MD)

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations constitute a powerful computational approach for investigating the structural stability, conformational flexibility, and dynamic behavior of biomolecular systems at atomic resolution. MD simulations are widely employed in structure-based drug discovery to investigate protein–ligand interactions beyond static docking predictions and to evaluate the temporal stability of ligand binding. 60 The most popular of the various MD techniques is classical molecular dynamics (cMD). It uses classical mechanics to explain the motion of atoms and molecules. cMD models interatomic interactions using force fields, which are mathematical functions that specify the system’s potential energy based on atomic positions. These force fields take into account important parameters such as bond lengths, bond angles, and dihedral angles. By numerically solving the equations of motion, MD simulations allow researchers to track the system’s evolution over time. 61,62 Molecular dynamics simulations were performed to investigate the stability and dynamic behavior of the selected CDK2–ligand complexes (V7 and V8) following molecular docking. The receptor–ligand complexes exhibiting the highest docking scores were prepared using the Protein Preparation Wizard and subjected to 100 ns production MD simulations using the Desmond simulation package. 63 Previous structure-based drug discovery studies have demonstrated that 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations provide adequate sampling for evaluating the stability and dynamic behavior of protein–ligand complexes in comparable systems. [64], [65], [66], [67], [68], [69] The system was set up in an orthorhombic water box (TIP3P water model) with a buffer zone of 10 Å using Desmond’s system builder tool. To keep the system neutral, counterions (Na+ and Cl−) were added. The simulations included an initial relaxation phase of 1 ns and were subsequently performed at 300 K and 1 atm pressure. Before analysis, the system was stabilized for 100 ns under an NPT ensemble. 70 Each selected protein–ligand complex (V7-CDK2, V8-CDK2, and the positive control-CDK2 complex) was independently subjected to a 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation. Trajectory convergence and structural stability were comprehensively evaluated using multiple complementary descriptors, including backbone RMSD, RMSF, protein secondary structure evolution (SSE), radius of gyration (Rg), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), molecular surface area (MolSA), ligand properties, and protein–ligand interaction analyses throughout the simulation period.

Results and discussion

Database division

Twenty seven molecules were allocated to the training set, and six were specifically selected for the test set. A progressive range of biological activity (pIC50) and structural diversity were used to divide the dataset.

Molecular alignment

With molecule 4 acting as the reference, every molecule in the dataset was correctly aligned (Fig. 1). Several validation techniques were used to thoroughly assess the predictive performance of the developed 3D-QSAR models (CoMFA and CoMSIA). First, the robustness of both models was validated by internal validation parameters. With R2=0.986, Q2=0.651, and SEE=0.069, the CoMFA model showed high statistical reliability, and the CoMSIA model performed similarly well with R2=0.985, Q2=0.665, and SEE=0.071. The predictive ability of the models on unknown data was further evaluated by external validation. Their strong predictive ability was demonstrated by the obtained external predictive coefficients (Rtext2=0.780 for CoMFA and Rtext2=0.985 for CoMSIA). Furthermore, CoMFA and CoMSIA’s stability and dependability were confirmed by the Qcv2 values of 0.713 and 0.937 obtained from the leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV), respectively. Y-randomization tests were performed to exclude the possibility of chance correlation. The results confirmed the robustness and predictive reliability of the developed models, as all randomized models yielded significantly lower R2 and Q2 values than the original CoMFA and CoMSIA models. Furthermore, the calculated CRP2 values exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.5, indicating that the observed correlations were not generated by random chance. These findings demonstrate that the models were not overfitted and possess genuine predictive capability.

The reliability of both models was further supported by the high F-values obtained from the Fisher test, reaching 295.800 for the CoMFA model and 275.800 for the CoMSIA model, confirming the statistical significance and robustness of the developed models. CoMFA and CoMSIA contour map analysis

The contour maps highlight important areas surrounding the molecule where particular chemical characteristics have a significant impact on its biological activity. The favorability of a descriptor at a specific location is indicated by color in these maps; for instance, a value of 80 indicates a highly favorable location for the corresponding property, whereas a value of 20 indicates an unfavorable position. Contours near certain functional groups suggest that fine-tuning these groups, such as enhancing their ability to donate or accept hydrogen bonds, could lead to improved activity. For instance, if contours appear around hydroxyl or acid groups, hydrogen bonding in these regions is crucial. Contours surrounding aromatic rings highlight the role of electrostatic effects. For the electrostatic field (panel e), the map shows that positions para to the benzene ring are favorable, while meta positions are unfavorable, and the COMFA (panel a) includes all of its descriptors. Therefore, in designing new molecules, introducing atoms with a strong electrostatic character in the para position and avoiding such groups in meta positions could optimize activity. Steric and hydrophilic interactions also play a crucial role, particularly in the hexene region. The contour maps suggest that introducing bulkier groups to enhance steric interactions or polar functional groups to improve hydrophilicity could improve binding affinity and overall activity. For alkyl chains, the contour maps indicate that reducing bulkiness while maintaining hydrophobicity could enhance interactions with the target protein, for example by shortening the chain or substituting smaller hydrophobic groups. Regions without contours indicate areas where modifications may have little impact, allowing optimization efforts to focus on the most influential structural features identified in the maps. Overall, these contour maps guide rational design by showing where modifications are likely to increase or decrease biological activity (see Fig. 2).

According to the results from the 3D-QSAR models, the newly designed compounds exhibit significant potential for inhibiting the proliferation of HCT-116 colorectal cancer cells, with the predicted pIC50 values suggesting potent activity at low concentrations. This indicates that these compounds could serve as promising agents for colorectal cancer treatment. Additionally, we plan to assess the pharmacokinetics of these molecules and explore their potential for biomedical applications. Prediction of the in-silico physicochemical and pharmacokinetic properties

The newly generated 20 compounds (V1–V20) were selected from the designed molecules based on their superior activity compared to the reference compound used in the model. Following this initial selection, the compounds were evaluated for physicochemical and pharmacokinetic properties using the SwissADME tool (Table 5) to assess their drug-likeness. According to Lipinski’s rule of five, ideal drug-like molecules should adhere to criteria such as LogP ≤ 5, hydrogen bond donors (HBD) ≤ 5, and hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA) ≤ 10. Additionally, Veber’s rules emphasize the importance of rotatable bonds (n-ROTB ≤ 10) and topological polar surface area (TPSA ≤ 140 Å2) to ensure good membrane permeability and oral bioavailability. [71], [72], [73] All 20 compounds, as indicated in Table 5, mainly adhere to these rules. Because of their advantageous drug-like qualities, V2, V5, V7, V8, V9, V12, V13, V14, and V15 stand out among the tested compounds. These compounds have a limited number of rotatable bonds, which indicates balanced molecular flexibility; moderate TPSA values, which ensure good permeability; acceptable LogP values, which range from 2.77 to 4.54; and optimal oral bioavailability. In particular, V7 and V8 show strong profiles with favorable LogP values (3.42 and 2.77, respectively), low rotatable bond counts (4 and 3 respectively), and moderate TPSA (50.85 Å2), indicating a high potential for permeability and bioavailability. With moderate LogP values (3.75–4.54) and TPSA values ranging from 77.15 to 118.19 Å2, V2, V5, and V9 also maintain good permeability and bioavailability. In contrast, compounds V10, V11, and V12 exhibit good drug-likeness but show deviations in one or more criteria, such as slightly higher LogP values. (V16–V20) violate multiple drug-likeness rules,such as Veber’s criteria, and their high number of rotatable bonds (V15–V20). Although (V10–V18) comply with Lipinski’s rule, which may impact their binding stability. Overall, V2, V5, V7, V8, and V9 emerge as the most promising candidates due to their optimal balance of drug-likeness parameters, bioavailability, and structural stability. Their synthetic accessibility scores (ranging from 7.71 to 9.34) further support their feasibility for synthesis and optimization, making them strong candidates for further development. Among them, V7 and V8 represent the best synthetic accessibility scores, with values of 7.95 and 7.71, respectively. Based on important criteria, the compounds’ pharmacokinetic analysis shows encouraging outcomes for a number of candidates. First, intestinal absorption is regarded as excellent when it surpasses 30%. All of the compounds tested (V1 to V15) have absorption levels that are better than 92%, demonstrating their high capacity for absorption in the human gut. Interestingly, V8 exhibits a perfect absorption rate of 100%, demonstrating its high oral bioavailability. Going on to the volume of distribution (VDss), compounds like (V1–V15) show high VDss values between 0.67 and 1.95, indicating that they are widely distributed throughout the body. Conversely, compounds with negative LogVDss values, such as (V15–V20), show poor distribution, indicating limited distribution. 74 Compounds such as V1, V2, V5, and V9 have low blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability, as indicated by LogBB values below -1. [75], [76], [77] As a result, they are unlikely to pass through the brain. The excretion profiles of compounds V1 to V20 suggest that all molecules exhibit relatively low total clearance values, ranging from 0.119 to 0.713, indicating that they tend to persist in the body and are not rapidly eliminated. Specifically, compounds such as V3 (0.281), V9 (0.119), and V19 (0.275) exhibit the lowest clearance, suggesting they may accumulate more in the body over time, while compounds like V11 (0.713) and V6 (0.687) show slightly higher clearance but still remain below the ideal threshold of 1.0, indicating slow elimination. In terms of toxicity, the majority of compounds do not present AMES toxicity, which is a favorable sign, indicating they are not likely to cause mutagenic effects. V12 and V13, however, exhibit AMES toxicity (YES). Compounds like V11, V14, V15, V16, V17, V18, V19, and V20 also exhibit hepatotoxicity (YES), suggesting that they may be potentially harmful to the liver and need more research. These results led to the elimination of molecules with subpar ADMET characteristics, leaving V2, V5, V7, and V8 as the most promising options for additional development and optimization.

Molecular docking

Re-docking the co-crystallized ligands into their corresponding target proteins verified the accuracy of the docking technique. An RMSD of 0.926 Å for CDK2 (6GUE) confirmed the close alignment between the re-docked poses and the X-ray crystallographic conformations, guaranteeing the accuracy of the docking method. In order to assess how well the designed compounds interacted with the target protein, docking scores were calculated as free binding energies (ΔG). Among the tested molecules, compounds V8 and V7 showed the strongest affinities, making them the top binders, according to the results (Table 7, Figs. 5,6). In particular, V8 outperformed the reference ligand with a ΔGb of −14.94 kcal/mol and V7 with a ΔGb of −13.33 kcal/mol. It was discovered that these substances shared a binding site with the control inhibitor, indicating a comparable interaction profile. These docked compounds were found to have average ΔGb values of −13.27 and −13.09 kcal/mol, respectively. However, due to limited hydrogen bonding and suboptimal hydrophobic complementarity, 4-(2-methyl-3-propan-2-yl-imidazol-4-yl)-N-(4-methylsulfonylphenyl)pyrimidin-2-amine shows weaker binding. The significantly lower binding affinities of V2 (ΔGb = −3.37 kcal/mol) and V5 (ΔGb = −3.40 kcal/mol) indicate poor interaction with the target, most likely because they lack essential hydrophobic or charged groups required for stable binding. The significantly lower binding affinities of V2 (ΔGb = −3.37 kcal/mol) and V5 (ΔGb = −3.40 kcal/mol) indicate poor interaction with the target, most likely because they lack essential hydrophobic or charged groups required for stable binding. V7 has several hydrophobic interactions, especially with TYR15, VAL18, LEU134, and ALA144, according to the molecular docking analysis, which helps to keep it stable in the binding pocket. It also strengthens its network of interactions by forming three important hydrogen bonds with ASP127, LYS129, and ASN132. The complex is further stabilized by the presence of a salt bridge with ASP145, indicating strong electrostatic complementarity. In a similar manner, V8 exhibits its capacity to sustain meaningful contacts with the target protein by participating in significant hydrophobic interactions with PRO155, VAL156, GLU172, and PRO271. Additionally, it contributes to its binding affinity by forming a strong hydrogen bond with PRO228. Alternatively, the positive control, while exhibiting some hydrophobic and hydrogen bonding interactions, does not demonstrate the same level of stabilization observed in V7 and V8. The superior binding profile of V7 and V8, characterized by a greater number of stabilizing interactions, suggests that these compounds hold stronger potential for effective inhibition. Given these promising results, compounds V7 and V8 will proceed to molecular dynamics simulations for further evaluation of their stability and behavior in a more physiologically relevant environment. To gain deeper insight into the binding mechanisms underlying the docking scores, a detailed protein–ligand interaction analysis was carried out for the top-ranked compounds V7 and V8. For V7, the docking pose revealed an extensive interaction network within the CDK2 active site. Multiple hydrophobic contacts were established with TYR15, VAL18, LEU134, and ALA144, reinforcing the anchoring of the ligand within the hydrophobic core Fig. 7 . Furthermore, V7 ensured directional stabilization by forming three crucial hydrogen bonds with ASP127, LYS129, and ASN132 at distances ranging from 1.78 to 2.64 Å. Crucially, a salt bridge interaction with ASP145 was detected, which further improved the complex’s stability by supplying strong electrostatic complementarity. Likewise, V8 showed a good binding pattern with hydrophobic interactions with PRO155, VAL156, GLU172, and PRO271 (see Fig. 8). The compound’s binding affinity was enhanced by a noteworthy hydrogen bond with PRO228 (1.97 Å), indicating that this contact might be crucial for ligand placement inside the catalytic pocket. When combined, V7’s numerous hydrophobic contacts, stable hydrogen bonds, and salt bridge, as well as V8’s reliable hydrophobic and hydrogen bonding interactions, account for their higher docking scores when compared to the reference ligand. These compounds’ capacity to create a more stable and robust binding mode is demonstrated by this thorough interaction analysis, which makes them attractive candidates for further molecular dynamics simulations to confirm their inhibitory potential (see Fig. 4).

Molecular dynamics (MD)

To gain deeper insight into the stability and conformational dynamics of the protein–ligand complexes, several key parameters, including RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration (Rg), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), molecular surface area (MolSA), and protein–ligand interaction profiles, were evaluated during the molecular dynamics simulations. The lead compounds V7 and V8 were compared with the positive control, which was used as the reference ligand because of its known biological activity. These analyses enabled a comprehensive assessment of the structural stability and binding behavior of the investigated complexes (Fig. 9, Fig. 10, Fig. 11). The RMSD profiles were analyzed to assess the structural stability of the investigated complexes throughout the 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations (Fig. 9). For the V7–CDK2 complex, the ligand RMSD initially increased and fluctuated between 10 and 20 ns before reaching a stable plateau around 1.75 Å after 20 ns. The protein backbone RMSD rapidly equilibrated and remained stable throughout the simulation. Similarly, the V8–CDK2 complex exhibited an initial increase in ligand RMSD followed by stabilization around 1.50 Å after approximately 40 ns, while the protein RMSD remained within a narrow fluctuation range, indicating good structural stability. A comparable behavior was observed for the positive control (PC)–CDK2 complex, where the ligand RMSD stabilized around 1.50 Å after 40 ns and the protein backbone rapidly reached equilibrium. The slightly higher fluctuations observed for the ligands compared to the protein suggest conformational adjustments within the binding pocket while maintaining stable interactions with the receptor. Overall, the RMSD results demonstrate that all investigated complexes remained structurally stable during the entire simulation period, confirming the favorable binding behavior of V7 and V8 toward the CDK2 active site. The convergence of the backbone RMSD profiles after the initial equilibration phase, together with the consistency observed for the complementary structural descriptors (RMSF, SSE, Rg, SASA, MolSA, and PSA), indicates that the 100 ns production simulations were sufficient to characterize the structural stability and dynamic behavior of the investigated complexes. The RMSF plot (Fig. 9) illustrates the fluctuations of each residue throughout the simulation. RMSF analysis was conducted for the CDK2 protein (293 amino acids), the reference molecule, and the two potential therapeutic candidates. The results indicate that the binding site residues exhibited only slight fluctuations. The low average RMSF values obtained for V7 (0.84 Å), V8 (0.86 Å), and the positive control (0.87 Å) further indicate limited residue flexibility and support the overall structural stability of the protein during the simulations. Furthermore, protein secondary structure element (SSE) analysis confirmed the structural stability of CDK2 throughout the 100 ns simulations. For the V7-CDK2 complex, the average secondary structure content consisted of 26.39% α-helices and 15.35% β-strands, corresponding to a total SSE content of 41.74%. Similarly, the V8-CDK2 complex maintained 26.45% α-helices and 15.99% β-strands, resulting in a total SSE content of 42.44%. The SSE profiles remained nearly constant during the simulations, indicating that ligand binding did not induce significant alterations in the overall protein fold and confirming that the secondary structure of CDK2 remained highly conserved throughout the simulation period. On the other hand, RMSF plots revealed minimal variations across the protein for all the examined complexes, except for certain specific residues. Notably, residues Thr41 and Glu42 reached values of 5 and 3.17 for V8, and 5 and 3.63 for PC. For compound V7, more pronounced fluctuations were observed at residues SER_46 and GLY_153, with respective values of 6.71 and 4.45 (Fig. 9). The four primary forms of protein–ligand interactions that usually take place are ionic, hydrophobic, hydrogen-bonding, and water bridges. Contact histograms verified that the V7-CDK2 complex was stable through P-L contacts. The V7-CDK2 histograms showed that the ligand and the active-site amino acids (Asp86, Asn74, Asp235, Thr231, Glu12, Tyr236, Asn73, Asp145, and Lys178) interacted via hydrogen bonds. Ile10, His71, Phe82, Leu134, Ile209, Pro234, and Pro238 were the main mediators of the hydrophobic contacts of V7, with an ionic interaction between the ligand and Val18. Furthermore, the CDK2 residues Lys9, Glu12, Gly13, Thr14, Tyr15, Asn73, Asn74, Asp86, Gln131, Asn131, Asn132, Asp145, Gly176, Lys178, Thr231, Ser232, Met233, Asp235, and Tyr236 interacted with the V7 ligand primarily through water bridges. The protein–ligand interactions involving V8 were also characterized. These interactions included several hydrophobic bonds with more than 9 residues of CDK2, in addition to 30 hydrogen bonds, with particularly strong interactions with Asn136 and Glu138. Furthermore, 55 residues interact with the ligand V8 through water bridges, and 13 residues form ionic interactions. In contrast, the reference molecule formed a complex with CDK2 through four hydrogen bonds with Lys33, Leu83, Asp86, and Lys89, hydrophobic bonds with Ile10, Tyr15, Val18, Ala31, Phe80, Phe82, and Leu134, and water bridges with Glu8, Ile10, Tyr15, His84, Gln85, Asp86, Lys89, and Gln131. Additionally, it formed only one ionic interaction, with the Asp86 residue of CDK2.

Additional ligand property analyses further supported the stability of the investigated complexes. Conclusion

In this study, a 3D-QSAR analysis was performed on 33 cannabis derivatives using CoMFA and CoMSIA techniques to develop reliable predictive models. These models were thoroughly validated through internal and external validation approaches, along with Y-randomization and LOOCV tests, ensuring their robustness and predictive accuracy. The contour maps produced by the CoMFA and CoMSIA models identified important structural areas where these compounds’ anticancer properties might be improved by chemical changes. These insights led to the proposal of twenty new molecules, all of which showed predicted inhibitory activities (pIC50) that were higher than those of reference compound 04. Among these, molecules V2, V5, V7, and V8 were particularly noteworthy due to their promising ADMET profiles and favorable drug-like properties. The potential of these candidates was further confirmed by molecular docking studies, which revealed that V7 and V8 had a strong binding affinity for the CDK2 active site, mainly through hydrophobic and hydrogen bonding interactions. Notably, when compared to the reference compound 4-(2-methyl-3-propan-2-yl-imidazol-4-yl)-∼N-(4-methylsulfonylphenyl)pyrimidin-2-amine, both V7 and V8 showed increased binding stability within the protein’s active site. In particular, V8 demonstrated a more stable binding profile by establishing multiple strong hydrogen bonds and water–bridge interactions and achieving a marginally lower RMSD. On the other hand, V7 demonstrated greater flexibility, displaying noticeable oscillations at particular residues, which could be useful in accommodating different protein configurations. The predicted activities of the twenty newly designed molecules are still computational, even though the 3D-QSAR models were created and verified using 33 experimentally characterized cannabis-derived compounds. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed for the two most promising candidates, V7 and V8, together with the positive control complex, to evaluate the stability and dynamic behavior of the protein–ligand complexes. The convergence and structural stability of the simulated systems were confirmed through complementary analyses, including RMSD, RMSF, protein secondary structure evolution (SSE), radius of gyration (Rg), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), molecular surface area (MolSA), ligand properties, and protein–ligand interaction profiles. These computational findings provide a strong rationale for subsequent in vitro and in vivo investigations to experimentally validate the anticancer potential of the identified compounds. Likewise, the docking and ADMET predictions provide valuable insights into binding affinity and drug-like properties; however, experimental validation remains essential to fully confirm their biological activity, pharmacokinetic behavior, and therapeutic potential. Overall, the present study establishes a robust computational framework for the rational identification and optimization of promising cannabis-derived CDK2 inhibitors for colorectal cancer.
