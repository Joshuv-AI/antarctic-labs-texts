Seroprevalence and associated factors of Epstein-Barr virus among blood donors in Sana’a, Yemen
Authors: Qais Yusuf Abdullah, Abdulrahman Abdullah Humaid, Khloud Mohammed Hasen Al-Matari, Wadhah Hassan Edrees, Wadee Abdullah Al-Shehari, Saad Al-Arnoot, Ahlam Mohammad Al-Matari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) is a widely distributed oncogenic virus that is associated with various human malignancies and autoimmune disorders. There are no data about the prevalence of EBV among healthy individuals, particularly healthy blood donors. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the seroprevalence of EBV and associated risk factors among male blood donors in the Sana’a capital of Yemen.MethodsThis study was designed as a cross-sectional study and conducted among 200 healthy male blood donors who attended the blood donation centers over a period of one year, from June 2023 to May 2024.ResultsOf the 200 blood donors, 80% and 8% were positive for anti-EBV-IgG and anti-EBV-IgM, respectively. A higher rate of anti-EBV-IgG was reported at 100% and 80.7%, respectively, among those aged 50–55 years and married participants. Moreover, anti-EBV-IgM was more found among those aged 40–49 years and single participants (9.6% and 8.4%, respectively). Both anti-EBV-IgG and anti-EBV-IgM were higher among blood donors inhabiting an urban area. The majority of participants with seropositivity for anti-EBV-IgG experienced swelling of lymph nodes (93.6%), nasopharyngitis (96.4%), fever (80.3%), and fatigue (80.6%). Furthermore, the highest rates of anti-EBV IgG were found in participants who chewed Khat (80.9%), used tobacco dipping (shammah) (96.7%), received blood transfusions (81.1%), and had previous surgery (86.4%). However, only tobacco dipping (shammah) showed a statistically significant association with anti-EBV IgG seropositivity (P=0.007).ConclusionThe prevalence of EBV was remarkably high among the population included in the study, reflecting its widespread presence in Yemen, which potentially leads to significant clinical complications in patients with weakened immune systems. These findings emphasize the importance of ongoing epidemiological surveillance, improved routine screening procedures for EBV among blood donors, and strengthened health awareness and education programs to limit and prevent the spread of the virus.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Blood donors, Epstein-Barr virus infections, Prevalence, Oncogenic viruses, Risk factors, Yemen

Introduction

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), commonly known as human herpesvirus 4, is highly prevalent and one of the most common human viruses. EBV belongs to the family Orthoherpesviridae and genus Lymphocryptovirus, and it is approximately 122–180 nm in diameter, enveloped, and double-stranded DNA [1]. The EBV is primarily transmitted through oral secretions, such as kissing, sharing drinks, food, or toothbrushes. It can also be transmitted through blood transfusions, sexual contact, organ transplants, or contact with inanimate objects contaminated with the virus [2]. Individuals infected with EBV typically experience symptoms of fatigue, fever, loss of appetite, rash, sore throat, swollen glands in the neck, muscle weakness and pain, and an enlarged spleen and liver [3].

Factors that increase the likelihood of people being infected with the EBV include environmental and socioeconomic factors such as poor social and economic conditions, living in crowded areas, and the level of education of parents [4]. In developing countries, children typically acquire the EBV infection during their first few years of life, and universal seroconversion is frequently observed by the age of three to four. Conversely, in developed countries, infection is frequently delayed until adolescence [5]. Globally, an estimated 95% of the healthy adult population is infected with EBV [1, 6]. The prevalence of EBV varies worldwide and increases with age. In the United States, the prevalence of EBV increased from 54.1% among children aged 6–8 years to 82.9% among adults aged 18–19 years [7]. Furthermore, the prevalence of EBV was reported to be 85.3% in the United Kingdom [8], 88.5% in Taiwan [9], and 86.1% in Bahrain [10].

EBV is considered a virus that has been identified as an oncogenic virus and is responsible for persistent infection in human B lymphocytes. Moreover, EBV uncommonly causes infectious mononucleosis and is closely associated to various malignant, non-malignant, and premalignant diseases [11, 12]. Moreover, it is estimated that EBV is responsible for approximately 200,000 cancer cases worldwide each year [13]. Additionally, EBV was identified as the primary cause of multiple sclerosis in a comprehensive study that spanned 20 years and involved a population of 10 million. A recent EBV infection resulted in a 32-fold increase in the risk of developing multiple sclerosis [14].

Blood-banking organizations implement stringent regulations to mitigate the risk of pathogen transmission during transfusions; however, concerns remain regarding the transmission of untested pathogens, including EBV [15]. Despite blood bank procedures to ensure blood safety, which include reducing white blood cells to reduce the EBV genome, the virus can still be detected in blood products. Therefore, these procedures are not sufficient to eliminate EBV, which could pose a potential risk to blood product recipients, particularly those with immunodeficiencies [15, 16]. Reports of EBV transmission among healthy blood donors are rare. However, three reports have documented the prevalence of the virus among blood donors at 97.9% in Qatar [17], 79.8% in Iraq [18], and 20% in Ghana [19]. Transmission of EBV through blood products has serious effects, especially in young children and people with weak immune systems, who are more likely to develop post-transfusion lymphoproliferative disorders (PTLD). This raises concerns about the long-term health of those who are at risk [15, 20]. Since EBV infection can lead to significant complications, including PTLD, healthcare providers must remain vigilant in managing these risks through careful selection and monitoring of blood products for patients who are at high risk [20, 21].

In Yemen, which lacks available resources and effective disease surveillance systems [22–24], previous studies have assessed the prevalence of some viruses among patients [25–28] and healthy blood donors [29, 30]. Very few studies have focused on detecting the prevalence of EBV among cancer patients [31, 32] and rheumatoid arthritis [33], and there is no data on the prevalence of EBV among blood donors. Furthermore, the technologies used to detect viruses among blood donors are not advanced, and the procedure does not include detection of the EBV in donor blood samples. This means that blood recipients, especially those with weakened immune systems, are at risk of complications caused by the EBV. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of EBV and associated risk factors among blood donors in the Yemeni capital, Sana’a. The findings of this investigation will be essential for health officials in Yemen to revise policies that are designed to mitigate the health risks and reduce the burden of infectious diseases associated with blood transfusion.

Materials and methods

Study area and period

This study was designed as a cross-sectional study and conducted over a period of one year, from June 2023 to May 2024, in the Sana’a capital of Yemen. In addition, this study was carried out among healthy blood donors who attended the blood donation centers, including Al-Kuwait University Hospital, the National Blood Transfusion and Research Center, and the National Center for Central Public Health Laboratories. These institutions were selected due to their high donor turnout, ensuring an adequate sample size for statistical analysis and epidemiological assessment. Study participant recruitment followed the guidelines set by the World Health Organization for blood donation.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study included all male donors between the ages of 18 and 55 years, as the majority of donors in Yemen are male due to prevailing sociocultural factors that restrict female participation in blood donations. They were in good health, meaning they were free of hepatitis B and C viruses, HIV, syphilis, and malaria parasites, as confirmed by the standard pre-donation screening procedures performed at blood donation centres. The participants provided informed consent and allowed blood sample collection. However, this study excluded donors younger than 18 or older than 60 years, those with a history of chronic diseases, those diagnosed with immunocompromise, and those who had experienced an acute infection before donation.

Sample size

According to a previous report from Yemen by Al-Mahbashi et al. [33], the prevalence of EBV among patients was 8%. The sample size was calculated using Cochrane’s formula (n = Z²P(1-P)/d²) with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error. The minimum required sample size was 113 participants. Therefore, the final sample size was increased to 200 blood donors for this study to provide a sufficient number of cases within each category and to enhance the statistical power.

Specimen collection

Approximately 5 ml of blood sample was collected from each participant under sterile conditions by venipuncture and transferred into a labeled sterile tube. The collected blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to separate the serum, which was later transferred to Eppendorf tubes and stored at -20 °C until serological testing.

Data collection

The necessary information was collected from study participants using a structured, face-to-face questionnaire adapted from previous studies [34–35], with modifications to suit the study setting. The questionnaire included sociodemographic information such as age group, place of residence (rural/urban), marital status (married/single), educational level (secondary/university), and income level (middle/low). Furthermore, risk factors were clearly defined: a density of more than three people per room was used to measure overcrowding. Additionally, clinical symptoms, such as lymphadenopathy, were recorded based on the donor’s self-reported medical history during the standard pre-donation screening process. The information was written on a questionnaire sheet that was originally made in Arabic and then translated into English after the data was collected.

Serological assays

The screening of EBV viral capsid antigen (VCA) IgM and IgG antibodies in prepared serum was performed by using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using a Thermo Scientific Multiskan® EX Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) using a commercially available kit, Anti-VCA IgM and Anti-VCA IgG (RD Ratio Diagnostics GmbH, Germany), based on the manufacturer’s instructions. Serological results were interpreted based on the optical density (OD) values: Positive: OD ratio > 1.1, indicating EBV seropositive. Indeterminate: OD ratio between 0.9 − 1.1, requiring retesting. Negative: OD ratio < 0.9, indicating no detectable antibodies.

Quality control measures

Quality control procedures were implemented throughout the study to guarantee accuracy, reliability, and reproducibility. These procedures included positive and negative controls incorporated into each ELISA test run to verify test performance. Furthermore, each sample was tested twice, and any sample that yielded indeterminate results was retested twice to verify the results.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS version 26; IBM Corporation, USA). Descriptive analysis was used to summarize the results of the subcategories of variables, which are presented in tables as frequencies and percentages. Associations between EBV seropositivity and potential risk factors were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square (χ²) and Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Furthermore, multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify the independent predictors of anti-EBV seropositivity. The strength of association was estimated, and the outcomes were expressed as Adjusted Odds Ratios (AOR) with their corresponding 95% Confidence Intervals (CI). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the study subjects

Two hundred (200) blood donors participated in this study. The largest percentage of donors (39%) were between the ages of 18 and 29, followed by the 30–39 age group at 33%, the 40–50 age group at 26%, and the 50–55 age group at only 2%. Regarding educational level, 52% of donors had a high school education or below, while 48% had a university education. The majority of participants (70%) were married, and 89% resided in urban areas. Regarding income status, 69% reported a medium income, while 31% reported a low income. Only 27% of the donors reported visiting a dental clinic regularly (Table 1).

Association between risk factor and prevalence of EBV

Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis revealed a statistically strong association between age (P = 0.004, AOR = 2.85) and anti-EBV-IgG prevalence, as well as overcrowding (P = 0.041, AOR = 2). However, educational level showed a statistically significant inverse association (P = 0.038, AOR = 0.42) with anti-EBV-IgG prevalence. Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis also revealed that Shammah users were 6.3 times more likely to have positive EBV IgM antibody test results than non-users (AOR = 6.30, 95% CI: 1.20–32.8, P = 0.031). In contrast, chewing Khat and smoking did not show a statistically significant independent association (P > 0.05) with positive EBV IgM antibodies (Table 6).

Discussion

Currently, there is no effective vaccine available to combat the EBV and prevent the risks it may cause in humans, although it was identified as a cancer-causing virus 60 years ago [36]. In this study, the prevalence of EBV was recorded at 80% for anti-EBV IgG antibodies among blood donors, which is comparable to similar studies recorded at 79.8% in Iraq [18] and 81.4% in Iran [37]. In contrast, this finding is lower than reported at 97.9% in Qatar [17], 96.4% in Turkey [38], and 91.0% in Jordan [35]. The discrepancy in results may be attributed to the fact that this study was conducted only on age groups over 18 years, which may explain the low incidence rate recorded in these results. Further comprehensive epidemiological studies are needed to examine the prevalence of EBV among healthy individuals of all age groups across Yemen.

The results of this study showed that 8% of donors had IgM antibodies, and this is consistent with those of a report from Yemen [23] but inconsistent with the findings from Qatar [17]. In addition, IgM antibodies are typically present in blood serum in the early stages of infection and disappear within 4–6 weeks of illness [39]. In contrast, IgG antibodies are detected in blood serum within 2–4 weeks after the onset of illness and remain detectable throughout life. Therefore, EBV VCA IgG is commonly used in epidemiological studies [9]. The presence of EBV-IgM antibodies in the blood donor is not conclusive proof of the virus’s presence, but it remains an important clinical indicator of a recent primary infection. In terms of blood transfusion safety, donors’ seropositivity of anti-EBV IgM can be considered a precautionary measure to reduce the risk of transmission of this virus to recipients, particularly those who are more vulnerable. Therefore, laboratory surveillance using advanced EBV detection techniques such as, rather than relying solely on serological testing, is crucial when transfusing blood to immunocompromised individuals.

According to global reports, the presence of antibodies to EBV varies among infected individuals based on age, forming a U-shaped pattern. Infants and individuals aged > 50 years exhibit increased antibody levels [40–41]. This report aligns with our findings, which revealed that age emerged as a clear risk factor for the prevalence of EBV IgG antibodies, and it also is supported by other reports [9, 35]. Furthermore, some studies conducted in Arabic nations [17, 18, 34, 42] as well as in Thailand [43, 44], Japan [45], and the USA [46] documented that the older age group was more susceptible to EBV infection. This increase among the elderly is attributed to the decline in cellular immune response associated with aging [41, 47]. Furthermore, it can be explained by the cumulative probability over time as a result of continuous exposure to this virus, which increases the chances of the accumulation of EBV IgG antibodies that persist after the initial infection in the body. These results emphasize the necessity of improved surveillance and early detection of EBV in the elderly to protect them from further complications.

These findings revealed that married individuals had slightly higher levels of EBV antibodies compared to single individuals, though this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.418). This is consistent with some previous studies that have shown that marital status is not a risk factor for EBV transmission [9, 34, 48]. Consequently, additional social variables, including the age at which individuals marry and the duration of their marriages, should be considered in future studies. Additionally, the hypothesis that early sexual contact is a risk factor for EBV transmission in comparable social and cultural contexts should be assessed.

Regarding residence, EBV prevalence was numerically higher among blood donors coming from urban areas, and these results were consistent with a previous report [35] and in apparent disagreement with the other reports [38, 18]. These differences in results may be due to sample size, study sample, study area, and diagnostic techniques used. Conduct research on the environmental and social factors in urban areas that may contribute to high infection rates and implement community health interventions to mitigate them.

Education plays a significant role in promoting public health and reducing the transmission of infectious diseases by increasing health awareness and adopting healthier behaviors. The results of this study documented that individuals with a low level of education are more susceptible to EBV infection, which is consistent with the results of previous studies [2, 35, 49] that indicated an association between education level and EBV infection rates. Therefore, education is a sign of behavioral and environmental factors that encourage the spread of viruses rather than a biological factor. Therefore, continued health education is crucial for reducing disease rates in the community.

This study is consistent with other literature, which has documented that EBV infection is closely linked to economic conditions, with early infection occurring in low-income communities and delayed infection in high-income populations [41]. It has been observed that low-income individuals were more susceptible to EBV infection. Furthermore, other studies have supported the findings of this study [34–35]. Therefore, developing successful prevention and control strategies requires ongoing research and studies to comprehend the social and economic factors related to EBV infection among various societal segments.

Our results were inconsistent with those of previous studies, which have shown that crowded living conditions play a significant role in increasing the risk of contracting the EBV, especially in early childhood [41, 50]. This finding revealed that there are no statistically significant associations between EBV seropositivity and smoking. These results contrast with a previous study conducted on patients with malignant and autoimmune diseases, which showed a strong association between infection with EBV and harmful habits such as smoking [8, 34, 51, 52]. Therefore, public health interventions and protective measures should be implemented to reduce smoking among the general population to mitigate the effects of EBV infection on cancer development.

Blood donors who chewed Khat had the numerically highest levels of anti-EBV-IgG. Chewing Khat is common in Yemen and has been linked to loss of appetite or malnutrition due to its ability to reduce the absorption of essential nutrients from the digestive system [53]. Furthermore, cathinone, one of Khat’s active ingredients, has been shown to affect the immune system [54–55].

Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis revealed that Shammah emerged as an independent risk factor for increasing the likelihood of recent EBV (IgM) infection. In Yemen, tobacco dipping (shammah) use was identified as a major risk factor for oral squamous cell carcinoma [56]. Consequently, it is imperative to establish strict policies that are designed to reduce the sale and distribution of tobacco (shammah) by imposing substantial taxes, as well as to implement awareness programs to inform the community about the hazards of tobacco use. In addition, this finding recorded a higher rate of EBV infection among individuals with a history of malaria infection, and this result was supported by other findings [57–58].

Notably, our analysis revealed no statistically significant association between EBV infection and some variables, likely due to the generally good health of donors, thereby diminishing their statistical impact. While including EBV testing in blood banks is crucial for enhancing blood transfusion safety, significant practical and financial challenges will limit the implementation of these protocols in resource-limited settings, such as Yemen. Although there is still a dearth of precise cost-effectiveness data, the initial screening costs may be outweighed by the long-term clinical costs of treating EBV-related complications, especially in children and in immunocompromised recipients. Given Yemen’s current dire economic situation, a viable and sustainable initial step toward enhancing blood quality in the local healthcare system could be a prioritized screening strategy that targets high-risk groups.

These results revealed that blood donors with blood type B had the highest prevalence of EBV, followed by those with blood type O, and the lowest prevalence among those with blood type AB blood. A similar study found that EBV infection was more prevalent among people with blood type AB and less prevalent among people with blood type O [18, 59]. The general relationship between EBV infection and ABO blood types is not fully understood and therefore requires further studies.

The strength and limitation of this study

The present investigation is the first to offer valuable information regarding the prevalence and risk factors of EBV among blood donors in Yemen, a population that has not been sufficiently investigated. These findings are crucial for public health policymakers, as they can use them to identify the most vulnerable groups and develop targeted interventions and preventive measures. These data also serve as the foundation for future research that will monitor the spread and transmission trends of this type of virus in Yemen. These estimates are crucial for providing clinicians with accurate data on the prevalence of EBV among blood donors, thereby enhancing the safety of medical procedures to prevent its transmission in blood banks. Despite the importance of this study, it has some shortcomings. This study only includes adult male donors, which restricts the generalizability of its findings to the entire Yemeni population. This study did not address the prevalence of EBV and its association with diseases it may cause, such as Hodgkin lymphoma. Furthermore, there aren’t enough resources for conducting EBNA-1 IgG or avidity tests, nor is there advanced diagnostic equipment for genetic testing for EBV, making this study crucial.

Conclusion

The results of this study showed a high seroprevalence of EBV antibodies among blood donors in the study area, suggesting prior exposure to the virus or chronic infection status. Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis showed that “shammah” is a separate factor that can predict recent EBV infection in the Yemeni community, while “crowding” and “age” are the main factors influencing the overall presence of EBV IgG antibodies in the population. Furthermore, urban residents showed higher rates of EBV antibodies than rural residents. Therefore, comprehensive studies on the epidemiology of EBV across the governorates of Yemen, including both sexes and age groups, are required to obtain a more accurate epidemiological picture. Therefore, effective management of EBV spread requires enhanced global cooperation to conduct comprehensive studies on the development of an effective vaccine and urgently seek approval for vaccines targeting all groups, particularly those at risk of developing EBV-associated diseases. EBV prevalence should be monitored in organ transplant recipients and patients with HIV. Furthermore, blood donation centers in Yemen should include EBV screening in their regular donor screening protocol. Additionally, it is essential to increase public awareness of EBV transmission, symptoms, potential complications, and preventive measures through educational programs and community outreach, particularly in younger individuals. In addition, future studies identifying EBV genotypes are necessary to more precisely understand the strain characteristics of the virus.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Science, Sana’a University, approved this study on 5 June 2024 (Reference No. BSD-03/09). This study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Protocol and Yemeni legal legislation. Permission to collect blood specimens and perform laboratory serological analysis was obtained through an official letter from the Faculty of Science addressed to the National Blood Transfusion and Research Center, Al-Kuwait University Hospital, and the National Center for Central Public Health Laboratories (NCPHL). This letter outlines the goals and significance of the study. Furthermore, written informed consent was obtained from all participants immediately after they received a brief explanation of the significance and scope of the study. Additionally, participants’ identities and privacy were protected through coding during the sample collection process, and the data were processed anonymously.
