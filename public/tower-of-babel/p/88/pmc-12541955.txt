Comprehensive analysis of GGCT genes in u’s triangle Brassica species: insights into abiotic stress responses and potential association of BnaGGCT11/26 with seed weight
Authors: Ali Shahzad, Muhammad Mudasir, Hameed Gul, Shareef Gul, Muhammad Shahzad, Modhi O Alotaibi, Afrah E Mohammed, Mohammed S Sheteiwy, Zaid Ulhassan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlutathione homeostasis, crucial for plant growth and stress adaptation, is regulated by the enzyme γ-glutamylcyclotransferase (GGCT). As the critical component of the glutathione recycling pathway, GGCT plays a vital role in maintaining cellular redox balance under normal and stressful conditions. While the GGCT gene family has been characterized in Arabidopsis thaliana, its diversity, evolution, and functional roles remain unexplored in the U’s triangle Brassica species, which includes important oilseed crops.ResultsWe identified and characterized 154 putative GGCT genes in U’s triangle Brassica species (Brassica nigra, 20; Brassica oleracea, 21; Brassica rapa, 18; Brassica juncea, 35; Brassica napus, 31; Brassica carinata, 29). Phylogenetic analysis grouped these GGCTs into three distinct clades, highlighting their structural and motif diversity. The diversity in cis-regulatory elements highlighted their role in growth and stress responses. Expression profiling of BnaGGCT genes revealed their differential expression patterns across the tissues at different developmental stages, such as BnaGGCT11 and BnaGGCT26, which are specifically expressed during late seed development phases. Furthermore, gene expression analysis under hormones and abiotic stresses indicated their contribution in stress responses as confirmed by eight selected BnaGGCT genes under drought and salt stresses. In addition, SNP analysis of BnaGGCT11 revealed a single-nucleotide variant that is significantly associated with seed weight and yield, potentially influencing gene function or translation efficiency.ConclusionCollectively, this study comprehensively identified GGCTs that enhance our understanding of the functional evolution of the GGCT gene family in U’s triangle Brassica species and lay the groundwork for further research into GGCT-mediated stress responses and seed development. In particular, the seed-specific expression of BnaGGCT11/26 and the SNP in BnaGGCT11 associated with seed weight and yield highlight their potential as candidates for marker-assisted breeding, but future functional validation will be required to substantiate their roles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07422-9.Keywords: GGCT, Brassica, Gene expression, Stresses, BnaGGCT11, Seed weight

Introduction

The γ-glutamyl cyclotransferase (GGCT) gene family encodes enzymes that are key players in the catabolism of glutathione (GSH), a tripeptide composed of glutamate, cysteine, and glycine, which acts as a crucial antioxidant in plants [1]. GSH is central to maintaining redox homeostasis, detoxifying reactive oxygen species (ROS), and enhancing plant stress tolerance [2, 3]. GGCT enzymes participate in the degradation of GSH through the γ-glutamyl cycle, producing 5-oxoproline, which is subsequently converted to glutamate to support various metabolic functions and cellular homeostasis [4–6]. Additionally, GGCTs contribute to detoxification by sustaining GSH turnover, providing substrates for glutathione S-transferase (GST)-mediated conjugation reactions that mitigate oxidative and xenobiotic stress in plants [7, 8].

In Arabidopsis thaliana, GGCT homologs (GGCT2;1, GGCT2;2, and GGCT2;3) demonstrate GSH degradation activity at physiologically relevant concentrations [9]. Additionally, these homologs have been experimentally confirmed to functionally complement a yeast mutant deficient in GSH degradation [5].These proteins contain a conserved GGCT-like domain (AIG2-like family1) [10] and, in some cases, a cation transport regulator (ChaC) domain The GGCT-like domain, typically consisting of approximately 137 amino acids, is involved in catalyzing the formation of pyroglutamic acid from γ-glutamyl dipeptides and plays central role glutathione metabolism [11]. They while also involve in some other biological processes, including pollen tube development, tolerance to heavy metals and salinity, and sulfur deficiency responses [12]. GGCT2 is a conserved plant protein containing a cation transport regulator (ChaC) domain, which plays a crucial role in mitigating heavy metal toxicity and oxidative stress by facilitating glutamate recycling and maintaining GSH homeostasis [5, 13, 14]. Genome-wide analyses of GGCT genes have been performed in major cereal crops such as rice and wheat. In rice, the expression of OsGGCT−1 and OsGGCT−2 genes was substantially elevated under drought and salt stress conditions, indicating their potential involvement in the tolerance mechanisms to abiotic stress [15, 16]. In wheat, the TaGGCT20 gene was found to be significantly linked with increased seed weight and its diameter (length and width), while the other gene family members (TaGGCT8, TaGGCT11, TaGGCT16, TaGGCT17, and TaGGCT20) were associated with drought and salt stress. These findings suggest that GGCT genes could be used as a marker-assisted selection tool to improve crop yield under abiotic stress [17]. However, although preliminary insights into GGCT gene function have been reported in cereals like rice and wheat, their functional characterization in the Brassica genus remains largely unexplored. Importantly, while no direct evidence currently links GGCT genes to seed traits in Brassica, studies in Arabidopsis have shown that GSH is essential for embryo development, seed maturation, and reproductive success [18–20]. Since GGCT enzymes regulate GSH turnover, variation in their activity could influence seed-related traits indirectly by modulating GSH availability during critical stages of seed development. This connection underscores a key knowledge gap and provides a strong rationale for investigating the GGCT gene family in Brassica.

The Brassica genus encompasses several economically significant cultivated crops. Among them, the six species (B. nigra, B. oleracea, B. rapa, B. carinata, B. juncea, B. napus) genetic relationships can be represented by the ‘U’ triangle theory [21, 22]. This theory posits that the diploid species served as the progenitors for amphidiploid species that developed through interspecific hybridization [23]. Whole-genome duplication (WGD) and triplication events have significantly shaped the Brassica genomes. In addition to the ancient α and β WGD events shared with A. thaliana, the Brassica lineage underwent an additional whole-genome triplication [24, 25], contributing to extensive gene duplication, chromosomal rearrangements, and functional diversification. The interplay of subgenome dominance, biased gene retention, and genome plasticity has driven the evolution of diverse morphotypes and adaptive traits in Brassica crops [26]. Due to their shared ancestry and varying ploidy levels, the U triangle species provide an excellent model for studying gene family evolution, polyploid genome dynamics, and diploidization processes [27, 28]. This comparative framework enables the dissection of how duplicated genes evolve across different genomic backgrounds and selective pressures [29], offering insights into gene diversification and subgenome-specific functional partitioning [27]. Despite the evolutionary and agricultural importance of Brassica species, the GGCT gene family remains poorly characterized in this genus. To address this gap, we identified members of the GGCT gene family in the U’s triangle Brassica species using A. thaliana GGCT genes as queries. We performed a comprehensive analysis, including gene structure characterization, phylogenetic relationships, syntenic analysis, and gene pattern. Furthermore, we conducted an association analysis of BnGGCT11 with seed weight and yield, and constructed a network of genes with transcription factors (TFs) and co-expressed genes to explore its potential functional relevance. Overall, these findings offer valuable insights into the evolution of GGCTs among Brassica species and provide a foundation for future studies on their roles in stress response and seed development.

Materials and methods

Identification of the GGCT proteins in U’s triangle Brassica species

To conduct the current study, we first acquired genome sequences, coding sequences, protein sequences, and annotation data for A. thaliana and U’s triangle Brassica species. Files for (A) thaliana (TAIR10) were obtained from TAIR website (http://www.arabidopsis.org), while data for five Brassica species, including (B) nigra (Sangam, v1.1), B. oleracea (JZS, v2.0), B. rapa (Chiifu, v3.5), B. juncea (Tumida T84-66, v2.0), and B. napus (ZS_HZAU, V1.0), were retrieved from the BRAD website (http://brassicadb.cn/). Finally, we used the BIO2DB website (http://Brassicadb.bio2db.com) to obtain the files for B. carinata. For the identification of GGCT proteins in these species, we performed the BLASTP analysis by utilizing the 12 GGCT protein sequences of A. thaliana as queries, with an E-value having a < 1e-5 threshold and minimum 50% alignment coverage [30]. Candidate proteins were further verified for the presence of GGCT-specific domains using PfamScan search (Table S1), the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (CDD), and the SMART database [31, 32]. Moreover, we used the ExPASy server (http://web.expasy.org/protparam/) to determine the physicochemical characteristics of GGCT proteins. Additionally, for the prediction of GGCT proteins’ subcellular localization, we used the online bioinformatics tool Plant-mPLoc (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/plant-multi/) [33].

Phylogenetic analysis

To perform the phylogenetic analysis of GGCT proteins, we used MEGA 11 software [34]. This phylogenetic analysis was conducted by using the 12 GGCT proteins from the A. thaliana along with the GGCT proteins from U’s triangle species. First, we aligned the protein sequences with MUSCLE and then constructed the phylogenetic tree using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method with Poisson model + Uniform Rates + Pairwise deletion, and 1000 bootstrap values. Finally, the constructed tree was visualized using the iTOL [35].

Analysis of chromosomal locations, duplications, and syntenic relationships of GGCT genes

To determine the chromosomal locations of GGCT genes, species GFF genome files were used to map them to the corresponding chromosomes, then MapChart software (v2.32) was used to visualize these chromosomal maps [36]. We conducted a comparative genomic analysis to determine the evolutionary dynamics of GGCT genes in A. thaliana and Brassica species. Nonsynonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates were calculated using TBtools, and the Ka/Ks ratio was used to infer selective pressures acting on these genes. Furthermore, synteny maps among orthologous GGCT genes were constructed using the Multiple Synteny Plotter, and their homologous relationships were visualized with Advanced Circos in Tbtools.

Gene structure and conserved motifs analysis of GGCTs

The exon–intron arrangements of GGCT genes were analyzed using the Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS 2.0, http://gsds.gao-lab.org/). Additionally, the conserved motifs in GGCT proteins were predicted using the MEME Suite (V5.5.0, https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme), with parameters set to a motif width of 6–200 amino acids, a maximum of 15 motifs, and an E-value threshold of < 1e − 5 [37]. Visualization of both gene structures and conserved motifs was carried out using TBtools [38].

Promoter analysis of GGCT genes

To perform the promoter analysis first 2000 bp sequences upstream of GGCT genes were extracted using TBtools, and then these promoter regions were submitted to the PlantCARE database (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) to predict potential cis-regulatory elements [39].

Gene expression pattern of BnaGGCTs

Expression data of BnaGGCTs were retrieved from the BnIR database (https://yanglab.hzau.edu.cn/BnIR) [40], which provides transcriptomic profiles under standard growth conditions as well as in response to diverse hormonal treatments, and abiotic stresses such as drought, heat, salinity, cold, freezing, and osmotic stress. The expression levels were normalized as log₂(TPM + 1) and graphically represented using TBtools for comparative visualization.

Plant growth, stress treatments and qRT-PCR analysis

We used the ZS11 for the BnaGGCT gene expression. First, we used the petri dishes with the wet filter paper for the seed germination. Once the seeds had fully germinated and developed complete hypocotyls and radicles, the seedlings were transferred to the hydroponic system, which contained the full-strength Hoagland solution. Subsequently, after two weeks, the seedlings were subjected to 20% PEG 6000 and 200 mM NaCl for induction of drought and salt stresses, respectively. Leaf samples were collected for RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis at 0, 3, 6, and 12 h time intervals. Samples were immediately put into liquid nitrogen to avoid degradation and then stored at −80 °C. Each experimental condition was replicated three times at the biological level. Furthermore, Total RNA was isolated from plant samples using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit, and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix Kit, following the manufacturer’s protocols. Gene-specific primers used for qRT-PCR were sourced from the qPrimerDB database (http://biodb.swu.edu.cn/qprimerdb) [41] and are listed in Table S2. qRT-PCR assays were conducted in accordance with the MIQE guidelines [42]. Bna.Actin7 was selected as the internal control for expression normalization because it has been widely validated and commonly used as a stable housekeeping gene, including stresses, in previous studies [30, 43–46]. The relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and are presented as mean ± SE of three biological replicates. Statistical significance was evaluated using Student’s t-test, with p < 0.05 considered significant.

SNP Association of the BnaGGCT11 with Seed Weight and Yield and Prediction of Functional Impact

The SNP information for the BnaGGCT11 was retrieved from the BnIR database. Among the identified SNPs in coding regions, one SNP was found to be associated with seed weight and yield. Trait association data and expression pattern of BnaGGCT11 at two seed development stages at the base of this SNP were also retrieved. The position, allele variation, and significance of association with seed-related traits were recorded and analyzed for potential functional relevance. To evaluate the potential functional consequences of the missense SNP in BnaGGCT11, a homology-based 3D structure of the protein was generated using AlphaFold, and then amino acid substitution was analyzed using the mCSM tool [47] to predict changes in protein stability. The predicted stability change (ΔΔG, kcal/mol) was recorded, where negative values indicate destabilization and positive values indicate stabilization.

Furthermore, the co-expression network of BnaGGCT11-related genes and their expression pattern was also retrieved from the BnIR database. Finally, gene expression data were visualized using TBtools after normalization as log₂(TPM + 1).

Results

Identification of GGCT genes in U’s triangle Brassica species

In the current study, 154 putative GGCT proteins in U’s triangle Brassica species have been identified by utilizing the 12 A. thaliana GGCT proteins. The distribution of GGCT proteins among the Brassica species is as follows: Brassica rapa (18), Brassica nigra (20), Brassica oleracea (21), Brassica juncea (35), Brassica napus (31), and Brassica carinata (29). In the absence of a standardized naming protocol for genes, we named them sequentially based on their chromosomal positions, starting with those on chromosome 1, and continuing with the remaining chromosomes (Table S3). A comprehensive analysis of GGCT proteins in Brassica species revealed a significant variation in their physiochemical properties. The protein length ranged from 99 (BnaGGCT16) to 924 (BcaGGCT15), with an average amino acid residues of 203 aa. Correspondingly, the molecular weights varied from 11,225 (BnaGGCT16) to 105030.43 (BcaGGCT15) daltons (Da). Regarding isoelectric point (pI), most GGCT proteins (122 out of 154) exhibited acidic properties with average pI values less than 6.5, while seven GGCT proteins were neutral, with pI values ranging from 6.5 to 7.5, and 25 GGCT proteins were alkaline with pI values greater than 7.5. In terms of protein stability, at the bases of predicted instability indexes of proteins, 84 GGCT proteins were predicted to be stable with an instability index less than 40, while the remaining 70 GGCT proteins were classified as unstable with an instability index greater than 40. Furthermore, the GRAVY values of GGCT proteins indicate that these proteins are hydrophilic, except for only two GGCT proteins (BniGGCT16, BcaGGCT15), which showed a hydrophobic nature. Subcellular localization prediction indicated that most GGCT proteins are primarily localized in the chloroplast, followed by the nucleus and cytoplasm (Table S3).

Phylogenetic analysis of the GGCTs in A. thaliana and U’s triangle Brassica species

We conducted the phylogenetic analysis to observe the evolutionary relationships of GGCT proteins between A. thaliana and the U’s triangle Brassica species. For this purpose, we constructed the neighbor-joining (NJ) tree by using 12 A. thaliana GGCT proteins along with 154 GGCT proteins of U’s triangle species. This analysis identified that GGCT proteins could be categorized into three distinct clades: GGCT-PS (γ-glutamyl cyclotransferase-plant specific), GGGACT (γ-glutamylamine cyclotransferase–like), and ChaC (Cation transport regulator homolog domain-containing GGCTs) (Fig. 1). The clade GGCT-PS was the largest taxonomic group, comprising a total of 92 members, including 7 members from (A) thaliana, 16 from (B) napus, 14 from B. carinata, 11 from B. rapa, 19 from B. juncea, 13 from B. oleracea, and 12 from B. nigra. In contrast, the clade GGGATC was the smallest, consisting of only 28 members, including 2 members from (A) thaliana, 5 from (B) carinata, 3 from B. rapa, 6 from B. napus, 3 from B. oleracea, 6 from B. juncea, and 3 from B. nigra. The ChaC clade covered 46 members, including 3 members from (A) thaliana, 4 from (B) rapa, 9 from B. napus, 10 from B. carinata, 5 from B. oleracea, 10 from B. juncea, and 5 from B. nigra (Fig. 1). Particularly, all GGCT members identified were specifically classified into previously established subgroups, together with Arabidopsis GGCT proteins [17], suggesting their functional similarities within subgroups.

Distribution and Chromosomal Positioning of GGCT Genes in U’s Triangle Brassica species

Chromosomal distribution revealed a non-uniform distribution across the chromosomes. Out of 154 GGCT genes, 47 were mapped to A-subgenome chromosomes, 45 to B-subgenome chromosomes, and 50 to C-subgenome chromosomes (Fig. 2). Additionally, the remaining 12 GGCT genes were located on putative random chromosomes (Table S3). Certain chromosomes exhibited a high density of GGCT genes, suggesting potential hotspots for GGCT gene evolution or function within these Brassica species. Such as, in Bra, chromosome A06 contained the maximum number of GGCT genes (4). In Bni, chromosome B08 contained the maximum 6 GGCT genes. In Bol, chromosome C07 contained 6 GGCT genes. In Bju, the maximum number of GGCT genes (6) was found at chromosome B02. Furthermore, the chromosomes C04 and C07 of Bna both harbored three GGCT genes, and chromosome B07 in Bca was enriched with 5 GGCT genes (Fig. 2).

Synteny and gene duplication analysis of GGCT genes in U’s triangle Brassica species

In the earlier studies, Brassica species displayed a broad range of co-axiality in protein-coding genes [25, 30]. Therefore, to determine whether orthologous GGCT genes are retained or deleted, we analyzed syntenic relationships between A. thaliana and U’s triangle Brassica species (Fig. 3). The findings indicated that the number of GGCT genes in allotetraploid Brassica species was lower than the number of diploid progenitors for each allotetraploid, suggesting that the orthologous GGCT gene pairs in Brassica species endured variation during polyploidization. Furthermore, several syntenic relationships among the GGCT genes were identified through synteny analysis. For instance, 13 BraGGCT, 14 BniGGCT, and 15 BolGGCT genes showed syntenic relationships with the 9, 10, and AtGGCT genes, respectively (Fig. 3, Table S4). Additionally, we found several orthologous GGCT gene pairs, including 18, 16, 29, 36, and 37 pairs between AtGGCT and BraGGCT, AtGGCT and BniGGCT, AtGGCT and BjuGGCT, BraGGCT and BjuGGCT, and BniGGCT and BjuGGCT, respectively. Moreover, we found 21, 30, 33, and 42 orthologous GGCTs between AtGGCT and BolGGCT, AtGGCT and BnaGGCT, BraGGCT and BnaGGCT, and BolGGCT and BnaGGCT, respectively. We identified 21, 30, and 38 orthologous GGCTs between AtGGCT and BcaGGCT, BniGGCT and BcaGGCT, and BolGGCT and BcaGGCT, respectively (Table S4). These findings revealed that GGCT gene pairs with syntenic relationships are broadly distributed across the genomes of A. thaliana and U’s triangle Brassica species.

Furthermore, investigating gene duplication within the GGCT gene family revealed substantial paralogous relationships. In Bju, 36 paralogous gene pairs were identified, of which 4 pairs were in the A subgenome, 6 pairs were in the B subgenome, and 22 pairs were between the two subgenomes, while the remaining 4 pairs were between the random chromosomes. Bna exhibited 33 paralogous pairs, with a subgenomic distribution of 5 gene pairs in A and 5 gene pairs in the C subgenome, while the remaining 23 pairs were between the two subgenomes. Correspondingly, Bca possessed 21 paralogous pairs, comprising one gene pair in subgenome B and 2 gene pairs in subgenome C, and 11 pairs were between the two subgenomes, while the remaining pairs were with the random chromosomes (Fig. 3d, e,f, Table S5). To explore the evolutionary constraints on GGCT genes in Arabidopsis and U’s triangle Brassica species, the rates of nonsynonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitutions, along with their ratio (Ka/Ks), were analyzed. The calculated Ka/Ks ratios were uniformly below 1 for all examined orthologous GGCT gene pairs (Table S4). A similar pattern of Ka/Ks ratios less than 1 was observed for paralogous gene pairs in Bju, Bna, and Bca (Table S5). These findings indicate that purifying selection has influenced GGCT genes over the course of their evolutionary history.

Conserved motif and gene structure analysis of GGCTs

To investigate the evolutionary relationships and functional interactions among members of the GGCT gene family, a comprehensive analysis of conserved protein motifs and structural features was conducted for all 154 identified GGCT proteins (Fig. 4). The results revealed that the exon count in GGCT genes within Brassica species typically ranged from 1 to 21. The members of the GGGACT group mostly possessed one exon, except for BnaGGCT24, BcaGGCT18, and BniGGCT12, which contained 2, 2, and 4 exons, respectively. In members of the ChaC group, the exon range ranged from 2 to 8 exons, while the highest range of exons exhibited by the GGCT-PS group members ranged from 4 to 21 exons. Furthermore, motif analysis revealed 15 conserved motifs within the GGCTs (Fig. 4a, Figure S1). These results revealed the motif specification within the groups, such as mostly GGCT-PS group members contain motifs 15, 3, 12, 1, and 6. Most members of group GGGACT contain motifs 5, 8, and 10, while most members of the ChaC group contain motifs 2, 7, 4, and 9. Furthermore, GGCTs with similar protein motif compositions exhibited distinct clustering patterns, aligning closely with the phylogenetic tree presented in Fig. 1. This observation suggests a correlation between conserved protein motifs, phylogenetic relationships, and functional conservation within the GGCT family across Brassica species.

Promoter analysis of GGCT genes

To explore the regulatory mechanisms controlling GGCT gene expression, we carried out a promoter analysis aimed at identifying potential cis-acting elements within the upstream promoter regions. These cis-regulatory elements play a crucial role in modulating gene transcription. By using the PlantCARE database, we identified four types of cis-elements, including hormone-responsive, light-responsive, development-related, and stress-responsive, suggesting their involvement in diverse physiological and environmental responses (Fig. 5, Table S6,S7). From these cis-regulatory elements, we found a total of 10 cis-elements that were related to hormone responsiveness, specifically for abscisic acid responsiveness one cis-element (ABRE) that present in 88.31% of GGCTs, for auxin responsiveness two cis-elements (AuxRR-core, TGA-element) that present in 26.62%, and 48.70% of GGCTs, respectively, for methyl jasmonate responsiveness also two cis-elements (CGTCA-motif, TGACG-motif) that present in 73.38% and 51.95% of GGCTs, respectively, for ethylene responsiveness one cis-element (ERE) that present in 57.14% of GGCTs, for gibberellin responsiveness three cis-elements (GARE-motif, P-box, TATC-box) that present in 35.06%, 27.92%, and 22.08% of GGCTs, respectively, and for salicylic acid responsiveness one cis-element (TCA-element) that present in 35.06% of GGCTs (Table S7).

Cis-regulatory elements in the promoter region of GGCTs in U’s triangle Brassica species and primarily categorized into hormone responsive, light responsive, plant growth and development, and stress-responsive elements. The cis-elements in promoter regions were predicted using PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) and drawn by TBtools (a) Cis-regulatory elements in Bni, Bol, and Bra (b) Cis-regulatory elements in Bju, Bca, and Bna

Furthermore, we identified 22 light-responsive cis-regulatory elements in the promoter region of all observed GGCT genes. The most commonly detected cis-elements were G-box (90.26%), Box 4 (76.62%), GT1-motif (75.97%), TCT-motif (64.28%), and AE-box (52.60%). Additionally, we identified 10 cis-regulatory elements related to plant growth and development. The most abundant element was related to anaerobic induction with one cis-element (ARE) that present in 90.91% of GGCTs, for root-specific expression one cis-element (as-1) that present in 73.38% of GGCTs, for secondary xylem development one cis-element (AAGAA-motif) that present in 65.58% of GGCTs), for endosperm expression one cis-element (GCN4_motif) that present in 30.52% GGCTs, for meristem expression one cis-element (CAT-box) that present in 27.92% of GGCTs, and for zein metabolism regulation one cis-element (O2-site) that present in 23.38% of observed GGCTs. The stress-responsive cis-elements contain both biotic and abiotic stress-related elements. The biotic stress-responsive elements included the W box, WRE3, and WUN-motif present in 37.01%, 46.10%, and 32.47% of GGCTs, respectively. The cis-regulatory elements related to abiotic stress responsiveness included the DRE core, LTR, MBS, MYB, MYC, STRE, and TC-rich repeats, which were present in 25.32%, 46.75%, 36.36%, 98.70%, 95.45%, 70.78%, and 51.30% of GGCTs, respectively (Table S7).

Expression analysis of the GGCT gene family members in different tissues of B. napus

To indicate the potential role of BnaGGCTs in B. napus, an analysis of their tissue-specific expression patterns was performed (Fig. 6a, Table S8). Some genes showed expression in almost all the tissues in all three groups, such as BnaGGCT6, BnaGGCT21, BnaGGCT5, BnaGGCT8, and BnaGGCT23 from the group GGCT-PS, BnaGGCT2 and BnaGGCT17 from the group GGGACT, while BnaGGCT25 and BnaGGCT15 from the group ChaC. Other genes showed a tissue-specific expression pattern, such as BnaGGCT30 and BnaGGCT13, expressed in flowering parts, especially in the buds. Similarly, BnaGGCT19 and BnaGGCT4 showed higher expression in the later stages of siliques development. BnaGGCT10 is highly expressed in the root and stem. Interestingly, here we found the two GGCT genes (BnaGGCT26, BnaGGCT11) from group GGCT-PS, which showed seed-specific expression, and they only expressed in the later phases of seed development. To further explore the expression of these two genes in seeds, we obtained the full expression profiles of these genes in all stages of seed development (Fig. 6b, Table S9), and we found that their expression was gradually increased in the later phases of seed development.

Expression analysis of the BnaGGCT genes under various hormonal treatments and abiotic stresses

Analysis of GGCT gene expression in roots and leaves revealed significant hormone-responsive patterns (Fig. 7, Table S10). While overall expression levels were generally low, specific genes exhibited distinct expression profiles. For example, BnaGGCT19, BnaGGCT18, and BnaGGCT3 demonstrated higher expression in root and leaf tissues upon ABA treatment. While BnaGGCT7, BnaGGCT29, BnaGGCT9, and BnaGGCT24 showed higher expression only in the leaves under ABA treatment. BnaGGCT18, BnaGGCT3, and BnaGGCT7 were also upregulated under the IAA, TZ, and BL treatments. BnaGGCT5 showed higher expression in both root and leaf under the JA treatment, while BnaGGCT7 was upregulated by JA treatment only in leaves. These findings suggest complex regulation of the GGCTgene expression by both tissue and hormone factors.

Expression validation of selected BnaGGCTs under drought and salinity stresses

Further to validate the expression pattern of BnaGGCTs under drought and salt stresses, we selected the eight BnaGGCT genes and performed qRT-PCR to assess their expression pattern under stresses. We assessed the expression level at four-time intervals (0, 3, 6, and 12 h) and observed significant variation (Fig. 9). We found that 7 genes (BnaGGCT3, BnaGGCT6, BnaGGCT7, BnaGGCT9, BnaGGCT18, BnaGGCT19, BnaGGCT24) were significantly upregulated under both stresses, while one gene (BnaGGCT22) showed significant downregulation under both stresses (Fig. 9a.b). However, the variations in these genes expression significantly changes over time.

Identification of BnaGGCT11 SNVs associated with seed weight and yield

It was observed that BnaGGCT11 and BnaGGCT26 are specifically expressed in seeds in the latter stages of development. We hypothesized a potential association with seed weight. To further investigate, we examined these genes for SNPs using the BnIR website (https://yanglab.hzau.edu.cn/). SNP analysis revealed that BnaGGCT26 exhibited minimal genetic variation, while BnaGGCT11 contained nine SNPs. Seven SNPs were located in introns, and two were found within exons. One exonic SNP (19752728) resulted in a missense variation, while the other caused a frameshift mutation, but it was observed in only a few genomes (Fig. 10a, Table S12). This SNP change from C to T, and this change is distributed in 1500 and 602 B. napus lines, respectively (Fig. 10b). Further analysis of SNP 19,752,728 revealed a significant association with increased seed weight and overall yield in lines carrying the C_ref allele compared to those with the T_alt allele (Fig. 10c, d). Furthermore, gene expression analysis at 40 DAP indicated higher expression levels in lines possessing C_ref variants compared to those with the T_alt variants, suggesting a potential link between this SNP and expression regulations (Fig. 10e). To further assess the functional relevance of the missense SNP (A07_19752728) in BnaGGCT11, we performed an in silico protein stability analysis using the mCSM tool. The substitution was predicted to have a destabilizing effect on the protein (ΔΔG = − 0.605 kcal/mol), suggesting a potential reduction in structural stability. Interestingly, the reference allele, which confers higher stability, was associated with increased thousand-seed weight and yield, as well as higher expression at 40 DAP seed development, compared to the alternate allele. Although the promoter region is usually linked with the expression regulation, this SNP change might affect the overall expression stability, which functions in a feedback loop mechanism. Collectively, these analyses suggest a potential role for BnaGGCT11 in regulating seed weight and subsequently contributing to overall yield. However, further functional characterization is required to clarify the molecular processes behind the activity of this gene.

Transcriptional regulation and Co-expression profile of BnaGGCT11

To access further details related to the BnaGGCT11 gene regulation, we screened out the TFs with the possibility of regulating the expression of this gene. For this purpose, we used the Plant Transcription Factor Database (PlantTFDB) and obtained the TFs that showed interaction with the promoter region of this gene (Fig. 11a, Table S13). In these TFs, 12 members of the DOF gene family, 3 members of the ARF gene family, and 2 members of the SHN gene family target the BnaGGCT11 gene. While one member of IDD, SOC, ATC3H, bHLH, AP2/B3-L, TFIII, PIF, REF, BBM, RGA, and ERF gene families target the BnaGGCT11 promoter region. Furthermore, co-expression analysis was conducted to clarify the network of interactions of genes with the BnaGGCT11 gene, and these genes demonstrated co-expression with 28 transcripts (Fig. 11a, Table S14). Functional annotation of these co-expressed transcripts revealed an association with proteins involved in key seed developmental processes and signaling pathways. The majority of gene expression was observed in the latter phases of seed development, starting approximately 40 days after fertilization (Fig. 11b, Table S15).

Discussion

Plant growth and development depend on glutathione (GSH), which also play a key role in mediating reactions to various abiotic stressors [48]. GSH regulates cellular processes by maintaining cellular redox homeostasis and acting as a primary antioxidant [49]. GGCT is an important metabolizing enzyme of GSH homeostasis in plants [15]. Glutathione degradation-related GGCT proteins have been identified in model plant species, such as A. thaliana and rice, and also in some other species [15, 50]. In a recent study, the GGCT gene family was characterized in wheat, where one family member was suggested to play a potential role in seed development and seed weight [17]. However, it has not yet been studied in the U’s triangle Brassica species. The Brassicaceae family serves as an excellent model for studying polyploidy and eukaryotic evolution due of its complex genome duplication history [25].

In the current study, 154 GGCTs were identified across the six species of Brassica and analyzed for their potential role in plant growth and stress responses. Genomic structure and its implications for biological function can be quickly understood through the comparative analysis of genomes from different species [51]. On the basis of phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 1), GGCTs were classified into three distinct subgroups, as previously reported in Arabidopsis, rice, and wheat [9, 17, 50]. Gene amplification through tandem duplication has frequently occurred during the evolutionary history of gene families [52], and such duplications can facilitate the emergence of novel genes and functional divergence [53–55]. In this study, Ka/Ks ratios of GGCT gene pairs continuously remained less than 1, indicating abundant gene duplication within the GGCT family in Brassica species (Table S4). These findings suggest that these genes have been influenced by purifying selection, which may be the cause of the expansion of GGCT genes in U’s triangle Brassica species. Nevertheless, it is important to note that Ka/Ks values are an average measure and may not capture recent or lineage-specific neofunctionalization events. Polyploid species, such as B. napus, often undergo subfunctionalization or acquire new functions in duplicated gene copies, which could lead to deviations from strict purifying selection in certain genes. The differentiation and evolutionary history of these genes were studied by comparing their gene structures and motif compositions. The gene structure and the motif analysis revealed the subgroup-specific occurrence. Despite a few outliers, the observed conservation of exon-intron numbers and motif combinations within recognized groups indicates functional restrictions on these structural components (Fig. 4).

The Brassica genus comprises major commercial crops worldwide, exhibiting susceptibility to various environmental stresses, which consequently diminishes productivity [56–59]. Previous studies have linked GGCT genes to plant development and stress responses in various plant species [7, 11, 16–18, 54], which suggests their functional importance in stress resilience. In the current study, the promoter analysis of the GGCT gene promoter region identified the cis-regulatory elements related to hormonal, light, growth, and stress responsiveness, which further supports the impression of their potential roles in hormone signaling, growth and development, and stress adaptation (Fig. 5, Table S6,S7). Furthermore, the expression profiling of BnaGGCT genes at different developmental stages in different tissues revealed that some genes have tissue-specific expression, such as BnaGGCT11 and BnaGGCT26. These two BnaGGCTs are highly expressed in the later phases of seed development, indicating their possible role in later seed-related traits (Fig. 6). Additionally, we analyzed BnaGGCTs expression under different hormonal and abiotic stresses using the RNA-seq database and validated the results for the 8 selected genes under salt and drought stresses through qRT-PCR and found that BnaGGCT genes were differentially expressed under both stress treatments (Figs. 7, 8 and 9). Overall, these results indicated the involvement of BnaGGCTs in the stress responses, especially salt and drought stresses.

Seed weight is the main component that influences the overall seed yield [60–63], and as we found the involvement of BnaGGCT11 and BnaGGCT26 in seed-specific expression pattern, we further analyze their SNP variations and their involvement in seed weight and yield by using the publicly available BnIR database (https://yanglab.hzau.edu.cn/BnIR) [40] which helps to identify the variations in the genome for various traits. The SNP analysis revealed that BnaGGCT11 possessed nine SNPs, of which one exonic SNPs (19752728) showed a statistically significant association with seed weight and overall yield, with lines carrying the C_ref allele compared to those lines that carried the T_alt allele (Fig. 10). Subsequent gene expression profiling at 40 DAP indicated higher expression levels in lines harboring the C_ref allele relative to the T_alt allele. This pattern suggests a correlation between allelic variation and transcript abundance; however the SNP resides within the coding region and is more likely to affect the protein stability which further predicted by the mCSM tool. Since gene regulation involves a range of transcription factors and co-expressed gene networks, numerous plant transcription factors have been implicated in the control of seed growth and development [64–67]. The seed-specific expression of BnaGGCT11/26 during late developmental phases suggests a potential role in redox regulation during seed filling. Previous studies in Arabidopsis have shown that glutathione metabolism is crucial for protecting developing seeds against oxidative stress and ensuring proper maturation [68]. Since GGCTs mediate glutathione turnover, their expression in Brassica seeds may similarly help maintain redox balance during storage reserve accumulation, thereby influencing seed weight. Supporting this hypothesis, the SNP identified in BnaGGCT11 was associated with reduced seed weight and yield in lines carrying the destabilizing allele, consistent with altered protein stability and potentially impaired glutathione recycling. We further analyzed the transcription factors that target the BnaGGCT11 promoter region and the co-expressed genes (Fig. 11). Twelve DOF, three ARF, and two SHN transcription factors were found to target the BnaGGCT11 gene, while one member each from the IDD, SOC, ATC3H, bHLH, AP2/B3-L, TFIII, PIF, REF, BBM, RGA, and ERF families targeted its promoter. Co-expression analysis identified 28 transcripts associated with BnaGGCT11, which are functionally linked to key seed developmental processes and signaling pathways. A majority of these genes showed high expression levels during the later phases of seed development, around 40 days after fertilization (Fig. 11b, Table S15). Within our TF network, families such as DOF and ARF are particularly noteworthy given their established functions in seed biology. DOF proteins regulate storage reserve accumulation, including prolamins, starch, and lipids, and also interact with DELLA proteins to modulate dormancy and germination via ABA/GA balance [66]. ARFs, as key mediators of auxin signaling, control embryo patterning, seed coat formation, and nutrient allocation [69]. The enrichment of these TFs suggests that BnaGGCT11/26 may act within transcriptional circuits that couple redox regulation with seed filling and maturation, Overall, these findings highlight a correlative association between BnaGGCT11 and seed weight and, consequently, influencing overall yield. While these findings provide a valuable foundation, further functional studies (overexpression, CRISPR knockout) will be essential to establish causality and elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Conclusion

In this study, 154 potential members of the GGCT gene family were identified across the U’s triangle Brassica species and classified into three distinct groups, consistent with previously established subgroups in A. thaliana, rice, and wheat. Further analysis highlighted substantial variation in their protein lengths, molecular weights, and isoelectric points. Cis-acting regulatory element analysis revealed hormone-responsive, stress-responsive elements, indicating a role in hormonal regulation and stress responses. Expression profiling of BnaGGCT genes indicates their involvement in different developmental processes and stress adaptive responses, and the qRT-PCR expression validation of selected genes under salt and drought stress supports these findings. Notably, BnaGGCT11 exhibited seed-specific expression during the later developmental phases, and a SNP within the exon of this gene showed a correlative associated with variations in seed weight and yield. Additionally, through the bioinformatics tools, the potential regulatory network involved in BnaGGCT11 functionality was predicted. Overall, these findings offer valuable candidates for future functional validation and lay the groundwork for future efforts in Brassica crop improvement.
