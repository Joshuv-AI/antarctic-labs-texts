Fabrication of Flexible 3D Electrospun Poly(trimethylene carbonate) Scaffolds with Superior Cellular Infiltration for Tissue Engineering
Authors: Minmin Zhang, Jiahui Jiang, Zhengchao Guo, Dirk W. Grijpma, Honglin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Although electrospinning has garnered significant attention, most studies focus on thermoplastic materials. The adaptation of elastomeric materials for electrospinning presents challenges, particularly due to their viscous behavior at room temperature (RT). In the present study, we introduce a versatile method for electrospinning rubber-like polymer poly­(trimethylene carbonate) (PTMC) utilizing an ethanol collector bath. Traditional electrospinning primarily yields sheet-like fibrous meshes, failing to create a three-dimensional scaffold with structural integrity. Here, we present an innovative approach to transform 2D fibrous meshes into 3D fibrous scaffolds. Both scanning electron microscopy and X-ray analysis confirmed that the resulting 3D scaffolds possess a fibrous structure with a significant pore size. When human mesenchymal stem cells were seeded onto these 3D fibrous scaffolds, they demonstrated superior cellular infiltration after 5 days of culture, in contrast to the superficial growth typically observed on conventional 2D fibrous scaffolds. Our approach presents a new avenue for developing 3D fibrous scaffolds with superior cellular infiltration by electrospinning elastomers, thereby expanding the repertoire of processable materials and techniques for scaffold fabrication and advancing the development of novel biomaterials in tissue engineering.

Introduction

Scaffold-based approaches utilizing biomaterials to create temporary structures that support cellular growth and infiltration have been extensively explored in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine. A variety of scaffold fabrication techniques, including 3D printing, self-assembly, melt molding, phase separation, freeze-drying, and electrospinning, have been employed to generate 3D porous structures. , Among these techniques, electrospinning has garnered significant interest due to its ability to fabricate fibrous structures that closely mimic the extracellular matrix (ECM). This technique is not only straightforward and cost-effective but also capable of processing a diverse array of materials, including natural and synthetic polymers as well as composites. However, conventional electrospinning typically yields a 2D fibrous membrane, which falls short compared to 3D scaffolds that better replicate the ECM environment of native biological tissues. Considerable efforts have been invested in the development of 3D electrospun scaffolds through strategies such as using 3D or liquid collectors, multilayer electrospinning, self-assembly, and postprocessing of spun fibers. , Despite their ability to construct 3D electrospun scaffolds to varying degrees, these strategies tend to be relatively complex and often necessitate specialized instrumentation. Recently, direct writing electrospinning for fabricating 3D electrospun fibers is an innovative technique that allows for the precise deposition of fibers in three-dimensional structures. , However, this technique has difficulty in producing 3D electrospun scaffolds with large dimensions, such as over 0.5 cm in height, due to charge accumulation and repulsion.

In addition to difficulty in producing a 3D structure, another notable limitation of conventional electrospun scaffolds is their small pore sizes, owing to the dense arrangement of fiber layers, which restricts cell penetration and migration into the scaffold. Consequently, this leads to the formation of cell layers only on the scaffold’s surface. Therefore, various attempts have been made to fabricate 3D electrospun scaffolds with larger pores to enhance cell infiltration, including salt leaching, the use of sacrificial fibers, and liquid collectors.

To date, most of the applied materials in electrospinning have been thermoplastic polymers; however, electrospinning elastomeric polymers, such as poly­(trimethylene carbonate) (PTMC), presents challenges due to their viscous behavior at room temperature. PTMC, a flexible and amorphous polymer, has attracted significant attention in regenerative medicine owing to its favorable properties. Unlike biodegradable thermoplastic polyesters such as polylactic acid (PLA) or polycaprolactone (PCL), which undergo bulk erosion, PTMC degrades enzymatically via surface erosion, thereby preserving its mechanical integrity throughout the degradation process. Additionally, PCL and PLA, particularly PLA, degrade into acidic byproducts (lactic acid for PLA, 6-hydroxycaproic acid for PCL). These acids accumulate locally, lowering the surrounding pH and triggering chronic inflammation or foreign body responsesdetrimental to tissue regeneration. , In contrast, PTMC degrades into neutral, nontoxic byproducts (trimethylene glycol and carbon dioxide), which are easily metabolized and eliminated. , Its surface erosion releases these byproducts gradually, preventing local accumulation and minimizing inflammatory reactions. A key limitation in utilizing PTMC for electrospinning is its viscous state at room temperature, which is due to its low glass transition temperature of approximately −20 °C. This often leads to irreversible deformation and even loss of fiber morphology. Thus, the core challenge in electrospinning PTMC is not the inability to form fibers but rather the difficulty in preserving the fibrous morphology after formation. To address this issue, a photo-cross-linkable polymer can be employed. This approach has been widely adopted to produce electrospun fibers with superior properties, including enhanced mechanical strength, improved thermal and solvent resistance, and controlled release profiles. − To date, electrospun PTMC fibers have only been obtained by electrospinning blends of PTMC with other polymers such as PCL, gelatin, and PLA. To the best of our knowledge, the direct electrospinning of photo-cross-linked PTMC has not yet been reported.

In the present study, we aimed to develop an approach to produce PTMC fibers with a stable fibrous structure via electrospinning (Figure ). To overcome the limitations associated with PTMC electrospinning, we used photo-cross-linked PTMC for electrospinning, together with an ethanol bath for fiber collection as an alternative to the traditional aluminum foil method. Furthermore, we presented a novel approach for transforming 2D PTMC mats into 3D PTMC fibrous scaffolds. The fiber morphology and 3D structure of the 3D PTMC scaffolds were examined by scanning electron microscopy and X-ray computed tomography, respectively. Human mesenchymal stem cells were seeded on the 3D PTMC fibrous scaffolds, and their cell viability and cellular infiltration were investigated. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first reported instance of the fabrication of 3D PTMC fibrous scaffolds.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Trimethylene carbonate (TMC) was purchased from Huizhou Foryou Medical Devices, China. Tin­(II)-2-ethyl-hexanoate (Sn­(Oct)2), methacrylic anhydride, hydroxy-2-methyl-1-propane-1-one (Irgacure2959), triethylamine, hydroquinone, 1,1,1-trimethylolpropane, and 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoro-2-propanol (HFIP) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Shanghai, China. Ethanol and dichloromethane (DCM) were obtained from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (China). All chemicals and agents were used as provided without further purification.

Polymer Synthesis

A photo-cross-linked three-armed PTMC macromer was synthesized as previously described. Briefly, under a dry argon atmosphere, TMC (51 g, 0.5 mol) was heated to 80 °C in a three-neck round-bottom flask. Once the TMC monomer melted, 1,1,1-trimethylolpropane (0.34 g, 2.55 mmol) and 5 drops of Sn­(Oct)2 were added as the initiator and catalyst, respectively. Next, the temperature was increased to 130 °C, and the reaction was proceeded for 48 h before being stopped by cooling to RT. After cooling, the PTMC oligomer was dissolved in dried dichloromethane, and triethylamine and hydroquinone were then added, followed by the dropwise addition of methacrylic anhydride under stirring. The reaction solution was stirred at room temperature for 120 h under a dry argon atmosphere. Subsequently, the resultant solution was extracted with DI water three times, and the organic phase was precipitated in cold methanol. The precipitate was dried overnight at room temperature, protected from light, and further dried under a vacuum at room temperature for 5 days. 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectra of PTMC and PTMC-MA were recorded with a Varian MercuryPlus 300 MHz spectrometer using CDCl3 as a solvent. The number-average molecular weight (M n) and degree of functionalization of the PTMC were calculated from the 1H NMR spectra.

Fabrication of Electrospun PTMC-MA Fibers and Their Assembly into 3D Scaffolds

A PTMC-MA solution was prepared by dissolving the polymer in a mixture solvent of CHCl3/DMF (v/v = 4:1) to achieve a final concentration of 20% (w/v). The solution was stirred overnight at a speed of 300 rpm at room temperature. The next day, 1 wt % photoinitiator Irgacure 2959 (relative to macromer) was added, and the solution was stirred for an additional hour prior to electrospinning. For electrospinning, the solution was loaded into a 5 mL syringe equipped with a blunted 27G stainless-steel needle, and an ethanol bath was used as the collector. Using a syringe pump (Multi-Phaser, Model), the electrospinning solution was fed at a rate of 1 mL/h. The applied voltage and tip-to-collector distance were set at 10–15 kV and 6–15 cm, respectively. Fibers were collected in an ethanol bath containing 0.12% (w/v) Irgacure 2959. During the electrospinning, fibers were irradiated with two UV light bulbs (365 nm, 0.32 mW/cm2 for each bulb), positioned at a distance of 10 cm from the ethanol bath. After electrospinning, fibers in the ethanol bath were postcured in a UV chamber (365 nm, 7.0 mW/cm2, Ultra Lum, San Diego, USA) for 40 min under a nitrogen atmosphere, with a UV lamp distance of 5 cm from the bath. For the fabrication of 3D PTMC scaffolds, the PTMC fibers in the ethanol bath were manually rolled into a 3D structure, which was then cured in the UV chamber to form a stable construct. The parameters for UV curing were the same as those mentioned above for the 2D electrospun membranes.

Scanning Electron Microscopy and Fiber Dimensions

The morphology of the electrospun fibers was examined using an SEM (Philips XL30 ESEM-FEG) operated at an applied voltage of 5 kV. Samples were washed with distilled water and lyophilized. Prior to SEM imaging, samples were gold-sputtered using a Cressington Sputter Coater 108 Auto, set at 30 mA for 40 s. Fiber diameter and distribution, together with porosity, were determined using ImageJ software by analyzing at least 30 fibers from one SEM image, with a total of 5 images per sample.

X-ray Computed Tomography

The pore structure of the prepared scaffolds was visualized by high-resolution microcomputed tomography (Micro-CT) using an eXplore Locus micro-CT scanner (GE Health Care) operated at 90 kV and 80 μA. A spatial resolution of 14 μm was employed for the 3D image reconstruction.

Mechanical Properties

The compressive mechanical properties of the 3D electrospun PTMC scaffolds were evaluated using a universal testing machine (CMT-6104, China). For static testing, scaffolds underwent unconfined uniaxial compression at a constant cross-head speed of 20 mm/min until failure. The compressive strength, breaking strain, and compressive modulus were determined from the resulting stress–strain curves. Three replicates were tested for each scaffold type. For dynamic compression testing, scaffolds were subjected to 30 compression cycles at a speed of 20 mm/min and a maximum strain of 40% to assess their recovery properties. Compressive strength was measured to quantify mechanical degradation during the cyclic loading.

Cell Culture and Seeding

Sprague–Dawley rat bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) transfected with red fluorescence protein were provided by Cyagen Biosciences (Guangzhou), China. MSCs were cultured in alfa-MEM medium (Gibco), composed of 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cyagen), 2 mM l-glutamine (Gibco), 0.2 mM ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 mg/mL streptomycin (Gibco), and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The culture medium was refreshed every 3 days. At approximately 85% confluence, the cells were harvested for scaffold seeding. Scaffolds were sterilized with 70% (v/v) ethanol for 15 min three times, rinsed with PBS three times, and immersed in cell culture medium overnight before cell seeding. The next day, the cell culture medium was removed from scaffolds, and MSCs were seeded onto each scaffold at a density of 2 × 104 cells/cm2. Cell distribution and their migration behavior into the scaffold were imaged using a Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (Leica, TCS SP8, Germany).

Cell Viability

Cell viability within fibrous scaffolds was determined by using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, at specified time points, the cell culture medium in sample wells was replaced with fresh medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Biotek-800TS).

Statistical Analysis

The quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation, with an N of at least three per group. Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 5.01 (GraphPad software, San Diego, U.S.A.). Statistical differences were determined, and A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of PTMC-MA

PTMC is a rubbery polymer characterized by poor dimensional stability, tackiness, and insufficient mechanical properties. Photo-cross-linking offers a relatively simple and efficient method to modify PTMC, enabling the fabrication of complex structures while enhancing its mechanical properties, stability, and durability. , Thus, in the present study, we utilized methacrylate-modified PTMC for electrospinning. The synthesis of photo-cross-linkable PTMC-MA involves a two-step process, as shown in Figure A. First, trimethylene carbonate monomer (TMC) undergoes ring-opening polymerization to produce polytrimethylene carbonate (PTMC). Subsequently, PTMC is reacted with methacrylic anhydride to yield PTMC-MA. The conversion of TMC monomer was determined using 1H NMR spectroscopy by comparing the proton peak area at 4.46 ppm for TMC with the proton peak area at 4.24 ppm for PTMC, indicating a conversion rate of approximately 96%. The obtained PTMC had a number-average molecular weight (M n) of approximately 20,000 g/mol. The presence of the double bond proton peaks of the methacrylate group at 5.57 and 6.11 ppm confirmed the successful reaction of the PTMC macromer with methacrylic anhydride. The degree of functionalization was calculated according to the method previously reported by Geven et al. The average degree of functionalization was around 78%.

(A) Synthesis scheme for PTMC and its subsequent functionalization with methacrylic anhydride to produce PTMC-MA. (B) Representative 1H NMR spectra for three-armed PTMC and PTMC-MA.

Electrospinning of PTMC-MA Fibers

For PTMC-MA electrospinning, we implemented two UV curing steps (during and after electrospinning). Initially, we did not incorporate UV curing during electrospinning. However, it turned out to be impossible to obtain a fibrous structure. Thus, the primary purpose of the first UV curing step during electrospinning is to enable the timely solidification of the fibers as they are formed. This real-time curing helps fix the fiber morphology immediately, preventing adhesion or structural distortion and ensuring the integrity of the individual fibers. After the 2D membrane was further transformed into a 3D fibrous structure, the spatial arrangement of fibers changed significantly. In this context, implementing a second UV curing step is necessary to enhance cross-linking at newly formed interfaces and keep the structural integrity of the final 3D construct.

Electrospinning parameters such as the tip-to-collector distance, applied voltage, and the type of collector significantly influence the morphology of the produced fibers. To optimize the conditions for electrospinning PTMC-MA fibers, various collectors were investigated (Figure A). PTMC-MA fibers were first electrospun onto aluminum foil as a static collector, which resulted in rapid fusion of fibers into a thin film within a few seconds, thereby losing their fibrous morphology. This phenomenon is attributed to the rubber-like property of PTMC, which exhibits a viscous state at room temperature. We then utilized a 1:1 ethanol/water (v/v = 1:1) bath collector. SEM imaging showed that PTMC-MA fibers fused significantly and lost the interfiber space. When an ethanol bath was employed as the collector, it yielded homogeneous and ultrafine fibers. Thus, we selected the ethanol bath for subsequent PTMC fiber collection.

Effects of electrospinning parameters on PTMC fiber morphology. (A) Influence of applied collectors on fiber morphology. (B) Influence of working distance (WD) on fiber morphology. (C) Influence of applied voltage on fiber morphology.

The needle-to-collector distance, also referred to as the working distance, has a notable influence on the characteristics of the resultant fibers. In general, larger working distances leads to increased flight time, improving the duration for solvent evaporation. However, an extended working distance decreases the electrical field strength, leading to instability of the charged jet. To investigate the effect of working distance on fiber morphology, we tested the working distances ranging from 6 to 15 cm while keeping other parameters constant. As can be seen in Figure B, the working distance had a strong influence on PTMC fiber morphology. At 6 cm, uniform fibers with nanoporosity on the surface were observed. Increasing the working distance to 10 cm, the fibers presented a molten morphology and almost lost their fibrous structure. At 15 cm, the fibers exhibited a fine and uniform morphology.

We also explored the impact of applied voltage on fiber morphology, as the applied voltage is the primary driving force behind the generation of electrospun fiber. As shown in Figure C, at 10 kV, the fibers displayed a poor morphology, whereas at 15 kV, they became uniform and ultrafine. Based on the above investigation, the optimal conditions for electrospinning PTMC were determined to be an applied voltage of 15 kV and a working distance of 15 cm.

Transition from a 2D Fiber Mat to 3D Scaffold

A 3D scaffold offers many advantages over 2D structures, such as more in vivo-like cell interactions, enhanced cell migration, and cell morphogenesis, which are critical for regulating cellular cycles and tissue functionality. We developed a feasible approach to convert 2D PTMC fibrous mats into 3D fibrous scaffolds. As shown schematically in Figure A, the 2D fibrous mat was rolled into a multilayered 3D structure by using a stainless-steel tube (1 mm diameter), followed by curing in a UV cross-linker. Upon removal of the tube, a freestanding multilayered 3D structure was obtained. Figure B shows a digital photograph of the 2D nonwoven mat, while Figure C displays the resulting tube-like 3D structure formed after transformation from this 2D nonwoven mat. SEM imaging revealed that the 2D nonwoven mats exhibited a fibrous architecture (Figure D), with an average fiber diameter of ∼1.7 μm (Figure E) and a porosity of approximately 89.4%. Micro-CT was used to visualize the structure of the 3D PTMC scaffold. Figure F shows that the pores were homogeneously distributed throughout the 3D scaffolds.

(C) Digital photos of 3D PTMC fibrous scaffolds. (D) SEM image of the wall structure of the 3D PTMC fibrous scaffold. (E) Analysis of fiber diameter distribution in panel D. The resulting 3D PTMC scaffolds demonstrated sponge-like characteristics and maintained integrity under physical manipulation, such as bending and squeezing (Figure A,B). Scaffolds with such elastic properties are particularly attractive because they can fully conform to irregularly shaped defects, such as those found in bone and soft tissue repair. , To evaluate the mechanical properties of the 3D PTMC scaffolds, we conducted static and dynamic mechanical compression tests. As shown in Figure C, the stress–strain curve indicates that 3D PTMC scaffolds are very soft and elastic, and they do not collapse within an 80% strain range. To assess the recoverable properties of 3D PTMC scaffolds, 30 cycles of compression tests were performed up to 40% strain (Figure D,E). The results demonstrated that 3D PTMC scaffolds exhibit good recovery performance without structural collapse, which is essential for the effective application of implantable scaffolds.

Cell Viability

Cell viability of MSCs on PTMC scaffolds was assessed by using a CCK-8 assay (Figure ). Cell viability steadily increased on all substrates over the culture time, indicating the proliferation of MSCs. No significant differences in cell viability were observed between the 2D PTMC mat and the control (tissue culture plate), demonstrating the biocompatibility of PTMC electrospun scaffolds and their capacity to support MSC proliferation. Notably, the 3D PTMC scaffolds had significantly higher cell viability than both the 2D and control groups, which may be attributed to their 3D architecture providing enhanced space for cell growth and proliferation.

Tissue culture plates were used as control. The statistical analysis was performed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. *p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01.

Cell Infiltration into Scaffolds

An ideal tissue-engineered scaffold should foster both cellular attachment and infiltration, and the balanced combination of both is demanded to eventually promote whole tissue formation. To evaluate the cellular responses on both 2D PTMC mats and 3D PTMC scaffolds, we seeded MSCs and examined their infiltration and growth. As illustrated in Figure , MSCs on 2D PTMC mats did not infiltrate below and most proliferated on their superficial layers; i.e., they largely remained on the surface after 5 days. In contrast, cells on 3D PTMC scaffolds infiltrated deep into the scaffold. The quantification data demonstrated that the average cell penetration depth in the 3D PTMC scaffolds is significantly greater than that in the 2D PTMC mats (Figure S1). This phenomenon was attributed to the larger pores of the 3D PTMC fibrous scaffolds that ease infiltration.

Conclusions

Electrospinning elastomeric materials such as PTMC at room temperature present challenges. In this study, for the first time, photo-cross-linked PTMC elastomer fibers were successfully fabricated via electrospinning using an ethanol bath as a collector. Conventional electrospinning typically results in sheet-like 2D fibrous meshes, failing to create 3D scaffolds. To address this issue, we developed an approach to convert these 2D electrospun fibers into cohesive 3D fibrous scaffolds with great structural integrity. SEM and X-ray computed tomography analyses revealed that the obtained 3D PTMC scaffolds possessed a porous structure and larger pores. In vitro cell culture studies demonstrated enhanced cell penetration and proliferation in 3D PTMC fibrous scaffolds compared with 2D PTMC mats. A limitation of this study is the limited reproducibility of the 3D PTMC scaffold architecture, which arises from the manual rolling of the 2D mats. Despite this drawback, the straightforward fabrication route yields 3D scaffolds that support markedly enhanced cellular infiltration, underscoring their potential application for tissue engineering and regenerative medicine.
