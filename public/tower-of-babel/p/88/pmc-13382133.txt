Multiple genetic engineering of porcine red blood cells improves compatibility with human blood
Authors: Pan Lu, Yong Wang, Jiaxiang Du, Jie Yan, Shaoping Deng, Dongsheng He, Zhipeng Ren, Ziqiang Dai, Dianyuan Li, Dengke Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTo address critical challenges in clinical blood shortages and limited preservation periods, particularly affecting emergency transfusions, transgenic pig red blood cells (RBCs) emerge as a promising solution. We performed multi-gene editing on pig RBCs by knocking out three Xeno antigen genes (GGTA1, B4GALNT2, CMAH) and introducing high expression of human CD55 and CD47, generating 5-gene edited (5GE) RBCs. After co-incubating with human blood, we assessed their survival rate, along with physicochemical indicators related to hemolytic reactions and inflammatory responses. We further compared the physicochemical properties between transgenic pig blood and human blood. 5GE RBCs showed no significant agglutination, only mild hemolysis, and markedly reduced cytotoxicity. Binding by human antibodies and complement was significantly diminished. This study demonstrates the strong potential of genetically engineered porcine RBCs for emergency human transfusion, offering a viable alternative to alleviate blood shortages, especially when human RBCs are unavailable.Keywords: genetical modification, Xenotransfusion, hemolytic reaction, immune rejection

Introduction

Blood transfusion serves as a critical life-saving intervention, particularly in cases of acute massive hemorrhage, severe anemia, and trauma surgery, where patients often require substantial volumes of fresh blood to sustain vital functions.1 In recent years, however, the supply of human blood has faced severe shortages, leading to insufficient availability of related blood products. Many hospital hematology departments are experiencing emergency-level deficits in blood reserves.

To address this issue, studies have explored the in vitro generation of red blood cells (RBCs) from hematopoietic stem and/or progenitor cells (HSCs/HSPCs) derived from adult donors or umbilical cord blood. Nevertheless, these sources remain limited and unsustainable.2 Xeno transfusion—the transfusion of blood from non-human animals to humans—has emerged as a promising alternative. Porcine RBCs (pRBCs) have historically been considered a promising candidate for Xeno transfusion due to their functional similarities to human RBCs (hRBCs), including comparable oxygen affinity as well as advantages in donor availability. However, despite these similarities, significant interspecies differences persist in key physicochemical properties and, more critically, in the expression of surface antigens, such as the immunogenic α-Gal epitope and other glycans that can trigger rapid immune rejection upon transfusion into humans. These immunological and biophysical disparities underscore the necessity for both a comprehensive characterization of pRBC properties and the implementation of targeted genetic modifications to enhance their compatibility with the human recipient.3,4 Genetically modified pRBCs offer not only a readily available source but also the potential for large-scale production of blood products that could be stored directly in hospitals for emergency use.5

However, several challenges hinder the clinical application of Xeno transfusion, including blood type incompatibility, immune rejection, and the risk of transmission of zoonotic pathogens.6 The porcine AO blood group system resembles the human ABO system, and compatibility can be improved by selecting type O pigs.7,8 Upon transfusion into humans, pRBCs are susceptible to hemolysis and agglutination mediated by preformed natural antibodies in human plasma that bind to Xeno antigens on pRBCs. This triggers complement activation and membrane attack complex (MAC) formation, resulting in RBC lysis. Moreover, the inability of human signal regulatory protein-α (SIRPα) on macrophages to recognize the “self” marker CD47 on pRBCs leads to phagocytic clearance.

The CRISPR/Cas system has revolutionized genome editing due to its simplicity and cost-effectiveness.9 Transgenic pigs have been successfully generated and applied in various biomedical contexts, including recent advances in xenotransplantation of pig hearts and kidneys into human patients.10,11,12 For instance, knockout of the α1,3-galactosyltransferase gene (GTKO) in pRBCs significantly reduces agglutination compared to wild-type (WT) cells, yet non-Gal antibodies continue to trigger complement-mediated rejection.13,14 Further genetic modifications, such as double-knockout (GTKO/CMAHKO) and triple-knockout (TKO) pigs, have demonstrated reduced antibody binding and complement-mediated hemolysis, improving plasma compatibility significantly. Previous in vivo studies with TKO pRBCs in non-human primates showed extended survival compared to WT pRBCs but were still limited to days, indicating that residual immune challenges remain. Nonetheless, even TKO pRBCs are rejected within days in non-human primate models due to residual immune recognition, underscoring the need for additional genetic engineering.15,16,17

Co-expression of human immunoregulatory proteins on pRBCs represents a promising strategy to prolong survival. CD55 (decay-accelerating factor, DAF) inhibits complement cascade progression and prevents MAC formation.18 CD47 (integrin-associated protein, IAP), through engagement with SIRPα on macrophages, delivers a “don’t eat me” signal that mitigates phagocytosis. The interspecies incompatibility between human SIRPα and porcine CD47 contributes significantly to macrophage-mediated clearance of pRBCs.19,20

In previous work, transgenic TKO pigs expressing human CD47 and CD55 were generated. While these modifications reduced binding of human IgG/IgM and conferred some protection, transgene expression was low, resulting in limited functional benefit.21 Studies by Akihiro et al. suggest that even low-level expression of hCD55 can inhibit complement-mediated hemolysis, though phagocytosis may persist. Enhancing expression levels may further prolong RBC survival post-transfusion.22

To date, achieving high and stable expression of human transgenes such as hCD55 and hCD47 on the surface of pRBCs remains challenging. In our study, we developed efficient gene-editing strategies to generate genetically engineered pRBCs that express high levels of human CD55 and CD47. We demonstrate that introducing these immunoregulatory proteins into TKO pigs significantly reduces hemolysis and enhances compatibility with human plasma, supporting the clinical potential of engineered pRBCs for Xeno transfusion.

Results

Generation and identification of transgenic pRBCs

Here, we created a type of multi-gene-edited RBCs. First, we knocked out the three genes GGTA1, CMAH, and B4GALNT2 in WT pigs to obtain TKO pigs. On this basis, we introduced human CD55 to obtain 4GE(TKO/CD55) pigs. Then, we introduced human CD47 into the 4GE pigs to obtain 5-gene edited (5GE) (TKO/CD55/CD47) pigs (Figure 1A). The human CD55 gene was inserted at the Rosa26 locus, based on TKO pigs. Further building on the TKO/CD55 pigs, the CD47 gene was inserted at the H11 locus (Figures 1B and 1C). The knockout of the three xenogeneic antigen genes was confirmed by Sanger sequencing, with frameshift mutations occurring in each allele (Figure 1D). RBCs isolated from the piglets were analyzed using flow cytometry, which confirmed the removal of α-Gal, Sda, and Neu5Gc, as well as the expression of human CD55 and CD47. Both 4GE and 5GE pRBCs had three antigens removed and expressed high levels of human CD55. The 5GE pRBCs additionally expressed high levels of human CD47. The expression levels of human CD55 and CD47 on the surface of the transgenic pig RBCs were significantly increased by about 10 times compared to those of hRBCs (Figure 1E).

The morphological and physicochemical analysis of pig and human red blood cells

The size and shape of porcine and hRBCs were examined using electron microscopy. The analysis revealed that hRBCs exhibited a typical biconcave discoid morphology, whereas pRBCs appeared irregularly rounded with an uneven surface (Figure 2A). No notable morphological differences were observed among RBCs from different genotypes (Figure S1). Complete blood count analysis was performed on samples from pigs of various genotypes. However, pigs showed a significantly higher RBC count than the established human reference range (Figure 2B). The number of white blood cells (WBCs) was also significantly higher than the human range. Moreover, the WBCs of the TKO-edited pigs wassignificantly higher than that of the WT type; there was no significant difference in the number of WBCs between TKO and 4GE, 5GE pigs (Figure 2C). The results indicated that porcine hemoglobin (HGB) levels were comparable to those in humans (Figure 2D). Peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) measurements showed that all pig genotypes exhibited values within the normal human reference range (95%–100%), indicating that the genetic modifications did not compromise oxygen-carrying capacity or gas exchange efficiency (Figure 2E). The HCT, PLT, and PCT levels of pigs were all higher than the normal range for humans. There was no difference among different genotypes (Figures 2F–2H). The mean corpuscular volume (MCV) and mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH) of pigs were significantly lower than the normal range for humans. This is due to the different cell morphology between pigs and humans (Figures 2I and 2J). The MCHC and RDW-SD were comparable to human reference ranges (Figures 2K and 2L). RBC distribution width-coefficient of variation (RDW-CV) showed some variation but remained within a range consistent with previous reports (Figure 2M). Electrolyte profiling showed mildly elevated potassium levels in pigs compared to human standards (Figure 2N), while sodium, chloride, and calcium concentrations were largely comparable (Figures 2O–2Q). Overall, most hematological parameters in pigs closely resembled those of humans, supporting the potential utility of porcine blood products as a feasible alternative for transfusion purposes.

pRBCs were incubated with human plasma to detect the levels of naturally preformed anti-pig IgG and IgM antibodies in the human plasma. After incubation with human plasma, WT pRBCs showed high levels of antibody binding; RBCs of TKO pigs exhibited a significant reduction in both IgG and IgM antibody binding. However, compared to the ABO-C group, some plasma samples still showed positive binding (Figures 3C and 3D). Additionally, when comparing different human blood types (A, B, O, AB), no significant differences in antibody levels were observed between pRBCs of different genotypes and human blood types (Figures S4 and S5). Complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) assays revealed that TKO significantly diminished complement-mediated lysis of pRBCs exposed to human plasma compared to WT. Furthermore, the expression of transgenic CD55 on the TKO background further markedly reduced cytotoxicity, whereas the introduc CD47 did not significantly alter cytolytic activity; the cytotoxicity of the complement system in 5GE pig RBCs remained higher than that in the ABO-C group (Figure 3E).

Fresh human plasma and RBCs were incubated to test the deposition of human complement C3b and C5b-9 on the cell surface, which was detected. Compared with WT, the deposition of C3b and C5b-9 on the surface of pig RBCs was significantly reduced by TKO. The binding of 4GE and 5GE to C3b in human plasma was further significantly lower compared to TKO. Meanwhile, there was no significant difference in the binding rates of 4GE, 5GE pRBCs and hRBCs. There is no significant difference in the binding rate of C5b-9 between 4GE and 5GE pRBCs. However, the binding rates of C5b-9 in 4GE and 5GE pRBCs are higher than those in the hRBCs control group, indicating that the expression of the complement regulatory protein human CD55 cannot completely inhibit the activation of the complement system in human plasma (Figures 3F and 3G).

Primary isolated monkey lung macrophages and human THP-1-induced macrophages were used to detect phagocytic activity. Macrophages were identified by CD68 for purity (Figure S6). The results of phagocytosis assays using human THP-1-derived macrophages and RBCs of different genotypes showed that, compared to WT RBCs, the phagocytosis rate of TKO RBCs significantly decreased. The removal of the three Xeno antigens markedly reduced recognition and phagocytosis by human macrophages. No significant difference was observed between 4GE and TKO RBCs. Compared to 4GE RBCs, the phagocytosis rate of 5GE RBCs by human macrophages significantly decreased, indicating that the expression of human CD47 can be recognized by human macrophages, thereby significantly inhibiting phagocytosis of pRBCs (Figures 3H and 3I). Primary macrophages were isolated and extracted from the lung of a rhesus monkey, and the phagocytosis results of RBCs from different genotypes were similar to those observed with human macrophages; 5GE pRBCs exhibited the lowest phagocytosis rate (Figures 3J and 3K).

Hemolytic reaction

Fresh human blood and pRBCs were co-incubated to detect the hemolytic reaction. Plasma was collected to measure indirect bilirubin, plasma-free HGB, LDH, CRP, IL1, IL6, IL8, and TNFα. The pRBCs of WT began to hemolysis within 1 h, and were completely lysed after 2 h. The pRBCs of TKO gradually hemolyzed within 6 h. The hemolysis rates of 4GE and 5GE pRBCs were significantly lower, and there was no significant difference between 5GE pRBCs and the hRBCs group (Figures 4A and 4B). Further analysis indicates that the pRBCs of WT induce a strong hemolysis reaction, accompanied by rapid increases in plasma free HGB, indirect bilirubin, LDH, and proinflammatory cytokines IL1, IL6, IL8, and TNFα. The pRBCs of TKO also trigger a hemolysis reaction, but it is significantly lower compared to WT. Expression of human CD55 on 4GE pRBCs further reduces the hemolysis reaction compared to TKO. Expression of human CD47 and 5GE does not significantly further improve the hemolysis reaction; only the secretion of inflammatory factors in the plasma, including IL1 and IL6, has been inhibited compared to 4GE, suggesting that the expression of human CD47 is still beneficial. Compared to the control human group, the hemolysis reaction of 5GE pig RBCs exists with weak hemolysis accompanied by the secretion of proinflammatory factors (Figures 4C–4J).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates the successful generation of multiple genetically engineered pRBCs designed to overcome critical barriers in Xeno transfusion. By combining the knockout of three major Xeno antigens (α-gal, Sda, Neu5Gc) with the transgenic expression of two human protective proteins (CD55 and CD47), we have developed a genetically engineered pRBC with significantly improved compatibility in the human immune system, as evidenced by a series of in vitro assays.

Our findings confirm that the TKO strategy effectively reduces the binding of human natural antibodies (IgG and IgM) to pRBCs.23 This is a crucial first step, as preformed antibodies against these carbohydrate antigens are a primary driver of hyperacute rejection, leading to rapid complement activation and pRBC lysis. The reduction in antibody binding observed aligns with previous studies on TKO pRBCs, validating our model. However, antibody-mediated rejection is multifaceted, involving not only initial binding but also subsequent complement cascade activation. The expression of human CD55 (DAF) directly addresses this next hurdle. Our hemolytic reaction and complement deposition assays provide strong evidence that CD55 expression on TKO pRBCs confers significant protection against CDC. CD55 functions by accelerating the decay of C3 and C5 convertases, thereby inhibiting the formation of the MAC. This result is particularly significant as complement-mediated lysis is a major cause of rapid RBC destruction in xenogeneic environments.24,25 If additional complement regulatory proteins such as CD46 or CD59 can be expressed, or complement inhibitor drugs can be combined, it would be a very promising application direction.26,27,28,29

Beyond humoral immunity, cellular immunity, particularly macrophage phagocytosis, presents another formidable barrier. The inability of human macrophage SIRPα to recognize porcine CD47 sends a pro-phagocytic “eat me” signal. Our phagocytosis assays clearly demonstrate that the expression of human CD47 on the engineered pRBCs drastically reduces their uptake by human macrophages. Human CD47 successfully engages with human SIRPα, transmitting a potent “don’t eat me” signal. Furthermore, the potential interaction between pRBC-expressed hCD47 and human thrombospondin-1 (TSP1), which could modulate signaling and potentially convert the “don’t eat me” signal into a pro-phagocytic or pro-apoptotic signal, remains unexplored. This finding is critical because even if pRBCs survive initial antibody and complement attacks, they would likely be swiftly cleared by the mononuclear phagocyte system in the absence of this self-recognition signal. The combination of TKO, hCD55, and hCD47 thus creates a multi-layered defense strategy, simultaneously targeting the distinct yet interconnected pathways of immune rejection; the effect of this genetic modification may require further evaluation through in vivo studies.

The 5GE pRBCs do not express human CD59, a key regulator of the MAC. Theoretically, the addition of human CD59 would provide more comprehensive complement regulation by directly inhibiting MAC formation. However, achieving high expression of CD59 on pRBCs has proven more challenging than for CD47 or CD55, possibly due to differences in protein structure, post-translational modifications, or the GPI anchor-dependent membrane attachment mechanism. We have generated preliminary edited pigs with weak CD59 expression on their RBCs, and functional characterization—including the assessment of MAC inhibition and in vivo survival—is currently underway. Future genetic engineering strategies should aim to optimize CD59 expression and incorporate it alongside hCD55 and hCD47 to fully mitigate complement-mediated rejection and extend pRBC circulatory persistence.

Our results indicate that, under the same conditions, the majority of hematological parameters of the edited pigs (TKO, 4GE, 5GE) are similar to those of the WT pigs. However, the number of WBCs in the edited pigs is generally higher than that of the WT pigs, suggesting a higher susceptibility of the edited pigs to pathogens. Additionally, a study showed that pigs raised in a conventional environment and an environment without specific pathogens (SPF) had their RBCs, HGB, and WBCs analyzed. The RBC and HGB levels of pigs in the SPF environment were consistently higher, while the WBC count of pigs in the SPF environment was significantly lower.30 This indicates that reducing exposure to pathogens under SPF conditions effectively inhibits the activation of the immune system. Future clinical use of pigs raised in an SPF environment will be more beneficial.

The irregular surface morphology observed in pRBCs compared to the typical biconcave discoid shape of hRBCs raises important questions about their circulatory persistence. Theoretically, this altered morphology could impact cellular deformability—a critical determinant of the ability to navigate through narrow capillaries and inter-endothelial slits in the splenic sinusoids. Reduced deformability would increase mechanical entrapment and subsequent clearance by the reticuloendothelial system, independent of immune-mediated recognition. Previous studies in non-human primates have demonstrated that even genetically modified pRBCs (e.g., TKO) are cleared within days post-transfusion, though these studies often lacked appropriate controls to distinguish between immune-mediated clearance and clearance driven by intrinsic biophysical properties. Currently, the rapid clearance of pRBCs in xenogeneic settings remains predominantly an immunological problem, with antibody binding, complement activation, and macrophage phagocytosis serving as the primary drivers. However, as genetic engineering strategies continue to advance and successfully mitigate these immune barriers—potentially extending pRBC survival from days to weeks—the relative importance of morphological and biophysical factors will likely become more apparent. At that stage, further engineering efforts may need to address not only immunogenicity but also the intrinsic structural properties of pRBCs to achieve long-term circulatory persistence.

While our in vitro results are highly promising, they must be interpreted within the context of the study’s limitations. The complex environment of the entire human body, including adaptive immune responses, cytokine interactions, and the spleen’s filtration function, cannot be fully replicated in a laboratory setting. Our engineered TKO/hCD55/hCD47 pRBCs represent a logical and significant progression from that baseline, and they warrant testing in appropriate pre-clinical animal models to assess their survival time in a living circulatory system. Furthermore, in addition to the known heterologous antigens, there are still other antigenic differences between pigs and humans. The complement activation products such as C3a and C5a are potent allergic toxins that can trigger systemic inflammatory responses, increased vascular permeability, and a sudden drop in blood pressure. These aspects need to be given special attention in future clinical applications.31,32,33,34

An important aspect not addressed in the current study is the biophysical characterization of the 5GE pRBCs under physiological shear stress. While we have demonstrated enhanced biochemical compatibility, pRBCs in the human circulatory system are continuously subjected to hemodynamic shear forces and must deform to pass through capillaries and splenic sinus endothelial slits. Consequently, future investigations such as rheometric, ektacytometry, or microfluidic approaches would be valuable to assess cellular deformability and fragility in human plasma under flow. Such studies would enable us to predict in vivo survival by quantifying deformability under simulated physiological shear conditions, thereby complementing the limitations of static biochemical tests, while also assessing safety by evaluating mechanical fragility under shear stress to ensure the cells do not undergo hemolysis in the circulation and avoid the release of free HGB which could induce nephrotoxicity or vascular dysfunction.

In conclusion, this study provides compelling in vitro evidence that multi-genetic modification of pRBCs—knockout of immunogenic Xeno antigens combined with the expression of human immunoregulatory proteins—synergistically reduces key immune responses. This strategy effectively tackles the triad of rejection mechanisms: antibody binding, complement activation, and macrophage phagocytosis. These engineered pRBCs represent a highly promising candidate for Xeno transfusion and could play a transformative role in alleviating the critical shortage of human blood products. Future work must focus on in vivo validation to confirm prolonged survival, large-scale production feasibility, and comprehensive safety profiling to move this promising therapy toward clinical reality.

Limitations of the study

The experimental study is based on in vitro data and cannot fully simulate the process of pig RBCs being transfused into live animals or humans. The longevity of these engineered pRBCs observed in vitro may not directly translate to in vivo survival. In the future, further transfusion of pRBCs into the body will enable a more in-depth analysis of the intensity of the hemolysis reaction within the body and a more detailed understanding of the mechanism of the immune response. After incubation of TKO RBCs with human plasma samples, there was a positive hemagglutination reaction and antibody binding. The related antigen-antibody reaction needs to be further analyzed. This will allow for a more effective assessment of its usability and the direction for future modifications. Another consideration is the potential risk of zoonotic disease transmission, which was not addressed in this study.

Experimental model and study participant details

Ethics statement

All pig and human experiments followed all relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations.

Experiments involved human and pig

All pig were generously offered by D. P, from Chengdu ClonOrgan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of The People’s Hospital of Jianyang City (2025-YJZZX-015-01-P), and carried out in compliance with the relevant ethical standards. Human blood samples were obtained from healthy, unmarried male volunteers who provided written informed consent prior to participation. The study protocol, including all experimental procedures involving human blood samples, was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of The People’s Hospital of Jianyang City (approval No. 2025-YJZZX-015-02-H). All animals used in this study were adult female. The human blood samples were obtained from donations by healthy individuals, the committee approving the experiments and confirming that all experiments conform to the relevant regulatory standards and informed consent was obtained from all subjects.

Method details

The construction strategy of editing vector

All expression plasmids and sgRNA that specifically target gene were synthesized by Genewiz Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All constructs were validated by sequencing. The sequence of target genes GGTA1, B4GALNT2/B4GALNT2 LIKE, CMAH synthesis of gRNA related primer GGTA1 (GenBank ID: (396733) targeting exon 4; CMAH (GenBank: 396918), targeting exon 1; B4GALNT2 (GenBank: 100621328) and B4GalNT2-like (NCBI Gene ID: 110255214) genes with similar sequences. The 3 gRNA were synthesized by Genewiz Biotechnology Co., Ltd. sgRNA that target sequence at Rosa26 site of pig genome, H11 site gRNA: and CDS of Homo sapiens complement regulatory protein CD55 (GenBank: NM_000574) CD47(GenBank: NM_198793.3).

Isolation of RBCs from human and pig blood

Human and pig whole blood was collected using a heparin sodium collection tube. The blood was centrifuged at 500 g for 10min. The upper layer of plasma is stored at −80° for future use, while the lower layer of red blood cells is washed at 500 g, 5min with PBS three times.

AO blood type identification of pRBCs

100μL of 5 × 105 RBCs were incubated with 2μL of anti-human blood type A antibody at 4°C in the dark for 30 min. Then, cells were washed with PBS and incubated with AF488-goat anti-mouse secondary antibody at 4°C in the dark for 30 min. Finally, cells were washed three times with PBS and analyzed on a flow cytometer (CytoFLEX, Beckman). The data were analyzed using the FlowJo software.

Identification and analysis of genotype

Extract DNA using pig blood. Specific primers targeting the deletion sites were designed for the deletion of the three genes (GGTA1, B4GALNT2, and CMAH) and the deletion was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. RBCs (5 × 105) were collected and stained with antibodies. α-Gal was stained by FITC conjugated Isolectin B4 (BSI-B4, SIGMA, L2895;1:500). Sda was stained using Fluorescein Dolichos Biflorus Agglutinin (DBA, Vector Laboratories, FL-1031;1:500). The Neu5Gc was assessed by chicken anti-Neu5Gc antibody (BioLegend, 146901; 1:2000), the secondary antibody was Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-chicken (Abcam, ab96947. 1:1000), and the chicken IgY Isotype used as negative control (BioLegend, 402101,1:2000). Human CD55 was determined by mouse anti human CD55 antibody (santa cruz, SC-59092, 1:50), human CD47 was determined by (FITC-mouse anti human CD47 monoclonal antibody (BD, 556045, 1:50). Samples were incubate with antibody at 4° in the dark for 30 min, then use PBS washed and analyzed on a flow cytometer (CytoFLEX, Beckman). The data were analyzed using the FlowJo software.

Hemagglutination assay

20μL RBCs(cell concentration: 1 × 107/mL)+ 20μL 50% concentration heat-inactivated plasma incubated at room temperature for 60min in a 96-well plate. Use a microscope for observation. Hemagglutination scores were recorded using the modified Marsh score. (Marsh WL. Scoring of hemagglutination reactions. Transfusion 1972;12:352-3).

Human anti-porcine IgG and IgM binding analysis

In a 5mL round-bottom tube, 100μL 50% concentration heat-inactivated plasma or PBS buffer (Solarbio, P1010) (antibody control) was incubated with 100μL 5 × 105pig cells for 30 min at 4°C(final plasma concentration of 25%). To prevent nonspecific antibody binding, 200μL 10% goat serum (Gibco, 16210072) was incubated with cells for 15 min at 4°C. Detection of IgM or IgG binding was performed by incubating with 100μL Alexa fluor488-conjugated Affnipure Goat anti-human IgG(H + L)(Jackson immunoresearch, 109-545-003,1:1000, Alexa fluor647-conjugated Affnipure Goat anti-human IgM(Jackson immunoresearch, 109-605-043, 1:1000)for 30 min in the dark at 4°C. Binding of IgM and IgG was assessed by flow cytometry. Binding of IgM and IgG was assessed by the relative geometric mean (relative GM) value, which was calculated as follows: relative GM = GM in each experiment plasma/GM in IgG or IgM antibody control.

Complement-dependent cytotoxicity assay

100 μL 50% heat-inactivated plasma or 100μL PBS buffer (complement/positive control) was incubated with 100μL 5 × 105 RBCs for 30 min at 4°C,(final plasma concentration of 25%). Add 400μL 30% rabbit complement (Cedarlane, C3010R) or 400μL 0.1% Triton ×-100(Sigma, V900502) (positive control) and incubate for 30 min at 37°C.Centrifuge at 500 g for 5min, gently transfer 100μL×3(total 300μL) supernatant to a new tube. Transfer of each 100μL×3 supernatant to 96-well plates and measure OD560nm. Each sample was tested in triplicate. Cytotoxicity is calculated as follows: (Experiment plasma group-complement control)/(positive control-complement control).

C3b and C5b-9 deposition assay

100μL 5 × 105 RBCs were added with final 25% concentration normal human plasma and followed by incubation at 37°C for 30 min. As a negative control, cells were treated with plasma heat-inactivated at 56°C for 30 min to deactivate complement. After incubation, the cells were washed and stained with a PE-conjugated antibody against C3b (1:100 dilution, 846104, BioLegend) or mouse anti-C5b-9 antibody (ab66768, Abcam, 1:100) and Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (ab150113, abcam,1:1000) in the dark at 4°C for 30 min. The cell samples were washed twice and immediately analyzed using a flow cytometer. Data were processed with FlowJo software. C3b deposition was presented as Mean Fluorescence Intensity (MFI), and statistical results were calculated using GraphPad Prism v8.

Antibody mediated macrophage phagocytosis analysis

THP-1 cells were seeded, and phorbol myristate acetate (PMA, 100 ng/mL) was added to the complete medium to induce maturation into macrophages. After 48 h, THP-1 cells were incubated with IFN-γ (20 ng/mL) and LPS (100 ng/mL) for more than 48 h to polarize them toward M1 phenotype. RBCs were suspended in 1640 and labeled with CFSE (MCE, HY-D0056,1:5000) with a final concentration of 4 × 106/mL. CFSE -labeled RBCs (4×105) were added to 1.5mL tubes containing 1640 medium, and incubated with lung macrophages or spleen phagocytes (1 × 105) at a ratio of 4:1, without (for direct phagocytosis) or with (for ADCC) recipient plasma (final plasma concentration of 25%) for 3 h at 37°C, 5%CO2. After incubation, cells were added RBC lysing solution 15min for lysis all free CFSE staining RBC and membrane attached RBC. The percentage of phagocytosed cells was determined by the percentage of macrophage that were CFSE positive by flow cytometry.

Hemolytic assay

5 × 105 Human RBC and 2 × 105 CFSE-labeled pig red blood cells(O-type human red blood cells were used as a negative control) were added to 1 mL of human fresh plasma within 5 × 106 human PBMC. The mixture was incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 6 h. The survival rate of CFSE-labeled red blood cells was detected by flow cytometry at 1 h, 2 h, 3 h, and 6 h. The supernatant was collected at 6 h for cytokine analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism statistical software (version 10, San Diego, CA). All values are presented as means ± SD and were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by an appropriate post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. The significance levels are as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001, ns, not significant.
