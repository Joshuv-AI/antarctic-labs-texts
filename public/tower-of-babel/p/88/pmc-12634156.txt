Revisiting Glomerulopathy in Clusterin Knockout Mice: A Mouse Model of Human Immunotactoid Glomerulopathy
Authors: Xinglan Li, Xingwei Cai, Qiunong Guan, Junya Tan, Xudan Yang, Changhong Wu, Caigan Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimClusterin (CLU) is a chaperone‐like glycoprotein, and CLU knockout (KO) mice spontaneously develop age‐dependent glomerulopathy with microtubule formation. However, its clinical relevance has not been confirmed yet. Immunotactoid glomerulopathy (ITG) is a clinicopathologic entity of glomerulonephritis characterised by glomerular deposits of electron dense microtubules. This study was to re‐examine the glomerulopathy in CLU KO mice as compared with the pathological features of human ITG.MethodsKidney specimens from both wild type C57BL/6 (B6) and CLU‐KO B6 mice were analyzed with haematoxylin and eosin, Masson‐Trichrome, and Congo‐red staining. IgG and DNAJB9 were detected by immunohistochemical staining, and the electron‐dense mesangial deposits by transmission electron microscopy. Proteins were identified by liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry.ResultsThere were approximately 75% of glomeruli affected by moderate to severe mesangial lesions in 24‐month‐old male CLU‐KO mice or 85% in 18‐month‐old female KO mice as compared with little or no glomerular pathology in WT mice at a similar age. The mesangial lesions were stained strongly positive with Masson‐Trichrome‐stained fibrosis, immunocomplex and anti‐IgG, weakly positive with Congo‐red, and negative with anti‐DNAJB9 antibodies. The electron‐dense material in the mesangium was structured with hollow microtubules with an average diameter of 50 nm. Functional enrichments with the STRING database revealed that secreted CLU‐bound proteins mainly mediated complement activation and were associated with glomerulonephritis development.ConclusionOur data confirm that the glomerulopathy in CLU‐KO B6 mice was similar to the pathological features of ITG and may therefore be considered as an experimental model of ITG. Further, CLU may negatively regulate the fibrillogenesis of ITG.Keywords: animal model, clusterin protein, fibrillogenesis, immunotactoid glomerulopathy, kidney disease

Introduction

Glomerulonephritis (GN) is a relatively rare kidney disease that can be induced by a variety of known factors (e.g., bacterial or hepatitis C virus infection, autoantibodies to glomerular antigens or circulating antibody/immune complexes) as well as unknown causes [1, 2]. This kidney disease is often difficult to treat, sometimes with no cure available, and it can progress to chronic kidney disease (CKD) and is a leading cause of end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD) [3]. Immunotactoid glomerulopathy (ITG) is one of the clinicopathologic entities of GN, and it is diagnosed by the glomerular deposition of microtubule formation [4, 5] that is presented by the hollow parallelly stacked microtubules at 9–55 nm diameter in the transmission electron microscopic (TEM) examination [5, 6]. The incidence of ITG is 0.04%–0.1% [5, 7, 8, 9], and its prognosis is indicated by the fact that approximately 20% of ITG patients are progressing to ESRD within 4 years [5, 9, 10] However, the pathogenesis of microtubule formation in ITG is poorly understood, in part because we have not identified any animal models for this condition.

CLU gene codes clusterin (CLU) protein and it in humans contains 10 exons, in which the first two exons are alternative (designated 1 and 1′) [11, 12]. This gene can be transcribed into mRNA isoform 1 or 2 by using alternative first exon (1 or 1′) and sharing exons 2–9. The mRNA isoform 1 is translated to the nuclear isoforms of CLU (nCLU) [13], and the mRNA isoform 2 has only one ATG translation start site in exon 2, right before the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)‐targeting signal that is translated to the pre‐secreted isoform of CLU (sCLU), a major product of the CLU gene [14]. The nCLU is translocated into the nucleus after translation and glycosylation, whereas the pre‐secreted sCLU is targeted to ER and Golgi bodies, in which CLU is glycosylated and cleaved at an internal bond between Arg‐205 and Ser‐206 to produce a mature sCLU, a secreted disulfide‐linked heterodimer of α‐ and β‐chains [15] that has been found in all of the physiological fluids including serum [15]. Murine Clu gene contains nine exons that are only transcribed to a single mRNA. The nCLU isoform in mice has not been reported as of yet. The protein sequence between mouse CLU and human sCLU has 75% similarity, and both have the same ER‐targeting signal peptide and the cleavage site [16] and similar primary structure including several large molten globule domains, amphipathic regions and coiled‐coil α‐helices [16, 17, 18, 19], which are typical of molecular chaperone—conformational adaptability to allow these CLU proteins to bind different serum proteins such as immunoglobulin (Ig), complements, ceruloplasmin, fibrinogen and albumin or lipids with high affinity and low specificity [17, 20, 21, 22, 23]. Thus, CLU in mice can be used as an experimental model for the investigation of human sCLU functions.

CLU‐knockout (KO) mice used for this study were generated by Dr. Aronow's group more than 20 years ago [24]. They initially reported that these CLU‐KO mice developed a progressive glomerulopathy with age, which was characterised by the mesanglial expansion and the deposits of immune complex (Igs and complement) [25]. We have noticed some similarities in the pathological features between the ‘glomerulopathy’ of aged CLU‐KO mice and ITG in humans [4, 7, 26], which however have not been confirmed in the literature yet. The objective of this study was to revisit the glomerulopathy of aged CLU‐KO mice with additional data and to verify if the CLU‐KO mice could be used as an experimental model of ITG in humans for future studies of the pathogenesis of the microtubule formation of ITG.

Materials and Methods

Animals, Ethical Approval and Experimental Groups

All methods were carried out in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. Both wild‐type C57BL/6 (WT B6) and CLU‐KO mice in the B6 background (homozygous KO) were received from the breeding colonies in the animal facility at the Jack Bell Research Centre (Vancouver, BC, Canada). Mice were housed in the specific pathogen‐free facility with a 12‐h light cycle, and they were kept in the shoebox microisolator (small size) with a filter top (maximum four mice per cage) that was provided with mouse chow (5015), sterile water in a bottle, and corn‐cob bedding or crinkle paper with nestlet and igloo or tunnel and a running wheel. The husbandry of these mice included a visual check twice a day, drinking water changes twice a week and cage changes once or twice a week or as needed.

To investigate whether the development of glomerulopathy in CLU‐KO mice was related to age and gender, four experimental groups were included: (1) male WT mice (three mice, 20–24 month‐old) as male control, (2) different age groups of male CLU‐KO mice (10 month‐old: three mice, 12 month‐old: three mice, 18 month‐old: seven mice, and 24 month‐old: six mice), (3) female WT mice (three mice, 18–22 month‐old) as female control and (4) different age groups of female CLU‐KO mice (each age group [10, 12, and 18 month‐old], three mice). The total number of mice used was 34. These mice were randomly selected as surplus mice from the breeding colonies of WT or CLU‐KO mice and cared for or monitored until the experimental endpoints by QG, and the renal histopathological phenotype of each mouse was primarily determined by XL and XC. A single mouse represented an experimental unit. After the pilot study confirmed the glomerulopathy in aging male CLU‐KO mice (18 or 24 month‐old) in the sample size (n ≥ 6) calculated as described previously [27], the sample size in each group was reduced to 3 in the remaining experiments following the 3Rs (replacement, reduction and refinement) principles of the use of animals in research. Mice at the humane endpoints (≥ 15% of bodyweight loss, severe hunching, paralysis or unable to access food or water or bloody urine) were excluded from the experiments. At both experimental and humane endpoints, the mice were euthanized using inhalant anaesthetic isoflurane, followed by CO2 according to the standard operating procedure (SOP: ACC CCM 2012‐03 Euthanasia of Adult Rodents) approved by the University of British Columbia Animal Care Committee.

Light Microscopic Examination of Histopathological Changes in Glomeruli

The kidney specimens were fixed with 10% neutral formalin, embedded in paraffin and sectioned. Approximately 5 μm thick tissue sections were used for examination of the histopathological changes in glomeruli with different histological stains.

Tissue sections were stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for examination of the severity of glomerular lesions that were graded into four categories according to a glomerular lesion scoring system as described in the literature [25]. Figure S1 showed a representative image of each lesion category: 0, normal or no lesion found; 1, lesion affecting < 25% of glomerular section; 2, lesion affecting 25%–50%; 3, lesion affecting 50%–75%; and 4, lesion affecting > 75%. The lesion category of at least 100 glomeruli in three non‐serial tissue sections per mouse was examined under a light microscope by two experienced pathologists (X.L. and J.T.), and the percentage of each lesion category was calculated. The damage score of the glomeruli in each mouse was calculated as follows: Total damage score = (a percentage of category 1 × 1) + (a percentage of category 2 × 2) + (a percentage of category 3 × 3) + (a percentage of category 4 × 4).

Periodic acid‐Schiff (PAS) staining was used for identifying the glomerular basement, membranes, mesangial matrix and tubular basement membranes as described by Chawla [28], Masson‐Trichrome staining for evaluation of glomerular fibrosis (accumulation of collagen, collagen fibres and fibrin) that was coloured in blue, following the protocol as described by Winson [29], and Congo red staining for demonstration of amyloid protein aggregation [30].

The positivity of IgG staining in the glomeruli was assessed by using immunofluorescence as described previously [25], and the presence of DNAJB9 and light chain restriction (κ and λ) by using immunohistochemical (IHC) staining as described previously [31]. The primary antibodies for the immunostaining in this study were listed in Table S1.

Electron Microscopic Examination of Glomerular Electron Dense Deposits

In order to confirm the structure of electron dense materials in the glomeruli of CLU‐KO mice, the glomeruli were first isolated from two aging mice (an 18‐month‐old female, and a 24‐month‐old male) using the protocol modified from the method as published previously [32]. In brief, the renal cortex tissues were minced into small pieces (~1 mm3) in ice‐cold PBS, followed by digestion in 1 mg/mL collagenase type V in PBS at 37°C for 20 min. The digestion was stopped by mixing with an equal volume of ice‐cold PBS containing 2% FBS. The digested tissues were filtered through a 100 μm stainless steel cell strainer. The remaining large pieces of the tissues were pressed through the strainer with the rubber plunger of a 5‐mL syringe. All filtrate was collected by centrifugation and the resultant tissue pellet was resuspended in 35 mL of ice‐cold PBS. The tissue homogenate was filtered again through a 40 μm plastic cell strainer and washed with ice‐cold PBS. The retained glomeruli along with tubular fragments on the top of the strainer were collected and transferred to a plastic culture dish. After 2 min of setting or the tubular fragments adhered onto the bottom of the culture dish, the glomeruli‐enriched supernatant was harvested and pelleted by centrifugation at 290g at 4°C for 5 min.

The isolated glomeruli or glomeruli‐enriched pellets were immediately fixed in 2% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer (pH 7.2), followed by post‐fixative 1% OsO4 in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer (pH 7.2). The samples were then dehydrated in a graded series of ethanol and embedded in London resin for sectioning. Sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and observed with Hitachi H7600 TEM (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) at the Imaging facility of the University of British Columbia (Vancouver, BC, Canada). All processing methods were described previously [33].

Urine Collection and Determination of Renal Function

Urine samples were successfully collected at the endpoint of experiments (WT: 18 and 22 months, n = 8, CLU‐KO: 12, 18 and 24 months, n = 8) by using a metabolic cage (3600M021, Tecniplast, Toronto, ON, Canada). In brief, the mice were individually housed in the metabolic cage for 8 h during the dark phase of a 12‐h light/dark cycle without adaptive training, and they had unrestricted access to the same food and drinking water ad libitum as in the ‘home’ cages. Urine specimens (1–1.5 mL per mouse) were collected and centrifuged at 5000g to pellet cellular debris, and the supernatant (urine) was stored in aliquots at−80°C until use. Creatinine levels in urine samples were measured by QuantiChrom creatinine assay kit (BioAssay Systems, Hayward, CA). Total protein levels in the urine specimens were measured using the Dimension Vista 1500 System (Siemens Healthineers Canada, Oakville, ON, Canada) in the Core Chemistry Laboratory at Vancouver General Hospital (Vancouver, BC, Canada).

Cell Culture

CLU null tubular epithelial cells (TECs) were isolated from the kidney cortex of a CLU knockout (KO) mouse as described previously and were immortalised or cloned by transfection with origin‐deficient SV40 DNA [34]. The phenotype of cloned murine TECs was confirmed by their expression of E‐cadherin and CD13 (alanine aminopeptidase) using fluorescence‐activating cell sorting (FACS) analysis, and were grown in complete K1 (K1+/+) medium supplemented with 5% foetal bovine serum and growth factor mixture as described previously [35].

Ectopic Expression of Human CLU in Cultured Mouse TECs

CLU null TECs were converted to CLU‐expressing TECs (TEC‐CLUhCLU) by stable expression of His‐tagged human CLU isoform I cDNA using the pHEX6300 vector as described previously [36, 37]. TEC‐CLUhCLU cells were grown and maintained in complete K1 medium in the presence of the selective antibiotic zeocin (up to 500 mg/mL), and were further selected by FACS based on the expression of pHEX6300‐encoded green fluorescent protein (GFP) [34]. More than 95% of cells in the TEC‐CLUhCLU culture were confirmed to have a high level of GFP by either flow cytometry or fluorescence microscopy.

Precipitation of CLU‐Bound Proteins

TEC‐CLUhCLU cells were incubated with K1+/+ medium containing 10% (v/v) human serum from a healthy donor. The culture supernatant was harvested after 24 h of incubation, followed by centrifugation to pellet cellular debris. The cleaned supernatant was mixed with Ni‐NTA‐agarose beads and incubated on a rotating shaker at 4°C overnight. The beads, along with His‐tagged CLU‐bound serum proteins, were pelleted from the supernatant by centrifugation and were washed with PBS three times (5 min each time) (Figure S2). The control beads were the Ni‐NTA‐agarose beads incubated with fresh human serum (10%)‐containing K1+/+ medium.

Protein Separation Using SDS‐PAGE

The bead pellets including control samples were mixed with the equal amount of SDS sample buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 6.8, 2% SDS, 10% glycerol, 0.1 M DTT and 0.02% bromophenol blue) and boiled for 5 min. The proteins, released from the beads, were fractionated by 10% SDS‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and were stained with Coomassie brilliant blue dye (Figure S2).

Liquid Chromatography‐Mass Spectrometry (LC–MS)

The His‐tagged CLU‐bound proteins on SDS‐PAGE were identified by using LC–MS as described previously with some modification [38, 39]. After destaining with 50% methanol in 10% acetic acid, the specific protein bands in TEC‐CLUhCLU medium samples as compared to nonspecific, background protein bands in fresh medium control and the serum were selected for protein identification using the LC–MS technique (Figure S2).

In brief, protein bands of interest were treated with 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate twice for 10 min and dehydrated with 50% acetonitrile twice for 10 min before drying under vacuum. Reduction and alkylation were carried out with 10 mM DTT for 35 min at 65°C followed by 55 mM iodoacetamide for 30 min at room temperature in the dark after removal of excess DTT. Gel pieces were washed twice with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate for 10 min and dried as above with 50% acetonitrile prior to digestion with trypsin (10 μg/mL). Supernatant peptide solutions were transferred to Eppendorf and gel pieces extracted 15 min twice with 50% acetonitrile in 5% formic acid. Extracts were pooled with initial supernatant and the volume brought to about 15 μL and transferred to sample plates for analysis.

An Orbitrap Fusion Lumos MS platform (Thermo Scientific) was used for analysis. Samples were introduced using an Easy‐nLC 1200 system (Thermo) with a 100 μm ID × 20 cm in house‐made column (ReproSil‐Pur 120 C18‐AQ 1.9 μm—Dr. Maisch GMbH HPLC). Prior to each sample injection, the analytical column was equilibrated at 400 bar for a total volume of 8 μL. After injection, sample loading was carried out for a total volume of 8 μL at 500 bar. The injection volumes for all samples were 4 μL and runs using a gradient of mobile phase A (water and 0.1% formic acid) and B (80% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid) at 0.3 μL/min, 3%–35% B from 2 to 40 min followed by 35%–40% B over 5 min, 40%–95% B over 3 and 6 min at 95%.

Data acquisition (control software version 3.1.2412.17) was with a data‐dependent method and MS2 in the Orbitrap. The Lumos was operated with a positive ion spray voltage of 2100 and a transfer tube temperature of 325°C. The default charge state was set as 2. Survey scans (MS1) were acquired in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 60 K, across a mass range of 375–1500 m/z, with an RF lens setting of 60, an AGC target of 4e5, and a max injection time of 86 ms in profile mode. For MS2 scans, charge state filtering of 2–5, and dynamic exclusion for 60 s with 10 ppm tolerances was used with a 1.2 m/z window prior to HCD fragmentation of 37%. MS2 data acquisition carried out in the Orbitrap used a 15 K resolution, a fixed first mass of 120 m/z, an AGC target of 1e5, and a max injection time of 54 ms in centroid mode.

The accumulated data were analysed using ProteinLynx Global Server software (PLGS) set with peptide and fragment mass accuracies of 25 ppm and 0.1 Da (Waters, Mississauga, ON, Canada), respectively. The peptide/protein search engine was applied to the SwissProt database. A search with similar parameters was also carried out using Mascot using the peaks generated in PLGS. Proteins with Mascot scores P32 and P2 peptide matches were considered statistically significant (p < 0.05) and included as affirmative identification.

Statistical Analysis

Data in each group are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were statistically analysed by using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.1) (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA). The difference of the mean between two groups was determined using a two‐tailed t test. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Different Characteristics of Glomerulopathy Development Between Female and Male CLU‐KO Mice

HE staining showed that the histopathological change of the glomeruli was mainly sclerosis accompanied by mesangial cells and mesenteric matrix hyperplasia (Figures S2 and 1A). Similar to the observation in a previous study [25], no inflammatory infiltration or necrosis in the glomeruli, regardless of the extent of glomerular lesions, was found, whereas there was little or no glomerular pathological change in WT mice (Figure 1A). Further, there were differences in both disease onset and severity between female and male mice. As shown in Figure 1B, the severe glomerular damage (score: 137.89 ± 8.04) was already seen in female mice at the age of 10 months, whereas there was barely any damage (score: 23.00 ± 1.16) in male mice at the same age (female vs. male: p < 0.0001, two‐tailed t test, n = 3) (Figure 1B), and up to 75% of glomeruli in these CLU‐KO mice exhibited moderate to severe mesangial lesions (score: 2–4) in 24‐month‐old male mice or 85% in 18‐month‐old female mice (Figure 1A). The peak levels of glomerular damage were seen in both male and female mice at the age of 18 months, but they were significantly higher in female mice than in male mice (female: 303.67 ± 13.20, n = 3; male: 171.67 ± 62.99, n = 7) (female vs. male: p = 0.0083, two‐tailed t test) (Figure 1B). It was also acknowledged that female mice over 20 months of age were euthanized due to the humane endpoint—poor health according to the animal care protocol for this study.

Clinicopathologic Features of Glomerulopathy in CLU‐KO Mice Similar to ITG

Histologically, the glomeruli of CLU‐KO kidneys exhibited deposition of PAS‐stained materials in the mesangium and the basement membranes of the glomerulus capillary (Figure 2B), and Masson‐Trichrome staining showed that the glomerular lesions contained the deposits of collagen and immune complexes that were detected in abnormal glomeruli (Figures S2 and 2D). Interestingly, some glomeruli were stained weakly positive with Congo‐red dye in the expanded mesangium (Figure 2F). There was little or no diagnostic glomerular pathological change in kidney sections of WT mice (Figure 2A,C,E).

The immunofluorescence staining of IgG showed that the mesangial lesions and capillary wall in aging CLU‐KO mice (both 10 and 18 months old) contained IgG proteins or fragments (Figure 3), which was indicated by a granular distribution pattern of IgG positivity with 1+ staining intensity, whereas the staining was negative in the sections of WT mice (Figure 3A).

Furthermore, IHC staining showed that the glomeruli were negative in the staining of both DNAJB9 (Figure 4A) and κ (Figure 4B), but were positive with λ (Figure 4C). In this assay, the positive staining with anti‐DNAJB9 antibody was found in epithelial tubules (Figure 4A). Correlation of Glomerulopathy With Kidney Dysfunction in CLU‐KO Mice

To further confirm the glomerulopathy in CLU‐KO mice, their kidney function was determined using both serum creatinine (SCr) and urinary protein‐to‐creatinine ratio (PCR) as measures of kidney function. As shown in Figure 5, the levels of SCr and PCR in CLU‐KO mice were significantly higher than those in WT groups (SCr: 3.664 ± 0.661 μM vs. 2.505 ± 0.661 μM, p = 0.0007; PCR: 23.005 ± 5.797 vs. 10.829 ± 3.526, p = 0.0002, two‐tailed t test, n = 8), suggesting that there was a correlation between the severity of renal tissue damage and renal dysfunction in CLU‐KO mice.

Predicting Physiological Functions of Serum CLU Using STRING Database

To understanding the mechanisms by which CLU deficiency caused ITG‐like kidney disease in CLU‐KO mice, we established a culture system—TEC‐CLUhCLU cells incubated with human serum in which secreted rhCLU by TEC‐CLUhCLU bound serum proteins in a natural manner (Figure S2). In addition to CLU protein, LC–MS analysis identified 26 different serum proteins that were precipitated with rhCLU, which were ‘binding’ proteins (i.e., FN1, ApoB, ApoD, A2M, PZP, HPX, HP, HPR and APCS), complements (i.e., CFH, CFHR, C3, C4, C1Q, C4BPA and FCN3), and antibody chains (Figure S2 and Table 1). The network of these proteins was analysed using the STRING database (www.cn.string‐db.org, version: 12.0) and was shown in Figure 6. The p value of protein–protein interaction (PPI) enrichment of the network of these proteins was < 1.0 × 10−16, suggesting that there were significantly more interactions between the proteins than expected for a random set of proteins of the same size and degree distribution drawn from the genome. Importantly, such enrichment also indicated that the proteins were at least partially biologically connected as a group.

Discussion

The ITG is diagnosed by the presence of the hollow parallelly stacked microtubule deposits in the glomeruli and 82% monoclonal of the lesions [4, 5] (Table 2). The initial study reported a progressive glomerulopathy in aged CLU‐KO mice in the Swiss Black outbred background (21 months old) that was characterized by the deposition of immune complexes (IgG, IgM, IgA, C1q, C3 and C9) in the mesangium. TEM examination showed the hollow tubule structure at an average of 80 nm diameter in the mesangial deposits [25]. The findings from this initial report made us wonder if the glomerulopathy of these mice was similar to ITG in humans—these mice might represent an experimental model of this clinicopathologic entity of GN. In the present study, we confirmed the severe glomerulopathy in aged CLU‐KO mice in B6 background, and female mice seemed more susceptible than male mice to this disease. The hollow microtubule structure in the mesangial deposits of the isolated glomeruli from CLU‐KO mice was confirmed by TEM as well. However, weak positivity was seen in the Congo‐red staining of the glomerular lesions in CLU‐KO mice that were initially reported negative [25]. In addition, our data showed the negative staining of DNAJB9 antigen in the glomerular lesions, in which the Ig including IgG is monoclonal (λ chain). Taken together, the results from both the initial observation of CLU‐KO mice [25] as well as from the present study suggested that as compared with the pathological characteristics of ITG, the pathological features of GN in CLU‐KO mice were much more similar to those of ITG in humans (Table 2).

In a human body, secreted CLU (sCLU) is one of the abundant glycoproteins in the circulation at a range of 35–353 μg/mL [40, 41, 42, 43], and it is nearly ubiquitous in the tissues [15]. The primary structure of both human and mouse sCLU is similar, and both can act as molecular chaperones, which is evidenced by binding various proteins or lipids with high affinity and low specificity [17, 20, 21, 22, 23]. Our previous study has identified fibronectin, alpha‐2 macroglobulin, ceruloplasmin, apolipoproteins, complement 8 and Ig heavy chain as sCLU‐bound proteins that are directly immune‐precipitated from the serum of WT mice [39]. sCLU from TEC‐CLUhCLU cells also binds these proteins or their counterparts in human serum‐containing culture medium (Table 1). Functional enrichments of sCLU‐bound proteins using STRING database suggest that these proteins mainly regulate the complement activities that are associated with GN development. Indeed, it has been well documented that sCLU in plasma inhibits the cytolysis of the complement membrane attack complex (MAC) by binding to the complement components [44, 45, 46, 47], and it mediates the clearance of cellular debris into non‐professional phagocytes [48, 49] and of late apoptotic cells via phagocytosis, resulting in the prevention of autoimmunity in the kidney [49, 50]. The serum levels of CLU in WT type B6 mice were 71.03 ± 40.47 μg/mL (n = 12) that were measured by using the mouse clusterin ELISA kit (ab199079, Abcam), and CLU‐deficient serum from CLU KO mice has higher activity in the activation of rabbit complement against cultured podocytes as compared with CLU‐containing serum from WT mice while their C3 levels were no different [39], suggesting that CLU in the serum functions as a negative regulator of complement activation, which however remains for further investigation.

GN in humans is a group of immune‐mediated kidney diseases showing different histopathological lesion patterns with many differential diagnoses for each entity [51, 52]. In renal biopsies of GN patients, CLU‐bound terminal complement complex including sCLU‐SC5b‐9 complex is found both in the specific immune glomerular deposition and in the ‘non‐specific’ deposition in areas of renal injury [42, 50, 53, 54, 55], and the levels of glomerular CLU staining are positively correlated with a reduction of proteinuria in a group of membranous GN (MGN) patients [54]. Serum levels of sCLU are markedly lower in patients with active membranous nephropathy (MN), focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), and in children with steroid‐responsive nephrotic syndrome (NS) compared to controls [56]. sCLU in plasma inhibits the cytolysis of MAC by binding to the complement components [44, 45, 46, 47], and the main function of sCLU‐bound serum proteins is predicted here to regulate the complement activation that is associated with GN development (Figure 7). Therefore, it is postulated that a low level of serum sCLU is a common immunopathogenesis impact factor for GN development and in combination with other different pathological factors (i.e., infection, autoantibodies, genetic immune disorders, etc.) among these GN patients, the histopathology of GN has been classified as different types of glomerular diseases such as MN, FSGS and NS as mentioned above. This hypothesis is supported by one of our experimental studies showing that CLU‐KO but not WT mice develop cBSA‐mediated GN [39].

Notwithstanding the serum levels of sCLU among ITG patients having been documented, the data from proteomic analysis show that CLU protein is detected only in the glomerular deposits of two out of seven ITG cases (data from two studies) [10, 57] although sCLU is one of the abundant glycoproteins in the circulation of normal persons. All these data including the development of ITG‐like glomerulopathy in aged CLU‐KO mice may suggest that the loss of the inhibitory role of sCLU causes the formation of immune complexes in GN and specifically the microtubule formation in the glomeruli of ITG patients in the presence of other unidentified pathological factors, which however need further investigation.

The Congo‐red stain is primarily used in pathology for detecting amyloid deposits in tissue samples. The glomerular lesions of both ITG and FGN are normally diagnosed with negative staining of Congo red dye—non‐amyloid fibrils [4, 5], but Congo red positivity was found in a case of ITG [5], suggesting that containing amyloid fibril or Congo‐red positively staining is atypical for classic ITG. In the initial study with CLU‐KO mice in Swiss Black outbred background, the mesangial lesions in the glomeruli were Congo red negative [25], but the present study with CLU‐KO mice in B6 background shows weakly positive for Congo red staining of mesangial lesions (~10%). This discrepancy between these two studies may be due to the difference in the Congo red staining protocol between laboratories, or the weak positivity with Congo red dye within the mesangial lesions in CLU‐KO in B6 background may suggest the presence of mild aggregation of amyloid proteins that may not be seen in Swiss Black outbred background. The proteomic analysis show that serum amyloid P‐component (APCS), a universal component of amyloidosis where it interacts with and stabilises amyloid fibrils [58, 59], is only found in the mesangial lesions of ITG (7 of 7 cases) but not in any cases of FGN (0 of 8 cases) [10, 57]. Further, the proteomic analysis of sCLU‐bound proteins in human serum‐containing culture medium from sCLU‐expressing cell cultures revealed that APCS was one of many serum proteins bound to sCLU (Table 2), and ‘Amyloidosis’ was one of diseases regulated by the sCLU‐bound proteins as listed in the Disease‐gene Associations enrichment. Taken together, it may be postulated that in the absence of sCLU in the circulation, APCS may be deposited in the glomeruli. However, whether the presence of APCS in ITG or the weak positivity of Congo red staining in CLU‐KO mice means a role of minimal amyloidosis in the structure of mesangial lesions with hollow microtubules requires further investigation.

DNAJB9 positivity has been used as a diagnostic marker for FGN in the pathology laboratory as DNAJB9 is a specific immunohistochemical marker for FGN, and it is not observed in any cases of amyloid fibrils or ITG [60, 61], The staining of DNAJB9 in the glomerular lesions of CLU KO mice was revealed to be negative, indicating that the glomerulopathy in these CLU KO mice is not similar to FGN.

One of the important findings in this study is that the glomerulopathy in female mice is more severe than that in male mice at the same age, which is indicated by the fact that the severe glomerulopathy occurred in female mice at the age of 10 months, but there was no pathological change in the kidneys of male mice at the same age, suggesting that the sex plays a crucial role in the development of the glomerulopathy in these mice. In a cohort of 109 ITG patients, 54.13% are women (Table 2), but it is unknown if their ages are matched with those of counterpart (men). It has been well documented that different sex hormones (oestrogen vs. testosterone) regulate the responses of immune cells, such as antigen presenting cells, T cells and B cells, differently [62]. In mice, oestrogen increases autoreactive B cell survival and induces B cell hyperactivity, including increasing Ig and auto‐antibodies, the number of plasma cells, and output of auto‐antibodies per B cell (please see review, [62]). Therefore, it is presumed that the levels of circulating antibodies in female CLU KO mice were higher than those in male CLU KO at the same age, and the antibodies, particularly IgG, may contribute to microtubule formation in the glomeruli of CLU KO mice. Further studies of the differences in the histopathology of this ITG‐like glomerular disease between male and female CLU KO mice are required.

The limitations of the present study could be found as follows (but not limited to): (1) the proteomics of the glomerular deposit of aging CLU‐KO mice was not investigated, so that whether it contains APCS just like in the glomerular lesions of ITG remains unknown; (2) the ITG‐like glomerulopathy was seen in CLU‐KO mice in the genetic background of both C57BL/6 and Swiss Black outbred, but the impact of the other genetic background on the development of the ITG‐like glomerulopathy in CLU‐KO mice needs to be fully investigated; (3) CLU protein is constitutively expressed in the plasma, the STRING analysis elegantly links CLU‐bound proteins to complement activation and GN, but the function validation of this group of proteins remains to be investigated in vivo. Finally, CLU expression can be induced in the kidney in response to stress such as injury; the contribution of local CLU protein to the prevention of the glomerulopathy is unknown.

Conclusions and Future Directions

Taken together, the current study and initial report [25] suggested that the pathological features of glomerulopathy in CLU‐KO mice were closely similar to ITG in humans (Table 2). These findings may not only indicate the inhibitory role of CLU in the development of glomerular deposition of hollow microtubules at least in mice but also imply that the CLU‐KO mice represent a murine model of clinicopathologic entities of GN in humans, more specifically for ITG. However, further studies of the mechanisms underlying the roles of CLU and sex in the development of ITG‐like GN or microtubule formation are needed.
