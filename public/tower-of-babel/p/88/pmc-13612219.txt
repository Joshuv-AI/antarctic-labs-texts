Rbm5 sustains leukemia stem cells through a Myc-driven transcriptional circuitry
Authors: Mengli Zhang, Shaela Fields, Qiong Zhang, Yuxin Ouyang, Ye Xin, Jianxiang Zhang, Hong Liu, Jianfeng Shen, Beisi Xu, Jia Chen, Depei Wu, Chunliang Li, Peng Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute myeloid leukemia (AML) represents a type of malignant hematological disease that is usually caused by the dysregulated developmental program of leukemia stem cells (LSCs). Here, we report that an unappreciated RNA-binding protein, Rbm5, selectively promotes murine leukemogenesis, maintains LSC self-renewal in vivo, and is dispensable for normal hematopoiesis. Rbm5 is highly expressed in LSCs, and its deficiency results in specifically defective LSC function, along with inhibition of self-renewal gene expression and induction of myeloid differentiation. Multi-disciplinary mechanistic investigations further identified Myc as the major and direct transcriptional target of Rbm5 in primary leukemia cells. Moreover, RBM5 not only interacts with MYC but also maintains its protein levels, thereby sustaining the Myc downstream transcriptional network through its proper genome-wide occupancy. Forced expression of Myc sufficiently rescued the Rbm5-depleted LSC defects. Thus, our study demonstrates that Rbm5 regulates the AML LSC program through non-canonical transcriptional mechanisms, providing a strong rationale for targeting Rbm5 therapeutically. In Brief. Zhang et al. illustrate the role of Rbm5 in sustaining the self-renewal program in leukemia stem. cells (LSCs) primarily through the Myc transcriptional network. Specifically, Rbm5 loss results in a significant decrease in Myc protein levels, thereby disrupting. the Myc downstream transcriptional network in LSCs. Notably, this effect is specific to LSCs, as. normal hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) do not exhibit such changes upon Rbm5 loss. Subject terms: Cancer stem cells, Acute myeloid leukaemia, Oncogenes

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a genetically heterogeneous hematological malignancy driven by a variety of complex gene transcription and epigenetic mechanisms [1–8]. Despite recent advances in targeted therapies [9–14], leukemia stem cells (LSCs) remain a key driver of drug resistance and relapse in AML [15–18]. Therefore, there remains a high unmet need to identify more druggable targets for the clearance of LSCs while sparing normal hematopoiesis.

Multiple lines of evidence have demonstrated that mutations and abnormal expression of RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) can promote leukemia development, making them promising druggable targets, such as the well-characterized RBM39 [19–25]. Mechanistically, most RBPs control leukemogenesis by post-transcriptionally regulating RNA splicing, translation, and even non-canonical transcriptional events [26–29]. Although a few RBPs have recently been reported to be crucial for LSC maintenance [30–34], more specific RNA-binding proteins that target LSCs but not hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) remain to be investigated. RBM5, a member of the RNA-binding motif (RBM) family of proteins, was originally identified as a putative tumor suppressor gene in various solid tumors and functions as a key modulator of apoptosis through the alternative splicing of multiple mRNAs [35]. In contrast, our recent study demonstrated that RBM5 is essential for the fitness of human AML cell lines by directly activating HOXA9, highlighting its context-specific role in blood malignancy [27]. However, the regulatory roles of RBM5 in an in vivo mouse disease model and the underlying mechanisms remain to be explored, especially its functions in LSCs and HSCs.

To mitigate this knowledge gap, we leveraged the power of a newly established conditional knockout mouse model to interrogate the detailed role of Rbm5 in blood malignancy and the normal hematopoietic system. Here, we found that RBM5 loss has a greater effect on LSCs than on normal HSCs. In a classic MLL-AF9 fusion gene-driven mouse AML model, Rbm5 deficiency significantly prolongs mouse survival, reduces the proportion of LSCs in the bone marrow, and inhibits the differentiation and clonogenic capacity of LSCs ex vivo. Furthermore, single-cell RNA sequencing analysis revealed that Rbm5 loss specifically decreased the number and proportion of LSCs and significantly downregulated the expression of the stemness gene Myc. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that RBM5 physically interacts with MYC and its known cofactors, thereby sustaining MYC-dependent activation of the self-renewal transcriptional program through an autoregulatory mechanism. Thus, our study suggests that Rbm5 plays a critical regulatory role in maintaining LSC stemness, with promise for novel therapeutic innovations.

Materials and methods

Mice

Rbm5fl/fl knock-in mice were generated via gene targeting by GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (as described in Fig. 1A). Vav-iCre transgenic mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. Rbm5-conditional-knock-out (CKO) mice were generated by crossing Rbm5fl/fl mice with Vav-iCre mice. All the mice were bred on a pure C57BL/6 J genetic background. The genotypes of the mice used in this study have been confirmed by genomic PCR and sequencing prior to use. All genotyping primers were included in Supplementary Table 1. C57BL/6 J (CD45.2) (#N000013) and B6-CD45.1(#T054816) used in this study were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd., and were randomly assigned to experimental groups.

Leukemic transplants and BM (Bone marrow) competitive transplantation

For the primary transplant of transformed MLL-AF9 c-Kit+ cells in leukemia initiation experiments, approximately 50,000 leukemic cells and 500,000 bone marrow support cells from syngeneic mice were injected into 8-week-old C57BL/6 J female mice that had been lethally irradiated with 900 cGy. In secondary transplants, about 200,000 bone marrow leukemic cells were transplanted into C57BL/6 J mice that had been sub-lethally irradiated with 450 cGy. When mice showed signs of systemic illness, such as immobility, a huddled posture, the inability to eat, ruffled fur, or self-mutilation, the animals were killed immediately by CO2 anesthesia or cervical dislocation.

For mouse MLL-AF9 cell transplantation, the Cas9-GFP-expressing MLL-AF9 cells were transduced with the Lenti-guide-puro-IRES-GFP plasmid. Transduced MLL-AF9 cells expressing GFP were adjusted to maintain a GFP-positive cell ratio of 30%. These cells were then injected intravenously into 8-week-old C57BL/6 J female mice (n = 7 per group) at a dose of 500,000 cells per mouse, following 450 cGy irradiation. To assess the leukemic burden of the transduced MLL-AF9 cells, bone marrow was harvested from sgRbm5 and sgNT mice 28 days post-transplantation, and GFP-positive leukemia cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.

For the competitive reconstitution analysis, a total number of 5 × 105 donor BM cells from Rbm5fl/fl or Rbm5Δ/Δ donor (CD45.2+) were mixed with 5 × 105 competitor BM cells (B6-CD45.1), and transplanted into lethally irradiated 8-week-old B6-CD45.1 female recipient mice by retroorbital injection. The chimerism in the peripheral blood was analyzed at 4, 8, 12, and 16 weeks after transplantation by flow cytometry for the congenic markers CD45.1 (Biolegend, #110728) and CD45.2 (Biolegend, #109830).

In vivo and in vitro rescue experiments

For functional rescue studies, MSCV-based retroviral plasmids (10 μg; either empty vector control MSCV-EV-P2A-Puro-PGK-GFP or constructs expressing Rbm5, or Myc with identical backbone) were complexed with 10 μL jetPRIME transfection reagent in the provided buffer and transfected into PLAT-E retroviral packaging cells. Viral supernatants were harvested 48 h post-transfection and used to transduce bulk leukemia cells derived from both Rbm5fl/fl and Rbm5Δ/Δ models. Following puromycin selection (1 μg/mL, 72 h), equal numbers of viable transduced cells (1 × 104) were plated in MethoCult methylcellulose medium (STEMCELL Technologies, #M3434) for standard colony-forming unit (CFU) assays. Alternatively, limiting dilution analysis (LDA) was performed by plating serial dilutions of cells. Colonies were quantified after 5 days of culture under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). For in vivo validation, 5 × 105 transduced cells were injected intravenously into lethally irradiated recipient mice to assess the leukemogenic potential.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and data analysis

Mouse scRNA-seq analysis was performed using the Chromium Single Cell 30 v2 Reagent Kit and Chromium Controller (10× Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Individual libraries were sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq × Ten or Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system with 30,000-40,000 read pairs per cell (Annoroad, China). The scRNA-seq data from mouse AML cells were demultiplexed, aligned to the mouse genome (mm10), and UMI-collapsed using the CellRanger toolkit (v.3.0.2, 10× Genomics). Cells that expressed fewer than 300 genes or over 10% mitochondrial gene content were excluded. Genes expressed in less than three cells per sample were excluded. The Seurat R package v.3.0.2 was used for data normalization, generating uniform manifold approximation and projection plots, and performing differential expression analysis. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to identify differentially expressed genes in each cluster compared with the others.

Software and statistical analysis

Prism software and R were used for data processing, statistical analysis, and result visualization (http://www.graphpad.com). The R language and environment for graphics (https://www.r-project.org) were used in this study for the bioinformatics analysis of the CRISPR screen and RNA-seq data. The R packages used for all analyses described in this manuscript were from the Bioconductor and CRAN. Data were analyzed with an unpaired Student’s t test (two-tailed), one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison test or two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparison test accordingly. The overall survival of in different groups was compared using the Kaplan-Meier method with a log-rank test. Sample sizes were chosen according to previously performed power analysis. For the animal experiment, at least a sample size of 3 mice per group was calculated and used; see figure legends for specific sample sizes, noted as “n”. No blinding was done. Gene expression, CFU assay, and proliferation experiments were performed on at least three independent occasions. Chromatin immunoprecipitation was performed in duplicate (2 technical replicates), and immunoblotting experiments were repeated at least three times. No data was excluded from analysis. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 (*, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001; **** P < 0.0001, and “n.s.” indicates no statistical significance).

Results

Rbm5 promotes murine AML development and progression

To investigate the role of Rbm5 in AML initiation and maintenance, we established a classic human-relevant in vivo model of AML pathogenesis. We utilized the MLL-AF9 retroviral transduction system to induce leukemogenesis in primary bone marrow cells from both wild-type and Rbm5 knockout mice (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Fig. S1A, B). To this end, we first performed a colony formation assay (CFA) using wild-type and Rbm5-deficient c-Kit+ cells transduced with the MLL-AF9 retrovirus. We observed a notable reduction in myeloid colony numbers in the Rbm5-deficient groups in primary plating, and a significantly more pronounced reduction in secondary plating (Fig. 1B). Consistently, we observed that MLL-AF9 transduction in wild-type c-Kit+ cells resulted in the upregulation of RBM5 expression levels, suggesting its functional requirement in myeloid leukemia (Supplementary Fig. S1C, D). Next, we transplanted the transformed wild-type and Rbm5-deficient c-Kit+ cells into half-lethal, irradiated recipient mice for an in vivo study of leukemogenesis. As a result, the host mice transplanted with Rbm5-deficient c-Kit+ cells showed a significantly delayed progression of leukemia (average latency, 72 versus 39 days) and a reduced disease burden in spleen compared to controls (Fig. 1C, D). Moreover, the second-round in vivo transplantation of leukemia cells collected from primary sick mice resulted in a progressive delay in leukemogenesis (average latency, 65 versus 36 days) in Rbm5-deficient cells compared to controls (Fig. 1E). Importantly, the Rbm5-deficient leukemia cells from the primary transplanted animals also exhibited a more mature morphology and a significant reduction in plating efficiency for colony formation and cell growth (Fig. 1F, G; Supplementary Fig. S1E, F). Correspondingly, Rbm5 loss led to a considerable increase in the frequency and median fluorescence intensity (MFI) of myeloid markers (CD11b and Gr1), and a substantial increase in apoptosis (Fig. 1H, I; Supplementary Fig. S1G). More strikingly, we validated the role of Rbm5 in the self-renewal of LSCs and found that the leukemic granulocyte-monocyte progenitor (L-GMP) cell population (defined as GFP+Lin-Sca1- IL-7R-c-Kit+CD34+CD16/32+) was significantly lower in the Rbm5 KO MLL-AF9 leukemia group than in control WT mice (Fig. 1J). Moreover, the in vitro limiting dilution assay (LDA) showed that the loss of Rbm5 remarkably impaired the stemness of AML cells (Fig. 1K). These effects were specific to Rbm5 loss, as ectopic expression of Rbm5 cDNA fully rescued colony formation in Rbm5-deficient primary leukemia cells (Fig. 1L).To further explore the detailed role of Rbm5 in leukemia maintenance, we utilized the CRISPR/Cas9 approach with two individual Rbm5-specific guide RNAs (Rbm5-sg1 and Rbm5-sg2) to disrupt Rbm5 in primary wild-type MLL-AF9-driven mouse leukemia cells expressing Cas9 protein, followed by functional validations such as competitive proliferation assay (CPA) (Supplementary Fig. S1H). Notably, Rbm5 depletion significantly suppressed the growth of mouse MLL-AF9 leukemia cells, as demonstrated by a notable reduction in GFP+ cells compared to the control both in vitro and in vivo (Supplementary Fig. S1I–K). To rule out potential off-target effects of CRISPR/Cas9, ectopic expression of the Rbm5-sg1-resistant Rbm5 cDNA fully rescued the growth defect in Rbm5-depleted cells (Supplementary Fig. S1L, M). Together, these results indicate that Rbm5 promotes leukemogenesis and is critical for the maintenance of mouse primary leukemia-initiating/stem cells in AML.

Rbm5 is dispensable for normal hematopoiesis

As a suitable AML therapeutic target, the impact of gene function is expected to be selective in hematopoietic malignancy. To this end, we comprehensively analyzed the hematopoietic compartment in the bone marrow and peripheral blood from different genotype groups of littermates by crossing the Rbm5fl/fl strain with the hematopoietic Vav1-Cre strain (Fig. 2A). In general, Rbm5 loss did not affect the expected Mendelian Ratio (Fig. 2B). Immunoblotting successfully confirmed the tissue-specific expression and deletion of Rbm5 in hematopoietic organs, such as the bone marrow and spleen, while remaining unchanged in other tissues and during most stages of organ development (Fig. 2C; Supplementary Fig. S2A, B). We next assessed different hematopoietic cell populations in detail from the bone marrow of both the wild-type and Rbm5 knockout littermates. In Rbm5 hematopoietic-deficient mice, there was no significant difference in the frequency and absolute number of phenotypic HSCs and different hematopoietic progenitors (Fig. 2D–G). The frequency and absolute number of myeloid, B cells, T cells, and early erythroid progenitors were also comparable between the wild-type and Rbm5 knockout mice in both bone marrow and peripheral blood (Fig. 2H; Supplementary Fig. S2C, D). Additionally, the blood cell count, including platelets, red blood cells, and lymphocytes, was normal in Rbm5-deficient mice (Supplementary Fig. S2E). To identify the in vitro functional output of hematopoietic stem progenitor cells (HSPCs), we performed colony assays and observed comparable numbers of myeloid progenitor colonies between wild-type and Rbm5 null c-Kit+ cells (Fig. 2I). To further evaluate whether Rbm5 depletion affects the repopulating capacity of HSPCs, we performed in vivo competitive repopulation assays. Total BM cells from the wild-type and Rbm5 knockout mice (CD45.2+) were mixed with competitor BM cells (CD45.1+) and transplanted into donor WT mice (CD45.1+). These chimeric mice were then monitored for 4 months. We found that the percentage of donor-derived total cells (CD45.2+) in PB was comparable between the two recipient groups (Fig. 2J). These data suggest that Rbm5 is dispensable for normal mouse hematopoiesis. Nevertheless, elucidating the role of RBM5 in stress hematopoiesis is essential for a comprehensive understanding of its physiological function, which remains to be investigated.

Rbm5 specifically sustains the activity of LSCs but not the normal HSCs

The functional assay indicates that Rbm5 sustains the maintenance of mouse primary leukemia stem/initiation cells but is dispensable for normal hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs). We sought to explore the underlying molecular mechanism to better understand its role. We hypothesized that RBM5 might control the MLL-fusion induced self-renewal gene signature in LSCs. To identify the gene expression profile in this heterogeneous cell population, we performed high-resolution single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) in wild-type (WT) and Rbm5 knockout (KO) MLL-AF9 primary leukemia cells from the same endpoint after primary transplantation, together with corresponding normal c-Kit+ HSPCs (Fig. 3A). We collected scRNA-seq data from a total of 51,517 cells, with more than 2000 genes per cell in each of the four groups (Fig. 3A). Next, the unbiased clustering analysis identified 15 cell populations with distinct gene expression profiles, including the specific CD34+ LSCs or HSPCs populations (Fig. 3B; Supplementary Fig. S3A). Notably, a decrease in LSCs was observed in the Rbm5 KO MLL-AF9 leukemia group compared with the control, accompanied by an increase in mature myeloid cell populations (Fig. 3C, D; and Supplementary Fig. S3B). In contrast, the frequency of normal HSPCs and mature cell populations was quite comparable between wild-type and Rbm5 KO c-Kit+ cells (Fig. 3D). This observation was further supported by the downregulation of stemness marker genes, such as Cd34, Hoxa9, Flt3, Myc, and Ikzf2, in the Rbm5 KO MLL-AF9 leukemia group compared to the Rbm5 KO c-Kit+ cells (Fig. 3E; Supplementary Fig. S3C). We next identified the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the Rbm5 KO LSCs, including 157 downregulated and 589 upregulated genes (Fig. 3F; Supplementary Table 2). In contrast, marginal genes showed a significant change in Rbm5 KO c-Kit+ cells relative to control (Supplementary Fig. S3D; Supplementary Table 2). Moreover, the enrichR analysis identified known oncogenic TFs, such as AF4 and ENL, among the downregulated genes (Supplementary Fig. S3E; Supplementary Table 3).

We extended our exploration to determine if RBM5 also sustains the maintenance of human primary leukemia stem cells. Indeed, RBM5 expression was higher in functional LSCs than in non-LSCs, as shown in a publicly available dataset (GSE76008) [36] (Fig. 3G). In line with its enrichment in LSCs, high RBM5 expression is associated with poor survival in pediatric AML patients (Fig. 3H). We further validated that suppression of RBM5 significantly reduced the frequency of CD34+CD38- LSCs in four individual AML patients, without affecting the frequency of CD34+ cells in two normal donors (Fig. 3I, J, Supplementary Fig. S3F). More importantly, suppression of RBM5 also reduced the frequency of CD34+CD38- LSCs and colony formation in patient-derived xenografts (PDXs) derived from primary cells with a KMT2A-r mutation (Supplementary Fig. S3G, H). Therefore, these data suggest a specific requirement for Rbm5 in sustaining the self-renewal program of LSCs but not in normal HSCs.

Rbm5 loss leads to the suppression of LSC self-renewal genes and activation of myeloid differentiation genes

Although scRNA-seq indicates LSC defects in Rbm5 knockout (KO) MLL-AF9, the number of gene transcripts is relatively lower due to technology limitations, as captured by 3’ UTR reads (10× genomics [37]). To gain genome-wide insights into how Rbm5 regulates the MLL-r induced self-renewal gene program, we performed a genome-wide transcriptome analysis by high-resolution bulk RNA sequencing in wild-type (WT) and Rbm5 knockout (KO) MLL-AF9 primary leukemia cells derived from bone marrow samples (Fig. 4A). As expected, the exons of Rbm5 flanked by two loxP cassettes, were efficiently deleted, as supported by their corresponding RNA-sequencing reads (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to compare the whole transcriptome across 14,043 transcripts. Consistently, the WT and KO groups, with three biological replicates, were separated by genotype, suggesting that loss of Rbm5 could result in a notable transcriptome change (Supplementary Fig. S4B). We then performed differential gene expression analysis between Rbm5 knockout (KO) and control MLL-AF9 leukemia cells. Using a stringent cutoff (FDR < 0.05 and Log2(fold change) >1), 335 increased genes and 302 decreased genes were identified in Rbm5 KO cells relative to the control (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table 4). Next, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) in Rbm5 KO groups compared to the negative control. As a result, the leukemia stem cell signature genes and Hoxa9 and Meis1 targets were highly enriched in the downregulated genes in Rbm5 KO leukemia cells. In contrast, the myeloid differentiation pathways, such as myeloid cell development and the myeloid CEBPA network, were enriched among the upregulated genes (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Table 5). Consistently, the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genome (KEGG) analysis revealed that Wnt and Rap1 signaling were enriched in the downregulated genes, while cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction and osteoclast differentiation were enriched in upregulated genes in Rbm5 KO cells (Fig. 4C; Supplementary Fig. S4C; Supplementary Table 5). Those downregulated genes included many well-known LSC self-renewal genes, and upregulated genes in myeloid differentiation were observed (Fig. 4D). Importantly, we confirmed that the expression levels of LSC signature genes, including Hoxa9, Flt3, Ikzf2, and Cd34, were significantly reduced. In contrast, the myeloid differentiation genes, including S100a8, S100a9, Fpr2, and C3, were significantly upregulated in bone marrow-derived Rbm5 KO primary leukemia cells (Fig. 4E, F; Supplementary Fig. S4D). The expression levels of Hoxa9, Flt3, Ikzf2, and Cd34 were consistently suppressed in spleen-derived Rbm5 KO primary leukemia cells (Supplementary Fig. S4E, F). Thus, these results suggest that loss of Rbm5 not only suppresses the transcriptional activity of self-renewal genes but also activates the myeloid differentiation genes in myeloid leukemia.

RBM5 controls the MYC transcriptional activity via physical interaction, maintenance of its protein, and proper genomic occupancy

Given the significant enrichment of MYC-related downstream targets from the GSEA analysis (Supplementary Table 5) and the well-established role of MYC in leukemogenesis and the maintenance of LSCs [38–42], we proposed that MYC might serve as a novel and major direct target of Rbm5 in leukemia cells. Indeed, several gene signatures relevant to the hallmark of MYC direct targets were significantly enriched among the downregulated genes in Rbm5 knockout cells (Fig. 5A, B). We then sought to validate the expression levels of Rbm5 and Myc in MLL-AF9 primary leukemia cells. Although the endogenous Myc mRNA was mildly changed, the Myc protein was robustly suppressed in Rbm5 knockout MLL-AF9 leukemia cells from both bone marrow and spleen (Fig. 5C, D). To further distinguish the direct regulation of the RBM5-MYC axis from the stress feedback loop, we used the auxin-induced degron (AID) system to acutely target RBM5. We hypothesized that direct regulation of MYC gene transcription would manifest acutely, while regulation of MYC protein levels would not depend on transcriptional activity. To test this, we used previously generated KMT2A-r leukemia SEM cells with bi-allelic endogenous RBM5 N-terminus AID knock-in and stable expression of OsTIR1(F74G). These cells were transduced with lentiviral vectors either constitutively expressing MYC-HaloTag or without, followed by detection of both endogenous and exogenous MYC levels within 24 h of induced RBM5 degradation (Fig. 5E). Notably, after 24 h of treatment with the small molecule 5-Ph-IAA, immunoblotting analysis showed a robust decrease in both endogenous MYC protein and exogenous MYC-HaloTag fusion protein, concurrent with the reduction of the AID-RBM5 fusion protein (Fig. 5F, G). In contrast, MYC mRNA levels remained unchanged within 24 h of 5-Ph-IAA treatment (Fig. 5F, G). Consistently, CRISPR-mediated RBM5 knockout in multiple human leukemia cell lines resulted in a significant decrease in both endogenous MYC mRNA and protein levels, with a more pronounced effect on the protein level (Fig. 5H; Supplementary Fig. S5A). To further confirm whether this effect is specific to leukemia, we examined Myc levels in both wild-type (WT) and Rbm5 knockout (KO) normal c-Kit+ HSPCs. Notably, both mRNA and protein levels of Myc expression remained unchanged in Rbm5 KO c-Kit+ cells compared to wild-type controls (Supplementary Fig. S5B). Collectively, our data suggest that RBM5 directly controls MYC protein levels as a critical mechanism, in addition to the previously reported RBM5-HOXA9 regulatory axis [27].

Given that MYC itself and its direct targets were inhibited after RBM5 loss, we hypothesized whether RBM5 could serve as a transcription cofactor for MYC in the leukemia context. To comprehensively integrate the RBM5-related protein network, we first performed immunoprecipitation using tag-fused exogenous RBM5 in the human leukemia cell line OCIAML2, followed by mass spectrometry analysis. Notably, we identified 658 RBM5-interacting proteins relative to an IgG control (Supplementary Fig. S5C; Supplementary Table 6). Consistent with its canonical role in RNA splicing, the majority of proteins identified in the interactome were enriched for RNA splicing function, as determined by protein network and GO enrichment analysis (Supplementary Fig. S5D). Notably, several key transcription factors were identified, including MYC and its known cofactor WDR5 (Supplementary Fig. S5C). The interaction between RBM5 and MYC was further verified in both human leukemia cells and HEK293T cells (Fig. 5I, J; Supplementary Fig. S5E). Such interaction was robustly and specifically disrupted after acute RBM5 degradation in previously generated KMT2A-r SEM leukemia cells (Fig. 5K; Supplementary Fig. S5F). We next asked whether loss of RBM5 alters MYC genome-wide occupancy, either globally or gene-selectively. Because ChIP for endogenous MYC is technically challenging, we instead performed MYC-HaloTag ChIP-seq in RBM5-AID SEM cells that stably express MYC-HaloTag fusion proteins, with or without 5-Ph-IAA treatment for 24 h. Principal-component analysis (PCA) of the resulting ChIP-seq profiles confirmed that untreated and 5-Ph-IAA treatment groups were separated from each other (Supplementary Fig. S6A), indicating that MYC-HaloTag occupancy is dynamically remodeled upon RBM5 loss. Notably, differential-binding analysis revealed 6,149 peaks with significantly reduced MYC-HaloTag signal after RBM5 depletion (Fig. 5L). Specifically, the MYC promoter itself, together with established MYC target loci such as NOP2 and NPM1, exhibited markedly diminished MYC-HaloTag binding occupancy, whereas a subset of other known targets remained unaffected (Fig. 5M; Supplementary Fig. S6B, C). Collectively, these data indicate that RBM5 is essential for stable MYC recruitment to a specific subset of genomic loci, including the MYC locus itself. Thus, our data suggest a particular requirement for RBM5 in MYC regulation in LSCs.

Myc overexpression can fully rescue the defects in Rbm5-deficient LSCs

Given the reduced Myc expression in primary transplanted RBM5-deficient leukemia cells, we hypothesized that Rbm5 may control LSCs through Myc. To test this hypothesis, we overexpressed Myc and the control vector in Rbm5-null primary leukemia cells and validated whether their reintroduction could restore the defects using the colony formation assay and the in vivo transplantation assay (Fig. 6A). Notably, we observed that Myc overexpression resulted in a robust increase in colony numbers and total derived cell numbers compared to the empty vectors (Fig. 6B–D). Importantly, Myc overexpression also restored stemness in Rbm5-deficient LSCs, as determined by in vitro limiting dilution assays (LDAs) (Fig. 6E). Inspired by these observations, Rbm5-depleted MLL-AF9 cells with or without Myc overexpression were transplanted into syngeneic host mice. Subsequently, we observed a significant rescue of the leukemic phenotype, as evidenced by reduced overall survival and increased leukemia burden in peripheral blood (Fig. 6F–H). More importantly, Myc overexpression also restored the leukemic granulocyte-monocyte progenitor (L-GMP) cell population from bone marrow in the Rbm5 KO MLL-AF9 leukemia group (Fig. 6I). Thus, our data suggest that Myc could fully restore the defects of Rbm5-deficient LSCs in vitro and in vivo.

Discussion

Here, using genetic mouse models, we showed that Rbm5 is indispensable for maintaining the LSC program both in vivo and in vitro, but not the normal steady-state hematopoiesis. Mechanistically, we found that Rbm5 controls the transcriptional activity of several key LSC signature genes, including the novel target Myc. Thus, our study demonstrates a differential requirement for Rbm5 in maintaining LSCs in myeloid leukemia, but not in HSCs during normal hematopoiesis, providing a potential therapeutic target for selectively targeting LSCs in AML.

Our study establishes the RBM5–MYC regulatory axis as a novel pathway in myeloid leukemia (Fig. 6J). Moreover, RBM5 not only physically interacted with MYC but also substantially affected its protein steady-state level relative to the transcriptional level. This could be explained by several possible mechanisms. First, the interaction between RBM5 and MYC may antagonize MYC’s interaction with known E3 ubiquitin ligases or co-repressors, thereby protecting it from degradation. Our RBM5 interactome dataset identifies MYC and its known co-activator WDR5, but lacks the well-known E3 ligase FBXW7, which targets the MYC protein [43, 44]. Notably, WDR5 is a known interactor of MYC that could facilitate target recognition and promote tumorigenesis by MYC [45, 46]. Therefore, RBM5 may exert a similar effect by recruiting WDR5, a hypothesis that requires further validation. Second, MYC is a well-established master regulator of ribosome biogenesis and protein synthesis [47]. The reduction in MYC protein level may also result from lower translational efficiency following RBM5 loss. Nonetheless, further studies were required to fully understand the regulatory mechanism underlying the interaction between RBM5 and MYC.

The function of RBM5 in normal hematopoiesis is poorly characterized, although it has been primarily studied in spermatogenesis and neuronal disease [48, 49]. We successfully generated a hematopoietic Rbm5 conditional knockout mouse model and provided a comprehensive analysis of different hematopoietic compartments, suggesting that loss of Rbm5 does not affect normal hematopoiesis in the steady state. This finding was further supported by single-cell RNA-seq analysis, which revealed normal HSC frequency and marginal transcriptome changes in Rbm5 knockout c-Kit+ cells. However, we also noticed that the Rbm5 heterozygous mice exhibited significantly higher blood cell counts than wild‑type controls at 17 weeks. This effect may reflect compensatory feedback triggered by the loss of one Rbm5 allele, such as upregulation of alternative genes, though this phenomenon and the underlying mechanism remain to be investigated. The differential requirement of Rbm5 in LSC and HSC is highly correlated with their relative expression levels, as we found that Rbm5 is expressed at much higher levels in MLL-AF9-transformed cells. Nevertheless, we could not completely rule out the potential role of Rbm5 in stress hematopoiesis (e.g., regeneration after myelosuppression, inflammation, or aging), which requires further characterization in other in vivo mouse models.

In our study, Rbm5 is highly expressed in LSCs and plays a critical role in their maintenance. Interestingly, RBM5-AS1 activity, encoded by the RBM5 locus, is critical for the functional activation of colon cancer stem-like cells [50]. Moreover, the RBM5 gene was among the 17 common metastasis signature genes identified across multiple solid tumor types [51]. These results, together with our findings, suggest that RBM5 may be a key player in maintaining the stemness of various cancer stem cell populations. Further studies profiling RBM5’s direct targets in LSC would help distinguish direct from indirect targets in myeloid leukemia. More intriguingly, we observed a significant upregulation of immune-responsive genes in Rbm5-deficient leukemia cells (Supplementary Table 5). The phenomenon likely depends on MYC transcriptional activity, as MYC has been shown to promote immune suppression in both solid tumors and blood cancers [52, 53]. Additionally, a recent study reported that leukemia with mutations in RNA splicing factors, such as SF3B1, can induce mis-splicing-derived neoantigens [54]. Nevertheless, it remains unknown yet how exactly the intrinsic RBM5-MYC oncogenic signaling activates extrinsic crosstalk with tumor-infiltrating immune cells, including CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and tumor-associated macrophages. This observation suggests that Rbm5 loss may synergize with immune therapies, such as PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, a possibility warranting further investigation.

In summary, we found that RBM5 sustains the LSCs via the novel target MYC. The identification of dysregulated RBPs in hematological malignancies has led to the rapid development of therapeutic strategies targeting specific RBPs. Overall, we propose that targeting RBM5 for the eradication of LSCs could be a promising new therapeutic target in leukemia.
