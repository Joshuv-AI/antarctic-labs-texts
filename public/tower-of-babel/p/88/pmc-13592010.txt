Factors associated with post spinal cord injury employment: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Bina Gyawali, Douglas Gross, Rija Kamran, Adalberto Loyola-Sanchez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo identify factors associated with post spinal cord injury (SCI) employment, exploring differences between high-income (HIC) and low/middle-income (LMIC) countries.MethodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis included, Embase, CINAHL, PsycINFO, and Scopus. Articles discussing post-SCI employment were included and excluded if data were not disaggregated by “employed/unemployed” or did not include SCI-only sub-groups. Factors reported in three articles were considered for meta-analyses, using a random effects model to estimate odds ratios (OR) and mean differences (MD).ResultsFifty-two articles were included. Forty-six studies were from HIC and six from LMIC. Twelve factors were significantly associated with post-SCI employment: Paraplegia (OR: 1.39), living alone (OR: 2.59), driving (OR: 4.76), no wheelchair use (OR: 0.44), (5), less activity limitations (MD: 0.67), being married (OR: 1.49), (7) being white (OR: 2.16), being younger at the time of study (MD: −0.24) and at the time of injury (MD: −0.30), time since injury (MD: 0.31), higher education (OR: 2.20), and income (OR: 0.15). Subgroup analyses showed that three of these factors were significantly associated with post-SCI employment only in HIC.ConclusionsAll factors significantly associated with post-SCI employment were identified in HIC studies; there is a paucity of evidence on factors in LMIC.

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a complex condition resulting in motor, sensory, and autonomic dysfunction leading to devastating impacts on the individual, both socially and economically. The incidence rate of SCI in HIC and LMIC has been reported from 13.1 to 163.4 per million and 13.0 to 220.0 per million people, respectively, while the prevalence rates vary from 490 to 526 per million and 440.0 per million people, respectively (1). A systematic review by Kumar et al. found that globally, the average age of people with traumatic SCI is 39.8 (SD ± 12.2), and the most common mechanism of injury is road traffic accidents, followed by falls (2). These people typically fall within the working age group (15–64 years, inclusive) as defined by global demographics (3). Participation in productive work is considered one of the major domains affected by SCI.

According to the International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health (ICF) framework, participation in any area of life, such as occupation, is the interaction between environmental and personal factors in individuals with impairments and activity limitations. Therefore, it is important to consider these factors while exploring the phenomenon of post-SCI employment. The ICF is the World Health Organization’s (WHO) framework to measure health and disability at individual and population levels (4). The components of ICF are body structure and function, activity, participation, and environmental and personal factors (5). The activity and participation components deal with the concepts of capacity and performance of a person with a certain health condition (5). SCI leads to the impairment of motor and sensory function, which causes mobility and hand activity limitations, giving rise to participation restrictions by affecting activities of daily life, which, in turn, affects major areas of life, including employment.

Individuals with physical disabilities like SCI experience challenges in obtaining and maintaining employment (6). Post-SCI employment can be of two types: (1) continuing the same job as before injury and (2) starting a new job. Employment rates of people with SCI vary widely across countries and depend on geographical location, opportunity, and expectation. Outcomes also vary due to discrepancies in the definition of employment, type of sample, and the time of measurement (i.e. time since injury). A Swiss community-based observational study reported that 45.7% of people experiencing SCI returned to pre-injury employment, 32.9% changed employers, and 21.4% never returned to paid employment after SCI, with an overall paid employment rate of 78.6% (7). In contrast, the paid employment rate after SCI has been reported as 35% in the USA (8), 49.9% in Australia (9), and 82% in South India (10). A cross-sectional survey in 22 countries across the world reported a 38% paid employment rate after SCI, with a wide variation across countries, ranging from 10.3% to 61.4% (11). Sturm et al. reported that out of 623 persons unemployed after SCI, 319 (51.2%) wished to work, among whom 220 (35.3%) subjectively perceived they were able to work (12). The mean duration of time to employment after SCI was reported as 4.9 years (13). Post SCI employment is positively associated with better quality of life as well as higher psychological, financial, and social wellbeing in people with SCI (14). The benefits of employment after SCI extend beyond financial compensation, providing a sense of identity, social connections, a reason to start a day, (15) and higher life expectancy (16).

The overall post-SCI employment rate is lower than the employment rate of the general population. Therefore, it is important to find better ways to support employment because this is not only associated with improved finances, but also with improving physical, emotional, and psychosocial wellbeing after a SCI (14, 15). Getting back to work and sustaining employment is undeniably challenging for a person with SCI because of the physical, emotional, and psychological body functions, activities, and environmental factors required to be successful in any remunerative employment.

Previous systematic and scoping reviews have consolidated different barriers and facilitators related to return to work following SCI (17–20). However, the strength of the association between these barriers and facilitators and the post-SCI employment outcome has not been clearly defined (21). A systematic review categorized the employment definition into three themes: (a) salary dependent (i.e. working for pay, gainfully employed, competitive employment, or earning minimum wage), (b) employed vs. unemployed (i.e., volunteer, retired, and homemaker were combined with the “employed” category in some studies, while others have categorized students, homemakers, and volunteers as “unemployed”), and (c) hours-dependent work (e.g. working at least 12 h per week) (22).

The diversity in the definition of post-SCI employment in the available literature makes it challenging to interpret rates of employment and factors associated with them. Bloom et al. reported that most of the literature on return to work after SCI comes from North America (59%), followed by Europe (22%), Asia (10%), Australia (10%), and Africa (1%), with no reports from South America (22). In addition, most of the literature in these regions comes from High-Income Countries (HIC), as defined by the “World Bank Country Classification by Income 2021–2022” (23). The paucity of evidence on post-SCI employment in Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) makes it difficult to understand which factors are most likely to influence this outcome in countries with low per capita income. Consequently, it is important to identify factors related to post-SCI employment considering the socio-economic status of the countries where research was conducted. There is evidence supporting that higher individual socioeconomic status is positively associated with success in post-SCI employment; however, it is unknown whether this situation applies to LMIC (24).

Consequently, there is a need to better understand the strength of associations between body structures and functions, activities, and environmental factors with the outcome of post-SCI employment. In addition, it is important to consider how these factors have a different impact depending on countries’ socioeconomic status (i.e. HIC vs. LMIC). Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search of MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, PsycINFO, and Scopus databases was conducted and included all the relevant articles from inception to June 23, 2024. To ensure all expectations of comprehensiveness were addressed, a health sciences research librarian at the University of Alberta was consulted to define the search strategy. Search terms included the two key concepts of the review: Employment and Persons with spinal cord injury. The full search strategy implemented is available as supplementary material (Appendix A). All retrieved articles were imported to COVIDENCE, a web-based software platform that streamlines the production of systematic reviews.

Study selection process

Two independent reviewers conducted the title and abstract screening and full-text review. Inter-rater reliability (IRR) was estimated using Cohen's Kappa test for two raters (once for the abstract screening and once for full text selection). Cohen’s Kappa was 0.66 for the title and abstract screening stage, indicating substantial agreement, while it was 0.98 for the full text review stage, indicating almost perfect agreement (26). All disagreements between the two reviewers were resolved by discussion with a third reviewer until a perfect agreement was achieved.

Data collection process

A standardized data extraction spreadsheet was developed by the author upon considering feedback from all review team members. Data extracted from included articles were (1) first author name, year of publication, and country of research conducted, (2) sample size, (3) research design, (4) data collection method, (5) definition of employment, including the total of participants employed and unemployed, and (6) factors studied. The author filled the spreadsheet after going through the full texts of the included articles. The second reviewer checked the correctness of the data filled in the spreadsheet upon reading the full texts of the included articles and left a comment wherever there was disagreement. Any discrepancies in data extraction were resolved first by consensus between the two reviewers, and if required, through discussion with the third reviewer.

Quality assessment

We assessed the quality of evidence behind each factor identified using a modification of the GRADE methodology (27, 28). We only considered the assessment of “risk of bias” and “inconsistency” because the concepts of “indirectness” and “imprecision” could not be applied consistently due to variations in studies’ methodological designs. As for publication bias, we assumed this existed because we only used studies published in the English language, so this factor couldn’t be utilized as a criterion to differentiate quality among the meta-analyses conducted.

Risk of bias was examined using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) and its extension for cross-sectional studies (29). The NOS appraises studies in three different domains: selection, comparability, and outcome. It contains 8 items within the 3 domains, and the total maximum score is 9. A study with a score from 7–9 is considered low risk, 4–6 is high risk, and 0–3 is very high risk of bias.

To identify the overall risk of bias of a factor, we categorized it into three different groups: low risk, moderate risk, and high risk. For each of the factors, if all (100%) of the articles included had low risk of bias, it was considered low risk; if 50% or more of the articles included had low risk of bias, it was considered moderate risk; and if less than 50% of the articles included had low risk of bias, it was considered high risk.

For inconsistency, the criteria utilized were: if the test of heterogeneity (I2) was less than or equal to 30%, then no inconsistency was assumed; if I2 was between 31% and 60%, we judged moderate inconsistency; if I2 between 61% and 75%, we judged as substantial inconsistency; and if I2 was between 76% and 100%, then inconsistency was judged as considerable. For the overall quality of evidence for each factor, I2 up to 60% was not penalized, and I2 between 61% and 100% was penalized; however, if the heterogeneity was explained by subgroup analysis (i.e. if I2 dropped during subgroup analysis), it was considered as no inconsistency and was not penalized. For example, for a particular factor, if the risk of bias was low; however, the inconsistency was between 61% and 100%, and the I2 did not drop while doing subgroup analysis, then the overall quality of evidence was decreased to the level immediately below its initial level of quality (i.e. dropping down from high quality to moderate quality or from moderate quality to low quality).

Statistical analysis

Meta-analyses were conducted using statistical software (i.e. Stata BE 17). Only factors that were reported on three or more articles qualified for meta-analysis. A random-effects model was used. Categorical and continuous data were analyzed, estimating log odds ratios and mean differences (Hedge’s g), respectively. 95% CI and p values were estimated considering a p < .05 as statistically significant. The I2 statistic was used to evaluate heterogeneity among the included articles.

Post-SCI employment factors categorization

The identified factors associated with post-SCI employment were classified according to the ICF domains. Subgroup analysis of factors were analyzed on the basis of the economic status of the countries where research was conducted, when possible. Countries were categorized as HIC and LMIC according to the World Bank classification (23).

Results

A total of 7,341 articles were identified from five different databases, 3,834 included after removing duplicates (refer to Fig. 1). A full text review of 130 articles was done after abstract screening. After screening full texts, 52 studies were eligible and included in the systematic review (refer to Table 1). Of these, 46 were from HIC, 5 from upper middle-income countries, 1 from lower middle-income countries, and none were from low-income countries. Among the articles included, 37 were cross-sectional studies, 14 were longitudinal studies, and 1 was a pilot study. Nineteen factors qualified for the meta-analysis. The following is the description of results organized by the factors explored during the meta-analyses.

Functional independence measure (FIM)

Five studies (n = 1,976) identified an association between FIM and post-SCI employment after SCI (refer to Table SOF). Our meta-analysis showed a significant association, meaning that the average FIM score of people employed was higher than that of those unemployed (Hedges’s g = 0.67) [refer to Fig. 2], with a medium quality of evidence, meaning that further research could change the conclusion. No subgroup analysis was possible, as there were no studies from LMIC. Annual income

Three studies (n = 1,491) identified an association between annual income and post-SCI employment (refer to table SOF). Our meta-analysis showed a significant association, meaning that there are higher chances of being employed if a person’s annual income is ≥$20,000 compared to <$20,000 (OR = 0.15) [refer to Fig. 7], with low quality of evidence, meaning further research could change the conclusion. Discussion

This systematic review identified that personal, impairment, and activity limitation factors are emphasized over environmental factors in the available literature on employment after SCI. There is a lack of high-quality prospective studies, with cross-sectional studies dominating the literature in this area. There is a paucity of evidence on factors associated with post-SCI employment in LMIC.

Mapping of identified factors according to the WHO ICF framework

All the components of the ICF model (i.e. body structure and function, activity, participation, personal, and environmental factors) were represented in the factors reported as associated with employment after SCI in the literature (refer to Table 4); however, none of the environmental factors qualified to be included in the meta-analysis as they were not explored in three or more articles. In addition, the majority of factors included in the meta-analysis were personal factors (refer to Table 5). This suggests that the main focus of the research in this area is on personal, impairment, and activity limitation factors, lacking scientific evidence on the effect of the environment on post-SCI employment. The increased focus on personal factors explored in the revised studies suggests that successful employment after SCI is mainly attributed to the individual characteristics of a person and not so much to the employment environmental demands. Considering that being successfully employed is the result of the capacity of an individual, including personal factors and health conditions, to effectively address the environmental work demands, our review demonstrates that the literature mostly focuses only on one aspect of the post SCI employment process. This is important to recognize as environmental factors are more susceptible to be modified compared to personal factors when considering interventions to increase the rate of employment after SCI. Therefore, future research should equally prioritize environmental factors to understand and overcome low rates of employment after SCI.

Vocational training was not a significant factor in the meta-analysis, and this is supported by high-quality evidence. This finding contradicts the existing literature, and this could be explained by the variety of components that vocational rehabilitation programs can have. In addition, it is important to mention that the three studies included in this review, which included vocational rehabilitation as a factor for post-SCI employment, were transversal. Therefore, the association between vocational rehabilitation and employment after SCI should be explored in a prospective longitudinal intervention study. As vocational rehabilitation is a complex intervention with so many variations, our results suggest we need to conduct further research to better understand which are the components of vocational training are more effective and how they interact with each other to promote successful employment after SCI.

Comparison of factors between HIC and LMIC

Out of fifty-two articles included in the study, only six articles were from LMIC, and forty-six articles were from HIC. This reveals a need to increase high-quality scientific production from LMIC on post-SCI employment. All nineteen factors that qualified for meta-analysis were explored in HIC, and six factors were not explored in LMIC (i.e. vocational training, pre-injury work intensity, time since injury, annual income, FIM, and ethnicity). Occupations that demand the use of analytical and interpersonal skills, the skills that cannot be substituted away by technology/machinery (e.g. researchers, artists, managers, etc.), are more common in HIC. This suggests that occupations that involve more physical intensity (e.g., heavy machine operators, manufacturing and agricultural laborers, etc.) are more common in LMIC (30). Pre-injury work intensity has not been explored in LMIC when it is of particular importance to know how work’s physical demands are related to employment after SCI in LMIC. Therefore, there is a dire need to explore pre-injury work intensity in LMIC. Moreover, the lack of association between education level and post-SCI employment in LMIC could mean that in these countries, return to work is dictated by necessity, as there are no stable government-based structures to provide income support to people with disabilities, which forces people to return to work in any fashion possible, regardless of their level of education.

Only six factors qualified for subgroup analysis because of the lesser number of articles included from LMIC. In a subgroup analysis of six factors, four were significant (i.e. level of injury, ability to live alone, education, and relationship) and two (i.e. type of injury and sex) were not significant overall. All four factors that were significant overall were significant in HIC; however, none were significant in LMIC. This result might have been influenced by a small sample size and the lesser number of articles included from LMIC compared to HIC.

Comparison of post-SCI employment rate between HIC and LMIC

Literature from India and Malaysia showed employment rates after SCI as 82% and 76.2%, respectively (10,31); whereas employment rates after SCI were 49.9% in Australia, 26.5% in Finland, 29.8% in the USA, and 32% in Canada (9,32,33). The post-SCI employment rate appears higher in LMIC as compared to HIC. This is likely because most LMICs do not provide disability benefits, and if the person is the primary income generator of the family, they are left with no option other than to work in some capacity.

Limitations

This review included literature only available in the English language. If any relevant articles were found in a non-English language, we tried to locate their English version; however, this was not always successful. Thus, some of the factors could have been systematically excluded, especially from Asia, Africa, and South America. Having most included articles from HIC (i.e., North America, Australia, and Europe) could restrict the ecological validity of our findings to LMIC. Similarly, the following factors included in our meta-analyses: “annual income”, “driving”, “employment at the time of injury”, “medical co-morbidities”, “pre-injury physical intensity”, “vocational training”, and “wheelchair use” had less than 5 papers, and according to the literature (33), this decreases the reliability of the estimates observed. Consequently, our interpretations about these factors could change with further research. The Inter-Rater Reliability (IRR) of abstract screening, which was estimated by Cohen's Kappa test for two raters, was 0.66, meaning substantial agreement; however, all disagreements between two reviewers were solved by discussion with a third reviewer until a perfect agreement was achieved. For quality assessment, we modified the GRADE methodology to account for variations in the methodological designs of the included studies. We only considered “risk of bias” – assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale and “inconsistency” of the original GRADE approach. This approach has not been validated and could have limited our interpretations on the quality of the evidence assessed. The dichotomization of the variable “education”, utilizing high school education as the cutoff point, was necessary to create four categories to conduct the meta-analysis. Nevertheless, it is important to recognize that this dichotomization removed group variability, which could have hidden a significant relationship between education level and post SCI employment status. Finally, the decision not to analyze those factors that were not studied in at least three different papers limits our description of possibly important factors associated with post-SCI employment. Several other factors not included in this review have shown relevance with post-SCI employment, such as workplace adjustment, co-worker support, or emotional support, most of which are environmental factors, but there is not a sufficient amount of research to draw conclusions about these factors at this moment. Moreover, our review is based on different types of observational studies, which limits the ability to conclude on the causality of the factors explored over the outcome of post SCI employment.

Future research

Cross-sectional studies dominate the literature available to date on post-SCI employment, which makes it hard to understand the cause-and-effect relationship within this phenomenon. Therefore, high-quality longitudinal studies should be conducted. Most studies exploring employment after SCI were conducted in HIC, which impacts the generalizability of the findings, considering geographical, economical, and cultural aspects. More research exploring factors associated with post-SCI employment in LMIC is warranted. Future research should focus on different types of work people with SCI are returning to. All of this will inform the decisions of rehabilitation professionals, who can help guide the employment process after SCI in a timely manner. Moreover, future research should focus on which components of vocational rehabilitation are more effective and how they interact with one another to optimize the rate of employment after SCI. Finally, conducting further research on other less-explored factors related to employment success after SCI, especially environmental factors, should be a priority.

Conclusion

All factors identified as significantly associated with post-SCI employment were explored in HIC. There is a paucity of evidence on factors associated with employment after SCI in LMIC, so more research is needed in this area. Personal, impairment, and activity limitation factors appear to be emphasized over environmental factors in the available literature, and there is a lack of high-quality prospective studies in this area. Moreover, the modifiable significant factors associated with post-SCI employment identified in this work could be considered in future vocational rehabilitation interventions.
