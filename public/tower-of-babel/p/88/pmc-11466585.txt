A comparative study on corrosion and mechanical properties of laser-cladded X2CrNiMoN22-5-3 duplex stainless steel on 42CrMo4 steel substrate
Authors: Mohammad Reza Borhani, Fareed Kermani, Reza Shoja Razavi, Mohammad Erfanmanesh, Masoud Barekat
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuplex stainless steel is an alloy that combines the advantages of austenitic stainless steel and ferritic stainless steel. It has excellent corrosion resistance, high strength, and good weldability. One of the main problems in marine shafts using X2CrNiMoN22-5-3 Duplex Stainless Steel (2205) is bending and warping over time. In this study, 42CrMO4 (MO40) steel was clad with 2205 dual-phase steel using direct metal deposition with wire to simultaneously utilize the mechanical properties, and corrosion resistance. Perform metallographic imaging, corrosion test, and tensile test, to maintain the quality of the cladding. The results show that the corrosion potential of 2205 cladding layer is −0.65 (including semi-passive zone), while the control sample of 2205 had a corrosion potential of −0.26, and the MO40 substrate had a corrosion potential of −0.1 (highlighting the advantage of the cladding in thermodynamics term). EIS results showed that the REISMO40=2325Ω, REIS2205=24315Ω, and REIS2205cladded=216133Ω. The corrosion rate for the substrate, control sample, and laser-clad sample were 0.33, 0.15, and 0.00095 mm/year, respectively, without significant loss in tensile properties. The final strength for MO40(with 2205 clad) reaches 672 MPa with a 14 % decrease from 788 for MO40. In this case, we still have a 36 % increase in strength compared to the 2205 sample. It seems that the bent problem of the pure 2205 marine shaft can be solved completely in this case and continues to be solved in our ongoing work.Keywords: Laser cladding, X2CrNiMoN22-5-3 alloy, Marine shafts, Mechanical and corrosion properties

Introduction

X2CrNiMoN22-5-3 Duplex Stainless Steel (2205) is a duplex stainless steel (ferritic-austenitic) known for its corrosion resistance due to the abundance of chromium and molybdenum in its chemical composition. The two-phase microstructure of the steel provides high resistance to chloride stress corrosion cracking. Although there are problems such as warping and bending in shafts made of this metal, it is widely used in the marine sector due to its good properties[1,2].

Additive manufacturing systems, such as powder bed, powder feed, and wire feed systems, use a variety of techniques, such as directional solidification, repeated melting, and rapid solidification to create unique properties that are not typically found in conventional manufacturing processes. These systems are capable of producing complex and high-quality parts for various industries due to their special capabilities [3].

Given the growing market for rare metals such as MO40 and its increasing use in common applications, this material has become an important area of research [4]. In addition to its economic impact, cladding of MO40 steel also improves its corrosion behavior and other properties. By selecting appropriate coating materials and failure techniques, the properties of MO40 steel can be customized to meet specific needs.

The results of Liu et al. [5] study shows that the increase in laser power causes an increase in the width of the coating layer and a significant increase in the dilution and depth of the molten pool. The best corrosion is also achieved at 2.3 kW power, probably because the microstructure of the design of this power is different and better.

The study by Valiente Bermejo et al. [6] focused on the microstructure and the impact of shielding gas and heat treatment on duplex stainless steel produced using 2205 wire and direct laser deposition (DLD). The research uses a 1.2 mm diameter wire and a continuous yttrium laser with a maximum power of 6 kW. The study reported that the amount of oxygen in the single-pass sample and the 10-layer sample was within the range of 111 ppm–160 ppm, which is considered standard. It was noted that if the oxygen content exceeds 200 ppm, it can lead to a significant decrease in the toughness of duplex steel. Therefore, the samples produced by the DLD method are not prone to oxygen absorption under proper protection conditions. The study also found that when N2 protective gas is used, the austenite values are higher compared to when Ar gas is used. In particular, when nitrogen shielding is used, the austenite value is doubled, indicating that the shielding gas selection has a significant effect on the microstructure of duplex stainless steel produced by DLD.

The study by Wen et al. [7] focused on the cladding of 2205 steel using the direct laser deposition method and examined its microstructure, and corrosion resistance. The average tensile strength and ductility after failure of the plated samples were reported to be 956 MPa and 40 % ductility, respectively, while the substrate properties were indicated as 760 MPa and 43 %. Based on these results, it was noted that the failure of all samples occurred in a ductile state. It was also found that increasing the ferrite/austenite ratio increased the corrosion resistance of the coating by 70 %. This shows that the microstructural composition of the plated material significantly affects the corrosion resistance.

Behbahani et al. [8] compared the microstructure, corrosion, and passivation properties of 316L stainless steel produced using WAAM and wrought alloys. The WAAM alloy has an inhomogeneous microstructure with residual delta ferrite, while the wrought alloy has a more homogeneous microstructure with the second MnS phase. This study found that the pitting corrosion potential in the passivation film of the WAAM alloy is slightly higher, and the defect content and defect propagation are lower than that of the wrought alloy.

The study by Li et al. [9] information on the microstructure and corrosion resistance of S32101 duplex steel claddings. An interesting example is the coating process using ER2209 wire with an underwater wire feeder. The observation that the polarization curve of the normal cladding is in the lower region suggests that the normal cladding has a lower corrosion resistance compared to the remelted sample. It is also intriguing that the remelted sample with 3 kW power exhibits higher corrosion resistance compared to samples with 5 kW power, 1 kW power, and the cladded sample. This finding indicates that the remelting process, particularly at 3 kW power, significantly enhances the corrosion resistance of the cladding. Overall, this study demonstrates the importance of the remelting process and its effect on the corrosion resistance of duplex steel coating layers, providing important information for optimizing the coating process to achieve better corrosion resistance.

Bercelli et al. [10] studied the effect of metal and arc additive manufacturing (WAAM) on the machining of duplex stainless steel (DSS). WAAM DSS showed little difference between samples of a few millimeters in length. This microstructure is caused by the concentration of ferrite at the weld bead interface, there is an extended γ/β phase at the base of the weld bead, and there is austenite at the weld bead interface.

X2CrNiMoN22-5-3 duplex stainless steel (2205) combines the best properties of both ferritic and austenitic stainless steel. Its high chromium, molybdenum, and nickel content provides excellent resistance to both groove and pitting corrosion. Additionally, its dual-phase microstructure makes it highly resistant to chloride stress corrosion cracking. However, a practical issue with 2205 steel is that shafts made from it can bend in service. The project aims to solve this problem by cladding MO40 steel with duplex steel 2205 using direct metal deposition(with wire). This approach will prevent industrial shaft bending while taking advantage of 2205 steel's excellent corrosion resistance due to its high content of chromium, molybdenum, and nickel. The cladding steps will be optimized using a single-pass approach, and the overlap and final shaft cover will be investigated. To ensure the quality of the metallographic images, various tests will be performed, including open circuit potential (OCP), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), potentiodynamic polarization (PDY) in 3.5 % NaCl solution, salt spray test, and tension test. Using MO40 steel, which is very cheap and readily available, in combination with the relatively expensive 2205 cladding, will provide significant economic benefits for industries. While the cost of laser cladding may increase slightly for large shafts used in the marine industry, this increase is insignificant and will still be approved due to the increased expected properties.

Being smart about corrosion resistance, yield strength, and economic concerns, we clad 2205 steel onto the MO40 shaft. This combination prevents industrial shafts from warping(In the case that the shaft is completely 2205) while taking advantage of the excellent corrosion resistance of 2205 steel due to its high chromium, molybdenum, and nickel content. Additionally, combining the cheap MO40 substrate(shaft) with the expensive 2205 cladding provides significant benefits to the industry and makes it highly profitable.

Based on previous studies [17,18] in this field, 50 % overlap was chosen for the samples with a two-direction pattern during cladding. This is due to the increase in the overlap percentage when melting the substrate. However, due to performance limitations (maximum power is 1 kW), a higher percentage cannot be controlled. For cladding the samples, optimal parameters of power 800 W, speed 13 mm/s, and wire feeding rate 7 mm/s were used based on the previous test (ST-Clad(7)). The transformation of ferrite to austenite for a given alloy is controlled by the cooling rate.

Percentage of austenite and ferrite

The main problem in welding two-phase steels is to achieve an approximate balance of ferrite and austenite while preventing the formation of detrimental phases such as sigma and chromium nitride during cooling and reheating. Low input temperature can lead to high ferrite content and precipitation of chromium nitride, while high input power or prolonged exposure to temperatures between 600 and 1000 °C can lead to brittle intermetallic phases such as σ, causing the product to fail [10,19]. As the heat input increases, the cooling rate will decrease, resulting in an increase in austenite content in the weld metal due to a longer time for ferrite-to-austenite transformation during cooling. In this study, the percentage of ferrite and austenite in the final sample was checked in three areas, and it was found to be 26.4 % ferrite and 73.6 % austenite in Fig. 10-a, 25.2 % ferrite and 74.8 % austenite in Fig. 10-b, and 12 % ferrite in Fig. 10-c (88 % austenite). Dual-phase stainless steels generally have a higher ferrite/austenite content ratio than austenitic stainless steels because they solidify to 100 % ferrite. The equivalent chromium to equivalent nickel ratio in these steels ranges from 2.25 to 3.5. At high temperatures (above the ferrite dissolution limit), the still remains 100 % ferritic [20]. Austenite can only nucleate and grow below the ferrite solution line. By controlling the temperature of the process and the cooling rate from this temperature, the ratio and distribution of ferrite and austenite in the processed product can be controlled. The amount of ferrite in the weld metal is controlled by the composition and thermal conditions of the weld. To overcome the effects of high cooling rates(which can increase the ferrite content), most filler metals have a higher nickel content than the base metal. Nitrogen is more useful in filler metal and base metal, although due to the tendency to form porosity, an upper limit of about 0.2 wt% is applied to filler metal. More nickel with more nitrogen effectively lowers the ratio and allows austenite to form at a higher temperature because of the higher ferrite melting point temperature.

Optical microscopy image of three regions of the 2205 steel cladded sample: a) 26.4 %, b) 25.2 %, and c) 12 % ferrite.

The solidification state of a material is determined by the equilibrium chromium-to-nickel ratio and can be effectively understood through the Ni-Cr-Fe quasi-two-phase diagram [21,22]. This diagram is a valuable tool for analyzing the solidification state. During welding, it is crucial to control the microstructure to achieve the desired amount of ferrite and avoid the formation of unwanted phases such as nitrides, carbides, or intermetallic compounds. When welding two-phase stainless steels, they typically solidify in a completely ferritic state, with austenite nucleating below the solidus temperature. The solidification sequence follows the pattern of γ+δ → δ+L → L, where δ represents ferrite delta and γ represents austenite. Additionally, rapid cooling of the weld metal can lead to a significant increase in ferrite content, resulting in a strong nitride precipitation reaction during cooling. This occurs when the ferrite content exceeds its solubility limit (1000 °C) and nitrogen is unable to segregate in austenite due to the lack of time, resulting in severe precipitation. This phenomenon has a similar effect on dual-phase steels as it does on ferritic alloys, significantly reducing flexibility, toughness, and corrosion resistance. Notably, despite the high cooling speed employed in this method, the percentage of formed ferrite is surprisingly lower than expected. According to the similar study [23], three primary factors contribute to the imbalance of ferrite and austenite: the presence of secondary austenite, chemical composition, and cooling rate. In the current research, the primary factor is the cooling speed, which is significantly accelerated due to the use of laser cladding and can reach up to 106 K/s [24,25]. The cooling speed itself is influenced by two factors: the amount of incoming heat and thermal conductivity. Notably, a high thermal gradient in the melting area and the use of a larger substrate (which exhibits self-quenching properties) disrupt the thermal balance. Consequently, different percentages of ferrite and austenite have been observed compared to other methods.

The laser cladded 2205 sample is thermodynamically superior in terms of corrosion resistance compared to the control sample and the MO40 substrate. In study by Ge et al. [26] The 2205 clading passive current density was higher, and its pitting potential and total resistance were lower compared to the base metal. Additionally, the existence of a pseudo-passive area is an advantage for the laser cladded 2205 sample. In two-phase stainless steels, the ferrite phase becomes brittle at 475 °C, while the formation of sigma phase occurs in the temperature range of 600 °C–950 °C. Chromium carbide can also be formed in the temperature range of 560–1050 °C [20,27]. As a result, rapid cooling may be necessary in the critical temperature range mentioned during the production process of these types of steels. The upper limit of the working temperature of these types of steels should also be carefully determined. It is likely that the higher corrosion resistance of the laser claded 2205 sample compared to the control sample is due to the high cooling rate during direct laser deposition. It is crucial to note that the control sample, which was isolated from the marine shaft, exhibited a significant disparity in results compared to the cladding sample. This disparity is attributed to the complex thermal history experienced by the part during the laser cladding process. Unlike traditional processes, laser cladding involves directional solidification [28,29], repeated melting, and rapid solidification [30,31], which can significantly impact the outcome.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS)

EIS is a technique that analyzes electrochemical systems by applying a sinusoidal potential and measuring the resulting current or vice versa. This technique has broad applications in various fields, including material science, chemistry, polymer science, and corrosion studies. Given its importance in modern research, it is essential to understand and effectively utilize EIS in contemporary research.

To evaluate the cladding quality and electrochemical corrosion resistance behavior of the cladding, the EIS test was performed on the cladding, substrate, and control sample in a frequency range of 100 kHz to 10 MHz. The results are shown in Fig. 12(a–d). The shape of a semicircle in the impedance plot indicates that the main mechanism of corrosion resistance is the charge transfer resistance through the electric double layer. The diameter of this semicircle represents the level of resistance to charge transfer, which is the dominant corrosion control mechanism at low frequencies. Notably, the diameter of the semicircle corresponding to the cladding is significantly larger than that of the substrate and control sample, indicating a much higher level of resistance in the case of the cladding. At medium frequencies, the cladding demonstrates corrosion resistance due to a reduction in frequency, allowing the corrosion deposits to fill the cladding pores and impede the access of electrolyte and corrosive ions to the substrate surface. This inhibits the transfer of electrons from the substrate surface to the electrolyte, effectively slowing down the corrosion reaction. Therefore, 2205 laser cladding exhibits superior corrosion resistance compared to the 2205 control sample. Abdo et al. [35] in research about the coating of 2205 steel showed that the Nyquist plot exhibited a depressed semicircle, with the diameter of the capacitive semicircle increasing with increased pulse energy. The Bode magnitude plot revealed two distinct regions, with a linear slope of −1 in the middle frequency range, indicating the response of the surface film's capacitive actions. The Bode phase plot showed that increasing pulse energy or heat input caused the phase angle to shift to a higher modulus, indicating an increase in solution resistance, and also revealed diffusion on the steel surface, which can affect corrosion resistance. Achieving the desired microstructure during the welding of two-phase stainless steels requires precise control of the welding input heat. Low input heat results in a high percentage of ferrite in the microstructure and the formation of chromium nitride. This can be attributed to the high cooling rate at low input temperatures, which impedes the permeation transformation of ferrite to austenite, leading to a higher ferrite content in the final microstructure. Moreover, the solubility of nitrogen in ferrite reduces as the temperature decreases, and if sufficient cooling time is allowed, nitrogen segregates to the austenite side. However, at low input temperatures with high cooling rates, nitrogen does not have enough time to penetrate the austenite, causing ferrite to become supersaturated with nitrogen and facilitate the precipitation of chromium nitride. Conversely, high input heat or prolonged exposure in the temperature range of 600–1000 °C can lead to undesirable phases such as sigma [19]. Based on these findings, it can be concluded that in the welding of special two-phase steels with chemical composition, the greatest influence on the mechanical properties and corrosion is the electronic products. Therefore, accurate control of the power source is essential for the successful welding of these steels. The heat control in this research was achieved through manipulation of input parameters, specifically influencing the dimensions and microstructure. Additionally, due to the self-quenching property of the substrate in relation to the cladding layer and the absence of heat accumulation effects and in-situ heat treatment, excellent properties have been attained.

Zhang et al. [36] used electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) to study the stability of the passivation film of 2205 duplex stainless steel in 3.5 % sodium chloride + 0.05 M sodium thiosulfate solution. The results show that the impedance arc radius first increases and then decreases when the polarization potential increases, indicating that the solutions containing Cl− and S2O3− slightly disrupt the passivation film to the passivation region at high anodic potential. Studies have shown that thiosulfate ions have an effect on the passivation behavior and can improve the stability of the passivation film. The impedance reaches a maximum of 0.2 VSCE, which shows very good passivation film resistance and protection properties. However, when the anodic potential is higher than 0.2 VSCE, rapid oxide cracking is observed even when the sample is in a well-passivated state. An electrical equation with two time constants was proposed to describe the electrochemical behavior and determine various properties of the electrolyte/sample interface.

Open circuit potential (OCP) corrosion test

In open circuit potential corrosion tests, the results showed that the corrosion potential of the cladding is higher than that of the base and the control samples (Fig. 13(a–d)). This means that the expected corrosion of the cladding sample is significantly lower than that of the other two samples. The study by Cheng et al. [37] measured the OCP of three specimens of 2205 DSS in a 3.5 % sodium chloride solution. The results show that the OCP values of the dual-phase and γ-phase samples are similar and higher than that of the γ-phase sample. The OCP curves also show a change in slope, which is related to the growth process of the passive film. Initially, the γ-phase sample has a higher potential increase rate due to its higher content of chromium, which benefits re-passivation performance. However, later on, the γ-phase sample has a higher potential increase rate due to the beneficial effect of nickel. Overall, the 2205 DSS sample has the highest potential increase rate, indicating that the interaction of α and γ-phase in the material favors the formation of passive film.

Hardness test

The difference in hardness between the designs is shown in Fig. 14, where each hardness value is the average of three measurements. Due to the fast speed during the DLD process, most of the strengthening elements are in solid form in microstructure, and changes in the supersaturation of the elements make a difference in hardness. In general, hardness gradually decreases from the lower layer to the upper layer. The decrease in hardness is mainly due to the accumulation of heat in the deposition process in this process. The presence of the matrix and the surrounding environment helps cool the air, increasing the strength of the material close to the matrix. Initially, the hardness decreases and continues, first slightly increasing, then decreasing. This can be attributed to the direct laser targeting of the deposit. The solidified structure consists of two zones and a honeycomb zone. During the coating process, heat is emitted from the substrate, and there is sufficient heat in the direction perpendicular to the substrate surface. In these areas, growth is competitive, and oriented columnar dendrites are formed. In other regions, the growth is similar to a honeycomb and the heat transfer at the interface is higher than at the surface. The distance from the dendrite branch connection point to the surface gradually decreases, affecting the structure and function of the part [13]. Fluctuations in the hardness of weld metal and the interface area can be attributed to multiple factors. Research, such as Weston's findings [38], suggests that in dual-phase steels, the hardness of the weld zone and heat-affected zone (HAZ) is higher than the base metal and weld metal. This phenomenon is largely attributed to the larger volume of the HAZ in dual-phase steels, which typically comprises a higher hardness zone and ferrite zone. Additionally, residual stress [39] can also be a secondary contributing factor to this phenomenon. It is worth noting that the base steel used in this research is tool steel, which naturally exhibits a higher hardness than the welding metal. This analysis aims to justify the observed fluctuations in hardness.

Tensile test

The Study by Zhang et al. [40] discusses the mechanical properties of 2205 duplex stainless steel (DSS) in a study that tested the material in different directions. The study found that the material's strength varies depending on the direction of the test, with higher strength in the top and bottom layers compared to the transverse direction. The material's mechanical performance is anisotropic, meaning it exhibits different properties in different directions. The study also found that the material meets the required strength standard specified in ASTM-A890, with a strength of 772–795 MPa.

The mechanical behavior, as well as the reliability and stability of the final parts, have been emphasized as the primary objectives of this research. Therefore, the assessment of the tensile strength of the samples was prioritized. Fig. 15 and Table 7 demonstrate that the strength reduction of the cladded sample is negligible and can be disregarded. The sample manufactured using the DLD method exhibits higher yield strength and ultimate strength compared to the control sample, albeit with less elongation. In conclusion, the tensile properties of DLD parts are generally on par with or superior to those of forged or cast materials. The Hall-Patch effect [41] suggests that reducing grain size enhances strength. Consequently, the fine-grained structures produced through rapid solidification display greater strength. The relatively rapid cooling rates during the DLD process result in microstructures that yield acceptable tensile strength.

The study by Haghdadi et al. [42] examined the mechanical properties of 2205 DSS produced by additive manufacturing and heat treatment. The results showed that the AM samples had higher tensile strength but lower ductility compared to conventionally wrought samples. The fracture surfaces of the samples revealed different modes of fracture depending on the microstructure and testing conditions. The impact toughness of the samples was strongly dependent on the microstructure, loading direction, and temperature, with heat treatment improving the toughness. The study also examined the deformation mechanism of the samples using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and found that twinning occurred within austenite but not ferrite.

The cladding sample exhibits reduced ductility and deviates from 2 other samples due to three primary factors: Super-melting [43]: Exposing the metal to an additional temperature, potentially exceeding 650 °C, can weaken the grain boundary, reducing flexibility, fatigue limit, and overall ductility. This is a concern even in superalloys, where the maximum temperature is limited to 760 °C. Rapid cooling rates during DLD [44,45]: Although fine microstructures and acceptable tensile strength are achieved, the process also introduces porosity, holes, and defects, which can negatively impact mechanical properties. The interface between the base metal and weld metal exhibits rapid changes in chromium, nickel, and copper elements due to thermal cycles from multipass cladding. Slow cooling rates can cause ferrite-to-austenite transformation, while rapid cooling can lead to intermetallic phase precipitation. The transformation of ferrite to austenite is controlled by the cooling rate and is influenced by factors such as temperature and alloy composition. Thermal histories [46,47]: Samples with vertical orientation exhibit higher porosity and residual stress, leading to lower tensile strength compared to those with horizontal orientation. Applying a HIP (hot isostatic pressing) process after DLD can significantly reduce anisotropic mechanical behavior by minimizing porosity.
