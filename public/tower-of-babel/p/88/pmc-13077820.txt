The bHLH transcription factor OsbHLH148 negatively regulates rice tiller number and grain yield through an integrated transcriptional network
Authors: Xinyu Chen, Binfeng Dai, Yuchen Zhou, Ji’an Bi, Chengqi Yan, Liequan Zhong, Yuhang Zhou, Liujie Xie, Bingsong Zheng, Chulang Yu, Jianping Chen, Yong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRice is a staple food crop worldwide, and the number of tillers per plant is an important agronomic trait influencing grain yield. Consequently, investigating the molecular mechanisms regulating rice tillering is of great significance.ResultsThis study systematically elucidated the biological function of the rice transcription factor OsbHLH148 in regulating tillering and yield formation. Through the generation of OsbHLH148-overexpressing transgenic lines, we observed a dwarf plant phenotype accompanied by a significant reduction in tiller number and a decrease of approximately 72% in grain yield per plant. OsbHLH148 protein was localized to both the cytoplasm and nucleus and exhibited a transcriptional activation activity. Integrated multi-omics analyses-including RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq), weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), and DNA affinity purification sequencing (DAP-Seq), revealed that OsbHLH148 modulates a gene network associated with protein phosphorylation, Mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis, thereby influencing tiller development. Furthermore, we identified and validated its direct target genes, such as MYB110, OsDREB1A, RLCK174, OsCIPK23, and MRLK26.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that OsbHLH148 may inhibit rice tillering by regulating potential target genes involved in hormone signaling pathways (such as auxin and gibberellin), calcium signaling, and carbon and nitrogen metabolism. This study offers potential applications of these genes for improving rice yield and provides valuable genetic resources as well as a theoretical basis for molecular design breeding aimed at modifying plant architecture and enhancing productivity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12724-9.Keywords: Rice tillering, Yield, Transcription factors, Multi-omics analysis, Target genes, Regulatory network

Background

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is not only a model organism for monocot research but also the staple food crop for the majority of the global population. Enhancing rice yield is a critical strategy for addressing food shortages driven by rapid global population growth [1–3]. Rice yield potential is governed by three key agronomic components: grains per panicle, panicle density, and grain weight. Among these, panicle number per unit area is significantly influenced by rice tillering capacity, making tillering a decisive factor for rice yield [4].

In recent years, several key genes regulating rice tillering have been successfully cloned and functionally characterized [5]. These include genes promoting axillary meristem (AM) formation, such as MONOCULM1 (MOC1), MOC2, MOC3, LAX PANICLE 1 (LAX1), and LAX2. Conversely, key negative regulators of tillering include TEOSINTE BRANCHED 1 (OsTB1) and TILLER AND DWARF 1 (TAD1). MOC1 encodes a nuclear-localized GRAS protein that controls AM formation during both reproductive and vegetative growth stages in plants. MOC1 and MOC3 are not only essential for tiller bud initiation but also regulate bud elongation. MOC3 binds directly to the promoter of the FON1 gene, thereby activating and upregulating its expression. When MOC3 is present, MOC1 acts as a co-activator of MOC3, further enhancing FON1 activation. FON1 positively regulates tiller bud elongation; loss-of-function fon1 mutants form normal buds but exhibit defective bud outgrowth, leading to reduced tiller numbers [6–7]. In the presence of gibberellins (GAs), the DELLA protein SLR1 is degraded, which releases its repression on stem elongation and concurrently leads to the degradation of MOC1, thereby reducing tiller number. In the absence of GAs, MOC1 stabilizes itself by interacting with SLR1 [8]. OsTB1, encoding a TCP family transcription factor acting downstream of strigolactones (SLs), is expressed throughout the rice axillary buds. It influences tiller number by controlling axillary bud growth. Transgenic rice overexpressing OsTB1 shows significantly fewer tillers due to impaired axillary bud formation, whereas the OsTB1 loss-of-function mutant fc1 exhibits a significant increase in tiller number. OsTB1 acts as a negative regulator of rice tillering by suppressing axillary bud growth [9]. Furthermore, studies indicate that numerous genes modulate tiller number either by regulating OsTB1 transcription or by directly interacting with the OsTB1 protein [10–16]. For instance, OsTB1 interacts with OsMADS57, weakening OsMADS57’s transcriptional repression of D14. Together, OsTB1 and OsMADS57 control tillering by modulating the downstream target D14 [10]. The LAX1 gene, a member of the bHLH transcription factor family, is specifically localized to the boundary region between the shoot apical meristem and nascent meristems. This factor plays a key regulatory role in the development of axillary bud primordia in rice. The LAX1 protein physically interacts with LAX2, and they cooperatively regulate AM formation [17–19]. However, the complete molecular network governing rice tillering remains to be fully elucidated.

The basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factor family represents one of the largest transcription factor families in plants. These proteins typically contain two highly conserved domains with distinct functions: an N-terminal basic domain composed of 15–20 amino acids, responsible for recognizing and binding to the E-box motif (CANNTG), with the most common form being the G-box (CACGTG); and a C-terminal helix-loop-helix (HLH) domain that mediates protein–protein interactions, facilitating the formation of homodimeric or heterodimeric complexes [20]. These dimers can specifically bind target DNA sequences, thereby participating in the regulation of downstream gene expression [21]. The number of bHLH genes varies considerably among different plant species. For instance, 183 bHLH genes have been identified in rice, 170 in Arabidopsis thaliana, and 213 in maize [22–24]. bHLH transcription factors play crucial roles in various plant growth and developmental processes, including responses to biotic and abiotic stresses, transduction of plant hormone signals, regulation of organogenesis, and control of flowering time [25–27]. Extensive studies have shown that many bHLH proteins act as core regulatory components within complex signaling networks, modulating downstream response pathways [28, 29]. For example, bHLH142 functions as a key switch in the transition and differentiation of the tapetum, acting upstream of TDR and EAT1 and directly regulating their expression, thereby inducing tapetal programmed cell death [30]. Meanwhile, OsbHLH061 functions as a negative regulator of iron homeostasis-related gene expression. By acting upstream of OsPRI1, it helps rice adapt to changes in iron supply and maintain internal iron balance [31]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, AtbHLH122 enhances resistance to drought and osmotic stress by suppressing the expression of cytochrome P450 enzyme CYP707A, which plays a key role in ABA catabolism, thereby promoting ABA biosynthesis [32]. bHLH transcription factors are also widely involved in plant immunity regulation. For instance, OsbHLH057 can bind to the AATCA cis-acting element in the promoter region of the defense-related gene Os2H16, positively regulating rice resistance to blast, sheath blight, and drought [33]. OsbHLH148 is involved in the JA signaling pathway to regulate drought tolerance: during early drought stress, it acts as an initial response factor regulating JA-responsive gene expression and antagonizes the regulatory effect of OsDREB1 through interaction with OsJAZ1 [34]. Additionally, it interacts with OsWRKY76 to synergistically activate OsDREB1B expression, thereby enhancing cold tolerance in rice [35]. Although the role of OsbHLH148 in drought and cold stress responses has been relatively well established, whether and how this gene participates in regulating plant growth and developmental processes remains unclear. Therefore, elucidating the potential function of OsbHLH148 in growth and development is crucial for further understanding the multifaceted roles of this gene.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Japonica rice (Oryza. sativa L. ssp. Japonica) cultivar Nip and OsbHLH148 overexpression transgenic plants (OsbHLH148-OE) were used for all experiments. Nip was collected, preserved, and propagated by our laboratory. The OsbHLH148-OE vector was constructed and then entrusted to Wuhan Boyuan Biological Technology Co., Ltd. for the generation of transgenic OsbHLH148-OE plants. These plants were subsequently cultivated, propagated, and used for experiments at the Zhejiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences in China. After surface sterilization, rice seeds were first cultivated on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (1/2 MS) medium for 6 days before being transferred to a conventional rice nutrient solution for hydroponic cultivation [36]. Seedlings were cultivated in a plant growth chamber under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod cycle, with temperatures maintained at 28 °C during the light phase and 25 °C during the dark phase [37].

The generation of transgenic plants

In order to obtain OsbHLH148-OE transgenic plants, the coding sequence (CDS) of OsbHLH148 was amplified using OsbHLH148-FLAG-F/R primers by KOD-FX (Toyobo, Japan) from Nip, and subsequently inserted into the 35 S::3×FLAG vector. The constructed vector was then introduced into calli induced from mature rice embryos via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation, yielding transgenic plants. All primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Protein sequence and phylogenetic analysis

The structurally and functionally important regions in the derived protein sequence were identified in the BLAST conserved domain of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). Furthermore, the secondary structure was determined by using the SOPMA program. The ClustalW software program from the European Bioinformatics Institute (http://www.ebi.ac.uk) aligns sequences with the default parameters. The neighbor connection tree is constructed by using MEGA11.0 software.

Transient expression assays in rice protoplasts

Rice protoplasts were prepared and transformed according to the method of Zhang et al.., 2011 [38]. Stems and leaf sheaths from 50 to 60 seedlings of rice were cut into approximately 0.5 mm strips and immediately transferred into 10 mL of freshly prepared enzyme solution. The enzyme solution consisted of 1% (w/v) cellulase Onozuka R-10 (Yakult, Japan), 0.5% (w/v) macerozyme Macerozyme R-10 (Yakult, Japan), and 0.25 M mannitol. Incubation was carried out for 5 h at 25 °C in the dark with shaking at 60 rpm. The resulting protoplasts were filtered twice, resuspended in 1 mL of MMG solution, and adjusted to a density of 5 × 106 cells/mL. Transfection was performed using 5–10 µg of plasmid prepared with the EndoFree Plasmid Midi Kit (CWBIO, Beijing, China), followed by a 20‑minute incubation at 25 °C in the dark. After washing with W5 solution, the cells were resuspended in 1 mL of W5 solution and kept statically overnight at 25 °C in the dark for subsequent experiments.

Subcellular localization analysis

The pAN580 vector, carrying a GFP reporter gene driven by the CaMV 35 S promoter, was employed to analyze the subcellular localization of the OsbHLH148 protein in rice protoplasts. For co-localization analysis, the marker fused to the mCherry subcellular marker nucleus was used. Fluorescence imaging was performed on a Zeiss 880 confocal microscope (Germany). The signals from GFP and mCherry were acquired through excitation at 488 nm and 546 nm, respectively.

Transcriptional activation assay

To assess the transcriptional activity of OsbHLH148, the yeast strain AH109 (Stratagene, USA), which carries both lacZ and HIS3 reporter genes, was employed in the study. The CDS of OsbHLH148 and various truncated variants of OsbHLH148 inserted into the pGBKT7 vector via BamHI and NdeI restriction enzymes to produce pGBKT7-OsbHLH148 fusion protein and various truncated variants fusion protein. The negative control pGBKT7, positive control pGBKT7-VP16, pGBKT7-OsbHLH148 and various truncated variants plasmids were transfected into the AH109 cells. The transfected yeast cells were coated on SD/-Trp medium plates and cultured at 30 °C for 3 days. The activity of transcriptional activation was detected on the SD/ -Trp/ -His/ -Ade + X-α-Gal selective medium.

Full-length CDS and various truncated variants of OsbHLH148 were inserted into the 35 S promoter-driven pGreenII-62sk-GAL4 vector via XbaI and EcoRI restriction enzymes to fuse with GAL4 DNA-binding domain as an effector for transcriptional activity assays in plant cells. The reporter (pGreenII-0800-GAL4-LUC) and effector (pGreenII-62sk-GAL4, pGreenII-62sk-GAL4-VP16, pGreenII-62sk-GAL4-OsbHLH148 and pGreenII-62sk-GAL4-various truncated variants) constructs were respectively co-transfected into rice protoplasts and used for dual-luciferase assay. The LUC and REN activities of fireflies were detected using the Dual-LUC Report detection Kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) and SpectraMax i3x. Three biological repetitions were carried out for this purpose. Normalize the LUC activity to the REN activity and calculate the LUC/REN ratio. Table S1 lists all the primers used to construct vectors.

RNA-seq sampling and data analysis

Leaf tissues from wild-type Nip and OsbHLH148-OE transgenic plants were used in this study. For each genotype, leaves of three independent plants were combined to form one biological replicate, with three replicates prepared in total. Total RNA was isolated using the TRIzol reagent kit (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) and confirmed by RNase-free agarose gel electrophoresis. Sequencing was conducted by Guangzhou Gidio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. on the Illumina HiSeq4000 platform.

RNA data were analyzed according to the method of Chen et al., 2020 [39]. Briefly, raw sequencing reads were processed with fastp (version 0.18.0) to obtain high-quality clean reads. Expression abundance was quantified using StringTie software in FPKM units to assess variation and expression differences between the two compared groups. Differential expression genes (DEG) analysis was performed with DESeq2. DEGs were selected using the following criteria: a false discovery rate (FDR) of less than 0.05 and an absolute fold change ≥ 2 (corresponding to a log2 fold change ≥ 1 or ≤-1). Genes meeting these thresholds were subjected to further analysis.

DAP-seq sampling and data analysis

OsbHLH148 was fused to a HaloTag. Rice genomic DNA was extracted using the CTAB method and fragmented via ultrasonication. A mixture of HaloTag Beads and the OsbHLH148-HaloTag fusion protein was incubated with 1 µg of the DNA library in 100 µL of PBS buffer. Incubation was performed on a rotary shaker at 4°C for 1.5 h. The beads were then washed five times with 200 µL of PBS buffer containing 0.005% NP40, resuspended in PBS, and the supernatant was removed. Bound DNA was eluted by adding 25 µL of EB buffer and heating at 98°C for 10 min. Subsequently, DNA fragments were end-repaired, adenylated at the 3’ ends, and ligated to Illumina sequencing adapters. Fragments of appropriate size (typically 100–300 bp, including adapters) were selected and PCR-amplified. The final library was subjected to PCR and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq™ 4000 (or similar platform) by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China).

Raw reads containing adapter sequences or low-quality bases were processed to obtain high-quality clean reads. Clean reads from each sample were aligned to the reference genome using Bowtie2 (v2.2.5). Read counts across regions from the transcription start site (TSS) to the transcription end site (TES), as well as 2 kb upstream and downstream, were calculated with deepTools (v3.2.0). Enriched genomic regions (peaks) were identified from the DAP-seq data using MACS2 (v2.1.2). A dynamic Poisson distribution was applied to calculate the p-value for each region based on uniquely mapped reads; regions with a q‑value < 0.05 were defined as peaks. Peak-associated genes were annotated using the ChIPseeker R package, which assigned peaks to genomic features such as intergenic regions, introns, downstream, upstream, and exons. Since transcription factor binding sites and histone modifications often exhibit conserved sequence patterns, motif analysis was performed using the MEME suite. Both MEME and DREME tools were employed to detect long and short consensus sequences. Peaks from different groups were merged using DiffBind (v2.8) to create a union peak set. Peak abundance was represented by calculating reads per million (RPM) values for each sample.

Relative luciferase (LUC) activity

The 2000 bp promoter sequence of the potential target gene was amplified and inserted into the BamHI/NotI-linearized pGreenII-0800-LUC vector to construct the corresponding reporter vector. The full-length coding sequence of OsbHLH148 was cloned into the BamHI/KpnI-linearized pGreenII 62-SK vector to generate the effector vector. The pGreenII 62-SK empty vector was co-transfected with the corresponding promoter-LUC reporter gene as the control experimental group. Rice protoplasts were prepared and subjected to dual-luciferase reporter assays. Firefly luciferase (LUC) and Renilla luciferase (REN) activities were measured using a dual-luciferase reporter assay kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) and a SpectraMax i3x spectrophotometer. The data from three biological replicates are presented as the LUC/REN ratio after normalization. All primer sequences used for vector construction are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

qRT-PCR

cDNA was synthesized from 2 µg of total RNA using M-MuLV reverse transcriptase (Takara, Japan). Subsequent real-time quantitative PCR was performed on a LightCycler 480 instrument with SYBR Green Master Mix (Novoprotein, China). The rice OsActin gene was used for normalization [40]. The primer sequences employed are listed in Table S1.

Protein purification

The monoclonal strain expressing the His‑OsbHLH148 recombinant protein was cultured overnight, subcultured at 1:100, and induced with 0.5 mM IPTG at 16 ℃ for 24 h when OD600 reached 0.6. After centrifugation, the cell pellet was suspended in non‑denaturing lysis buffer, treated with lysozyme, and disrupted by sonication. The supernatant obtained by centrifugation was incubated with equilibrated BeyoGold™ His‑tag Purification Resin, loaded onto a column, and washed with non‑denaturing wash buffer. The target protein was then eluted with non‑denaturing elution buffer, and the collected eluates contained the purified protein.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assays

This experiment begins by preparing a biotin-labeled single-stranded DNA probe via a terminal transferase reaction, followed by annealing to generate the double-stranded probe. Subsequently, the labeled probe is incubated with the target protein in binding buffer at room temperature for 30 min to form protein-nucleic acid complexes. The complexes are then separated by electrophoresis on a pre-run 6% non-denaturing polyacrylamide gel in 0.5X TBE buffer at 100 V for approximately 90 min. Following electrophoresis, the samples are transferred from the gel onto a nylon membrane using a semi-dry transfer system and cross-linked via UV irradiation. Finally, the membrane is incubated with streptavidin-HRP conjugate, and the biotin-labeled signal is detected and visualized using a chemiluminescent substrate.

Statistical analysis

Differences were evaluated for statistical significance using Student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA with significance levels indicated as follows: **** p < 0.0001, *** p < 0.001, ** p < 0.01, * p < 0.05, and ns (not significant) for p ≥ 0.05. All quantitative data were derived from three independent biological replicates and are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Result

OsbHLH148 regulates rice tillering and thereby influences grain yield

To elucidate the biological function of OsbHLH148, we cloned its 900 bp coding sequence and constructed it into an overexpression vector, 35 S:: 3×FLAG, followed by genetic transformation. Using qRT-PCR analysis, we identified three independent overexpression lines, all exhibiting significantly elevated expression levels of OsbHLH148 (Fig. 1A).

Data are presented as mean ± SD of three biological replicates (B) Whole plants of Nip and OsbHLH148-OE at the heading stage. White scale bar = 20 cm. To investigate the role of OsbHLH148 in rice, we analyzed OsbHLH148-OE transgenic rice lines cultivated in the field. A comparison between wild-type and overexpression lines revealed a significant decrease in plant height in the latter. (Fig. 1B-C). Statistical analysis of tiller numbers revealed that the OsbHLH148-OE lines had significantly fewer tillers than the WT, with approximately half the number of tillers observed in the WT (Fig. 1E). To further verify the function of OsbHLH148, we generated OsbHLH148 knockout mutants (OsbHLH148-ko). Agronomic trait analysis revealed that the tiller number in OsbHLH148-ko lines was higher than that in the WT (Figure S1). These results indicate that OsbHLH148 is involved in regulating tillering in rice.

After harvest, we evaluated yield-related traits, including panicle number, grains per panicle, and filled grains per panicle, thousand-grain weight, and yield per plant. The results showed that the panicle number, grains per panicle, and filled grains per panicle were significantly reduced in the OsbHLH148-OE transgenic lines compared to the WT (Fig. 1D-J). Based on these yield-related parameters, the yield per plant was calculated to be 7.29 g for WT, and 2.12 g, 1.97 g, and 1.83 g for OsbHLH148-OE-1, OsbHLH148-OE-2, and OsbHLH148-OE-3, respectively. This represents a 72.0% reduction in yield in the OsbHLH148-OE lines compared to the WT. In contrast, the yield per plant of OsbHLH148-ko lines was significantly higher than that of the WT (Figure S1). Together, these results demonstrate that OsbHLH148 primarily affects rice yield by regulating tiller formation.

Identification and analysis of OsbHLH148

Bioinformatics analysis revealed that the OsbHLH148 protein contains a characteristic HLH (helix-loop-helix) domain spanning amino acid residues 136–191, with an E-box motif located in close proximity. This domain architecture is typical of transcription factors involved in DNA binding and protein dimerization. Physicochemical profiling using ProtParam indicated that OsbHLH148 has a molecular weight of 32.91 kDa and a theoretical isoelectric point of 6.10. The protein exhibits a high instability index (69.79), an aliphatic index of 58.55, and a negative grand average of hydropathicity (-0.695), corresponding to a relatively hydrophilic and unstable polypeptide. This indicates that the protein encoded by OsbHLH148 is classified as an unstable protein and has hydrophilicity. The TMHMM database analysis tool showed that this OsbHLH148 protein was an extracellular protein (Figure S2). Analysis of the OsbHLH148 protein secondary structure using the GOR4 program demonstrated a composition of 32.78% alpha-helices, 12.71% extended strands, and 54.52% random coils. The multiple alignment analysis of the amino acid sequence encoded by OsbHLH148 and bHLH148 of other species is shown in the Figure S3. The phylogenetic tree of bHLH148 of rice and other species was constructed using the MEGA 11.0 software program. The results show that the amino acids encoded by OsbHLH148 have a good correlation with bHLH148 of Panicum virgatum (Fig. 2A).

Subcellular localisation of the OsbHLH148 protein and the expression pattern of OsbHLH148

To analysis the subcellular localization of OsbHLH148 protein, we constructed a C-terminal GFP fusion expression vector, OsbHLH148-GFP, and co-transformed it with a nuclear marker plasmid, NLS-mCherry (red fluorescence), into rice protoplasts. After 16 h of cultivation, when observed under a confocal microscope, it was observed that the green fluorescence signal of OsbHLH148-GFP overlapped with the red nuclear signal. At the same time, in some cells, the green fluorescence signal was also found in the cytoplasm. (Fig. 2B). These results indicate that the OsbHLH148 protein localizes to both the cytoplasm and the nucleus.

To further explore the expression pattern of the OsbHLH148 gene in rice, total RNA was extracted from the roots, stems, leaf sheaths, leaves, and floral organs of Nipponbare (Nip) plants. Subsequent qRT-PCR analysis after reverse transcription showed that the OsbHLH148 gene was expressed in all examined tissues, with the highest expression level in roots, followed by leaves, and the lowest expression in stems (Fig. 2C).

OsbHLH148 has a transcriptional activation activity

To investigate whether OsbHLH148 possesses gene expression activation or repression functions, this study conducted a transcriptional activation analysis of OsbHLH148 in a yeast system. The results demonstrated that OsbHLH148 exhibits significant transcriptional activation activity (Fig. 3A-B). Members of the bHLH superfamily contain two highly conserved and functionally distinct domains: a basic region and a helix-loop-helix (HLH) region. The basic region, composed of 15–20 amino acids, recognizes E-box and G-box cis-acting elements, thereby regulating the expression of downstream target genes. However, previous studies have indicated that bHLH domain proteins may also possess transcriptional repression activity. To verify this property, we constructed a fusion expression vector of OsbHLH148-GAL4 BD-LUC, with GAL4 BD-LUC serving as the negative control and VP16-GAL4 BD-LUC as the positive control. The experiments showed that both OsbHLH148 and OsbHLH148-136-191aa significantly enhanced the expression level of the reporter gene (Fig. 3C-D), confirming that it possesses transcriptional activation activity.

RNA-Seq identification of genes affected by OsbHLH148

To elucidate the regulatory network governed by OsbHLH148 in controlling tiller development and yield formation, RNA-Seq was performed on total RNA extracted from the leaves of both wild-type and OsbHLH148-OE plants at the heading stage under conventional cultivation conditions. The Illumina platform generated 41.9–46.6 million raw reads for each cDNA library (15 cDNA libraries in total). Clean reads were obtained to 95.35–98.90%, after excluding the low-quality reads. Meanwhile, stringent quality assessment and data filtering, high-quality clean reads were obtained, containing 95.92%-97.38% Q20 bases and 89.94%-93.0% Q30 bases. A summary of the sequencing data quality is provided in Table S2. The high-quality reads were aligned to the rice reference genome using HISAT2, demonstrating efficient mapping. The overall alignment rate for clean reads ranged from 84.37% to 97.08%, and the proportion of reads that were uniquely mapped to specific genomic locations ranged from 81.59% to 93.66%.

To evaluate reproducibility, pairwise Pearson’s correlation coefficients (R values) were computed across all samples. As shown in Figure S4, the R values of the comparison among the three biological replicates of each sample ranged from 98.4% to 99.7%. Additionally, principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated a high degree of consistency in expression profiles among replicate samples, confirming that the samples in the present experiment were reliable and suitable for further analysis.

To dissect the changes in the gene expression, DEGs between the WT and OsbHLH148-OE plants were investigated. Genes showed significant changes in expression (≥ two-fold, i.e., log2 fold change ≥ 1 or log2 fold change ≤ − 1, FDR < 0.05) were selected for further analysis. The results suggested 4005, 2571, 2410, 4869 differential genes were identified between Nip and OsbHLH148-OE-1, Nip and OsbHLH148-OE-2, Nip and OsbHLH148-OE-3, Nip and OsbHLH148-OE-4, respectively (Fig. 4B). There were 1642 overlap DEGs between these several comparison groups (Fig. 4C). Out of the 1642 overlap DEGs identified between the WT and OsbHLH148-OE, 1153 DEGs were functionally annotated. The GO term enrichment analysis of the 1153 DEGs revealed that most DEGs in the biological process group were classified into “protein phosphorylation” (GO: 0006468, p value = 1.53 × 10− 16), “phosphorylation” (GO: 0016310, p value = 6.34 × 10− 17), and “phosphorus metabolic process” (GO: 0006793, p value = 3.03 × 10− 12). Of the molecular function group, “protein kinase activity” (GO: 0004672, p value = 7.28 × 10− 15) and “kinase activity” (GO: 0016301, p value = 1.01 × 10− 12) were the most abundant GO terms, followed by “phosphotransferase alcohol group as acceptor” (GO: 0016773, p value = 1.05 × 10− 12) (Fig. 4D).

Identification of Conserved DEGs via Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA)

To comprehensively decipher the gene regulatory network and identify key genes within OsbHLH148 that influence tillering and yield, we performed WGCNA. Based on the similarity of their expression patterns, the filtered genes were clustered into 18 distinct modules (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, the gene expression profiles in each module were correlated with all samples to generate a heatmap depicting the module-sample relationships. As expected, we found that the gene expression profiles in the brown module showed significant differences between Nip and OsbHLH148 (Fig. 5C). This indicates that the genes in this module may affect the tillering and yield of rice.

This study employed DAP-Seq to systematically screen for direct target genes of the transcription factor OsbHLH148. Sequencing performed on the Illumina platform across two biological replicates yielded approximately 70 million high-quality reads. Among these readings, 57.47%-59.46% were uniquely mapped to the rice genome (Fig. 6A). Meanwhile, the MACS2 algorithm (version 2.2.7.1) was adopted to identify the binding site of OsbHLH148. In two biological replicates, filtering was performed between the duplicate samples to retain the peaks that occurred in both samples. Our analysis revealed that there were 7087 enriched 200-bp peaks, with a q-value < 0.05. Among them, 1566 (22.10%) were located within the 2-kb range upstream of the transcriptional initiation site, 596 (8.37%) were located within the 2-kb downstream of the transcriptional termination site, and the remaining (69.53%) were distributed in the 5’ UTR, 3’ UTR, Exon, Intron, and intergenic regions (Fig. 6B). To verify the direct interaction between OsbHLH148 and the aforementioned target genes, we further performed EMSA for in vitro binding analysis. The results showed that the OsbHLH148 protein was able to bind to specific sequence probes derived from the promoter regions of these genes (Fig. 9). The addition of a 20-fold excess of unlabeled competitor probe significantly attenuated the signal of the protein-probe complex, indicating that the binding was sequence-specific. Moreover, the OsbHLH148 protein failed to bind to the corresponding mutated probes, further confirming the specificity of its DNA binding to the target genes. Together, these results demonstrate that OsbHLH148 can directly bind to and regulate the transcriptional activity of the above-mentioned target genes.

Discussion

OsbHLH148 is a key negative regulator of rice tillering and yield

Research has demonstrated that several members of the bHLH transcription factor family, such as MYCs, BEEs, PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTORS (PIFs), PACLOBUTRAZOL RESISTANCE (PRE), and BES1‑INTERACTING MYC‑LIKE (BIM), play key roles in diverse cellular processes in plants, including morphogenesis, shade avoidance response, epidermal cell differentiation, and stress adaptation [41–42]. bHLH transcription factors constitute a group of proteins characterized by a ~ 60-amino-acid domain comprising two distinct regions: a basic region and an HLH region. Located at the protein’s N-terminus, this motif contains 13–17 highly conserved amino acid residues (predominantly histidine, glutamate, and arginine) and functions to achieve specific recognition between the bHLH monomer and the DNA-protein binding site. The ensuing HLH segment is composed of two amphipathic α-helices connected by a variable loop, which permits dimerization and target binding of these proteins. Accumulated evidence indicates that bHLH transcription factors play key roles in diverse plant developmental and physiological events, spanning morphogenesis, stomatal differentiation, and cell elongation [43–46]. Here, we identified OsbHLH148 as a significant transcription factor in rice. This protein contains a canonical bHLH domain, and its overexpression markedly inhibited rice tillering, resulting in dwarfism and a drastic reduction in grain yield per plant (approximately 72%) (Fig. 1). We also assessed relevant agronomic traits in the OsbHLH148 homozygous knockout mutants, which showed a significant increase in tiller number and yield compared to Nip (Figure S1). This indicates that OsbHLH148 is a key negative regulator of tillering and yield formation in rice. However, it remains insufficient to attribute the substantial 72% yield reduction observed in OsbHLH148‑OE plants solely to the decrease in tiller number. This significant yield loss likely involves more complex physiological or developmental impairments, such as potential pollen abortion, reduced seed‑setting rate, or other unidentified developmental abnormalities. Currently, the underlying mechanisms are still under intensive investigation. Building on the findings of this study, our subsequent work will systematically elucidate the specific role of OsbHLH148 in reproductive development, particularly in pollen fertility.

We found that the OsbHLH148 protein localizes to both the cytoplasm and nucleus and is expressed in roots, stems, leaf sheaths, leaves, and floral organs of rice, with the highest expression level observed in roots (Fig. 2). Studies have shown that most transcription factors are not stably localized in the nucleus but rather exhibit dynamic nucleocytoplasmic distribution. These factors are typically synthesized, stored, or degraded in the cytoplasm, and upon activation by specific signals (such as hormones, stress, or developmental cues), they are transported into the nucleus to carry out their transcriptional regulatory functions. For example, in the plant ABA signaling pathway, the transcription factor ABI5 is predominantly distributed in the cytoplasm under non-stress conditions, while it undergoes nuclear translocation upon ABA signal induction. Our study found that OsbHLH148 protein is also detected in both the nucleus and the cytoplasm, suggesting that its localization may be regulated by relevant mechanisms. Furthermore, research indicates that the RRM-type transcription factor PIBP1 shows significant nuclear accumulation in protoplasts of the rice cultivar Nip, but cytoplasmic distribution is still observed in 40.3% of the cells. PigmR, through its interaction with PIBP1, further promotes the nuclear accumulation of PIBP1, thereby activating immune responses. These examples suggest that the subcellular localization of the transcription factor OsbHLH148 is regulatable and may represent a key step in the fulfillment of its function. Functional analysis revealed that OsbHLH148 possesses transcriptional activation activity, suggesting that OsbHLH148 likely influences rice tillering by regulating downstream target genes (Fig. 3).

Integrated multi-omics analysis reveals the regulatory network of OsbHLH148

Through integrated multi-omics analysis, we gained deep insights into the regulatory network of OsbHLH148. RNA-Seq analysis indicated that OsbHLH148 overexpression triggered extensive transcriptome reprogramming, with DEGs significantly enriched in processes closely related to growth and development, such as protein phosphorylation, kinase activity, and the MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 4D-E). WGCNA further pinpointed a “brown module” significantly associated with yield traits; the hub gene network within this module is likely to play a central role in regulating yield (Fig. 5). DAP-seq further enabled genome-wide mapping of OsbHLH148 binding sites, which exhibited robust enrichment at TSS of putative target genes, thereby confirming the specificity of the assay (Fig. 6B). A high degree of overlap was observed between the OsbHLH148 target genes identified by DAP-seq and the DEGs identified by RNA-seq (Fig. 7). Among the thousands of differentially expressed genes, only 67 showed overlap between DAP‑seq and RNA‑seq, indicating minimal concordance between the two datasets. This limited overlap may be attributed to the fact that DAP‑seq captures the in vitro binding capacity of OsbHLH148 to all potential affinity sites, many of which may require specific co‑factors, post‑translational modifications (e.g., phosphorylation), or specific stress/developmental signals to activate transcription in vivo. The RNA‑seq data were obtained from samples collected at a key tillering developmental stage, reflecting regulatory events active during that period. Many binding sites may function under other conditions, such as different stresses or growth stages. Additionally, individual transcription factors are often embedded in complex regulatory networks. The binding of OsbHLH148 could be antagonized by other repressors or may require cooperative activators to drive transcription. Genetic redundancy or feedback mechanisms may also buffer its impact on individual target gene expression, resulting in binding events that do not lead to significant changes in mRNA levels. Despite the small number of overlapping genes, we consider that these 67 direct targets support the proposed regulatory model and provide high‑confidence evidence for the core regulatory functions of OsbHLH148. This set of core genes-both directly bound and differentially expressed-offers high‑quality and functionally focused direct evidence for the role of OsbHLH148 in regulating tillering. Significant functional enrichment points to tillering‑related processes: GO analysis of these 67 genes revealed significant enrichment in functional categories such as ABC transporter complex, ATPase‑dependent transmembrane transport, and plasma membrane protein complex (Fig. 7C). These processes are closely related to hormone transport (e.g., auxin, strigolactone), intercellular communication, and energy supply-all known to be central to the initiation and growth of tiller buds. KEGG analysis also showed enrichment in pathways including secondary metabolite biosynthesis and diterpenoid biosynthesis, suggesting that OsbHLH148 may influence tillering through the regulation of specific metabolites. The DAP-Seq experiment directly identified OsbHLH148 binding sites across the genome and uncovered 12 novel cis-acting elements (Fig. 8A). Subsequently, the overlapping genes between the DAP-seq targets and the RNA-seq DEGs were designated as putative direct target genes (DTGs) of OsbHLH148.

By integrating transcriptome and DNA binding data, we successfully screened a set of downstream target genes directly regulated by OsbHLH148. Some of these targets were validated through dual-luciferase assays and qRT-PCR (Fig. 8B-D). These target genes encode various proteins, including bHLH proteins, ubiquitin ligases, and kinases, which may collectively constitute the core network through which OsbHLH148 regulates tillering.

OsbHLH148 regulates plant height and tillering via the MYB110-OsAP2-39 pathway

Studies reveal that the transcription factor MYB110 (Os10g0478300) is phosphate-dependent and coordinates lodging resistance with yield formation in rice by negatively regulating plant height. MYB110-OX plants exhibit reduced plant height. Studies have shown that OsAP2-39 (Os04g0610400) and OsPIL13 (Os03g0782500) are target genes of MYB110. OsAP2-39 is a key factor regulating the ABA/GA balance to control rice plant height, while OsPIL13 is a crucial regulator responsible for reduced internode elongation in rice. Under phosphorus supply conditions, MYB110 activates OsAP2-39 expression while repressing OsPIL13 expression, thereby regulating plant height. Compared to the wild type, loss-of-function of MYB110 increased plant height, stem strength, grain size, and yield [47]. Therefore, our results indicate that OsbHLH148 positively regulates MYB110. The increased expression of MYB110 leads to decreased plant height and reduced yield. On the other hand, the plant hormone GAs inhibit tiller bud growth in rice, and the MYB110 target gene OsAP2-39 acts as a transcriptional regulator involved in GA inactivation. Overexpression of OsAP2-39 alters the expression levels of genes related to GA signaling and ABA biosynthesis, causing various phenotypic changes in transgenic rice, including a 55% reduction in plant height, a 75% decrease in tillering, a 74% reduction in leaf number, delayed heading by 1–2 weeks, and increased sensitivity to dehydration, ultimately resulting in yield loss due to reduced biomass and panicle traits [48] Therefore, it can be speculated that the effect of OsbHLH148 on plant height and tillering may be achieved by upregulating the expression of MYB110, which in turn elevates the level of OsAP2-39, thereby influencing the GA/ABA balance and growth. However, since OsAP2-39 was not confirmed as a direct downstream target of MYB110 in our study, we cannot exclude the possibility that other genes or pathways downstream of MYB110, or independent pathways unrelated to it, also contribute to the phenotype. Furthermore, network analysis indicates a ratio imbalance between GA and ABA in overexpression lines. Future work will employ targeted metabolomics or liquid chromatography-based assays to measure relevant phytohormone levels, thereby validating the predicted regulatory network and elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms involved.

OsDREB1A-mediated calcium signaling leads to growth inhibition

Another target gene of OsbHLH148, OsDREB1A (Os09g0522200), is an AP2/EREBP transcription factor in rice with transcriptional activation activity that plays a regulatory role in the expression of many stress-activated genes. In Arabidopsis, overexpression of OsDREB1A can induce the expression of stress-related genes and enhance tolerance to drought, high salt, and cold stress [49]. Studies found that OsDREB1A can activate the transcription of the cyclic nucleotide-gated ion channel OsCNGC9 (Os09g0558300), thereby promoting extracellular Ca2+ influx, increasing intracellular calcium concentration, inducing the expression of cold stress-related genes, and improving chilling tolerance in rice [50]. Ca2+ serves as an important signaling molecule; influx of high concentrations of extracellular calcium leading to increased cytosolic calcium concentration can trigger the accumulation of ROS in cells [51]. This may contribute to the growth inhibition observed in OsbHLH148 overexpression lines, affecting agronomic traits such as plant height and tiller number, and ultimately impacting rice yield. In the future, we will focus on calcium flux and calcium-mediated signaling processes, aiming to further validate the reliability and robustness of the associated regulatory network.

OsbHLH148 functions as a transcriptional activator, yet the expression of its target gene OsDREB1A is reduced in overexpression lines. We propose that the observed downregulation of the target gene in these lines may be attributed to the competition for its recruited co-regulatory factors, leading to insufficient availability for effective transcription initiation. Alternatively, excessive OsbHLH148 may alter its protein interactions, favoring association with co-repressors, or trigger distinct post-translational modifications at high concentrations, thereby switching its function from an activator to a repressive state. More in-depth mechanistic studies are warranted to further elucidate this phenomenon in the future.

The role of receptor kinases and auxin signaling in tillering regulation

Receptor-like kinases (RLKs), due to their structural and functional characteristics, play crucial roles in perceiving and transducing signals related to plant development. For instance, the FON1 gene, which is homologous to Arabidopsis CLAVATA1, encodes a leucine-rich repeat receptor kinase essential for inflorescence meristem development in rice [52]. MOC3 directly bind to the FON1 promoter and activate its expression. Although MOC1 cannot directly bind the FON1 promoter, it acts as a co-activator of MOC3, further activating FON1 expression in the presence of MOC3. FON1 positively regulates tiller bud outgrowth. Loss-of-function mutants of FON1 form normal buds but exhibit defects in their outward elongation, leading to reduced tiller numbers [7]. In our study, we found that OsbHLH148 also regulates MRLK26 (Os05g0261700). MRLK26 gene is positively regulated by OsbHLH148, and qRT-PCR results confirmed upregulation in OsbHLH148-OE lines.

TMK family proteins have been reported as key components in auxin signal transduction, regulating the activity of a series of downstream substrates, such as AHA, IAA32/34, MKK4/5-MPK3/4, and TAA1, through phosphorylation, thereby mediating auxin signaling to control plant growth and development [53–56]. On the other hand, auxin has been reported to negatively regulate rice tiller formation [57]. The rice genome comprises 12 PIN genes that encode auxin efflux transporters, with four and one belonging to the PIN1 and PIN2 subclades, respectively [58]. OsPIN1, functioning as a polar auxin transporter, plays a role in auxin-regulated rice tillering. Due to auxin-mediated inhibition of axillary bud growth, suppression of OsPIN1 expression via RNA interference enhances tillering in transgenic rice relative to wild-type plants [57]. A similar phenotype was observed in plants overexpressing OsPIN2 and OsPIN3t [59–60]. However, tiller number decreased in plants overexpressing OsPIN5b [61]. The gene RLCK174 (Os04g0685900) encodes a receptor-like cytoplasmic kinase, a receptor protein kinase TMK1 precursor, which may play a significant role in auxin signaling, consequently affecting auxin levels, and ultimately contributing to the reduced tillering and yield observed in OsbHLH148 overexpression lines.

Heat shock proteins and metabolism-related genes modulate tillering

Researchers identified NAL11, which encodes a DnaJ domain-containing heat shock protein. Increased expression of this allele results in relatively fewer tillers, thicker stems, and larger panicles. Conversely, a mutant allele, nal11, with aberrant splicing in the second exon leading to loss of function, exhibits multiple tillers, small panicles, and thin stems [62–63]. In our study, the OsbHLH148 target gene OsDjC62 (Os08g0452900) also encodes a heat shock protein DnaJ. We speculate that OsbHLH148 may regulate rice tillering through the positive regulation of OsDjC62 expression.

Integration of sugar, nitrogen, and potassium signaling regulates rice tiller bud development

Researchers identified a key gene, DTN1, regulating tiller number in rice, which encodes a fructose-1,6-bisphosphate aldolase. Mutation of DTN1 led to reduced photosynthetic rate, decreased sucrose and other sugar contents in leaves and axillary buds, and consequently, a reduction in tiller number. Tiller formation in the dtn1 mutant was insensitive to nitrogen supply. Further investigation revealed that DTN1 regulates sugar content, which positively influences the expression of the nitrogen response gene NGR5, thereby affecting tiller bud development in rice [64]. Xuan et al. found that after growth in NH4+ solution for 4 days, the primary roots of the cipk23 mutant were significantly shorter than those of the wild type, and the NH4+ content in mutant cells was significantly lower, indicating an NH4+-sensitive phenotype in cipk23 [65]. Furthermore, Zhang et al. identified a novel rice tillering regulator gene, Tn1a, which negatively regulates tiller formation, and characterized its interacting protein OsCCC1. OsCCC1 encodes a K+/Cl− cotransporter involved in the positive regulation of rice tillering. Tn1a may inhibit tiller bud elongation by interacting with OsCCC1, potentially altering intracellular K+ content [66]. OsCBL1 and OsCIPK23 (Os07g0150700) are upstream regulators of OsAKT1. Potassium uptake, which is mediated by OsAKT1, is potentiated by the protein complex consisting of OsCBL1 and OsCIPK23 [67–68]. Therefore, OsbHLH148 may positively regulate OsCIPK23 expression, subsequently altering intracellular NH4+/K+ content, influencing rice tiller bud development, and ultimately affecting rice yield.

This study has constructed a preliminary regulatory network model for rice tillering. It should be noted that the model is currently based primarily on indirect associations between reported gene functions and phenotypes, lacking direct experimental evidence for several connections. For instance, while it is proposed that OsbHLH148 may positively regulate OsCIPK23 to influence ammonium/potassium ion homeostasis, thereby affecting tiller bud development-an inference consistent with the known ion transport regulatory function of OsCIPK23-direct measurements of ion content and dynamics in OsbHLH148 overexpression or mutant backgrounds remain to be performed. Similarly, whether OsbHLH148 acts through carbon-nitrogen metabolism, hormone signaling (e.g., auxin, gibberellin), or calcium signaling pathways is still hypothetical. To verify the reliability of the model, follow-up studies need to establish more direct experimental correlations. This includes determining changes in the contents of carbohydrates, nitrogen metabolites, and related hormones (such as auxin and gibberellin) in OsbHLH148 genetic materials, as well as monitoring the dynamic responses of calcium signaling during stress or developmental processes. Quantitative measurement and analysis of plant hormones, carbon‑nitrogen metabolism, and signal transduction will become the key focus for the next step in validating the model.

Conclusions

This study delineates the biological function of the transcription factor OsbHLH148 in modulating rice tillering and yield formation. Transgenic plants overexpressing OsbHLH148 displayed phenotypes characterized by reduced plant height, significantly decreased tiller number, and lower grain yield per plant. Integrated multi-omics analyses revealed that OsbHLH148 modulates tiller development through its transcriptional regulatory roles, directly or indirectly governing gene networks associated with protein phosphorylation, MAPK signaling, and the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites. Furthermore, several direct target genes-including MYB110, OsDREB1A, RLCK174, OsCIPK23, and MRLK26-were identified and experimentally validated. These findings suggest that OsbHLH148 likely suppresses rice tillering by modulating hormone signaling pathways (such as auxin and gibberellin), calcium signaling, and carbon-nitrogen metabolism (Fig. 10). This study provides important theoretical insights into the molecular mechanisms by which bHLH transcription factors regulate plant architecture and yield in rice, and offers a valuable genetic resource and conceptual foundation for molecular design breeding aimed at improving plant architecture and increasing yield.
