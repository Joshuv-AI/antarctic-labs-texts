Double Cardiac Rupture and Still Alive
Authors: David Te-Wei Kuan, Ting-Tse Lin, Kuan-Chih Huang, Chun-An Chen, Jen-Kuang Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

In the contemporary era of primary percutaneous coronary intervention, mechanical complications such as ventricular septal rupture (VSR) or left ventricular free wall rupture caused by an acute myocardial infarction (AMI) have become rare, with incidences of approximately 0.2% and 0.01% to 0.52% respectively.1,2 Notably, the rate of left ventricular free wall rupture has decreased significantly over recent decades.3 Delayed presentation of AMI poses a major risk for developing mechanical complications, with associated mortality rate rising to as high as 40%.4 In this report, we present a case of AMI complicated with both VSR and left ventricular pseudoaneurysm (LVP). The case is unique in that the patient survived without definite treatment for over 1 year after the initial insult.

Case Presentation

A 62-year-old man was referred to our cardiology clinic for further management of breathlessness. Initially, the patient presented to the emergency department of another hospital with recurrent central chest tightness for several days. An electrocardiogram showed ST-segment elevation in the inferior leads, and emergent primary percutaneous coronary intervention was performed with a drug-eluting stent implanted in the right coronary artery (RCA). The left anterior descending artery and left circumflex artery were patent. The patient remained hemodynamically stable and was discharged 4 days later. Other medical history included hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and type II diabetes mellitus. Medications upon discharge were aspirin, prasugrel, bisoprolol, dapagliflozin, metformin, and saxagliptin. Notably, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was not performed during hospitalization.

Ten months after the initial event, the patient developed progressive worsening exertional dyspnea and orthopnea. Physical examination revealed a raised jugular venous pressure of 10 cm H2O. On auscultation, a grade II pansystolic murmur was noted at the left sternal border without radiation along with bilateral basal crackles. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed mild dilatation of the left ventricle and right ventricle (RV). No late peaking or midsystolic notching was observed on the spectral Doppler profiles of the tricuspid regurgitation or the right ventricular outflow tract, respectively. The inferior wall was thinned and akinetic with preserved global left ventricular systolic function (left ventricular ejection fraction, 63%). A VSR with left-to-right shunt was identified. Pulmonary-to-systolic blood flow ratio (Qp/Qs) was 1.5, which was calculated by measuring the cross-sectional area and velocity-time integral of both right ventricular outflow tract and left ventricular outflow tract. No significant mitral regurgitation or pericardial effusion was seen. The estimated right ventricular systolic pressure was 57 mm Hg. Based on the presentation, a diagnosis of AMI complicated by VSR was suspected. Laboratory investigation showed an elevated N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide level of 1,633 pg/mL. The electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with single ventricular premature complex and pathological Q waves in the inferior leads. Chest x-ray revealed cardiomegaly with mild pulmonary vascular congestion.

Given the presence of heart failure in association with VSR, surgical repair was recommended. However, the patient declined surgery and was referred to our cardiology clinic for evaluation of percutaneous transcatheter closure 20 months after the AMI.

A thorough assessment was conducted following the current visit. Right heart catheterization demonstrated a 13% step-up in oxygen saturation from the right atrium to the RV. The right pulmonary arterial wedge pressure was 15 mm Hg, and the mean pulmonary artery pressure was 21 mm Hg. The Qp/Qs was elevated at 1.9, confirming a hemodynamically significant left-to-right shunt. Coronary angiography showed significant stenosis in the posterior descending artery without evidence of in-stent restenosis in the RCA. A 3.3 cm inferobasal LVP was identified on computed tomography (Figure 1, Videos 1-2), which had not been detected on prior TTE. Transesophageal echocardiography revealed mutual communication between the pseudoaneurysm with both the left ventricle and the RV (Figure 2, Videos 3-5). The peak systolic pressure gradient from the LVP to the RV was 81 mm Hg, while the pressure gradient across the VSR was 20.7 mm Hg (Figure 3). Both VSR and LVP were also visualized on the left ventricular angiography (Video 6). After heart team discussion, percutaneous transcatheter closure of the VSR and LVP was deemed not feasible due to the complexity of the anatomy. Given the relatively young age and absence of severe comorbidities, surgery remained the intervention of choice. Although initially reluctant, the patient consented to surgery after being informed of the imminent risk of pseudoaneurysm rupture. A 2-patch technique was performed to repair both the VSR and LVP. This led to a successful recovery and the patient was discharged with scheduled follow-up in the surgical outpatient clinic. Postoperative TTE confirmed complete resolution of the left-to-right shunt across the ventricular septum with no residual flow to the LVP (Figure 5, Video 7). Discussion

Despite the decreasing incidence of mechanical complications after AMI, the mortality rate remains high. Typically, these complications occur 3 to 5 days after infarction, with the exception of LVP, which tends to have a delayed presentation from weeks to years. Risk factors include advanced age, female sex, history of heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and first-time AMI.5 The coexistence of VSR and LVP following myocardial infarction is extremely rare. In this case, the VSR, a Becker type 3 rupture, occurred through thinned and infarcted myocardium, leading to the formation of an LVP, a condition associated with a high risk of rupture.4,6

Multimodality imaging plays a pivotal role in the comprehensive evaluation of structural complications. Transthoracic echocardiography is widely accessible and easy to perform as the first-line imaging modality for suspected mechanical complications following AMI. It can be supplemented by transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), which offers superior resolution and additional anatomical views that are inaccessible via the transthoracic approach. However, both techniques are inherently operator dependent. Computed tomography and cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging provide detailed anatomical delineation and are particularly valuable for characterizing the relationship of abnormal cardiac structures to surrounding tissues. Right heart catheterization remains a valuable invasive tool for confirming VSR through demonstration of step-up in intracardiac oxygen saturation and for quantifying shunt magnitude via Qp/Qs calculation. In the case we presented, the 13% step-up in oxygen saturation from the right atrium to RV indicated there was mixing of oxygenated blood at the level of RV. The Qp/Qs ratio is calculated by obtaining blood samples from the aorta, superior vena cava, inferior vena cava, pulmonary artery, and pulmonary vein for oxygen saturation measurement. A ratio of greater than 1.0 signifies the presence of left-to-right shunt, and a ratio of greater than 1.5 indicates hemodynamically significant shunting. In our institute, we do not routinely advance a catheter to the pulmonary vein for blood sampling. Instead, after measuring the wedge pressure, a blood sample was aspirated while maintaining the catheter in place. This was an indirect representation of pulmonary vein oxygen saturation.

Urgent surgical repair in conjunction with coronary artery bypass grafting is the traditional standard of care for mechanical complication of AMI. Without intervention, the mortality rates for AMI-related VSR and LVP are 94% and 48%, respectively.5,7 Uniquely, this case involved an RCA myocardial infarction leading to an LVP, which remained stable for over a year, likely due to the decompression via shunting into the RV. Recent advances in tools and procedural experiences have expanded the role of transcatheter closure in selected VSR cases, with a reported success rate up to 89%.5,8 Nevertheless, evidence of transcatheter repair of LVP remains limited compared to VSR.9 Some case reports have described simultaneous occurrence of VSR and LVP requiring early surgical intervention shortly after infarction.10, 11, 12 To date, there are no published reports documenting simultaneous percutaneous closure of both VSR and LVP, underscoring the rarity and complexity of such presentations.

Conclusion

Delayed presentation of AMI significantly increases the risk of mechanical complications. A comprehensive and patient-centered assessment with multimodality imaging is critical for accurate diagnosis and heart team decision-making. This rare coexistence of VSR and LVP following inferior AMI underscores the unique physiological adaptations that can mitigate catastrophic outcomes such as cardiac tamponade or sudden death, illustrating the resilience of life.
