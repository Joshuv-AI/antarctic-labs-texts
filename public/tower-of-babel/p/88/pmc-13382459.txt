Table salt as a salivary stimulant and buffer substitute in calf diets: Effects on growth performance, rumen fermentation, and economics
Authors: A Pormohammad, M Chaji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated sodium chloride (NaCl) as a natural salivary stimulant and a substitute for conventional buffers in calf diets. We hypothesized that varying the amount and method of NaCl provision would modulate salivation, rumen fermentation, and growth performance. Fifty Holstein calves (46.43 ± 2.19 kg BW; 10 ± 5 days old) were assigned to five treatments in a completely randomized design: T1 (Control, NRC-salt mixed in feed); T2 (NRC-salt, 15% pre-fed); T3 (2 × NRC-salt, mixed); T4 (2 × NRC-salt, 15% pre-fed); T5 (NRC-salt + 0.5% NaHCO₃ buffer). Treatments significantly (P < 0.05) affected dry matter intake (DMI), feed conversion ratio (FCR), nutrient digestibility, saliva and rumen pH, and volatile fatty acid (VFA) profiles. Although T5 (buffer) promoted the highest saliva volume and DMI, T4 elicited the highest saliva pH and the most favorable FCR, owing to enhanced salivary buffering capacity and a shift in rumen fermentation toward greater propionate production, despite a lower ruminal pH. Blood parameters remained unaffected across treatments. Health indicators improved in all salt-supplemented groups, with T4 showing the lowest fecal score. An economic analysis projected annual savings of approximately $5,593 for a 5,000-head herd when replacing NaHCO₃ with the T4 NaCl strategy. We conclude that strategic supplementation with NaCl (specifically at twice the NRC level with a portion pre-fed) can effectively enhance salivary buffering, improve feed efficiency, and serve as a cost-effective natural alternative to chemical buffers in pre-weaned calf nutrition.Keywords: NaCl, Calf saliva, Feed conversion ratio, Rumen acidosis, Rumen pH

Introduction

To meet the energy demands of rapidly growing calves, diets high in fermentable starch and low in effective fiber are commonly fed. However, these diets can alter rumen fermentation patterns and volatile fatty acid (VFA) production. The accumulation of fermentation acids, particularly lactic acid, reduces rumen pH and increases the risk of subacute rumen acidosis (SARA). Subacute rumen acidosis is a metabolic disorder characterized by prolonged periods of moderately depressed rumen pH resulting from rapid fermentation of highly fermentable carbohydrates and insufficient ruminal buffering capacity. In the present study, SARA was defined as a rumen pH below 5.5 for more than 3 hours per day. This condition disrupts rumen microbial balance, impairs fiber digestion, and ultimately leads to reduced growth, poor feed efficiency, increased digestive disorders, and potentially death (Quille et al., 2024). To mitigate this risk, buffering agents such as sodium bicarbonate are often included in calf starter diets in large-scale operations (COOP, 2023; Kansas State University, 2023). As a standard component of starter diets (COOP, 2023; Kansas State University, 2023), sodium chloride (NaCl) supplies essential sodium and chloride. Salt can stimulate salivation through activation of oral salt taste receptors. Dissolved Na⁺ ions enter epithelial sodium channels (ENaC) in taste receptor cells located on the tongue, leading to depolarization and transmission of neural signals via the facial and glossopharyngeal nerves to the nucleus of the solitary tract in the brainstem. This center activates the salivatory nuclei, which increase parasympathetic output to the major salivary glands, thereby enhancing salivary secretion. Recent studies suggest that higher salt levels may stimulate feed acceptance and increase chewing time in calves, potentially boosting salivary flow and buffering capacity (Khairy et al., 2022; Baker et al., 2021). The salivary glands play a key role in secreting bicarbonate and phosphate, which are critical for maintaining a stable rumen environment. Beyond its nutritional role, sodium is also involved in regulating ion transport processes in salivary gland epithelial cells. Changes in systemic sodium availability can influence Na⁺ transport mechanisms and salivary electrolyte composition, potentially affecting the buffering capacity of saliva entering the rumen. However, evidence is inconsistent, as others have reported that elevating salt levels, particularly beyond nutritional requirements, does not significantly alter salivary secretion volume (Hough et al., 2023). Moreover, the timing of salt provision may be important. Offering a portion of salt before the main ration could stimulate salivation and chewing activity prior to concentrate intake, thereby increasing the delivery of alkaline saliva to the rumen at the onset of fermentation and improving ruminal buffering dynamics. Therefore, further investigation into the effects of salt on saliva composition and volume in dairy calves, especially under practical conditions, is warranted. Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of varying the amount and method of NaCl provision—as a salivary stimulant and natural alternative to conventional buffers—on the growth and performance of Holstein calves.

Materials and methods

The experiment was conducted over 54 days at the Neginfam Khuzestan commercial dairy complex, located 10 km from Izeh city (latitude 31°41′N, longitude 49°51′E), and Agricultural Sciences and Natural Resources University of Khuzestan.

Animals and experimental treatments

Fifty female Holstein calves aged 4 to 19 days (mean ± SD: 12 ± 7 days) were used and randomly allocated to five experimental treatments (n = 10 calves per treatment). Postpartum care included monitoring of vital signs, feeding of colostrum at 38°C, umbilical disinfection, and treatment of any superficial wounds with chlortetracycline spray (Zabransky et al., 2023). Calves were housed in individual metal pens (1.8 m × 1.1 m × 1.2 m) in a semi-open barn until 54 days of age. Colostrum was fed within 2 h of birth, with two subsequent meals of 3–4 L per day. From day 3 onwards, calves received 6–7 L of whole milk daily in two meals, adjusted to 10% of live body weight (Azevedo et al., 2023). Clean drinking water was available ad libitum. Starter feed was offered according to NRC, 2021 recommendations (Table 1).

Calves were randomly assigned to one of five dietary treatments upon entry to the calf facility, ensuring similar distribution of age among treatments. Calves were not blocked by arrival date. The NRC-recommended salt level was defined as 0.5% of the starter diet (DM basis), and the high-salt treatments received 1.0% salt (twice the NRC recommendation). The treatments were: 1) T1 (Control; NRCmix): starter feed containing the NRC-recommended salt level (0.5% NaCl); 2) T2 (NRCS15): 15% of the daily NRC salt requirement (total = 0.5% NaCl) offered before feeding for saliva stimulation, with the remainder mixed in the feed; 3) T3 (2 × NRCmix): salt provided at twice the NRC-recommended level (1.0% NaCl), mixed in the feed; 4) T4 (2 × NRCS15): twice the NRC-recommended salt level (1.0% NaCl), with 15% offered before feeding; 5) T5 (NRCmix+B): NRC-recommended salt level (0.5% NaCl) plus 0.5% sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO₃) as a buffer.

The basal starter diet was formulated to meet or exceed the nutrient requirements of pre-weaned dairy calves according to NRC, 2021. The starter was offered fresh each morning, and feed refusals were recorded daily before the next feeding. The chemical composition and ingredient profile of the basal starter diet are presented in Table 1. Salt and sodium bicarbonate treatments were incorporated into the starter feed according to the experimental design to ensure uniform intake across treatments. In treatments with pre-feeding salt (NRCS15 and 2 × NRCS15), 15% of the assigned daily NaCl amount was offered separately immediately before the morning milk meal to stimulate salivation. The pre-feeding salt portion was accurately weighed using a digital scale with a precision of 0.01 g and manually placed into the calf’s mouth to ensure complete consumption. The remaining 85% was thoroughly mixed with the daily starter allowance. Calves were weighed on a digital scale at four time points: upon transfer from the maternity pen (≥2 h post-birth), and on days 14, 21, and 54 (at transfer to group pens) (Zabransky et al., 2023). No mortality or twinning occurred during the study.

Digestibility

Apparent total-tract digestibility was determined using acid insoluble ash (AIA) as an internal marker. Fecal samples were collected from all calves over days 34–39, approximately 2 h before and 4 h after the morning feeding, and pooled per calf. Sampling at these time points was intended to account for potential diurnal variation in marker excretion and to obtain representative samples across the feeding cycle when using AIA as an internal marker. Samples were oven-dried at 55°C for 48 h, ground through a 1-mm screen, and stored at –20°C for analysis (Ji et al., 2023).

The chemical composition of feed, feed residues, and feces was analyzed for: crude protein (Method 990.03), ether extract (Method 954.02), ash (Method 942.05) (AOAC, 2012), acid detergent fiber (ADFom; Method 973.18) (AOAC, 2012), and neutral detergent fiber (NDFom; Van Soest et al., 1991). AIA content was determined in feed and fecal samples and used as an internal marker to calculate apparent digestibility coefficients for the total gastrointestinal tract according to recent updates on internal marker methodology (Arbaoui and de Vega, 2025). Digestibility was calculated based on the dry matter of the starter feed consumed; milk intake was excluded from these calculations (Ghorbani et al., 2020).

Saliva collection and analysis

Salivary secretion was quantified on the final day of the experiment (day 54, prior to the morning feeding) following the method of Srinivas et al. (2017). Saliva samples were collected at the same time of day for all calves, prior to the morning feeding, to minimize potential diurnal variation in salivary secretion and salivary pH associated with feeding activity. Calves' mouths were rinsed with distilled water. A pre-weighed sterile non-woven pad (Aesop®, China) was placed sublingually using forceps for 60 seconds. The pad was then removed, immediately weighed, and the procedure was repeated twice more per calf. The mean increase in pad weight was recorded as the estimated salivary secretion rate (g/min). Salivary pH was measured immediately using a calibrated pH meter (WTW 3110, Germany).

Body weight, feed intake, and feed conversion ratio (FCR)

Body weights were recorded at the four time points described above. Daily starter feed intake was calculated as the difference between feed offered and orts after 24 h. Average daily dry matter intake (DMI) was determined by dividing total dry matter consumed during the experimental period by 54 days. Average daily gain (ADG) was calculated as the change in body weight between consecutive weighings divided by the number of days in the interval. The feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated as DMI divided by ADG (Arbaoui et al., 2025).

Blood sampling and analysis

To avoid potential interference with other final sampling procedures and minimize additional stress to the calves at the end of the experiment, blood sampling was conducted on day 51. At 3 h post-morning feeding, blood samples were collected from the jugular vein into heparinized vacuum tubes. Plasma was separated by centrifugation (3,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C) and stored at –20°C.

Plasma concentrations of albumin (ALB), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), glucose (GLU), triglycerides (TG), cholesterol (CHO), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), urea, and uric acid were analyzed photometrically using commercial diagnostic kits (Pars Azmoon Co. and Biochem Diagnostics, Tehran, Iran) and an automatic biochemical analyzer (Mindray BS 200, China).

Rumen fermentation parameters

On day 45, approximately 3 h after the morning feeding, ruminal fluid was collected via stomach tube. This sampling time was selected to capture the active phase of ruminal fermentation, when volatile fatty acid production is typically elevated and ruminal pH reflects post-prandial fermentation dynamics. A single ruminal fluid sample was collected from each calf at this sampling time. The top portion of the ruminal fluid was discarded to minimize saliva contamination, and pH was measured immediately with a digital pH meter (WTW 3110, Germany).

For ammonia nitrogen (NH₃-N) analysis, 5 mL of strained ruminal fluid was mixed with 5 mL of 0.2 M HCl and stored at –20°C. Concentration was determined using the phenol–hypochlorite method (Broderick and Kang, 1980) with a spectrophotometer (Bio Rad, Libra S22, UK), as detailed by Coelho et al. (2025).

For volatile fatty acid (VFA) analysis, 1 mL of 10% (w/v) metaphosphoric acid (Merck, Germany) was added to 5 mL of strained ruminal fluid, and samples were stored at –20°C. Concentrations of acetate, propionate, butyrate, valerate, isobutyrate, isovalerate, and caproate were analyzed by gas chromatography (Agilent 6890N, USA) following Zhang et al. (2024).

Calf health indicators

Health was assessed daily via fecal score, and at regular 3–4-day intervals via rectal temperature and heart rate (House, 2019).

Fecal score: Consistency was scored by a trained observer using a 5-point system (1 = very firm/dry; 2 = relatively firm/formed; 3 = pasty/semi-loose; 4 = loose/watery; 5 = very watery). A score ≥4 was classified as diarrhea (Zhang et al., 2023).

Rectal temperature: Measured at regular intervals (every 3 to 4 days) with a digital thermometer. Fever was defined as >40.0°C (normal range: 38.5–39.5°C).

Heart rate: Measured by auscultation over 60 seconds. Values >140 beats per minute were considered indicative of potential stress or illness (House, 2019).

Results

Feed intake

The effects of the experimental treatments on milk and dry matter intake (DMI) are presented in Table 2. Treatments significantly affected starter DMI (P < 0.05). The highest starter DMI, as well as total DMI (starter + milk), was observed in T5 (NRC-level NaCl + 0.5% NaHCO₃). In contrast, the lowest total DMI was recorded for T4 (2 × NRC NaCl with 15% pre-feeding). Supplementation with NaCl at twice the NRC recommendation (T3) significantly increased DMI compared to the control (T1). However, stimulating salivation via NaCl pre-feeding in T2 and T4 did not significantly affect DMI compared to the control. This pattern suggests that the response of calves to salt supplementation may depend not only on the dietary level of NaCl but also on the method of its provision. Pre-feeding NaCl prior to the morning meal may alter short-term feeding behavior or salivary responses, which could influence starter intake differently from when salt is directly incorporated into the diet. However, because all calves had equal ad libitum access to the same starter diet, comparisons of starter DMI among treatments remained valid.

Growth performance

The experimental treatments significantly affected calf growth performance parameters, including average daily gain (ADG), final body weight, total weight gain, and feed conversion ratio (FCR) (P < 0.05). Calves in T4 (2 × NRC NaCl with 15% pre-feeding) exhibited the most favorable (lowest) FCR among all treatments (P < 0.05).

For T4, ADG, final body weight, and total weight gain were significantly higher than in the control (T1) and T2 (NRC-level NaCl with pre-feeding) (P < 0.05). Differences in final body weight and ADG between T4 and T3 (2 × NRC NaCl) or T5 (NaHCO₃ buffer) were numerical but not statistically significant. However, differences in FCR and total weight gain between T4 and these treatments (T3, T5) were statistically significant (P < 0.05).

Notably, the T4 treatment (2 × NRC NaCl with 15% pre-feeding) resulted in the greatest improvement in feed efficiency, reducing FCR by 22.63% compared with the industry-standard buffer treatment (T5). Although differences in ADG between T4 and T3 or T5 were not statistically significant, calves in T4 achieved comparable growth performance with lower total feed intake, indicating improved efficiency of nutrient utilization.

Apparent nutrient digestibility

Treatments significantly affected apparent total-tract nutrient digestibility (P < 0.05; Table 4). Inclusion of double-level NaCl (T3, T4) and the NaHCO₃ buffer (T5) increased nutrient digestibility, with the buffer treatment showing the highest mean values (P < 0.05). Compared to the control, pre-feeding NaCl at the NRC level (T2) decreased nutrient digestibility, whereas pre-feeding at the double level (T4) improved the digestibility of all evaluated nutrients.

The contrasting responses between T2 and T4 suggest that the effect of NaCl pre-feeding on nutrient digestibility may depend on the level of salt supplied. While pre-feeding NaCl at the NRC level (T2) slightly reduced digestibility, increasing the level to twice the NRC recommendation (T4) improved digestibility of all evaluated nutrients, indicating a potential dose-dependent response. As expected, the buffer treatment (T5) showed the highest digestibility values, consistent with the known role of sodium bicarbonate in stabilizing rumen fermentation. However, the improvement observed in T4 suggests that higher NaCl supplementation combined with pre-feeding may partially mimic some functional effects of buffering strategies.

Saliva volume and pH

Treatments significantly affected both saliva volume and pH (P < 0.05). The estimated mean daily saliva secretion ranged from 46.10 to 50.20 L/day, with the highest value in T5 and the lowest in T4 (Table 5). No significant difference was found between the buffer treatment (T5) and the control (T1). Inclusion of double-level salt (T3) and pre-feeding salt (T2, T4) reduced saliva volume compared to the control, while the buffer (T5) increased it, resulting in the highest secretion rate (P < 0.05).

The lowest saliva pH was observed in the control (T1), while the highest value occurred in T4 (P < 0.05). The addition of double-level NaCl (T3, T4), pre-feeding NaCl (T2, T4), and use of the buffer (T5) all significantly increased saliva pH compared to the control (P < 0.05). The highest pH was recorded in T4, which differed significantly from all other treatments (P < 0.05).

An important mechanistic finding was observed in T4, which showed the lowest estimated saliva volume but the highest saliva pH among treatments. Although this pattern may initially appear contradictory, it is physiologically plausible because saliva alkalinity in ruminants depends largely on its ionic composition rather than on secretion volume alone. Pre-feeding NaCl at the higher level in T4 likely increased systemic sodium availability, which can stimulate the secretion of sodium and associated bicarbonate ions from the salivary gland ductal cells. Because bicarbonate is the principal buffering component of ruminant saliva, an increase in its concentration can elevate saliva pH even when the total saliva output is reduced. Consequently, the saliva produced in T4 may have contained a higher concentration of alkaline buffering ions, particularly Na⁺ and HCO₃⁻, resulting in a more alkaline secretion despite the lower estimated volume. This indicates that the buffering-related response observed in T4 was driven more by changes in salivary ionic composition and buffering capacity than by saliva quantity alone. The observed saliva volume and pH values remained within physiologically plausible ranges for young calves.

Ruminal fermentation parameters

Treatments significantly affected ruminal fluid pH and ammonia nitrogen (NH₃-N) concentration (P < 0.05). The highest ruminal pH was observed in the control (T1), while the lowest occurred in T4 (Table 6). Increasing salt to double the NRC level (T3) alone did not affect ruminal pH compared to the control. However, when combined with pre-feeding (T4), ruminal pH decreased significantly (P < 0.05). Ruminal pH in T4, T3, and the buffer treatment (T5) did not differ significantly from each other, but all were significantly lower than the control (P < 0.05).

Discussion

Feed intake

Increasing the dietary salt level did not uniformly affect feed intake, as the response was significantly influenced by the method of provision. When salt was doubled and mixed into the starter (T3, 2NRCmix), both starter and total dry matter intake (DMI) increased significantly compared to the control. In contrast, T4 (double salt with pre-feeding) resulted in reduced starter DMI.

This disparity may be partly explained by the physiological regulation of intake depending on the mode of salt consumption. Consuming salt separately within a short time frame may transiently increase the osmolality of the upper gastrointestinal contents. Such transient changes could stimulate osmolality- and sodium-sensitive receptors and influence hypothalamic pathways involved in appetite and thirst regulation, which may have contributed to the short-term reduction in DMI observed in T4 (Allen et al., 2019). However, osmolality was not directly measured in the present study; therefore, this interpretation should be considered a plausible physiological explanation rather than a confirmed mechanism.

Conversely, uniform inclusion of salt in the diet (T3) prevents acute osmotic fluctuations. The absence of an inhibitory effect, coupled with the observed intake increase, may be attributed to improved dietary homogeneity, enhanced palatability from a more stable digestive environment, and/or indirect stimulation of water consumption. Avoiding sudden spikes in osmolality prevents acute compensatory thirst, promoting a regulated water intake pattern concurrent with feeding and creating a favorable physiological milieu for continuous starter consumption (Gizowski and Bourque, 2018). These findings align with reports that behavioral and nutritional responses to sodium depend more on the pattern of provision than on absolute dietary level (Suttle, 2022; Naveen et al., 2024). Thus, the method of mixing salt with feed is a key factor modulating its effect on calf DMI. The highest total DMI was observed in T5 (NRCmix+B), primarily due to increased starter intake. Rapid organic acid production from starter consumption can lower rumen pH, stimulating acidity-sensitive chemoreceptors and initiating vagal-mediated satiety signals that curb further intake (Allen et al., 2019). The added buffer likely minimized pH depression, attenuating these feedback signals and enabling more sustained daily consumption. Despite reduced DMI in T4, the significant improvement in feed conversion ratio (FCR) indicates that intake alone does not dictate performance. The altered salt provision in T4, while constraining short-term intake, appears to have modulated the ruminal environment, shifting fermentation toward greater propionate production and enhancing energy utilization efficiency. This suggests improved calf growth may depend more on metabolic response quality and utilization efficiency than on absolute DMI (Pishdadi et al., 2024).

Growth efficiency

The improved FCR in T4, despite lower intake, indicates greater retention of net energy for growth. This response may have resulted, at least partly, from complementary effects of supplemental sodium on ruminal fermentation pattern, glucogenic precursor supply, and systemic electrolyte homeostasis. Importantly, this interpretation should not be taken to indicate that the NRC sodium recommendation is generally inadequate, but rather that sodium supplementation elicited a functional performance response under the conditions of the present calf model. First, sodium may have contributed to extracellular fluid regulation, osmotic balance, and systemic acid–base homeostasis, which are physiologically important in rapidly growing preweaned calves. Because sodium retention, renal electrolyte excretion, and systemic acid–base variables were not directly measured, the contribution of these processes to improved growth efficiency should be interpreted cautiously.

Second, enhanced electrolyte stability may reduce the activity of ATP-consuming ion pumps (e.g., Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase, Na⁺/H⁺ exchangers), thereby diminishing the metabolic costs associated with maintenance and allowing more metabolizable energy to be allocated toward growth (White et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2023; Proctor & Shaalan, 2021). However, ion pump activity and ATP expenditure were not directly measured in the present study; therefore, this explanation should be considered a possible physiological interpretation rather than a confirmed mechanism. Nevertheless, the relationship between electrolyte balance and performance indices remains variable across studies (NRC, 2021).

Supplemental sodium may also have contributed to improved growth efficiency by modifying ruminal fermentation rather than by improving ruminal buffering. The elevated salt level shifted fermentation toward greater production of glucogenic propionate. From a growth perspective, this provides a more stable and readily utilizable energy source, as glucose derived from propionate via gluconeogenesis supports muscle growth and protein accretion (Pishdadi et al., 2024). Although a shift toward glucogenic products does not invariably improve performance (NRC, 2021), propionate is the primary gluconeogenic precursor in ruminants. Therefore, an increased propionate proportion reflects enhanced availability of metabolizable energy for growth, aligning with the demands of growing calves. The observed changes in volatile fatty acid (VFA) concentrations and acetate-to-propionate ratio in T4 may thus partially explain the superior growth efficiency and FCR (Pishdadi et al., 2024). In addition, a higher ruminal propionate concentration may have increased the concentration gradient for propionate absorption across the ruminal epithelium. Propionate can be absorbed through multiple pathways, including passive diffusion of the undissociated acid and carrier-mediated monocarboxylate transport mechanisms. Therefore, the more propionic fermentation profile observed in T4 may have increased propionate flux into the portal circulation, thereby providing additional glucogenic substrate for hepatic gluconeogenesis. However, monocarboxylate transporter activity, epithelial intracellular pH regulation, and the actual rate of propionate absorption were not measured; thus, this mechanism should be considered a plausible physiological explanation rather than a confirmed causal pathway.

Nutrient digestibility

The combination of double salt inclusion and pre-feeding (T4) increased nutrient digestibility, whereas pre-feeding salt at the NRC level (T2) had an inhibitory effect. Enhanced digestibility at higher salt levels can be attributed to an improved ruminal ionic environment and microbial enzyme activity. Incorporating more salt directly into the diet delivers more sodium and chloride into the rumen, stabilizing ruminal fluid osmolality and ion concentration. This ionic stability creates a more favorable physiological condition for microorganisms, facilitating substrate uptake, supporting extracellular enzyme activity, and promoting effective microbial growth. Consequently, microbes degrade cell wall components, particularly fibrous fractions, more efficiently (Niepes & Bestil, 2023; Liu et al., 2023). This mechanism also explains the reduced digestibility in T2, the lower amount of pre-fed salt may not have been sufficient to establish the same level of ionic stability achieved in T4. Because both T2 and T4 involved pre-feeding but differed in salt quantity, the results suggest that a higher sodium input may be required to reach a threshold at which ruminal ionic conditions become sufficiently stable to support optimal microbial activity.

It should be noted that altering dietary sodium does not always significantly impair ruminal fermentation and digestibility; responses depend on the range of sodium variation and the prevailing feeding regimen (NRC, 2021).

Saliva volume and pH

The reduction in salivary secretion volume, particularly in T4, was likely due to enhanced active Na⁺ reabsorption in the salivary glands, lowering ductal osmolality. This diminishes the osmotic gradient for water movement from plasma into the ducts, reducing salivary volume while increasing ionic concentration and buffering capacity. Previous studies support this mechanism. Ricci et al. (2021) demonstrated that alterations in salivary gland stimulation and secretion rate in cattle can modify salivary osmolality and bicarbonate/phosphate concentrations, influencing pH and buffering capacity. Similarly, Castillo-Lopez et al. (2021) showed that ruminant salivary composition depends strongly on secretion rate and ductal ionic reabsorption, with reduced flow often associated with a relative increase in ionic and buffering constituents.

A noteworthy finding was the higher salivary pH observed in T4 (pH 10.15) despite reduced volume. This pattern provides clear evidence of enhanced buffering capacity and corroborates the mechanistic interpretations of Ricci et al. (2021) and Castillo-Lopez et al. (2021). Typically, reduced chewing and rumination decrease salivary secretion and bicarbonate/phosphate delivery, impairing ruminal buffering (Beauchemin, 2018). The contrasting response in T4 indicates a mechanism-driven adaptation distinct from this conventional pattern.

Ruminal fermentation parameters

The reduced ruminal pH in T4 appears to be associated with a more intensive fermentation pattern rather than improved ruminal buffering. Supplying sodium above the NRC recommendation, particularly when part of the sodium was provided before feeding, may have increased osmotic stimulation and encouraged water intake before or around starter consumption. This could have increased ruminal liquid availability and improved ruminal fluidity during the early phase of starter fermentation. Higher salt intake stimulates water consumption, increasing rumen fluid volume, accelerating dilution and outflow, and reducing feed particle retention time. Such shifts in rumen kinetics favor rapid fermentation pathways, contributing to lower pH (White et al., 2024; Singer et al., 2024). However, rumen liquid volume and dilution rate were not directly measured in the present study; therefore, this explanation should be considered a hypothesis requiring confirmation in future experiments that directly quantify rumen fluid dynamics. Nonetheless, ruminal pH does not respond linearly to changes in volume or fluid dynamics, as adaptive mechanisms such as increased VFA absorption and bicarbonate secretion by the rumen epithelium can mitigate acidifying effects (Baaske et al., 2020). Although salivary pH was higher in T4, salivary secretion volume was reduced; therefore, the lower ruminal pH cannot be simply interpreted as evidence of greater salivary buffering input. Rather, the lower pH in T4 likely reflects greater fermentation intensity and increased production of fermentation acids, particularly propionate, from rapidly fermentable starter carbohydrates.

The increased propionate concentration and decreased acetate-to-propionate ratio indicate a shift toward glucogenic fermentation. As propionate is the primary precursor for hepatic gluconeogenesis, this altered profile may partially explain the improved growth performance and FCR in T4 (Table 3). A more propionogenic pattern provides a stable, efficient energy source for tissue accretion (Pishdadi et al., 2024). Thus, the fermentation shift induced by higher dietary salt—potentially mediated by through? improved ruminal fluidity, greater hydration and dispersion of starter particles, and increased microbial access to rapidly fermentable non-fiber carbohydrates such as starch and soluble sugars—may be a key mechanism underlying the enhanced growth efficiency in T4. Because fermentation of non-fiber carbohydrates generally yields proportionally more propionate than fiber fermentation, this mechanism provides a plausible explanation for the higher propionate concentration and lower acetate-to-propionate ratio observed in T4. However, water intake, ruminal liquid volume, starter particle hydration, dilution rate, and propionate absorption rate were not directly measured; therefore, these proposed pathways should be interpreted as plausible physiological explanations rather than confirmed causal mechanisms.

Blood parameters

The absence of significant changes in blood indices indicates that metabolic fluctuations induced by different dietary salt levels remained within a safe physiological range. The tendency toward increased blood lipid concentrations with higher salt intake is consistent with previously reported alterations in fluid and electrolyte homeostasis (Bernal et al., 2023). Although this trend was not statistically significant, it may warrant monitoring in longer-term studies to determine whether sustained exposure to elevated dietary salt could influence lipid metabolism over extended periods. The maintenance of all parameters within normal ranges confirms that physiological adjustments were well-regulated and did not impose adverse metabolic stress.

The lack of significant differences may be attributed to adaptive mechanisms, including adequate water intake and effective blood homeostasis maintenance (NRC, 2021). All measured blood parameters were within the normal physiological ranges reported for healthy animals (Kaneko et al., 2008; Mohri et al., 2007; Aydogdu et al., 2018). These findings indicate that neither dietary salt level nor the pre-feeding strategy adversely affected metabolic status, liver function, or lipid metabolism. Overall, dietary salt level and pre-feeding had no significant effect on blood indices.

Fecal score and health status

Reduced fecal scores and diarrhea incidence in salt-supplemented groups can be attributed to sodium's role in modulating intestinal osmotic pressure and stabilizing water-electrolyte balance. Increased luminal Na⁺ availability enhances the activity of sodium-coupled co-transporters (e.g., for glucose, amino acids) in enterocytes. This active sodium absorption facilitates osmotic water movement from the lumen into the interstitial space and cells, resulting in more solid intestinal contents, improved fecal consistency, and lower scores. Furthermore, maintaining stable concentrations of key ions (especially sodium and chloride) in the intestine reduces electrolyte fluctuations and lowers the risk of osmotic diarrhea (Nicola et al., 2023). Other studies confirm a negative association between salivary pH and diarrhea incidence (Riley et al., 2024), supporting the link between improved electrolyte homeostasis and enhanced gut health in calves.

Economic analysis

The marked improvement in FCR in the double-salt treatment with pre-feeding (T4) substantially reduced the production cost per kilogram of weight gain. This outcome, achieved without altering the base diet price by simply substituting salt for buffer, resulted in considerable economic savings at an industrial scale. This supports the concept that enhancing energy utilization efficiency—even without increased feed intake—can have a pronounced positive economic impact. However, the magnitude of economic benefit may vary depending on regional feed and salt prices, management conditions, and calf population dynamics. Therefore, individual farms may benefit from conducting their own cost-benefit analyses before implementing similar feeding strategies under commercial conditions.

Conclusion

The results demonstrate that managing both the level and mode of sodium chloride supplementation can function as a natural salivary stimulant and a practical alternative to conventional buffers such as sodium bicarbonate. Although salt supplementation reduced total daily saliva secretion, it increased salivary pH, indicating improved buffering capacity. Specifically, T4 (twice the NRC salt with 15% pre-fed) improved ruminal fermentation patterns, particularly by reducing the acetate-to-propionate ratio, and enhanced feed conversion efficiency and growth performance. Although ruminal pH decreased, the increased propionate production suggests a shift toward more efficient glucogenic fermentation rather than detrimental acidification. Furthermore, replacing sodium bicarbonate with salt maintained physiological and performance responses while improving economic efficiency at the herd level. Overall, optimizing the level and timing of salt supplementation may provide a practical and cost-effective strategy to support rumen fermentation efficiency and growth performance in milk-fed calves
