Restrictive abortion policy climate is associated with increased depression symptoms among women in the United States: Findings from a 25-year longitudinal study
Authors: Sarah McKetta, Catherine Gimbrone, Julie Maslowsky, Kara E Rudolph, Sara K Redd, Katherine Keyes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose:To examine associations between state-level abortion restrictiveness and depression among female participants in a large, US sample surveyed from 1990 to 2015.Methods:Eligible participants were female respondents in the Monitoring the Future panel study followed from adolescence into adulthood (N = 19,881 female respondents with 50,995 unique observations). Depression symptoms were assessed using a four-item index. State-level restrictive abortion policy climate was quantified using an annual index of 18 policies, standardized across states and years. We modeled associations using log-binomial multilevel models, adjusting for individual (e.g., race/ethnicity, age, urbanicity) and state-level (e.g., unemployment, income inequality, political and legislative composition, demographic composition, gender wage gap) confounders. We conducted tests to assess robustness of findings, including using a negative control outcome (motor vehicle crashes), alternative populations (male respondents), and subgroup tests by religiosity.Results:A one-standard deviation increase in state abortion restrictiveness (equivalent to approximately 4 more restrictive laws) was associated with a 7% increase in depression symptoms among females (adjusted risk ratio [RR] = 1.07, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.02–1.11). As hypothesized, associations among our alternative sample were not statistically significant (males, RR = 1.03, 95% CI 0.99–1.08, though estimates did not statistically differ by sex) as were associations with our negative control outcome (motor vehicle crashes). As hypothesized, associations were most pronounced among female respondents reporting low religiosity (RR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.01–1.15), and not significant among those reporting high religiosity.Conclusion:Restrictive abortion policies are associated with higher depression symptoms among women. Legal protections for reproductive health care may benefit mental health.Keywords: Mental health, Reproductive policy

Introduction

Access to abortion—the elective termination of a pregnancy via procedure or medication—is a key component of reproductive autonomy (Cates et al., 2003; Grimes et al., 2006; Upadhyay et al., 2015; Foster, 2021). Access to safe and affordable abortion has facilitated opportunities for individuals to advance their own education and career aspirations, as well as to control when (or if) they have children (Foster, 2021; Foster et al., 2018; Hyatt et al., 2022; Collective, 2018). Attempts to undermine abortion access have been described as a violation of human rights, reproductively coercive, and an affront to reproductive justice (Hyatt et al., 2022; Calkin et al., 2022; Ferree, 2002).

While abortion is a common (Jones et al., 2020) and safe (Berer, 2000; The Safety and Quality of, 2018; Raymond and Grimes, 2012) healthcare service that the majority of Americans support under most circumstances (Center, 2017; Saad, 2016; Public Opinion on Abortion, 2025), it has remained politically controversial and a frequent target of restrictive legislation in the United States. Federally, abortion was legally protected 1973–2022. In 2022, the Supreme Court overturned the federal constitutional right to abortion in Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization, returning the authority to determine abortion legality to states (Errigo, 2023). Many states then implemented total abortion bans; as of December 2025, 13 states ban abortion entirely (KFF, 2025). However, even in the decades when abortion was federally legal, state-level variation in restrictive legislation persisted and created substantial barriers for those seeking abortion (Joyce, 2011). Between 2011 and 2017, states passed over 400 restrictive abortion laws (Nash et al., 2018; Llamas et al., 2018) and by 2020 over half of women ages 15–44 (approximately reproductive age) lived in states with four or more restrictive abortion laws in effect. (Abortion statistics by state) Such restrictive legislation includes gestational limits; mandatory waiting periods between an intake appointment and an abortion; two-visit laws requiring patients to be in the clinic at least once prior to the procedure; parental involvement laws requiring notification of or consent from one or both parents for minors; mandatory ultrasounds; funding and insurance restrictions; and provider licensing barriers or burdensome building regulations (Kreitzer, 2015). As a result, an individual’s state of residence was, and remains, a crucial determinant of whether they can get an abortion, at which point in the pregnancy, and under what circumstances.

It is well-established, both within the US and globally, that restrictive abortion legislation worsens physical health, including higher rates of mortality (Grimes et al., 2006; Cates et al., 1978; Singh and Ratnam, 1998; Stevenson and Root, 2024; Singh and Gallo, 2024) and adverse birth outcomes (Redd et al., 2021, 2022). The mental health burden of restrictive abortion legislation, however, is still being understood. For pregnant people, living in a state with more restrictive abortion laws is associated with increased symptoms of depression and increased stress (McKetta et al., 2024; Hall et al., 2025). Some of these associations are mediated through pregnancy intention (McKetta et al., 2024), but even people with intended pregnancies are vulnerable: when faced with pregnancy complications or emergencies, pregnant people are more likely to experience care delays in places where abortion rights are limited (Fuentes et al., 2016; de Londras et al., 2022). These delays are major stressors and are associated with symptoms of stress, anxiety, and depression (Wasser et al., 2024). People living in states that limit public funding for abortions are more likely to experience postpartum depression (Medoff, 2014), and depression symptoms during the perinatal period increase the risk of suicide (Slomian et al., 2019). Indeed, ecological studies have demonstrated that reproductive-aged women in states with more restrictive abortion climates are likelier to die by suicide than those in more supportive climates (Zandberg et al., 2022).

Although prior studies examining the mental health consequences of restrictive abortion legislation have primarily focused on pregnant individuals and the experience of being denied abortion (Foster, 2021; Masten et al., 2024; Miller et al., 2020), restrictive abortion legislation may impact health more broadly. For example, two studies using the Household Pulse Survey (Anderson et al., 2024; Mukhopadhyay, 2024) demonstrated that abortion restrictions instituted after the Dobbs decision were associated with increases in symptoms of anxiety and depression among a broad sample of US residents, not just reproductive-aged women; therefore, being denied an abortion may not be the only mechanism mediating associations between restrictive policies and adverse mental health. A vast literature examining the experience of people who are socially marginalized has demonstrated that laws and policies curtailing their civil rights—also known as structural stigma—impact them not only through directly limiting their access to services, but also indirectly, because the presence of such laws represent a hostile, discriminatory social climate (Hatzenbuehler et al., 2024; Lattanner et al., 2025; Link and Hatzenbuehler, 2016). People targeted by hostile and discriminatory climates experience adverse psychological processes (Hatzenbuehler, 2009), such as internalized discrimination (Hatzenbuehler, 2009; Mitchell et al., 2021), hypervigilance (Mitchell et al., 2021), and emotional dysregulation (Hatzenbuehler et al., 2009). These in turn lead to distress and adverse mental health, including depression (Lattanner et al., 2022). In the case of restrictive abortion legislation, which may be appraised by many women as hostile and discriminatory, these associations are likely heterogenous. Because some Americans—in particular, highly religious Americans (Public Opinion on Abortion, 2025; Newport, 2022)—support abortion restrictions, they are unlikely to appraise restrictions as legally obstructive, discriminatory, or hostile.

While pre-post analyses of abortion bans implemented after the Dobbs decision have shown negative associations with mental health, a more detailed understanding of the mental health burden of restrictive state-level abortion legislation over the past several decades has been methodologically complex. A major challenge is that restrictions are highly collinear and frequently passed as part of a legislative package that includes multiple restrictions enacted at once (Colman et al., 2011; Macinko and Silver, 2012); because of this, it is not always feasible to isolate and examine the impact of a single policy change. This is a limitation, because analyzing population mental health changes that may result from abortion legislation requires ruling out sources of confounding, which can bias the results. Where people live is not randomly assigned, and their state of residence determines not only their exposure to health policies (including abortion legislation) but also other meaningful state-level exposures, like state political composition (Kreitzer, 2015), which predict both enactment of restrictive abortion policies and population mental health (Bialer and McIntosh, 2017).

In the present study, we addressed prior methodological limitations by drawing on best practices from parallel literature examining the impact of policy climate on marginalized groups. For studies examining the mental health impact of hostile civil rights policy climates or structural stigma for minority groups (e.g., sexual, racial, ethnic minorities), a suite of tools has been used to address the same methodological barriers encountered here (Hatzenbuehler et al., 2024; Lattanner et al., 2025). A key approach to inference when quasi-experimental design choices are not available is the use of theoretically-motivated robustness testing, including negative controls. This approach is common in basic sciences; namely, experiments are conducted under conditions intended to produce null results, and verification of the null result strengthens the validity of primary study finding. In observational population health research, this can be done by demonstrating specificity of the hypothesized relationship in the sample and outcome of interest, and the absence of a relationship in a sample or outcome hypothesized to be unrelated to the exposure (Lipsitch et al., 2010; Shi et al., 2020; Weisskopf et al., 2016).

In summary, the present study examined the association between restrictive abortion policy climate and depression symptoms. We focus on depression symptoms due to their high prevalence in women of reproductive age (1 in 20 women) (Guo et al., 2018) and their strong association with suicidality (Riera-Serra et al., 2024), which has been linked with abortion restrictiveness (Zandberg et al., 2022). Our first aim was to estimate the association among women of reproductive age. We hypothesized a positive association. Our second aim was to demonstrate specificity of this association, theoretically supported by the multiple mechanisms described above. Guided by theoretically-motivated robustness testing, we hypothesized a) we would not observe an association between restrictive abortion policy climate and motor vehicle crashes (a negative control outcome); and b) that the association would be attenuated or absent or among specific subgroups who are less likely to appraise these laws as negatively impactful, i.e., men, and highly religious women. While prior research suggests that both men and women reported higher rates of adverse mental health symptoms after the Dobbs decision (Anderson et al., 2024; Mukhopadhyay, 2024), we hypothesized that any association would be less pronounced among men relative to women, given that restrictions are more salient to women’s experiences of both health-seeking and discrimination. Religiosity, in particular religious attendance (Newport, 2022), is a strong predictor of support for abortion restriction (Public Opinion on Abortion, 2025). While not every religious denomination has proscriptions against abortion, we chose to use religiosity to define strata for robustness testing because religiosity has consistently been shown to be associated with anti-abortion beliefs over many decades of polling (Public Opinion on Abortion, 2025; Newport, 2022; Hoffmann and Johnson, 2005; Harris and Mills, 1985; Barkan, 2014). We therefore hypothesized that associations between restrictive abortion policy climate and depression symptoms would be attenuated or be absent among more religious women, who are less likely to be opposed to abortion restrictions and therefore are less likely to perceive these laws as burdensome to their health needs or discriminatory against them as women.

Methods

We examined these associations among respondents in Monitoring the Future (MTF) panel study who were surveyed between 1990 and 2015 (Miech et al). The study years were chosen to align with our measure of abortion policy climate, described in detail below. MTF is a national, prospective cohort study of young people followed into adulthood. Respondents were initially surveyed their senior year of high school and followed through adulthood, with surveys administered every 2 years until age 30, and then every five years beginning at age 35. Recruitment is ongoing, and therefore respondents born later had fewer opportunities for follow-up. Data are available by request through the National Addiction & HIV Data Archive Program.

A subset of the respondents was queried about depression symptoms. Sex was ascertained at baseline and dichotomized as male or female. Gender was not queried consistently. We restricted the sample to those interviewed between ages 19–45, to align with other surveys and national statistics of reproductive aged people thought to be most impacted by abortion laws (Abortion statistics by state; Osterman et al., 2024; Thornburg et al., 2024). While older adults may also experience adverse mental health consequences as a result of abortion restrictions, prior literature suggests that people in mid-life are most impacted (Liu et al., 2023). The eligible sample of adult respondents surveyed between 1990 and 2015 at ages 19 through 45 who received questionnaires regarding depression symptoms and lived in the 50 United States (excluding Washington, DC) included 19,881 female respondents with 50,995 observations and 15,723 male respondents with 38,618 observations.

Outcome.

The outcome of interest was depression symptoms, measured using an index of four items (Keyes et al., 2019): “Life often seems meaningless,” “The future often seems hopeless,” “It feels good to be alive,” and “I enjoy life as much as anyone.” Responses were Likert-style, ranging from “Disagree” (1), “Mostly disagree” (2), “Neither” (3), “Mostly agree” (4), to “Agree” (5). The latter two items were reverse-coded so that higher scores corresponded to higher levels of depression symptoms and all 4 were summed to create a total score, with a possible range between 4 and 20. Reliability ranged from a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.79 to 0.85 across all years and waves using these indicators (McKetta et al., 2022). We examined depression scores measured continuously (henceforth, “depression score”) as well as dichotomously using a cut-off of 10 symptoms or more, which corresponded to the 87th percentile of symptoms among female and 84th percentile among male respondents (henceforth, “depression symptoms”). We took this approach to be consistent with prior literature using MTF data and to ease interpretability, given that there is no empirically validated cut-off score using this measure with these data (Keyes et al., 2019; Gimbrone et al., 2022).

Exposure.

We examined state-level restrictive abortion policy climate for each year of observation using a policy index of 18 restrictive abortion policies created by Redd et al., 2021, 2022 These were coded using policy data from National Association for the Repeal of Abortion Laws Pro-Choice America’s State Government Law and Policy databases and Temple University’s Abortion Law Project. Each law was cross validated across both sources, or (if not present in both sources) via original legal research using NexisUni. The policies included were 1) abortion facility licensing requirements; 2) bans on insurance coverage of abortion for state employees; 3) bans on insurance coverage of abortion in health exchange plans; 4) bans on insurance coverage of abortion in all private insurance plans; 5) bans on public funding of abortion; 6) gestational age limits; 7) hospitalization requirements; 8) medication abortion administration only by licensed physician; 9) medication abortion administration in physical presence of patient; 10) mandatory counseling requirements; 11) mandatory ultrasound requirements; 12) mandatory waiting period requirements; 13) “partial-birth” abortion bans; 14) parental involvement laws; 15) procedural abortion provision only by licensed physician; 16) physician hospital admitting privilege requirements; 17) provider refusal clauses; and 18) second physician requirements. The coded laws were only available through 2015.

The exposure was time varying. Policies were coded dichotomously for every state and year (“1” being present, “0” being absent) beginning with the year each policy was enacted. If a policy was enacted at any point in the year, it was coded as “1” for the year. Policies that were enjoined but ultimately declared unconstitutional were coded as “0” the year they were declared unconstitutional, but “1” for the duration of enjoinment after enactment, given that policy enactment may influence provider and patient behavior regardless of injunction status (Hatzenbuehler et al., 2017).

The number of policies in each state in each year was then summed into a count variable, ranging from 0 to 18. We take this approach for several reasons: first, to harmonize with prior research measuring overall restrictiveness abortion climate; and second, because there is no existing validated way to measures restrictive abortion legislation and therefore no consensus on the best approach to weighting various restrictive laws. Because laws are frequently passed together and highly collinear, we mean-standardized this index across all states and years such that a value of zero represents the average number of restrictive policies (approximately 6 laws), and a 1-unit increase represents a one standard deviation change in the index (approximately 4 laws), consistent with previous work (Redd et al., 2021, 2022). Therefore, a 1-unit increase represents a relatively more restrictive policy environment, which can happen one of two ways: when laws become more restrictive within a single state or when laws in other states became more permissive and but remain stable in one’s own state. This approach allows state policies to be comparable over years. The average number of laws for each state (summed across all 18 possible) is shown in Supplemental Fig. 1. In Supplemental Fig. 2, we show the number of states with each individual law over time. In Supplemental Fig. 3, we show the number of laws in each state over time. Within states, enactment of laws was largely stable or monotonically increasing (i.e., becoming more restrictive).

We linked these exposures to the state of residence for eligible respondents, which they reported at each survey. Participants lived in all 50 states, and 83% of them lived in the same state for the duration of the study. In sensitivity analyses, we examined associations only among people who never moved.

Approach to bias.

To account for potential confounding and the possibility of spurious associations between restrictive abortion policy climate and mental health, we used approaches consistent with best practices for examining associations between hostile policy climates and health outcomes in the groups targeted by those policies (Hatzenbuehler et al., 2024; Lattanner et al., 2025): confounding control and robustness tests (Lipsitch et al., 2010; Shi et al., 2020).

Confounding control.

First, we controlled for both state-level and individual-level confounders. Individual-level covariates included race/ethnicity, collected at baseline; because the sample is majority non-Hispanic White, we dichotomized race/ethnicity as non-Hispanic White or not. To account for individual urban-rural differences that influence both abortion availability (Chakraborty et al., 2022) and depression (Snell-Rood and Carpenter-Song, 2018), we controlled for urbanicity, operationalized as urban (a city with greater than 50,000 residents), suburban (living in a suburb of a city with greater than 50, 000 residents), or rural area/small town (living on a farm, in the country, or in a city with fewer than 50,000 residents) based on self-report at each survey. We additionally controlled for respondent age at each survey.

Regarding state-level control variables, we identified confounders that are common causes of both restrictive abortion legislation and depression. All state-level confounders were time-varying, corresponding to the year the respondents were surveyed. State-level economic deprivation influences both health policy (Miller, 2005) and depression (Hawton and Haw, 2013), so we controlled for the unemployment rate. We chose this specific economic measure, because it is also associated with abortion utilization (Gonzalez and Quast, 2022) and adverse mental health (Lo and Cheng, 2014). We additionally controlled for income inequality, operationalized as the Gini coefficient, a measure ranging from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality). We further controlled for state racial composition using two variables, percentage Black or African American and percentage White, given they represent the most common racial groups and were measured consistently across all US demographic surveys. State level economic and demographic indicators were made available from the American Community Survey and Current Population Survey.

State-level political composition is associated with both abortion policy enactment (Kreitzer, 2015; Medoff, 2002) and depression (Gimbrone et al., 2022). To control for these, we included a measure of the percentage of state residents who voted for the Republican candidate in the presidential election closest to the time of survey, using data from the MIT Election Lab. Because this is only assessed every four years, linear interpolation was used to singly impute the percentage of Republican voters for non-election years. The percent of state legislators who are male is a predictor of abortion policy implementation (Kreitzer, 2015) as well as a predictor of women’s depression (McLaughlin et al., 2011), which we therefore controlled for in adjusted models. These data were created by Rutgers University Center for American Women and Politics and made available by University of Michigan Institute for Public Policy and Social Research (Grossmann et al., 2021).

To isolate the impact of restrictive abortion legislation from other sources of sex-based inequality, we additionally controlled for the wage gap, calculated as the ratio between men’s and women’s median earnings for each state in each year, using data from the American Community Survey and Current Population Survey.

Robustness testing.

Next, we conducted several tests of the specificity of the associations – i.e., whether associations between restrictive abortion legislation and depressive symptoms are present for the groups that we hypothesize to be less affected, and whether restrictive abortion legislation is associated with an outcome that we hypothesize to be unrelated.

We first examined the association between restrictive abortion legislation and depression symptoms among men. We hypothesized that we would observe an attenuated or absent association between restrictive abortion policy climate and depression symptoms among male respondents. We next examined whether the association between restrictive abortion policy climate and depression symptoms was less pronounced among those least likely to have negative feelings about the policies, i.e., highly religious participants. We hypothesized that the association between restrictive abortion climate and depression symptoms would be absent among highly religious women. Religiosity was operationalized dichotomously; in response to the prompt “How important is religion in your life?” it was coded as low (“Not important,” “A little important”) or high (“Pretty important,” “Very Important”).

We next examined specificity using a negative control outcome (Lipsitch et al., 2010; Weisskopf et al., 2016): a health outcome not plausibly associated with restrictive abortion policy climate. If there were an association between restrictive abortion policy climate and our negative control outcome, this would be evidence suggesting the presence of residual confounding. Negative control outcomes should have a similar confounding structure as the primary outcome in relation to the exposure of interest but should themselves not be related to the exposure (Shi et al., 2020). In the present study, many other self-reported health behaviors or health outcomes would be inappropriate negative controls because, while they may have similar confounding structures, they are plausibly related to restrictive abortion legislation. We used motor vehicle crashes as a negative control outcome for several reasons. First, like depression, motor vehicle crashes vary by state (Moosavi et al., 2019) and are sensitive to other area-level features (Saeednejad et al., 2020) – like public resources and investment in health infrastructure (Dumbaugh et al; Noland, 2003), community norms (Factor et al., 2007), deprivation (Morency et al., 2012; Taylor et al., 2022), and other health policies (Evans, 1987; Naimi et al., 2018) – which are plausible confounders. Second, motor vehicle crashes should not be related to abortion policies. Next, this measure harmonizes with prior ecological work using motor vehicle deaths as a negative control outcome for suicides (Zandberg et al., 2022). Surveys querying motor vehicle crashes were given to a subset of participants at every follow up between the ages of 19 and 30. Participants were asked how many motor vehicle collisions occurred while they were driving in the past 12 months, with ordinal responses including none, one, two, three, or four or more further dichotomized to “none” vs. “any.”

Analytic approach.

We examined associations between restrictive abortion legislation and depression symptoms using 3-level multilevel model. For the dichotomous outcome, we used a log-binomial distribution, because we were interested in estimating risk ratios, and logistic models may overestimate the risk, given that depression is prevalent in this sample (Keyes et al., 2019). For the continuous outcome, we used a Poisson distribution. To account for repeated observations of individuals within states, we included a random intercept for each respondent nested within their state of residence at the time of each survey. Individual observations were level 1, participants were level 2, and states were level 3. For main associations among female participants we first examined models without any covariate adjustment, then with adjustment for state and individual level covariates, using main terms for all covariates with an interaction for age by race/ethnicity (based on public opinion polls showing major variation across those two demographic indicators (Public Opinion on Abortion, 2025)). To ensure no state-level covariates contributed to multicollinearity, we assessed variance inflation factor (criterion: >10) and tolerance (criterion: <0.1) (Tsagris and Pandis, 2021). No covariate met criteria for concern. All robustness tests were fully adjusted for covariates. Subgroup analyses with male participants and by religiosity were examined in stratified models. To test differences across sex and religiosity strata, respectively, Wald tests of interaction terms from non-stratified models were conducted and then evaluated on the additive scale. Analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 and figures were created in R.

Missing data.

The modal source of missing in MTF is attrition, so all observations were weighted using attrition weights (Patrick et al., 2022). Attrition weights were developed by MTF principle investigators and described in detail in Patrick et al. (Patrick et al., 2022) Briefly, these were calculated as the inverse probability of participation at each follow-up based on the following covariates measured at age 18: gender, race/ethnicity, college plans, truancy, high school grades, number of parents in the home, religiosity, parental education, alcohol use, cigarette use, marijuana use, other illicit drug use, region, cohort, and sampling weight correcting for over-sampling of age 18 substance users. To evaluate the potential for bias related to differential attrition, we conducted a missing bias analysis, examining the differences in the distribution of study variables based on whether or not the participants were retained (McKetta et al., 2023a; McKetta et al., 2021). We also performed sensitivity analyses examining the results without attrition weighting. The second source of missing in MTF is item non-response, which was minimal in this sample (96% fully observed). We used multiple imputation to impute missing values (n = 10 imputed datasets) and combined estimates using Rubin’s rules (Campion and Rubin, 1989; Rubin, 1987).

Results

Table 1 shows the distribution of study variables across levels of restrictive abortion policy climate. Due to variability in exact numbers and percentages in imputed datasets, the complete case sample is shown in Table 1, and restrictive abortion policy climate was dichotomized as high/low using a median split. The eligible sample included 19,881 female respondents with 50,995 unique observations totaling 148,893 person-years and 15,723 male respondents with 38,618 observations totaling 113,360 person-years (for both sexes mean = 7 years, interquartile range = 10 years). Respondents lived in all 50 states; the average number of observations per state was 1792 (range: 127 – 7749) corresponding to on average 807 unique respondents (range: 82 – 3497). On average, each respondent received 2 surveys (administered every 2 or 5 years; range:1–8 surveys). Because a small proportion of participants moved states, the average number of individual surveys per state was also 2 (range: 1–8). Overall, respondents living in highly restrictive abortion policy climates were very similar to those living in less restrictive climates across individual characteristics, though people living in more restrictive states were more likely to be White (77% vs 72%), live in a rural area or small town (24% vs 28%), and were older (mean age 35 vs 24) than those in less restrictive states. Regarding state-level characteristics, more restrictive climates had lower Gini coefficients (0.59 vs. 0.60), lower gender-based wage gaps (1.92 vs 1.96), higher percentages of male state legislators (81% vs 75%), Republican voters (52% vs 44%), and Black residents (14% vs 12%).

Table 2 shows model results for associations between restrictive abortion policy climate and depression symptoms among female respondents. In unadjusted models, we saw a non-significant association between restrictive abortion policy and depression symptoms (risk ratio [RR]: 0.99, 95% CI 0.96–1.02; RR for depression score: 1.00, 95% CI 0.99–1.00). With adjustment for individual and state level confounders, we observed a positive association, such that a 1-standard deviation increase in restrictive abortion policy climate was associated with a 7% increase in risk of depression symptoms (RR: 1.07, 95% CI 1.02–1.12). Fig. 1 shows the predicted probability of depression symptoms as a function of restrictive policy climate, averaged over all imputed estimates and fixed at the average covariate levels. With covariate adjustment, a 1-standard deviation increase in restrictive abortion policy climate was associated with a 1% increase in the depression score (RR: 1.01, 95% CI 1.01–1.02). Sensitivity analyses among female respondents who never moved states (83% of sample) were similar in magnitude and interpretation (RR: 1.05, 95% CI 1.00–1.10).

We then conducted three tests to examine robustness of these findings, all adjusted for individual- and state-level confounders. First, we examined the association between restrictive abortion policy climate and depression symptoms and score among male respondents (n = 15,723 participants with n = 38,618 observations), and we observed a nonsignificant adjusted association (RR: 1.04, 95% CI 0.99–1.08; RR for depression score: 1.01, 95% CI 1.00–1.01). Tests for interaction by policy climate and sex, however, did not show that this estimate statistically varied from estimates in main models with female participants (relative excess risk due to interaction: 0.00, 95% CI −0.04 to 0.06). We next examined subgroups of female respondents delineated by religiosity. Among female respondents who reported high religiosity, we observed a nonsignificant adjusted relationship between restrictive abortion policy climate and depression symptoms (RR: 1.03, 95% CI 0.97–1.10; RR for depression score: 1.01, 95% CI 1.00–1.02). The association remained positive among those who reported low religiosity (RR: 1.08, 95% CI 1.01–1.15; RR for depression score: 1.02, 95% CI 1.00–1.03 [p < 0.05]) and was slightly higher in magnitude than the association in the full sample of female respondents. Tests of interaction between policy climate and religiosity confirmed that these values differed (relative excess risk due to interaction: 0.06, 95% CI −0.11 to 0.02). Finally, we examined motor vehicle crashes as a negative control. Among the female respondents who received questionnaires regarding motor vehicle crashes (n = 4095 participants with n = 15,209 observations), we observed no significant adjusted association between restrictive abortion policy climate and motor vehicle crash probability (RR: 0.94, 95% CI 0.88–1.01). Because the subsample of females who responded to the motor vehicle crash questions was smaller than the eligible sample for the main analysis, we also confirmed that the adjusted associations between restrictive abortion policy and depression symptoms were equivalent to the full sample in this subsample (RR: 1.13, 95% CI 1.05–1.22; RR for depression score: 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.04).

The missing bias analysis examining differential attrition is shown in Supplemental Table 2. Participants were more likely to be retained if they were in states with more restrictive abortion policy climates, but also more likely to be retained if they had lower depression scores and a lower prevalence of depression symptoms. This type of selective attrition would bias results towards, rather than away, from a null association. Models without attrition weighting yielded extremely similar results and interpretation to those in the main analyses (among women, adjusted RR for depression symptoms: 1.06, 95% CI 1.02–1.10; RR for depression score: 1.01, 95% CI 1.00–1.02 [p < 0.05]).

Discussion

State level restrictive abortion policy climate was positively associated with symptoms of depression in a sample of 19,881 female respondents ages 19–45 surveyed between 1990 and 2015. Our robustness tests confirmed our hypotheses that these associations were specific to the subgroup most likely to oppose abortion restriction and that unrelated outcomes (motor vehicle accidents) were unaffected by these laws.

As we hypothesized, the associations were more pronounced among female participants. Although our test for interaction by sex did not meet statistical significance, the stratified estimate among male participants was smaller in magnitude than the association among female respondents. While we interpret the totality of the findings to be consistent with our hypotheses, the lack of significantly different associations by sex warrant further inquiry; prior associational research in the Household Pulse Survey also found a lack of moderation by sex, suggesting that both men and women are vulnerable to the mental health consequences of restrictive abortion legislation (Anderson et al., 2024). However, quasi-experimental findings after the Dobbs decision suggested that worsening depression symptoms were exclusively concentrated among women, though this research only examined a single type of restrictive law (Thornburg et al., 2024). The present research considered restrictive abortion climate holistically, and while men are not the direct targets of this legislation, they may be impacted both directly (e.g., through family formation processes) and indirectly (e.g., as allies) (Foster, 2025). Alternate samples are required to better explicate to what extent this association varies by gender identity, which we were unable to evaluate in the present work.

These findings contribute to the small but growing literature demonstrating potential adverse mental health consequences of restrictive abortion legislation (McKetta et al., 2024; Zandberg et al., 2022; Mukhopadhyay, 2024). In line with our study findings, people living in states that enacted more abortion restrictions in the wake of the Dobbs decision also reported more symptoms of depression (Anderson et al., 2024; Mukhopadhyay, 2024). We provide evidence that, even prior to Dobbs, state level legislation restricting abortion access was already associated with adverse population mental health. Whereas many states now have total bans on abortion procedures, the most common restrictive laws in our sample (Supplemental Fig. 2) were related to requirements over who could provide abortion services (e.g., provider refusal clauses and licensed physician requirements) as well as limitations on minors and people using public insurance. These laws were limiting abortion access decades before the Dobbs decision, with restrictive policies increasing rapidly in number after the 2010 midterm elections (Abortion rights could be dismantled).

Our findings provide empiric evidence regarding negative population mental health associations of these restrictive policies. These findings are also important for combating misinformation related to abortion and mental health. A common myth echoed in anti-abortion literature is that abortion causes depression and other adverse mental health issues, particularly after a 2011 meta-analysis suggested that people had higher risks of depression after abortion (Coleman, 2011). Methodological critiques of this study noted, among other concerns, that the comparison group was misclassified, the timing of depression was not consistently ascertained, and only 4 of the 44 eligible studies were included in the meta-analysis (Steinberg et al., 2012). Contemporaneous systematic reviews (Health, 2011; Major et al., 2009) and subsequent quasi-experimental findings from the Turnaway Study demonstrated that receiving a wanted abortion reduces, rather than increases, depression risk (Foster, 2021; Miller et al., 2020; Francisco, 2018). Nevertheless, the meta-analysis remains highly cited, and its findings are distributed by anti-choice advocates (A Woman; Colman and Joyce, 2012). For example, some states require distribution of this misinformation to pregnant people seeking abortion (US Census Bureau; Homan, 2019). While we did not examine the mental health effects of abortion procedures, ours is among the growing literature demonstrating that, at the population level, restrictions to abortion are associated with higher, rather than lower, risks for depression. While the associations were modest in magnitude—we observed a 7% increased risk of depression symptoms as restrictive abortion climate increased—small effect sizes have dramatic population impacts for health conditions that are common. Nationally, one in 20 women ages 18–45 meets criteria for depression (Guo et al., 2018)—or approximately three million women (US Census Bureau). An 7% increase would represent a population impact of over 200,000 more women with depression.

We hypothesize that multiple mechanisms may mediate the associations for this population—not only may individuals be unable to access (or fear being unable to access) timely care, particularly in the event of an unplanned pregnancy or pregnancy complication or emergency (McKetta et al., 2024), but they may appraise these laws as discriminatory or targeting their civil liberties, regardless of their own perceived risk of needing abortion care. Extensive scholarship examining targeted policies as indicators of structural stigma has demonstrated that restrictive legislation targeting a person’s identity worsens mental health through multiple adverse psychological processes (Hatzenbuehler et al., 2024). For example, people living in states with hostile climates may experience distress from anticipating discrimination or internalizing negative stereotypes (Hatzenbuehler, 2009). Women living in more restrictive states may similarly experience these stress processes, but religious women may be less likely to experience these laws as harmful because they appraise them as pro-child, rather than anti-woman. It remains unknown to what extent these psychological processes may mediate the observed associations, and these are an important future direction for scholarship in this area. The emerging literature on structural sexism (McKetta et al., 2022; Homan, 2019; Price et al., 2021)— which encompasses the institutionalized derogation of women’s rights, including through laws and policies—may provide some insights. Our adjusted models controlled for the sex-based wage gap and the percent of state legislators who are male, two indicators of structural sexism (McKetta et al., 2022; McKetta et al., 2023b). This approach distinguishes restrictive abortion policy from other sources of structural sexism, which would be alternative explanations for any observed associations. Reproductive rights, including not only restrictive abortion policies but also abortion clinic availability, have been conceptualized as indicators of structural sexism (McLaughlin et al., 2011; Homan, 2019) and have been linked to increased depression symptoms among women (McLaughlin et al., 2011; Chen et al., 2005). However, restrictive abortion policies have distinctive characteristics that set them apart from other indicators of structural sexism, and these may be uniquely detrimental for mental health. First, these laws specifically target health access. Next, unlike other forms of sexism which represent entrenched power imbalances between men and women (e.g., in legal representation, economic mobility) (McKetta et al., 2022), these laws may be perceived as qualitatively different because they represent a state action targeting women’s rights, rather than merely an unaddressed, damaging status quo. As a result, women may perceive these laws as directly limiting their autonomy, which in turn damages mental health (Balkir et al., 2013).

However, research on other discriminatory policies has demonstrated that people do not need to be explicitly cognizant of them, or recognize them as discriminatory, to be negatively impacted by their effects (Ford et al., 2024). For example, people may experience abortion delays without realizing they are due to legal mandates. Indeed, residents in states undergoing legislative change related to abortion frequently do not know the legality of abortion procedures, as was shown in Ohio in 2018–2019 when an abortion ban was being passed through the legislature but was never enacted: during this time, about 1 in 3 women aged 18–44 did not know abortion was still legal (Gallo et al., 2021). While the present study was unable to evaluate whether participants accurately appraised their own legal environments, doing so will be an important component for illuminating the mechanisms by which the policy environment impacts mental health—i.e., through limiting health care access, through institutionalized sexism, through adverse psychological processes, or through some other mechanism. Explicating these pathways, potentially using mixed methods approaches, will benefit not only our understanding of how these laws impact women’s mental health but also will contribute to further triangulating to what extent these associations are causal.

There are several important strengths to this study. We used theory-driven robustness tests to test our findings against alternative hypotheses. Observational research is vulnerable to confounding; particularly when the exposure is structural or distal to the outcome, uncontrolled structural confounders can lead to spurious associations. Robustness testing in this analysis was reassuring and consistent with research recommendations regarding stigmatized populations (Hatzenbuehler et al., 2024; Lattanner et al., 2025) Of note, the primary source of confounding was likely political composition, consistent with previous work, which demonstrated that voting patterns and state party control were highly predictive of enactment of restrictive abortion laws (Kreitzer, 2015).

Another strength of the present study is its long observation period, spanning 25 years. During that time abortion legislation varied dramatically from state to state, with the mean number of restrictive laws in the period ranging from 0 (e.g., California) to 12 (e.g., Missouri) (Supplemental Fig. 1). However, our measure of abortion policy climate was only current through 2015, and we were therefore unable to examine associations in the most recent decade. Policy watchdog organizations tracking abortion legislation are underfunded, and abortion laws change frequently and rapidly – creating methodological challenges to maintaining an up-to-date, time-varying database of state-level abortion laws (Maslowsky et al). Efforts to collect and publish time-varying legal data for the use of social scientists are ongoing (Maslowsky et al). Therefore, the measurement approach to restrictive abortion policy may limit to what extent these findings are generalizable to more recent samples. The present study provides critical evidence regarding mental health associations with restrictive abortion legislation in the pre-Dobbs era; however, since Dobbs, 13 states have banned abortion entirely. Our index, which weights restrictive laws equally, does not contain indicators for total abortion bans—because such bans were not legal during the study period. It remains unknown whether an index assessing abortion policy climate will be the appropriate way to measure restrictiveness from 2022 onward.

A limitation regards our outcome measure, which examined depression symptoms using self-report, rather than clinical evaluation. However, this approach is consistent with other national surveys (Keyes et al., 2019; Kaur et al., 2024; Villarroel and Terlizzi, 2020), and has the benefit of capturing smaller changes in symptomatology that may provide useful information about population mental wellbeing even if the symptoms do not achieve the threshold of clinical diagnosis (Villarroel and Terlizzi, 2020). We ascertained the exposure and outcome in the same survey, but did not have more granular information about depression timing (or policy timing) beyond study year. Therefore, the specific timing of both policy enactment and depression symptoms were measured with some error. The model may therefore be misspecified, which would introduce bias of an unknown extent. However, even with these limitations, these findings were consistent across all sensitivity analyses and in relation to prior literature. Another limitation was our inability to examine how this relationship may be exacerbated among people who are socially minoritized due to their race, ethnicity, or sexual orientation. Because of the entrenched overlap between economic resources and race in the United States due to historic patterns of segregation and structural discrimination (Bailey et al., 2017; Krieger et al., 2004), socially minoritized people are more likely to utilize abortion services to achieve reproductive autonomy (Chakraborty et al., 2025; Jones and Jerman, 2017). They are therefore the most likely to be impacted by legislation limiting abortion access. Due to changes in how race, ethnicity, and sexual orientation were measured in MTF over the study period, we did not have consistent, specific measures of these social minority categories for this sample. We were unable, therefore, to assess if these subgroups also disproportionately experienced depression symptoms in more restrictive abortion policy climates—though we hypothesize that they did. We similarly did not explore interactions of abortion policy climate with other structural climates, though systems of oppression mutually reinforce each other and can impact population health in tandem, particularly for people who are oppressed across multiple social identities (Everett and Agénor, 2023).

We chose religiosity as a stratifying variable because of the strong relationship between religious observance—especially the intensity of religious involvement (Harris and Mills, 1985)—and support for abortion restriction in the United States (Hoffmann and Johnson, 2005; Harris and Mills, 1985; Petersen and Mauss, 1976; Liebertz and Bunch, 2021). This relationship is highly stable and has persisted for decades (Petersen and Mauss, 1976; Liebertz and Bunch, 2021). Of course, people who are highly religious come from a variety of different backgrounds and faith traditions that interact with their own individual perspectives on abortion rights, so the subgroup estimate may mask a large amount of heterogeneity. As we were using this stratifying variable primarily as a robustness test, further examining heterogeneity within religious subgroups is beyond the scope of this paper.

An alternative explanation for our observed association is that people with better mental health may move away from states with more restrictive abortion climates, leaving behind people who are more prone to depression. Our sensitivity analysis among people who never moved found that the associations were extremely similar in magnitude and interpretation to those in the full sample, suggesting that this is unlikely to be a major determinant of our observed associations. If this were a determinant, we would anticipate the association to be more pronounced among non-movers (McKetta et al., 2017). Finally, we did not have information regarding participants’ locations within the states, which may be an important source of heterogeneity. People who live in states with restrictive laws that border nearby states with more supportive laws have very different health-seeking experiences than those who live further away from supportive states (Pleasants et al., 2022; Thompson et al., 2021), and these differences may meaningfully impact mental health as well. This limitation may lead to interference or spill-over effects, which could bias the estimates towards a null finding.

Abortion remains an important component of health care, and laws curtailing abortion access threaten that care. Restrictive abortion legislation is one component of an effort to limit health care access through regulations regarding which care can be provided (Covvey and Bratberg, 2024) and covered by insurance (Johnston and McMorrow, 2020). Our findings add to a growing body of literature illuminating the possible public health consequences of legislative efforts to limit health care access, among the general population, not only those who may become pregnant or seek abortion services. Our findings may be particularly relevant given the precipitous change in the legal landscape after the Dobbs decision, where in many states the laws have become even more obstructive (Whittum and Rapkin, 2022).
