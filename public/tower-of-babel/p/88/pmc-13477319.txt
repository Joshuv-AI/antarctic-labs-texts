Modeling the Societal Impact of Methamphetamine‐Associated Heart Failure
Authors: Paulino Alvarez, Martin Cadeiras
Published: 2026

I am I and my circumstance, and if I do not save it, I do not save myself. —José Ortega y Gasset

A 40‐year‐old male is at the heart failure (HF) clinic, accompanied by his family, waiting to be evaluated for heart–lung transplantation. He has a history of stage D HF with reduced ejection fraction and has been supported by a left ventricular assist device (LVAD) for the past 3 years. His clinical course has been complicated by right ventricular failure due to severe precapillary pulmonary hypertension treated with 3 pulmonary arterial hypertension specific medications. He was diagnosed with HF after several emergency department visits; he was admitted for cardiogenic shock and underwent LVAD implantation. He started using methamphetamine at a young age and quickly began using it regularly. With support from his family and personal determination, he hasn't used any drugs for the past 7 years and is currently working.

He is not alone in his struggle, but this is one of the not‐so‐frequent success stories. Approximately 2 million Americans reported methamphetamine use in the previous year. 1 Methamphetamine is associated with >15 000 deaths due to overdose and 10 000 HF admissions annually. 2 Methamphetamine related mortality and HF hospitalizations are increasing in the United States. 3 , 4 Patients admitted with methamphetamine‐associated HF (MethHF) are younger, more likely to be male, have a higher prevalence of cardiogenic shock, are more likely to be uninsured or Medicaid only, and are more likely to leave against medical advice less likely to receive guideline‐directed medical therapy compared with hospitalized patients with HF without methamphetamine use. 5 , 6

Quantitative data on the societal impact of MethHF remain scarce. In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), Zhu et al. take another step toward addressing this knowledge gap by using a Markov microsimulation model. 7 The simulation included 10, 000 hypothetical patients with severe MethHF who transitioned between 2 health states (mild and severe MethHF) and 1 absorbing state (dead), using transition probabilities estimated from a previously reported cohort with updated follow‐up data on 381 patients with MethHF. 8 The authors found that the cost of illness (COI) for MethHF is 4 times that of non‐MethHF, and if hospitalizations for MethHF remain constant in the United States, this would amount to $502 billion over the next 20 years. In MethHF, loss of productivity accounts for a larger share of the COI than in non‐MethHF (38.3% versus 28.7%). Approximately 88% of direct costs attributable to medical expenditures in MethHF were related to HF hospitalizations. These COI estimates likely represent only the tip of the iceberg even not including emergency department visits, rehabilitation services, non‐HF hospitalizations, productivity costs due to morbidity, direct nonmedical costs incurred by patients, or interventions for Stage D MethHF, such as home inotropes, LVAD, or heart transplantation, or pulmonary hypertension medications, among other factors. For example, the mean cost of LVAD implantation ranges from $122 953 to $271 472, and the average wholesale price per year for a combination of 3 pulmonary hypertension medications is $888029. 9 , 10

Despite these staggering costs, the mortality rate and COI per quality‐adjusted life year for MethHF were 2.7 and 5.7 times those for non‐MethHF, respectively. Higher COI is associated with lower quality‐adjusted life years.

One of the main drivers of this extremely high COI and poor outcomes is that MethHF is usually diagnosed at stage C or D in the context of a hospitalization for decompensated HF. The authors included this challenging aspect of MethHF in their model by having all patients start in a severe MethHF state, defined as LVEF ≤35% and New York Heart Assocation Class III/IV—which, in their cohort used for derivation of transition probabilities, had an all‐cause mortality rate of 66.7% over a median follow‐up of 5 years.

What is the way forward? Early detection and intervention for MethHF before a HF hospitalization, along with specialized post‐ischarge care, seem to be a potential target to mitigate the societal impact of this condition.

MethHF medical therapy includes the 4 traditional guideline‐directed medical therapy pillars and 1 additional pillar focused on cessation of methamphetamine use. Patients with MethHF have a high prevalence of factors that interfere with treatment adherence across multiple domains, including mental health disorders, socioeconomic disadvantages, limited access to care, and multiple comorbidities. 11 The implementation of multidisciplinary MethHF clinics has been described, offering guideline‐directed medical therapy in conjunction with contingency management to treat stimulant‐associated cardiomyopathy. This multidisciplinary comanagement care delivery model, known as Heart Plus, led to a 5‐fold increase in outpatient care engagement alongside a 53% decrease in acute care use. All participants reported a reduction in stimulant use. 12 With improved clinical stability, patients were better able to engage with outpatient resources addressing social determinants of health, including case management, social work, and housing and food assistance programs. Patients were also empowered to take an active role in their health, reassured that their health care providers were genuinely invested in their well‐being. The potentially reversible nature of methamphetamine cardiomyopathy and the impact of cessation on recovery have been well described. 13 Targeted multidisciplinary interventions may also open the door to exit strategies, such as LVAD and transplant, for patients with Stage D MethHF. 14 , 15 Bridging with ambulatory inotropic support has also been described. 9

However, a significant limitation is the small proportion of patients who are eligible for or able to complete these programs. Low engagement rates and pervasive psychosocial barriers demand a deeper level of planning, one grounded in a thorough understanding of the factors that initiate and sustain this behavior, and supported by comprehensive, shared multidisciplinary initiatives rooted in the community.

Addressing this challenge requires a coordinated, multilayered approach. First, leveraging multiple data sources with geospatial mapping can identify problem hubs where efforts should be concentrated. Second, existing community resources can be mobilized and unified to address the issue in a coordinated manner; Safety Net health systems, which disproportionately serve patients with substance use disorders, are particularly well positioned to anchor these efforts. Third, MethHF must be recognized as a shared community problem, engaging stakeholders across multiple levels, from payers and medical centers to employers and government agencies. Fourth, lasting, community‐specific action plans must be developed that acknowledge this crisis not as the result of individual failure but as the consequence of years of structural barriers and policy gaps. Fifth and finally, responses must be dynamic and adaptive, tailored to the evolving needs of each community, shifting from a posture of stigmatization toward one of targeted support and evidence‐based solutions that extend well beyond individual willpower.

We congratulate the authors on this important study that reveals the ongoing and projected public health impact of MethHF. While systemic change and policies are being planned and developed, we, as physicians, will continue fighting to save lives, 1 patient at a time.
