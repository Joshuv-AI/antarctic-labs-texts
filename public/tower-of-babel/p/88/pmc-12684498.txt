Socioeconomic Status, Race, and Ethnicity in Management of Pediatric Supraventricular Tachycardia
Authors: Karina Javalkar, Lynn A Sleeper, Jane Newburger, Audrey Dionne
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAn electrophysiology study (EPS) with ablation is often the preferred approach to management of supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) and/or preexcitation (Wolff‐Parkinson‐White syndrome) in children and adolescents. There are inequities in arrhythmia management in adults, but less is known in children. We aimed to determine whether socioeconomic inequities affect referral for EPS in children with SVT or Wolff‐Parkinson‐White syndrome.MethodsThis retrospective observational study used the Pediatric Health Information System (49 children’s hospitals’ administrative data) from 2016 to 2022. We included children with SVT/Wolff‐Parkinson‐White syndrome at centers performing EPS and excluded those with congenital heart disease. The zip code‐based Childhood Opportunity Index (COI; range 1–100) and race and ethnicity were predictors. Multivariable analysis included COI, age, race and ethnicity, census region, and preexcitation. The outcome was a procedure code for EPS.ResultsAmong 37 674 children with preexcitation (10 165) or SVT without preexcitation (27 509), 9840 (26.1%) underwent EPS. The median COI was 58 (interquartile range [IQR], 31–81). The sample was predominantly White non‐Hispanic (58%), followed by Hispanic (16%) and Black non‐Hispanic (12%). Children who underwent EPS had a higher COI than those who did not (median, 59 [IQR, 34–82] versus 57 [IQR, 30–81]; P<0.001). Black non‐Hispanic patients had lower EPS rates than White non‐Hispanic (P<0.001) and Asian (P<0.001) patients. Hispanic patients had lower EPS rates than White non‐Hispanic (P=0.001) patients. In multivariable analysis, lower COI, race and ethnicity, region, and preexcitation predicted EPS.ConclusionsIn this multicenter study of pediatric patients, we found disparities in use of EPS for SVT/Wolff‐Parkinson‐White syndrome. In adjusted analysis, Black non‐Hispanic and Hispanic children and those with a lower COI were less likely to undergo EPS.Keywords: ablation, electrophysiology, equity, pediatrics, supraventricular tachycardiaSubject Categories: Catheter Ablation and Implantable Cardioverter-Defibrillator, Pediatrics, Race and Ethnicity, Social Determinants of Health

Supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) is the most common pediatric tachyarrhythmia. 1 Management options include (1) observation, (2) medical management, or (3) electrophysiology study (EPS) including, when possible, an ablation. 2 EPS is often the preferred approach in older children and adolescents, and avoids the need for daily medication and ongoing cardiology follow‐up. It is also associated with improved outcomes such as exercise capacity and quality of life. 3 , 4

Health inequities in access to pediatric cardiology services are well‐documented in the literature. For example, inequities exist in prenatal diagnosis of critical congenital heart disease, evaluation of chest pain, and access to cardiac surgical centers. 5 , 6 , 7 However, studies specific to pediatric electrophysiology are limited. Among studies in adults, racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic disparities are well documented in patients undergoing invasive versus medical management for atrial fibrillation, the most common tachyarrhythmia in older age. Specifically, adults from racial and ethnic minority groups and those with lower neighborhood household income were less likely to undergo EPS when adjusting for clinical risk factors. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11

With regard to pediatric SVT, a recent single‐center study showed racial disparities in access to an EPS. 12 However, no published studies to date include multicenter patient data or examine socioeconomic status as predictors of undergoing EPS for pediatric SVT. Understanding disparities in management of SVT can inform interventions to bridge care gaps to improve equitable clinical outcomes. 13

The aim of this study was to understand the relationship between socioeconomic status, measured by the Childhood Opportunity Index, and undergoing an EPS for SVT or preexcitation. 14 We hypothesized that patients with greater childhood opportunity are more likely to undergo EPS than those with lower childhood opportunity. The secondary aims were to examine the association of race and ethnicity, insurance status, and region with performance of an EPS/ablation.

METHODS

This retrospective multicenter observational study used data from the Pediatric Health Information System (PHIS) database. The PHIS uses hospital administrative databases to provide clinical and billing information for all inpatient encounters in 49 participating children’s hospitals that are part of the Children’s Hospital Association. Encounter types include emergency department visits, outpatient surgeries and procedures, and inpatient admissions. In select hospitals, data about clinic visits are also available. All data and materials have been made available by the Children’s Hospital Association and can be accessed at https://www.childrenshospitals.org/content/analytics/product‐program/pediatric‐health‐information‐system.

Participants

Inclusion criteria were (1) age ≤18 years, (2) diagnosis of SVT or preexcitation (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision [ICD‐10] codes I471 or I456), and (3) evaluation at any PHIS participating hospital between calendar years 2016 and 2022. Patients with congenital heart disease were excluded from the analysis. We also excluded patients from 1 institution that does not perform ablations. This study was reviewed and deemed exempt by the Boston Children’s Hospital Institutional Review Board given that this was a publicly available, deidentified database. No informed consent was required.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the presence of a Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code (93653, 93619, 93620, or 93621) or a Clinical Transaction Classification (CTC) code (511134, 511135, or 511139) for an EPS or ablation procedure. Internal validation was performed by comparing the number of EPS/ablations performed from our center in the PHIS database using these codes and an institutional internal database and found comparable numbers.

Predictors

The predictor examined was the nationally normed neighborhood Childhood Opportunity Index (COI), a composite index of children’s neighborhood opportunity, which is a measure of socioeconomic status. The index is based on the zip code documented in the medical record for each patient. The COI (2.0) comprises 29 neighborhood indicators derived from public sources including the Census Bureau, National Center for Health Statistics, US Department of Education, and the Environmental Protection Agency, as well as a school‐level proprietary data set. 15 Given that it spans these domains, the COI was able to assess childhood opportunity in a more multidimensional fashion than traditional measures such as neighborhood median income, educational achievement, or patients’ insurance status. The range of scores is 0 to 100, with higher numbers indicating greater neighborhood childhood opportunity. The scores are normalized such that the score represents a national percentile. 13 , 14 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Additional predictors examined included race and ethnicity based on US Census categories, insurance status, and age at the time of admission. For many encounters, the diagnosis and EPS were in the same admission; for those in which the diagnosis was made and the EPS was performed at a later date, the time between diagnosis and EPS was obtained.

Statistical Analysis

The distribution of the COI in our study was not normally distributed and skewed toward higher COI. Therefore, nonparametric testing was used with regard to analysis of the COI. Primary analysis consisted of comparing the medians and distributions of COI between the EPS and non‐EPS groups using the Mann‐Whitney test. Subgroup analysis was also conducted for those with preexcitation and without preexcitation, given that the clinical threshold for EPS in those with preexcitation may be different. A χ2 test was used to assess association between EPS and the 8 racial and ethnic groups. When the omnibus test was significant, pairwise comparisons were also performed and adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. Multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate covariate‐adjusted associations between EPS and the following candidate predictors: age, COI, race, census region, preexcitation, sex, and insurance type. Significant predictors in the univariate analyses were then included in the multivariable regression. Interaction analysis was also performed for the COI, race and ethnicity, and census region in the multivariable regression. Given significant interactions between these predictors, we additionally performed stratified analysis using the Mann‐Whitney test to compare the median and distribution of the COI between the EPS and non‐EPS groups for each racial and ethnic group as well as for each census region. Last, we selected those who had separate encounters for diagnosis of SVT/Wolff‐Parkinson‐White syndrome (WPW) and EPS, and determined the time between the initial encounter to the encounter for EPS. We assessed the association of COI with this time period using linear regression, and the association with race and ethnicity with this time period using Mann‐Whitney tests. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. Data analysis was conducted in SPSS version 29.0.2.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY) and R 4.4.2 (RStudio, Boston, MA). 16

RESULTS

Sample Characteristics

The analytic cohort included 37 674 unique patients with a diagnosis of preexcitation (N=10 165) or SVT without preexcitation (N=27 509). Of the cohort, 9840 (26%) underwent EPS. The cohort (Table 1) was 48% female patients and had a mean age of 12.7 (SD ±9.8) years. The majority of patients in the sample had commercial or private insurance (56%), followed by government or public insurance (41%). The median childhood opportunity index for the sample was 58 (interquartile range [IQR], 31–81; range 0–100). The cohort was predominantly White non‐Hispanic (58%), followed by Hispanic (16%), Black non‐Hispanic (12%), other race (6.5%), and Asian (2%). There were 1836 patients with unknown race or ethnicity (5%). The unknown race and ethnicity group comprised 606 (33%) from a single center that had a high rate of EPS, 715 (39%) from the Northeast region, and a median COI of 64 (IQR, 33–84).

Race and Ethnicity

Among the total cohort, as well as the subgroups with and without preexcitation, the likelihood of undergoing EPS was significantly associated with race and ethnicity (Table 2, P<0.001). Higher rates of EPS were seen among White non‐Hispanic, Asian, Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander, those of other races, and those with unknown/missing race compared with those of Black non‐Hispanic, Hispanic, and American Indian/Alaska Native races and ethnicities. In pairwise comparison of racial and ethnic groups, Black non‐Hispanic patients had lower rates of EPS than patients with White non‐Hispanic (P<0.001), Asian (P<0.001), and other race and ethnicity (P<0.001). Hispanic patients had lower rates of EPS than White non‐Hispanic patients (P=0.001) and those of other races and ethnicities (P<0.001).

Insurance

In the overall cohort, insurance type was not a predictor of undergoing EPS. However, insurance type was a significant predictor of undergoing EPS among the group who had SVT without preexcitation, such that the lowest rates of EPS in this subgroup were those who had self‐pay insurance, compared with commercial/private, government/public, or other insurances (Table 2).

Multivariable Analyses

A multivariable logistic regression model including age, race, COI, insurance, census region, and presence of preexcitation as candidate predictors showed that those with Black non‐Hispanic, Hispanic, and American Indian/Alaska Native races and ethnicities had lower adjusted odds of undergoing an EPS than those with White non‐Hispanic race. Higher COI and presence of preexcitation were predictors of greater adjusted odds of EPS, and older age was predictive of lower odds of undergoing EPS (Table 3). The Northeast region had greater odds of EPS, whereas the South region predicted lower odds of EPS. Interaction terms for COI by race and ethnicity were performed and demonstrated a significant interaction between COI and unknown race and ethnicity, but no other significant interactions. Interaction terms for COI by census region demonstrated a significant interaction between higher COI and the Northeast region.

Time to EPS

Among the patients who were seen in the emergency department, clinic, or admitted to the hospital for SVT/WPW (separate than an encounter for EPS), the association of higher COI and EPS was still present (COI for those with ablation 59 [IQR, 33–83] versus COI for those without ablation 55 [IQR, 29–81]; P=0.044). The median time from of the initial visit SVT/WPW to EPS was 3 months (IQR, 2–13). The time from diagnosis of SVT/WPW to EPS was not associated with COI, race and ethnicity, or insurance type.

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective multicenter study, we found that patients with SVT or preexcitation who underwent EPS/ablation had higher neighborhood childhood opportunity and were more likely to be White non‐Hispanic or an unknown/missing race compared with those who did not undergo an EPS/ablation. In multivariable analysis, childhood opportunity, preexcitation, geographic region, and race and ethnicity were independent predictors of undergoing an EPS.

This study examines disparities in pediatric EPS/ablations in a multicenter, multiyear cohort using a neighborhood‐level index of socioeconomic status. Neighborhood‐level composite indices of socioeconomic status provide an avenue to examine factors beyond traditional measures such as neighborhood income and poverty or a patient’s insurance status. 17 , 18 They allow assessments that consider the natural and built environments, community resources, geographic access to care, and structural racism. The COI has been widely used to examine disparities in other aspects of pediatrics and pediatric cardiology, 13 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 incorporating aspects unique to children such as early childhood and elementary education and school performance, safety, single‐parent households, and public assistance rates. 14 A study of pediatric outpatient cardiology care showed that low COI was associated with higher gaps in care (defined as a lack of follow‐up within 3.35 years). 19 However, another recent single‐center study showed that COI was not associated with differential rates of ablations in pediatric patients with SVT. 24 Our study contrasts this in a multicenter cohort and shows that higher COI predicted greater rates of EPS for SVT and preexcitation, although this difference was small but statistically significant. This was additionally present in our multivariable model, adjusting for race and ethnicity, patient insurance, region, and preexcitation. In addition to COI, a key finding of our study was the lower rates of EPS/ablation in Black non‐Hispanic and Hispanic patients in both the univariable, pairwise, and multivariable analyses. Our study findings suggest that social determinants of health, racial and ethnic, and geographic disparities exist in the management of pediatric SVT and preexcitation. Undergoing a procedure such as an EPS can further exacerbate the effects of social determinants of health, and identifying and addressing barriers in pediatric cardiology may be necessary to eliminate disparities. Furthermore, ongoing efforts to eliminate provider bias and improve patient–provider relationships and trust may also work to bridge gaps in care.

There is a complex relationship between COI and race and ethnicity, but the association of race and ethnicity in our study was demonstrated to be present beyond the interaction with COI. As a social construct, the effect of race and ethnicity on health disparities may be related to differential access to care, provider bias, and the overall effects of structural racism. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 The COI does not include neighborhood racial and ethnic composition, but underlying structural racism can lead to increased barriers to financial stability, transportation, employment (including obtaining adequate time off work for a child’s procedure), and access to health care, which are elements in the COI. A previous single‐center study demonstrated that non‐White versus White pediatric patients were less likely to undergo EPS/ablation for SVT. 12 We similarly found racial and ethnic disparities in EPS across a larger, multicenter cohort, suggesting that these disparities are prevalent across institutions and region. Additionally, the effect of race and ethnicity on EPS persisted in the multivariable model including COI and insurance status. Although socioeconomic status and race and ethnicity are strongly correlated, our analysis demonstrates that there may be an independently decreased rate of EPS/ablation among Black non‐Hispanic and Hispanic patients. In addition to the structural barriers that minoritized groups may face, other factors that may be implicated in racial and ethnic disparities include institutional and provider bias, as well as potential distrust of the health system by minoritized groups due to historic injustices against these populations. 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 Within racial and ethnic groups, COI was not associated with EPS except for in those patients whose race and ethnicity were unknown, which further suggests that the disparities are predominantly related to race and ethnicity.

Our study findings parallel those of adult electrophysiology patients, for whom there are disparities in catheter ablation for atrial fibrillation. 8 , 11 , 35 , 36 In these patients, catheter ablation can improve symptoms, and in selected patients with symptomatic atrial fibrillation, it is the preferred approach. 37 For pediatric reentrant SVT, there are similar indications for catheter ablation. 38 , 39 Preexcitation, or WPW, also carries and increased risk of sudden death, and therefore EPS may be more strongly recommended to risk‐stratify and address the preexcitation. Overall, the number of patients with WPW who underwent ablation in our sample was low relative to this recommendation. This may suggest a limitation of our administrative data to capture patients who underwent an EPS/ablation at a different institution than where they obtained a diagnosis of WPW, the limitations of the coding to identify all patients who underwent an EPS/ablation, or that increased rates of EPS/ablation in patients with WPW are not being currently achieved in practice for reasons that should be explored in future studies. Because EPS is more likely to be strongly recommended in patients with preexcitation than in those with other SVT mechanisms, we examined the presence of preexcitation specifically in our study. In multivariable analysis, preexcitation independently predicted greater odds of undergoing an EPS. When examining those with and without preexcitation separately, there were disparities by race and ethnicity and COI, but interestingly, insurance status was only significant for those without preexcitation. Specifically, the proportion of patients in our cohort with self‐pay undergoing ablation was significantly lower than in other insurance groups. We hypothesize that the more elective nature of EPS in this group may explain these differences. Findings are also similar to those of Sooy‐Moosey et al, who showed that patients with missing insurance information were less likely to undergo ablation during their study period. 12

Our findings may additionally represent socioeconomic disparities in initial diagnosis of SVT/WPW, because our cohort of patients with this diagnosis had a median COI above the 50th percentile nationally. Our cohort also included a higher percentage of non‐Hispanic White patients, and fewer Hispanic patients than the US population of children based on the Census bureau. 40 Other studies of children with SVT/WPW have also shown overrepresentation of White patients compared with the general population of the United States in their study cohorts. 41 , 42 SVT/WPW is not known to have an association with race and ethnicity, and therefore we would hypothesize that the racial and ethnic distribution of those diagnosed with SVT/WPW would match that of the US population. This, combined with a higher median COI for our cohort compared with the national median, suggests that there may be socioeconomic and racial and ethnic inequities in receiving a diagnosis of SVT/WPW. This hypothesis could be explored in future population studies.

Limitations of our study include the retrospective nature of the study, as well as reliance on hospital administrative data and correct coding of race and ethnicity, diagnoses, and procedures. These data were limited in more detailed information about the severity or type of SVT, as well as outcomes such as success rates of the EPS that were performed. Because the PHIS is a primarily inpatient database, many patients’ only encounter in the database was that of their EPS procedure. We may have missed patients with milder forms of SVT who never required an emergency department visit, hospitalization, or EPS. However, we were able to examine a substantial cohort of patients who had an initial encounter with a diagnosis of SVT or preexcitation and had an EPS at a later date. The use of the neighborhood‐level COI was a significant strength of this study. However, because this is a large, deidentified database without specific patient addresses recorded, only zip code‐level COI (as opposed to census tract‐level COI) was able to be determined. Census tract‐level data, which concentrate on smaller geographic areas, provide more granularity to differences in neighborhoods. The large sample size increased the power to detect differences between the study groups that may have been statistically significant and important on a population level, but less clinically significant about individual patients. We had a substantial number of patients with missing racial and ethnic data and found that these patients had increased rates of EPS, many of whom from a single center. This limitation may have been related to center variability in recording of race and ethnicity, because there was center variability in rates of EPS. We addressed these limitations with our multivariable analysis with interaction terms as well as stratified analyses.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study demonstrates that performance of EPS/catheter ablation in children with SVT is significantly associated with race and ethnicity, COI, and geographic region. Future studies should focus on understanding mechanisms of disparities to improve outcomes. Ultimately, working to eliminate these barriers could lead to improved access to EPS for patients with SVT or preexcitation, with the goal of improving quality of life and outcomes for all.
