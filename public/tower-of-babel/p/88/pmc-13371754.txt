Identification of Contacts With High Rates of Missed and Asymptomatic Infection Following an Outbreak of Ebola Virus Disease: A Seroepidemiological Study in Likati Health Zone, Democratic Republic of the Congo
Authors: Olivier Tshiani Mbaya, Koya Ariyoshi, Laura Skrip, Christian M Kahusu, Anthony Suangi, Ange Mubiala, Antoine Tshomba, Kubo Yoshinao, Gregoire Mumbere, Jean-Jacques Muyembe, Sabue Mulangu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEbola virus disease (EVD) is often regarded as severe and highly fatal, but growing evidence suggests that subclinical or minimally symptomatic infections occur and frequently go undetected. During the 2017 outbreak in Likati Health Zone, only 8 cases were confirmed despite many reported exposures. We evaluated the extent of asymptomatic or unrecognized Ebola virus infection and associated factors among contacts of reported cases.MethodsIn November 2017, we conducted a cross-sectional community-based serosurvey among contacts originally identified through Ministry of Health records and newly identified through additional post-outbreak investigations. Participants provided blood samples and completed questionnaires on demographics, exposures, and symptoms within 4 weeks of symptom onset of the EVD case with whom contact was reported. Sera were tested for anti-EBOV nucleoprotein IgG by ELISA. Seropositive individuals were classified as asymptomatic (no symptoms) or unrecognized (≥1 symptom). Secondary attack rate (SAR) was estimated, and logistic regression assessed associations with sociodemographic factors, exposure level, and symptoms.ResultsAmong 180 participants (79 originally identified; 101 newly identified), 33 (18.3%) were seropositive. Of these, 19 (58%) reported symptoms, and 14 (42%) were asymptomatic. Any EVD-related symptom was associated with higher odds of seropositivity (OR 2.48, P = .021), though no specific symptom was significant. Asymptomatic individuals had higher antibody titers than symptomatic seropositive contacts (P = .009). The overall SAR was 19.1% (95% CI: 15.9–23.0). High exposure level strongly predicted seropositivity (OR 11.2, 95% CI: 3.8–33.3).ConclusionsAsymptomatic infections occurred, including among contacts missed during the response, highlighting the need for exposure-based serologic assessments in EVD investigations and raising questions about immune responses and the true disease burden in outbreak-affected settings.Keywords: asymptomatic and paucisymptomatic infections, Democratic Republic of the Congo, Ebola virus disease, Likati, seroepidemiology

Ebola virus disease (EVD) is a life-threatening illness with a case fatality rate ranging from 25% to 90%, depending on the viral species [1–5]. Clinical presentation typically progresses from “dry” symptoms, such as fever and fatigue, to “wet” symptoms, including diarrhea, vomiting, and bleeding, with hemorrhage occurring in approximately 40%–50% of cases [6]. Although EVD is widely recognized as severe and highly fatal, increasing evidence indicates that Ebola virus (EBOV) infection may present across a broader clinical spectrum, including mild or subclinical forms. Following the 2014–2016 West Africa outbreak, seroepidemiological studies among contacts of confirmed cases identified substantial proportions of asymptomatic and previously unrecognized infections, particularly among quarantined close contacts [7–11].

The transmission potential of pauci- or asymptomatic infections remains poorly understood, yet these cases may hold important immunological and epidemiological implications [12]. They may contribute to understanding protective immunity and guide vaccine and therapeutic development. Additionally, individuals with mild or asymptomatic infections have been reported to experience long-term sequelae, underscoring the need to better characterize the full clinical spectrum of EBOV infection [13–15].

On 12 May 2017, nearly 1 year after the West Africa epidemic, the Ministry of Health of the Democratic Republic of the Congo declared an EVD outbreak (classified as Orthoebolavirus zairense) in Likati Health Zone, Bas Uele Province [16]. The outbreak originated from a single index case and resulted in 8 cases across 3 transmission generations, with 277 contacts traced. Although effective response efforts likely contained the outbreak [16, 17], delayed recognition and surveillance gaps raised concerns about undetected infections [18]. We therefore conducted a post-outbreak survey to assess asymptomatic and unrecognized EBOV infections among identified contacts.

METHODS

Study Design, Study Population, and Sampling Strategy

We conducted a cross-sectional, community-based study in November 2017 in the Likati HZ, located 1452 km to the north of Kinshasa (the capital of DRC), in Bas-Uele Province (Figure 1). This study aimed to enroll close contacts (defined as any person who has been exposed to a probable or confirmed EVD case [16]) of the 8 EVD cases detected during the outbreak in Likati HZ. Investigations were carried out 4 months after the official declaration of the end of the outbreak.

To identify the close contacts, we employed a convenience sampling strategy using two complementary approaches (Figure 2). First, we referred to the MoH contact list to locate and recruit as many of the 277 contacts originally identified as part of the outbreak surveillance. Local health professionals and community trackers familiar with their communities were engaged and trained to locate, identify, and sensitize contacts still residing in the HZ. The team attempted to visit all communities where the 8 EVD cases had resided and which were assumed to have the highest concentrations of close contacts. Contacts absent during the first visit were revisited twice more. If they had moved, we attempted to trace their new address, unless inaccessibility due to distance and/or terrain prevented further pursuit.

Flow diagram of study participants and classification in disease groups. Abbreviations: EBOV, Ebola virus; EVD, Ebola virus disease.

Recognizing that outbreak response efforts may not have identified all contacts and that some contacts who had been registered might be unreachable, we also surveyed health areas within Likati for newly identified contacts (ie, previously “missed contacts”). This approach aimed to identify additional individuals who had been in contact with reported EVD cases per the definition of “close contact,” but who had not been captured by the surveillance system. The occurrence of “missed” contacts was expected if the contacts had been absent during contact tracing or had been reluctant to disclose their exposure during the active outbreak. Trained local health professionals and community trackers conducted investigations around the households of EVD cases to identify additional contacts. Following community sensitization, individuals who presented themselves to the study team were evaluated and retrospectively validated against the definition of a “close contact.” The newly identified contacts were then registered and invited, the following day, to a central site—located within the visited health area—to be enrolled in the study and undergo the associated procedures.

All contacts—originally registered by the MoH response team and newly identified by the study team—were invited to provide written informed consent and to participate. For participants under 18 years of age, interviews were conducted in the presence of at least one parent or guardian. None of the participants reported ever being laboratory confirmed with or suspected of having EVD. Contacts who reported only post-recovery exposure to EVD survivors (ie, no contact during the period of acute disease) were excluded.

Epidemiological and Clinical Data Collection

Data on sociodemographic information, exposure pathways, and symptoms were collected using a standard questionnaire adapted from the World Health Organization manual on the implementation and management of contact tracing for EVD [19]. For the originally registered contacts, data collected during the present study on identity, sex, age, residence of the contact, name of the linked EVD case, and relationship with the case were validated against the originally collected data from contact tracing during the active outbreak period. After completing the interview, each participant provided a blood specimen.

To assess the type and intensity of exposure, participants were asked both open-ended and closed-ended questions about whether they had touched, carried, or embraced the patient during the illness or the corpse of an EVD case, or handled the patient's clothing or bodily fluids. Exposure levels were categorized hierarchically as low (no physical contact or contact before severe symptoms), medium (touching a symptomatic acute EVD case), or high (carrying or embracing an acutely ill or deceased EVD case or contact with body fluids or waste) [20–22]. Each level was mutually exclusive.

Participants were asked to recall symptoms that occurred within 4 weeks after exposure to a symptomatic EVD case. To minimize bias, they were first asked to freely recall any symptom experienced during that period, followed by a checklist of 16 EVD-related symptoms or signs: fever, loss of appetite, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, headache, abdominal pain, unexplained bleeding, myalgia, arthralgia, difficulty breathing, shortness of breath, hiccups, red eyes, fatigue, or sore throat.

Blood Sampling and Testing

Venous blood samples were obtained, processed in the field, and shipped to the Instituts National de Recherche Biomédicale (INRB) in Kinshasa for analysis. Serum aliquots were cryopreserved and tested using an ELISA for EBOV anti-EBOV Nucleoprotein (NP) IgG antibodies, adapted from previously published protocols [23]. In brief, 96-well ELISA plates were coated overnight at 4°C with 1.2 µg recombinant EBOV NP (Prod#REC31728, The Native Antigen Company, Oxfordshire, England) and blocked with casein buffer (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, United States). Serum samples (1:100) were incubated for 60 minutes at 37°C, washed 6 times with .2% Tween 20 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, United States), and incubated with peroxidase-conjugated F(ab)2 fragment Donkey anti-human Fc (1:5000) (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, Inc., West Grove, PA) for 30 minutes. Color was developed with o-phenylenediamine (Sigma) and stopped with 1.8 M H2SO4. Absorbance was read at 490 nm (Victor X3 Plate Reader, PerkinElmer, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States). Seropositivity was defined as the mean OD of naïve controls plus 3 standard deviations. Samples were tested in duplicate, with positive and negative controls included.

Outcome Classification

Disease classification was adapted from previous categorization used in other serostudies [24–26], combining serological reactivity with reported clinical symptoms. Participants were categorized as (1) uninfected seronegative for EBOV IgG, (2) asymptomatic with seropositivity but without any of 16 EVD-related symptoms, and (3) unrecognized EVD cases with seropositivity and ≥1 EVD-related symptom. It was assumed that seropositivity during this study indicated infection during the outbreak and following exposure to the recognized cases.

Statistical Analysis

Epidemiological and serological data were available for all participants and analyzed descriptively and analytically. Continuous variables were described using medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), while categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between serological groups (seropositive vs seronegative for anti-EBOV NP IgG) and category of contacts (contacts initially identified during the outbreak vs contacts newly identified during the post-epidemic survey) were performed using Fisher's exact test. Unadjusted logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with seropositivity. Due to the small absolute number of events (ie, seropositive contacts), we did not undertake an adjusted analysis.

The secondary attack rate was calculated overall and by infection generation after accounting for the original EVD cases and the newly identified secondary infections. The number of infections in MoH-registered contacts not located during the survey was estimated. It was assumed that the rate of seropositivity among contacts not located was the same as the rate of seropositivity among MoH-registered contacts found and tested in the survey. 95% confidence intervals (binomial exact) were calculated for secondary attack rates to quantify the uncertainty surrounding the proportion estimates.

Lastly, the threshold value of the optical density of anti-EBOV IgG was compared between the asymptomatic contacts (ie, seropositive participants reporting no symptoms) and unrecognized EVD cases (ie, seropositive participants reporting any EVD-related symptoms) using the Mann–Whitney test.

Across all analyses, the level for statistical significance was set at P < .05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27 and GraphPad Prism version 9 software.

Ethics Statement

The study protocol was approved by the National Ethical Committee of the University of Kisangani (ref. #: CER/003/GEA/2017). Written informed consent was obtained from all adult participants. Dual consent, including at least the written consent of a parent or guardian and the written assent of children deemed physically, psychologically, and mentally capable of giving their opinion, was obtained for minors aged 13–17 years, in accordance with the regulations in force in the DRC. For children under 13 years of age, parents or guardians were responsible for providing consent.

RESULTS

Identification of Recorded and Previously Missed Contacts

A total of 180 contacts from 6 health areas consented and were enrolled in the study. We successfully traced 79 of 277 (28.5%) contacts listed in the official Congolese MoH registry from the 2017 Likati outbreak response. An additional 101 previously “missed” contacts were identified through our post-outbreak in-depth investigation as being linked to the 8 EVD cases during their periods of acute illness (Figure 2). Among the 198 (71.5%) original contacts remaining, most of them could not be found (99.5%), and only 1 individual (0.5%) was found but declined to participate in the study. However, no statistically significant differences were observed for age, sex, and location between the originally registered contacts who were found and those not found (Supplementary Table 1).

The contacts originally registered by the MoH, and those contacts newly identified after the outbreak, did not significantly differ across key sociodemographic, exposure-related variables and OD values for NP EBOV IgG (Supplementary Table 2 and Figure 1). Newly identified contacts were more likely to report having diarrheal symptoms following contact.

Characteristics of Contact Populations

Characteristics of the 180 contacts are described in Table 1. More than half of the enrolled contacts were male (109 [60.6%]). Contacts were a median of 37 years old (IQR, 28–47) with ages ranging from 10 to 75 years. The most frequently reported occupation was “farmer” (104 [57.8%]), followed by “student or teacher” (29 [16.1%]). The proportion of contacts employed as health care workers was relatively small (18 [10.0%]). Most contacts resided in Muma village (98 [54.4%]), followed by Mabangu village (38 [21.1%]) and Nambwa village (24 [13.3%]). Most contacts were identified as family members of the cases (113 [62.7%]) (Table 1).

The most common exposure type reported was touching or caring for an acute EVD case without gloves, reported by 41.7% (75) of the contacts (Table 1). Additionally, 18.9% (34) had transported an EVD case to a health center, while 16.7% (30) touched the corpse of an EVD case. Levels of exposure were classified as low in 43.9% (79), moderate in 31.7% (57), and high in 24.4% (44) of contacts within a 4-week period following the onset of symptoms in the EVD case.

Serological Status

Thirty-three contacts (18.3%) tested positive for anti-EBOV NP IgG antibodies. Of these, 19 (58%) reported symptoms, and 14 (42%) were asymptomatic. In the analysis by contact group, 13 out of 79 (16.5%) contacts originally registered by the MoH and 20 out of 101 (19.8%) newly identified “missed” contacts tested positive for anti-EBOV NP IgG antibodies.

Reconstruction of the EVD transmission chain, incorporating the previously unrecognized infections (Figure 3), yielded an estimated overall secondary attack rate (SAR) of 19.1% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 15.9, 23.0) (Supplementary Table 3). Supplementary Figure 2 shows the distribution of detected severely symptomatic cases, unrecognized EVD cases, and asymptomatic EBOV infections across transmission generations (G0–G4). The estimated effective reproduction number per transmission generation showed a 12-fold increase in G1 (1 case to 12 secondary cases), a 1.5-fold increase in G2 (12 to 20), and a 0.64-fold decrease in G3 (20 to 8) for the number of secondary infections generated. The overall proportion of infections with any symptoms (detected cases or unrecognized cases) was 66% (95% CI: 49%–80%), with a peak of 29% (95% CI: 16%–46%) observed during G2 (Supplementary Figure 1).

Table 2 summarizes the characteristics associated with EBOV IgG seropositivity in an unadjusted analysis. Interestingly, compared to those who reported being farmers, contacts reporting an occupation of “hunter or fisherman” had more than 6 times the odds of seropositivity (OR 6.37, 95% CI: 1.3–31.0, P = .022). Other sociodemographic characteristics and relationship with the detected EVD case did not differ between seropositive and seronegative individuals. No specific symptom was individually associated with seropositivity. Most exposure types were significantly associated with seropositivity. The strongest association was observed for “cleaning blood/vomit/nasal discharge,” with individuals reporting this exposure type having nearly 13 times the odds of seropositivity compared to individuals not reporting the exposure type (OR 12.8, 95% CI: 4.9–33.4, P < .001). Compared to contacts with low exposure levels, the odds of seropositivity among those with moderate exposure levels were not significantly different. However, contacts with high exposure levels had significantly higher odds of seropositivity than those with low exposure levels (OR: 11.24, 95% CI: 3.8–33.3, P < .001).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that the 2017 Likati EBOV outbreak was associated with a high number of infections epidemiologically linked to the 8 reported cases but previously undetected. Several newly identified infections were among contacts not captured during outbreak-time surveillance activities. By conducting a post-outbreak survey to identify additional contacts, the present study builds upon past serosurveys among known contacts to identify rates of infection among missed contacts.

Characteristics of the 2017 Likati Outbreak as Compared to Previous Outbreaks

After accounting for the unrecognized EVD cases and asymptomatic infections identified through this study, the 2017 Likati outbreak is consistent with previously reported EVD outbreaks. According to a meta-analysis [25], the estimated overall household SAR in EVD outbreaks has been estimated at 12.5% (95% CI: 8.6%–16.3%). For Likati, the SAR based on originally detected cases was 2.5%. This initially low estimate is due, in large part, to unrecognized cases that were subclinical or mild, and/or among contacts not included in the MoH surveillance listing. Thus, the 19.1% estimated attack rate, accounting for previously undetected infections, is consistent with previous outbreaks, but the clinical presentation of infections may have differed, and transmission was attributable to the limited number of severe cases.

Figure 3 illustrates the natural progression of the outbreak, accounting for newly identified infections. The effective reproduction number in the first generation reached approximately 12. However, the subsequent transmission rapidly declined, and the outbreak did not expand further beyond the third generation well before May 14, when the first responders arrived at the site [18]. This suggests that mild and subclinical infections contributed little, if any, to secondary transmission in the Likati outbreak, as might be expected under prevailing understanding [17]. Further research remains urgently needed to elucidate the actual contribution of pauci-/asymptomatic individuals to secondary transmission.

Possibility of Natural Host Resistance

The limited number of severely symptomatic cases diagnosed after the index case of EVD may suggest the existence of underlying natural host resistance to disease progression in the Likati population. The intervention response to the Likati outbreak occurred 46 days after the index case became symptomatic, suggesting that the epidemic had already peaked before public health control measures were widely implemented. The local population was not well-educated about prevention measures, and a substantial proportion of contacts were exposed at high risk, such as having cared for EVD cases with bare hands. Such high exposure contacts would have been expected to result in more cases with severe disease, particularly in this “naïve” population, as had been observed in the West African population. This observation raises the hypothesis of a genetic resistance profile of the Likati population against the EBOV. Immunological and genetic determinants that may confer protection against EVD severity are still unknown. Asymptomatic EBOV infection may constitute an attractive pool of subjects to identify the basis of resistance mechanisms, as other diseases exemplify [27].

Strategy of the Current Investigation Based on Clinical Diagnosis of EVD

Most EVD outbreaks have occurred in remote rural and forested areas of Africa, where response efforts are complicated [18]. There have been well-documented logistical challenges in responding to outbreaks, and historical experiences of disenfranchisement have created community mistrust of response efforts, rendering the effectiveness of outbreak-time surveillance suboptimal. Similar factors were reported during the Likati outbreak and may have contributed to limited surveillance coverage, leading to a high number of missed contacts and unrecognized EVD cases [16, 17]. For the post-outbreak serosurvey, a strong community-based approach, involving local social workers, health professionals, and community leaders, significantly contributed to reducing mistrust and promoting participation.

In addition to emphasizing the value of developing positive rapport with communities during outbreaks through early and effective engagement strategies, the current findings on the frequent occurrence of asymptomatic infections in the Likati outbreak highlight potential limitations of symptom-based epidemiological investigations in estimating true outbreak size. Our findings raise the possibility that a non-negligible number of self-limiting, small-scale outbreaks like the Likati outbreak might be occurring unnoticed in a context of frequent asymptomatic contacts. The true underlying immunological profiles in settings should therefore not be assumed. Knowing the proportion of asymptomatic infections has implications for assessing vaccine efficacy and for determining the indication or relevance of post-exposure prophylaxis for contacts.

Definition of Asymptomatic EBOV Infection

Asymptomatic EBOV infection has been reported in studies among household or community contacts of EVD cases, with a wide range in the detection of EBOV IgG Abs, depending on the method of detection used [15, 20–22, 24, 26, 28]. In this study, we determined antibodies detection against one of the significant viral antigens, NP, using an adapted ELISA assay previously developed and validated [23].

It is noteworthy that significantly higher levels of Ab titers, reflected by a higher OD, were observed among asymptomatic individuals than in individuals with unrecognized EVD infection. This might reflect a strong immune response among the asymptomatic individuals who mount a robust immunity [13] that can prevent the virus from spreading and quickly control the infection without clinical manifestations. This ability can ultimately constitute the basis for immunotherapy against EVD. Moreover, the characterization of the inflammatory response in asymptomatic EBOV infection still needs to be well defined, and the different roles played by specific immune markers that provide protection need deep exploration.

Limitations

Our study contributes new evidence to the growing body of literature on asymptomatic EBOV infections and unrecognized EVD cases. However, this evidence should also be interpreted in the context of the study's limitations. First, the small sample size used in our study may limit the power of statistical inference. Recognizing the difficulty of tracing all the contacts investigated during the outbreak, we did at least attain a sample size that helped to draw meaningful conclusions, consistent with other larger studies conducted in different regions. Second, the self-reporting of symptoms may have been subject to recall bias due to the time elapsed since the exposure events and other factors. To minimize this bias, we conducted the study relatively quickly after the outbreak. We also validated study responses from the original contacts against information provided during the outbreak. Also, to reduce impact of social desirability bias and enhance comfort in answering the study team, we included trained community leaders in the study team. Lastly, the proportion of asymptomatic and unrecognized EVD cases may be overestimated if we consider that some participants would have acquired the virus from a different source. However, we strongly believe in the quality of the data collected due to the epi-linkage between all contacts and EVD cases diagnosed during the Likati outbreak.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this observational study conducted among contacts of reported EVD cases reveals that the Likati outbreak was larger in scale than originally thought. Despite the setting being EVD naïve, a high rate of mild unrecognized cases and asymptomatic infections was detected among people who had contact—in many cases, high exposure contact—with severely sick cases. The epidemiological implications of mild and asymptomatic infections remain unclear, and more studies are needed to understand whether they could confer population-level protection or, conversely, result in the emergence of a new outbreak due to EBOV persistence. Further investigations could leverage the current findings to define determinants of natural genetic resistance to EBOV and to address questions related to long-term immunity during future exposures. Moreover, pending a better understanding of outbreak-time and post-outbreak implications of mild and asymptomatic infections, additional measures for exposure assessment and symptom screening may need to be considered.
