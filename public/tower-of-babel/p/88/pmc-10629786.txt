Efficacy and safety of golimumab in patients with non-radiographic axial spondyloarthritis: a withdrawal and retreatment study (GO-BACK)
Authors: Cindy L J Weinstein, Paula Sliwinska-Stanczyk, Tomas Hála, Marina Stanislav, Anjela Tzontcheva, Ruji Yao, Yuliya Berd, Sean P Curtis, George Philip
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe GO-BACK study was designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of golimumab (GLM) treatment withdrawal in adults with non-radiographic axial spondyloarthritis (nr-axSpA) who demonstrate inactive disease during a 10-month open-label (OL) GLM run-in.MethodsEligible participants received OL GLM in period 1. In period 2, participants who achieved inactive disease were randomized 1:1:1 to receive double-blind (DB) treatment with monthly placebo (PBO, treatment withdrawal) or continued GLM treatment given monthly (GLM QMT) or every 2 months (GLM Q2MT). Participants who did not have a disease flare continued DB treatment for ∼12 months. Participants with a disease flare discontinued DB treatment and resumed monthly OL GLM. Primary endpoint compared the proportion of participants without a disease flare in the continued GLM treatment groups (QMT or Q2MT) vs PBO in a multiplicity-controlled, step-down fashion. Safety follow-up continued for ∼3 months after last treatment.ResultsA total of 188 patients, out of the 323 enrolled, were eligible for participation in period 2. Both GLM QMT and GLM Q2MT were superior to treatment withdrawal (PBO) in preventing disease flare (P < 0.001), with a treatment-difference vs PBO of 50.4% and 34.4% for the GLM QMT and GLM Q2MT groups, respectively. The time-to-first flare was significantly longer (log-rank P < 0.0001) with GLM treatment compared with PBO. Of 53 participants (in Q2MT or PBO) who had a confirmed disease flare, 51 (96.2%) attained a clinical response within 3 months of restarting OL GLM. Adverse events were consistent with the known GLM safety profile.ConclusionAmong participants with active nr-axSpA who attained inactive disease after 10 months of GLM treatment, continued GLM treatment is well tolerated and provides superior protection against disease flares compared with GLM withdrawal. Introduction

The chronic and potentially progressive nature of non-radiographic axial spondyloarthritis (nr-axSpA) necessitates long-term treatment [1–3]. NSAIDs continue to be the first line therapy; patients who have had an inadequate response or intolerance to NSAIDs have been shown to benefit from TNF inhibitors (TNFi) [4, 5].

Golimumab (GLM) is one of four TNFi approved for nr-axSpA in the European Union. GO-AHEAD, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase III trial in nr-axSpA, demonstrated superior efficacy and comparative safety of monthly GLM treatment compared with placebo (PBO) [6]. The open-label extension portion of this study demonstrated that the significant improvements in disease activity observed in GLM- compared with PBO-treated patients over 16 weeks were maintained in participants who continued GLM for up to 52 weeks as well as participants who switched from placebo to GLM [7]. Since studies such as these indicate that sustained remission (inactive disease) or low disease activity is achievable in patients with nr-axSpA, it is reasonable to consider withdrawal or reduced dosing paradigms [8, 9]. Few clinical studies have examined the effects of TNFi treatment reduction or withdrawal in patients with nr-axSpA who have reached treatment target goals [10–12]. The current study is the first to examine the efficacy and safety of continued GLM treatment (full- or reduced-treatment) compared with withdrawal in patients who have achieved sustained inactive disease.

Methods

Study participants

Study entry criteria were designed to enrol a study population consistent with the EU-approved indication for nr-axSpA. The study enrolled adult participants, aged 18–45 years (inclusive), with active nr-axSpA and objective signs of inflammation who had a history of inadequate response or intolerance to ≥2 NSAIDs. Participants were required to have had a diagnosis of active nr-axSpA for ≤5 years since the onset of symptoms, chronic back pain for ≥3 months, and were to have met prespecified criteria adopted from the Assessment of the SpondyloArthritis International Society (ASAS). All participants were to have been naïve to TNFi and other biologic agents used to treat immune-mediated diseases. Participants were excluded if they exhibited signs and symptoms of other systemic inflammatory conditions from screening to baseline. A full list of entry criteria is provided in the Supplementary Material, available at Rheumatology online.

Study design

GO-BACK was a phase IV, randomized, double-blind, parallel-group withdrawal study (protocol 038; EudraCT: 2015–004020-65; NCT: 03253796), which was performed from November 2017 to March 2021 across 62 study centres and nine countries in Europe.

After screening, there were two main study periods (Fig. 1). In period 1 eligible participants were treated with open-label (OL) GLM 50 mg, subcutaneously administered every month (QMT) for ∼10 months. During this time, participants completed the BASDAI questionnaire twice monthly to evaluate clinical response, defined as an improvement in BASDAI score of ≥2 or ≥50% relative to month 0. Participants who achieved a clinical response by month 4 remained on monthly GLM and continued to have their disease status monitored. Participants who achieved an inactive disease status, defined as an Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Score (ASDAS) of <1.3 at both months 7 and 10, were eligible to enter period 2. Participants who did not achieve a clinical response at month 4 or who did not attain inactive disease at either month 7 or month 10 were to be discontinued from the study.

Study design. An interactive voice/web response system was used to assign treatment, including double-blind randomization (1:1:1 ratio) in period 2. In period 2, eligible participants were randomized in a 1:1:1 ratio to monthly placebo (PBO, treatment withdrawal), or continued GLM treatment given monthly (GLM QMT; full-treatment) or every 2 months (GLM Q2MT; reduced-treatment). Participants received blinded treatment for ∼12 months while being monitored for a disease flare. If a participant suspected a ‘flare’ or if a BASDAI score increased by ≥2 compared with month 10, ASDAS was used to confirm the flare. Participants who had a confirmed disease flare, defined as two consecutive ASDAS assessments that both showed an absolute score ≥2.1 and/or a post-withdrawal increase of ≥1.1, were discontinued from blinded treatment and, after ≥10 days from the last dose of period 2 blinded treatment, were retreated with OL GLM QMT for at least 3 months. During retreatment, participants were monitored for clinical response. A clinical response to retreatment was defined as a BASDAI score improvement of ≥2 or ≥50% improvement (relative to the mean of the two BASDAI scores around the time of the ‘flare’) that occurred within 3 months of restarting OL GLM. Participants who did not obtain a clinical response within 3 months of OL GLM retreatment could continue treatment through month 22 or discontinue treatment prematurely per the discretion of the investigator.

For all study participants, regardless of early termination, a safety follow-up phone contact was to occur ∼3 months after the last dose of study treatment to capture any treatment-emergent adverse events.

Efficacy endpoints

Among participants who had attained an inactive disease status in period 1 and received ≥1 dose of blinded treatment (full analysis set population; FAS), the primary efficacy endpoint was the proportion of participants without a disease flare (in each of the continued GLM groups vs PBO) during up to 12 months of double-blind treatment in period 2.

Secondary efficacy endpoints included time-to-first ‘flare’ during double-blind period 2, as well as the proportion of participants who had a disease flare in the treatment withdrawal (PBO) or reduced-treatment (GLM Q2MT) groups who showed a clinical response after OL GLM retreatment. Disease activity measures were also evaluated before and after a disease flare.

Exploratory endpoints evaluated predictors of ‘flare’ and further characterized clinical response before and after a flare.

Safety assessments

Safety and tolerability were assessed by clinical review of all relevant parameters including adverse events (AEs), laboratory tests and vital signs measurements. Prespecified safety events included serious infections, serious opportunistic infections, active tuberculosis, malignancies and serious systemic hypersensitivity (including anaphylactic reaction).

Statistical methods

Efficacy

Efficacy data were analysed by study period and summarized separately for the time before and after OL GLM retreatment for a disease flare, using the respective baselines (Supplementary Material, available at Rheumatology online).

The primary endpoint was a multiplicity-controlled step-down comparison of GLM QMT vs PBO, followed by GLM Q2MT vs PBO, with statistical significance defined by a two-sided α = 0.05. The proportion of participants without a disease flare was analysed using the Miettinen and Nurminen method, with stratification based on CRP level (>6.0 mg/l or ≤6.0 mg/l) and a weighting scheme based on sample size weights. Participants who discontinued period 2 before a disease flare were counted as having a ‘flare’. To further characterize the primary endpoint, full-treatment (GLM QMT) was also compared with combined reduced-treatment/withdrawal (GLM Q2MT + PBO) and reduced-treatment (GLM Q2MT).

Secondary and exploratory analyses assessing clinical response to OL GLM retreatment after a disease flare were based on the Flare FAS population, defined as all FAS participants in the reduced-treatment and withdrawal groups (GLM Q2MT or PBO) who had a disease flare during double-blind period 2. Participants who had a disease flare and did not undergo retreatment or discontinued prior to reattaining inactive disease were counted as having a ‘flare’ without clinical response to retreatment. The Clopper–Pearson exact binomial method was used to calculate a 95% CI on the proportion of participants retreated with OL GLM after a ‘flare’ who showed a clinical response.

Time-to-event analyses (time to first ‘flare’ and time to clinical response after a ‘flare’) were summarized using Kaplan–Meier analysis and a stratified log-rank test. Participants who discontinued or were lost to follow-up without having a disease flare were censored at the time of study discontinuation or the time of last data available, respectively.

Safety

The safety population included all participants as treated who received ≥1 dose of study treatment. Among participants who had a disease flare while on blinded treatment, safety data were summarized for the time before and after OL GLM retreatment.

Institutional review board/ethics committee approvals and informed consent

The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board or independent ethics committee at each investigational site and was conducted in accordance with applicable regulations and the ethical principles of Good Clinical Practice as defined by the International Conference on Harmonization and Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study participation.

Results

Patient disposition

A total of 698 participants were screened for eligibility and 323 were enrolled (Fig. 2). The most common reasons for study ineligibility included meeting the modified New York criteria for AS (34.9%), not having evidence of active SI joint inflammation or a CRP >6.0 mg/l (28.5%), or not meeting the ASAS classification criteria (24.8%).

Participant disposition. Completed study is defined as those participants who completed at least 22 months of study treatment (either double-blind or open-label after a disease flare) and had a safety follow-up contact not less than 76 days from the last dose of study treatment. AS: ankylosing spondyloarthritis; ASAS: Assessment of the SpondyloArthritis International Society; ASDAS: Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Score; CRP: C-reactive protein; GLM: golimumab; MRI: magnetic resonance imaging; NY: New York; QMT: monthly dosing; Q2MT: every 2 months dosing

Among the 323 enrolled participants, 116 (35.9%) discontinued during period 1. Most of these discontinuations (84.5%) were due to failure to achieve a clinical response or an inactive disease status, which was recorded as ‘lack of qualifying event’. Among participants who completed period 1, 18 did not continue into period 2 due to an ineligible ASDAS score, resulting in 189 participants entering period 2 (safety population). One participant, who did not meet the ASDAS criteria for inactive disease status, was randomized and received one dose of GLM Q2MT in error. The remaining 188 eligible participants represent the FAS population. Overall, 175 (93.1%) participants completed period 2 on double-blind treatment or OL retreatment.

Participant baseline characteristics

Baseline demographic and disease-related characteristics were consistent with the target patient population and generally comparable across the study periods and treatment groups (Supplementary Table S1, available at Rheumatology online). In the FAS population (Table 1), participants ranged from 18 to 45 years, with a median age of 31.5 years. Participants in the PBO group tended to be older (higher proportion >30 years) than in either of the GLM groups. All participants were White and the majority were male (70.2%) and non-obese (91.0%). The majority of participants with a primary diagnosis of nr-axSpA were in the early stage of disease, with 59.6% having a disease symptom duration <1 year. All participants had a history of chronic back pain and 63.3% had a CRP >6 mg/l. Of those tested for the human leucocyte antigen (HLA-B27), 86.3% (138 of 160) were positive. Among the participants who had an MRI of the SI joint, 97.4% (75 of 77) had signs of active inflammation suggestive of sacroiliitis. Screening ASDAS score (median 3.8; range 2.1–6.3) and BASDAI score (median 7.0; range 4.1–9.4) were indicative of high disease activity. Other measures of spondyloarthritis characteristics, including total back pain (TBP), Patient Global Disease Assessment (PGD), and Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (BASFI), were also consistent with high disease activity and functional limitations.

Efficacy

Proportion of participants without a disease flare during period 2 double-blind treatment (primary efficacy endpoint)

The proportion of participants without a disease flare was significantly (P < 0.001) higher in both GLM groups (84.1% in GLM QMT and 68.3% in GLM Q2MT) compared with the PBO group (33.9%) (Fig. 3). Only eight participants (none in GLM QMT, five in GLM Q2MT, three in PBO) discontinued period 2 prior to a flare.

Additional pre-specified analyses conducted to further characterize the primary endpoint showed that maintenance on the full-treatment regimen (GLM QMT) resulted in a lower incidence of flare than the combined reduced-treatment/withdrawal regimens (GLM Q2MT + PBO) and the reduced-treatment regimen (GLM Q2MT) (Supplementary Table S2, available at Rheumatology online).

Incidence of disease flare and time to first flare

About one-third (63 of 188) of the participants in the FAS population experienced a disease flare, with an incidence of 61.3%, 23.8%, and 15.9% in the PBO, GLM Q2MT and GLM QMT groups, respectively (Supplementary Table S3, available at Rheumatology online).

Time-to-first flare was significantly (log-rank P < 0.0001) longer for participants in the continued GLM groups (GLM QMT and GLM Q2MT) compared with the PBO group, with a notable separation between the GLM groups and the PBO group observed starting around month 2 (Fig. 4). Within 4 months of treatment withdrawal, the probability of a flare was about 50% compared with about 15% for the continued GLM groups.

Characterization of clinical response after open-label GLM retreatment for a disease flare

A total of 53 participants in the GLM QMT or PBO groups had a confirmed disease flare (Flare FAS) and only one discontinued prior to OL GLM retreatment. Nearly all participants (96.2%) attained a clinical response within the first 3 months of GLM retreatment, although fewer participants (71.7%) sustained the response for all 3 months (Supplementary Tables S4–S6, available at Rheumatology online). The response occurred rapidly, with a 90% probability of a clinical response within 1 month (Supplementary Fig. S1, available at Rheumatology online) and a negligible (≤0.5%) likelihood of achieving a response after 3 months of retreatment (Supplementary Fig. S2, available at Rheumatology online).

Disease activity before and after a disease flare

Prior to a disease flare, overall disease activity among participants entering period 2 with inactive disease (100% of FAS population) who continued GLM treatment remained the most stable relative to treatment withdrawal. The GLM QMT group had the highest proportion of participants maintaining inactive disease (ASDAS <1.3) at all time points (Supplementary Fig. S3, available at Rheumatology online). Similarly, ASAS-20 and BASDAI-50 response rates were highest among participants in the GLM QMT group and significantly better than PBO (Supplementary Table S7, available at Rheumatology online).

After GLM retreatment for a disease flare, over 90% of participants in the reduced-treatment or treatment withdrawal groups showed improvement across all indices of disease activity (Supplementary Table S8, available at Rheumatology online).

Exploratory endpoints

Further characterization of disease activity

Clinical response patterns were generally consistent across the exploratory endpoints characterizing disease activity by study phase, and supportive of continued GLM treatment, particularly with monthly dosing (Supplementary Figs S4–S17, available at Rheumatology online).

Evaluation of predictors of a disease flare and predictors of clinical response to retreatment

There were no notable predictors of disease flare or clinical response to GLM retreatment after flare (Supplementary Figs S18 and S19, available at Rheumatology online).

PK and immunogenicity

Neither anti-drug antibody (ADA)/neutralizing antibody (NAb) status nor GLM serum concentrations were associated with disease flare or clinical response to retreatment (Supplementary Figs S18 to S27 and Supplementary Tables S9–S11, available at Rheumatology online).

Safety

AEs were generally as expected for the study population and consistent with the known safety profile for GLM. No deaths or serious drug-related AEs were reported during the study, and few participants experienced serious AEs (10 participants) or discontinued study treatment due to an AE (eight participants) (Table 2). During double-blind period 2, the overall incidence of AEs (79 participants) and non-serious drug-related AEs (11 participants) were similar across the treatment groups; three participants experienced SAEs (one per treatment group) and no discontinuations due to AEs occurred in the GLM QMT group (Table 2). Across all study periods, the most common AEs (incidence >20%) occurred in the infections and infestation system organ class, which included a total of three serious infections (pilonidal cyst, bacterial sinusitis, acute pyelonephritis) (data not shown). No opportunistic infections, new or reactivated tuberculosis, congestive heart failure or malignancy were reported.

Discussion

TNFi continue to be recommended as one of the first biologic treatments for active nr-axSpA after failure or intolerance to NSAIDs due to their established risk–benefit profile [2, 3]. GLM was approved for treatment of active nr-axSpA in the EU based on the phase III GO-AHEAD trial, which demonstrated superior efficacy and comparable safety of monthly GLM compared with placebo. As the OL extension portion of this study also showed that improvements in disease activity were maintained in participants who continued GLM for up to 52 weeks, as well as in participants who switched from PBO to GLM, it was reasonable to consider withdrawal treatment paradigms. A recent meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials evaluating lower doses or extended dosing intervals of TNFi (including GLM) used in the treatment of axSpA concluded that maintaining the standard dosing regimen probably improves the sustained effect on disease activity and helps to prevent disease flare [13]. The present GO-BACK study, which was designed to evaluate both the effect of GLM withdrawal on disease activity and the impact of GLM retreatment after a disease flare, reinforces the currently labelled GLM dosing regimen (posology) for the nr-axSpA indication.

GO-BACK study participants, who had moderate-to-severe active nr-axSpA at screening, achieved an early clinical response (month 4) and sustained inactive disease (ASDAS <1.3 at both months 7 and 10) when receiving the standard monthly dose of GLM. As expected among participants with sustained inactive disease, both continued GLM treatment regimens (GLM QMT and GLM Q2MT) were shown to be superior to full-treatment withdrawal (PBO) based on the proportion of participants without a disease flare, with a greater treatment difference seen in the labelled GLM QMT dosing (50.2%) than in the reduced dosing (34.4%) regimen. This finding is consistent with the C-OPTIMISE study (including radiographic and nr-axSpA), which showed a significantly higher proportion of patients without flares (ASDAS ≥2.1 at two consecutive visits or ASDAS >3.5 at any visit) receiving full or reduced certolizumab dosing compared with PBO; however, unlike the GO-BACK trial, the treatment differences in each of the continued certolizumab groups relative to PBO were similar (63.5% full dosing; 58.8% reduced dosing) [12]. Other randomized withdrawal clinical trials have similarly demonstrated that complete withdrawal of TNFi results in a high risk for disease flare [10–12].

Further characterization of the primary endpoint indicated that maintenance on the full-treatment regimen (GLM QMT) provided more benefit in reducing the incidence of a disease flare than the combined reduced-treatment/full withdrawal regimens (GLM Q2MT + PBO) or the reduced-treatment regimen (GLM Q2MT). The lower proportion of participants treated who did not experience a disease flare with GLM Q2MT (68.3%) compared with GLM QMT (84.1%) also suggested that more participants had inadequate symptom control following reduced GLM treatment. Indeed, the proportion of participants with clinically important disease worsening (ASDAS change ≥1.1) was higher in the GLM Q2MT group (34.9%) than in the GLM QMT group (22.2%), although both were significantly better than full-treatment withdrawal (66.1%). The overall trend of lower ASDAS, BASDAI, BASFI, TBP and PGD scores with continued-treatment vs reduced-treatment was also consistent with this observation. Further evaluation of GLM QMT vs GLM Q2MT was not performed as this study was primarily designed for placebo-controlled comparisons. Additionally, due to the low number of flares in this study, further subgroup analysis that might inform upon predictors of flare is not possible.

Overall, approximately half of the study participants developed anti-GLM antibodies. The high incidence of ADA is not unexpected and has been observed in other GLM studies using the same highly sensitive drug-tolerant assay [14–16]. Although some studies in rheumatic conditions have suggested that immunogenicity may influence clinical response in patients treated with TNFi [17, 18], no associations have been made in previous GLM rheumatological studies to date [14, 16]. The absence of an association may be due to predominantly low titres. In the current study, in which only a handful of participants exhibited high ADA titre (>1000), ADA status was not a predictor of flare. Additionally, serum GLM concentrations were not associated with disease flare.

One of the theoretical risks associated with a reduced-treatment regimen is the potential for patients to be less responsive to retreatment. In this study, ADA status did not impact the ability to reattain a clinical response after retreatment, as nearly all participants who had a disease flare and were retreated with monthly GLM were able to achieve a rapid clinical response (>90% within 1 month of retreatment). However, only about three-quarters of participants who attained a clinical response were able to sustain it throughout the 3-month retreatment period. A trend of increasing ADA-positive status was noted with the reduced-treatment and full-withdrawal regimens; the potential for further anti-GLM antibody formation with long-term reduced dosing cannot be ruled out as patients in this trial generally flared early and were promptly switched to monthly GLM. Taken together, these data further support the established benefits of treatment persistence with the approved dosing regimen.

Numerous clinical trials and ongoing pharmacovigilance have shown GLM to be generally safe and well tolerated. The current study reinforces the safety and tolerability of the use of GLM in patients with nr-axSpA. No deaths or serious drug-related AEs were reported during any phase of the study. Additionally, the incidence of serious AEs, non-serious drug-related AEs and study treatment discontinuations due to AEs was low. As expected, the most frequently reported AEs occurred in the infections and infestations system organ class. One theoretical benefit of a reduced-treatment regimen would be to potentially reduce the risk of serious side effects associated with TNFi. When this study was initiated, the main labelled risks of GLM treatment included serious infections (including opportunistic infections and tuberculosis), congestive heart failure, malignancy and infusion-related reactions. In comparison with the C-OPTIMISE study in which all serious AEs occurred in participants within the full dosing regimen, during this study three participants experienced treatment-emergent serious AEs in period 2, one occurring in each of the three treatment groups. Most treatment-emergent AEs occurred early in the study (period 1). Collectively, the study results do not suggest any notable safety benefit of a reduced-treatment regimen or meaningful impact of anti-GLM antibodies.

In conclusion, in this study of patients with active nr-axSpA who had attained inactive disease after receiving open-label GLM during a 10-month run-in, continued treatment with GLM QMT (full-treatment) or GLM Q2MT (reduced-treatment) was superior to placebo (full-treatment withdrawal). Monthly GLM maintenance treatment (GLM QMT) was most beneficial in reducing the incidence of disease flares as well as in improving clinical signs and symptoms of nr-axSpA compared with treatment withdrawal (GLM Q2MT and PBO). Reattainment of clinical response after retreatment with GLM QMT for a disease flare was achievable for most participants, albeit about 25% fewer sustained the response. GLM was generally well tolerated; the safety results were consistent with the known safety profile of GLM.
