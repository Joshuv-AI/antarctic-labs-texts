Valence-State-Dependent Surface Lattice Oxygen in CeO2‑Modified VPO Catalysts: Elucidating the Mechanism of n‑Butane Selective Oxidation to Maleic Anhydride
Authors: Wenjiao Li, Shupeng Guo, Heqin Guo, Yaqiong Su, Yong Xiao, Minggui Lin, Litao Jia, Debao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The selective oxidation of n-butane to maleic anhydride (MA) over the vanadium phosphorus oxide (VPO) catalyst stands as a prominent example of industrial selective alkane functionalization. Despite its commercial maturity, the ambiguous relationship between the catalytic performance and vanadium valence states (Vox) or surface lattice oxygen (OS‑L) persists in generating debate and uncertainty regarding the identity of the active sites in VPO catalysts. Herein, we reconcile this debate by demonstrating that the catalytic efficiency is governed by valence-state-dependent OS‑L rather than by merely the OS‑L content. Using time-resolved H2-TPR experiments, we successfully distinguished two distinct types of valence-state-dependent OS‑L: V5+-OS‑L (OS‑L related to the V5+ phase) and V4+-OS‑L (OS‑L related to the V4+ phase). Their specific functions were then elucidated through a combination of in situ reactant-specific H2-TPR experiments (utilizing n-butane, 1,3-butadiene, or propylene as probes) and 18O-isotopic labeling studies. The results clearly demonstrate that V5+-OS‑L is identified as the site for the initial hydrogen abstraction from n-butane, while V4+-OS‑L facilitates the crucial epoxidation step to form MA. Furthermore, by employing CeO2 as a modulator, we precisely tune the V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio through the formation of Ce–O–V bonds, as evidenced by the X-ray absorption near edge (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analysis. An optimal V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio for MA synthesis is 2.22–2.82; otherwise, it would be more conducive to a nonselective reaction. This work introduces a novel design paradigm for the development of high-performance VPO catalysts, offering a comprehensive framework for the rational design of heterogeneous oxidation catalysts via precise control of valence-specific oxygen chemistry.

Introduction

Catalytic valorization of light alkanes is at the forefront of sustainable chemical synthesis, offering a route to transform alkanes into high-value oxygenated compounds, and effectively avoiding the wasting of abundant byproducts in the refining and shale gas production processes. The selective oxidation of n-butane to maleic anhydride (MA) over a vanadium phosphorus oxide (VPO) catalyst is the only industrially implemented alkane selective oxidation. , This process involves a series of steps: the gradual dehydrogenation of n-butane to 1-butene and subsequent 1,3-butadiene, followed by oxygen insertion and cyclization to produce MA. Generally, this transformation requires the removal of eight hydrogen atoms and consumes 3.5 mol of oxygen per mole of MA produced. The catalytic performance is influenced by various parameters, including the surface P/V ratio, surface lattice/absorbed oxygen ratio, Vox, and crystal structure, as well as the specific surface area. − Decades of research have validated the Mars-van Krevelen (MvK) mechanism, identifying (VO)2P2O7 (V4+) and minor VOPO4 (V5+) as the dominant active phases, with OS‑L serving as the reactive oxygen species. The V5+ phase can exist as various polymorphs (αI-, β-, γ-, δ-VOPO4), each with distinct structural features and oxygen reactivity that influence catalytic performance. − It is recognized that Vox and OS‑L content are consistently identified as the most critical factors governing the catalytic activity and MA selectivity. ,, However, the interdependent nature of the various factors influencing the performance of catalysts has increased the difficulty of researching the intrinsic active sites of VPO catalysts.

The influence of the V5+/V4+ ratio, the P/V ratio, and other factors on Vox and OS‑L content complicates the identification of intrinsic active sites. Consequently, despite the commercial maturity of the VPO catalyst, ,, the catalytic mechanism, particularly the role of Vox and OS‑L, remains a subject of significant debate. According to the literature, the V5+ phase may be related to n-butane dehydrogenation, ,, while the V4+ phase may be involved in the epoxidation of alkenes. Notably, the entire oxidation process is necessarily accompanied by the utilization of active OS‑L species. Recent studies have employed low-activity TiPO catalysts to investigate the mechanism governing the selective oxidation of n-butane. Online mass spectrometry techniques were used to capture intermediates, including furan and 2-furanone, thereby providing mechanistic insights into how the dehydrogenation of n-butane primarily proceeds at Brønsted acid sites, while the formation of MA is achieved through the MvK mechanism. However, the applicability of these findings to highly active VPO catalysts remains limited due to the extremely low efficiency of n-butane oxidation on TiPO catalysts. Direct evidence concerning the role of V5+ and V4+ phases remains insufficient, while investigations of the reactive oxygen species required for the reaction are more complex and relatively scarce. Researchers have reported that the active OS‑L is sourced predominantly from the bulk (VO)2P2O7 phase, and the transformation from inactive to active lattice oxygen species significantly enhances the selectivity of MA. Various alternative strategies have been developed to enhance the performance of VPO catalysts, including modification with ionic liquids (ILs) , or deep eutectic solvents (DESs) to tune the surface acidity, redox properties, and morphology. A systematic review by He et al. comprehensively summarizes the roles of ILs as structure-directing agents, electronic modulators, and interfacial modifiers in catalytic materials, thereby providing a valuable methodological framework. Although these advancements have expanded our understanding of the active sites, further evidence regarding the active OS‑L and its dynamic behavior remains elusive.

The lack of a consensus on the optimal level of Vox, coupled with the inconsistent influence of OS‑L content on the MA yield, constitutes a long-standing challenge. , Given this, we hypothesize that the “OS‑L activity” is linked to its specific Vox center, rather than merely the “OS‑L content”. To test this, we propose innovative research experiments that focus on the correlation between OS‑L and Vox. The experiments further subdivide OS‑L into V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L, and explore their synergistic effects. To achieve this aim, we selected CeO2 with different morphologies (confirmed by the XRD patterns and Raman spectra in Figure S1) as modifiers due to their well-established oxygen storage capacity and the redox properties, which originate from the facile Ce4+/Ce3+ redox cycle and associated oxygen vacancy formation. CeO2 was chosen not only for its well-established oxygen storage capacity but also because its redox properties (Ce4+/Ce3+ratio) and oxygen vacancy concentration are highly morphology-dependent. Nanorods (exposing (110) and (100) planes), nanocubes ((100) planes), and nano-octahedra ((111) planes) exhibit distinct surface Ce3+ fractions and vacancy densities, enabling systematic modulation of the electronic interaction with the VPO catalyst. We anticipated that these morphology-dependent properties would allow the precise tuning of the V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio through the controlled formation of Ce–O–V bonds, a degree of control difficult to achieve with conventional metal dopants.

Combining online mass spectrometry and in situ isotope analysis, we aim to directly probe the individual roles of V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L, thereby reconciling existing controversies and enhancing the understanding of the MvK mechanism for the n-butane selective oxidation to MA over the VPO catalyst. Ultimately, this will enable the rational design of MvK-type catalysts with high OS‑L utilization efficiency.

Results and Discussion

Discrimination of Valence-Specific Lattice Oxygen Species

To experimentally verify our hypothesis regarding the existing various types of OS‑L, we first employed H2-TPR experiments to probe the distribution of oxygen species of CeO2/VPO catalysts. Building on this, we have innovatively designed time-resolved H2-TPR to dynamically classify and track the evolution of the OS‑L species during the phase transformation of VPO catalysts.

As shown in Figure (a), the H2 consumption peaks observed in the ranges of 400–600 °C, 600–720 °C, and 720–850 °C can be attributed to the consumption of OS‑L related to the V5+ phase (V5+-OS‑L), OS‑L related to the V4+ phase (V4+-OS‑L), and bulk lattice oxygen related to both V5+ and V4+ phases (V5+,4+-OB‑L), respectively. To validate this assignment, we performed time-resolved H2-TPR experiments (Figure b). It should be noted that VPO catalysts may undergo structural evolution during temperature-programmed experiments above 400–500 °C. , However, our TG-DTA analysis (Figure S6) shows that the major phase transformation from precursor to active phase occurs below 450 °C. Therefore, while some surface restructuring may occur, the main reduction features can be reliably attributed to the reduction of V5+ and V4+ species in the working catalyst.

(a) H2-TPR curves of CeO2/VPO catalysts and VOPO4 and (b) the time-resolved H2-TPR curves of pure VOPO4 pretreated at mild deoxygenation conditions for different times, until VOPO4 was completely transformed into pure (VO)2P2O7.

In Figure (b), pure VOPO4 (confirmed by the XRD patterns in Figure S2) shows two H2 consumption peaks, representing V5+-OS‑L and V5+-OB‑L, respectively. After a pretreatment for just 1 min, the concentration of V5+-OS‑L dropped sharply, contrasting with a gradual increase in V4+-OS‑L and a marked rise in V5+, 4+-OB‑L. As the pretreatment time increased to 5 min, the decline of V5+-OS‑L and the growth of V4+-OS‑L both continued gradually, while the level of V5+, 4+-OB‑L stabilized. With further extension of the pretreatment time, the pure VOPO4 sample was completely transformed into (VO)2P2O7 (confirmed by the XRD patterns in Figure S2). Consequently, the peaks corresponding to V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L completely disappear, leaving only the stable V5+, 4+-OB‑L peak.

It should be noted that the pure VOPO4 phase used in our time-resolved H2-TPR experiments may exist as multiple polymorphs, depending on the preparation and pretreatment conditions. A recent review by Sang and colleagues systematically summarizes the crystallographic evolution of VPO catalysts, detailing the structural diversity of V5+-containing VOPO4 polymorphs (αI-, αII-, β-, γ-, δ-VOPO4) and their distinct catalytic behaviors in n-butane oxidation. Previous studies have shown that different VOPO4 polymorphs exhibit distinct oxygen reactivity and reduction behavior. ,, In our experiment, the VOPO4 precursor was prepared via a well-established method that typically yields a mixture of hydrated VOPO4 phases.

During the slow deoxygenation process, phase transformations among different VOPO4 polymorphs may occur prior to complete conversion to (VO)2P2O7. Consequently, the reduction peaks attributed to V5+-OS‑L in our H2-TPR analysis reflect the average behavior of V5+-associated oxygen species, potentially incorporating contributions from multiple VOPO4 polymorphs. The consistent trends observed across various catalysts, especially the correlation between V5+-OS‑L consumption and n-butane activation, indicate that the fundamental role of V5+-associated lattice oxygen in C–H activation is robust across diverse V5+ local coordination environments. However, the specific influence of distinct VOPO4 polymorphs on the reactivity of V5+-OS‑L necessitates further investigation utilizing well-defined single-phase VOPO4 materials.

Another important consideration is the potential for phosphorus loss during high-temperature treatments. VPO catalysts are known to experience phosphorus volatilization under reaction conditions. Although such loss could influence reduction profiles, the strong correlation between H2-TPR-derived oxygen speciation and catalytic performance (Table and Figure S4b) indicates that the quantified V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L species are characteristic of the catalytically active surface rather than being attributable to degradation processes. This correlation provides evidence that the oxygen species identified by H2-TPR are functionally relevant to the working catalyst.

Although H2-TPR curves of complex oxides such as VPO can exhibit overlapping reduction processes, our time-resolved H2-TPR experiments with online mass spectrometry enabled clear observations. The changes in the sequential H2-consumption behavior directly reveal the evolution of oxygen species related to Vox during the phase transition from pure VOPO4 to pure (VO)2P2O7. These observations allow us to reasonably attribute the first reduction process to V5+-OS‑L and the second one to V4+-OS‑L, and confirm that the attribution of OS‑L related to V ox is reasonable. Having distinguished V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L within the total OS‑L, we next employed in situ probing actions to directly assign their specific functions within the n-butane oxidation process.

Isotopic Probing Function of Vanadium Valence-Specific OS‑L

The Crucial Role of V5+-OS‑L in the Dehydrogenation of n-Butane

In order to distinguish the roles of V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L, in situ n-butane/H2-TPR experiments were employed (Figure ). The OS‑L unconsumed in the VPO catalyst was quantified after n-butane dehydrogenation for different times, and the results are listed in Table S1. As shown in Figure (a,b) and Table S1, prolonged n-butane exposure leads to the rapid consumption of V5+-OS‑L on both B-VPO and R-VPO catalysts, while V4+-OS‑L remains largely unchanged. Thus, V5+-OS‑L is the primary site for the initial n-butane dehydrogenation.

(a) Consumption of OL for B-VPO and (b) R-VPO in the in situ n-butane/H2-TPR experiments, (c) generation of exhaust gas on B-VPO catalyst during the n-C4H10/TPRO experiments, and (d) MS signals of 16O2 in the TPRO section curves of VPO catalysts.

Given that the altered OS‑L content (the black curves in Figure a,b) was observed even in VPO catalysts treated at 430 °C for 0 min compared with Figure (a), the dynamic reaction process necessitates further investigation. Therefore, in situ n-C4H10/TPRO experiments were performed. This technique was employed to directly monitor the reaction behavior of n-butane while also assessing the consumption of OS‑L during the reaction process. The signals of H2O, CO x , and 1-butene were detected immediately upon the introduction of n-butane (Figure c). This observation indicates that n-butane undergoes rapid activation and dehydrogenation upon contact with VPO catalysts, and this process may lead to the consumption of V5+-OS‑L from the catalyst. Therefore, the oxidation of the catalyst by O2 after 60 min of dehydrogenation was observed. Figure (d) shows the corresponding O2 consumption profile, and the main O2 consumption peak of all catalysts is ∼500 °C, which can be attributed to the regeneration of V5+-OS‑L (Figure a).

This finding further demonstrates that the active sites for n-butane dehydrogenation are the V5+-OS‑L species. The removal temperature of V5+-OS‑L decreases as it is consumed, and this trend is more pronounced on the R-VPO catalyst compared with the B-VPO catalyst. This indicates that V5+-OS‑L is more easily removed and utilized on the R-VPO catalyst than on the B-VPO catalyst.

By labeling active OS‑L using isotope probing techniques, the intermediate products in its reaction with n-butane can be clearly traced. The fundamental question of whether the two OS‑L types are consumed via a selective pathway, however, remains unresolved. To elucidate this matter, we conducted 18O2-cyclic in situ n-butane/H2-TPR experiments to trace the footprint of the supplemented oxygen species. First, the VPO catalyst was pretreated in an Ar atmosphere at 250 °C for 1 h to eliminate the interference from surface adsorbed oxygen (OS‑A). This pretreatment temperature was chosen based on (as shown by the O2-TPD curves in Figure S3), which confirms that 250 °C is sufficient to remove surface adsorbed oxygen and hydroxyl groups without affecting lattice oxygen species, thereby ensuring that the subsequently detected H2 18O originates solely from the reaction of V5+-18OS‑L with n-butane-derived hydrogen. Figure (a) depicts the 18O2 consumption processes of the catalysts. Two distinct peaks centered at around 500 and 700 °C can be observed, assigned to V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L, respectively. The oxidation temperature of the R-VPO catalyst is significantly lower than that of the B-VPO catalyst. This indicated that the reduced R-VPO catalyst is more easily oxidized and can revert to its initial state, thereby ensuring the stability of the entire redox reaction. Figure (b) illustrates the evolution of n-butane, 1-butene, and 1,3-butadiene after the introduction of n-butane into the VPO catalysts following 18O2 supplementation (VP18O catalysts). Figure (c),(d) show that H2 18O is generated on the VP18O catalysts. The consumption of n-butane is accompanied by the production of H2 18O and 1-butene; however, 1,3-butadiene is not detected. The absence of 1,3-butadiene is likely attributable to its rapid conversion under the experimental conditions, preventing its detection within the measurement system.

The isotopic tracing results clearly demonstrate the distinct catalytic behaviors between the two catalysts. The isotopic product distributions (Figure ) reveal a fundamental difference in the oxidation pathways. On B-VP18O, C18O and C16O18O are detected alongside C16O2, indicating nonselective carbon oxide formation. In contrast, R-VP18O produces only C16O2, with no detectable C18O or C16O18O, reflecting the markedly superior selectivity for MA. Neither catalyst generates C18O2.

The Crucial Role of V4+-OS‑L in the Epoxidation of 1,3-Butadiene

In order to further illustrate the roles of V4+-OS‑L, in situ 1,3-butadiene/H2-TPR experiments were employed (Figure ). As the time of introduction of 1,3-butadiene increases, V4+-OS‑L diminishes rapidly and eventually disappears for B-VPO and R-VPO catalysts, while V5+-OS‑L remains largely unchanged. This indicates that upon contact with the VPO catalyst, 1,3-butadiene mainly reacts with V4+-OS‑L, whereas the consumption of V5+-OS‑L lags significantly. Consequently, we propose that V4+-OS‑L may act as an active OS‑L responsible for the epoxidation of 1,3-butadiene. To further elucidate the role of V4+-OS‑L, we employed two probe molecules: 1,3-butadiene (the direct intermediate in n-butane oxidation) and propylene (whose epoxidation product is more readily detectable, serving as a sensitive reporter for oxygen insertion capability). Although 1,3-butadiene is a direct intermediate, under our experimental conditions, its epoxidation product could not be reliably detected due to instrumental limitations and the complexity of the reaction. Propylene (via propylene oxide) provides a well-established and sensitive method for detecting epoxidation activity, thereby offering conclusive evidence of the role of V4+-OS‑L in the oxygen insertion step. To further substantiate this conjecture, the 18O2-cyclic in situ 1,3-butadiene/H2-TPR experiments were employed to trace the product composition arising from the reaction between the VPO catalyst containing 18OS‑L and 1,3-butadiene (Figure ).

Two distinct peaks observed around 500 and 700 °C can be attributed to the V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L species, respectively. The catalysts primarily replenished V4+-OS‑L, indicating that the reaction with 1,3-butadiene mainly consumed the V4+-OS‑L species. However, due to equipment limitations and the intrinsic complexity of the reaction, the target products from the reaction between V4+-OS‑L and 1,3-butadiene were not detected. Propylene consumption occurs concurrently with the generation of H2O, CO2, PO, and furan. The R-VPO catalyst yields a greater quantity of epoxide products compared to the B-VPO catalyst (Figure c). Furthermore, postreaction analysis reveals complete depletion of V4+-OS‑L on catalysts in comparison to its prereaction state (Figure d). These results conclusively demonstrate that V4+-OS‑L is the primary active species for the epoxidation step.

The Crucial Role of the V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L Ratio

Therefore, V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L of VPO catalysts exert distinct effects at different stages of the selective oxidation of n-butane to MA. The ratio of these species influences the n-butane oxidation reaction. This profound contrast in the performance of VPO catalysts might originate from the distinct ratios of V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L. This structure–activity relationship is further illuminated by the performance of VPO catalysts with a pure V5+ phase composition, where n-butane conversion is low despite an apparently high MA selectivity (Table ). This paradox indicates that without the synergistic V4+-OS‑L, the reaction initiated by V5+-OS‑L cannot propagate sustainably. The selective oxidation of n-butane to MA is a multistep process wherein V5+-OS‑L is responsible for the dehydrogenation of n-butane, whereas V4+-OS‑L participates in the subsequent step. The cooperation between V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L is essential to sustaining the catalytic cycle.

To quantitatively verify this synergistic mechanism and its dependence on the balance between V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L, we systematically evaluated a series of CeO2/VPO catalysts. Their catalytic performance was correlated with the V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio (Table ), as determined by H2-TPR and XPS (Figure S4a XPS O1s spectra), respectively. The results are illuminating: while the C-VPO catalyst possesses a higher OS‑L content, its MA yield is only slightly improved compared with that of the B-VPO catalyst, whereas the R-VPO catalyst with reduced V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L content achieves the highest MA yield. Moreover, no clear correlation was observed between the V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L content and the catalytic performance; however, a volcano-type relationship exists between the V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio and MA yield (Figure S4b). This indicates that the balance between the two oxygen species, rather than their individual quantities, governs the selective oxidation efficiency. Further analysis reveals a volcano-type relationship between the V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio and MA yield (Figure S4b). The ascending branch of the volcano (ratio < 2.22) corresponds to insufficient V5+-OS‑L for efficient n-butane dehydrogenation, while the descending branch (ratio > 2.82) reflects excessive V5+-OS‑L that promotes nonselective overoxidation of intermediates to CO x . The optimal ratio window of 2.22–2.82 represents a kinetic balance where the rates of dehydrogenation (mediated by V5+-OS‑L) and epoxidation (mediated by V4+-OS‑L) are well matched, minimizing the steady-state concentration of partially oxidized intermediates susceptible to overoxidation. The R-VPO catalyst exhibits a favorable V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio compared to the other catalysts, which contributes to its high MA yield. The catalytic activities at different temperatures (Figure S5a,c) and GHSV (total gas hourly space velocity) (Figure S5b,d) were investigated within the range of experimental error. R-VPO catalyst exhibits an excellent MA yield due to its highest n-butane conversion, while simultaneously maintaining a high MA selectivity. To ensure the reliability of our findings, we further characterized the thermal stability (Figure S6TG-DTA profiles of precursors), microstructure (Figure S7 SEM images of precursors, catalysts, and used catalysts), and phase composition (Figure S8, FT-IR, XRD, and Raman patterns of precursors and catalysts) of CeO2/VPO catalysts. These results confirm that the textural properties, such as specific surface area and morphology, remain largely unchanged at low introduction levels of CeO2. TG and DTG analyses (Figure S6) show that all precursors undergo three distinct weight-loss stages, with the major phase transformation to active (VO)2P2O7 completed below 450 °C. The specific surface areas of the activated catalysts (Table S2) range from 13.2 to 16.0 m2/g, and after 8 h on stream, the surface area decreases by less than 8% for all samples, indicating good thermal stability under reaction conditions. The primary change lies in the distributions of V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L (Table ). These revealed that the active OS‑L on the VPO catalyst, referred to as “OS‑L activity”, is mainly composed of V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L, rather than being defined by “OS‑L content”.

Building on these insights, we further elucidate how CeO2 with various morphologies modifies V ox for the VPO catalyst to varying degrees, thereby modulating the distribution of V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L on the catalyst surface. The R-VPO catalyst exhibits a suitable V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio, which effectively promotes n-butane dehydrogenation and epoxidation, resulting in the formation of MA with minimal peroxide reactions. This might be the reason that a high MA yield can be consistently achieved with the R-VPO catalyst (Figure S9e, stability test of the R-VPO catalyst).

In summary, the results from the 18O-isotopic labeling experiments provide direct evidence for the involvement of V5+-OS‑L in the initial C–H activation of n-butane. Structurally, the VPO catalyst consists of a large number of V4+ phases and a small number of V5+ phases, which jointly provide the necessary OS‑L for the multistep oxidation process. Given that the n-butane reaction demands a significant amount of lattice oxygen, it is probable that V4+-OS‑L serves as the primary source of OL for the epoxidation process, as supported by its preferential consumption during 1,3-butadiene and propylene oxidation experiments. Consequently, the catalytic performance is governed by a synergistic interplay between V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L.

Identification of an Optimal V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L Ratio

Having established the critical roles of V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L, we aimed to determine the optimal balance between them and the mechanisms for achieving this balance. Our initial results revealed that the addition of R-CeO2 to the VPO catalyst produces a favorable V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio, whereas the incorporation of C-CeO2 or O-CeO2 results in an excessively high ratio due to the increased concentration of oxygen vacancies (OV) (Table S3, Ce3+ contents determined from XPS). The higher the Ce3+ content in CeO2, the greater the oxygen vacancy density, which aids in the formation of Ce–O–V bonds on the catalyst surface, facilitating electron transfer from V to Ce. This electron transfer stabilizes the V5+ species, thereby increasing the V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio. Consequently, this ratio follows the order R-VPO (2.22) < C-VPO (4.83) < O-VPO (5.34), which is consistent with the increasing trend in Ce3+ content in the corresponding CeO2 modifiers. To elucidate the mechanism by which R-CeO2 modulates the V5+/V4+ ratio, X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) techniques, including X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES), extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS), and Wavelet Transform EXAFS (WT-EXAFS), were employed to investigate the chemical state of Ce and its influence on Vox for the VPO catalyst (Figure ).

Additionally, the shift in the peak position of the R-VPO catalyst can provide direct evidence for the formation of V–O–Ce bonds. Figure (c) reveals that the V valence state of B-VPO and R-VPO catalysts lies between VO2 and V2O5, demonstrating that the Vox of these two samples is mostly within +4 and +5. According to the near-edge absorption, the V absorption energy of the R-VPO catalyst is greater than that of the B-VPO catalyst, which indicates that Vox of R-VPO is higher than that of B-VPO. Figure (b) shows that R-VPO has two white line absorption peaks at 5728.1 and 5736.7 eV, while CeO2 has two such peaks at 5732.0 and 5738.5 eV. This indicates that both samples contain the coexistence of Ce3+/Ce4+ mixed oxidation states. Among them, compared to CeO2, the white line of the R-VPO catalyst is located at a lower energy level, suggesting that the valence state of Ce in the R-VPO catalyst is lower and the ratio of Ce3+/Ce4+ is increased. Figure (d)­(f) show that the R-VPO catalyst shifts toward larger K values and extends to a higher R region compared with the B-VPO catalyst. This indicates that the bond lengths of some V–O bonds in the R-VPO catalyst have increased, related to the formation of V–O–Ce bonds.

The EXAFS analysis provides direct evidence for the formation of V–O–Ce bonds in the 5R-VPO catalyst, which underpins the electronic interaction between CeO2 and the VPO catalyst. To confirm that this electronic interaction was specific to the in situ formation during calcination, we prepared a mixture of preformed R-CeO2 nanorods and the 5Rs-VPO catalyst. This catalyst exhibits a significant decrease in MA selectivity along with the reduced MA yield; however, its MA yield is similar to that of the B-VPO catalyst (Table S4, catalytic activities of xR-VPO catalysts). This further demonstrates that using the CeO2 nanorod precursor enables Ce to form Ce–O–V bonds with the VPO catalyst during calcination. The critical importance of in situ bond formation is further underscored by a control experiment using a physically mixed sample (Rs-VPO), prepared by first calcining R-CeO2 nanorods and then mixing them with the PVPO precursor, followed by activation. As shown in Table S4, Rs-VPO exhibits a markedly lower MA yield (41.0%) compared with 5R-VPO (51.4%), despite having an identical nominal composition. Characterization confirms that Rs-VPO lacks the V–O–Ce bond signature observed in 5R-VPO (Figure ). This result provides direct evidence that the formation of V–O–Ce bonds and the consequent tuning of the V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio requires in situ formation during co-calcination and cannot be achieved by simple physical mixing. The absence of such bonds in Rs-VPO leads to an unfavorable oxygen species distribution and inferior catalytic performance. In contrast, when the stable CeO2 nanorods are used, no electronic effect was observed. Therefore, mixing the CeO2 precursor with PVPO and undergoing calcination activation together, the formation of V–O–Ce bonds within the catalyst occurs alongside electron transfer from V to Ce, thereby regulating the V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio.

Consequently, varying the amount of R-CeO2 may modulate the electron transfer from V to Ce, thereby enabling precise tuning of the V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio on the VPO catalyst (Figure S9a, the modification process of the catalyst with different V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratios). To verify this hypothesis, the chemical states of the modified catalysts were examined by XPS (Figure S9b, XPS Ce 3d for CeO2 with different morphologies). There are no Ce 3d-related peaks at low R-CeO2 content; however, the distinct Ce 3d peaks emerged on the 7R-VPO catalyst as the R-CeO2 content increased (Figure S9c, XPS Ce 3d for xR-VPO catalysts). Concurrently, the O 1s peak shifted to a lower binding energy with the increasing R-CeO2 content (Figure S9d, XPS O 1s for B-VPO and xR-VPO catalysts). This shift indicates an increase in electron density around oxygen atoms within the VPO catalyst, suggesting the occurrence of electron transfer from V to Ce and the consequent modulation for Vox. Similarly, H2-TPR experiments were performed to analyze the effect of the R-CeO2 content on the V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio. All xR-CeO2/VPO samples exhibit three typical reduction peaks, and with the increase in the amount of R-CeO2, the first reduction peak broadened significantly (Figure S10a). Notably, a distinct shoulder peak emerged at the 7R-VPO catalyst. The change suggests that the introduction of R-CeO2 modulates the valence state of V on the VPO catalyst and modifies the distribution of V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L, thereby altering their redox capabilities.

As anticipated, the thermal stability (Figure S11) and morphology (Figure S10c) of VPO catalysts remain largely unaffected, whereas the V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio follows a volcano-shaped trend, with the increasing introduction of R-CeO2 (Figure S10b). Furthermore, it is demonstrated that the MA yield experienced a clear trend of rising first and then declining with the increased V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio, reaching its peak on the 5R-VPO catalyst (Figure e). The optimal ratio range (2.22–2.82) represents a kinetic balance where the rates of dehydrogenation are well matched, minimizing nonselective pathways. Compared with the B-VPO catalyst, the MA selectivity for the 3R-VPO catalyst has been significantly improved (Table S4). To gain deeper insight into the performance, we conducted 18O-cyclic in situ n-butane/H2-TPR characterization on the 3R-VPO catalyst and compared it with the B-VPO and 5R-VPO catalysts.

Similar to the B-VPO and 5R-VPO catalysts, the 3R-VPO catalyst exhibits dual 18O consumption peaks at around 500 and 700 °C (Figure a). Notably, compared with the B-VPO and 5R-VPO catalysts, the 3R-VPO catalyst demonstrates enhanced 18O uptake at ∼700 °C (associated with V4+-OS‑L regeneration), potentially correlating with its superior MA selectivity. The 3R-VPO catalyst further decreased the reoxidation temperature to ∼460 °C, indicating accelerated oxygen replenishment kinetics. Figure (a),(b) demonstrate that there is negligible production of C18O, C16O18O, or C18O2, confirming that a certain V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio can effectively promote n-butane conversion and further generate MA.

Collectively, these isotopic and product analyses demonstrate the following: (1) n-butane first combines with V5+-OS‑L to produce 1-butene and H2O; (2) subsequently, V4+-OS‑L and alkenes form MA through an oxygen insertion reaction; and (3) on the VPO catalyst, an optimal V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio balance (2.22–2.82) facilitates the efficient conversion of n-butane to MA.

The Proposed Catalytic Mechanism

Based on the collective evidence from time-resolved H2-TPR experiments, 18O-isotopic labeling, and XAFS studies, we propose a refined catalytic cycle for the selective oxidation of n-butane to MA over the CeO2-modified VPO catalyst (Figure ). This cycle highlights the indispensable and complementary roles of the two valence-dependent lattice oxygen species (V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L) and elucidates the promoting effect of CeO2.

A refined catalytic cycle for the selective oxidation of n-butane to MA over the CeO2-modified VPO catalyst.

The two active OS‑L species (V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L) play distinct roles in the selective oxidation of n-butane, which proceeds through redox reactions at sites related to V4+ and V5+. It is widely accepted that n-butane dehydrogenation is the decisive step in the entire reaction. The catalytic cycle initiates by the abstraction of a H atom from an n-butane molecule by V5+-OS‑L, which cleaves a C–H bond to form a butyl radical (C4H9 •) and a V5+–OH species (eq ) (Figures S12 and S13). The butyl radical subsequently undergoes rapid β-H elimination to form 1-butene, while the hydrogen radical combines with the V5+–OH group, releasing a water molecule (Figure S14) and reducing V5+-related sites to V4+-related sites (eq ). This step is unequivocally attributed to V5+-OS‑L, as confirmed by its rapid consumption and the production of H2 18O from V5+-OS‑L in the isotopic labeling experiments.

While V5+-OS‑L activates the n-butane, the functionalization of the intermediate is facilitated by V4+-OS‑L. It is generally believed that the conversion of 1-butene to 1,3-butadiene follows a mechanism similar to that in Figure (Step 1). The further oxidation of the intermediate 1,3-butadiene relies on the supply of V4+-OS‑L. This oxygen species, being more nucleophilic, inserts into the alkene intermediate to form key C–O bonds, leading to the epoxidation steps to MA (eq ). The synergistic interaction between these oxygen species creates a catalytic cycle, the efficiency of which is governed by their kinetic balance. A compelling question arises regarding how to maintain a high steady-state concentration of the active V5+-OS‑L, which is consumed during the initiation stage. The critical role of CeO2 is to facilitate the reoxidation of sites related to the reduced form of V4+, thereby completing the catalytic cycle. Through the formed V–O–Ce bond, an electron is transferred from V4+ to Ce4+, thereby reducing the latter to Ce3+ and generating an OV on the CeO2 surface (eq ). This electron transfer stabilizes the density of V5+-OS‑L on the catalyst surface.

The electronic transfer process mediated by CeO2 can be further understood as follows: upon formation of the V–O–Ce bond, the electron density around V decreases, indicating partial oxidation of V4+ to V5+. Concurrently, Ce4+ is reduced to Ce3+, generating an oxygen vacancy on the CeO2 surface. This vacancy can be replenished by gaseous O2, completing the redox cycle. The Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio thus directly influences the steady-state concentration of V5+-OS‑L; a higher Ce3+ fraction (more vacancies) promotes electron transfer and favors V5+ formation, but excessive Ce3+ also enhances nonselective overoxidation. This electronic interplay between Ce and V is the key to understanding the morphology-dependent catalytic behavior.

Consequently, the overall catalytic performance is determined by the dynamic equilibrium between dehydrogenation and oxygen insertion capabilities, quantitatively reflected in the V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio. An optimal ratio, ranging from 2.22 to 2.82, signifies an ideal kinetic balance. This ratio guarantees a sufficient quantity of V5+-OS‑L for the efficient dehydrogenation of n-butane, while simultaneously providing an adequate number of V4+-OS‑L to rapidly functionalize the alkene intermediates toward MA, thereby minimizing their residence time and subsequent overoxidation. The presence of the CeO2 promoter facilitates the V4+ to V5+ reoxidation step through the formation of Ce–O–V bonds, ensuring a high steady-state concentration of active OS‑L necessary to maintain this efficient catalytic cycle. A deviation from the optimal ratio disrupts the synergy between dehydrogenation and epoxidation, resulting in nonselective oxidation. The V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratios of other VPO catalysts with good performance are found to fall in the range of 2.22–2.82, further validating the generality of this design principle (Table S5).

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have demonstrated that the catalytic performance of the VPO catalyst is governed not only by the total OS‑L content, but also by the synergistic interplay between two distinct valence-state-dependent oxygen species (V5+-OS‑L and V4+-OS‑L). The specific functions of the two oxygen species are as follows: V5+-OS‑L abstracts hydrogen atoms from n-butane and undergoes rapid regeneration by being oxidized by O2, while V4+-OS‑L is strongly suggested to provide the active OS‑L for the epoxidation of butene to MA.

Most importantly, an optimal kinetic balance between these two species with a V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio range of 2.22–2.82 is crucial for a high MA yield. The balance ensures efficient dehydrogenation while minimizing overoxidation.

The incorporation of R- CeO2 into VPO catalyst effectively modulates the V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio, whereas the addition of C-CeO2 or O-CeO2 leads to an excessive ratio that is detrimental to the reaction. The 3R-VPO and 5R-VPO catalysts demonstrate outstanding MA yields, attributed to a suitable V5+-OS‑L/V4+-OS‑L ratio.

Beyond the VPO system, our findings may establish a general design principle for MvK-type oxidation catalysts: the rational manipulation of valence-specific lattice oxygen associated with different elementary steps represents a powerful strategy for optimizing selectivity. This insight, emphasizing the dynamic balance of multiple active oxygen species, may open new avenues for developing highly efficient catalysts for the selective oxidation and functionalization of alkanes.
