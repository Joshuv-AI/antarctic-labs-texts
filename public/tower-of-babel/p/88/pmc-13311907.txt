Ultrasound-assisted extraction of Ehretia macrophylla wall. Fruit phenolic acids: Process optimization, characterization, and lung protective activities
Authors: Cailian Fan, Xining Geng, Ning Ding, Shutong Huang, Xiaoxiao Deng, Haili Shan, Hanghang Wang, Shiping Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ehretia macrophylla Wall. fruit (EMF) is rich in phenolic acids and is commonly used as an ethnomedicine against respiratory diseases. In this study, ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) was employed to obtain the optimal extract of EMF phenolic acids (EMFPA). The chemical components of EMFPA were characterized, and its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and lung protective activities were systematically evaluated. Using a Box-Behnken response surface methodology design, the optimal UAE conditions were determined to be ultrasound power of 350 W, a time of 67 min, a temperature of 53℃, and a liquid-to-solid ratio of 45:1 mL/g, resulting in the highest extraction yield value of EMFPA (84.32 ± 0.21 mg CA/g), which was significantly higher than that obtained with ethanol reflux extraction (ERE). A total of 31 phenolic acids were identified in EMFPA by UPLC-Q-TOF/MS, including protocatechualdehyde, caffeic acid, and rosmarinic acid derivatives. Quantitative analysis revealed that UAE yielded higher major phenolic acids than ERE, especially salvianolic acid A. The antioxidant activity of UAE-EMFPA was substantially higher than that of the ERE-EMFPA, and the UAE-EMFPA had a concentration dependent inhibitory effect on NO, TNF-α, and IL-6 generation in the LPS-induced MLE-12 cell inflammatory model. Furthermore, EMFPA treatment markedly reduced the W/D ratio, alleviated inflammatory cell infiltration, and mitigated the histopathologic injuries in LPS-induced acute lung injury mouse model. These findings fully confirm that UAE is a superior method for EMFPA extraction, and that EMFPA is a potential natural active for developing lung-protective functional foods or pharmaceuticals.

Introduction

Ehretia macrophylla Wall. (Boraginaceae) is an annual deciduous shrub widely distributed across South China, as well as in India, Myanmar, Vietnam, Japan and other countries in the Southeast Asia region [1]. The fruit of this species, known as Ehretia macrophylla Wall. fruit (EMF) or wild loquat, has been traditionally used for treating inflammatory diseases of the throat, stomach, and intestine [2]. Previous phytochemical studies on EMF led to the isolation and characterization of various bioactive compounds, such as phenolic acids and polysaccharides [2], [3], [4]. With increasing cultivation of Ehretia macrophylla Wall., the yield of EMF has increased correspondingly, highlighting an urgent need for its high-value utilization [2]. However, current researches on EMF remains limited to the separation of individual components, lacking a systematic evaluation regarding the most appropriate extraction methods and pharmacological activities. This knowledge gap hinders the product design process [5]. Therefore, the characterization of the chemical composition, structure, and functionality profile of the total phenolic acids obtained from EMF were necessary.

With increasing haze pollution level, rising occupational exposure to dust, and the ongoing COVID-19 epidemic, lung injury is becoming an important public health problem [6]. Lung injury is a complex pathophysiological process triggered by various internal or external stimuli, which is typically associated with significant inflammation and pulmonary fibrosis [7]. Currently, supportive treatments including anti-inflammatory and antioxidant drugs, and mechanical ventilation are primarily used to alleviate the pathological symptoms of lung injury and delay disease progression. However, their efficacy remains limited [8], [9]. Therefore, the discovery of novel, safe and effective drugs capable of mitigating lung injury holds significant clinical value for improving patient outcomes. As important bioactive compounds, natural phenolic acids exhibit potent anti-inflammatory and free radical scavenging activities, demonstrating great potential for the treatment of lung injury [10], [11], [12], [13]. Several studies have identified EMF as an alternative raw material rich in active components, which can be utilized to develop pharmaceutical products with lung-protective effects [2], [3], [4]. Therefore, a detailed study of the protective effect of EMF on the lungs is of paramount importance.

To date, ethanol reflux extraction (ERE) is commonly used to isolate phenolic acids from EMF [2]. Conventional extractions using reflux and macerations typically require a long time and excessive solvent use, and potentially degrade heat-labile bioactive compounds [14], [15]. To overcome such drawbacks, novel extraction techniques have been recently developed, such as ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE), supercritical fluid extraction, microwave-assisted extraction and enzymatic extraction [16]. Among these emerging methods, UAE has attracted significant research interest. This technology uses high-frequency sound energy to create cavitational bubbles in the solvent during extraction [17]. The formation, growth and subsequent collapse of such microbubbles produce strong mechanic effects like turbulences, localized heating, and elevated pressure zones. All these physical effects serve to rupture cell structures, increase membrane permeability, and substantially enhance mass transfer from the plant material into the solvent [18], [19]. This method outperforms the existing techniques in terms of reducing the time required for extracting features, minimizing solvent use, increasing extraction efficiency, and maintaining functionality in the extracted compounds [18].

Therefore, this study aimed to optimize the UAE conditions to maximize the yield of EMFPA using a response surface methodology design with independent process factors. We also performed in vitro assays for evaluating its antioxidant activity and anti-inflammatory effects, alongside an in vivo assessment of its protective effect on the lungs. The findings of the present study could provide valuable insights for utilizing EMF as a food supplement or a potential therapeutic candidate for the treatment of pulmonary diseases.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The mature fruits of Ehretia macrophylla Wall. were obtained from the Plant Variety Breeding and Demonstration Center of Pingdingshan University, Henan Province, China. Vouchers of EMF were examined and identified by Prof. Shiping Cheng, an authority on plant taxonomy at our university. The voucher specimen (No. EMF-2024–9-1 with weight of 500 g) was deposited at the Key Laboatory for Germplasm Innovation and Application of Ecological-Economic Woody Plants at Henan Pingdingshan University.

The harvested fruits were rinsed with clean water prior to processing for removing surface impurities. They were freeze-dried in a vacuum freeze dryer (FD-1A-50 model produced by Beijing Boyikang Laboratory Instruments) at −50 °C and 10 Pa for 48 h to preserve its phytochemical constituents. The obtained dried product underwent mechanical grinding (FW100, Tianjin Taisite Instrument Co.) followed by passing through an 80-mesh sieve for the required fineness. The powdered product was collected in containers and maintained until its use.

Reagents

The chemical standards of protocatechualdehyde, caffeic acid (CA), rosmarinic acid 4-O-β-D-glucoside, salvianolic acid A, rosmarinic acid, salvianolic acid B, methyl rosmarinate (≥98% pure based on HPLC analysis results), which were purchased from Chengdu PUSH Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Chengdu, China). Antioxidant capacity assay kits based on three different methods (DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP) were obtained from Iseju Biotechnology Company (Lianyungang, China). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Inc. (St. Louis, MO, USA). TNF-α, IL-6 kits were obtained from MEIMIAN Co., Ltd. (Yancheng, China). Proteins were quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) reagent kit from NCM Biotech (Suzhou, China).

UAE of EMFPA

Single-factor experiments

Preliminary experiments were conducted to determine the optimal ethanol concentration required for the UAE of EMFPA. The extraction was evaluated across an ethanol concentration range of 0–80% in 10% increments, while maintain all other parameters constant: ultrasound power 300 W, extraction time 30 min, extraction temperature 50 ℃, and liquid-to-solid ratio 30 mL/g. The results showed that the extraction efficiency of EMFPA increased with increasing amounts of ethanol (from 30 to 60%), peaked at 60% and then decreased as the concentration increased (Fig. S1). Therefore, 60% ethanol was selected for further optimization. Subsequently, the influence of four key factors, including ultrasonic power (from 200 to 400 W with an increment of 50 W), extraction time (30, 60, 90, or 120 min), extraction temperature (30, 40, 50, 60, or 70 ℃) and liquid-to-solid ratio (10:1, 20:1, 30:1, 40:1, or 50:1 mL/g), on EMFPA yield was studied.

For each optimization experiment, the sample mass was fixed at 1.0 g. The exact amount of EMF powder was weighed into 100 mL closed Erlenmeyer flasks followed by adding 60% v/v ethanol according to the set ratio. The closed flask was placed inside an ultrasonic extractor machine (XMF-500UGF, Xiaomei Kunshan Ultrasonic Instrument Co., Ltd., China) and the extraction conditions were set as described. After extraction, the mixture was centrifuged (5000 rpm; 10 min) at 4 ℃ and the supernatant was decanted. The residue was extracted again under identical conditions, and the two liquid fractions obtained after each of these extractions were pooled. The mixture of two liquid phases was further condensed in a rotary evaporator (RE-52AA, Shanghai Yarong Biochemical Instrument Factory, China) at 60 °C under decreased pressure and the obtained residue was dissolved in methanol to quantify the EMFPA content. EMFPA content was determined using a modified Folin–Ciocalteu method [20]. Briefly, 0.5 mL of the sample solution, as prepared above, was subjected to the following procedure: The sample was mixed with 0.25 M Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (0.5 mL) and left for 3 min at room temperature. Then, 1.0 mL of 15% sodium carbonate solution (w/v) was added, mixed well by vortexing, and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min and the supernatant fraction was collected to determine the total phenolic content by measuring absorbance at 760 nm using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader (USA). A series of CA standard solutions (0, 0.0125, 0.025, 0.05, 0.1, and 0.2 mg/mL) were used to obtain a calibration curve described by the linear equation y = 4.5234x + 0.1724 (R2 = 0.9916). The recovery rate of EMFPA could be calculated as:EMFPAyield(mgCA/g)=(C×V×D)/m

where C is the total phenolic content concentration of the supernatant (mg/mL), V is the supernatant volume (mL), D is the dilution factor, and m is the mass of the EMF material (g).

ERE of EMFPA

The EMF powder was mixed in 60% ethanol and used as the solvent during the extraction procedure, which was performed twice at 80 ℃, while maintaining a liquid-to-solid ratio at 45 mL/g. The obtained extracts were collected for further experiments. The following steps and yield calculation of EMFPA for ERE were identical as those used for UAE.

Characterization of EMFPA

Phenolic acids were quantified using LC-MS/MS. The lyophilized EMFPA extract (0.5 g) was dissolved in 10 mL 70% MeOH/H2O, resulting in a concentration of 50 mg/mL. The samples were then centrifuged (13200 rpm for 10 min) and the supernatant was used for UPLC-Q-TOF/MS analysis.

Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 µm particles), maintained at 35 °C, with a constant mobile phase flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. A two-component solvent gradient was utilized, in which component A consisted of water containing 0.1% formic acid, whereas eluent B consisted of acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid. A programmed gradient was applied, as follows: 2% B increased to 15% over 4.5 min, raised to 40% B from 4.5 to 10 min, elevated to 55% B at 12 min, and further reached 80% B at 15 min. Eluent B was then adjusted to 100% at 16 min and held constant for 1 min, before rapidly returning to 2% B within 30 s (17.0–17.5 min) and then held until the 18 min time point. The sample was introduced by injecting a volume of 2 µL.

Analytical measurements were obtained using an electrospray ionization mass spectrometer operated in the positive and negative polarity mode, where for positive ion detection, the source potential on the capillary was set as either + 3.0 kV (for positive ions) or −2.0 kV (for negative ions). The main cone voltage was set as either 40.0 V (positive ions) or 35.0 V (negative ions), with an additional second cone potential of 4.0 V). The temperature of the ionization source was kept constant at 100 °C, with a desolvation temperature of 300.0 °C, desolvation gas flow rate of 800.0 L/h, curtain gas flow rate of 30.0 L/h, nebulizer gas flow rate of 60.0 L/h, source temperature of 0 °C, and collision gas (Ar) flow rate of 0.0 mL/min. The collision energy was set as −30 eV for all compounds in the MSE mode using Ar as the collision gas. For MSE acquisition, the energy was set as 5.0 eV for low energy scans, and 20.0–50.0 eV (ramped) for high energy scans. Mass calibration was performed using sodium formate, while leucine enkephalin was utilized as the lock mass reference (positive ion m/z 556.2771 and negative ion m/z 554.2615). The LockSpray was set to 5.0 µL/min and the centroid data collection mode was used for all analyses.

In vitro antioxidant activity assays

For determining antioxidant activity, stock solutions for EMFPA and ascorbic acid (positive control) with different concentrations (4, 2, 1, 0.8, 0.4, 0.2, 0.1, 0.05, 0.025, and 0.0125 mg/mL) were used. Radical scavenging activity (DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP) by EMFPA was measured as described in the previous report [21].

Assessment of anti-inflammatory effects in vitro

Determination of cellular viability

DMEM/F12 covered with 10% FBS and penicillin (100 U/mL) plus streptomycin (100 μg/mL) was used to culture MLE-12 cells. Cells were grown under standard cell culture conditions in a Thermo Fisher Scientific Incubator. The cytotoxic effect of EMFPA was measured using the CCK-8 assay. The cells were seeded into 96-well plates with a density of 8000 cells/well for 24 h at normal cell culture conditions. The initial media were replaced for new ones with or without different EMFPA concentrations ranging from 1 and 1000 µg/mL (1000, 800, 400, 200, 100, 50, 25, 10, and 1 µg/mL, respectively). Cells were further incubated for another day; then, 10 μL CCK-8 solution was added to each well and maintained for another 3 h. Absorbance values were measured at 450 nm.

Analysis of inflammatory mediators in cell culture

MLE-12 cells were plated in a 96-well plate at 8 × 103 cells/well, and incubated for 24 h before treatment. The cells were then treated with different concentrations of either EMFPA or dexamethasone (DEX) for 3 h. Following pre-treatment, we replaced the culture medium with new medium containing 1 µg/mL LPS, and incubated for 24 h. Untreated controls were maintained in regular media, whereas the LPS-treated group was administered LPS alone. At the end of the incubation period, the supernatant was collected from the cell culture medium by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 ℃. Commercial kit assays were used to detect the levels of NO, TNF-α, and IL-6 in the collected supernatant.

Evaluation of pulmonary protective effect in animal model

Experimental animal grouping and model preparation

SPF certified male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 w, 20–22 g) were purchased from Guangdong Zhiyuan Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China (License No. SCXK (Yue) 2021–0057). All animals were maintained under standard conditions (room temperature, 50 ± 5% relative air-humidity), with a 12 h light/dark cycle. The mice were acclimatized for one week prior to the initiation of the experiment. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Review Committee at Pingdingshan University (No. PDSU-20240045).

Experimental animals were systematically allocated into six treatment groups: the control group (Control group, n = 8), the LPS-induced acute lung injury (ALI) model group (Model group, n = 8), the low-dose EMFPA intervention group (EMFPA-L group, 50 mg/kg/day, n = 8), the medium-dose EMFPA intervention group (EMFPA-M group, 100 mg/kg/day, n = 8), the high-dose EMFPA intervention group (EMFPA-H group, n = 8), and the positive drug group (Dexamethasone, DEX, 2 mg/kg/day, n = 8). To induce ALI, each mouse received a single intraperitoneal injection of LPS (8 mg/kg) on the left ventral side. Mice administered an equivalent volume of sterile normal saline served as the normal control group. Prior to LPS challenge, mice of each group, except those in the Control and Model groups, received oral EMFPA administration once daily for five consecutive days as a preventive intervention.

Sample collection

Following treatment with LPS or EMFPA for 24 h, all mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium. The mouse blood samples were collected from the orbital sinuses and then centrifuged at 3000 rpm/min for 15 min at 4 ℃. All serum samples were then stored at −80 ℃ until further analysis. To avoid cross-contamination between the two lungs, the left main stem bronchus was ligated using a thread and 0.3 ml of sterilized phosphate buffered saline was injected thrice into the right lung to collect BALF. After centrifugation under identical conditions, the supernatant was collected for measuring the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. The lungs were then removed and several portions were fixed in 4% formaldehyde for histological analysis, whereas the remaining tissue was stored at −80 ℃ for further research.

Lung pathological analysis

Lung tissues fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 µm thickness and stained with haematoxylin and eosin. Pulmonary histopathological changes were examined using light microscopy.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± SD. All statistical analyses were conducted using Graphpad Prism version 9.0. Group differences were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Optimization of ultrasound-assisted extraction condition

Single-factor experiments results

The effect of different levels of ultrasonic power (200, 250, 300, 350, and 400 W) on the yield of EMFPA was initially studied in a factorial manner. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, increasing the ultrasonic power from 150 to 350 W enhanced the extraction yield, which later decreased for power greater than 400 W. This decrease likely resulted from the structural degradation of phenolic compounds at high ultrasonic power [22]. Concurrently, as displayed in Fig. 1B, the longer extraction time (between 15–60 min) resulted in higher yields until a maximum value was reached at 60 min, whereas further increasing the process duration led to lower yields, probably because of the thermal degradation of phenolic constituents [23]. In terms of temperature, Fig. 1C showed that the yield of EMFPA increased with rising temperatures (from 30 to 50 °C), but declined after reaching 50 °C. This phenomenon is attributable to the dual effects of temperature, wherein moderate heating accelerates the extraction process, whereas excessively high temperatures induce the oxidation and degradation of phenolic acids [24]. Furthermore, Fig. 1D illustrated that higher concentrations of phenolic acids could be extracted as the liquid-to-solid ratio increased from 10 to 40 (mL/g). This trend occurred because an adequate solvent volume ensures the complete dissolution of phenolic acids, whereas an excessive solvent volume increases the cost and difficulty of subsequent separations [25]. Based on the one factor-one level method, the optimal operating parameters were ultrasound power level 350 W, treatment time 60 min, extraction temperature 50 °C, and the liquid-to-solid ratio 40:1 mL/g.Fig.1Effects of ultrasonic power (A), ultrasonic time (B), temperature (C), liquid-to-solid ratio (D) on EMFPA yield.

Response surface methodology optimization results

The results of the experiments according to Box-Behnken response surface design were presented in Table 1. A second order polynomial equation was used for the analysis, giving rise to the following regression equation: Y = 83.44 + 1.69A + 1.90B + 1.15C + 1.19D − 1.77 AB − 0.205AC − 1.467AD − 2.82BC − 1.23BD + 0.795CD − 6.64A2 − 6.97B2 − 7.35C2 − 4.54D2. The ANOVA table for the model was presented in Table 2. The model was determined as highly significant (F = 272.81, p < 0.001) or non-significant based on lack of fit (F = 0.9799, P = 0.5577 > 0.05). The results indicated that this model was suitable for describing the experimental results with a high R2 (0.996) and adjusted R2 (0.993). According to statistical results, parameters A, B, C, and D; the interaction terms AB, AD, BC, BD, and CD; and the quadratic terms A2, B2, C2, and D2 exerted a significant effect (p < 0.01) on EMFPA yield. The 3D response surface plots and their respective contour plots were presented in Fig. 2. According to the calculation formula of the regression equation, the optimal extraction conditions were obtained with ultrasonic power 350 W, extraction time 67.2 min, extraction temperature 53 ℃, and liquid-to-solid ratio 44.8:1 mL/g (Fig. S2). For practical application, these were slightly modified to ultrasonic power 350 W, extraction time 67 min, operating temperature 53 ℃, and a liquid-to-solid ratio 45:1 mL/g. An experimental EMFPA yield of 84.32 ± 0.21 mg CA/g was obtained according to the above mentioned optimized parameters, demonstrating a good agreement with the theoretical value of 83.65 mg CA/g. Characterization of EMFPA

Based on the comparison of retention time, fragmentation ions and other information between standard samples and MS peaks, a total of 31 phenols from EMFPA were identified, including protocatechualdehyde, caffeic acid, rosmarinic acid 4-O-β-D-glucoside, rosmarinic acid, salvianolic acid A, salvianolic acid B, and methyl rosmarinate (Fig. 3 and Table 3). Furthermore, the extraction yield of EMFPA via UAE reached 84.32 ± 0.21 mg CA/g, which was significantly higher than that of ERE (70.04 ± 0.17 mg CA/g). Based on the quantitative analysis performed using an external standard two-point calibration curve, the detailed analysis revealed changes in the contents of protocatechualdehyde, caffeic acid, salvianolic acid A, rosmarinic acid, salvianolic acid B, and methyl rosmarinate in both extraction methods (Fig. 4 and Table 4). The contents of rosmarinic acid, salvianolic acid A, and salvianolic acid B in UAE-EMFPA were markedly elevated, especially salvianolic acid A. Notably, methyl rosmarinate was only detected in UAE-EMFPA (2.6 ± 0.13 μg/g). Conversely, the levels of protocatechualdehyde and caffeic acid were relatively higher in ERE-EMFPA. In vitro antioxidant activity

The antioxidant activities of the UAE-EMFPA and ERE-EMFPA were measured using DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP assays, utilizing ascorbic acid as a reference. As shown in Fig. 5, the scavenging activities of DPPH and ABTS radicals by UAE-EMFPA was higher compared with that of ERE-EMFPA. A similar trend was observed in the reduction of Fe3+ ions, with corresponding EC50 values of 0.41 ± 0.04 mg/mL, 0.26 ± 0.01 mg/mL, and 0.9 ± 0.02 mg/mL. These results demonstrated that UAE significantly increased the antioxidant activity of EMFPA compared with that obtained using conventional ethanol reflux extraction. The higher antioxidant activities found for UAE-EMFPA may be attributed to ultrasonic cavitation, which effectively disrupts cell walls, assists in releasing bioactive components, and preserving the chemical structure and biological activity of phenolic acids [25]. In summary, UAE was determined as a more efficient method than the conventional method for preparing EMFPA with high antioxidant activity.Fig.5Antioxidant activities of EMFPA in vitro. In vitro anti-inflammatory activity

The effect of UAE-EMFPA on MLE-12 cell viability was determined using the CCK-8 assay. The results displayed in Fig. S3 indicated that no significant toxicity occurred within a range of 1–400 µg/mL (p > 0.05), whereas treatment at 800–1000 μg/mL significantly decreased cell viability (p < 0.01). A working range between 1 and 400 µg/mL then was selected for further investigation. As illustrated in Fig. 6, LPS significantly increased the levels of NO, TNF-α, and IL-6 compared with those of untreated cells (p < 0.01). Pretreatment with UAE-EMFPA (25–400 µg/mL) significantly reduced the overproduction of all these pro-inflammatory cytokines, revealing a significant dose-dependent response pattern (p < 0.01). In vivo lung protective activity

The protective effect of EMFPA on lung injury was evaluated using an animal model of LPS-induced ALI. As presented in Fig. 7A and 7B, compared with that in the normal control group, the lung coefficient and W/D body weight ratio in the LPS model group were significantly increased (p < 0.01), and EMFPA administered at the doses of 100 and 200 mg/kg/day caused a significant dose-related decrease of these values (p < 0.05), confirming that EMFPA could reduce alveolar edema. Furthermore, hematoxylin and eosin staining analysis showed severe alveolar structural destruction, interstitial oedema, and leucocytic infiltration in the model group, whereas pretreatment with EMFPA significantly reduced all these symptoms (Fig. 7C). The histopathological score for lung injury (Fig. 7D) also confirmed this result wherein the EMFPA-M and EMFPA-H groups exhibited a much smaller histopathological score compared with that of the LPS treated groups (p < 0.01). Notably, EMFPA-H treatment showed protective effects comparable to those of the positive control drug DEX. Furthermore, as illustrated in Fig. 7E-J, compared with those of the control group, the levels of inflammatory factors IL-6 and TNF-a were significantly increased in the BALFs, lung tissues, and serum from the model group (p < 0.01). Pre-treatment with EMFPA prior to LPS application reduced the IL-6 levels in a dose-dependent manner (p < 0.01). Similarly, TNF-a concentration in the BALFs as well as in the serum significantly decreased following EMFPA administration (p < 0.05). In contrast, no differences were observed for the IL-6 level in the lungs under any of these treatments. Conclusion

In this study, we optimized the conditions of UAE for the extraction of EMFPA and systematically investigated its in vitro antioxidant, anti-inflammatory effects, and in vivo lung-protective effect. Based on single factor experiments and Box-Behnken response surface design, the optimal UAE conditions were an ultrasonic power of 350 W, an extraction time of 67 min, an extraction temperature of 53 ℃, and a liquid-to-solid ratio of 45 mL/g. Under these conditions, the EMFPA recovery was significantly higher than that obtained with ERE. Quantitative analysis further revealed that the total content of major phenolic acids in EMFPA extracted using UAE was significantly higher than those obtained with ERE, especially for salvianolic acid A. In vitro assays demonstrated that UAE-EMFPA exhibited strong antioxidant capacities, outperforming those of ERE-EMFPA. Furthermore, the present study revealed that EMFPA inhibited the overproduction of inflammatory mediators such as NO, TNF-α, and IL-6 in MLE-12 cells in a dose-dependent manner. Moreover, the UAE-derived EMFPA alleviated alveolar tissue injury in ALI mouse model by reducing the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-6 and TNF-α. Collectively, these data suggest that UAE is a stable and effective method for extracting EMFPA, which holds promise for development of drugs or nutraceuticals for treating lung injury due to its strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities.
