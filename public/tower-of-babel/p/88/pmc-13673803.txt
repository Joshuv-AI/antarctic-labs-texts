An evaluation of cytokine responses and antiseizure medication levels during mild upper respiratory infections in children with epilepsy
Authors: Elif Acar Arslan, Aslı Begüm Özkan, Esra Özkaya, Betül Diler Durgut, Beril Dilber, Tülay Kamaşak, Sevim Şahin, Ali Cansu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and aimThis study aimed to investigate the effect of upper respiratory tract infections on serum antiseizure medication levels in children with idiopathic epilepsy and the relationship between that condition and inflammation.Materials and methodsForty-nine patients aged 2–18 years presenting to our paediatric neurology clinic who were under follow-up with a diagnosis of idiopathic epilepsy and who were receiving valproate or carbamazepine therapy were included in this study. All patients were using either valproic acid (n = 31) or carbamazepine (n = 18). Patients were evaluated at the time of presentation with symptoms of upper respiratory tract infection and during the control period one month later. Serum antiseizure medication, interleukin-17 A and interleukin-23 levels, complete blood count, alanine transaminase levels, creatinine levels, albumin levels, erythrocyte sedimentation rates, and C-reactive protein levels were measured during infection and during the control period one month later. Simultaneous electroencephalography examinations were also performed.ResultsNo provoked seizures occurred in any patient during the infection period. Serum valproic acid levels were higher in patients during the infection period than in those same patients during the control period after one month, although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.073). There was also no significant difference in carbamazepine levels (p = 0.484). While no difference was observed in the interleukin-23 values between the two periods in patients receiving valproic acid, these values were greater in the patients who received carbamazepine during the infection period (p = 0.039).ConclusionThe findings of this study suggest that mild upper respiratory tract infections do not cause clinically significant alterations in serum valproate or carbamazepine levels.

Introduction

Epileptogenesis is a process of brain damage involving spontaneous seizure development as a result of molecular and cellular changes [1]. Important aetiological causes include infections and inflammations triggered as a result [2]. Idiopathic generalized epilepsy represents approximately one-third of all epilepsies. First-generation drugs, including valproic acid (VPA) and carbamazepine (CBZ), are frequently employed in the treatment. VPA is a histone deacetylase inhibitor antiseizure medication (ASM) with anti-inflammatory effects [3]. VPA therapy may suppress Th1 and Th17 cell responses and cytokine production. Insufficient information is currently available concerning changes in VPA levels during infections [4, 5]. CBZ acts by enhancing the inactivation of sodium channels, thereby reducing high-frequency repetitive firing of action potentials. It also affects synaptic transmission.

Infections may modify the biotransformation of ASMs. Research has documented that drug clearance decreases during infections such as influenza, rhinovirus, adenovirus, herpes simplex, and infectious mononucleosis, largely because of interferon induction [6].

Certain ASMs may impair components of the innate immune system, thereby potentially altering the risk of infection. The increase in seizure frequency observed during infectious episodes can potentially be attributed to factors such as fever, cytokine release, and metabolic stress. However, the specific mechanisms by which upper respiratory tract infections influence inflammatory and immune parameters or whether seizure exacerbation is triggered by infection-related reductions in serum antiseizure drug levels remain unclear and have not been adequately investigated [7].

Researchers have suggested that inflammatory processes in the brain may play a common and important role in the pathophysiology of seizures and epilepsy [8]. IL-17 A and IL-23 are involved in host defence, and the synthesis of these genes also increases during viral infections [9]. According to the latest research, IL-17 A and its indicator, IL-23, may act as inflammatory mediators responsible for epileptogenesis. Serum levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17 A, IL-23, and tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α increase following generalized seizures and status epilepticus [10]. Molecules such as IL-17 A, IL-17 F, IL-6, IL-22, TNF alpha, and alpha-3 integrin are produced following IL-23 stimulation. IL-17 serves as a bridge between the natural and acquired immune systems. Both IL-17 A and IL-23 may be recognized as pro-inflammatory cytokines. In support of this hypothesis, compared with those in healthy control subjects, significantly elevated IL-17 A levels have been detected during the interictal period in patients with epilepsy, indicating a sustained proinflammatory state even outside active seizure episodes [10, 11].

The most frequently seen childhood infections are those of the upper respiratory tract. These include nasopharyngitis, viral or bacterial tonsillopharyngitis, acute otitis media, acute rhinosinusitis, and croup [12]. Insufficient data are currently available concerning how ASM levels are affected during periods of infection. The primary outcome of this study was to investigate the effect of mild upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs) on plasma ASM levels. The secondary outcome was to evaluate changes in selected cytokine levels during this period and to determine whether these cytokines had an additional effect on ASM levels.

Patients and methods

This prospective observational study was conducted at the Clinic of Paediatric Neurology, Department of Paediatrics, Karadeniz Technical University Faculty of Medicine. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Karadeniz Technical University Faculty of Medicine (Approval No. 2016/129-24237859; Decision No. 67) Ethics Committee, and the study was supported by the Karadeniz Technical University Research Fund Research Fund (Project No. 6872).

Inclusion criteria

Children aged 2–18 years with idiopathic epilepsy who were using only VPA or CBZ for ≥ 6 months, who presented with URTI symptoms within 24 h, and who did not use other medications. URTI during the symptomatic phase was defined as the presence of one or more of the following symptoms: cough and runny nose or nasal congestion, with or without fever; cough and sore throat, with or without fever; runny nose, nasal congestion, and earache, with or without fever; and any single upper respiratory symptom accompanied by fever.

Eligibility was restricted to outpatients with a paediatric respiratory severity score (PRESS) of 0; patients with scores exceeding 1 were excluded according to the PRESS criteria described by Miyaji et al. [13].

Exclusion criteria

Patients with symptomatic epilepsy, cognitive impairment, structural brain findings, or who received polytherapy were excluded.

Study design

This study involved the collection of venous blood samples from all participants at two distinct time points: first, during the symptomatic phase of upper respiratory tract infection, and, subsequently, one month later, following clinical confirmation of complete recovery and the absence of infection-related symptoms. Epidemiological data indicate that infection-related symptoms generally resolve within 3–4 weeks. A one-month interval was therefore selected as the control period [14]. The control group consisted of patient serum samples collected during the interictal period.

Serum concentrations of ASMs, including VPA and CBZ, as well as the proinflammatory cytokines IL-17 A and IL-23 and other systemic inflammatory markers, were measured in the collected samples. Electroencephalography (EEG) was performed at both time points to monitor potential alterations in epileptiform activity associated with infection status.

The samples were centrifuged for 15 min at 3000 rpm for serum separation and were stored at -80 °C until use. IL-17 A and IL-23 levels were measured using the ELISA method in serum samples thawed to room temperature on the same day. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) values were determined using the kinetic capillary photometric method on a THL ALIFAX device. C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were measured using the turbidimetric method on a Beckman Coulter AU5800 device, while alanine transaminase and serum albumin levels were measured using the spectrophotometric method on an AU5800 device. Complete blood count values were measured from blood samples in EDTA tubes on a Beckman Coulter LH 780 device. Serum levels of ASMs were quantified using a validated immunoassay method (Beckman Coulter AU5800). EEG tests were performed using an international electrode system on a 64-channel digital EEG device (Neurofax EEG-1200 Tokyo, Japan) in the paediatric neurology department. The tests were also evaluated in the paediatric neurology department by paediatric neurologists.

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0, with p values < 0.05 considered to indicate statistical significance. The normality of the distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between the infection period and the one-month control period for the same patients were performed using the paired t test for normally distributed variables and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for nonnormally distributed variables. Differences between rates of categorical variables in dependent groups were analysed using the McNemar test. Spearman’s correlation test was applied to determine correlations among measurement variables.

Results

Forty-nine patients—25 girls and 24 boys—with a mean age of 11 ± 3.1 years (min 6, max 17 years) were enrolled. Demographic data and upper respiratory infection findings were recorded (Table 1). Serum acute phase reactants (CRP, ESR, and white blood cell values) during the infection period and at control evaluations after one month were recorded (Table 2).

Valproate group (n = 31)

Thirty-one (63.3%) of the 49 patients received VPA therapy. The mean duration of treatment in the VPA group was 42.0 ± 33.9 months. Drug levels during the infection period were not higher than those during the control period (p = 0.073) (Table 3). Serum drug levels were within normal therapeutic ranges in 22 of the 31 patients who received VPA at the most recent routine evaluations prior to infection. The levels in the remaining nine patients were below the therapeutic limits. Drug levels during the infection period were higher in these patients than those during the control period (p = 0.026).

Carbamazepine group (n = 18)

Eighteen (36.7%) of the 49 patients received CBZ therapy. The mean duration of treatment in the CBZ group was 28.0 ± 24.2 months. No significant difference was observed between these patients’ drug levels during the infection period and those during the control period one month later (p = 0.484) (Table 3). Serum drug levels were within normal therapeutic ranges in 14 of the 18 patients at the most recent routine evaluations prior to infection. No significant difference was observed in these patients’ drug levels between the two periods (p = 0.644).

Correlations between IL-17 A and IL-23 levels

A statistically significant positive correlation was observed between serum IL-23 and IL-17 A levels during the infection period (r = 0.308; p = 0.033). Similarly, a stronger positive correlation between IL-23 and IL-17 A was detected during the control period, one month after the resolution of infection (r = 0.559, p < 0.001). These findings indicate that IL-23 and IL-17 A are positively correlated both during active infection and in the postinfectious, asymptomatic phase.

The relationships between serum IL-17 A, IL-23 and CRP levels; white blood cell count; and the ESR during the infection and control periods were also investigated. Serum IL-17 A levels were positively correlated with white blood cell counts during the infection period (r = 0.297; p = 0.038) (Fig. 1).

Serum IL-17 A and IL-23 levels in patients receiving VPA therapy were compared between the infection period and after one month. The levels of both IL-17 A and IL-23 increased during the infection period, although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.236 and p = 0.433, respectively) (Table 3).

Serum IL-17 A and IL-23 levels during the infection period and after one month were also compared among the patients receiving CBZ therapy. No difference was detected in the IL-17 A levels (p = 0.173). A difference was observed between the IL-23 levels in the infection and control periods (p = 0.039) (Table 3). The mean IL-23 concentration was 295.35 ± 109.50 pg/mL during the infection period and 251.41 ± 70.98 pg/mL in control samples after one month. Although IL-23 levels were higher during the infection period among patients with normal basal CBZ levels (those within therapeutic ranges at the most recent controls) (n = 14), the difference was not significant (p = 0.055).

Patients were divided into two groups on the basis of epilepsy type according to the 2017 International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) classification: focal epilepsy and generalized epilepsy. The focal seizure group consisted of 18 patients (36.7%) (focal aware seizures 18.3%, focal impaired awareness seizure 12.2%, focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures 6.1%), and the generalized epilepsy group consisted of 31 patients (63.3%) with generalized tonic–clonic seizures. All the patients were classified as having genetic/idiopathic epilepsy. Cases with generalized epilepsy were defined as those with entirely generalized motor tonic–clonic seizures. In terms of treatment, all patients received ASM monotherapy. All patients’ magnetic resonance imaging examinations were normal. No cognitive disability was present at the start of treatment or during follow-up. No statistically significant difference in serum IL-17 A or IL-23 levels was detected between the focal epilepsy group and generalized epilepsy group during the infection period (p = 0.612 and p = 0.378, respectively) or after one month (p = 0.667 and p = 0.564, respectively).

Electroencephalography findings

EEGs were first performed at the time of diagnosis among all the patients who were focal in 21 (n = 21) cases and were compatible with generalized epileptiform discharges in 28. The results of EEGs (n = 49) performed during the infection and one-month control periods were compared. Epileptiform discharges persisted in 24 (n = 24) of the patients who underwent EEG during the infection period, while the EEGs of 25 (n = 25) patients were normal. Epileptiform discharges were observed on the EEGs of 20 patients during the control period after one month, whereas the EEG findings were normal for 29 patients (n = 29). No significant difference was observed in terms of the EEG findings between the infection and control periods (p = 0.44). None of the patients included in the study developed seizures during the infection period or at presentation after one month.

Discussion

This important study focused on investigating the individual effects of uncomplicated URTIs on ASM levels and inflammatory cytokines in children with idiopathic epilepsy. Experimental and clinical studies in recent years have led to the hypothesis that inflammatory processes may play a joint and important role in the pathophysiology of seizures and epilepsy [8]. Respiratory tract infections in particular have been identified as significant triggers of status epilepticus [15, 16]. Differences in plasma drug concentrations and inflammatory marker levels between the infection phase and the control phase were evaluated one month later. Drug levels were measured during the first stage of the study. Since drug metabolism can be influenced by numerous factors, the findings of this study suggest that the IL-17 A-related pathway might be useful as a new therapeutic option for epilepsy.

The findings of this study revealed that CBZ and VPA levels remained stable during mild infections and suggested that their serum concentrations may contribute to their anti-inflammatory effects. While drug levels remained within therapeutic ranges, IL-23 levels increased significantly (p = 0.039) in CBZ users, may possibly reflecting its weaker anti-inflammatory properties than those of VPA despite the available data are insufficient to draw definitive conclusions. The positive correlation between the levels of IL-17 A and IL-23 in both periods may support the idea of their combined involvement in inflammation and seizure pathophysiology. By excluding potential confounding factors such as seizure-related inflammation, polytherapy, and complicated infections, this study demonstrated that uncomplicated URTIs may provide a reliable clinical model for evaluating the baseline association between mild infection-related inflammation and serum ASM levels in paediatric patients with epilepsy.

High levels of interaction occur between VPA, which is responsible for drug metabolism, and other drugs during infection. Its clinical and toxic effects are weakly correlated with the total serum concentration [17, 18]. In addition to increasing gamma-aminobutyric acid levels, VPA exhibits antiepileptic activity by blocking voltage-gated sodium, potassium, and calcium channels. Since VPA binds to serum proteins, particularly albumin, at high levels, low plasma albumin levels represent a very important problem [19]. CBZ acts as a voltage-gated sodium channel blocker. It is largely metabolized by cytochrome P450, particularly the CYP3A4 system, and binds to α1-acid glycoprotein in plasma [20]. Some of the active metabolite binds to albumin. Since drug binding decreases in cases of hypoalbuminaemia, the total drug concentration may be altered in this condition due to an increase in the free drug fraction [21]. Albumin levels were therefore determined in all patients to confirm that they were within normal limits. Studies have shown an increase in seizure frequency as a result of a decrease in plasma ASM levels due to drug‒drug interactions [22, 23]. Patients receiving a single ASM and no other treatment were therefore included in the present study. Studies have reported a decrease in VPA levels during antibiotic use in particular [22, 23]. In this study, care was taken so that only the effects caused by the infection could be observed. Uncomplicated infections were therefore included, because high virulence may affect seizure tendency and drug levels. Both VPA and CBZ levels in serum are affected by the antiepileptic dosage used, other drugs used, patient age, sex, body weight, autoinduction of drug metabolism, and a number of genetic variations, particularly concerning CBZ [24, 25]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have investigated interactions during infection. Our intention was for this research to serve as a baseline study for antiseizure medications and to provide a reference for comparison purposes in future studies involving more complex situations (such as multiple-drug use and high-virulence infections).

The pathophysiology of epileptogenesis-associated leucocytosis is explained in terms of leukocyte migration to the periphery due to high levels of catecholamines. C-reactive protein is an acute phase plasma protein whose levels increase during inflammatory events such as infection and trauma. It is employed as a marker to indicate disease severity [26]. In the present study, a significant positive correlation was observed between IL-17 A levels during the infection period and white blood cell counts (r = 0.297; p = 0.038). The correlation between white blood cell count and IL-17 A may reflect inflammatory activity during the infection period. However, no conclusions can be drawn regarding seizure-related inflammation because no seizures occurred during the study period whereas CRP and ESR predominantly reflect infection-related inflammation.

Compared with healthy controls, an increased incidence and severity of seizures have been reported in patients with status epilepticus and coexisting infection [16, 27]. These studies revealed that patients with infection at the onset of status epilepticus had significantly higher serum CRP levels and white blood cell counts than patients with no infection did. This in turn highlights the contribution of infections and seizures to the inflammatory response. In the present study, the mean serum white blood cell counts, CRP levels, and ESRs of the patients with URTIs followed up with a diagnosis of epilepsy were significantly higher than those in the control period one month later (p < 0.001).

Epileptogenesis occurs as a result of inflammation, and recurring seizures are thought to emerge as a result [28]. In their study of 70 patients, Li et al. reported a significant increase in interictal plasma IL-17 A levels than those in controls [29]. In a retrospective study involving 519 patients with idiopathic generalized epilepsy, Wang et al. reported elevated interictal plasma and cerebrospinal fluid IL-17 A levels and reported a significant association with seizure severity [30]. IL-17 A and IL-23 may act as two important inflammatory markers in both epileptogenesis and infection. The results of the present study support the hypothesis that plasma IL-17 A and IL-23 concentrations may vary between the infection phase and the control phase one month later in patients with epilepsy on the basis of the idea that both cytokines play key roles in neuroinflammatory pathways and may be modulated by the combined effects of acute infection and the underlying epileptic condition. The IL-23/IL-17 axis may play important roles in the development of chronic inflammation and in host defence against infection. IL-17 can accelerate neutrophil chemotaxis to this region during viral infections [6]. This finding may reflect a subgroup difference observed in the present study; however, the underlying mechanism remains uncertain.

The values obtained during the infection-free period after one month were subsequently used as the control group in this research. Elevated plasma IL-17 A and IL-23 levels were observed in patients who received VPA between the infection and control periods, although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.236 and p = 0.433, respectively). VPA therapy has been shown to suppress Th1 and Th17 cell responses in vitro and in vivo. One study investigating cytokine synthesis in dendritic cells reported decreases in plasma TNF-α, IL-23, and IL-6 synthesis following VPA therapy [28].

No significant difference in plasma IL-17 A levels was detected between the infection and control periods in patients receiving CBZ therapy in this study. However, the IL-23 levels in patients receiving CBZ therapy were higher than those in the control period (p = 0.039). The significant increase in IL-23 observed during the infection period in patients treated with CBZ, but not in those treated with VPA, may be attributable to the greater IL-23-suppressing ability and anti-inflammatory effects of VPA compared with CBZ.

EEGs performed during URTIs and during the control period one month later were also evaluated. The results were classified as pathological EEG findings showing epileptic activity or normal EEG findings. When the infection and control periods were compared in terms of electroencephalography findings, slightly more patients had pathological findings during the infection period. Preserved EEG stability despite mild cytokine elevation during mild URTIs suggests that the inflammatory response may remain subclinical in nature.

This study has a number of limitations. First, the patients’ baseline pretreatment cytokine levels were unavailable before the initiation of VPA or CBZ therapy, limiting any interpretation of immunological suppression. Had these values been available, we would have been better able to conclude how much these were suppressed with VPA. Second, certain viral infections exhibit increased central nervous system tropism. However, routine viral panel testing was not performed in this research because only outpatients with mild infections were included. In addition, no patient experienced active seizures during the mild URTI period, which may be partly explained by the absence of any detectable change in epileptiform discharges on EEG. Nevertheless, the EEG assessment was qualitative in nature, and subtle abnormalities may have been missed. Only mild URTIs were assessed, and the findings cannot therefore be extrapolated to more severe infections. Patients with mild infections and low or subfebrile fever were deliberately selected since medications commonly used during febrile episodes may affect serum ASM levels. This in turn imposed a limitation in evaluating the in vivo effect of fever on ASMs. The relatively small sample size and the absence of a formal a priori power calculation represent limitations of this study and may have reduced the statistical power to detect small intergroup differences. Seizure frequency may represent a potential confounding factor affecting cytokine levels. However, no seizures occurred during the infection period, and patients with complicated seizures were excluded. Nevertheless, individual differences in seizure frequency may have partially influenced cytokine levels. Although the difference did not reach statistical significance, the IL-17 A levels increased during the infection period. However, these observations should be interpreted cautiously and may partly be explained by the exclusion of patients with complicated seizures. Additional limitations include the relatively small sample size, the use of peripheral (noncentral nervous system) cytokine measurements, and the single-centre design of the study.

This study is important in terms of infection and ASMs because this is a relatively understudied area. Although the exclusion of patients with moderate to severe infections or high fever prevented the use of additional medications for symptom control, patients with epilepsies involving frequent seizures, those receiving polytherapy with multiple antiseizure medications, or those with different aetiologies were also excluded. Nevertheless, these criteria allowed the establishment of a homogeneous baseline cohort, providing a foundation for further research, especially for more severe infections. It is also valuable in terms of providing baseline information for future studies involving more complicated groups in which infection triggers seizures.

In conclusion, this study revealed no significant change in serum ASM levels between URTIs and the control period after one month in asymptomatic patients with idiopathic epilepsy who received VPA or CBZ and whose normal cognitive and cranial imaging examinations remained unchanged at follow-up. No seizures were triggered during URTIs in any patient in the study group. This may be due to drug levels remaining unchanged. The positive correlation observed between patients’ serum IL-17 A levels during the infection period and white blood cell counts may suggest an association between seizures and neuroinflammatory processes.

However, the observation of higher IL-23 levels during infection in patients receiving CBZ than in those receiving VPA may suggest a potential subgroup difference in cytokine-related responses between these treatments. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size and exploratory design, and further controlled studies are needed to clarify significance of this observation.
