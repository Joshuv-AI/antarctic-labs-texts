Effects of adding food ingredients rich in dietary fiber and polyphenols on the microstructure, texture, starch digestibility and functional properties of Chinese steamed bun☆
Authors: Guoze Wang, Ying Yuan, Fang Chen, Hongqing Huang, Feng Luo, Rongzheng Duan, Qin Zhou, Jiaqiang Luo, Peng Luo, Li Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study incorporated pitaya (dragon fruit), kudzu root, and Hericium erinaceus (DKH) as functional ingredients into Chinese steamed bun (CSB) to investigate their impact on nutritional composition, textural properties, microstructure, starch digestibility, and postprandial glycemic response. The optimal DKH incorporation ratio was determined using single-factor and response surface methodology experiments. Compared to traditional CSB, DKH enrichment significantly increased dietary fiber and phenolic content. However, DKH incorporation significantly affected textural properties, resulting in increased hardness and reduced elasticity and cohesiveness relative to wheat flour CSB. Microstructural analysis revealed that DKH disrupted the gluten network structure. DKH fortification significantly decreased in vitro starch digestibility and the estimated glycemic index (EGI), with the starch hydrolysis rate decreasing by 19.2 % and EGI decreasing from 94.6 ± 1.3 to 80.3 ± 0.8. Additionally, DKH-CSB showed enhanced antioxidant capacity and favorable modulation of gut microbiota, demonstrating its potential as a functional low-glycemic-index food.Keywords: Starch digestibility, Dietary fiber, Glycemic index, Chinese steamed bun, Functional food

Introduction

Chinese steamed bun (CSB), a staple food in northern China, is characterized by its simple preparation, convenience, and affordability. Traditional CSB is made primarily from refined wheat flour (WF), which contains a high proportion of starch but low levels of minerals, vitamins, and dietary fiber (DF). Consequently, it is classified as a high glycemic index (GI) food (GI 85–100). After consumption, the starch granules are rapidly hydrolyzed by α-amylase and α-glucosidase, resulting quick absorption and a sharp postprandial increase in blood glucose. This characteristic makes CSB unsuitable for individuals with diabetes and similar metabolic disorders. Additionally, regular intake of CSB may increase the risk of chronic disorders, including obesity, diabetes mellitus, and cardiovascular disease, regardless of baseline health status (Wang et al., 2023). Therefore, reformulating CSB with nutrients-rich ingredients to lower its GI represents a promising strategy to meet modern dietary and glycemic control needs. Previous studies have demonstrated that incorporating bioactive compound-rich or fiber-enriched ingredients, such as kiwifruit, seaweed, monk fruit seeds, and Bambara groundnut (Vigna subterranea), can improve the textural and physicochemical properties of CSB and help reduce its glycemic index.

Several studies have demonstrated that nutrients such as dietary fiber, polyphenols, and proteins can reduce starch digestibility in CSB. This effect occurs primarily by inhibiting the activity of α-amylase and α-glucosidase or forming physical barriers around starch granules, thereby decreasing the amount of glucose available for absorption (Li, Shen, et al., 2023). Pitaya (dragon fruit), a member of the Cactaceae family, is widely cultivated across tropical and subtropical regions and is an excellent source of vitamins, dietary fiber, anthocyanins, and other bioactive compounds. Over the past decade, the utilization of betaines (natural pigments) and pitaya pectin in food products has gained increasing research attention. Evidence suggests that betacyanin, a type of betaine extracted from red pitaya, possesses anti-diabetic properties and can modulate glycemic responses (Adeshirlarijaney & Gewirtz, 2020). Kudzu root (Pueraria lobata) and Hericium erinaceus, recognized as medicinal edible homologous materials, exhibit diverse physiological functions, including antioxidant activity, blood glucose regulation, and immune enhancement, owing to their high levels of bioactive components such as phenolics and dietary fiber. Previous studies suggest that combined supplementation with puerarin (from kudzu) and insoluble dietary fiber (from Hericium erinaceus) can modulate the gut microbiota in obese mice, thereby regulating lipid metabolism and reducing obesity (Wang, Wang, et al., 2025). Therefore, incorporating dragon fruit, kudzu, and Hericium erinaceus, collectively referred to as DKH, into CSB not only enhances its nutritional profile and imparts a distinctive color but also offers the potential to low its GI.

This study aimed to improve the nutritional quality and reduce the GI of traditional CSB. Ingredients rich in dietary fiber and phenolics, including DKH, were incorporated as partial substitutes for wheat flour. The formulation was optimized using single-factor experiments and response surface methodology (RSM). The functional properties of the optimized DKH-CSB were evaluated using in vitro simulated digestion and postprandial glycemic response tests. Overall, this work elucidates the potential of DKH as a functional food ingredient and provides a scientific basis for developing low-GI, high-fiber CSB.

Materials and methods

Reagents and materials

Refined wheat flour (Shenyang Xiangxue Flour Co., Ltd., Shenyang, China), whole wheat flour (Jinshahe Flour Industry Group Co., Ltd., Hebei, China), Hericium erinaceus powder (Tianbaihui Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Fujian, China) and kudzu powder (Zhongxiang Guosen Yuan Kudzu Powder Industrial Co., Ltd., Hubei, China) were purchased from commercial suppliers. Fresh red dragon fruit (Guizhou, China) and yeast (Angel Yeast Co., Ltd., Yichang, China) were obtained from local sources. Glucoamylase (100,000 U/mL) was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Trypsin (130 U/mg; derived from porcine pancreas) was obtained from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). α-Amylase (3700 U/g; derived from Bacillus subtilis), pepsin (3000 U/g; derived from porcine stomach), and pancreatin (4000 U/g; derived from porcine gastric mucosa) were all purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Unless otherwise specified, all chemicals were of analytical grade.

Dough and CSB preparation

The formulations of wheat flour (WF) dough, whole wheat flour (WW) dough, and DKH composite dough are presented in Supplementary table 1, and the preparation process of CSB is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Briefly, water was measured and mixed with an appropriate amount of yeast, followed by activation. The activated yeast mixture was then combined with the flour blend and transferred to a dough mixer (KM-904, KONKA, China). The dough was kneaded at low speed for 5 min, followed by high-speed kneading for 10 min to develop the gluten network and produce a smooth, homogeneous dough. Dough fermentation was performed for 60 min at 30 °C and 70 % RH in a controlled-environment chamber. The fermented dough was divided into 50 g portions and proofed for an additional 20 min to obtain WF, WW, and DKH doughs. Each portion was steamed at 100 °C for 10 min using an induction cooker (BD-50F, SUBOR, China) and then stewed for 2 min, yielding WF-CSB, WW-CSB, and DKH-CSB, respectively. After steaming, all CSB simples were conditioned at ambient temperature conditioning for 60 min before analysis.

CSB sensory evaluation and measurement of specific volume and width-to-height ratios

Sensory evaluation (SE) of DKH-CSB was conducted by a 10-member trained panel, following the Chinese National Standard (GB/T 35,991–2018) with slight modifications. Ethical approval was not required for the sensory evaluation. All panelists voluntary consented to participate and allowed the use of their data. The specific volume (SV) of CSB was measured according to the GB/T 35,991–2018 standard, with slight modifications, using the volumetric displacement method with millet seeds in a calibrated cylinder. The diameter and height of each CSB sample were measured using a digital caliper (DL8015, Deli, China) to calculate the width-to-height ratio (WHR). A comprehensive scoring model was applied, assigning weighting coefficients of 0.75 for sensory attributes, 0.20 for SV, and 0.05 for WHR. Single-factor experiments and response surface methodology

The supplementation levels of pitaya, kudzu, Hericium erinaceus, and yeast were investigated using single-factor experiments to evaluate their effects on DKH-CSB via sensory evaluation. Based on, the preliminary results from these single-factor tests, the formulation was optimized using a three-level, four-variable Box-Behnken design. Nutrient composition analysis of CSB

Nutrient analyses were performed in accordance with Chinese National Standards. Moisture content was determined by oven drying (GB/T 5009.3–2016), protein content by Kjeldahl method (GB 5009.5–2016), ash content by incineration (GB 5009.4–2016), lipid content by Soxhlet extraction using petroleum ether (GB 5009.6–2016), and dietary fiber by the enzymatic-gravimetric method (GB 5009.88–2014). Total available carbohydrates were measured using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid method. All nutrient values were expressed as g/100 g of dry weight. The total caloric value (kcal/100 g) was calculated using standard energy conversion factors for macronutrients.

Color measurement of CSB

Chromaticity of the CSB samples was measured using a calibrated chroma meter (CR-10 Plus, Konica Minolta, China). Color was measured in the CIE Lab* space: L* (+white/−black), a* (+red/−green), and b* (+yellow/−blue). Measurements were conducted at room temperature on both the external surface and the internal longitudinal section of each CSB sample.

Texture profile analysis (TPA)

CSB samples were cut into cubic specimens, each with a volume of 1 cm3. TPA was performed using a TA.XT Plus texture analyzer (Stable Micro Systems, UK), following the method described by Li et al. (2020) to assess hardness, springiness, cohesiveness, and chewiness. Measurements were conducted using a TA/36 aluminum compression platen probe, with pre-test, test, and post-test speeds of 2.0 mm/s, 1.0 mm/s, and 5.0 mm/s, respectively, a target deformation of 50 %, and a trigger force of 1 N.

Structural performance analysis

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)

FT-IR spectra of gold-sputtered CSB powder were acquired directly on the attenuated total reflection accessory of an IRTracer-100 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan) over a wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1. Spectra were averaged over 64 scans at a resolution of 4 cm−1. Data processing and analysis were performed OMNIC software, and characteristic peaks were annotated to identify functional groups.

Microstructural analysis of CSB

The influence of DKH on the gluten network was assessed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Lyophilization CSB samples were mounted on SEM stubs (Sigma 360, ZEISS, Germany) using conductive adhesive and sputter-coated with gold under vacuum. Microstructural images were captured at magnifications of 1500× and 5000× using an accelerating voltage of 5 kV for subsequent analysis.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM; STELLARIS 5, Leica, Germany) was employed to visualize the distribution of proteins and starches within the steamed bread samples. Frozen sections (20 μm in thickness) were prepared from samples embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound. Sections were stained sequentially with 50 μg/mL fluorescein isothiocyanate for 30 s, washed, and then incubated with 50 μg/mL rhodamine B for 30 s at room temperature in the dark. FITC was excited using a 488 nm laser (green channel; emission collected at 490–560 nm), while RHB was excited using a 561 nm laser (red channel; emission collected at 570–630 nm). Images were captured at a magnification of 630× .

Antioxidant properties of CSB

Extraction and determination of phenolic compounds

Exactly 5 g of CSB was homogenized in 50 mL of 80 % (v/v) methanol in a conical flask. The mixture was extracted under light-protected condition at 50 °C for 2 h using a thermostatic shaker. After the extraction process, samples were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 5 min, and the resulting supernatant was stored at 4 °C until further analysis.

The total phenolic content (TPC) was quantified following the method described by Czaja et al. (2019). Sample absorbance values were interpolated against a standard curve, and results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE)/g. The total flavonoid content (TFC) was measured colorimetrically following the method described by Petković et al. (2020). Calibration curve-derived calculations reported as mg rutin equivalents (RE)/g based on a calibration curve.

Determination of antioxidant activity of CSB

The antioxidant capacity of CSB was evaluated using DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging assay kits (JL-T0645 and JL-T0625, Jonln Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Postprandial blood glucose response

The glycemic response of CSB was assessed in normoglycemic adults (n = 5; fasting glucose ≤6.1 mmol/L, with stable body weight over the preceding 60 days). All participants were informed about the experiment, their obligations, and their right to withdraw at any time without providing a reason. Following ethical approval and a fasting period (8–12 h), baseline blood glucose levels were measured. Each participant consumed 30 g CSB within 5 min. Serial fingertip blood samples were collected at different intervals (0, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min) and analyzed using a glucometer (GA-6, Sinocare, China). Time-glucose curves were constructed, and AUC was calculated using the trapezoidal method.

In vitro simulated digestion and fermentation

In vitro simulated digestion

Simulated digestive fluids, including simulated salivary fluid (SSF), simulated gastric fluid (SGF), and simulated intestinal fluid (SIF), were prepared according to Maccaferri et al. (2012). SSF was obtained by dissolving 156 mg of α-amylase in 50 mL of 1 mmol/L CaCl₂ solution. SGF was prepared by adding 35.40 mg of porcine pepsin to 150 mL of gastric electrolyte solution, followed by the addition of 1.5 mL of 1 mol/L CH₃COONa solution and adjusting the pH to 2.0. SIF was formulated by adding 50 mL of 4 % porcine bile salts, 25 mL of 7 % porcine pancreatin supernatant, and 13 mg of trypsin to 25 mL of intestinal electrolyte solution, then adjusting the pH to 7.0. For in vitro digestion, 3 g CSB homogenate (in 2 mL H₂O) underwent sequential enzymatic treatment. In the oral phase, 5 mL of SSF was added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C with sharking at 100 rpm, 5 min, followed by pH adjustment to 2.0. Gastric digestion was performed by adding 5 mL of SGF and incubating at 37 °C with sharking for 2 h. In the intestinal phase, the required pH was adjusted to 6.8, followed by the addition of 20 mL of SIF, and incubation at 37 °C with sharking for 2 h. After digestion, the resulting products were freeze-dried and stored them at 2–8 °C for subsequent use.

In vitro colonic fermentation

The basal medium was prepared by dissolving 2.5 g peptone, 4 g yeast extract, 0.1 g NaCl, 2 g NaHCO₃, 0.5 g L-cysteine hydrochloride, 0.5 g bile salts, 0.4 g glucose, 1 mg resazurin, and 2 mL Tween 80 in 1 L of distilled water. The pH was adjusted to approximately 7.0, and the medium was sterilized by autoclaving (121 °C, 15 min). After cooling, the sterilized medium was stored until use.

Fresh mouse fecal samples were collected and immediately diluted 1:10 (w/v) with PBS under anaerobic conditions. The suspension was homogenized and centrifuged (3000 rpm, 10 min). For in vitro fermentation, 0.1 g of the test sample was added to 6 mL of basal medium, inoculated with 4 mL of fecal inoculum, and incubated for 24 h. Aliquots were collected at different time points (0, 6, 12, and 24 h) and centrifuged (4000 rpm, 10 min).

Detection of beneficial bacteria proliferation

The relative abundance of bacterial genera, including Lactobacillus and Akkermansia, was quantified using quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Gene-specificprimers for these targets are listed in Supplementary table 8.

qPCR assays were conducted using total DNA as the template with the specific primers. Each 25 μL reaction mixture contained 1 μL each of forward and reverse primers, 12.5 μL of SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Takara), 2 μL of template DNA, and sterile distilled water to a final volume of 25 μL. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 95 °C for 15 s, with a final extension at 60 °C for 5 s.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was conducted in triplicate, and results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel 2019 and SPSS software (version 27.0.1). Graphical representations were created using GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2), Origin (2016), and OMNIC (version 8.2.0.387). RSM design and analysis were performed using Design-Expert (version 13). Significant differences between groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For datasets meeting homogeneity of variance assumptions (Levene's test, P > 0.05), Bonferroni post hoc testing was applied, while Tamhane's T2 method was employed for heteroscedastic data (P < 0.05). Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results and discussion

The optimal production formula for DKH-CSB

Based on a comprehensive evaluation, the CSB formulated with 90 g of pitaya, 40 g of kudzu root powder, 20 g of Hericium erinaceus, and 5 g of yeast achieved the highest overall score in the respective single-factor tests. As shown in Fig. 1a-d, the overall score initially increased and then decreased with increasing pitaya content. This trend may be attributed to the insufficient fruit aroma and color at lower pitaya levels (< 90 g), which resulted in a gradual increasing in score. In contrast, excessive pitaya reduced the specific volume and extensibility of the CSB, leading to shrinkage. When the kudzu root powder content was 35 g, the lower amount caused uneven coloration. However, an excessive amount of kudzu powder (> 40 g) produced an overly dark color and an undesirable earthy aftertaste, significantly reducing sensory evaluation scores. An optimal level of approximately 40 g of kudzu root powder yielded the highest loaf volume and sensory scores, characterized by uniform color, favorable appearance, and a distinct aroma. Similarly, the addition of excessive Hericium erinaceus intensified bitterness and astringency, while surplus yeast induced sourness and increased coarseness in the CSB, both of which lowered the overall score. Furthermore, Table 1 shows the sensory evaluation scores of the CSB. Although DKH-CSB received the lowest overall score, its general acceptability remained marginally acceptable. The incorporation of DKH compromised the performance in terms of both elasticity and flavor of the steamed bread. However, the presence of dragon fruit contributed to an enhanced color, allowing DKH-CSB to garner consumer preference in this aspect.

Based on the results of the single-factor experiments, and using the comprehensive score as the evaluation criterion, four response variables, namely the addition levels of pitaya, kudzu root, Hericium erinaceus and yeast, were selected for the RSM. Supplementary table 6 and table 7 present the experimental results and analysis. As indicated in Supplementary table 7, the regression model was highly significant (P < 0.0001), while the lack-of-fit term was not significant (P = 0.4145). The coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.9789) and the adjusted R2 (R2adj = 0.9542) demonstrated a strong model fit with minimal error. The interactive effects between pitaya (A), Hericium erinaceus (B), kudzu root powder (C), and yeast (D) addition levels are illustrated in Fig. 1e-g. Based on the polynomial regression equation derived from the RSM results, the optimal formulation was identified using Design-Expert 13 software by maximizing sensory score, specific volume, and width-to-height ratio. The optimal formulation was determined to be 143 g wheat flour, 100 g pitaya, 38 g kudzu root powder, 15 g Hericium erinaceus powder, and 4 g yeast. The predicted overall score generated by Design-Expert was 79.73, while the score calculated using the formula was 80.09. The close agreement between the predicted and calculated scores confirms the validity and reliability of the model. The DKH-CSB prepared under these optimal conditions exhibited a smooth surface, bright color, plump texture, moderately porous crumb structure, and a distinct fruity aroma.

Nutritional composition analysis of CSB

As shown in Table 2, the incorporation of DKH (a composite of pitaya, kudzu root, and Hericium erinaceus) significantly altered the nutritional composition of CSB. DKH-CSB exhibited significant changes (P < 0.05) in caloric value, dietary fiber content, energy value, and carbohydrate content compared to wheat flour CSB (WF-CSB). The protein and fat contents of DKH-CSB decreased with the addition of DKH. This reduction may be attributed to the inherently lower protein and lipid content of pitaya pulp (approximately 0.4 % protein) compared to wheat flour. Although Hericium erinaceus possesses high protein and low-fat characteristics, its low substitution level had minimal influence on the overall protein content of DKH-CSB. Remarkably, the dietary fiber content of DKH-CSB was 153 % higher than that of WF-CSB. Adequate dietary fiber intake may enhance intestinal function and reduce the risk of colon cancer, hypercholesterolemia, obesity, and diabetes.

These findings demonstrated that optimal DKH supplementation not only improves the physicochemical properties and flavor profile of CSB but also enhances its nutritional value, particularly by increasing dietary fiber content. The integration of nutrient-rich DKH with traditional wheat-based products represents a promising strategy for developing novel functional foods.

Quality analysis of CSB

Food particle size is a critical factor affecting digestion and the subsequent postprandial glycemic response. Particle size analysis of the different powders (Table 3) revealed that both DKH and WW powders had significantly larger particle sizes compared to conventional wheat flour (P < 0.05). A finding supported by Mandalari et al. (2016), who demonstrated that larger particle sizes act as a physical barrier during in vitro digestion, limiting the surface area accessible to α-amylase, hindering water penetration, and ultimately reducing starch hydrolysis and glucose release. Besides, DKH demonstrated the highest WHC (Table 3). The parameter refers to the ability of hydrocolloidal proteins to absorb and retain water under gravitational force. These protein-water interactions are fundamental to product quality, influencing key functional properties like viscosity and the retardation of staling, thereby underscoring the importance of WHC in shaping the texture of the final product.

Texture profile analysis (TPA), a key method for evaluating flour-based products, quantifies attributes such as hardness, springiness, cohesiveness, and chewiness through controlled force-induced deformation. Hardness and chewiness are negatively correlated with steamed bread quality, whereas springiness shows a positive correlation (Cao et al., 2019). Notably, the incorporation of functional ingredients often compromises bread quality (Liu et al., 2018). As depicted in Fig. 2a-b, incorporation of DKH significantly affected the textural properties of CSB. Hardness exhibited a significant increasing trend, while chewiness, cohesiveness, and springiness decreased significantly (P < 0.05). The reduction in cohesiveness, which reflects the internal binding strength of the crumb, may be attributed to dilution of the gluten-starch matrix resulting from the substitution of wheat flour with DKH components that are inherently gluten-free. Furthermore, the high dietary fiber content in kudzu root powder and Hericium erinaceus powders contributed to increased hardness. Fiber, as a rigid structural component, impeded optimal gluten network formation (Wang et al., 2021), resulting in reduced loaf volume, a coarser internal structure, and consequently diminished springiness and cohesiveness.

Color is a critical indicator for assessing CSB quality. The addition of red pitaya and Hericium erinaceus powder significantly altered both the crust and crumb color of CSB (Fig. 2c). Specifically, partial substitution with pitaya and Hericium erinaceus decreased L* (lightness) and b* (yellowness) values while significantly increasing a* (redness) values, imparting a distinct reddish hue to DKH-CSB. This color shift is primarily attributed to the high betacyanin content in red pitaya pulp. Compared to the crumb of DKH-CSB, the crust exhibited higher a* and b* values, likely due to intensified Maillard and non-enzymatic browning reactions at the elevated surface temperatures during steaming (Çelik & Gökmen, 2020). Moreover, color is a key factor influencing consumer acceptability and preference for food products. Notably, betacyanin may contribute to the color acceptability of DKH-CSB.

Changes in structural properties of CSB gluten network

DKH inhibited the formation of the gluten protein network

The short-range ordered structure of starch molecules in CSB was analyzed using FT-IR. Fig. 2d shows the FT-IR spectra of the three CSB groups. All sample groups exhibited spectra similar to WF-CSB, indicating that no new covalent bond interactions occurred between molecules.

A peak observed in the 3000–3600 cm−1 rang is attributed to O—H stretching vibrations, originating from intramolecular or intermolecular hydrogen bonds in gluten proteins (Du et al., 2022). Compared to WF-CSB, both WW-CSB and DKH-CSB exhibited reduced peak intensity in this spectral region. This attenuation may result from the fiber fraction disrupting the original hydrogen bonds between starch chains, increasing the number of free hydroxyl groups available to from hydrogen bonds with starch molecules. WW-CSB and DKH-CSB also exhibited lower peak intensity in the 2800–3000 cm−1 region, suggesting partial disruption of C—H bonds. DKH-CSB displayed lower amide I peak intensity than WF-CSB, likely due to fiber-induced interference with the gluten structure. Because hydrogen bonds are the primary stabilizing force for the amide I region (Song et al., 2023), their disruption by fiber further decreased peak intensity.

These findings demonstrate that DKH incorporation inhibits gluten protein network formation in CSB. DKH-CSB is rich in dietary fiber, which particularly in the form of soluble fiber, interferes with the development of a robust, continuous gluten network through several mechanisms: (1) significant competition for water molecules, resulting in insufficient gluten hydration; (2) indirect reduction in the efficiency of hydrogen bond network formation; (3) physical obstruction; and (4) dilution effects (Zhao et al., 2025). Moreover, the polysaccharides in DKH are expected to weaken the gluten network in CSB by functioning via the documented mechanism whereby polysaccharides compete for water molecules and disrupt gluten-water hydrogen bonds, consequently reducing hydrogen bonding within the protein network (Yang et al., 2024b).

DKH disrupted the gluten network structure

The microstructure of CSB was examined using SEM (Fig. 3b-c). Upon hydration, wheat flour proteins self-assemble into a gluten network via disulfide crosslinking, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interactions, with glutenin and gliadin constituting the primary structural components (Yang et al., 2024a). WF-CSB exhibited a complete, continuous, and relatively uniform gluten network, with spherical starch granules having smooth surfaces embedded within the gluten matrix. In contrast, both WW-CSB and DKH-CSB displayed a more compact and dense structure, likely due to their higher dietary fiber content.

Fiber enhances water-holding capacity via capillary effects and its rough surface texture. Consequently, higher fiber content increases water absorption, contributing to a denser structure (Bilgic & Sensoy, 2023). High levels of fiber reduces the gluten protein content in CSB and promote the cleavage of disulfide bonds into free sulfhydryl groups, thereby hindering gluten formation (Zhou et al., 2021). This fiber-induced disruption of the gluten network structure corresponds with the observed decrease in CSB elasticity, consistent with texture profile analysis results.

The gluten structure was further visualized using CLSM, with Fig. 3d showing the solid gluten protein network within CSB. As shown in the figures, WF-CSB exhibited an interwoven and uniformly distributed structure of gluten proteins and starch, characterized by a continuous and integrated gluten network with larger gas cell pores. In contrast, both WW-CSB and DKH-CSB, which contained higher levels of wheat bran fiber and DKH-derived fiber, demonstrated a disrupted uniformity in gluten protein distribution. These samples showed smaller air cells and a denser network, indicative of a weakened gluten structure, consistent with SEM observations. This effect may result from friction between fiber fragments and protein chains, leading to thinning and breakage of protein fibrils (Han et al., 2018). Previous study indicate that the extent of gluten network damage is positively correlated with fiber content, and irregularly shaped fibers further reduce the homogeneity of the gluten structure (Mamat et al., 2023).

Analysis of the functional characteristics of CSB

DKH enhanced the antioxidant properties of CSB

Flavonoids and polyphenols exhibit a broad range of biological activities, including antioxidant, antibacterial, and anti-inflammatory effects, which are beneficial to human health (Criste et al., 2020). As shown in Fig. 4a, DKH-CSB demonstrated approximately 1.6-fold and 4.7-fold increases in TPC and TFC, respectively, compared to WF-CSB (P < 0.05). This increase indicates that the incorporation of DKH significantly elevated the polyphenol and flavonoid content in CSB. Furthermore, during the fermentation of DKH-CSB, certain constituents, such as anthocyanins and alcohol from red pitaya, are decomposed and transformed into flavonoid and phenolic compounds, thereby further increasing TPC and TFC.

Functional properties of Chinese steamed bun. (a) Total phenolic content and total flavonoid content, (b) Antioxidant capacity assessed by DPPH/ABTS radical scavenging, (c) Cholesterol adsorption capacity under simulated gastrointestinal conditions, (d-e) Proliferation of probiotics during simulated colonic fermentation.

Antioxidant activity refers to the capacity to mitigate oxidative stress by neutralizing free radicals or inhibiting their formation, thereby maintaining redox homeostasis and protecting cells from damage (Qiao et al., 2024). Result from CSB antioxidant activity assays (Fig. 4b) revealed that, compared to the control, DKH-CSB exhibited significantly enhanced DPPH· and ABTS· radical scavenging activities (P < 0.05). Specifically, DKH-CSB showed approximately 1.3-fold and 1-fold increases in DPPH· and ABTS· radical scavenging activities, respectively. These findings are consistent with the results of TPC and TFC determinations, including a strong positive correlation between flavonoid/polyphenol content and DPPH/ABTS· radical scavenging capacity, consistent with the findings reported by Bozkus (2025). Polyphenols, a group of plant-derived antioxidant compounds that include phenolic acids and flavonoids, possess strong free radical scavenging capabilities and are associated with reduced risks of diseases such as cardiovascular disorders (Wan et al., 2020). Furthermore, polysaccharides, which are abundant in Hericium erinaceus and kudzu, may enhance antioxidant activity through two distinct mechanisms: by donating protons to quench the stable DPPH· radical and by donating electrons to convert ABTS· into its non-radical form (Mirzadeh et al., 2020). Additionally, the β-cyanin protein present in pitaya not only enhances its color but also exhibits antioxidant activity. Therefore, incorporating of DKH into CSB can effectively increase its TPC, TFC, and overall antioxidant capacity.

The DF in DKH enhanced the cholesterol adsorption capacity

The CAC of different CSB samples was determined under conditions simulating the gastric environment (pH 2.0) and the intestinal environment (pH 7.0), with results shown in Fig. 4c. In the simulated intestinal environment, CAC values were ranked as follows: WW-CSB (1009 ± 92 μg/g) > DKH-CSB (851 ± 101 μg/g) > WF-CSB (763 ± 47 μg/g). In contrast, under simulated gastric conditions, the cholesterol adsorption capacity of all samples was significantly lower (0.18–0.38 mg/g) compared to the intestinal environment. Specifically, CAC values were 379 ± 74 μg/g for WW-CSB and 337 ± 153 μg/g for DKH-CSB.

Excessive cholesterol intake contributes to chronic diseases such as hyperlipidemia and cardiovascular disorders. Dietary fiber-polyphenol complexes can reduce cholesterol absorption (Du et al., 2021). This process further inhibits cholesterol synthesis and increases fecal cholesterol excretion, ultimately lowering systemic cholesterol levels. Additionally, studies indicate that the surfaces of dietary fibers contain active groups capable of chelating cholesterol, thereby promoting its absorption and contributing to lowered blood lipid levels (Xi et al., 2023).

These results demonstrate that the higher dietary fiber content in DKH-CSB and WW-CSB confers superior cholesterol adsorption capacity compared to WF-CSB.

DKH enhanced the regulatory effect of CSB on beneficial bacteria

Lactobacillus and Akkermansia are crucial probiotic genera within the gut microbiota, and enhancing their abundance through dietary supplementation is essential for maintaining intestinal health. This study employed an in vitro fermentation model to analyze the effects of CSB on the growth of Lactobacillus spp. and Akkermansia spp. As shown in Fig. 4d-e, all three CSB groups significantly promoted the proliferation of these bacterial genera throughout the fermentation period. The proliferation rate was higher during the initial 0–12 h phase, followed by slower growth from 12 to 24 h. Notably, DKH-CSB induced significantly higher proliferation of both Lactobacillus and Akkermansia at 12 h and 24 h compared to WF-CSB. This beneficial effect is primarily attributed to the higher fiber content in DKH-CSB.

Adequate dietary fiber intake reduces the risk of chronic non-communicable diseases (Rezende et al., 2022). Beyond its role in lowering chronic disease risk, dietary fiber serves as a substrate for fermentation by beneficial gut bacteria, thereby influencing the composition and metabolic activity of the intestinal microbiota and functioning as a prebiotic. Prebiotic dietary fibers act as selective substrates for beneficial gut bacteria, including Lactobacillus (Markowiak & Slizewska, 2017). Previous studies have reported that supplementation with insoluble dietary fiber from Hericium erinaceus increases the abundance of Akkermansia (Wang, Zhou, et al., 2025). During fermentation gut microbiota convert dietary fiber into short-chain fatty acids, which provide energy for colonic epithelial cells and selectively promote the growth of beneficial gut bacteria (McNeil, 1984). These findings demonstrate that DKH-CSB exerts a significant proliferative effect on intestinal probiotics.

DKH reduced starch digestion in CSB

This study employed in vitro methods to assess the effect of substituting WF with DKH on starch digestion in CSB. The results demonstrated that DKH significantly influenced the RS content, starch hydrolysis rate, EGI, and postprandial glycemic response (Fig. 5a-e). Fig. 5a shows the proportional distribution of starch fractions in the three CSB groups. The incorporation of DKH increased the RS and SDS content while reducing the RDS content. Starch is the primary component of CSB. RDS is rapidly digested and absorbed in the mouth and small intestine, releasing energy. However, excessive RDS intake can lead to significant postprandial blood glucose fluctuations. SDS is digested more slowly but is completely fully absorbed in the small intestine, contributing to glycemic homeostasis. Intake of RS provided additional benefits, including the prevention of colon cancer, improvement of gut health, reduction of the glycemic index, and modulation of serum cholesterol levels (Li et al., 2021). As digestion progressed, glucose levels in the digesta of all CSB groups showed an overall increasing trend (Fig. 5c). Higher glucose levels at the same digestion points indicate greater starch hydrolysis and more rapid glucose absorption, resulting in faster increases in postprandial blood glucose. Across all digestion time points, the WF-CSB group exhibited significantly higher glucose release than the other two groups. In contrast, the DKH-CSB group demonstrated significantly lower glucose release compared to the other two groups (P < 0.05). Starch digestibility increased over time in all groups, with the highest hydrolysis rate occurring within the first 30 min. In the in vitro simulated starch digestion, the hydrolysis rate reached 71.53 % for WF-CSB but only 52.37 % for DKH-CSB. The EGI, used to predict the in vitro glycemic response, reflects the rate of starch digestion and correlates well with in vivo results. Fig. 5b presents the HI and EGI of the three CSB types during digestion, with the WF-CSB HI set as 100. Both DKH-CSB and WW-CSB groups exhibited significantly lower HI and EGI values than WF-CSB (P < 0.05), with DKH-CSB showing the most pronounced effect.

In vitro starch digestibility and glycemic response of Chinese steamed bun. (a) Proportional distribution of starch fractions, (b) Hydrolysis index and estimated glycemic index, (c) Glucose release profiles during simulated intestinal digestion; (d) Starch hydrolysis rate under simulated gastrointestinal conditions, (e) Postprandial blood glucose response curves.

Collectively, these findings indicate that incorporating of DKH significantly inhibits starch digestion and reduces the GI of CSB. Wang et al. (2020) found that incorporating shiitake stipe powder significantly reduced the release of reducing sugars after in vitro digestion. Tang et al. (2019) also highlighted that protein-starch interactions can affect amylase activity and starch hydrolysis. The inhibitory effects of DKH-CSB can be attributes to its rich composition of polyphenols, flavonoids, and dietary fiber. Polyphenols can reduce starch hydrolysis and slow glucose release, while flavonoids not only inhibit starch-hydrolyzing enzymes (Qin et al., 2024) but also bind to starch via non-covalent interactions, thereby suppressing digestion (Huang et al., 2022; Li, Zhang, et al., 2023). Additionally, dietary fiber, which is largely indigestible, develops gel-forming properties after gelatinization. These gels form a matrix barrier that limits enzyme access to starch, consequently inhibiting glucose release. Moreover, the compacted microstructure of CSB produced by DKH substitution may physically hinder interactions between starch and digestive enzymes. Together, these mechanisms synergistically attenuate the starch hydrolysis rate and EGI of CSB.

Postprandial blood glucose response

After consumption of CSB, postprandial blood glucose levels increased significantly in all three groups. Participants consuming WF-CSB reached a maximum blood glucose level of 7.79 ± 0.23 mmol/L at 45 min. In contrast, those consuming DKH-CSB reached a maximum level at 7.45 ± 0.14 mmol/L at 45 min, while the WW-CSB group exhibited an earlier peak at 30 min, reaching 7.44 ± 0.20 mmol/L (Fig. 5e). Consequently, the WW-CSB group reached a maximum earlier than the WF-CSB and DKH-CSB groups. Following the peak, blood glucose levels declined in all groups, with the DKH-CSB group demonstrating a comparatively faster reduction than the WF-CSB group. At 120 min postprandial, all groups had nearly returned to baseline glycemia. The incremental AUC for DKH-CSB (755.9 ± 8.20 mmol/L·min) was significantly lower than that of WF-CSB (813.4 ± 12.12 mmol/L·min) (P < 0.05). Collectively, these findings indicate that the DKH-CSB group exhibited the lowest overall glycemic response. Incorporation of DKH into steamed bread contributed to reduced postprandial glycemic fluctuations, a lower peak glucose level, and accelerated postprandial glucose recovery, consistent with its in vitro digestion profile.

Conclusion

This study successfully optimized the formulation and processing parameters for incorporating DKH (a composite of pitaya, kudzu root, and Hericium erinaceus) into CSB through single-factor and response surface methodology experiments, while comprehensively evaluating its impact on nutritional composition, quality attributes, microstructure, and functional properties. Compared with WF-CSB, DKH-CSB contained significantly higher levels of crude fiber, total phenolics, and total flavonoids, along with a lower caloric value. Texture profile analysis revealed that the dietary fiber in DKH significantly altered textural properties of CSB, increasing hardness while decreasing cohesiveness, springiness, and chewiness (P < 0.05). Microstructural examination indicated that DKH components formed a physical barrier on the surface of starch granules and disrupts gluten protein interactions, thereby weakening the gluten network structure. More importantly, the interaction of DKH-derived dietary fiber with the dough system reduced starch digestibility while simultaneously enhancing antioxidant activity, cholesterol adsorption capacity, and the ability to promote beneficial gut microbiota (Fig. 6).

Conventional Chinese steamed bun demonstrates low nutritional density yet elevated glycemic index. This study engineered functional CSB through incorporation of dragon fruit, kudzu, and Hericium erinaceus (collectively termed DKH) ingredients abundant in nutritious with focused investigation into texture profiles, microstructural characteristics, functional features, and starch digestion behavior. Results revealed that the incorporation of DKH worsened the TPA of CSB. DKH fortification significantly enhanced beneficial constituents including dietary fiber and polyphenols, but the high fiber content destroyed the gluten network structure of CSB. More importantly, DKH enhanced multiple functional properties of CSB, including antioxidant activity and the proliferation of beneficial bacteria. In addition, it reduced the starch hydrolysis rate and the GI of CSB. These findings establish DKH's potential as functional additives for developing fiber-enriched, low-GI staple foods.

The study successfully developed a functional CSB, that exhibits low-GI and high nutrient density. This work demonstrates the viability of improving the nutritional and functional profile of traditional staples through recipe optimization, thereby laying the groundwork for a systematic evaluation of its health benefits and the elucidation of its underlying mechanisms Moreover, the proposed mechanisms behind the antioxidant and prebiotic effects require validation through controlled in vivo models. The result of postprandial blood glucose needs to be confirmed in a larger and more diverse cohort to ensure their robustness and generalizability.
