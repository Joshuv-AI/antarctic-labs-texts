True AP imaging during lumbar medial branch radiofrequency neurotomy: Interobserver reliability
Authors: Patrick H. Waring, W. Evan Rivers, Duncan L. Bralts, D. Keith Granger II, Timothy P. Maus, Belinda Duszynski, Michael B. Furman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTrue segmental imaging, in AP and lateral planes, is necessary to accurately place an RF cannula contiguous with the targeted medial branch and achieve effective pain relief with lumbar radiofrequency neurotomy (LMBRFN). True lateral imaging and its interobserver reliability during LMBRFN have been described. A complementary true AP imaging technique has recently been described, but its interobserver reliability has yet to be studied.ObjectiveThis study aims to determine the interobserver reliability of the recently described true AP imaging technique for LMBRFN.MethodsAfter obtaining IRB exemption, true and untrue AP images were collected from consecutive LMBRFN procedures performed during the normal course of the primary author's (PW) practice. A 100-slide testing set was formulated. Each slide contained a true and an untrue counterpart AP image depicting the same single-level RF cannula placement targeting an L3 or L4 medial branch or an L5 dorsal ramus. After slide randomization, a Google Forms test was created to capture observer responses. Two sets of observers were used to represent novice (DB,KG) and experienced (MF,ER) observers. Each observer independently reviewed the testing slide set, recording a total of 300 responses for the three binary decisions on each slide: which image was true, which corrective maneuver (oblique or tilt) was required to make the untrue image a true image, and finally, which was the direction of the appropriate corrective maneuver (right or left oblique/cranial or caudal tilt). Each observer's test was compared to the answer key established by two non-observer authors (PW,TM). Interobserver agreement for both the novice and experienced groups was determined for each of the three decisions using the Kappa score calculation. Individual observer performance was also determined.ResultsFor the determination of the true image, the novice group's interobserver agreement (Kappa score) was 0.98 (0.94,1.0); the experienced group's was 0.96 (0.91,1.0). For the oblique or tilt corrective maneuver decision, the novice group's Kappa was 1.0; the experienced group's was 0.98 (0.94,1.0). For the direction of the oblique corrective maneuver, the novice group's Kappa was 1.0; the experienced group's was 0.88 (0.75,1.0). For the direction of the tilt corrective maneuver, the novice group's Kappa was 0.96 (0.88,1.0); the experienced group's was 0.92 (0.81,1.0). Individual observer performance data on the total of 300 decisions ranged from 98% (an experienced observer) to 100% (a novice observer).ConclusionsObtaining true AP imaging for LMBRFN by the newly described technique is supported by significant interobserver reliability that ranges from substantial to perfect. This AP imaging technique, when combined with true lateral imaging, provides true lumbar segmental imaging. True segmental imaging is recommended not only for LMBRFN but for other commonly performed lumbar spine procedures.

Introduction

Lumbar medial branch radiofrequency neurotomy (LMBRFN) is commonly used to treat low back pain mediated by facet joint medial branches [1]. The RF cannula's active tip must be parallel to and near the medial branch so that the RF thermal energy creates a finite, prolate spheroid-shaped tissue lesion that captures the medial branch [2]. True imaging must be performed segmentally (determined level-by-level) due to the typical lordotic and scoliotic curvature of the spine [3]. For this paper, “true segmental imaging” will henceforth be called “true imaging”. True imaging includes both true AP (frontal plane) and true lateral (sagittal plane) imaging. True imaging is necessary to determine the RF cannula's relation to the targeted medial branch [1,3]. A detailed true lateral imaging technique and its interobserver reliability have previously been described [4,5]. A technical note recently outlined a precise approach to true AP imaging for LMBRFN (Fig. 1, Fig. 2), but its interobserver reliability has yet to be analyzed [6]. Methods

Study design

This study represents a retrospective review of prospectively acquired AP fluoroscopic images obtained to determine the interobserver reliability of true AP imaging during LMBRFN. The study received an IRB exemption from WIRB-Copernicus Group (WCG® IRB), formerly Western IRB®.

Obtaining images during routine clinical practice

Images were collected in September 2024 from consecutive L4-5, L5-S1 LMBRFN procedures performed during the primary author's (PW) routine clinical practice. The LMBRFN procedures were performed according to the primary author's typical practice using International Pain and Spine Intervention Society (IPSIS) LMBRFN and true imaging techniques [1,4,6].

RF cannulae were positioned at the L3 and L4 medial branches and the L5 dorsal rami. True AP views were obtained by maneuvering the fluoroscope in the axial plane using right or left oblique rotation such that the spinous process was positioned midway between the pedicles [6]. Next, cranial or caudal tilt fluoroscopic movements were utilized to align the superior endplate cortical bone, creating as “sharp” an endplate image as possible [6]. During this process of obtaining true AP images, multiple initial untrue and final true images of proper cannula placements were saved and analyzed. Using these saved images, 100 comparison slides were created, each containing a true and an untrue image of the same-segment RF cannula placement.

Creating the teaching and testing slide sets

In addition to a twelve-slide teaching set comprising twenty-four true/untrue comparison images (Fig. 3, Fig. 4), the primary author (PW) created a similar hundred-slide testing set with two hundred comparison images. All images were saved as de-identified images. Before employing the randomization process (using a Google Slides shuffler program), the true image was alternated right-to-left with the untrue image on alternate slides to prevent observers from guessing which image was usually true. The testing slide set was formulated from thirteen left-sided and fourteen right-sided RF cannula placements. Cannula placements in the testing comparison slides were nearly evenly distributed among L3, L4 medial branch and L5 dorsal ramus targets. Corrective maneuvers in the testing answer key comprised a roughly equal representation of 54 oblique and 46 tilt maneuvers. While right and left oblique corrective maneuvers were equally represented in 54 slides, cranial tilt was considered the corrective maneuver in 25, and caudal tilt in 21. A Google Form was created to capture observer selections.

Observer selection and data collection

Two fellows (DB,KG) from the senior author's (MF) training program were chosen as novice observers, and two interventional pain instructors with many years of clinical practice (ER,MF) were selected as experienced observers. To provide instruction on the true AP imaging technique (6), the primary author conducted a brief individualized conference using the teaching slide set with each novice and experienced observer. The observers were instructed on how to distinguish a true from an untrue AP image and how to identify the proper corrective maneuver (oblique versus tilt). Each observer then independently reviewed the randomized, de-identified testing slide set. Each observer was first required to determine which image was true on each of the one hundred Google Form slides and which maneuver, oblique or tilt, would be needed to correct the untrue image. Finally, if the corrective maneuver was determined to be oblique, observers were asked whether the oblique maneuver should be right or left. Likewise, if the corrective maneuver was determined to be tilt, they were asked whether the tilt maneuver should be cranial or caudal. The total number of decisions made by each observer was three hundred. The observers were instructed not to interact with each other regarding the testing slides and to take their time on the testing process to avoid test fatigue, given the large number (300) of total decisions.

Statistical analysis

First, a tally was taken to determine the number of times the two novice observers agreed on the true AP image. A separate tally of true AP image agreement was also taken for the two experienced observers. Next, the number of times the novice observers agreed upon the proper oblique or tilt corrective maneuver to make the untrue image true was determined; the same oblique or tilt tally was obtained for the experienced observers. Next, the number of times the novice observers agreed on the direction of oblique (right or left) or tilt (cranial or caudal) corrective maneuvers was determined; the same process was used to determine the agreement between the experienced observers regarding the direction of the corrective maneuver. Finally, the degree of agreement between the novice observers and between the experienced observers for the true/untrue, oblique/tilt corrective maneuver, and the direction of the corrective maneuver decisions was determined using Cohen's Kappa interobserver analysis [7]. Kappa scores can be assigned to descriptive categories that range from no agreement (<0), slight agreement (0–0.20), fair agreement (0.21–0.40), moderate agreement (0.41–0.60), substantial agreement (0.61–0.80), and near-perfect agreement(0.81–0.99) to perfect agreement (1.0) [8].

Only ten total observer errors were made. When errors did occur, they occurred on different comparison slides with no duplication of wrong choices among the observers. Discussion

The interobserver agreement of true segmental AP imaging for LMBRFN has been previously studied, with the data presented in abstract form at the 2022 Spine Intervention Society Annual Meeting [9]. True AP segmental imaging was defined by “spinous processes positioned midway between pedicle shadows and ‘squared-off' superior endplates.” Two experienced observers reviewed 100 randomized AP image slides. On each slide, a single AP image depicted two properly placed (IPSIS technique) RF cannulae at adjacent levels for lesioning the L3 and L4 medial branches or the L4 medial branch and L5 dorsal ramus. At least one segment was considered true by the test designer. The Kappa score for determining which segment was true was 0.99 (near-perfect). The observers also agreed nearly perfectly regarding which segment best represented proper technique. An almost perfect correlation existed between true AP segmental imaging and the determination of appropriate cannula placement. Although not peer-reviewed, these data suggest that true imaging and proper RF cannula placement are closely correlated, and that observers can reliably distinguish true from untrue AP imaging. However, until the recently published technical note, a detailed approach to true AP imaging, including clear guidance on specific corrective fluoroscopic maneuvers to correct an untrue AP image, had yet to be formulated.

The current study's results indicate that after a brief education regarding this recently described true AP imaging technique, independent observers demonstrate a high degree of interobserver reliability not only in the determination of true versus untrue images, but also in the prediction of the proper corrective maneuver (oblique versus tilt) and the direction of the oblique maneuver (right versus left) or tilt maneuver (cranial versus caudal).

The current study also attempted to investigate whether experienced or novice observers would perform differently. While the first novice observer achieved a perfect score for each slide's three-part question, selecting the correct answer for all 300 choices, the second novice observer missed only two decisions, for an almost perfect score of 298/300 (99%). The first experienced observer scored the same as the second novice observer, with a near-perfect score of 298/300 correct (99%). The second experienced observer missed six decisions but still scored at a very high level, with 294/300 correct (98%)—all observers, whether novice or experienced, performed well. The results of this study indicate high interobserver reliability for the described technique of obtaining true AP (frontal plane) imaging in LMBRFN. When combined with the results from the earlier study, which showed substantial or greater interobserver reliability for obtaining true lateral (sagittal plane) imaging [5], the use of true segmental imaging for LMBRFN is supported. True segmental imaging allows for the precise localization of the RF cannula's lesioning tip in three-dimensional space in relation to the observed bony targets, and by inference, the medial branch or L5 dorsal ramus [3]. Furthermore, true segmental imaging has been recommended for many other lumbar spine interventions [1], but the interobserver reliability of a critical component, true AP imaging, had not been established until the present study.

Strengths/limitations of the study

Because there were no duplicate errors among the observers and fewer than 1% (10/1200) observer errors, this study's dataset may prove useful for true AP imaging education for both novice and experienced interventional pain physicians. Except for the lower-limit confidence interval for the experienced observers regarding the direction of the oblique corrective maneuver (Table 3), all observers (novice and experienced) reached perfect or near-perfect agreement for all true AP imaging decisions, lending credence to the proposed technique for true AP imaging.

However, an argument can be made that the concept of true AP imaging is so widely understood that observers would logically perform well and have high interobserver agreement without needing a description of a true imaging technique or education. A follow-up study comparing novice/experienced interobserver agreement and test performance before exposure to true AP imaging technique/education to the same parameters post-exposure (using a reshuffled slideset) would help determine the effect of prior knowledge on test results.

Conclusion

Based on the results of this study, the described technique of obtaining true segmental AP imaging for LMBRFN is supported by high levels of interobserver reliability that range from substantial to perfect. This AP imaging technique, when combined with the previously described true lateral imaging technique (also supported by substantial or greater interobserver reliability), should be used for the proper LMBRFN performance and documentation [1,5]. True segmental imaging is also recommended for many other commonly performed lumbar spine procedures [1].
