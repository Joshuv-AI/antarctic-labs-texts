Is It Time to Redefine the FMR Phenotype?
Authors: Alex Pui-Wai Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: functional mitral regurgitation, heart failure, transthoracic echocardiography

Functional mitral regurgitation (FMR) remains a complex and heterogeneous entity, with management strategies often guided by trial-derived paradigms that have yet to be fully reconciled in clinical practice. The landmark COAPT1 (Cardiovascular Outcomes Assessment of the MitraClip Percutaneous Therapy for Heart Failure Patients with Functional Mitral Regurgitation) and MITRA-FR2 (Percutaneous Repair with the MitraClip Device for Severe Functional/Secondary Mitral Regurgitation) trials, along with the recent RESHAPE-HF23 (Randomized Investigation of the MitraClip Device in Heart Failure: Second Trial in Patients with Clinically Significant Functional Mitral Regurgitation), have underscored the need for a deeper understanding of FMR phenotypes, particularly as transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) becomes increasingly adopted. In this context, the study by Lien et al4 published in this issue of JACC: Asia, provides a timely and morphologically detailed classification of FMR, offering insights that may help bridge the gap between echocardiographic phenotype and clinical outcome.

The authors retrospectively analyzed 699 patients with at least moderately severe FMR from 2 tertiary centers, classifying them into 3 morphological phenotypes: ventricular FMR with symmetrical tethering, ventricular FMR with asymmetrical tethering, and atrial functional mitral regurgitation (AFMR). Their approach, rooted in mitral valve morphology rather than arbitrary left ventricular ejection fraction cutoffs, represents a pragmatic step toward a more pathophysiology-aware classification. Notably, they further subdivided AFMR into central jet and posterior jet (hamstrung posterior leaflet) subtypes, the latter suggesting a more advanced atrial remodeling stage.

Several findings from this study deserve emphasis. First, although patients with AFMR exhibited better unadjusted survival compared with ventricular FMR subgroups, this advantage dissipated after multivariable adjustment, implying that underlying ventricular and atrial dysfunction—rather than phenotype per se—drives outcomes. Second, the study identifies low diastolic blood pressure (≤73.7 mm Hg), reduced left atrial reservoir strain (≤14.8%), and impaired apical 4-chamber left ventricular longitudinal strain (≤9.3%) as independent predictors of adverse outcomes, providing tangible echocardiographic and hemodynamic markers for risk stratification. Third, the high prevalence (40%) of hamstrung posterior leaflet in AFMR highlights a clinically relevant subtype that may be under-recognized and potentially less responsive to standard M-TEER.

However, the study also raises important questions. The lack of association between traditional morphological metrics and adjusted outcomes suggests that conventional echocardiographic parameters alone may be insufficient to guide therapy. Instead, the data reinforce the importance of integrating advanced techniques like strain imaging and hemodynamic assessment into the routine echocardiographic evaluation of FMR.5 The findings also call for prospective validation of these phenotypic classifications in cohorts undergoing M-TEER, particularly to determine whether ventricular FMR with asymmetrical tethering (resembling disproportionate mitral regurgitation) and ventricular FMR with symmetrical tethering (resembling proportionate mitral regurgitation) respond differently to intervention.

From an imaging perspective, the use of apical 4-chamber left ventricular longitudinal strain rather than global longitudinal strain is a practical adaptation to real-world imaging limitations, although it may not fully capture global left ventricular mechanics. Similarly, the semi-automated strain analysis used here aligns with emerging trends toward automation in echocardiography, enhancing reproducibility in clinical practice.

In conclusion, Lien et al4 have provided a morphologically rich framework for classifying FMR, moving beyond oversimplified dichotomies of “proportionate” and “disproportionate” regurgitation. Their work underscores that although phenotype may inform mechanism, outcome is driven by a confluence of factors including ventricular and atrial strain, blood pressure, and right ventricular function. As we enter an era of personalized structural heart interventions, such phenotypic insights—coupled with functional and hemodynamic data—will be crucial in selecting the right patient for the right treatment at the right time.

Future studies should focus on validating these classifications in therapeutic cohorts, particularly those undergoing transcatheter repair, and exploring whether strain-guided management can improve outcomes in this challenging patient population.
