Single-stage surgical repair of congenital aortopulmonary window associated with interrupted aortic arch in two toddlers: a case report
Authors: Junxiang Pan, Mengqi Zhao, Xiaoya Zhang, Enrui Zhang, Yongqiang Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCongenital aortopulmonary window (APW) associated with interrupted aortic arch (IAA) is a rare and severe form of congenital heart disease. The optimal surgical timing and strategy remain challenging, particularly beyond infancy. We report two toddlers diagnosed with APW and IAA who underwent one-stage surgical repair. At the time of surgery, the children were 17 and 22 months old, with body weights of 6.6 kg and 8.0 kg, respectively. Both patients survived and experienced no perioperative complications. During postoperative follow-up of 12 and 19 months, respectively, the children demonstrated New York Heart Association (NYHA) class I cardiac function, with normal intracardiac anatomy and no evidence of residual shunting, vascular stenosis, or pulmonary hypertension. These cases indicate that delayed one-stage repair beyond infancy can be performed safely and effectively in selected patients with preoperative evaluation and individualized surgical planning.Keywords: Aortopulmonary window, Interrupted aortic arch, One-stage repair, Toddler

Background

Congenital aortopulmonary window (APW) associated with interrupted aortic arch (IAA) is an extremely rare cardiac anomaly. The phenotype in which the right pulmonary artery arises from the ascending aorta and the ventricular septum is intact is referred to as Berry syndrome [1]. Owing to its rarity, most literature consists of isolated case reports. Affected infants typically develop early heart failure, and severe cases are life-threatening. Early neonatal or infant surgical repair is generally recommended, as delayed correction may lead to irreversible pulmonary vascular disease and loss of operability. We report two uncommon cases diagnosed beyond infancy and successfully treated with single-stage repair during the toddler period.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 17-month-old boy (weight 6.6 kg) was admitted with “growth retardation and dyspnea for one year”. Physical examination showed ruddy lips, pectus carinatum, coarse breath sounds, and a grade 2/6 systolic murmur at the left sternal border (3rd–4th intercostal space). Oxygen saturation was 95–99%.

Chest X-ray showed cardiomegaly with prominent pulmonary markings and mild prominence of the pulmonary artery segment (cardiothoracic ratio 0.64) (Fig. 1a). Echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 65% and a left ventricular diastolic diameter (LVDd) of 34 mm. A perimembranous ventricular septal defect (VSD) measuring approximately 10 mm was detected, with 30% aortic override. A large patent ductus arteriosus measuring approximately 8 mm was observed at the junction of the descending aorta and pulmonary artery. In addition, a 22 mm APW extending to the origin of the right pulmonary artery demonstrated bidirectional shunting (Fig. 1b). The transverse aortic arch measured 8 mm, with interruption distal to the left subclavian artery (Fig. 1c). Contrast-enhanced CT revealed an APW extending to the right pulmonary artery (Fig. 1d) and IAA. Cardiac catheterization revealed an aortic pressure of 81/49 mmHg (mean 60 mmHg) and a pulmonary artery pressure of 80/16 mmHg (mean 37 mmHg). Final diagnosis: APW (IIB), IAA (A), VSD, overriding aorta, patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), biventricular enlargement, bilateral superior vena cava.

Preoperative evaluation of Case 1. (a) Chest X-ray of Case 1. (b) Echocardiography showing APW. (c) Echocardiography showing IAA. (d) Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrating APW. APW aortopulmonary window; IAA interrupted aortic arch

Under general anesthesia with low-flow cerebral perfusion, one-stage repair addressed the IAA, APW, VSD, and PDA. Cardiopulmonary bypass was established via ascending aortic and bicaval venous cannulation. The proximal right and left pulmonary arteries were mobilized and then temporarily clamped. After systemic cooling, the ascending aorta was clamped proximal to the APW, and cold histidine–tryptophan–ketoglutarate (HTK) cardioplegia was administered. The right atrium was opened, and the ventricular septal defect was closed using a patch. After cooling to 25 °C, the aortic cannula was inserted into the innominate artery, and low-flow cerebral perfusion was maintained at 30 mL/kg/min with the supra-aortic vessels clamped. The ductus arteriosus was divided, the pulmonary arterial end was double-ligated, and the descending aorta was mobilized to a normal segment with complete excision of ductal tissue. The APW was opened, and the aortic side was closed using continuous 5 − 0 Prolene sutures. The distal ascending aorta was incised up to the level of the left subclavian artery, followed by end-to-side anastomosis with the descending aorta to restore antegrade aortic flow. The pulmonary artery incision was reconstructed using a glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardial patch. The heart resumed spontaneous rhythm, and weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass was uneventful. Modified ultrafiltration was performed. Intraoperative pulmonary artery pressure was 51/18/33 mmHg (approximately 65% of systemic pressure). No blood pressure gradient was detected between the upper and lower extremities.

Intraoperatively, inhaled nitric oxide and intravenous vasoactive support (dopamine, milrinone, nitroglycerin, and isoproterenol) were used to facilitate weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass. Cardiopulmonary bypass time was 162 min, aortic cross-clamp time was 120 min, and low-flow cerebral perfusion time was 47 min. Postoperatively, the patient was transferred to the ICU and continued on inhaled nitric oxide and intravenous vasoactive support (isoproterenol, dopamine, milrinone, and nitroglycerin) for cardiopulmonary support. Hemodynamic stabilization was achieved within 46 h, allowing discontinuation of mechanical ventilation. After 7 days in the ICU, intravenous vasoactive agents were discontinued, and the patient was transferred to the general ward. The postoperative course was uneventful, with no complications observed.

Postoperative echocardiography showed normalization of LVDd (29 mm), preserved LVEF (65%), complete APW closure, restored arch continuity, and reduced pulmonary systolic pressure (from 76 mmHg to 33 mmHg). After discharge, oral bosentan was administered for 3 months and then discontinued after normalization of pulmonary artery pressure and satisfactory clinical recovery. At 1-year follow-up, the child was in New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class I, oxygen saturation of 98–100% and normal echocardiographic findings.

Case 2

A 22-month-old girl (weight 8 kg), was admitted due to “heart murmur detected for over one year”. She had growth retardation and a grade III pansystolic murmur with thrill. Oxygen saturation was 85–97%.

Chest X-ray showed increased pulmonary vascular markings, cardiomegaly (cardiothoracic ratio 0.62), and prominent pulmonary artery segment (Fig. 2a). Echocardiography showed an LVEF of 65% and an LVDd of 41 mm. A large patent ductus arteriosus measuring approximately 8 mm in diameter was identified at the junction of the descending aorta and pulmonary artery. A 20 mm APW extended to the right pulmonary artery origin (Fig. 2b). The aorta was interrupted distal to the left subclavian artery, with no distal blood flow detected. Contrast-enhanced CT showed a 14 mm APW on the left posterolateral wall of the ascending aorta, extending to the origin of the right pulmonary artery (Fig. 2c). The aorta was interrupted distal to the origin of the left subclavian artery (Fig. 2d). Cardiac catheterization revealed an aortic pressure of 68/40 mmHg (mean 54 mmHg) and a pulmonary artery pressure of 68/41 mmHg (mean 54 mmHg). Baseline hemodynamic measurements, including pulmonary vascular resistance index (PVRI), were obtained while breathing room air and repeated after administration of 100% oxygen (10 L/min) to assess pulmonary vasoreactivity. The PVRI decreased from 6.76 to 5.84 WU·m² after oxygen inhalation. Final diagnosis: APW (IIB), IAA (A), patent ductus arteriosus, and severe pulmonary hypertension.

Preoperative evaluation of Case 2. (a) Chest X-ray of Case 2. (b) Echocardiography showing APW. (c) Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrating APW. (d) Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrating IAA. APW aortopulmonary window; IAA interrupted aortic arch

Under general anesthesia with low-flow cerebral perfusion, one-stage repair addressed the IAA, APW and PDA. The cannulation strategy and initial operative steps were similar to those described for Case 1. After cooling to approximately 20 °C, low-flow cerebral perfusion (30 mL/kg/min) was initiated. The supra-aortic vessels were clamped. The APW was opened while preserving adequate pulmonary arterial tissue on the aortic side, and the aortic side was then closed. The ductus arteriosus was divided, and the descending aorta was further mobilized with complete excision of ductal tissue up to a normal-caliber segment. The inferior wall of the aortic arch was incised, and end-to-side anastomosis was performed between the descending aorta and the aortic arch. After thorough deairing of the aortic root, the aortic cross-clamp was reapplied, the aortic cannula was returned to its original position, and systemic perfusion was restored. The aortic anastomosis was inspected, with no active bleeding observed. The main and right pulmonary arteries were augmented using autologous pericardium. After adequate deairing, the ascending aorta was unclamped, and spontaneous cardiac rhythm resumed. Following separation from cardiopulmonary bypass, intraoperative pulmonary artery pressure was 29/14/19 mmHg. Transesophageal echocardiography showed normal cardiac and valvular function, and no blood pressure gradient was detected between the upper and lower extremities.

Intraoperatively, inhaled nitric oxide and intravenous vasoactive support (milrinone and nitroglycerin) were used to facilitate weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass. Cardiopulmonary bypass time was 124 min, aortic cross-clamp time was 87 min, and low-flow cerebral perfusion time was 40 min. Postoperatively, the patient was transferred to the ICU and received inhaled nitric oxide and intravenous vasoactive support (dopamine, milrinone, and nitroglycerin) for cardiopulmonary support. On postoperative day 2, due to transient hemodynamic instability, dobutamine and epinephrine were added. The patient required mechanical ventilation for 52 h and was successfully extubated thereafter. Hemodynamics improved by postoperative day 7, and vasoactive agents were gradually tapered. By postoperative day 14, intravenous vasoactive agents were discontinued. The postoperative course was otherwise uneventful, with no additional complications observed.

Postoperative echocardiography showed an LVEF of 65% and an LVDd of 27 mm, complete APW closure without residual shunt, and restored aortic arch continuity with normal antegrade flow. Pulmonary artery systolic pressure decreased from 54 to 27 mmHg. Postoperative contrast-enhanced CT confirmed successful repair of the aortopulmonary window and restoration of aortic arch continuity without residual interruption or significant arch stenosis (Fig. 3). At 19-month follow-up, the child was in NYHA functional class I with 98–100% oxygen saturation and normal echocardiographic findings.

Discussion and conclusions

APW accounts for approximately 0.2% of all congenital heart defects and is extremely rare when associated with IAA [2]. The pathogenesis is thought to be related to abnormal neural crest development and gene mutations such as NKX2-5, TBX1, and GATA6 [3]. Among these patients, the phenotype characterized by APW, IAA, the right pulmonary artery arising from the ascending aorta, and an intact ventricular septum is known as Berry syndrome, as observed in Case 2 [1]. The rarity and anatomical complexity of this condition make individualized evaluation and management essential.

Current management of APW associated with IAA is largely focused on early repair during the neonatal or early infant period. The largest neonatal cohort reported by Konstantinov et al. included 20 patients with APW and IAA, with single-stage repair achieving low mortality and acceptable mid-term outcomes, though aortic arch reintervention remained frequent [2]. Similarly, Hu et al. described 16 infants with Berry syndrome (median age 90.5 days, range 8–170 days) undergoing one-stage repair, with low perioperative mortality and acceptable freedom from reoperation over 10 years [4]. Due to the large left-to-right shunt, APW associated with IAA often results in early heart failure and rapidly progressive pulmonary hypertension. Therefore, early diagnosis and timely single-stage repair are essential to separate the pulmonary and systemic circulations, prevent irreversible pulmonary vascular disease and myocardial damage, and improve long-term outcomes.

However, the optimal management of patients diagnosed beyond infancy remains less well defined. Reports of late-presenting APW suggest that selected patients can still undergo successful one-stage repair, provided that thorough preoperative evaluation including assessment of pulmonary vascular resistance and operability, is performed. Das et al. reported 12 patients presenting beyond 1 year of age (median age 7.5 years), all of whom underwent successful repair with no mortality and progressive reduction in pulmonary artery pressures during follow-up [5]. Similarly, Gowda et al. described a 24-year experience involving 55 patients aged 5 months to 31 years and demonstrated excellent long-term outcomes despite a mean preoperative PVRI approaching 8 WU, provided that pulmonary vascular resistance remained responsive to oxygen [6]. According to published consensus and the 5th World Symposium on Pulmonary Hypertension, a PVRI > 8 WU·m² is generally considered a contraindication for surgery, whereas a PVRI between 4 and 8 WU·m² requires individualized assessment. Acute vasodilator testing may indicate preserved pulmonary vascular reactivity (e.g., a 20% decrease in PVRI), but a positive response alone does not define operability [7]. Similarly, Aggarwal et al. demonstrated that late-presenting patients with APW may still be suitable candidates for surgical repair when pulmonary vascular resistance remains responsive to oxygen [8]. Both of our patients were diagnosed relatively late, however, hemodynamic evaluation confirmed that they were suitable candidates for surgical repair. In our Case 2, although the PVRI remained within the intermediate range, the patient’s relatively young age, favorable pulmonary vascular development, absence of evidence of irreversible pulmonary vascular disease, and suitable anatomy collectively supported the decision to proceed with complete repair. Therefore, surgical decision-making in patients presenting beyond infancy should be based on a comprehensive assessment of pulmonary vascular resistance, pulmonary vascular reactivity, pulmonary vascular development, anatomical suitability, and overall clinical condition rather than age alone [9]. In carefully selected patients without evidence of significant irreversible pulmonary vascular disease, complete repair remains a reasonable therapeutic option.

Surgical strategies should be individualized according to defect size, location, and coronary anatomy. For APW repair, common methods include direct closure, patch repair, and one-stage correction of associated anomalies. In our cases, adequate aortic tissue allowed direct closure without a patch, minimizing postoperative aortic stenosis or dilation, while the pulmonary side was augmented with autologous pericardium to prevent narrowing. Although no evidence of aortic obstruction has been observed during follow-up, the large size of the APW defects (20 and 22 mm) and the use of direct aortic-side closure warrant continued surveillance, as the current follow-up period remains too short to fully assess the long-term risk of proximal ascending aortic narrowing. Intraoperatively, a margin of native aortic tissue was preserved during closure to reduce the risk of postoperative stenosis. The avoidance of prosthetic patch material was also intended to minimize the potential risk of late aneurysmal dilation. Long-term follow-up is therefore required to assess aortic morphology and growth. For IAA repair, techniques include end-to-end anastomosis, end-to-side anastomosis, patch augmentation, and reconstruction using synthetic grafts. In our cases, the aortic arch was reconstructed with end-to-side anastomosis, effectively enlarging the hypoplastic arch and avoiding prosthetic material, thereby reducing the risk of restenosis. Intraoperatively, careful attention must be paid to the risk of left main bronchial compression. For anastomoses under significant tension, augmentation with an autologous pulmonary artery patch may be considered [10].

Survival beyond infancy may lead to severe pulmonary hypertension and Eisenmenger syndrome if left untreated [11]. In patients with elevated pulmonary artery pressure, atrial fenestration may be considered to facilitate postoperative hemodynamic stability [12]. However, in both of our cases, the patients were relatively young, preoperative evaluation showed no evidence of severe or irreversible pulmonary vascular disease, and intraoperative assessment demonstrated a satisfactory reduction in pulmonary artery pressure after complete repair, with preserved right ventricular function following separation from cardiopulmonary bypass. Therefore, atrial fenestration was not performed. Both patients had stable postoperative courses without pulmonary hypertensive crisis or low cardiac output syndrome.

The prognosis depends on associated cardiac anomalies and repair quality. Postoperative surveillance should focus on the risks of aortic arch restenosis, pulmonary artery stenosis, and pulmonary hypertension [13, 14]. In a multicenter study [2], 51% of patients required aortic arch reintervention and 6% pulmonary reintervention. Hu et al. [4] reported on 16 patients with Berry syndrome and found that the freedom from reintervention rates at 1, 5, and 10 years were 84.8%, 75.4%, and 75.4%, respectively. Roubertie et al. [15] reported no deaths or reinterventions over 6 years using a single-patch technique. In our cases, both patients remained free of residual shunts or vascular stenosis during follow-up (12 months and 19 months, respectively), with normalized pulmonary pressures and favorable short-term outcomes, though long-term follow-up is required. Although APW associated with IAA is rare, it is not a newly described condition, and the novelty of this report is therefore limited. Nevertheless, successful single-stage repair beyond infancy remains uncommon.

In summary, APW associated with IAA is rare and complex. Early surgical intervention is recommended once diagnosed. For older children, satisfactory outcomes can still be achieved with single-stage repair, provided that comprehensive preoperative assessment confirms operability.
