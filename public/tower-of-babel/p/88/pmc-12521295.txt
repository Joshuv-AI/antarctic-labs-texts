Phenotypic Characterization of Null Mutants of ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB in S. enterica Serovar Typhimurium
Authors: Indu Pant, Shashidhar Ravindranath
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe phenotypic characteristics of the ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants of Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium were comprehensively analyzed to elucidate the roles of these nucleoid-associated proteins in metabolism and stress responses. Both ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants exhibited enhanced growth on carbon sources such as ribose, mannose, fructose, glycerol, galactose and sucrose compared to the wild-type strain. Conversely, the wild-type strain demonstrated superior growth on xylose. Notably, the ΔcbpB mutant experienced a prolonged lag phase of ~ 20 h when cultured in M9 medium supplemented with maltose. In M9 medium where alanine or arginine served as the sole nitrogen source, both mutants outperformed the wild-type strain in growth, indicating potential alterations in nitrogen metabolism pathways. The ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants were non-motile and exhibited reduced cell sizes compared to the wild-type. Specifically, the ΔcbpA mutant was approximately 0.8 times smaller than the size of the wild-type, while the ΔcbpB mutant was about 1.3 times smaller. The D10 value, representing the radiation dose required to reduce the bacterial population by 90%, was 113 Gy for the wild-type, 140 Gy for ΔcbpA and 104 Gy for ΔcbpB, suggesting differential radiation sensitivities among the strains. Upon heat exposure, the ΔcbpA mutant exhibited a fivefold reduction in viable cell number compared to the wild-type and ΔcbpB, indicating increased heat sensitivity. No significant differences were observed among the strains in response to oxidative stress induced by hydrogen peroxide. After 72 h in 15% salt conditions, the wild-type strain showed a fivefold reduction in cell numbers, whereas both mutants exhibited only a fourfold reduction, suggesting enhanced salt tolerance. These findings underscore the pivotal roles of CbpA and CbpB proteins in regulating metabolic pathways and mediating stress responses in S. Introduction

Nucleoid-associated proteins (NAPs) are DNA-binding proteins that were initially overlooked in terms of their role in maintaining bacterial physiology. Traditionally, they were recognized primarily for their function in the structural organization of genetic material, while their involvement in modulating bacterial physiology and metabolism remained underappreciated [1]. The inability to initially understand the role of NAPs in bacterial physiology stemmed from their non-specific interactions with DNA. However, it is now evident that NAPs significantly influence cellular physiology by mediating genome compaction and relaxation [2, 3]. NAPs regulate critical cellular processes such as transcription, translation and replication which are vital for bacterial survival during periods of nutrient fluctuation commonly encountered in natural environments [4].

The CbpA protein was first discovered in E. coli [5]. This protein was associated with AT rich region; which introduces curves in the DNA [5]. CbpA exhibits DNA-binding affinity comparable to other nucleoid-associated proteins such as H-NS and HU. Its abundance has been observed to increase in nutrient-starved cells [6]. It reaches around 15,000 molecules per cells after two days of starvation [7]. But no function has been assigned to this protein in starved cells. The activity of CbpA protein is repressed by Fis protein in the rapidly growing culture. The CbpA protein is analogue of DnaJ protein. CbpM is another protein which helps CbpA protein in its DNA-binding and co-chaperone activities [8].

Similarly, right origin binding protein (Rob) or Curve DNA-binding protein B (CbpB) is another nucleoid-associated protein, which was identified after a random screening of curved DNA-binding proteins from E. coli [9]. The expression of Rob protein enhances at the onset of stationary phase in Luria broth and phosphate starvation medium [9]. This protein shares 50% similarity in amino acid sequences to soxs and mar genes which have a role in oxidative and multiple antibiotic resistance operon. But apart from this, no other function has been assigned to this protein.

Among the DNA-binding proteins in E. coli, CbpA and Rob remain among the least studied, and their roles in stress responses and metabolic regulation are not well understood. Therefore, this study focuses on investigating the functions of CbpA and Rob in regulating cellular physiology in S. Typhimurium.

Materials and Methods

Bacterial Strains, Reagents and Growth Conditions

The Knockout strain of DNA-binding proteins (CbpA and CbpB) was constructed in wild-type strain of Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhimurium strain LT2 (MTCC 98). Luria broth and M9 medium (HiMedia, India) were used for all the growth-related experiments. The growth curves were performed at 37 °C at 150 rpm. LB agar plates supplemented with kanamycin (50 µg/mL) or carbenicillin (100 µg/mL) (HiMedia, India) were used as per requirement. All the reagents were autoclaved or filter sterilized before use.

Generation of Gene Knockout and Complemented Strains for the ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants

The construction of ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB gene knockouts in Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium was accomplished using the Quick & Easy E. coli Gene Deletion Kit by Red®/ET® Recombination (Gene Bridges, Heidelberg, Germany) as described previously [10, 11]. The homology arms, each 50 base pairs long and matching the sequences adjacent to the chromosomal insertion site, are added to the functional cassette via PCR. The sequence for the functional cassette are provided in the kit [Red®/ET® Recombination, Gene Bridges (Heidelberg, Germany)] and primer sequence used are detailed in the Supplementary table (Table 1S). Then simultaneously, the Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium strain selected for null mutants creation is transformed with the pRedET expression plasmid and selected on ampicillin (100 µg/mL) containing plates and incubated at 30 °C. The S. Typhimurium transformed with pRedET was then used for further experiments. It was allowed to grow at 30 °C in Luria broth (LB) till it reaches an OD of 0.4. To induce expression of the Red/ET recombination genes, l-arabinose was added to a final concentration of 0.3%, and the culture was incubated at 37 °C for 45 min to 1 h. Post-induction, cells were washed three times with ice-cold water to prepare electrocompetent cells. The purified PCR product containing the homology arms was electroporated into these cells using a voltage of 2500 V for 6 ms. To enhance transformation efficiency, the electroporation pulse was administered twice, as suggested by the kit protocol. Following electroporation, cells were immediately supplemented with fresh LB broth and incubated at 37 °C for 3 h to facilitate recombination. Subsequently, cells were centrifuged and plated on LB agar containing kanamycin (50 µg/mL). Only those colonies in which the target gene had been successfully replaced by the FRT-PGK-gb2-neo-FRT cassette grew on the kanamycin-containing plates.

To ensure that the kanamycin cassette is not causing any change in the phenotype of the cell, the pcp20 vector was used for the removal of kanamycin cassette from the genome of knockouts following the method described previously [12]. The knockouts were confirmed using PCR-based methods and sequencing.

The cbpA and cbpB genes in Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium were complemented using pBAD TOPO and Pet-28a plasmid (Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA) respectively. A 300 bp (gene coordinate; 4,842,684—4,843,870) upstream region of the cbpB gene, encompassing its native promoter, was amplified and cloned alongside the cbpB coding sequence and cloned into the pBAD TOPO vector in our laboratory. This recombinant plasmid was then transformed into the ΔcbpB mutant strain.

Similarly, a 500 bp (gene coordinate; 1,198,987–1,200,499); upstream region of the cbpA gene, containing its native promoter, was cloned upstream of the cbpA coding sequence. The resulting construct was introduced into the ΔcbpA mutant strain. Transformants were selected on LB agar plates supplemented with ampicillin (100 µg/mL). The complemented strains are referred to as cbpA* and cbpB* in the subsequent sections. Utilizing native promoters facilitated constitutive expression of the respective genes in the complemented strains. The complemented strain were confirmed by real-time PCR and restoration of phenotypes in both ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants.

Phenotypic Characterization Using SEM

Fresh cultures of wild-type S. Typhimurium, ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants were streaked onto LB agar plates. A single colony from each strain was then mounted onto an aluminum stub using double-sided carbon tape and examined under a scanning electron microscope (SEM) at various magnifications. Imaging was performed in variable pressure mode at 65 Pa and 20 kV. The SEM analysis was conducted using a Quanta 200 SEM (FEI Company, Hillsboro, OR, USA) at the facilities of Icon Analytical Equipment Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India. To rule out colony-to-colony variation, at least three colonies per culture were evaluated and their length and width were measured three times.The cell dimensions are given as mean ± SD.

Growth of S. Typhimurium, ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB Mutants on Different Carbon Sources

The changes in growth profile of S. Typhimurium, ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants was assessed using various carbon sources. Initially, all strains were grown in Luria Broth (LB) for 24 h, then washed three times with 0.85% saline. The cells were subsequently inoculated into 96-well microtiter plates containing either LB (a complex medium) or M9 (a chemically defined minimal medium).

M9 medium is widely used for cultivating E. coli and other bacteria under controlled laboratory conditions due to its defined composition, which reduces experimental variability. The standard M9 formulation includes essential inorganic salts—Na₂HPO₄·7H₂O (64 g), KH₂PO₄ (15 g), NaCl (2.5 g) and NH₄Cl (5.0 g); providing necessary sources of nitrogen and phosphorus. To evaluate carbon source utilization, M9 was supplemented with 0.4% of individual sugars: glucose, glycerol, trehalose, ribose, mannose, fructose, galactose, xylose, sucrose and maltose.

Each strain was inoculated at a 1:10 dilution, and bacterial growth was monitored overnight at 37 °C using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader (Winooski, Vermont, USA). All assays were performed in three independent experiments, each with triplicate measurements. The fold change in growth curve between wild-type and mutants were determined using specific time points (or OD values at that time). Fold change is expressed as mean ± SD.

Growth of S. Typhimurium, ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB Mutants on Different Nitrogen Sources

The growth of S. Typhimurium, ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants was evaluated in the presence of various nitrogen sources. Cultures were initially grown in Luria Broth for 24 h, followed by three washes with 0.85% saline. The cells were then inoculated into 96-well microtiter plates containing media supplemented with 10 mM of individual nitrogen sources including alanine, arginine, histidine, methionine, proline, and leucine. Each culture was diluted at a 1:10 ratio before inoculation. Bacterial growth was monitored overnight at 37 °C using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader. Each assay was repeated three times, with triplicates in each experiment. The fold change in growth curve between wild-type and mutants were determined using specific time points (or OD values at that time). The fold change expressed as mean ± SD.

Motility Assay

The motility of S. Typhimurium wild-type, ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants was evaluated using Motility Test Medium (HiMedia) as described previously [13]. The agar medium was prepared according to standard protocols and 10 µL of each bacterial culture was spotted at the center of the plate. Following 24 h of incubation, the motility was assessed by measuring the diameter expressed as (mean ± SD) of bacterial spread from the point of inoculation. Each assay was conducted in triplicates and repeated three times to ensure consistency and reproducibility.

Response of ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB Mutants to Temperature, Hydrogen Peroxide, Osmotic and Radiation Stress

The response of wild-type and mutants toward different stresses were evaluated by viability assay. For all the stress studies S. Typhimurium strain LT2 was used as positive control and mutant’s survivability was assessed against the wild-type. Cells were inoculated in fresh LB and cells were allowed to grow until they reach an OD of 0.4. The cells were then washed with 0.85% saline. The culture was re-suspended in saline prior to exposure to stress. After the stress treatment, the end point was evaluated by enumeration of surviving bacteria by plate count methods (spread plate). The bacteria that survived the stress were serially diluted using 0.85% saline and then plated on plate count agar (PCA). The CFU/mL was determined using the formula; Number of bacteria X Dilution factor/Volume of dilution plated. The graphs were plotted between Log CFU/mL as Y ordinate and different stress as X abscissa. All the stress conditions were repeated three times in triplicates. For each stress point, the average log CFU/mL was plotted relative to the corresponding condition. The values are represented as mean ± SD.

The response of mutant strains to heat shock was assessed relative to the wild-type by exposing cells to 55 °C for time intervals of 0, 5 and 10 min. The temperature stress study was repeated three times, with triplicates in each experiment. The average log CFU/mL was plotted relative to the time (in minutes). The values are represented as mean ± SD.

Oxidative stress was tested by exposing S. Typhimurium, ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB to different concentration of hydrogen peroxide (0, 10, 20, 30 mM) for 30 min. The oxidative stress study was repeated three times, with triplicates in each experiment. The average log CFU/mL was plotted relative to concentration of hydrogen peroxide (mM). The values are represented as mean ± SD.

Wild type and mutants were also tested for osmotic stress. The exponentially growing cells of S. Typhimurium, ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB were exposed to 15% (2.5 M) salt stress for a period of 72 h. The changes in cell number in response to salt stress were calculated by counting CFU/ml at 0, 1, 24, 48 and 72 h. The osmotic stress study was repeated in triplicate across three independent experiments. The average log CFU/mL was plotted relative to the time (in minutes). The values are represented as mean ± SD.

For evaluating the sensitivity to radiation of wild-type and mutants, the cells of S. Typhimurium, ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB (107 CFU/mL) were taken and exposed to different doses of gamma radiation (100, 200, 300, 400 Gy) in gamma chamber (GC5000, Board of Radioisotope and Technology, Mumbai, India). The dose rate was 0.083 kGy/min. The D10 was calculated by plotting a graph between Log CFU/mL and gamma doses [14]. The radiation sensitivity assay was performed in three independent experiments, each conducted in triplicate. The average log CFU/mL was plotted relative to the dose (Gy). The values are represented as mean ± SD.

Membrane-Disrupting Agents

To evaluate sensitivity to membrane-disrupting agents, wild-type S. Typhimurium and its corresponding ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants were grown overnight in LB medium. Stationary-phase cultures were then serially diluted (10⁸–103 CFU/mL), and 2 µL of each dilution was spotted onto LB agar containing either 2% SDS or 0.5% sodium deoxycholate [15]. Bacillus cereus served as a positive control to verify the activity of the detergents, as Gram-positive bacteria are characteristically susceptible to detergents. Following a 16-h incubation at 37 °C, the plates were digitally photographed to document the results.

For quantitative analysis, wild-type and mutant strains were cultured overnight (37 °C, 150 rpm, LB broth). Following a wash with 0.85% saline, cultures were adjusted to ~ 10⁷ CFU/mL; this initial concentration was confirmed by plating. The cells were then treated with either 2% SDS or 0.5% sodium deoxycholate for 24 h. A Gram negative (E. coli) and a Gram negative (Bacillus cereus) strain were included as negative and positive controls for detergent sensitivity respectively. Viable cell counts were determined by plating after the 24-h exposure. The entire procedure was performed in triplicate in three independent experiments. The graph depicts the average log CFU/mL (Y-axis) as a function of time (X-axis), with values expressed as mean ± SD.

Competition Assay

The co-culture assay was carried out as described previously [16]. Overnight cultures of wild-type S. Typhimurium, ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants were grown in Luria Broth (LB). The ΔcbpA or ΔcbpB mutants (10⁷ CFU/mL) were co-cultured with 10⁷ CFU/mL of the wild-type strain in LB medium to assess competitive survival. The experiment was conducted for a month with readings taken every ten days interval. Mutants survival was quantified by plating on LB agar supplemented with kanamycin, while wild-type survival was determined by subtracting the colony counts on kanamycin-containing plates from the total colony counts on non-selective LB agar. Colony-forming units were enumerated using the plate count method. The experiments were repeated three times independently in triplicates. The wild-type S. Typhimurium and ΔcbpA or ΔcbpB mutants survived were reported as log CFU/mL expressed as mean ± SD.

Long-Term Starvation Studies

S. Typhimurium, ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB were first cultured overnight in Luria Broth. Subsequently, all strains were incubated in LB medium at 37 °C for a duration of six months. Their long-term survival was assessed monthly using the plate count method. Cell counts were determined at one month intervals over a 6-month duration using the plate count technique. All experiments were performed in triplicates and repeated thrice to ensure reproducibility. The graph depicts the average log CFU/mL (Y-axis) as a function of time (X-axis), with values expressed as mean ± SD.

Gene Expression Analysis

The cells of wild-type S. Typhimurium, ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB were grown in Luria broth medium for a period of 24 h. The cells were then pelleted and used for RNA isolation. The RNA was isolated using TRIzol method [17]. The cDNA was synthesized using the TransScript cDNA synthesis kit (TransGen Biotech, China). Using cDNA, real-time expression of the genes involved in metabolism and stress tolerance was evaluated. The real‐time PCR (qPCR) was carried out as described earlier [18]. The genes used were Phosphofructokinase (STM3254), 6-phospho-3-hexuloisomerase (STM2756), Glucose-6-phosphate isomerase (STM2757), PTS system glucose-specific EIICB component (STM2758),5-dehydro-2-deoxygluconokinase (STM4430), PTS sugar transporter subunit IIC (STM3770),2-oxo-3-deoxygalactonate kinase (dgoK), PTS system fructose IIA component (STM3772), fliC, sodC,ynaF,sodA, htrA,rpoH and rpoS. The 16srRNA was used as a reliable internal control due to its stable and constitutive expression across various bacterial growth conditions. Therefore, it was used as a housekeeping gene to normalize the expression levels of target genes, ensuring accurate and consistent quantification in gene expression studies. The experiments were performed triplicates and repeated three times independently. The fold change was considered significant when > 1 for upregulation or < 1 for downregulation. Standard deviation (SD) was used to analyze real-time gene expression PCR results, performed in Microsoft Excel. Significant results were obtained at P < 0.05 by performing two sample t-test analysis. The results were analyzed as per the MIQE guidelines [19] using REST-MCS software [20].

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicates and independently repeated three times to ensure reproducibility. The data set for all the analysis was represented as mean ± SD. All graphs were generated using OriginPro 8.5, and statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test, with a significance threshold set at P value < 0.05 with 95% confidence interval. The RT-PCR normalization was done using REST-MCS software. Real-time gene expression PCR data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel, with standard deviation (SD) calculated to assess variability and statistical significance determined using t-test at P < 0.05.

Results

Construction of ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB Deletion and Complement Strains

The specific functions of the DNA-binding proteins CbpA and CbpB in S. Typhimurium remain unclear. To investigate their roles, this study generated gene knockout mutants for cbpA and cbpB using the Red recombination system [10, 11]. The resulting ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants were confirmed through PCR-based assays (Fig. 1Si, ii) and whole genome sequencing (data not shown), which verified the presence of precise in-frame deletions.

Complementation strains were constructed by cloning the cbpA gene, along with its native 500 bp upstream promoter region, into the pET28a vector and the cbpB gene with a 300 bp native promoter into the pBAD-Topo vector. Cloning was confirmed by restriction digestion and PCR analysis.

Functional complementation was further validated phenotypically: the ΔcbpB strain, complemented with its respective gene, showed a shortened lag phase when grown in M9 medium containing 0.4% maltose (Fig. 1a). Similarly, the complemented ΔcbpA strain exhibited restoration of motility (Fig. 1b).

Size Measurements Using SEM

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was conducted to examine the morphology and size of the wild-type and mutants. Both the wild-type and mutants exhibited a rod-shaped morphology. However, the mutants appeared smaller in size compared to the wild-type. The average length of wild-type cells was 1.924 µm ± 0.18, while the ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants measured 1.74 µm ± 0.2 and 1.39 µm ± 0.24, respectively. This indicates that ΔcbpA cells were approximately 0.8-fold smaller (*P < 0.05) and ΔcbpB cells were 1.3-fold smaller (*P < 0.05 at 95% CI) than the wild-type cells. Representative SEM images are provided in the supplementary material (Fig. 2Si–iii).

In M9 medium supplemented with maltose, the ΔcbpB mutant exhibited a prolonged lag phase of nearly 24 h (Fig. 1a). Interestingly, both mutants demonstrated enhanced growth compared to the wild-type when utilizing mannose, fructose, sucrose, galactose ribose and glycerol (Fig. 3Si). The mutants reached an OD of ~ 1 in 7 h; whereas the time required to achieve similar OD by wild-type was more. The fold change in the growth profile was ~ 1.4 (*P < 0.05 at 95% CI) in M9 medium substituted with ribose, fructose, glycerol, galactose, sucrose and mannose. In contrast, the wild-type strain showed superior growth on xylose (Fig. 3Sii). The wild-type took 7 h to reach an OD of 0.6, whereas mutants took 10 h to reach similar OD in M9 + Xylose; which means mutants were 0.5-fold slower (*P < 0.05 at 95% CI) in growth in comparison to wild-type S. Typhimurium. No significant (P > 0.05) growth differences were observed among the strains in M9 medium supplemented with glucose or trehalose (Fig. 3Sii).

Motility

Motility assays were performed to assess the movement of the ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants. Both mutants were found to be non-motile as indicated by their limited diffusion in soft agar (Fig. 1b). The wild-type S. Typhimurium displayed a motility halo with a diameter of 8.5 ± 0.002 cm, while the ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants showed significantly reduced diffusion zones measuring 1.1 ± 0.001 cm and 1.5 ± 0.004 cm, (*P < 0.05 at 95% CI) respectively. Complementation restored motility to a considerable extent, with the complemented ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB strains showing halo diameters of 8 ± 0.005 cm and 4 cm ± 0.002, (*P < 0.05 at 95% CI) respectively.

Response of the Null Mutants of ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB to Temperature, Hydrogen Peroxide, Radiation and Osmotic Stress

The wild-type S. Typhimurium strain exhibited a 3-log reduction in viable cell count after 5 min at 55 °C and a 4-log reduction after 10 min. In comparison, the ΔcbpA mutant showed 2-log reduction at 5 min and a 5-log reduction at 10 min in cells number. The ΔcbpA mutant was most sensitive to the heat stress; showing significant five log reduction in number (*P < 0.05 at 95% CI) after 10 min as detected using One-Way Anova followed by Tukey’s test at 95% confidence interval. The ΔcbpB mutant was the most heat-tolerant, displaying only a 1-log (*P < 0.05 at 95% CI) reduction at 5 min and a 2-log reduction at 10 min (*P < 0.05 at 95% CI) (Fig. 2a).

When sensitivity to oxidative stress was assessed, both wild-type and mutant strains showed a one‐log reduction in cell number; Tukey’s test revealed no significant difference between them after oxidative stress exposure (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2b).

The D10 value, indicating the radiation dose required to reduce the viable cell population by one log, was 113 Gy for the wild-type strain, 140 Gy for the ΔcbpA mutant and 104 Gy for the ΔcbpB mutant. These results suggest that the ΔcbpA mutant demonstrates the highest resistance to gamma radiation at (*P < 0.05) and 95% CI (Fig. 3a). The ΔcbpA mutant was 1.2-fold more (*P < 0.05 at 95% CI) resistant then wild-type; whereas ΔcbpB was 0.9-fold less sensitive (*P < 0.05 at 95% CI) to radiation stress. The ΔcbpA mutant was 1.3-fold more radiation resistant (*P < 0.05 at 95% CI) then ΔcbpB mutant. Under osmotic stress conditions (15% NaCl; 2.5 M), after 72 h, both ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants showed a fourfold reduction in cell number, whereas the wild-type strain exhibited a fivefold reduction. This indicates that the mutants possess 1.2-fold (*P < 0.05 at 95% CI) greater resistance to salt-induced stress compared to the wild-type S. Typhimurium at 95% CI (Fig. 3b).

Stress Studies Against Membrane-Disrupting Compounds

The sensitivity of the ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants to membrane-disrupting agents was assessed using 2% SDS and 0.5% deoxycholic acid. Both mutants exhibited increased sensitivity to these compounds compared to the wild-type S. Typhimurium (Fig. 5Si). To quantify this effect, the wild-type and mutant strains were exposed to the membrane-disrupting agents, and viable cell counts were determined at 0 and 24 h using plate count methods. The results revealed a threefold (*P < 0.05 at 95% CI) reduction in cell number in the mutants following treatment, indicating a compromised ability to withstand membrane-disrupting agents (Fig. 5Sii, iii).The statistical significance was confirmed by One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test at P < 0.05 at 95% confidence interval.

Competition Studies and Long-Term Starvation Studies

Both ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants exhibited reduced fitness compared to the wild-type S. Typhimurium when subjected to competitive growth. During co-culture with the wild-type, the mutant population declined steadily. In first 10 days, wild-type S. Typhimurium reduced to (1 ± 0.25) × 106 CFU/mL (*P < 0.05, 95% CI); whereas ΔcbpA reduced to (2 ± 0.45) × 105 CFU/mL (*P < 0.05, 95% CI) and ΔcbpB reached (1.6 ± 0.45) × 105 CFU/mL (*P < 0.05, 95% CI); in next 20 days ΔcbpA reached (1 ± 0.3) × 103 CFU/mL (*P < 0.05, 95% CI) and ΔcbpB reached (1.36 ± 0.3) × 103 CFU/mL (*P < 0.05, 95% CI) whereas wild-type S.Typhimurium still maintained at (1 ± 0.55) × 106 CFU/mL (*P < 0.05, 95% CI) and after one month, both the mutants were completely outcompeted [ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants number reached to 0 (*P < 0.05, 95% CI) compared to wild-type in a months’ time, *P < 0.05, 95% CI]. However, when grown independently in Luria broth, both the wild-type and mutant strains remained viable for up to 6 months, showing only a two-log reduction in cell numbers (*P < 0.05 at 95% CI) over this period (Fig. 6S).

Gene Expression Analysis

Gene expression analysis revealed that both ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants showed upregulation of genes associated with metabolism (STM3254, STM2756) and changes in gene expression related to stress survival (rpoS, htrA); in ΔcbpA mutant there is upregulation of rpoS gene and downregulation of htrA gene; whereas upregulation of both htrA and rpoS gene in ΔcbpB mutant. The expression of genes related to oxidative stress response (sodC) and motility (fliC) were downregulated in both the mutants (Fig. 4a, b). The fold change of > 1 or < 1 was taken as significant (*P < 0.05 at 95% CI) for evaluation of upregulated or downregulated genes. These findings suggest that the nucleoid-associated proteins CbpA and CbpB play roles in regulating cellular stress tolerance and metabolic processes. However, the precise mechanisms underlying this regulation remain to be elucidated.

Discussion

Curved DNA plays a vital role in several cellular processes, including transcription initiation, termination, recombination and DNA replication [9]. These DNA regions are typically characterized by the periodic arrangement of short A/T-rich sequences aligned with the helical repeat of the DNA molecule and are often found upstream of promoter regions [9]. This structural feature suggests that nucleoid-associated proteins (NAPs) may bind to these regions to regulate various physiological functions [21]. Among NAPs, histone-like nucleoid structuring protein (H-NS) is one of the most thoroughly investigated in Escherichia coli and is known to regulate more than 60 genes which are involved in metabolic pathways [22]. The capacity of H-NS to interact with curved DNA and impact diverse biological pathways implies that other CBPs may also regulate unique aspects of cellular behavior.

This study focused on two poorly characterized curved DNA-binding proteins, CbpA and CbpB (also referred to as right origin binding protein, RBR). Despite their association with nucleoid architecture, these proteins are non-essential for viability, as evidenced by the survival of null mutants lacking either gene. To date, comprehensive phenotypic analyses of these genes remain limited. Named for their affinity toward curved DNA, CbpA and CbpB have been identified in several members of the Enterobacteriaceae family, including Escherichia coli, Salmonella, Shigella, Pseudomonas and Klebsiella as well as in other bacterial families such as Mycobacteriaceae and Staphylococcaceae. Phylogenetic analysis and Clustal alignment highlighting the evolutionary relatedness of these proteins are provided in the supplementary data (Fig. 7S). The widespread distribution of CbpA and CbpB homologs suggests that they may have broad functional relevance. Our findings indicate that these proteins may play important roles in cellular adaptation to environmental stresses and in nutrient utilization.

Specifically, mutants lacking cbpA or cbpB exhibited enhanced utilization of sugars such as ribose, mannose, fructose, galactose, glycerol and sucrose compared to the wild-type S. Typhimurium. Conversely, the wild-type strain outperformed the mutants in xylose metabolism (Table 1). Interestingly, the ΔcbpB mutant demonstrated a prolonged lag phase of over 20 h in M9 medium supplemented with 0.4% maltose (Fig. 1a). Real-time gene expression analysis revealed a three- to fourfold upregulation (*P < 0.05 at 95% CI) of several metabolic genes including phosphofructokinase (STM3254), phosphor transferase IIC component (STM2758), sugar kinase (STM4430) and 6-phospho-3-hexuloisomerase (STM2756) which may explain the observed differences in metabolic efficiency. The phosphofructo kinase perform committed rate limiting step and hence is essential for functioning of pathways utilizing mannose, fructose, sucrose and galactose as carbon source; therefore, mutants may be showing differential growth in these carbon sources. 6-Phospho-3-hexuloisomerase is an enzyme that works in certain bacterial sugar assimilation pathways, especially the Entner–Doudoroff (ED) pathway. 6-Phospho-3-hexuloisomerase catalyzes the isomerization between 6-phospho-3-hexulose and 6-phosphogluconate (6-PG). This step connects sugar metabolism of substrates like mannose, fructose or glycerol derivatives into the central ED pathway. The phosphotransferase system (PTS) is a major carbohydrate transport system in bacteria. It couples sugar uptake with phosphorylation using phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) as the phosphate donor. A sugar kinase is an enzyme that catalyzes the phosphorylation of a sugar molecule, usually at the hydroxyl group of the first (C-1) or sixth (C-6) carbon, using ATP (or sometimes another nucleoside triphosphate) as the phosphate donor. The significant changes (*P < 0.05 at 95% CI) in these proteins/enzymes may be helping the mutants to use different carbon sources efficiently and expands metabolic flexibility beyond glucose. Similar metabolic regulation by NAPs has been documented in E. coli; altered levels of Pck, AceF and PfkA, Pgk, GlpK and atoA were reported in H-NS mutant of E.coli; another study showed that H-NS mutant of E.coli was not able to use 42 carbon an 19 nitrogen source [23, 24]. Similarly, IHF mutant of Pseudomonas putida had altered levels of triose phosphate isomerase, which is best known as glycolytic enzyme that interconvers the 3 carbon sugars dihydroxyacetone phosphate [25]. The ccr1 like nucleoid associate proteins in Streptomyces has been shown to affect secondary metabolism [26]; all these highlights toward the conserved nature and importance of NAPs in regulating metabolism.

Beyond metabolic changes, the mutants also exhibited pronounced defects in motility. Both ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB strains were non-motile, which correlated with a significant twofold downregulation (*P < 0.05) of the fliC gene. Similar motility impairments due to the loss of nucleoid-associated proteins (NAPs) have been reported in other organisms. The HU mutants of Cytophaga hutchinsonii, H-NS and Fis mutants of Erwinia chrysanthemi [27–30] and H-NS mutant (Rv3852) of Mycobacterium tuberculosis [30] highlighting a potentially conserved role of NAPs in the regulation of bacterial motility.

A reduction in cell size was also observed in both ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants. Similar morphological changes have been reported in Porphyromonas gingivalis, where deletion of the huβ gene affected cell division and iron acquisition pathways [31]. Furthermore, the increased sensitivity of the mutants to membrane-disrupting agents suggests a possible role for CBPs in the regulation of fatty acid synthesis and membrane biogenesis. A previous study has shown that the fatty acid synthase were enriched in nucleoid region, which points toward relationship between nucleoid and cell membrane [32].

Under stress conditions, mutants lacking curved DNA-binding proteins exhibited distinct response patterns. The ΔcbpA mutant showed greater resistance to gamma radiation but was more sensitive to heat stress compared to the ΔcbpB mutant (*P < 0.05). Both mutants displayed enhanced resistance to osmotic stress; however, no significant differences were observed in oxidative stress tolerance relative to the wild-type strain. Real-time gene expression analysis indicated altered expression of key stress-related genes including rpoS, htrA, sod and rpoH suggesting that CBPs play a role in modulating the cellular stress response. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the involvement of nucleoid-associated proteins such as HU, H-NS, and Dps in promoting bacterial stress adaptation. Dps protein protect the cells from oxidative stress, gamma radiation and UV radiation by binding to the nucleic acid and compacting the genome in such a way that its not affected by the harmful environmental conditions. Similarly, HU was shown to respond to anaerobiosis, acid stress and high osmolarity and H-NS mutants were showing high sensitivity to low pH and high osmolarity [33–35]. The wild-type S. Typhimurium contains several genes that may have functions similar or redundant to those tested in the mutants, potentially aiding their survival under stress conditions. This redundancy could explain the mutants’ ability to cope with stress despite the lack of significant changes in the expression of certain genes analyzed by RT-PCR. The principal mechanism of stress protection is likely similar to that of Dps: condensing the DNA so that external environmental stressors cannot damage the genome, which needs further validation by other experiments.

RT-PCR analysis revealed differential htrA gene expression in the ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants. The ΔcbpA strain showed reduced htrA expression, which correlated with its increased sensitivity to heat stress. The htrA gene encodes a periplasmic serine protease critical for protein quality control under stress. Reduced htrA expression is associated with impaired survival at elevated temperatures, while its overexpression enhances thermotolerance. Similar roles of htrA in heat stress adaptation have been reported in Escherichia coli [36].In contrast, the ΔcbpB mutant exhibited elevated htrA levels, suggesting a compensatory mechanism that may enhance thermotolerance. These findings indicate a potential regulatory role for cbpA in modulating htrA expression and consequently, the organism’s heat stress response. The importance of htrA in stress resistance is well established across multiple microorganisms. In S. Typhimurium, both htrA and dnaK contribute to thermotolerance [37]. Similarly, htrA plays a key role in stress adaptation and virulence in Haemophilus parasuis [38]. In Listeria monocytogenes, htrA mutants exhibit impaired growth under sublethal heat stress, further highlighting its significance in cellular stress responses [39].

Additionally, both ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants exhibited upregulation of the rpoS gene. In Escherichia coli, RpoS functions as a key sigma factor orchestrating the general stress response by regulating a wide array of genes that promote survival under adverse conditions. Elevated rpoS expression enhances survival under multiple stresses, including osmotic, oxidative, and acid stress. Its central role in stress adaptation has been well documented in Escherichia coli [40].

Phenotypically, the ΔcbpA and ΔcbpB mutants exhibited several similarities. Both strains demonstrated impaired maltose utilization and slower growth in xylose compared to the wild-type. Conversely, enhanced growth was observed in ribose, mannose, fructose, galactose, glycerol and sucrose. Notably, despite their similarities, the mutants displayed significant differences in stress resistance (Figs. 2, 3), which is the central observation of this study.

These findings suggest that CbpA and CbpB are both essential for proper regulation of maltose and xylose metabolism, as the absence of either protein compromises this process. Interestingly, the deletion of a single CBP gene appears to improve the utilization of certain sugars, indicating a possible de-repression of metabolic pathways. Both mutants shared phenotypes such as increased salt tolerance, loss of motility, changes in carbon and nitrogen metabolism and heightened sensitivity to membrane-disrupting agents. However, their responses to heat and gamma radiation stress differed markedly (Tables 2, 3), indicating functional divergence.

This combination of shared and distinct phenotypes suggests that many cellular functions are co-regulated by CbpA and CbpB with maltose metabolism being the most prominent example of synergistic regulation. In contrast, the improved utilization of other sugars in the absence of either protein hints at complex, possibly opposing regulatory influences. While both proteins bind curved DNA, they differ in sequence, structure, and genomic context (Supplementary Table S2), pointing to distinct mechanisms of action.

In the current study, we established correlations between CBP deletion and phenotypes but could not directly link the genes to specific pathways, as the effects of CbpA and CbpB are global in nature. A detailed analysis of individual phenotypes and their underlying physiology is warranted to fully define their regulatory roles. Even previous work has relied solely on proteome- and transcriptome-based approaches when characterizing the global regulatory roles of these proteins [23, 24].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that CbpA and CbpB contribute both overlapping and unique functions in regulating metabolism, motility and stress response. Their deletion disrupts a fine-tuned balance of gene activation and repression. This study does not explore the precise mechanisms by which curved DNA-binding proteins influence various stress responses and metabolic regulations. Therefore, to validate the proposed relationships between gene expression changes and the phenotypes observed, further mechanistic research will be essential. The authors, however, hypothesize that the regulation of different pathways may occur through the binding of these DNA-binding proteins to the promoter regions, thereby modulating their function, which is difficult to study as these proteins interact with DNA in non-specific manner. Further studies, including detailed stoichiometric analyses and interaction profiling with other nucleoid-associated proteins are needed to unravel their precise roles in global gene regulation.
