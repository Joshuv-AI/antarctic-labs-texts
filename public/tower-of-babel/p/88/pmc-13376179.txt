Liver Cancer in Methylmalonic and Propionic Acidemias: A Rare Complication? A Clinico‐Pathological Study of 24 Livers
Authors: Léa Zloty, Mouna Aoun, Carmen Capito, Anaïs Brassier, Guillaume Morcrette, Ugo Cucinotta, Laurène Dehoux, Claire Mayer, Jean‐Paul Duong Van Huynh, Apolline Imbard, Jean‐François Benoist, Christophe Chardot, Manuel Schiff, Pascale de Lonlay, Monique Fabre, Florence Lacaille
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn methylmalonic (MMA) and propionic acidemias (PA), liver or liver‐kidney transplantation (Tx) is indicated for metabolic decompensations, kidney failure (MMA), and to improve quality of life. Liver cancer was reported in five patients with MMA. We characterized the pathology of 23 explanted livers and one cancer to investigate for pre‐cancerous changes. We included seven patients with PA, 16 with MMA, and a patient with cancer after kidney Tx for MMA. Liver function tests, alpha‐foetoprotein, and liver ultrasound were collected. Routine and special stains were performed. Abnormalities were observed in liver tests or ultrasound in half of the patients. Two had cirrhosis (one MMA, one PA). The maximum alpha‐foetoprotein was 28 ng/mL. The key lesion was clusters and nodules of clear cells in 83%: distended hepatocytes with central nuclei, sharply demarcated from the parenchyma, in the periportal area. These cells contained less glycogen than the surrounding liver; macro‐vacuolar steatosis was observed in 20%. Fibrosis was present in all but two, mostly stage 1 (67%), and mild lymphocytic inflammation in the portal tracts. Large‐cell dysplasia was observed in the three oldest patients (one PA, two MMA). The phenotype of the clusters and nodules highlighted mitochondrial and LFABP loss. Abnormal labelling of glutamine synthetase was seen at distance from the nodules. The liver cancer was a hepatocellular carcinoma. Liver abnormalities were observed in all patients. The clusters and nodules of clear cells likely originate from propionyl‐CoA accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction. This abnormal pathology pleads for early liver Tx. Regular liver monitoring is recommended with alpha‐foetoprotein and ultrasound.Keywords: clear cell cluster and nodule, hepatocellular carcinoma, liver cancer, liver transplantation, methylmalonic acidemia, organic aciduria, propionic acidemia

Introduction

Methylmalonic (MMA) and propionic acidemias (PA) are rare inherited disorders of protein metabolism [1]. Both disorders lead to long‐term neurocognitive dysfunction, renal failure (MMA), and cardiomyopathy (PA). Significant liver disease usually is absent, but laboratory, ultrasound, and pathological abnormalities are found [2].

Secondary mitochondrial dysfunction, due to toxic metabolites and mitochondrial energy deficiency [3, 4, 5, 6, 7], is probably key to long‐term complications, as demonstrated in a mouse model of kidney disease [8].

Although the defect is ubiquitous, the rationale for liver transplantation (Tx) is to provide a large amount of normal “buffering” enzymes, reducing decompensations and organ damage [9, 10]. When renal failure develops in MMA, combined liver and kidney Tx is indicated. Early isolated liver Tx may delay renal failure [11, 12]. In PA, the cardiomyopathy variably evolves after Tx [13, 14, 15, 16].

We described in 2008 the first liver cancer in MMA [17]. With the hypothesis that mitochondrial dysfunction could induce pre‐cancerous lesions, we re‐examined this tumour, together with 23 native livers explanted for Tx. The identification of dysplasia could support the recommendation for early liver Tx in organic acidemias.

Patients, Material, and Methods

This retrospective observational study recorded the liver laboratory parameters, imaging, and pathology from 23 children who received a liver Tx for PA and MMA in our institution over 25 years (1993–2019). Twelve patients underwent an isolated liver Tx (seven with PA and five with MMA), and 11 received a liver‐kidney Tx for MMA.

The liver biopsy and necropsy from the boy who died of liver cancer after kidney Tx for MMA [17] were also re‐analyzed (Patient P24).

Our study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. In accordance with French legislation, patients or their legal representatives were informed during routine consultations that their medical data might be used for research purposes; lack of opposition was systematically recorded. All data were anonymized and stored securely in a password‐protected database, complying with French and European data protection regulations.

General Data

We recorded demographics, pre‐Tx complications, age at Tx, liver function tests prior to Tx (especially episodes of abnormal liver tests), serum alpha‐foetoprotein (AFP) levels (highest and median), episodes of elevated ammonia or lactate, abnormalities in liver imaging (ultrasound—US), and in patients with MMA, the mean level of urinary methylmalonic acid (MMAc) in the year before Tx, as a marker of metabolic control if there was no renal dysfunction: good metabolic control was defined as MMAc urinary excretion less than 5000 μmol/mmol creatinine. For patients with renal dysfunction, plasma MMAc concentration was used instead, as urinary MMAc is reduced and not suitable for follow up; good control was defined as plasma MMAc less than 500 μmol/L [1, 18].

Pathological Analysis

1. Routine staining was performed on 10 samples of formalin fixed tissue per native liver. Two pathologists (MA, MF) and a paediatrician (LZ) reviewed the slides. The preneoplastic and neoplastic liver lesions were reviewed by two paediatric liver oncopathologists (GM, MF). They were stained with HES (hematoxylin–eosin‐saffron) and the specific liver stains: Masson's Trichrome, PicroSirius, Gordon‐Sweet reticulin, Perls, periodic acid‐Schiff (PAS) and PAS diastase.

In the lobules, we recorded the presence of hepatocyte injury including necrosis, ballooning, steatosis (micro‐ or macro‐vacuolar), and inflammation. The type and intensity of steatosis were quantified according to the NASH CRN activity scoring system (three grades, less than 33%, 33%–66%, more than 66% of hepatocytes) [19]. Inflammation was scored in three grades (mild I1, moderate I2, severe I3), and according to its location (portal, lobular, mixed) and composition (lymphocytic, macrophagic, mixed if associated to polynuclear cell infiltrate). Iron in hepatocytes, Kupffer cells, and portal macrophages was scored according to the Deugnier scheme in four grades [20]. The presence of glycogen was analysed using PAS and amylase.

Hepatocyte changes including dysplastic foci were classified according to the International Working Party classification of dysplasia [21]. Large‐cell dysplasia was defined as a group of hepatocytes with cellular and nuclear enlargement, nuclear pleomorphism and multinucleation, anisonucleosis, prominent nucleoli, and preserved nucleo‐cytoplasmic ratio. Small cell change was defined as hepatocytes showing decreased volume, increased nucleo‐cytoplasmic ratio, mild nuclear pleomorphism, hyperchromasia and cytoplasmic basophilia. We analysed architecture including pseudo‐glandular or acinar formation and thickened trabeculae (up to three cells thick) in lobules, clusters or nodules with the loss, maintenance, or accentuation of the reticulin framework within these lesions, the distribution of portal tracts, the presence of aberrant unpaired arterioles and capillarization of sinusoids.

Portal fibrosis, interportal fibrosis, and cirrhosis were quantified according to the METAVIR scoring system in four stages. PicroSirius red staining was used to distinguish between stage F2 (few fibrous bridges, limited area of fibrosis) and F3 stage (numerous fibrous bridges with an increased area of fibrosis).

2. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) study. Two representative formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded blocks from each case were selected. To characterise clear clusters and nodules as well as pre‐cancerous lesions, we used antibodies directed against the HepPar1 (Hepatocyte Paraffin 1), AFP, Glypican‐3, β‐catenin, Glutamine synthetase (GS), Liver Fatty Acid Binding Protein (LFABP), and C‐reactive Protein (CRP).

The distribution of mitochondria in the cytoplasm of hepatocytes was estimated using the marker HepPar1 antibody directed against carbamoyl‐phosphate synthetase I (CPS1), located in the mitochondrial matrix. The typical pattern is granular cytoplasmic staining due to mitochondrial binding. It recognizes normal liver tissue and both benign and malignant liver‐derived tissues including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [22].

Testing for expression of AFP and Glypican‐3 in normal or dysplastic hepatocytes was first performed. AFP is expressed focally in hepatoblastoma and HCC [23]. Glypican‐3 is a marker of malignancy [24].

Glutamine synthetase (GS), responsible for scavenging ammonium, is normally expressed as a rim of 2–3 layers of strongly positive hepatocytes around the hepatic veins [25]. GS, targeted by β‐catenin, is a useful marker to point out the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway activation in the differential diagnosis of liver tumours [26, 27].

Liver Fatty Acid Binding Protein (LFABP) plays a crucial role in the intracellular transport of fatty acids in the liver. Its normal expression in the cytoplasm can be lost in HCC and in adenomas [28].

To test for progenitor cell hyperplasia, we used anti‐EpCAM and anti‐cytokeratins 7 (CK7) and 19 (CK19) antibodies. EpCAM is a cell membrane glycoprotein expressed on healthy epithelia and in various carcinomas, and in the liver on hepatic progenitors. CK7 is expressed in ductal cells, and CK19 in both bile ducts and ductules. Cells of bile ductules are postulated to be hepatic progenitor stem cells and metaplastic hepatocytes [29].

Finally, to study microvascular abnormalities, we analysed the expression of anti‐CD34, anti‐actin, anti‐H‐caldesmon and D2‐40 antibodies. CD34 is an intercellular adhesion glycoprotein, expressed when the distortion of the normal microcirculation results in capillarization of sinusoids. Actin, alpha‐smooth muscle‐type, is present in myofibroblasts. H‐caldesmon is found in smooth muscle (media of arteries) and not in myofibroblasts (media of veins). D2‐40 recognizes podoplanin on lymphatic membranes, differentiating them from blood capillaries.

Nine patients (56%) had moderate liver test abnormalities (transaminases or GGT), 15 (94%) had regularly elevated ammonia and lactate levels. The mean AFP level was 11 ng/mL (N < 10). Six children had abnormal levels during follow‐up, with a maximum of 34 ng/mL in P17. There was no correlation between AFP levels and age at Tx, neither in MMA nor in PA. On US, more than half of the patients had a liver span exceeding the 95th percentile, with seven patients having hyperechogenicity, one a heterogeneous liver, and no nodules observed.

The mean pre‐Tx urinary MMAc level for patients without renal failure was 6600 μmol/mmol creatinine. For patients with renal failure, the mean pre‐Tx plasma MMAc was 1350 μmol/L.

The indication for liver‐kidney Tx was renal failure and metabolic control in the first patients (P13–P23). Isolated liver Tx was performed in patients with moderate renal failure to slow down the worsening (P8, P11), and in the others to improve quality of life, support development, and prevent or delay renal failure (P9, P10, P12).

Histological Lesions and Main Phenotypes

The livers appeared enlarged, with a smooth surface, of pale‐beige colour.

In most cases, clear hepatocytes were found in light clusters or nodules. Clusters were aggregates of distended hepatocytes with central nuclei and enlarged cytoplasm that was mostly free of vacuoles. They were sharply demarcated from the surrounding parenchyma and were in contact with the portal tracts. The nodules were also clearly defined and involved several lobules (Figure 1). In livers without nodules or clusters, isolated hepatocyte clarification was scattered throughout the lobules. Clusters or nodules were found in 19/23 livers (83%), clusters in 5/7 PA and 13/16 MMA, nodules in 2/7 PA and 7/16 MMA. The size of the nodules correlated with the age at Tx: the median age of the 6 patients with more than 10 nodules (more than 100 in P11 and P16) was 14, for 6.5 in the others. All but one of these clusters or nodules had a reduced glycogen content. Steatosis was present in 1/7 patients with PA and 4/16 with MMA, the oldest ones. It was predominantly macrovacuolar, in MMA grade 1 in 3 patients and grade 3 (with also microvesicular steatosis) in the oldest one (P13), grade 2 in P7 with PA. After Gordon‐Sweet silver staining, the reticulin network was reduced in clear nodules.

Nodule of light cells molded in contact with a portal space (HES ×16).

These clusters/nodules were best identified using HepPar1. Of the 18 patients with clear clusters or nodules, 72% showed a decrease in cytoplasmic expression (Figure 2). Expression of LFAB was decreased in 7/18 cases. A few cells, at the edge of clusters or nodules, were also stained with C‐reactive protein (CRP) in six livers (three each with PA and MMA). They were associated with either F3/F4 inflammatory fibrosis or severe steatosis. No expression of Glypican‐3, AFP, GS, CK7, nor CD34 was present.

Decrease and focal loss of granular cytoplasmic staining within a nodule due to mitochondrial binding with the marker HepPar1 antibody (“Hepatocyte Paraffin 1”), directed against carbamylphosphate synthetase I (CPS1), an enzyme located in the mitochondrial matrix of hepatocytes (HepPar1, ×14).

Dysplastic small foci with large cell changes were incidental microscopic lesions affecting less than 1% of hepatocytes. The nuclei were hyperchromatic and pleomorphic, often multinucleated (Figure 3), with a normal nucleo‐cytoplasmic ratio. Neither small cell changes nor pseudoglandular or acinar formation was observed. The cells were not stained with AFP nor Glypican‐3, and there was no loss nor overexpression of GS. All three patients with large cell changes underwent Tx as teenagers (P7, P13, P16). P13 and P16 had received GH. In P7's liver, there was mild fibrosis (F1) and inflammation (I1), grade 2 macrovacuolar steatosis, as well as more than 10 clear nodules. P13 had cirrhosis with mild inflammation (I1) and clear cells. P16 had fibrosis (F2), inflammation (I1), and severe microvacuolar steatosis (G3). No abnormal expression of AFP, Glypican‐3, β‐catenin, GS was found.

Nest of dispersed, large dysplastic cells with hyperchromasia, bi‐ or tri‐nucleation and preservation of the nucleo‐cytoplasmic ratio in a patient with propionic acidemia. (HES ×32).

Architectural anomalies were always seen. The lobules were clearly accentuated by distinct interlobular connective tissue, giving the liver a “pig‐like” appearance. Portal or septal fibrosis was present in most cases (87%), with thin septa. Severe fibrosis (F3) or cirrhosis was present in two patients with PA (P1, P4) and three with MMA (P13, P17, P23) (Figure 4). Thin bridging fibrosis or fibrous bands subdividing the liver into regenerative nodules were highlighted on the reticulin and trichrome stains.

Cirrhosis, with moderate portal inflammation and without dysplastic foci of hepatocytes, in a patient with MMA (HES ×25).

The inflammation was only portal and lymphocytic, mostly grade 1, with no lobular activity, Mallory bodies, nor lipogranuloma. Iron deposits were only present in six patients with MMA, five with kidney failure and anaemia.

Apart from dysplasia, no expression of AFP and Glypican‐3 was observed. The membranous β‐catenin expression was normal in 70% of cases. In eight cases (three with PA and five with MMA), there was mild patchwork overexpression in the perivenous area.

GS expression always was abnormal, extending over 5–6 rows of centrilobular hepatocytes. In four patients with PA and 10 with MMA, the staining was more extensive, with a map‐like pattern. In cirrhotic nodules, the expression was lower.

Apart from clear clusters/nodules, the expression of LFABP showed in six cases (two with PA) a declining portal‐to‐central gradient resulting in an attenuation around the centrilobular veins. The cytoplasmic expression was diffuse, low to moderate in other cases.

Hyperplasia of progenitor cells stained with EpCAM and CK7 was evident alongside the architectural abnormalities. The interlobular bile ducts were normal.

Most (91%) patients had mild abnormalities of portal microcirculation with capillary hyperplasia (mild to moderate), sometimes associated with lymphatic dilation in the portal space and septa. In livers with F2 or F3 fibrosis and I1 or I2 inflammation, CRP expression was focal along the limiting plate of portal spaces.

Liver Cancer

This already reported patient (P24) with MMA underwent kidney Tx for renal failure and poor metabolic control (mean plasma MMAc 470 μmol/L) at 9.7 years of age [17]. He had received GH for 10 months. The liver cancer was diagnosed 15 months after Tx on fatigue, hepatomegaly, and AFP 73 ng/mL. The CT scan revealed a huge, necrotic 20 cm × 10 cm × 7.5 cm liver mass replacing most of the right lobe and involving the left lobe. He underwent a biopsy, with a diagnosis of Hepatocytic Malignant Tumour‐Not Otherwise Specified (THM‐NOS) (mixed hepatoblastoma and HCC). As the tumour was unresectable, the boy underwent one cycle of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, but died of tumour progression and Leigh syndrome. The pathology (biopsy and necropsy) was re‐examined 18 years later. The non‐tumoral liver (necropsy) was largely destroyed; in well‐preserved areas, there were a few clear clusters or nodules (less than 10 per slide), rare dysplastic foci (large and binucleated) (Figure 5), grade 1 micro‐ and macro‐vacuolar steatosis, variable glycogen load, no iron deposits, mild inflammation (I1) and fibrosis (F1, focally F2).

Non tumoral liver in the patient with MMA and cancer: Light nodule with focal steatosis, large cell foci of dysplastic cells with hyperchromatic nuclei and preserved nucleocytoplasmic ratio (black arrow) (HES).

The biopsy showed large areas of tumour necrosis, and trabeculae or sheets of eosinophilic hepatoid cells with hyperchromatic central nuclei and granular cytoplasm, bordered with endothelial cells. No steatotic cells, clear cells, nor osteoid or chondroid mesenchymal contingents were seen. At necropsy, the tumour was enclosed by a thick capsule, and the proliferation was mostly of epithelial hepatoid cells. Thick trabeculae were separated by a fibrous stroma. The cells were hepatocytic, polygonal, with clear limits and sometimes highly stained eosinophilic, oncocytic cytoplasm. There were giant polygonal multinucleated cells, with hyperchromatic nuclei, in acini and macro‐trabecular patterns, without steatosis (Figure 6). The nuclei were enlarged, with a very large nucleolus, a highly contrasted chromatin, and frequent mitosis. A minor component was made of small basophilic cells with few cytoplasm. Neither foetal, embryonic nor osteoid, chondroid, squamous tissue was present. The peri‐tumoral liver was atrophic.

Hepatocellular carcinoma, poorly differentiated: Proliferation of hepatoid cells with anisocytosis, anisonucleosis, large hyperchromatic pleomorphic nuclei cells, several giant cells, acinar structures, thick trabeculae separated by sinusoidal cells. (HES ×40).

Immunostaining showed a membranous immunoreactivity of β‐catenin, without nuclear or cytoplasmic labelling, against a diagnosis of hepatoblastoma. The expression of GS was mild, focal, present in only 20% of cells. CD34 highlighted the capillarization of endothelial cells at the edge of tumour trabeculae. IHC showed intense staining of HepPar1 in 80% of cells confirming their hepatocytic origin, variable cytoplasmic expression of LFABP, only high in some trabecular areas, and AFP and Glypican‐3 in 5%. The diagnosis of rhabdoid or fibrolamellar tumour was excluded. The final diagnosis was revised for a poorly differentiated HCC, according to WHO classification.

Discussion

We report for the first time the extensive pathological characterization of 23 livers from patients with organic acidemias, who underwent Tx from early childhood to adolescence. As we previously reported the first cancer in MMA, we specifically explored pre‐malignant lesions.

All livers appeared normal clinically, except for two cases of cirrhosis. Based on negative diagnostic workups, we hypothesize that cirrhosis could develop in organic acidemias, possibly due to secondary mitochondrial dysfunction [3, 4, 5, 6]. Many livers showed some degree of fibrosis, which may represent an earlier stage. Two patients experienced bouts of liver failure similar to Reye syndrome [30]. Liver tests were mildly abnormal in most patients, and AFP levels were often mildly elevated. Although US could be abnormal, no patient had nodules. Liver elastometry, as a surrogate of liver fibrosis, was not available in this study.

In a previous study, we reported laboratory tests and liver imaging in 12 patients with MMA and 16 with PA (none of which included here) [2]. Over 60% of them showed abnormalities, mainly increased GGT (1.5–14 times the upper normal value) or AFP levels, which correlated with the patient's age, in contrast to our findings. Abnormalities were more frequent in those with a severe neonatal presentation.

In our study, liver pathology was consistently abnormal, showing the presence of abnormal light‐coloured hepatocyte clusters and nodules in most cases. They increased in number and size with age. Dispersed light hepatocytes seemed to come first. Such aggregates of clear cells have also been described in disorders of ureagenesis, OTC (ornithine transcarbamylase) and CPS (carbamylphosphate synthetase) deficiencies, and hyperammonemia‐hyperornithinemia‐homocitrullinuria (HHH), sharing the biochemical end‐result of impaired citrulline production in the mitochondria [31]. But the histological pattern is different, associated with glycogen overload and diffuse microvesicular steatosis. In our cases, the pale hepatocytes had reduced glycogen content and macrovacuolar steatosis.

Our study provides strong evidence for mitochondrial alterations [3, 4, 5, 6], identifying abnormally clear hepatocytes with partial loss of Hepar1 signal, a mitochondrial marker. Mitochondrial ultrastructural abnormalities were previously described in a patient with MMA and no overt liver abnormalities: the liver biopsy showed enlarged and swollen mitochondria, dissolution of contents, depolarization, crystal depletion along with functional mitochondrial respiratory chain deficiency [2, 32]. The absence of previous description of these clear clusters or nodules in organic acidemias probably is due to the analysis of needle biopsies only.

Although the mechanism of liver disease in organic acidemias is unknown, mitochondrial energy deficiency might play a role [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Elevated levels of protein propionylation are linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to a reduction in respiration driven by complex I in patient‐derived fibroblasts. Exposure to propionate levels found in patients with PA has experimentally been shown to increase protein propionylation and decrease mitochondrial respiration [33, 34]. Stable isotope analysis and liver perfusion studies have demonstrated that high levels of propionate and its metabolites can inhibit tricarboxylic acid cycle flux and cause a significant decrease in energy production in perfused rat livers [35].

Lobular abnormalities are well explained by phenotypic studies focusing on urea cycle and intracellular fatty acid transport pathways. The GS reaction is responsible for half of the detoxification of ammonia by converting ammonia and glutamate to glutamine. The urea cycle is preferentially localized to the periportal and midzonal hepatocytes; GS is mainly expressed in the perivenous hepatocytes and removes ammonia that escaped the urea cycle [36, 37]. In our patients, excess ammonium clearance is translated into GS overexpression.

The clusters emerging in the periportal zone exhibited low levels of LFABP expression. LFABP is crucial for the intracellular transport and metabolism of fatty acids, including oxidation and energy production, lipid synthesis and secretion, and regulation of gene expression related to lipid metabolism. Dysregulation of LFABP has been implicated in various metabolic disorders, including MASLD (metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease). Immunocytochemical staining for LFABP showed a declining portal‐to‐central hepatocellular gradient [38].

Steatosis has also been observed in animal models [5], and may be specific to the condition or related to the high‐fat, high‐sugar diet. Inflammation also could be specific or secondary to steatosis, like in MASLD [39]. In four liver biopsies from patients with abnormal liver tests, findings included steatosis, fibrosis, and cirrhosis in one case [2]. In our series, five livers showed macrovacuolar steatosis. MASLD is the most common cause of chronic liver disease in children. The main pathogenesis of MASLD‐related HCC is the lipid accumulation in hepatocytes, which causes chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and DNA damage, which contribute to genomic instability. Up to 20%–30% of MASLD‐related HCC cases develop in the absence of overt cirrhosis. HCC cases may develop in children with MASLD with a normal serum AFP level, and AFP elevations may be seen without HCC [40].

There is also an association between organic acidemias and tumorigenesis in patients with L‐2‐hydroxyglutaric aciduria and in somatic perversion of function mutations in IDH1 and IDH2 genes. For other tricarboxylic acid cycle dysfunctions, germline‐inactivating mutations in one allele combined with somatic inactivation of the remaining normal allele in the genes encoding for fumarase or succinate dehydrogenase lead to tumour development. The accumulated organic acids are viewed as onco‐metabolites, and the subsequent tricarboxylic acid cycle flux alteration would promote tumorigenesis [31]. Whether this exists in patients with MMA and PA is suggested here. Mitochondrial dysfunction is most likely involved in the genesis of liver cancer.

AFP or Glypican‐3 (usually present in liver cancers) [21, 22, 23, 24] were not expressed, even if there was dysplasia. Other abnormalities could be pre‐cancerous markers, such as the cytoplasmic β‐catenin, indicating a focal activation of the carcinogenic Wnt/β‐catenin pathway, associated with an overexpression of GS [24]. The decrease in LFABP and CRP expression, observed in clusters and nodules, is usually consistent with adenomas [28, 41].

We did not incidentally find a cancer, but some small foci of large cell dysplasia in three patients. These patients with fibrosis or cirrhosis had mildly abnormal AFP levels, normal or mildly elevated GGT, more than 10 nodules, and they were the oldest at Tx, suggesting that longer exposure to metabolites plays a role. In most liver diseases, the risk of cancer increases with cirrhosis; here, the combination of fibrosis and toxic metabolites may be synergistic. Two of the five patients with MMA who received GH had dysplastic cells. Immunosuppression and GH treatment were additional risk factors in the patient with cancer, who was younger. Novel findings were the extensive characterization of this cancer using a large panel of antibodies. The canonical Wnt pathway is activated in most hepatoblastomas, with a nuclear‐staining pattern. In our case, the staining pattern of β‐catenin was only membranous, supporting the revised diagnosis of HCC.

Four more cases of liver cancer in MMA were reported, one hepatoblastoma, a HMN‐NOS, and 2 HCC (Table 4). The hepatoblastoma, the most frequent liver tumour in young children, could be coincidental in a 19‐month‐old child. The same is true for the second tumour in a 4‐month‐old patient, first reported as hepatoblastoma, and renamed after the finding of HCC foci; however, the age, level of AFP and pathology are suggestive of hepatoblastoma. The two HCC developed in young adults with liver fibrosis or cirrhosis [42, 43], further suggesting that the length of exposure to toxic metabolites is a risk factor. To date, no case of liver cancer was reported in patients with PA, although one of our patients had dysplasia.

We recommend a surveillance algorithm with liver tests, AFP levels, and liver US twice a year. For patients with abnormalities in two consecutive tests, a combination of MRI and contrast US should be considered. Any nodule should be biopsied along with the surrounding liver tissue. In cases of multiple visible nodules or dysplastic lesions, liver Tx should be urgently considered.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that MMA and PA are associated with pathological liver disease, with lobular and architectural abnormalities. The presence of clusters and nodules of clear cells is the key marker of liver disease. Portal or septal fibrosis is present in most cases, more severe in older patients. Large‐cell dysplasia is rare, and may be more severe in older patients, with a risk of cancer, particularly in MMA. With improved diagnostic and treatment, more patients with MMA and PA may become at risk. Liver Tx is usually proposed to improve metabolic control and quality of life; however, since the size of the nodules correlated with the age at Tx, our findings provide additional justification for earlier Tx. The use of livers from patients with MMA and PA for domino Tx is discouraged due to the increased carcinogenic risk associated with immunosuppression, except in very selected cases (age and diagnostic) where the recipient is fully informed.
