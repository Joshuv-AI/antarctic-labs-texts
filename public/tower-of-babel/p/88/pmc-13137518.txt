Critical illness at extreme age: outcomes and clinical characteristics of centenarian ICU admissions in Australia and New Zealand
Authors: Nattaya Raykateeraroj, Laurence Weinberg, Sean Yip, Carl Reyneke, Jacob Zand, Bernice Wang, Jemin Suh, Anoop Koshy, David Pilcher, Dong-Kyu Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe centenarian population is increasing globally, yet data describing intensive care unit (ICU) outcomes in this age group remain limited. We aimed to describe the clinical characteristics and outcomes of centenarians admitted to ICU and to evaluate the performance of commonly used severity-of-illness scores for mortality prediction.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included all index ICU admissions of patients aged ≥100 years recorded in the Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society Adult Patient Database between 2010 and 2024. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included ICU mortality, discharge destination, and survival at 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years. Competing-risk regression was used to assess factors associated with mortality. Discrimination of SOFA, APACHE III, and ANZROD was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC).ResultsAmong 460 centenarians, the median age was 100.9 years (IQR 100.3–102.0), and 67.2% were female. Surgical admissions predominated (67.2%). Treatment limitation orders were documented in 33.9% of patients, whereas invasive mechanical ventilation was used in 10.3%. In-hospital mortality was 13.7%, and ICU mortality was 3.9%, with most deaths occurring early during hospitalisation. Among hospital survivors, 54.1% were discharged home, with a median post-discharge survival of 21.9 months. Acute illness severity was strongly associated with mortality in adjusted competing-risk models, whereas chronological age was not. ANZROD demonstrated superior discrimination for ICU mortality (AUROC 0.90) and hospital mortality (AUROC 0.82) compared with SOFA and APACHE III.ConclusionIn this binational cohort, ICU admission among centenarians was infrequent and occurred in a highly selected population. Treatment limitation orders were common and mechanical ventilation was uncommon; approximately half of hospital survivors were discharged directly home. In-hospital mortality was relatively low and more strongly associated with acute illness severity than with chronological age. Background

Global demographic data demonstrate a marked increase in the centenarian population over recent decades [1], with projections suggesting a rise to 2.5 million by 2050 [2]. This demographic shift is expected to be accompanied by greater tertiary health care utilisation and rates of hospitalisation among very elderly adults owing to increased frailty, multimorbidity, and critical illness [3–5]. Although the provision of critical and intensive care for adults older than age 80 is well characterised [6–10], centenarians (≥100 years) are seldom analysed as a distinct group. Consequently, empirical data to guide decision-making for this population remain limited [11, 12].

Centenarians differ fundamentally from younger elderly patients in biological aging, comorbidity burden, frailty, and physiological reserve, which may modify both short- and long-term outcomes following critical illness [13–15]. Despite limited direct demographic comparisons [11, 16], these differences are likely to shape clinical trajectories, decision-making, and outcomes after intensive care unit (ICU) admission, including discharge disposition and survival [17].

Prognostic uncertainty is further compounded by the limited evaluation of commonly used severity-of-illness scores in this extreme age group. Tools such as Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation III (APACHE III), and Australian and New Zealand Risk of Death (ANZROD) were developed and validated predominantly in younger ICU populations [18–22], and their discriminative performance and clinical utility in patients aged 100 years and older remain unclear.

Accordingly, we aimed to describe the clinical characteristics, management patterns, and outcomes of centenarians admitted to intensive care units, and to examine factors associated with short- and long-term mortality, including the performance of commonly used severity-of-illness scores (SOFA, APACHE III, and ANZROD) in predicting ICU and hospital mortality.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This was a retrospective cohort study using data from the Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society (ANZICS) Adult Patient Database (APD) [23]. The study was approved by the Alfred Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (Project No. 253/24), with a waiver of informed consent due to the retrospective design and use of de-identified data. Analyses commenced following ethics approval and adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [24].

Data source and study population

De-identified data were sourced from the ANZICS APD, a binational clinical quality registry capturing demographics, clinical variables, interventions, severity scores, and outcomes from ICUs in Australia and New Zealand. The study population comprised all index ICU admissions for patients aged ≥100 years between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2024. The ANZICS APD captures formally admitted ICU patients, including both emergency and elective admissions, and does not include ward-based or high-dependency unit admissions. Readmissions and inter-hospital transfers without outcome data were excluded.

Collected variables included demographics (age, sex), admission type (medical or surgical), pre-admission living status, comorbidities, treatment goals at ICU admission (categorised as full active management, treatment limitation order, or palliative care pathway), severity-of-illness measures (SOFA, APACHE III, and ANZROD), Glasgow Coma Scale score (GCS) on admission, ventilation status, ICU and hospital lengths of stay, and discharge outcomes.

ANZROD (Australian and New Zealand Risk of Death) is a regionally calibrated mortality prediction model derived from the ANZICS registry that incorporates diagnostic category, acute physiological variables, and case-mix adjustment to estimate the probability of in-hospital death using data available at ICU admission [22, 25].

Surgical admissions were classified as elective if ICU admission followed a procedure that could be delayed for >24 h, according to ANZICS APD definitions; all others were classified as emergency.

Treatment goals at ICU admission were defined as follows: full active management indicated no documented limitation to life-sustaining therapy at the time of ICU admission; treatment limitation order indicated a documented restriction on specific life-sustaining interventions present at ICU admission; and palliative care pathway indicated admission for comfort-focused care in patients not expected to survive the index hospitalisation. This variable reflects treatment intent at ICU admission and does not capture subsequent changes during the ICU stay.

Outcomes

Primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included ICU mortality, discharge destination (home, nursing home, rehabilitation, transfer, or death), ICU and hospital length of stay, and survival at 30 days, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years.

Prespecified exploratory analyses assessed prognostic factors associated with short- and long-term mortality, time-to-event outcomes, post-discharge survival, and the discriminative performance of commonly used severity-of-illness scores.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were exploratory and conducted using complete-case data in R (version 4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the survival [26], cmprsk [27], rms [28], and pROC packages [29]. Variables with more than 5% missing data were excluded from inferential analyses; variables with 5% or less missingness were analysed without imputation (Table S1). Continuous variables were summarised as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were summarised as counts (percentages) and compared using Fisher’s exact test.

Competing-risk methodology was used, with discharge alive treated as the competing event. Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models were applied to estimate associations between severity scores and the cumulative incidence of death. SOFA was prespecified for ICU mortality and APACHE III for in-hospital mortality, with all models adjusted for age, sex, and admission type (medical vs surgical). Alternative model specifications were examined to address collinearity, and proportionality was assessed using time–covariate interactions. Given substantial missingness in Clinical Frailty Score (CFS) (54.7%), frailty was not included in the primary multivariable models. A prespecified complete-case sensitivity analysis was conducted in patients with available CFS data (frailty-complete subset) to evaluate the association between CFS and mortality using Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models.

Post-discharge survival among hospital survivors was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, with median survival and survival probabilities at 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Survival trajectories were stratified by SOFA score quartiles to explore discrimination across severity strata.

Discrimination for severity-of-illness scores for ICU and hospital mortality was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), with 95% CIs estimated by the DeLong method. Exploratory pairwise comparisons were performed, and bootstrap resampling (2,000 replicates) was used to assess the robustness of discrimination estimates. All severity score evaluations were internally derived within this cohort; external validation in independent centenarian populations is necessary before clinical implementation. Admission sources and discharge destinations were visualised using bar charts and cumulative plots. All tests were two-sided (α = 0.05).

Results

Between 2010 and 2024, 464 centenarian ICU admissions were recorded in the ANZICS APD, representing 0.019% of 2,406,763 total ICU admissions during the same period. Over this time, the estimated centenarian population increased from approximately 3,700 in 2010 to 5,547 in 2021 [30], corresponding to a mean annual ICU admission rate of 5.5 per 1,000 centenarian population across Australia and New Zealand (Figure S1). Of these, 460 admissions were included in the final analysis (Figure S2).

Baseline characteristics

The median age was 100.9 years (IQR 100.3–102.0) and did not differ between survivors and non-survivors. Survivors were more frequently female (69.3% vs 54.0%). Non-survivors had greater acute illness severity, reflected by lower admission GCS scores and higher APACHE III, SOFA, and ANZROD values (Table 1).

Surgical admissions predominated (67.2%), with 54% elective and 46% emergency. Surgical admissions were most commonly orthopaedic, followed by gastrointestinal and trauma-related procedures. Medical admissions were heterogeneous, most frequently rhythm disturbances, trauma, stroke, and sepsis-related conditions (Table S2). Treatment goals differed significantly by survivor status, with full active management more common among survivors and treatment limitation order or palliative care pathways more frequent among non-survivors (p <0.001). Invasive ventilation was used in 10.3% of centenarians overall, with a higher, though not statistically significant, frequency among non-survivors.

ICU mortality

ICU mortality was 3.9% (n = 18), accounting for 28.6% of all in-hospital deaths. Competing-risk cumulative incidence curves indicate that ICU death occurred early during the ICU stay, with the highest incidence observed within the first 2–5 days following ICU admission, after which the cumulative incidence remained low (Fig. 1B).

In Fine–Gray competing-risk analysis, illness severity was strongly associated with ICU mortality. Each 1-point increase in SOFA score was associated with a higher subdistribution hazard of ICU death in univariable analysis (sHR 1.61, 95% CI 1.31–1.97; p <0.001), and this association remained robust after adjustment for age, sex, and admission type (adjusted sHR 1.69, 95% CI 1.32–2.15; p <0.001).

Sensitivity analyses using alternative severity scores yielded consistent associations for both ICU and hospital mortality, with no material change in effect estimates (Table S3). In the frailty-complete subset, higher Clinical Frailty Score was associated with increased in-hospital mortality, whereas estimates for ICU mortality were imprecise given the small number of events (Table S4).

Care trajectory and disposition

Most centenarian ICU admissions originated from the community, with the majority admitted directly from home, while a smaller proportion were transferred from nursing homes or other hospitals (Fig. 2A). Very few patients were admitted from rehabilitation facilities.

Post-discharge survival among hospital survivors

Among centenarians who survived to hospital discharge, post-discharge survival declined steadily over time (Fig. 3). Median survival was 21.9 months (95% CI 18.0–25.6). Estimated survival probabilities were 91% (95% CI 87–94) at 6 months, 81% (95% CI 76–86) at 1 year, and 68% (95% CI 63–73) at 2 years following discharge.

Discrimination of severity-of-illness scores

Receiver operating characteristic curves for ICU and hospital mortality are shown in Fig. 4. For ICU mortality, ANZROD demonstrated higher discrimination than SOFA and APACHE III, with AUROC values of 0.90 (95% CI 0.84–0.96), 0.80 (95% CI 0.66–0.93), and 0.78 (95% CI 0.63–0.93), respectively.

For hospital mortality, discrimination was similarly higher for ANZROD (AUROC 0.82, 95% CI 0.77–0.87) compared with APACHE III (AUROC 0.73, 95% CI 0.66–0.81) and SOFA (AUROC 0.71, 95% CI 0.64–0.78). ANZROD showed numerically higher discrimination for ICU and hospital mortality compared with SOFA and APACHE III, whereas differences between SOFA and APACHE III were small and generally not statistically significant (Table S5).

Discussion

Key findings

Over the 15-year study period, 460 centenarians were admitted to ICU across the binational registry. Although admissions increased over time, this cohort represents a minute fraction of total ICU activity, reflecting highly selective admission practices at extreme age. In-hospital mortality occurred in a minority of patients and was concentrated early during the ICU and hospital stay. Among patients who survived to hospital discharge, post-discharge survival extended beyond the index admission, and approximately half of patients were discharged home at hospital separation. Acute illness severity measures were consistently associated with ICU and hospital mortality across competing-risk models, whereas chronological age showed limited variation within this population. Survival differed by admission type, with higher survival observed among surgical compared with medical admissions. Commonly used severity-of-illness scores demonstrated discrimination for short-term mortality, with differences in discrimination across scoring systems.

Relationship to prior studies and literature

Hospital mortality in this centenarian cohort lies toward the lower end of previously reported rates in centenarian-specific hospital-based analyses (approximately 10%–25%) [31, 32].

In contrast, ICU cohorts of very elderly patients aged ≥80 years in Australia and New Zealand have reported hospital mortality between approximately 15% and 24% [7, 9], with rates of approximately 27% observed in multinational cohorts of acutely admitted octogenarians and nonagenarians [33]. These ICU cohorts predominantly comprised acutely admitted medical patients with higher illness severity at admission.

By comparison, centenarians in our registry had lower admission severity scores, and two-thirds of admissions were surgical, many elective, reflecting a distinct case mix. The observed outcomes therefore reflect a highly selected ICU population rather than all hospitalised centenarians. Consistent with this profile, prior studies have demonstrated lower mortality following surgery compared with medical admissions in elderly ICU populations [34], including comparable outcomes between centenarians and nonagenarians undergoing hip fracture surgery [16].

In our cohort, ICU length of stay was short and similar between survivors and non-survivors, and competing-risk analyses showed that both discharge and death occurred early. Short ICU stay has likewise been reported in very elderly cohorts in Australia and New Zealand [7, 9]. The elective nature of many admissions likely contributed to early discharge among survivors, although the influence of treatment intent cannot be fully determined from registry data.

Favourable survival among hospital survivors, with median survival exceeding 21 months and a high proportion discharged home at hospital separation, suggests that ICU admission at extreme age does not invariably portend poor longer-term outcomes. Although functional status and quality of life could not be assessed directly, the high proportion of patients discharged home suggests that meaningful recovery may be possible in selected centenarians.

Acute illness severity, rather than chronological age, was the dominant predictor of mortality. This finding is consistent with evidence questioning age-based prognostication in critical care and emphasising the greater relevance of physiological reserve and acute disease burden in very elderly patients [6, 17, 35]. Recent binational data in nonagenarians likewise report frailty as an independent predictor of mortality [36]. The limited prognostic contribution of age in our cohort may reflect survivorship and selection effects among centenarians [13–15].

Regarding risk stratification, prior studies have demonstrated acceptable discrimination of SOFA- and APACHE-based models in octogenarians, although performance at extreme age has remained uncertain [18, 19]. The superior discrimination of ANZROD observed in our study is consistent with its regional calibration to contemporary case mix and admission practices [22, 25], and aligns with recent evidence of improved performance in nonagenarian and centenarian populations [12].

Strengths and limitations

This multicentre, binational study represents the largest dedicated investigation of centenarian ICU admissions to date. Use of the ANZICS APD provides high-quality, prospectively collected data with comprehensive capture of short-term outcomes, discharge destinations, and 2-year survival, enhancing internal validity. Application of competing-risk modelling with covariate adjustment and bootstrap validation strengthened the robustness of estimates despite the small number of ICU deaths.

Several limitations warrant consideration. This study represents a retrospective secondary analysis of a prospectively maintained clinical registry that was not specifically designed to address centenarian-focused research questions. Centenarians admitted to ICU are likely a selected group with greater physiological resilience, as frailer individuals may not be referred or may receive ward-based palliative care. As the registry captures only admitted patients, individuals who were declined ICU admission or managed outside the ICU are not represented. Consequently, the proportion of centenarians considered but not admitted to ICU and their comparative outcomes could not be examined. The broadly similar comorbidity burden between survivors and non-survivors is consistent with possible selection processes. Although frailty was collected, it was not incorporated into the primary analyses because of substantial missingness; results from the frailty-complete sensitivity analysis are therefore exploratory and should be interpreted with caution.

Treatment goals at ICU admission were available within the registry and differed significantly between survivors and non-survivors. Although this provides important contextual insight into goals of care at the time of ICU admission, the database does not capture subsequent escalation or withdrawal decisions during the ICU stay. Consequently, observed mortality may partly reflect treatment intent in addition to acute physiological severity.

Generalisability may be limited by differences in ICU admission thresholds, end-of-life practices, and healthcare systems outside Australia and New Zealand. The predominance of surgical admissions, reflecting preoperative selection and potentially more reversible pathology, further limits applicability to unselected or predominantly medical centenarian populations. Although elective and emergency surgical admissions were distinguishable, more granular perioperative data, including baseline functional trajectory and frailty measures, were not available. Validation of severity-of-illness scores was exploratory and internal; external validation in independent centenarian cohorts remains necessary. Finally, although two-year survival data are a strength, functional status, cognitive outcomes, quality of life, and cause-specific post-discharge mortality were not available and represent important areas for future research.

Clinical implications

The relatively low in-hospital mortality and favourable post-discharge survival observed in this cohort suggest that ICU admission should not be determined by chronological age alone. Consistent with recent international expert consensus on the trajectory of very old critically ill patients [37], our findings align with the principle that ICU triage decisions should be guided by individual patient characteristics, including acute illness severity, admission context, treatment intent, comorbidity burden, and baseline functional status, within a shared decision-making framework. The heterogeneity of outcomes observed within this cohort reflects the variability of prognosis across clinical contexts at extreme age. Accordingly, individualised assessment is likely to be more informative than age-based thresholds when supporting goals-of-care discussions and resource allocation.

Differences in outcomes between surgical and medical admissions further highlight the importance of admission type and reversibility of the acute condition when considering prognosis at extreme age. While the evaluated severity-of-illness scores demonstrated varying discrimination, the higher performance of ANZROD compared with SOFA and APACHE III supports a potential role as a prognostic adjunct within Australian and New Zealand settings, noting that score-based estimates should complement, rather than replace, clinical judgement. Importantly, these performance estimates apply only to centenarians who were admitted to ICU and should not be extrapolated to inform decisions about whether a patient should or should not be admitted to critical care.

Prospective studies incorporating standardised frailty assessment, functional recovery, cognitive outcomes, and patient-reported quality of life are needed to better characterise survivorship following ICU admission in centenarians. Linkage with hospital-wide referral data or prospective capture of ICU triage decisions would enable a more comprehensive assessment of admission practices at extreme age and better delineate selection effects. External validation of commonly used severity-of-illness scores in independent centenarian cohorts across different healthcare systems is essential to assess generalisability. Longer-term follow-up examining post-discharge trajectories, healthcare utilisation, and patient and family perspectives may further inform person-centred critical care for this growing population.

Conclusion

In this large binational cohort of centenarians admitted to intensive care, ICU admission was infrequent and occurred in a highly selected population. In-hospital mortality was relatively low and occurred early during hospitalisation. Half of survivors were discharged home and demonstrated substantial post-discharge survival. Acute illness severity measures were consistently associated with ICU and hospital mortality, whereas chronological age showed limited prognostic contribution within this extreme age group. Regionally calibrated severity scores, particularly ANZROD, showed favourable discrimination for short-term mortality in this cohort. These findings support the importance of individualised clinical assessment rather than reliance on chronological age alone.
