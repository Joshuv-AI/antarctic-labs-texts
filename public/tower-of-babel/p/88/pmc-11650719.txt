PP2A activation overcomes leptomeningeal dissemination in group 3 medulloblastoma
Authors: Nazia Nazam, Michael H Erwin, Janet R Julson, Colin H Quinn, Andee M Beierle, Laura V Bownes, Jerry E Stewart, Kyung-Don Kang, Swatika Butey, Elizabeth Mroczek-Musulman, Michael Ohlmeyer, Elizabeth A Beierle
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeptomeningeal dissemination (LMD) is the primary cause of treatment failure in children with group 3 medulloblastoma (MB). Building on our previous work on protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) activation in MB, here we present preclinical and molecular data on the effects of two novel classes of PP2A activators on disease processes of LMD in group 3 MB. The PP2A activators used in this study are ATUX-6156 and ATUX-6954 (diarylmethylcycloamine sulfonylureas), and ATUX-1215 and ATUX-5800 (diarylmethyl-4-aminotetrahydropyran-sulfonamides). Treatment with these compounds led to suppression of the endogenous PP2A inhibitor, cancerous inhibitor of PP2A (CIP2A), enhanced phosphatase activity (10–60%), and reduced MB viability, migration, and invasion, prerequisites for MB cells to access the cerebrospinal fluid, affecting the initiation stage of LMD. PP2A activator treatment of MB cells led to apoptosis mediated via caspase 9/PARP signaling due to decreased phosphorylation of Bad, impeding the dispersal stage of LMD. Cell proliferation and LMD-driving cellular traits and molecules pertinent to the third stage, colonization, were also affected. Treatment with ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 prevented LMD in an intraventricular murine model of MB, possibly mediated by disruption of the CCL2-CCR2 axis by altered NF-kB phosphorylation via disrupted AKT signaling. The present investigation offers proof-of-principle data for PP2A-based reactivation therapy for Group 3 MB and provides the first indications that PP2A reactivation may challenge the current paradigm in targeting the 3-stage process of MB LMD. Further investigations of PP2A activators are warranted as these compounds may prove beneficial as therapeutics for MB.Keywords: PP2A, medulloblastoma, leptomeningeal dissemination, diarylmethyl cycloamine sulfonylureas, pyran sulfonamides

Medulloblastoma (MB), a malignancy developing in the cerebellum, is the most common solid tumor afflicting the pediatric population, and the second most common pediatric malignancy surpassed only by leukemia (1). Based on differences in genomic features and clinical behavior, MB is classified into four major molecular subtypes including WNT, Sonic Hedgehog, group 3, and group 4 (2, 3). Group 3 MBs account for approximately 25 to 30% of all MB diagnoses, but bear the worst prognosis (2, 4), with 5-year overall survival (OS) just slightly higher than 50%, compared to 70% for the other 3 subtypes (4, 5). One of the major contributors to this poor outcome is leptomeningeal dissemination (LMD), the predominant pattern of metastasis involving tumor spread to the leptomeninges of the brain and spinal cord (6). LMD remains an understudied domain of MB research. Consequently, novel therapeutic strategies designed to target LMD and effectively treat this devastating malignancy are warranted.

Serine/threonine protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) holoenzyme dephosphorylates an array of substrates that mediate a multitude of cell signaling pathways. PP2A is suggested to be a tumor suppressor across various cancers (7, 8, 9). The activity of PP2A is inhibited in human malignancies, including brain cancer, promoting malignant transformation of normal cells (10, 11, 12, 13). PP2A inhibition by either genetic mechanisms or overexpression of its endogenous inhibitor proteins, inhibitor 2 of protein phosphatase 2A (I2PP2A, SET) or cancerous inhibitor of protein phosphatase 2A (CIP2A), promotes tumorigenesis and correlates with therapeutic resistance (14, 15, 16). Pharmacologic PP2A reactivation has shown promising results in overcoming multikinase inhibitor tolerance (17) and apoptosis resistance (13) in MB and other brain cancer cells. However, to our knowledge, to date there have been no studies focusing on translational impact of PP2A reactivation correlating with the stages of LMD in MB.

Genetic and functional preclinical studies are needed to fully decipher the mechanisms underlying LMD in MB and to identify new therapeutic targets (18). Hence, the present study focuses on elucidating the plausible role of novel small molecule PP2A activators in the three-stage process of LMD in group 3 MB; initiation, dispersal, and colonization (6).

Results

Novel PP2A activators as experimental therapeutics

The pyran sulfonamides are next generation PP2A activators isosteric with the previously described tricyclic-sulfonamide prototypes (11, 19), and the relation between the two were discussed by Pillai et al (20). Structures of compounds used in the present study are shown in Figure 1A. ATUX-1215 and ATUX-5800 retain key elements of the PP2A activator pharmacophore in the prototypes, and in ATUX-6156 and ATUX-6954 the main difference being replacement of the sulfonamide hydrogen bond, NH, donor with a sulfonyl urea NH donor. In vitro microsome stability testing revealed superior stability in ATUX-6156, ATUX-5800, and ATUX-1215 compared to ATUX-6954. Therefore, these three compounds were advanced to in vivo testing. Fast clearance from plasma mediated by oxidative metabolism limited the bioavailability after bolus oral dose in the prototype tricyclic sulfonamides (20). However, this issue can be suppressed by fluorination on the diarylmethyl moiety; compare ATUX-5800 with ATUX-1215 (Fig. 1A, top panel) and ATUX-6954 and ATUX-6156 (Fig. 1A, bottom panel). Fluorine substitution on the aromatic ring increases the percentage of the compound remaining at 30 min by a factor of approximately two in microsomes (Fig. 1B) and decreases clearance in vitro (Fig. 1B) and in vivo (Fig. 1, C and D) by a factor of approximately two. The major distinction between the two compound types is manifest in the brain exposure after oral dose with brain/plasma ratio of 1.3 and 0.4 for ATUX-5800 and ATUX-1215, respectively, versus 0.02 for ATUX-6156 (Fig. 1E). This lack of detectable ATUX-6156 in the brain led us to focus our attention on the ATUX-1215 and ATUX-5800 compounds.

ATUX compounds activate PP2A in MB PDX cells

PP2A is highly expressed in the human brain (21). In MB, it acts as a tumor suppressor and is confirmed to be downregulated in this cancer, leading to activation of tumorigenic signaling pathways (9). Hence, we aimed to assess the therapeutic reactivation of PP2A using the selected PP2A activators in human group 3 MB patient-derived xenograft (PDX) cells. The phosphatase activity of PP2A was observed to increase (10–50%) following treatment of human group 3 MB PDX D425 cells with increasing concentrations (0–5 μM) of ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 for 4 h (Fig. 2A). Significantly enhanced phosphatase activity (30–80%) was seen in human group 3 MB PDX D341 cells treated with ATUX-1215 and ATUX-5800 (0–6 μM) for 4 h (Fig. 2B). These compounds increased PP2A activation in a third group 3 human MB PDX cell line, D384 (Fig. S4A). The ATUX-6156 and ATUX-6954 compounds also activated PP2A in D425 and D341 (Fig. S1, A and B, respectively). Extent of response varied with cells and activators employed.

Our earlier work with PP2A activators in neuroblastoma (11) and the investigations of other researchers (12) prompted us to investigate PP2A activation through effects on the endogenous PP2A inhibitors, CIP2A and I2PP2A/SET. In concordance with increased phosphatase activity, decreased CIP2A expression was observed in D425 (Figs. 2C, left panel and S8A), D341 (Figs. 2D, left panel and S9A) and D384 (Fig. S4B) irrespective of the PP2A activator employed. Expression of I2PP2A was increased in the D425 or D341 cells by ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 (Figs. 2, C and D, right panels, respectively and S9A), potentially a cellular compensatory effect. Other PP2A activators had similar effects on expression of endogenous PP2A inhibitors (Figs. 1, C and D and S4B). Thus, these compounds decreased expression of CIP2A, the endogenous inhibitor of PP2A, potentially serving as a mechanism for increased PP2A activity.

PP2A activators reduce viability in group 3 MB cells

Cell viability was determined using alamarBlue assays. D425 cells were treated with increasing concentrations of ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 (0–20 μM) for 24 h. The lowest lethal dose 50% (LD50) for the chosen time interval is selected. Dose-response curves demonstrated a LD50 that was slightly lower in the cells treated with ATUX-1215 compared to ATUX-5800 (Fig. 3, A and C). D341 cells were treated with ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 (0–10 μM) for 72 h. Dose-response curves demonstrated LD50 ranging between 5.0 to 6.2 μM for ATUX-1215 and ATUX-5800 (Fig. 3, B and C). Similar findings were noted in D384 cells (Fig. S4, C and D). Treatment of MB PDX cells with other PP2A activating compounds resulted in LD50 concentrations similar to those of ATUX-1215 and ATUX-5800 (Fig. S2, A–C). These findings show that both candidate compounds, ATUX-1215 and ATUX-5800, decreased MB viability; however, the responses were cell line dependent (Figs. 3C and S4D).

The observation that these compounds affected viability raised a question regarding whether these findings were secondary to apoptosis. We investigated the effects of PP2A activators on apoptosis in D425 and D341 cells treated with increasing concentrations of compounds for 24 h (D425) and 72 h (D341). Immunoblotting revealed decreased expression of total PARP with ATUX-1215 and ATUX-5800, indicating apoptosis (Figs. 3, D and E and S9B). These compounds also led to increased cleaved caspase 9 (Figs 3, D and E and S9C), further supporting apoptotic events. Since phosphorylated Bad (pBAD) confers protection to cells from the effects of apoptotic stimuli, we investigated the effects of PP2A activators on phosphorylation of Bad (pBad). There was evidence of decreased pBad in D425 and D341 cells treated with these PP2A activators (Figs. 3, D and E and S9D). Evidence of apoptosis was seen following treatment of D425 cells with ATUX-6156 and ATUX-6954 with increased PARP cleavage and decreased pBAD (Fig. S2D) and D384 cells treated with ATUX-1215 and ATUX-5800 leading to increased caspase 9 cleavage and decreased pBAD (Fig. S4E). These data provide evidence for PP2A activation supporting apoptosis.

Since AKT signaling contributes to apoptosis and tumor cell viability, and PP2A is known to negatively regulate AKT function (22), we next investigated the effects of PP2A compounds on expression and phosphorylation of AKT oncoprotein. D425 and D341 cells were treated with increasing concentrations of ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 (0–5 μM for D425) and (0–6 μM for D341). Immunoblotting of D425 and D341 PDX whole cell lysates showed decreased phosphorylation of AKT with increasing concentrations of ATUX-1215 and ATUX-5800 (Figs. 3, F and G and S9E), suggesting that deactivation of AKT signaling may be contributing toward decreased viability and apoptosis seen following treatment with these PP2A activating compounds.

PP2A activators decrease proliferation in MB PDX cells

AKT signaling also regulates cell proliferation, so we further investigated the effect of these activators on MB proliferation. D425, D341, and D384 cells were treated with increasing concentrations of PP2A activators for 72 h, and proliferation measured with CellTiter96. Decreased proliferation was noted in all PDXs with the selected PP2A compounds (Fig. S3, A–F). Immunoblotting of D425 PDX whole cell lysates treated with ATUX-6156/6954 also showed decreased phosphorylation of AKT with increasing concentrations of these activators (Fig. S3G).

MB migration and invasion are reduced with PP2A activators

Leptomeningeal disease involves dissemination and seeding of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and engages the invasion-metastasis cascade; therefore, we investigated the effects of PP2A activators on cell migration and invasion. D425 cells were treated with ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 (0–5 μM) and allowed to invade for 24 h. Migration was significantly decreased with both compounds compared to controls (Fig. 4, A and C). D341 cells were treated with ATUX-1215 (0–4 μM) and ATUX-5800 (0–6 μM) and allowed to migrate for 24 h. Migration was significantly decreased with treatment (Fig. 4, B and D). Cells were treated in a similar fashion for invasion studies. PP2A activating compounds significantly decreased invasion in both PDX cell lines (Fig. 4, E and F). Representative photomicrographs of the migration and invasion inserts are shown below the graphs for migration (Fig. 4, C and D) and for invasion (Fig. 4, G and H). Similar findings were encountered with the other PP2A activating compounds in migration assay (Fig. S2, E and F). Collectively these results show that PP2A activators inhibit MB cell migration and invasion.

PP2A activators decrease tumorsphere formation of MB PDX cells

Cancer cell stemness is thought to affect tumor cell dispersal. The ability of cancer cells to form tumorspheres in nonadherent culture conditions is one measure of stemness. We investigated the effects of PP2A activators on tumorsphere formation using tumorsphere formation assay evaluated with an extreme limiting dilution analysis. ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 (5 μM) treated D425 cells had significantly (p < 0.0001) decreased tumorsphere forming ability (Fig. 5A). ATUX-1215 (4 μM, Fig. 5B) and ATUX-5800 (6 μM, Fig. 5C) treatment significantly (p < 0.0001) decreased ability of D341 cells to form spheres in nonadherent conditions. Tumorsphere forming ability of D384 cells treated with ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 was similarly decreased (Fig. S4, G and H). Treatment with ATUX-6156 or ATUX-6954 (1.5 μM) resulted in a significant decrease (p = 0.0001) in tumorsphere forming ability in both the D425 (Fig. S5A) and D341 (Fig. S5B) PDX cells. Thus, PP2A activation decreased tumorsphere forming ability indicating a less stem cell-like cancer cell phenotype.

Treatment with ATUX-6156 or ATUX-6954 decreases viability of 3D bioprinted tumors

ATUX-6156 is orally bioavailable with transient peak plasma concentration of around 18 μM at 4 h post oral dose (Tmax), Figure 1, C and D [rule of thumb: for MW = 500 g/mol, 1000 ng/ml = 2.0 μM], and plasma concentration is >2.0 μM for 1 to 8 h post oral dose at 30 mg/kg. We wished to advance to in vivo experiments; however, the brain-to-plasma concentration ratio of ATUX-6156 was less than 0.02 (Fig. 1E) indicating that this compound does not penetrate the blood–brain barrier in a significant quantity. Further, flank tumor models do not accurately represent the usual environment for MB and are not gold standard evaluations for this tumor type. For these reasons, we did not pursue ATUX-6156 or ATUX-6954 further in MB animal model studies. Instead, we used a novel ex vivo model of 3D bioprinting MB microtumors to assess the effectiveness of these PP2A activators. Employing 3D bioprinted D425 tumors, we found that both ATUX-6156 (Fig. S6A) and ATUX-6954 (Fig. S6B) significantly decreased viability, demonstrating preclinical efficacy with PP2A activation in MB.

Treatment with ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 provides survival benefit in an intracerebellar murine model of MB

Figure 1, C and D shows systemic exposure for ATUX-5800 and ATUX-1215. Both compounds have good oral bioavailability, and consistent with the microsome stability data, Figure 1B, the in vivo clearance of ATUX-1215 is about half that of ATUX-5800, shown in Figure 1C, which translates into about two-fold greater systemic exposure for ATUX-1215 versus ATUX-5800 where area under the curve is 22,400 versus 12,900 h·ng/ml, respectively (Fig. 1C). ATUX-1215 has sustained plasma levels of around 4 μM for 2 to 8 h post oral dose at 30 mg/kg (Fig. 1B). ATUX-5800 shows better partition into brain tissue (whole brain homogenate) versus ATUX-1215 (Fig. 1E), and the brain concentrations at 2 h post oral dose are roughly equivalent at around 2 μM for both compounds after oral dose at 30 mg/kg (1164 ng/g versus 960 ng/g, ATUX-5800 versus ATUX-1215, Fig. 1E). For these reasons, ATUX-5800 having better brain penetrance, and ATUX-1215 having a greater plasma concentration, both ATUX-5800 and ATUX-1215 were investigated in in vivo models.

We employed an intracerebellar orthotopic MB murine model. Animals bearing D425 tumors were treated with 75 mg/kg PP2A activators via oral gavage. Treated animals trended toward increased survival compared to vehicle-treated controls (14.5 versus 12 days for ATUX-1215 versus vehicle, and 14 versus 12 days for ATUX-5800 versus vehicle, p = 0.6) (Fig. S7).

ATUX-1215 and ATUX-5800 prevent leptomeningeal dissemination/metastasis in an intraventricular murine model of MB

PP2A activation significantly affected MB cell motility, and MB has a propensity to enter the CSF and metastasize along the leptomeninges. Hence, we assessed the effects of PP2A activation on leptomeningeal metastasis using a murine model of MB intraventricular dissemination. Animals were assessed for leptomeningeal spread using bioluminescence imaging. Representative bioluminescence images through 3 weeks post initiation of treatment with ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 in animals bearing luciferase-expressing D425Luc or D341Luc tumors are shown in Figure 6A (left panels and right panels, respectively). Bioluminescence (total flux, photons/second; p/s) for tumor burden in the brain and spinal cord was significantly lower with ATUX-1215 versus vehicle-treated control animals in both D425 Luc (Fig. 6B) and D341 Luc (Fig. 6C). ATUX-5800 initially reduced leptomeningeal metastasis in D341 Luc (Fig. 6D) animals, but this effect was not sustained for the duration of the experiment as the animals were followed for survival. Representative images (4× magnification) of H&E stained spinal columns of untreated and treated D341 Luc animals are shown in Figure 6D (left panel). The fold change metastasis is reduced in both ATUX-1215 and ATUX-5800 treated mice compared to vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 6D, right panel). Since we had seen a decrease in the expression of CIP2A in MB cells treated in vitro (Fig. 2D), we investigated CIP2A expression via immunohistochemistry (IHC) in leptomeningeal metastasis. There was strong CIP2A staining in the metastasis of the vehicle-treated animals (Fig. 6E, left panels) and virtually no staining in the metastases of animals treated with ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 (Fig. 6E, middle and right panels, respectively). Negative control staining with rabbit IgG was completed (Fig. 6E, left lower panel inset).

ATUX-1215 and ATUX-5800 treatment affects the CCL2/CCR2 molecular axis

Expression of CCR2 or CCL2 predicts metastasis and the CCL2/CCR2 signaling axis represents the first specific biological target for treatment of MB metastases (23). Since we had seen an effect on LMD in vivo, we evaluated the effects of ATUX-1215 and ATUX-5800 on the expression of these proteins in vitro. CCL2 and CCR2 protein expression was decreased in the D425 MB cells following treatment with ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 (Figs. 7A, left panel and S9F). In the D341 cells, CCR2 was decreased, but CCL2 was variably affected by ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 (Figs. 7B, left panel and S9F). We found a similar decreased expression in D384 cells (Fig. S4F, left panel). Treatment of D425 and cells with PP2A activators attenuated phosphorylation of NF-κB, the active form, at some concentrations (Figs. 7A, right panel and S9G). ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 treatment of D341 cells decreased phosphorylation of NF-κB at all concentrations (Figs. 7B, right panel and S9G). Results for NF-kB were similar in D384 cells (Fig. S4F, right panel). This reduction in NF-kB phosphorylation may serve as the driver of attenuated CCR2 and CCL2 expression thereby contributing to decreased leptomeningeal spread.

CIP2A is associated with decreased survival in MB

Analysis of the primary human MB samples for patient survival database indicates CIP2A gene overexpression predicts reduced OS in human MB (p = 2.87e−03, Fig. S8A). Specifically, group 3 MB patients harboring overexpressed CIP2A gene exhibit inferior survival compared to those with low gene expression (p = 0.05, Fig. S8B).

We conclude that PP2A activation via reduced CIP2A, downregulates PI3/Akt survival signals leading to apoptotic induction, simultaneously affecting CCL2/CCR2 via NF-kB signaling (Fig. 8A). Hence, a decrease in the 3-stage process of LMD via this axis as demonstrated in the cartoon (Fig. 8B).

Discussion

Outcomes for children with group 3 MB remain poor and many survivors suffer from debilitating life-long toxicities and side effects related to the interventions intended to treat their disease. Leptomeningeal spread is the main cause for treatment failure and is seen most often in group 3 tumors. These dismal outcomes prompted us to concentrate on group 3 tumors, as the need for new therapies for this subset of tumors is immense.

Commensurate with our previous work investigating the role of PP2A in MB, we hypothesized that novel PP2A activating compounds would provide a promising avenue for such therapeutics. We aimed to provide preclinical evidence of efficacy combined with an understanding of the effects of these activators on the molecular mechanisms that underlie the 3-stage process of LMD cascade, as emphasized and envisioned in recent studies focused on MB (6, 18).

The tumor suppressor phosphatase, PP2A, is known to be dysregulated and functionally inactivated in many cancers including MB (10, 11, 12, 13, 24). Evidence suggests that inhibited PP2A in cancer could primarily be due to overexpressed PP2A inhibitor proteins or modifications of either the scaffolding, catalytic, or regulatory subunits of the PP2A holoenzyme protein complex, and both mechanisms may be involved in MB (14, 15). We found that increased gene expression of CIP2A correlated with OS in MB. These findings are similar to those of Cavalli et al. (25). Other analyses confirm alterations of the various regulatory subunits of the PP2A holoenzyme in MB. Using publicly available gene expression datasets, investigators identified overexpression of genes PPP2R2C, in Sonic Hedgehog subgroup, PPP2R2B in group 3 tumors, and PP2R2A, in group 4 tumors (26). These three genes encode for multiple regulatory subunits of PP2A, with aberrant expression of these regulatory subunits resulting in PP2A dysfunction. Hence, investigation of PP2A as a specific gene therapeutic target would hold promise for group 3 MB. In the current study, phosphatase activation was observed for both chemical types in all the MB PDX cells under study. We attribute most of the increased PP2A activity to decreased protein expression of the endogenous PP2A inhibitor, CIP2A. There may be several explanations for these findings. First, CIP2A is an inducible PP2A-B56 regulatory (inhibitor) protein, and it is downregulated at the level of transcription by PP2A activation driven by NF-kB (27, 28), and we noted a decrease in NF-kB phosphorylation in our studies. Second, the PP2A activating compounds have been shown to act at the protein level on CIP2A (11, 27), which is likely the effect of compound-mediated stabilization of cytosolic PP2A-B56α from the disassembling effects of CIP2A (28). Nonetheless, we postulate that functional CIP2A inhibition by these novel PP2A activating compounds is a direct target effect and a contributor to enhanced activity of phosphatase activity in our study.

The 3-stage process of the LMD cascade begins when MB cells escape from the primary tumor mass in the cerebellum and enter the CSF, the initiation stage. The biologic traits required for disseminating MB cells to complete this first initiation stage consist of cell viability, motility, and stem cell-like state (6). In the current study, pharmacologic PP2A activation led to decreased MB PDX cell viability and motility. Other studies have shown similar results with FTY-720, an immunosuppressive drug that targets I2PPA, leading to derepression of PP2A activity (9, 28). Further, targeting MB PDX cells with ATUX-6156/6954 or ATUX-1215/5800 resulted in a significant decrease in the ability of the cells to form tumorspheres, one of key phenotypes of MB stem cell-like cancer cells (29). Other investigators have noted decreased cancer cell stemness with FTY-720 mediated PP2A activation in glioma cells (30). Treatment of gastric cancer cells with OP449, a peptide SET inhibitor designed to increase PP2A activity, resulted in a decrease in expression of the stemness marker protein Nanog (30). Thus, the current novel PP2A activators hinder the primary tumor cells in the cerebellum from entering the CSF and forming metastases by affecting the initiation stage of LMD through their effects on viability, motility, and stemness.

In the second step of LMD, the dispersal stage, surviving tumor cells transit through the CSF. The traits responsible for this stage of LMD are apoptosis and survival signaling. There was evidence of apoptosis seen in the MB PDX cells treated with ATUX-1215 and ATUX-5800. Treatment with these PP2A activators resulted in dephosphorylation of Bad. Dephosphorylated Bad interacts with Bcl-2 and forms heterodimers which inactivate Bcl2 resulting in apoptosis. We observed cleavage of the initiator caspase 9 with loss of total PARP expression following PP2A activation, demonstrating the apoptotic phenotype of PP2A-treated MB cells. Likewise, other investigators have reported that PP2A activation promotes apoptosis (31). In neuronal cancers specifically, FTY-720-induced PP2A activation induced apoptosis in human glioma (32) and neuroblastoma cells (11, 33). Further, in the current studies, PP2A activation decreased Akt-mediated survival signaling in MB PDX cells. Similar observations were made in lung and breast cancer where PP2A activation inhibited Akt signaling (34). Altogether, these data indicate that PP2A activation impedes the dispersal stage of LMD in these group 3 MB.

Surviving tumor cells, post transition through the CSF, implant on leptomeningeal surfaces of the spinal cord to establish a proliferating metastatic nidus, the colonization stage. LMD-driving molecules and cellular traits pertinent to this stage include proliferation and inflammation. In the current study, PP2A activators significantly affected proliferation of MB PDX cells. The decreased proliferation observed is attributed to deactivation of AKT, with similar observations made by earlier investigators linking AKT activation with decreased PP2A activity in MB (34). Park and others used data from the Medulloblastoma Advanced Genomic International Consortium database, which contains 520 patient samples, and found a positive correlation between elevated expression of the PI3K/AKT pathway with poor survival in patients with group 3 tumors (35). Other investigators have shown that activated PI3K/AKT signaling is associated with MB proliferation (36, 37). Studies have also demonstrated the effects of PI3K/AKT signaling on MB cancer cell stemness (38, 39). We therefore believe that PP2A-induced deactivation of AKT may be a mechanism for the decreased stemness and tumor proliferation observed following treatment with ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800.

Tumor dissemination to the leptomeninges is postulated to occur by passive spread through the CSF, although this idea has not been proven empirically. Recently, dissemination has been shown to occur through the hematogenous compartment, which may inform efforts to identify inhibitors of metastatic dissemination (40). Importantly, CCL2 and its cognate receptor CCR2 (together forming the CCL2/CCR2 signaling axis) have been shown to facilitate leukocyte trafficking out of the hematogenous compartment, and dysregulation of the axis has been implicated in driving the dissemination and progression of metastasis in many other malignancies (23). Expression analysis of group 3 primary and metastatic MB tumors has identified CCL2 as a differentially expressed gene between these compartments, and copy number gain in group 3 primary tumors predicts higher rates of metastatic dissemination (40). Forced expression, or knockdown, of CCL2 in rarely metastatic xenografted cell lines significantly increased and decreased metastatic rates, respectively, strongly suggesting a role in mediating metastatic dissemination as also observed in our study (40).

The delivery of targeted therapies across the blood-brain barrier poses significant challenges in the clinical translation of promising compounds. In the current study, ATUX-6156 and ATUX-6954 showed promising results in the in vitro studies. However, subsequent bioavailability studies revealed a low brain:plasma ratio, so as proof of concept, we tested these compounds using a 3D bioprinted ex-vivo model. These compounds significantly decreased viability of 3D bioprinted tumors which provides proof of concept for the preclinical efficacy of this class of compounds for MB. In addition, these ex vivo studies demonstrate the future applicability of this technology to provide a means for completing high-throughput drug studies, identifying the most promising compounds to take to in vivo studies, and expediting the identification of novel, individualized therapies (41, 42, 43).

In contrast, ATUX-1215 and ATUX-5800 demonstrated good blood-brain barrier penetration and reduced MB leptomeningeal disease. However, we note that the ATUX-1215 compound performed better than ATUX-5800 in decreasing leptomeningeal disease, despite a lower brain penetration. One explanation for this finding may be rooted in recent evidence that MB may metastasize through circulating tumor cells in the blood (40). Since ATUX-1215 has more prolonged exposure in vivo by virtue of greater oxidative stability and lower clearance versus ATUX-5800, it may affect metastasizing tumor cells in the circulation more effectively than ATUX-5800, providing a simple explanation for the better overall effect noted in the current study.

In summary, the present investigations offer proof-of-principle data for PP2A-based reactivation therapy for group 3 MB. The use of human PDX MB cells is important as it provides a more clinically relevant model for the preclinical study of novel compounds than established cell lines. The study results provide the first indications that PP2A reactivation may challenge the current paradigm in targeting the 3-stage process of MB LMD, and that small molecule activators of PP2A may prove to be beneficial as future therapies for group 3 tumors (44, 45).

Experimental procedures

Patient-derived MB xenograft cells

Three group 3 human pediatric MB xenografts (46, 47) were used in the experiments and include D425 Med (D425), D341 Med (D341) and D384 Med (D384). These PDX tumors were generously provided by Dr D. D. Bigner and have been previously described (48, 49). The PDXs were maintained in athymic nude mice (Fredricks, Charles River, Wilmington, MA), and the cells were not propagated in cell culture. Tumors were dissociated using a human Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, San Diego, CA) per manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were washed in RPMI 1640 medium, spun (150g × 6 min), and debris removed using a 70 μm cell strainer (Corning Inc). For the cell-based experiments, dissociated cells were maintained under standard culture conditions at 37 °C and 5% CO2, in neurobasal medium (Life Technologies) supplemented with B-27 without Vitamin A (Life Technologies), N2 supplement (Life Technologies), amphotericin B (250 μg/ml, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc), gentamicin (50 μg/ml, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc), L-glutamine (2 mM, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc), epidermal growth factor (10 ng/ml, Miltenyi Biotec) and fibroblast growth factor (10 ng/ml, Miltenyi Biotec). Each biologic replicate represents a PDX from a different animal. The MB PDXs were verified within the last 12 months using short tandem repeat analysis (University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB) Genomics Core). All cells were deemed free of Mycoplasma infection by testing with the Universal Mycoplasma Detection Kit (30–1012K, American Type Culture Collection (ATCC)).

Reagents and antibodies

Primary antibodies used for immunoblotting included: rabbit polyclonal anti-CIP2A (ab99518) from Abcam; rabbit polyclonal anti-SET (55201-AP) from Proteintech; rabbit monoclonal antibodies-anti-PARP (9532), anti-cleaved caspase-9 (20750S), anti-Bad (9239S), anti-pBad (5284S), anti-vinculin (13901S), anti-NF-κB (8242S), and anti-pNF-κB (3033S), and rabbit polyclonal antibodies-anti-total AKT (9272) and anti-phospho-AKT (S473; 9271) from Cell Signaling Technology; mouse monoclonal anti-β-actin from Sigma (A1978, Sigma-Aldrich). Rabbit polyclonal anti-cleaved PARP (ab3565) and mouse monoclonal anti-GAPDH (MAB374) were from Millipore Sigma. Mouse monoclonal anti-CCL2 (AM06749PU-N) and rabbit polyclonal anti-CCR2 (TA336782) were from Origene (Origene Technologies Inc). ATUX-6156, ATUX-6954, ATUX-1215 and ATUX-5800 were supplied by Atux Iskay, LLC.

PP2A activity assay

Cells (D425, 1 × 106 cells) were treated with ATUX-6156 or ATUX-6954 (0, 1.5, 2.5 μM) and ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 (0, 2.5, 5 μM) for 4 h. D341 (1 × 106 cells) were treated with ATUX-6156 or ATUX-6954 (0, 1.5, 3 μM) and ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 (0–6 μM) for 4 h. For the same cell number of D384 cells, doses used for ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 were 0, 2.5, and 5 μM. Posttreatment, cells were lysed using NP-40 lysis buffer (pH 7.9). PP2A activity was measured using a PP2A Immunoprecipitation Phosphatase Assay Kit (17–313, Millipore Sigma) per manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, protein lysates were incubated with PP2A antibody at 4 °C with continuous rotation for 2 h. Following the addition of assay buffers and malachite green solution, the plate was read at an absorbance of 650 nm using a microplate reader (Epoch Microplate Spectrophotometer, BioTek Instruments). Phosphatase activity was extrapolated from a standard curve. Data are reported as fold change ± SD compared to no treatment.

Cell viability and proliferation assays

An alamarBlue assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc) was performed to assess cell viability following treatment with ATUX-6156 or ATUX-6954 and ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800. D425 or D341 cells were plated in 96-well plates and treated with ATUX-6156 or ATUX-6954 (0–10 μM), or ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 (0–20 μM). For viability assessment in D384 (1.5 × 104 cells), 0 to 20 μM concentrations of ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 were used. For D425 and D384 cells, alamarBlue dye (10 μl) was added after 24 h treatment, and the plates were read using a microplate reader (Epoch Microplate Spectrophotometer) to record the absorbance at 570 nm, using 600 nm as a reference wavelength. For D341 cells, alamarBlue dye (10 μl) was added after 72 h treatment and plates read as these cells turned the dye over more slowly. Experiments were completed in biologic triplicates, viability reported as fold change ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and the respective lethal dose 50% (LD50) in each cell line was calculated.

Proliferation was assessed using the CellTiter 96 Aqueous One Solution Cell Proliferation assay (Promega). D425 and D341 cells (5 × 103) were plated in 96-well plates and treated with ATUX-6156 or ATUX-6954 (0–10 μM) or ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 (0–20 μM). Increasing treatment concentrations of ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 in the range of 0 to 20 μM were used for D384 cells. After 72 h, CellTiter 96 dye (10 μl) was added to each well, and the absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (Epoch Microplate Spectrophotometer) at 490 nm with 600 nm as a reference. Experiments were repeated in biologic triplicates, proliferation reported as fold change ± SEM, and half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) calculated for each activator.

Immunoblotting

Whole cell lysates were prepared in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton x-100, 1% sodium deoxcholate, and 0.1% SDS) or mTOR buffer (for immunoblotting for Akt and pAkt, 40 mM Hepes, pH 7.5, 120 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM pyrophosphate, 10 mM glyceropyrophosphate, 50 nM NaF, 1.5 mM sodium vanadate, 0.3% CHAPS with phosphatase inhibitor (P5726, Sigma-Aldrich)). The lysates were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were determined using a Micro BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), separated by electrophoresis on SDS-PAGE gels, and transferred to Immobilon-P polyvinylidene fluoride transfer membrane (Millipore Sigma). Precision Plus Protein Kaleidoscope Standards molecular weight markers (161–0375, Bio-Rad) were used to confirm the expected size of target proteins. Antibodies were used in accordance with the manufacturers’ recommended protocol with desired modifications. Samples were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence using Luminata Classico and Luminata Crescendo Western horseradish peroxidase substrates (Millipore Sigma). Anti-β-actin, -GAPDH, or -vinculin was used as an internal control to ensure equal protein loading between samples.

Cell motility assays

Migration and invasion were assessed using modified Boyden chamber assay using 6.5 mm Transwell inserts with 8 μM pore polycarbonate membranes (Corning Inc) in 24-well culture plates. The bottoms of the inserts were coated with human laminin (10 μg/ml, AG56P, Millipore Sigma). For invasion, the inserts were additionally coated on top with Matrigel (1 mg/ml). D425 cells were pretreated with ATUX-6156 or ATUX-6954 (0, 1.5 μM) and ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 (0, 2.5, and 5 μM) for 24 h and then 5 × 105 cells plated onto the top of the insert. D341 cells were pretreated with ATUX-6156 or ATUX-6954 (0, 1.5 μM) and ATUX-1215 (0, 2, 4 μM) or ATUX-5800 (0, 4, and 6 μM). Doses were based on LD50 concentrations, as nonviable cells will not move. The insert was then placed into a well containing 300 μl of pretreated media containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco). After 24 h, the cells on top of the inserts were removed using a cotton swab. The inserts were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde prior to staining with crystal violet. The Laxco SeBa digital microscope system was used to take pictures of the inserts at predetermined locations at 40×, and then cells were quantified using ImageJ software (Ver 1.49; https://imagej.net/ij/). Experiments were repeated with biologic triplicates and migration or invasion reported as mean fold change ± SD.

In vitro limiting dilution tumorsphere assay

D425 and D341 cells were treated with ATUX-6156 or ATUX-6954 (0, 1.5 μM) and ATUX-1215 or ATUX-5800 (0, 5 μM) and plated into neurobasal culture media in 96 well ultralow attachment plates using serial dilutions with 1000, 500, 100, 50, 20, 10, or 1 cell per well for cells with at least ten replicates per dilution. D341 cells were treated with ATUX-6156 or ATUX-6954 (0, 1.5 μM) ATUX-1215 (0, 4 μM) or ATUX-5800 (0, 6 μM) while D384 cells were treated with ATUX-1215 (0, 6 μM) or ATUX-5800 (0, 8 μM). After 1 week, the wells were examined for sphere formation. The presence or absence of spheres in each well was determined by a single researcher blinded to the treatment groups. Extreme limiting dilution analysis software was used to analyze the data (http://bioinf.wehi.edu.au/software/elda/).

Ex vivo 3D-bioprint model

D425 cells (1 × 107) were added to 1 ml of a bioink composed of 1% sodium alginate and 6% gelatin and gently mixed to create a homogenous bioink mixture. The bioink was extruded into a 96 well-plate through a 22-gauge 0.50″ blunt tip needle at a pressure of 10 kPa for 0.4 s to create 10 mm3 bioprinted tumor per well. Bioprinted tumors were crosslinked with 40 μl of 2% calcium chloride for 5 min, washed with PBS, and then 200 μl of the respective media were added to each well. Following 24 h of incubation at 5% CO2 and 37 °C, tumors were treated with increasing doses of ATUX-6156 or ATUX-6954 or vehicle control in quadruplicate. After 24 h of treatment, viability was assessed via alamarBlue staining.

Orthotopic cerebellar MB model

All animal experiments were conducted following approval by the University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) (approval number, IACUC-09633). D425 cells (5 × 105 cells in 25% sterile methylcellulose) were used. For the cerebellar injection, female six-week-old athymic nude mice (Fredricks) were anesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg) and buprenorphine (0.5 mg/kg) and placed into a stereotactic frame. Intracerebellar injections were performed using a 250 μl Hamilton syringe with a 30-gauge needle at a rate of 2.5 μl/minute, using the following stereotactic coordinates; 1 mm to the right of midline, 2 mm posterior to the lambdoid suture, and 2 mm below the skull surface. On the day of injection, the mice were randomized (n = 10 per group) to receive vehicle, ATUX-1215, or ATUX-5800 (75 mg/kg) by oral gavage twice daily with saline bolus subcutaneous injection (10 ml/kg) once daily. These doses were based upon previous data in the literature (9) and the mouse pharmacokinetic data for ATUX-1215 and ATUX-5800, Figure 1. The animals were followed for OS and were monitored regularly for the development of neurologic symptoms. They were euthanized once they developed neurologic symptoms (e.g. hemiparesis, lethargy, and anorexia) or when IACUC criteria for euthanasia were met.

In vivo LMD tumor model

For these experiments, we used D425Luc or D341Luc cells which express firefly luciferase. These cells have been previously described (46). Briefly, these cells have been infected with lentiviral particles generated from the pGreenFire1 vector (System Biosciences) to stably express GFP luciferase (50). Six-week-old, female, athymic nude mice (Fredricks) underwent injection of D425Luc or D341Luc cells (5 × 105 cells in 5 μl methylcellulose) into the right lateral ventricle using stereotactic guidance as a model of CSF dissemination (51). The procedure was completed as described above but used the following stereotactic coordinates: 1 mm to the right and 0.5 mm posterior to the bregma, and 2.3 mm below the skull surface. Beginning on the day of injection, mice were randomized (n = 10 per group) to receive either vehicle or PP2A activators, ATUX-1215, or ATUX-5800 (75 mg/kg), twice per day via oral gavage, as described above. Bioluminescence imaging was performed 1 week following injection, and then on subsequent days post treatment using the IVIS Lumina Series III Imaging System (PerkinElmer). All mice were euthanized on postinjection day 23 when most of the animals had begun to develop neurologic symptoms. Tumor growth was evaluated by quantifying the bioluminescence signals using the integrated fluxes of photons within each area of interest using the Living Image Software Package 3.2 (Xenogen-Perkin Elmer; https://www.perkinelmer.com.cn/lab-products-and-services/resources/in-vivo-imaging-software-downloads.html?_ga=2.29909951.1041849854.1637318373-1604795059.1637318373).

Immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded animal tumor specimens were cut (6 μm sections), placed on positive slides, baked at 70 °C for 1 h, deparaffinized, rehydrated, and steamed. Standard H&E staining was completed. H&E quantification was done by a board-certified pathologist blinded to the treatment groups.

For CIP2A IHC, slides were incubated with primary antibody (rabbit polyclonal anti-CIP2A (ab99518), Abcam) at 1:100 dilution overnight at 4 °C. The slides were washed with PBS and rabbit secondary antibody (R.T.U. biotinylated universal antibody, Vector Laboratories) was added for 30 min at 22 °C. Staining reaction was applied for 30 min at room temperature with VECTASTAIN Elite ABC reagent (PK-7100, Vector Laboratories) and Metal Enhanced DAB Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 8 min. All slides were counterstained with hematoxylin. A negative control was included for each IHC run (rabbit IgG, 1 μg/ml, EMD Millipore).

R2-database analysis for MB dataset

R2 Genomics Analysis and Visualization Platform was accessed for mRNA expression data from primary human MB samples (25) to evaluate the association of CIP2A gene expression with OS in MB in general, and with group 3 survival specifically.

Statistical tools and analyses

Experiments were performed with at least three biologic replicates. Data reported as the mean ± SD or standard error of the mean (SEM) as deemed appropriate (52). To demonstrate the population-level mean and variation, SDs are rarely preferred, however occasionally the variability of the estimated means (SEM) suits the situation better than the SD to infer how far the estimated mean is from the true population mean (52). Data between groups were compared using two-tailed Student’s t test as appropriate. GraphPad Prism 8.Ink software (https://www.graphpad.com) was used to plot the survival curves and survival evaluated with log-rank statistics. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Biorender.Com was used to draw schematic cartoons (XJ26ANDYMF and EU26ANE7QD).

Experimental therapeutics

Diarylmethyl-4-aminotetrahydropyran sulfonamides, ATUX-5800, and ATUX-1215, were prepared by the methods disclosed in Ohlmeyer, patent application WO2023/023594. ATUX-6156 and ATUX-6954 (diarylmethylcycloamine sulfonylureas), were prepared as described in Ohlmeyer, patent application WO2021/188949. Compounds are characterized by NMR, mass spectrometry and LC and are >98% purity. Details of structure-activity relationships will be reported elsewhere. In vitro microsome stability studies were carried out by Eurofins. In vivo mouse pharmacokinetic studies were carried out by Charles River Laboratories.
