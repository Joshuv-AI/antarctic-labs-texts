PSKR1-mediated phosphorylation of βCA2 activates its carbonic anhydrase activity to integrate CO2 and heat signals for enhanced thermotolerance in tomato
Authors: Jianrong Lv, Anran Wang, Chenxin Yue, Haiming Ying, Shuting Ding, Changqi Wu, Wenshan Zai, Shaoyong Huang, Chengxi Li, Feng Wan, Fei Li, Huan Liu, Christine H Foyer, Liang Wang, Kai Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlants are increasingly subjected to concurrent heat stress and elevated CO2; however, how they perceive and integrate these combined stresses remains poorly understood. Here, we identify the β-carbonic anhydrase βCA2 as a central signaling hub in tomato that decodes these combined cues. We demonstrate that βCA2 physically interacts with the receptor kinase PSKR1 at the plasma membrane and that PSKR1-mediated phosphorylation of βCA2 is significantly enhanced under simultaneous elevated CO2 and heat stress. We further identify Ser231 as the crucial phosphorylation site on βCA2. Phosphorylation at Ser231 activates βCA2 and is essential for the accumulation of phosphatidic acid, a lipid signaling molecule that protects plants from heat damage. These findings provide new insight into the complex integration of environmental stress signals through βCA2 and underscore the critical role of CO2–heat interactions in plant stress responses. Our work not only deepens understanding of plant adaptation to climate change but also provides potential targets for developing climate-resilient crops.Key words: elevated CO2, thermotolerance, β-carbonic anhydrase, phytosulfokine receptor 1, phosphorylation, phosphatidic acid

Introduction

Plants, as sessile organisms, are inherently vulnerable to environmental stress. Rapid climate change poses major risks to crop growth, with elevated CO2 and heat stress acting as major drivers (Chaudhry and Sidhu, 2022). The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change projects that atmospheric CO2 concentrations may reach 700–1000 μmol mol−1 by the end of this century, alongside a global temperature increase exceeding 2.0°C (Lee et al., 2023). However, most studies have examined these factors in isolation. Moreover, although elevated CO2 is often considered beneficial for C3 plants (Ainsworth and Long, 2005), this advantage is increasingly challenged by concurrent heat stress, threatening global food production (Hasanuzzaman et al., 2013). Understanding how plants perceive and adapt to the combined effects of elevated CO2 and temperature is therefore crucial for ensuring crop resilience and food security.

The interaction between elevated CO2 and heat is complex and varies across species. In many cases, elevated CO2 partially mitigates heat-induced damage in both C3 and C4 plants (Li et al., 2015; Ahammed et al., 2021; Selim et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024). For instance, in Arabidopsis thaliana, elevated CO2 reduces heat-induced biomass loss from 76% to 52% (Zinta et al., 2014). In tomato, CO2 enrichment to 700 μmol mol−1 increases fruit yield by 29%, along with improvements in total sugar content, Brix, and other quality traits (Rangaswamy et al., 2021). Similarly, in potato, concurrent elevated CO2 and heat boost tuber yield by 20.3%, offsetting the negative effects of heat alone (Lee et al., 2020). Elevated CO2 also sustains biomass in soybean and alters seed fatty acid composition (Palacios et al., 2019). However, this protective effect is not universal. In wheat, although elevated CO2 maintains photosynthetic rates at 40°C, it fails to prevent a catastrophic grain yield loss of approximately 40% (Chavan et al., 2019). These findings highlight both the potential and limitations of elevated CO2 in alleviating heat stress, and call for in-depth dissection of the molecular, physiological, and metabolic mechanisms that mediate this response to enhance crop resilience and quality.

Plants have evolved complex mechanisms to cope with heat stress under elevated CO2 by integrating physiological and molecular signaling. For example, in tomato, elevated CO2 triggers RBOH1-dependent apoplastic H2O2 accumulation, promoting stomatal closure to balance water retention with thermal regulation (Zhang et al., 2019). Additionally, elevated CO2 increases the thermal optimum of photosynthesis and suppresses photorespiration, thereby sustaining carbon assimilation and protecting photosystems from heat damage (Pan et al., 2018). These physiological changes are driven by dynamic hormonal and lipid signaling networks. For instance, ethylene-mediated activation of ERF1 upregulates heat shock proteins (HSP70 and HSP90) to enhance protein stability and stress protection (Pan et al., 2019). Concurrently, elevated CO2 reprograms lipid metabolism to maintain membrane integrity, notably by stimulating phosphatidic acid (PA) production, a key lipid that activates stress response pathways (Park et al., 2023). Ultimately, these pathways strengthen plant antioxidant systems, effectively mitigating oxidative damage, a hallmark of heat stress (Ahammed et al., 2021). However, current understanding remains fragmented and sometimes contradictory. For example, CO2-induced stomatal closure conserves water (Xu et al., 2016) but also reduces evaporative cooling, potentially exacerbating heat stress (Mishra et al., 2008). These observations underscore the need to focus on the initial perception and integration of CO2 and heat signals, as evidence suggests that CO2, as a gaseous signaling molecule, can rapidly modulate HT1 kinase activity (Hashimoto et al., 2006) and dephosphorylation of the plasma membrane H+-ATPase (Ando et al., 2022) within minutes, pointing to upstream sensing or rapid-response mechanisms that enable the simultaneous perception of elevated CO2 and heat.

CO2 perception and signaling in plants are complex. After entering cells, CO2 is rapidly hydrated to HCO3− and H+ by carbonic anhydrases (CAs) (Hines et al., 2021). This initial conversion is critical, as Arabidopsis β-type CA loss-of-function mutants are insensitive to elevated CO2 (Hu et al., 2010). Bicarbonate is then perceived by downstream proteins, such as RHC1 (Tian et al., 2015) or mitogen-activated protein kinases (Takahashi et al., 2022), which regulate key processes such as stomatal movements. CAs are ancient, conserved zinc-containing metalloenzymes present across organisms, from prokaryotes to mammals (Rudenko and Ivanov, 2021). In algae and higher plants, they comprise α, β, and γ classes (Rudenko et al., 2021). βCAs are highly expressed in leaves, second only to Rubisco, accounting for 0.5%–2% of soluble protein (DiMario et al., 2017). As enzymes that catalyze CO2 hydration, βCAs are strong candidates for sensing CO2 and shaping plant responses to elevated atmospheric CO2.

Emerging evidence also links βCAs to heat responses, with increased transcript levels and enzymatic activity under heat stress in species such as rice (Tianpei et al., 2015) and Pennisetum glaucum (Kaul et al., 2011). Beyond their roles in stomatal regulation and photosynthesis, which bolster heat resistance, βCAs can mitigate oxidative damage and modulate lipid metabolism during heat exposure (DiMario et al., 2017; Polishchuk, 2021). These findings suggest that βCAs may contribute to elevated CO2-induced thermotolerance; however, the mechanisms linking CO2 signals to downstream pathways remain unclear. Given that βCAs are structural enzymes, they likely rely on downstream mechanisms for signal transduction. Consistently, recent work in tomato highlights a role for βCAs in basal immunity, mediated by protein–protein interactions and post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation (Hu et al., 2021). Notably, receptor-like kinases (RLKs), key components of plant phosphorylation signaling, respond rapidly to external environmental changes (Soltabayeva et al., 2022). Together, the above evidence underscores the importance of defining how βCAs are linked to elevated CO2-driven heat tolerance.

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), a globally important C3 crop and a model system for stress biology, provide an ideal platform for studying combined stress signaling. In this study, we identify βCA2 as a central regulator of elevated CO2-mediated thermotolerance. We show that βCA2 is directly phosphorylated by the receptor kinase PSKR1, with Ser231 serving as the key phosphorylation site required for heat resistance. Mechanistically, this signaling module operates upstream of the lipid messenger PA, whose accumulation is essential for enhanced thermotolerance. These results establish a signaling pathway linking CO2-responsive βCA2 to stress adaptation, offering potential targets for engineering climate-resilient crops and advancing our understanding of environmental signal integration in plants.

Results

The tomato carbonic anhydrase βCA2 positively regulates elevated CO2-induced heat tolerance

We established a standardized tomato system in which elevated CO2 confers clear thermotolerance. Exposure of wild-type (WT) plants to heat stress (HS, 45°C) under elevated CO2 (Ele, 800 μmol mol−1) significantly mitigated wilting and reduced electrolyte leakage compared with control conditions (ambient CO2 [Amb], 400 μmol mol−1, 25°C) (Figures 1A and 1B), validating this system for genetic and molecular analyses.

To identify the βCA involved, we examined the expression of four βCA homologs. Although all were induced by HS, only βCA2 was specifically upregulated under the combined condition of HS and Ele (Figure 1C). We then generated knockout mutants (βca2#1 and βca2#2) and overexpression lines (OE-βCA2#1 and OE-βCA2#6) (Supplemental Figures 1A and 1B). Following HS, βca2 mutants exhibited more severe wilting than the WT and OE lines and elevated CO2 reduced damage in the WT and OE plants but had no effect in the mutant lines (Figure 1D). These phenotypes were corroborated by electrolyte leakage and Fv/Fm measurements (Figures 1E and 1F). At 25°C, electrolyte leakage and Fv/Fm did not differ significantly among the lines, with or without CO2 treatment. Heat damage in βca2 mutants could not be alleviated by elevated CO2, and the extent of damage alleviation in OE lines was lower than that in the WT. We further quantified total CA catalytic activity across genotypes (Figure 1G). At 25°C, consistent with the transcript profiles (Figure 1C), elevated CO2 induced a significant increase in CA activity in the WT, a response that was absent in βca2 mutants. Although HS alone increased CA activity in all lines, the additional increase conferred by elevated CO2 was observed only in the WT. In contrast, this CO2-driven enhancement was abolished in the mutants and was less pronounced in the OE lines than in the WT. These results demonstrate that βCA2 is required for the positive regulation of elevated CO2-mediated heat tolerance in tomato.

βCA2 interacts with PSKR1 at the plasma membrane in a CO2-responsive manner

To explore the mechanisms underlying elevated CO2-induced thermotolerance involving βCA2, we performed immunoprecipitation (IP)–mass spectrometry (MS) analysis using βCA2 as bait and identified 283 potential interactors (Supplemental Table 2). Given the plasma membrane localization of βCA2, confirmed by protoplast extraction (Figure 2A), we focused on proteins annotated as plasma membrane-localized in the UniProt database. Among 25 candidates (Supplemental Table 2), we identified phytosulfokine (PSK) receptor 1 (PSKR1), a leucine-rich repeat (LRR) RLK. RLKs are key transmembrane proteins that link environmental stimuli to cellular responses (Soltabayeva et al., 2022). Consistent with this role, PSKR1 regulates a broad range of processes related to plant growth, development, and stress tolerance (Zhang et al., 2018; Ding et al., 2023, 2024).

AlphaFold3 modeling predicted a high-confidence interaction between βCA2 and PSKR1 (pTM score > 0.5; Supplemental Figure 2), and a bimolecular fluorescence complementation assay confirmed this interaction at the plasma membrane (Figure 2B). Given the central role of CO2 in this physiological response, we next investigated whether this interaction is influenced by CO2 levels. To test this, we supplemented assays with bicarbonate to mimic elevated CO2 conditions. CoIP, split-luciferase, and pull-down assays all confirmed a direct interaction between βCA2 and PSKR1, with a significant increase under elevated CO2 (Figure 2). This was quantified as a 1.5-fold increase in the coIP assay and was corroborated by a stronger luminescence signal in the split-luciferase assay. These findings identify PSKR1 as a plasma membrane partner of βCA2, with their interaction modulated by CO2 levels.

PSKR1 acts upstream of βCA2 to mediate elevated CO2-induced thermotolerance

To investigate the role of PSKR1 in thermotolerance, we used two PSKR1 knockout mutants (pskr1#2 and pskr1#4) (Hu et al., 2023) and a previously generated OE line (OE-PSKR1#3) (Ding et al., 2023). After HS, pskr1 mutants exhibited increased heat sensitivity, whereas OE-PSKR1 plants displayed enhanced tolerance. Notably, the protective effect of elevated CO2 against heat damage was significantly reduced in pskr1 mutants compared with the WT (Supplemental Figure 3).

To determine the genetic relationship between βCA2 and PSKR1, we generated the βca2 pskr1#1 double mutant through hybridization and compared its thermotolerance with that of the single mutants and the WT (Figures 3A–3C). At 25°C, no significant differences in electrolyte leakage or Fv/Fm were observed among genotypes. After HS, however, all mutants were more thermosensitive than the WT. Crucially, elevated CO2 significantly mitigated heat damage in the WT and, to a lesser extent, in the pskr1 mutant; however, this effect was completely lost in both βca2 and βca2 pskr1 double mutants. We extended the enzymatic analysis to pskr1 and double mutants (Figure 3D). At 25°C, the CO2-induced upregulation of CA activity was observed only in the WT. The additional increase triggered by elevated CO2 under HS was nearly abolished in the pskr1 mutant, mirroring the loss of function observed in the βca2 mutant. Notably, the enzymatic profile of the double mutant more closely resembled that of the βca2 single mutant. These findings indicate that the βca2 pskr1 double mutant phenocopies the βca2 mutant, suggesting that βCA2 functions downstream of PSKR1 in the signaling cascade that integrates CO2 and heat signals.

PSKR1 phosphorylates βCA2 at the conserved Ser231 site to enhance CO2-mediated thermotolerance

We hypothesized that PSKR1 regulates βCA2 activity through direct phosphorylation, consistent with its role as a kinase. To test this, we first examined the in vivo phosphorylation status of βCA2. Consistent with bioinformatic predictions of potential phosphorylation sites (Supplemental Figure 4A), βCA2 phosphorylation was detected, induced by HS, and further enhanced by elevated CO2, with the highest levels observed under the combined treatment (Figure 4A). Notably, this stress-induced phosphorylation was abolished in the pskr1 mutant, demonstrating that PSKR1 is required for this modification in vivo.

To identify the phosphorylation site, we performed an in vitro kinase assay followed by liquid chromatography (LC)–MS/MS, which revealed three candidate residues: Ser41, Thr49, and Ser231 (Supplemental Figure 4; Supplemental Table 7). Among these, only Ser231 lies within the Pro_CA domain, which is critical for βCA2 catalytic activity (Figures 4B and 4C). We generated site-directed recombinant proteins (βCA2S41A, βCA2T49A, βCA2S231A, and βCA2S41/T49/S231A, with the indicated residues substituted with Ala) fused to a glutathione S-transferase (GST) tag and confirmed that Ser231 is the primary phosphorylation site. S231A of βCA2almost completely abolished PSKR1-mediated phosphorylation in vitro (Figure 4D). Furthermore, elevated CO2 directly enhanced phosphorylation at Ser231, an effect that was lost in βCA2S231A both in vitro (Figure 4E) and in transient Nicotiana benthamiana expression assays (Figure 4F). Importantly, the Ser231A mutation did not affect βCA2 subcellular localization or its interaction with PSKR1 (Supplemental Figure 5), indicating that the observed effect is specific to phosphorylation.

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that Ser231 is highly conserved across diverse plant species, underscoring its functional significance (Supplemental Figure 6). To investigate whether phosphorylation at this site directly modulates catalytic activity, we performed in vitro enzymatic assays using purified recombinant GST-tagged proteins (Figure 4G). The βCA2S231A variant exhibited reduced enzymatic activity compared with βCA2, whereas βCA2S231D displayed enhanced catalytic efficiency. To further assess the physiological relevance of this modification, we performed transient complementation assays in the βca2 mutant background. Under HS conditions, plants expressing the non-phosphorylatable βCA2S231A exhibited severe wilting, similar to the empty vector control. In stark contrast, plants complemented with βCA2 or the phosphomimetic βCA2S231D (Ser-to-Asp substitution) displayed significantly enhanced thermotolerance (Figure 4H). These observations were supported by electrolyte leakage measurements, which showed reduced membrane damage in the complemented lines (Figure 4I). Together, these results demonstrate that PSKR1-mediated phosphorylation of βCA2 at the conserved Ser231 site functions as a molecular switch that translates combined environmental stress signals into a protective physiological response.

βCA2 regulates CO2-responsive transcription and PA accumulation to mediate CO2-dependent thermotolerance

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms of βCA2, we performed a transcriptomic analysis (NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA): PRJNA1238215) of WT and βca2#1 plants under HS in ambient CO2 or elevated CO2. Among 36,374 detected transcripts, the abundances of only 1,335 differed between the WT and βca2 under control conditions (Supplemental Table 3), indicating minimal basal effects of the mutation. Following HS, however, 8,533 transcripts showed changed abundance in the WT and βca2 under different CO2 conditions (Figure 5A; Supplemental Table 4). We identified 488 genes that required functional βCA2 for heat-induced upregulation specifically under elevated CO2 (Figure 5B; Supplemental Table 5), which we termed βCA2-dependent CO2-induced genes. A heatmap of these genes further confirmed that their upregulation required both functional βCA2 and elevated CO2 (Figure 5C).

Given the substantial changes in membrane composition and fluidity under HS, we performed KEGG pathway analysis of the 488 genes. The analysis strongly pointed to pathways related to cell membrane composition and lipid signaling, including the phosphatidylinositol signaling system and inositol phosphate metabolism (Figure 5D; Supplemental Table 6). These findings led us to focus on PA, a key phospholipid that functions both as a precursor for the biosynthesis of other membrane lipids and as a signaling molecule in plant stress responses. We next examined the expression of genes involved in PA metabolism and found that PLDb1, which encodes a PA-synthesizing enzyme, and PAH1L, which encodes a PA-regulating phosphatase, were expressed at significantly higher levels in the WT than in βca2#1 under heat and elevated CO2 (Figure 5E).

Consistent with the gene expression data, PA levels increased in both genotypes under heat, but only the WT showed a significant additional increase under elevated CO2 (Figure 5F), suggesting that PA contributes to elevated CO2-induced thermotolerance. To test this hypothesis, we supplied exogenous PA to plants. This treatment enhanced thermotolerance in the WT and βca2 mutant, reducing wilting and electrolyte leakage irrespective of CO2 concentration (Figures 5G and 5H), thereby establishing PA as a key downstream component of thermotolerance. In addition, we measured PA levels in the complementation lines. In the βca2#1 background, CO2-enhanced PA accumulation under HS was restored by expression of either βCA2 or the phosphomimetic βCA2S231D. In contrast, this effect was abolished in lines expressing the non-phosphorylatable βCA2S231A (Supplemental Figure 6). These findings demonstrate that phosphorylation of βCA2 at Ser231 is a critical regulatory step that enables CO2-responsive PA accumulation, ultimately enhancing plant thermotolerance.

Discussion

As climate change accelerates, plants increasingly face the compounded stress of elevated CO2 and heat, but how these signals are perceived and integrated remains poorly understood. Our work addresses this question by establishing a signaling pathway in tomato that links environmental sensing to physiological adaptation. We demonstrate that βCA2 acts as a signal integrator that converts CO2 and heat signals into enhanced thermotolerance. Specifically, PSKR1, an LRR RLK, phosphorylates βCA2 at Ser231, and this modification is significantly enhanced under combined CO2 and heat stress. This phosphorylation functions as a molecular switch that activates downstream signaling pathways, particularly PA accumulation, which is essential for the heat stress response (Figure 6). The following sections provide further evidence supporting this mechanism.

Elevated CO2 enhances PSKR1-mediated βCA2 phosphorylation, which in turn regulates lipid metabolism to promote PA accumulation, leading to improved thermotolerance in tomato. Solid black arrows indicate direct interactions, whereas dashed black arrows represent relationships that require further validation.

First, our findings identify βCA2 as a key sensor–transducer in the context of combined CO2 and heat stress. Genetic experiments and total CA activity analyses confirmed its essential role, as βca2 mutants were more sensitive to heat and failed to benefit from elevated CO2 (Figure 1). Although the enzymatic activity assay used a surrogate substrate, previous studies have shown that the esterase activity of CA shares the same zinc hydroxide mechanism within the active site as the physiological hydration of CO2 (Tavallali et al., 2009). Therefore, the observed increase in activity implies a conformational change in the active site that facilitates substrate access or catalysis. While elevated CO2 is known to mitigate heat stress (Ahammed et al., 2021), the underlying mechanisms remain fragmented and sometimes contradictory (Pan et al., 2018, 2019; Zhang et al., 2019; Park et al., 2023), highlighting a critical gap in understanding how these dual signals are sensed and integrated. Our study addresses this gap by shifting the focus upstream and identifying βCA2 as an initial signaling molecule that decodes combined environmental cues. Although metabolites such as sugars can act as secondary signals (Wang et al., 2024), the membrane-permeable nature of CO₂ makes it an ideal primary signal for directly initiating stress responses. The role of CO2 as a gaseous signaling molecule is well established (Xu et al., 2016), and CAs are strong candidates as CO2 sensors because of their high catalytic efficiency in CO2 hydration (Supuran, 2008). Although βCAs have been increasingly linked to stress responses, including heat (Tianpei et al., 2015) and salinity (DiMario et al., 2017), often through their antioxidant properties (Polishchuk, 2021), their precise signaling roles have remained unclear. Our study begins to address this gap by identifying βCA2 as a key mediator of CO2-enhanced thermotolerance. A key question is what confers specificity to βCA2 over its homologs βCA1, βCA3, and βCA4. Our transcriptomic data showed that, while all four βCA homologs were induced by heat, consistent with previous reports (Wang and Zhan, 2024), only βCA2 was specifically upregulated under the combined stress of elevated CO2 and heat. Moreover, subcellular localization analyses confirmed that βCA2 resides at the plasma membrane, consistent with a role in signal transduction. Taken together, its distinct expression pattern and membrane localization position βCA2 as a key node for integrating CO2 and heat signals.

Second, we identify a CO2-sensitive signaling module involving the receptor kinase PSKR1 and the enzyme βCA2 (Figures 2, 3, and 4). How βCAs transduce signals remains a central question in plant biology. Receptor kinases are known to phosphorylate βCAs, thereby regulating complex processes such as development (Huang et al., 2017) and immunity (Hu et al., 2021). This concept is further supported by studies on human βCAs, in which phosphorylation acts as a potent switch that can dramatically alter catalytic activity and inhibitor binding (Huang et al., 2023). On this basis, we hypothesized that βCA2 integrates CO2 and heat signals through direct phosphorylation by an upstream kinase. We identified PSKR1 (Supplemental Table 2), a member of the LRR RLK family (Morillo and Tax, 2006). Its plasma membrane localization (Hartmann et al., 2014) makes it well suited to perceive extracellular signals and transduce them into the cell. Our results confirm a direct interaction between PSKR1 and βCA2, which was significantly strengthened under elevated CO2 (Figure 2). A previous genome-wide analysis showed that members of the PSKR gene family in rice are transcriptionally responsive to heat stress (Nagar et al., 2020); however, the detailed mechanistic basis underlying the involvement of PSKRs in heat stress responses has remained unclear. Our study addresses this gap by demonstrating that PSKR1 mediates elevated CO2-induced thermotolerance through phosphorylation of βCA2 (Figure 3 and Supplemental Figure 3). PSKR1 has a defined peptide ligand, PSK, a plant hormone involved in stress responses (Zhang et al., 2018). PSK has been shown to promote the growth of A. thaliana under heat stress (Wu et al., 2025), and our transcriptomic data further reveal upregulation of PSK precursor genes (Solyc02g092110) under combined elevated CO2 and heat treatment, reinforcing activation of the PSKR1 pathway under dual stress. Whether PSK itself modulates elevated CO2-enhanced thermotolerance remains an open question, and future studies using PSK-deficient lines and exogenous PSK application will help clarify its role.

Phosphorylation events can occur within minutes in plants (Ando et al., 2022), supporting our hypothesis that phosphorylation serves as a rapid and critical molecular switch in plant responses to environmental stress. The dynamic regulation of protein phosphorylation by environmental cues is a cornerstone of plant signaling. For instance, PSKR1 phosphorylation status is modulated by aluminum ions to regulate toxicity sensing (Xu et al., 2025), whereas GRACE1-mediated phosphorylation of βCA3 responds to pathogen signals (Hu et al., 2021). Our work extends this concept by demonstrating that the PSKR1–βCA2 phosphorylation event acts as an integrator that responds synergistically to elevated CO2 and heat (Figure 4). We identify Ser231 as the key phosphorylation site on βCA2, and biochemical analyses confirmed that phosphorylation at this residue directly enhances βCA2 enzymatic activity, establishing Ser231 as a critical site governing both signal perception and catalytic output. In addition, the inability of the βCA2S231A variant to rescue thermotolerance, contrasted with the restoration observed with βCA2S231D, confirms that phosphorylation—rather than the mere presence of βCA2—functions as the critical regulatory switch. This phosphorylation-dependent gating mechanism, in which a kinase activates enzymatic function, represents a recurring theme in signal transduction.

Third, our transcriptomic analysis revealed that a specific subset of heat-responsive genes requires βCA2 for induction under elevated CO2, with significant enrichment in lipid signaling pathways (Figure 5). The plasma membrane is a primary site for sensing environmental stress (Hofmann, 2009), and, given the membrane localization of PSKR1 and βCA2, it is logical that downstream responses involve membrane-associated pathways. KEGG analysis of βCA2-dependent, CO2-responsive genes showed significant enrichment in pathways such as phosphatidylinositol signaling, glycerolipid metabolism, and fatty acid biosynthesis (Supplemental Table 6). Among these, genes regulating PA homeostasis, including PLDb1 and PAH1L, have been reported to play important roles in plant heat stress responses (Urrea Castellanos et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2025). Notably, the rapid kinetics of the heat stress response, in which PLD activation and PA accumulation occur within minutes of heat exposure (Mishkind et al., 2009), strongly implicate PA as a key early signaling component in this system. PA is a well-established lipid signaling molecule central to plant stress responses (Testerink and Munnik, 2005; Kong et al., 2024). Previous studies have linked PA surges to thermotolerance across species. In maize, PA is the only lipid significantly increased under heat stress (Chen et al., 2010). In Arabidopsis, PA promotes thermotolerance through ethylene, Heat Shock Factor (HSF)HSF, or HSP pathways (Wang and Huang, 2017). In tall fescue, PA-enhanced thermotolerance has been attributed to lipid reprogramming (Zhang et al., 2023b). In addition, PA is closely linked to CO2 signaling, with supplemental CO2 promoting PA biosynthesis via PLD pathways and enhancing chilling tolerance in pepper (Park et al., 2023). Our results establish a direct link between PSKR1–βCA2 signaling and PA accumulation, showing that the increase in PA levels under combined stress depends on βCA2 and, crucially, on its phosphorylation at Ser231 (Supplemental Figure 7). Exogenous PA application rescued thermotolerance in the βca2 mutant, placing PA downstream of βCA2 in the signaling cascade. To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly link PA accumulation to the PSKR1–βCA2 signaling axis, providing new insight into lipid-mediated regulation of plant heat stress responses.

In summary, our study demonstrates a signaling mechanism that couples CO2 sensing to heat protection in tomato, redefining βCA2 as a signal-integrating node that links environmental perception to stress adaptation. This work addresses the open question of how plants perceive and integrate CO2 and temperature cues. Beyond advancing mechanistic understanding, the PSKR1–βCA2–PA module and its specific phosphorylation site provide tractable targets for improving thermotolerance in crops, with lipid signaling serving as a key readout for selection or engineering. We anticipate that further mapping of the spatial and temporal dynamics of this module across tissues and environmental conditions will support climate-resilient breeding and cultivation strategies in a warming, CO2-rich world.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Tomato (S. lycopersicum cv. ‘Condine Red’) was used in this study. Plants were grown at 25°C/22°C under a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle, with 80% ambient humidity and a light intensity of 500 μmol m−2 s−1. Plants were initially grown in trays and then transferred to pots containing a peat:vermiculite mixture (3:1, v/v). Approximately 5-week-old plants at the 4- to 5-leaf stage were used for each treatment.

For CRISPR–Cas9-mediated generation of βca2 mutants, target sequences were designed using the CRISPR tool (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/CRISPR2/) and cloned into the pHEE401 vector. The construct was transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and then introduced into tomato. Mutations were confirmed by DNA sequencing, and homozygous F1 plants were used for further analyses. To obtain OE lines, βCA2 cDNA amplified by PCR was inserted into the pAC004-35S-HA vector. The βca2 pskr1 double mutant and OE-βCA2 pskr1 plants were obtained by crossing. For site-directed mutagenesis of βCA2, the desired mutations were introduced by PCR, and the resulting sequences were inserted into pGWB417, followed by transformation into the βca2 mutant by vacuum infiltration. Homozygosity of these lines was confirmed by PCR-based sequencing. OE-PSKR1 and OE-βCA2 lines were verified by western blot analysis using an anti-hemagglutinin (HA) antibody, and mutant lines carrying point mutations in βCA2 were identified using an anti-MYC antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Primer sequences are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

CO2, heat stress, and exogenous PA treatments

In this study, tomato and tobacco plants were grown in climate chambers (Qiushi, China) under CO2 concentrations of 400 or 800 μmol mol−1, representing ambient CO2 (Amb) and elevated CO2 (Ele) conditions, respectively. After 2 d of acclimation, half of the plants were transferred to separate climate chambers and subjected to HS treatment (45°C), whereas the other half were maintained at the control temperature (25°C). Plants were sampled after 4 h for RT–qPCR, relative electrolyte leakage measurements (Pan et al., 2019), and chlorophyll fluorescence analysis of the maximum quantum yield of Photosystem II (PSII) (Fv/Fm) (Pan et al., 2019). After 6 h, representative plants were imaged. For exogenous PA treatments, tomato plants were sprayed twice with 20 μM PA before HS.

RT–qPCR and RNA sequencing analysis

Leaves of 4-week-old WT and βca2 plants were collected for RNA extraction using an Easy Plant RNA Extraction Kit (Zhejiang Easy-Do Biotech). Total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Kit (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China). RT–qPCR was performed with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Takara, Japan) on a LightCycler 480 II detection system (Roche, Germany). RNA sequencing libraries were prepared and sequenced in paired-end mode on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform by LC-Bio Technologies (Hangzhou, China).

Carbonic anhydrase activity assay

Carbonic anhydrase (CA) activity was quantified using a commercial assay kit (catalog no. BC5445; Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The assay is based on the esterase activity of CA, which catalyzes the hydrolysis of 4-nitrophenyl acetate to produce p-nitrophenol. The reaction rate was determined by monitoring the increase in absorbance at 405 nm. For in vivo analysis, leaf samples were collected from the same leaf position of the respective tomato lines after 4 h of exposure to different temperature and CO2 treatments. For in vitro analysis, purified recombinant proteins were used directly as the enzyme source. The reaction was initiated by adding substrate solution to the samples and incubating at 37°C for 5 min, after which absorbance was measured immediately. CA activity was calculated based on a standard curve and normalized to total sample mass (U g⁻¹) or the amount of purified protein used (U mg⁻¹).

IP–MS analysis

Leaves of 4-week-old WT and OE-βCA2 plants were collected. Protein extraction was performed using a native extraction buffer as described previously (Zhang et al., 2023a). The extracts were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was incubated with anti-HA beads at 4°C for 2–3 h, washed 3 times, and resuspended in 5× SDS loading buffer. After heating at 95°C for 10 min, proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE and analyzed by LC–MS/MS.

Vector construction

For BiFC assays, coding sequences were cloned into the p2YC and p2YN vectors. For split-luciferase assays, the pCAMBIA-GW-nLUC and pCAMBIA-GW-cLUC vectors were used. For coIP, subcellular localization, and in vivo phosphorylation assays, PSKR1 and βCA2 cDNAs were recombined into pFGC1008-35S-HA-C and pAC402-35S-EGFP-C, respectively. For pull-down assays and in vitro phosphorylation site verification, βCA2 and PSKR1JK cDNAs—encoding the inner juxtamembrane domain and the protein kinase domain—were inserted into pGEX-4T-1 and pET28a, respectively. Point mutant plasmids were generated by site-directed mutagenesis (New England Biolabs) and then introduced into the pGWB417 vector. Primer sequences are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

Protein–protein interaction assays

For BiFC assays, Agrobacterium cultures were co-infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves as previously described (Zhang et al., 2018). Fluorescence signals were detected using a Carl Zeiss LSM 780 confocal microscope. For coIP assays, Agrobacterium was infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves for 36 h. Leaves were then treated with 1 mM HCO3− for 1 h and harvested. Total leaf proteins were extracted and incubated with GFP-Trap magnetic agarose for 2–3 h at 4°C. After washing with buffer, proteins were detected by western blotting using anti-GFP or anti-HA antibodies. For split-luciferase assays, PSKR1-nLUC and βCA2-cLUC were cotransfected into N. benthamiana leaves as previously described (Wang et al., 2025). After 48 h, leaves were treated with 1 mM HCO3− for 1 h. Leaf discs were excised and incubated in luciferin solution, and interaction intensity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA). For pull-down assays, GST-βCA2 and GST-EV fusion proteins were expressed and purified from Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3). The fusion proteins were incubated with glutathione agarose beads for 15 min at room temperature, followed by incubation with His-PSKR1JK for 2–3 h at 4°C. The beads were then washed, and bound proteins were analyzed by western blotting using an anti-His antibody.

Subcellular localization

βCA2 was cloned into the pAC402 vector and transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaves as previously described (Zhang et al., 2018). After 36–48 h of infiltration, protoplasts were isolated (Li et al., 2025). GFP fluorescence was observed and imaged using an A1 confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon, Japan) with excitation at 488 nm and emission at 500–540 nm.

Phosphorylation assay

In vitro phosphorylation site verification was performed as previously described (Liang et al., 2025). His-PSKR1JK and GST-βCA2 were purified and incubated in kinase buffer with gentle shaking for 2 h at room temperature. The reaction was terminated by adding 5× SDS loading buffer, and proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE. The in-gel GST-βCA2 band was excised and analyzed by LC–MS using an LTQ Orbitrap Elite system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For in vivo phosphorylation analysis, the corresponding constructs were transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaves and then subjected to different CO2 concentrations or temperature treatments. Total leaf proteins were extracted using IP buffer and incubated with GFP-Trap magnetic agarose (for tobacco samples) or HA-Trap magnetic agarose (for tomato samples). After rotation for 2 h at 4°C, proteins were analyzed by western blotting using anti-pSer/pThr or anti-pSer antibodies.

Statistical analysis

At least three independent biological replicates from different plants were included for each experiment. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Differences among means were analyzed using Tukey’s test at the 5% significance level. All bar charts were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.0.
