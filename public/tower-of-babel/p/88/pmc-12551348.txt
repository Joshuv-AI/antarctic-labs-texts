Quantitative analysis of time-related parameters in uroflowmetry curve patterns in patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia and bladder outlet obstruction: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Xiao Zeng, Hong Shen, Deyi Luo, Tao Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study was designed to quantitatively analyze time-related parameters in uroflowmetry curve patterns in patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia and bladder outlet obstruction, and to investigate their potential clinical implications.MethodsA total of 63 patients were included in this retrospective, single-center study. The benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) with benign prostatic obstruction(BPO) group consisted of 37 patients, while the nromal group included 26 individuals. Urodynamic study traces and clinical data were analyzed based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. Time-related parameters, including: Time to Qmax (T1), The time from Qmax to the end of uroflow (T2), and Uroflow time (T3 = T1 + T2), as well as corresponding ratios, were quantified and compared between the two groups.ResultsSignificant differences were observed in several time-related parameters between the BPH with BPO group and the normal group. There following parameters demonstrated statistically significant differences: T1 [5 (3.25, 7) s vs. 8.5 (6, 11.5) s, p = 0.00], T2 [23 (15, 34.5) s vs. 10 (6.75, 13.25) s, p = 0.00], T3 [30 (20, 40.5) s vs. 19 (14.75, 23.25) s, p = 0.00], T1/T2 [0.20 (0.13, 0.30) vs. 0.89 (0.65, 1.12), p = 0.00], T1/T3 [0.18 ± 0.10 vs. 0.47 ± 0.10, p = 0.00], T2/T3 [0.82 ± 0.10 vs. 0.54 ± 0.09, p = 0.00].The BPH with BOO group exhibited a shorter time to Qmax onset but significantly prolonged overall voiding duration, a pattern that was consistent with the distinctive “comet tail-like” morphology frequently observed in their uroflowmetry curves. Furthermore, several time-related parameters were identified as potential independent risk factors for bladder outlet obstruction index (BOOI).ConclusionThe time-related parameters in uroflowmetry curve patterns in BPH patients with BPO show distinct characteristics compared to the normal group. These findings suggest that such parameters may serve as valuable diagnostic indicators for bladder outlet obstruction, though further studies with larger sample sizes are needed for confirmation.Keywords: Uroflowmetry curve pattern, Benign prostatic hyperplasia, Quantitative analysis, Bladder outlet obstruction

Introduction

According to the definition provided by the International Continence Society (ICS), uroflowmetry quantifies the urinary flow rate as the voided volume per unit time (mL/s) [1]. Uroflowmetry, a simple, non-invasive diagnostic tool, is typically performed prior to a multichannel urodynamic study (UDS), although it can also be conducted independently as a standalone assessment [2]. It is widely used as a preliminary screening modality for detecting lower urinary tract dysfunction (LUTD) [3]. However, guidelines from both the American Urological Association (AUA) and the European Association of Urology (EUA) emphasize that only pressure-flow studies (PFS) can reliably differentiate between detrusor underactivity and bladder outlet obstruction (BOO), a distinction that uroflowmetry alone is incapable of making with diagnostic accuracy [4, 5].

Uroflowmetry parameters are volume-dependent and subject to interindividual variability, with influencing factors including age, sex, voided volume (VV), voiding posture, and the patient’s psychological state at the time of testing [6, 7]. Among these parameters, the maximum flow rate (Qmax) is considered the most clinically relevant. Qmax is influenced by detrusor contractility, bladder outlet obstruction (BOO), and VV, and can be used as a screening tool for LUTD [8]. Qmax values are most reliable when VV ranges between 150 and 400 mL [9]. The ICS recommends the VOID mode for standard uroflowmetry reporting, which includes Qmax, VV, and post-void residual volume (PVR) [1]. However, recent studies have emphasized the diagnostic value of uroflow curve morphology in both children and adults presenting with BOO symptoms [10]. The shape of the uroflow curve can offer initial insights into the underlying pathophysiology of LUTD [11]. Although the ICS does not mandate the inclusion of curve patterns in standard reports, emerging evidence suggests that curve morphology provides valuable diagnostic information. Normal voiding typically results in a smooth “bell-shaped” pattern curve, a constrictive obstruction usually results in a “box-like” flow curve, BOO results in a “flattened asymmetric (streaking)” flow curve with a slowly declining end part, Detrusor underactivity (DU), which often presents clinically with abdominal straining to assist voiding, is typically associated with an “intermittent” or “wave-like” uroflowmetry curve pattern [12]. However, there is a lack of quantitative research on morphological features of uroflowmetry.

BPH is a common condition among elderly men. Affected individuals may present with benign prostatic enlargement (BPE), BPO, lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), or various combinations of these components [13]. While uroflowmetry serves as a valuable screening tool for BOO, PFS remain the gold standard for diagnosing BPO [14]. Currently, there is a limited body of research exploring the predictive value of uroflowmetry-derived parameters other than Qmax for identifying BPO, particularly those quantitative indicators related to uroflow pattern morphology. This study introduces a set of time-related parameters derived from uroflowmetry that may quantitatively characterize flow patterns, with the aim of evaluating their potential utility in predicting BPO.

Materials and methods

Study sample

This study is a single-center retrospective analysis with a relatively small sample size, and the data were collected from the Urodynamic Center of West China Hospital of Sichuan University between January 2024 and January 2025. Initially, male patients were identified through a comprehensive database search. Subsequently, two urologists with more than 10 years of experience in urodynamics independently reviewed and screened the urodynamic traces based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for the BPO group in this study were as follows:1. Male patients clinically diagnosed with BPH 2. VV during the uroflowmetry study ranging from 150 to 400 mL 3.With complete basic information 4. Clear urodynamic trace 5. With uroflow during the voiding phase in PFS 6. Bladder outlet obstruction index (BOOI) greater or equal to 40. 7.All urodynamic studies were conducted using a standard device, and all underwent rigorous quality control processes. 8. Bladder contraction index (BCI) greater or equal to 100. The inclusion criteria for the non-BPO group were as follows:1.Male patients 2.Age > 18 3.BOOI less than 20, other criteria are consistent with the inclusion criteria for the BPO group. 3. Bladder contraction index (BCI) greater or equal to 100. Exclusion criteria include: (1) History of urethral and pelvic floor surgery (2) Urethral stricture (3) Diabetes (4) History of neurological disorders or failure to meet the inclusion criteria outlined above.

Urodynamic study

Urodynamics were carried out in the setting position with a Laborie Triton equipment following GUP guidelines [1, 3]. All patients were perfused with 37 °C sterile normal saline with a filling rate in mL/min of roughly 10% of the largest voided volume (The largest voided volume can be obtained from the patient’s voiding diary).

Medical ethics

This study received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of West china hospital of Sichuan University (No.2024 − 873), and was conducted in strict adherence to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis in this study was conducted in R4.3.2, SPSS21.0, and GraphPad Prism 8 software. Data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. If the distribution of numerical data in BPH + BPO and Normal group was normal and homogeneous, the student t test was used for inter group differences test, and mean ± standard deviation (SD) was used for statistical description. Conversely, if the distribution of numerical data in BPH + BPO and Normal group was skewed or unequal, the Mann-Whitney U rank sum test was used for inter group differences test, and M(P25,P75) was used for statistical description.The Pearson correlation and Spearman rank correlation were used to assess the relationship between T1,T2,T3,T1/T2,T1/T3, T2/T3 and BOOI or BCI in BPH + BPO and Normal group, separately. Simple regression and multiple linear regression were used to screen the influencing factors of BOOI, and forest plot was used to display the results of regression analysis. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of enrolled patients

After initial screening, a total of 245 patients with eligible clinical data and UDS traces met the preliminary criteria. Following application of the full inclusion and exclusion criteria, 63 patients were ultimately included in the final analysis. The BPH with BPO group comprised 37 patients, while the normal group included 26 patients. The mean age of the BPH with BPO group was 64.54 ± 9.83 years, compared to 31.08 ± 8.27 years in the normal group, representing a statistically significant difference (p = 0.00).(Fig. 3; Table 1).

Morphological characteristics of uroflowmetry curve in BPH with BPO group and normal group

In the normal group, the uroflowmetry curve typically exhibited a smooth, symmetric “bell-shaped” pattern, with T1 and T2 being nearly equal. The curve was symmetrically distributed around the Qmax, reflecting a normal voiding pattern. In contrast, the BPH with BPO group demonstrated a characteristic “comet tail” shaped uroflowmetry pattern, marked by a pronounced asymmetry between T1 and T2. Specifically, T1 was substantially shorter than T2, indicating that Qmax was reached rapidly, while both T2 and T3 were significantly prolonged. (Figs. 1 and 2)

Discussions

Free uroflowmetry is widely utilized in the screening of LUTDs due to its non-invasive nature, low cost, and ease of implementation. It is particularly valuable for the diagnosis and evaluation of treatment outcomes in patients with BPH. Currently, neither the ICS nor the SUFU guidelines for interpreting free uroflowmetry results emphasize the diagnostic significance of the uroflow curve morphology. However, previous studies have demonstrated that uroflowmetry curves possess meaningful clinical value. For instance, some researchers argue that diagnostic interpretation should not be limited to conventional parameters alone, but should also consider the morphological characteristics of the flow curve [15]. Other investigators further propose that the flow curve shape and traditional uroflowmetry parameters are complementary in clinical assessment. Nevertheless, the statistical utility of the flow curve pattern remains limited, as it cannot currently be expressed in a standardized mathematical format [16]. Some pediatric urologists have noted that terminology used to describe the temporal morphology of the uroflowmetry curve in children varies considerably across clinical settings. They further emphasize that the interpretation of uroflow curve patterns is often subjective [17–19]. As a result, the International Children’s Continence Society (ICCS) recommends employing descriptive verbal terminology to characterize uroflowmetry curves, rather than relying solely on objective parameters. Consequently, the interpretation of uroflow patterns is often regarded as more of an “art” than a strict science, relying heavily on the clinical experience and judgment of the interpreting physician [21].

Although the clinical utility of uroflowmetry curve morphology remains a topic of debate, it is widely acknowledged that certain LUTDs present with characteristic and recognizable uroflowmetry patterns in clinical practice. For instance, a normal uroflowmetry curve typically appears as a smooth, symmetrical “arc-shaped” or “bell-shaped” profile. A constrictive obstruction, such as urethral stricture, generally produces a “plateau-like” or “box-shaped” curve with a relatively flat peak and prolonged flow time. In contrast, a compressive obstruction, such as BPO, often results in a “flattened, asymmetric” curve characterized by a rapid ascent to Qmax followed by a slowly tapering tail. In patients with detrusor underactivity (DU), the uroflowmetry curve typically demonstrates a pattern of “intermittent urination” or a “wave-like” morphology. Notably, the troughs of this wave-like pattern consistently return to the baseline (0 mL/s), indicating complete cessation of flow between contractions. This pattern should be distinguished from the “wavy” or oscillatory flow curve observed in detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia (DSD), where the flow does not return to the baseline due to simultaneous sphincter contraction and detrusor activity, resulting in incomplete interruption of the urinary stream [1, 12, 20, 22]. In our study, we referred to this characteristic uroflowmetry curve observed in patients with BPO as the “comet tail-like” pattern. Although the terminology may vary across studies, the morphological features of this pattern are consistent with descriptions reported in the existing literature. As previously highlighted by some researchers, the absence of standardized quantitative descriptors for uroflowmetry curve morphology significantly limits its clinical utility. This challenge quantifying disease-specific flow curve patterns that are frequently observed in clinical practice served as the impetus for our investigation. Accordingly, we performed a quantitative analysis of time-related parameters derived from uroflowmetry curves in patients with BPO, with the aim of objectively characterizing these patterns and exploring their potential clinical significance.

In patients with BPO, the uroflowmetry curve may initially appear nearly normal during the first third of the voiding phase, despite a reduced Qmax. However, the latter portion of the curve typically extends into a distinct “tail,” characterized by a progressively declining flow rate [22, 23]. This observation is consistent with the morphological features noted in our cohort, which we describe as exhibiting a “comet tail-like” alteration in the flow curve. To objectively characterize this pattern, we conducted a quantitative analysis of time-related parameters derived from the uroflowmetry traces. Specifically, three parameters were evaluated: T1 – time to reach Qmax; T2 – time from Qmax to the end of uroflow; T3 – total uroflow time (T1 + T2). In the BPH patients included in our analysis, the median value of T1 was 5 (3.25, 7) seconds, and the median value of T3 was 30 (20, 40.5) seconds. These findings are consistent with traditional curve morphology, in which T1 accounted for less than one-third of T3, indicating that Qmax is achieved within the early phase of voiding. It is important to note that, similar to Qmax, the overall uroflowmetry curve pattern can be influenced by the VV. Therefore, VV during testing should conform to the volume recommendations specified in current guidelines to ensure reliability and comparability of results [1, 3, 9].

To further elucidate the differences in time-related parameters between patients with BPO and non-BPO controls, a comparative analysis was conducted between the BPH with BPO group and the normal group. The results revealed that the T1 value in the BPH with BPO group was significantly shorter than that in the normal group (p < 0.00), whereas the T2 and T3 values were significantly longer in the BPH with BPO group compared to controls (p < 0.00). These findings are consistent with the distinct morphological features observed in the two groups. In the BPH with BPO group, Qmax was achieved more rapidly (shorter T1), but voiding continued for a longer duration (prolonged T2), resulting in a characteristic “tail-like” curve morphology. By contrast, the normal group exhibited a symmetrical “bell-shaped” curve, with T1 and T2 values nearly equivalent, as reflected by the observed parameters [8.5 (6, 11.5) s vs. 10 (6.75, 13.25) s]. To further characterize these differences, we derived three ratio-based indicators: T1/T2, T1/T3, and T2/T3. The BPH with BPO group demonstrated significantly lower T1/T2 and T1/T3 ratios compared to the normal group (both p < 0.00), while the T2/T3 ratio was significantly higher. Among these, the T1/T3 ratio was particularly indicative of group-specific flow pattern characteristics. In the normal group, the mean T1/T3 ratio was 0.47 ± 0.10, approximating 1/2, which aligns with the symmetrical distribution of the uroflowmetry curve around the Qmax. In contrast, the BPH with BPO group exhibited a significantly lower T1/T3 ratio of 0.18 ± 0.10, reflecting a rapid ascent to Qmax followed by a prolonged descending “comet tail” phase in the flow curve.

We further analyzed the time-related parameters in both the BPH with BPO group and the normal group, and compared them with conventional urodynamic indices. Within each group, correlation analyses were performed between time-related parameters and both the BCI and BOOI. However, no statistically significant correlations were found between the time-related parameters and either BCI or BOOI within the individual groups. To better understand the relationship between these variables, simple regression analysis was conducted. The results showed that age, VV, T1, T2, T3, T1/T2, T1/T3, T2/T3, MCC, SD, BC, BCI, and PVR were all significantly associated with BOOI. Subsequently, a multivariate linear regression analysis was performed, yielding a statistically significant model (F = 39.6, p < 0.001). The model’s coefficient of determination (R²) was 0.884, with an adjusted R² of 0.862, indicating that approximately 86.2% of the variance in BOOI could be explained by the included independent variables. These findings suggest that time-related parameters extracted from the uroflowmetry curve in BPH patients may provide diagnostic value in evaluating BOO. Nonetheless, prospective validation in larger cohorts is warranted to confirm the clinical applicability of these parameters.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings, and the results should be interpreted with caution. Second, the absence of longitudinal follow-up data prevents assessment of the temporal stability and reproducibility of the identified parameters over time. Moreover, potential confounding factors such as concomitant medications, comorbidities, and lifestyle factors were not fully controlled for, which could have influenced the uroflowmetry results. Finally, external validation in larger, multi-center cohorts is necessary to confirm the robustness and clinical utility of these time-related indicators in diverse patient populations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides a preliminary characterization of time-related quantitative parameters derived from uroflowmetry curve patterns in patients with BPH and BPO, and compares these parameters with those observed in non-BPO controls. The findings suggest that these time-based indicators may offer potential clinical utility in the diagnosis and evaluation of BPO. However, given the retrospective design and limited sample size, further validation in larger, prospective, multi-center studies is warranted to confirm the reproducibility and clinical applicability of the identified parameters.
