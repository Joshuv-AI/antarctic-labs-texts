Pharmacological advances in HIV treatment: from ART to long-acting injectable therapies
Authors: Krzysztof Łupina, Klaudia Nowak, Dominika Lorek, Adrian Nowak, Anna Romac, Ewelina Głowacka, Jakub Janczura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) remains a global public health challenge, affecting millions worldwide despite significant advancements in antiretroviral therapy (ART). While ART has transformed HIV into a manageable chronic condition, long-term adherence, drug resistance, and access disparities continue to hinder treatment success. Recent research has focused on developing alternative therapeutic strategies, particularly long-acting injectable (LAI) therapies and immunotherapeutic approaches to improve adherence and potentially achieve viral remission. This review explores the evolution of pharmacological advancements in HIV treatment, highlighting the transition from daily oral ART to long-acting formulations such as cabotegravir, rilpivirine, and lenacapavir. LAI therapies reduce the burden of daily adherence, enhance treatment efficacy, and decrease stigma, particularly in vulnerable populations. Additionally, novel immunotherapeutic strategies such as broadly neutralizing antibodies, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and chimeric antigen receptor T cell therapy are being investigated for their potential to induce long-term viral suppression or cure. Despite these promising developments, several challenges remain, including resistance-associated mutations, accessibility issues, and long-term safety concerns. This review summarizes recent clinical trials, discusses the benefits and limitations of emerging HIV therapies, and outlines future research directions. The continued advancement of LAI therapies and immunotherapeutics holds great potential to improve treatment outcomes, expand global access to care, and move closer to a functional cure for HIV.

Introduction

Since its identification in the early 1980 s, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) has remained a major global health challenge, affecting millions of individuals worldwide [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 39.9 million people were living with HIV in 2023, with 65% of cases concentrated in the African region, which underscores persistent inequalities in HIV care [2]. Although global HIV incidence has declined overall, regions such as Eastern Europe, Central Asia, and sub-Saharan Africa continue to experience rising infection rates, while adolescents and key populations remain disproportionately affected [2]. In the same year, over 600,000 individuals died from HIV-related causes, emphasizing the ongoing burden of the epidemic despite advancements in treatment and prevention [2]. Over the past four decades, HIV treatment has undergone a groundbreaking transformation, evolving from early monotherapy, such as zidovudine approved in 1987, to highly active antiretroviral therapy, introduced in 1996, which revolutionized care by combining multiple drugs to suppress viral replication and delay disease progression [3]. The introduction of antiretroviral therapy (ART) has transformed HIV from a fatal disease into a manageable chronic condition, significantly extending life expectancy when initiated early and maintained consistently [1]. However, ART is not a cure, and HIV remains a significant threat to global public health, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where access to medical care, diagnostics, and treatment can be limited [4]. Despite the success of ART in suppressing viral replication, several challenges persist, including long-term adherence, drug resistance, and healthcare disparities, which continue to compromise treatment outcomes [5]. Adherence remains a critical issue, as missing daily doses can lead to virologic failure and the development of drug-resistant HIV strains. Additionally, stigma and discrimination contribute to non-adherence, as individuals living with HIV may avoid or discontinue treatment due to fear of social repercussions [5]. Given these challenges, research is increasingly focused on developing alternative treatment strategies that provide long-term viral control, reduce dependence on lifelong medication, and potentially lead to a functional or sterilizing cure [6]. This urgent clinical need has driven the pursuit of innovative therapeutic modalities that address barriers to adherence, mitigate cumulative drug toxicity, and move beyond viral suppression toward eradication or durable remission. In response, the field has shifted toward long-acting injectable therapies (LAI) and novel immunotherapeutic approaches, with the aim of enhancing treatment efficacy, improving adherence, and ultimately achieving sustained viral remission [6]. LAI therapies, such as cabotegravir (CAB), rilpivirine (RPV), and lenacapavir, have emerged as promising alternatives to daily oral ART, offering prolonged drug exposure with less frequent dosing. Besides ART and LAI therapies, immunotherapeutic strategies are being explored as part of efforts to achieve durable viral suppression or a cure [7]. This review examines the latest pharmacological advancements in HIV treatment, highlighting the potential of long-acting injectables and immunotherapeutic innovations in shaping the future of HIV care.

Search strategy and criteria

A comprehensive search was performed using PubMed, Scopus, EMBASE, Web of Science, and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) databases. The literature search was conducted using the following keywords: ("HIV treatment"OR"antiretroviral therapy"OR"long-acting injectables"OR"broadly neutralizing antibodies"OR"CAR-T therapy"OR"immune checkpoint inhibitors") AND ("adherence"OR"drug resistance"OR"pharmacokinetics"OR"HIV latency"OR"pre-exposure prophylaxis"). The search was restricted to studies published between 2019 and 2025. Additionally, national health reports and guidelines from the European Medicines Agency (EMA), WHO, and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) were reviewed for policy perspectives on HIV treatment advancements. In total, we reviewed 371 articles and reports from EMA, WHO, and CDC. The inclusion criteria for abstracts were those that addressed (1) efficacy and safety of long-acting injectable HIV therapies, (2) mechanisms of novel HIV immunotherapies, (3) global prevalence and outcomes of multidrug-resistant HIV, (4) impact of adherence and accessibility on treatment outcomes, and (5) pharmacological and immunological advancements in HIV cure research. Only studies published in English between 2019 and 2025 were considered. Studies were excluded if they focused solely on basic virology without clinical relevance, included in vitro or animal model results without human application, were non-peer-reviewed reports, editorials, or opinion pieces, or lacked sufficient data on clinical outcomes or treatment strategies. After applying these criteria, 88 studies were selected.

HIV life cycle and correlated target for drug discovery

The HIV-1 life cycle involves targeting immune cells, particularly T lymphocytes, through sequential but sometimes concurrent steps. Initially, the virus attaches to the host cell via an interaction between gp120 and the CD4 receptor, with coreceptors CXCR4 and CCR5 facilitating T-cell and macrophage infection, respectively [7]. The choice of co-receptor is determined by the viral Env protein and defines viral tropism: CCR5-tropic (R5) viruses predominate during early-stage infection and preferentially infect memory CD4+ T cells due to their higher CCR5 expression, particularly effector memory (TEM) cells [8]. In contrast, CXCR4-tropic (X4) viruses are more commonly associated with advanced disease and are capable of infecting naive CD4+ T cells, although these cells exhibit limited CCR5 expression, making them generally resistant to R5-tropic strains [8–11]. Dual-tropic viruses can utilize both co-receptors and may exhibit preferential binding to either CCR5 (“dual-R”) or CXCR4 (“dual-X”) [9]. Notably, viruses utilizing CXCR4 are typically not involved in initial transmission events [10]. Once viral RNA enters the cytoplasm, reverse transcriptase (RT) converts it into DNA [12]. This viral DNA forms a pre-integration complex (PIC), which is transported to the nucleus, where integrase (IN) facilitates its integration into the host genome [3, 13]. The viral envelope glycoprotein and Gag-Pol polyprotein are synthesized in the endoplasmic reticulum, transported to the plasma membrane, and assembled into new virions capable of infection [12, 13]. HIV protease is essential for this process, because it is responsible for cleaving viral polyprotein precursors into mature, functional proteins required for assembling infectious virions [14]. This proteolytic processing is crucial for the maturation of the virus; without active protease, the virus remains non-infectious and cannot propagate effectively. This makes the viral protease a relevant target of another drug class known as protease inhibitors [14]. Not only does HIV infect T cells and macrophages but also CD4-expressing microglia and dendritic cells, which contribute to trans-infection [15, 16]. These cell types not only serve as viral reservoirs but may also contribute to persistent inflammation and viral dissemination. The interaction of HIV virions with CCR5 and CXCR4 on myeloid and neuronal cells has been implicated in chronic neuroinflammation and HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders [17]. A number of drugs are currently available that work at different stages of HIV replication. For example, HIV entry inhibitors prevent infection by targeting distinct steps. Attachment inhibitors such as fostemsavir (FTR) block gp120-CD4 binding [18]. Post-attachment inhibitors, such as ibalizumab (IBA), prevent CD4 interaction [19]. Co-receptor binding inhibitors such as maraviroc block CCR5 [20]. Reverse transcriptase inhibitors (RTIs) are key in combination antiretroviral therapy (cART). They include nucleoside RT inhibitors (NRTIs) such as abacavir (ABC), emtricitabine (FTC), lamivudine, tenofovir (TAF), and ZDV, all of which have been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [21–23]. Non-nucleoside RT inhibitors (NNRTIs) act allosterically, with six FDA-approved options: nevirapine (NVP), delavirdine (DLV), efavirenz (EFV), etravirine (ETR), RPV, and doravirine (DOR). Resistance mutations limit their use, and some, like DLV, have been discontinued due to severe side effects [22, 24, 25]. Protease inhibitors (PIs) prevent viral maturation by blocking HIV protease, requiring multiple mutations for resistance [26]. This group includes atazanavir, tipranavir, fosamprenavir, darunavir, and ritonavir, all FDA-approved [23, 27]. Integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs), such as raltegravir, dolutegravir (DTG), CAB, bictegravir (BIC), and elvitegravir, inhibit viral DNA integration and offer high efficacy. CAB is notable for its intramuscular and enteral administration [28]. Oral antiretroviral therapy

The introduction of ZDV in 1987 marked the beginning of ART, initiating decades of rapid development in HIV pharmacotherapy. Current ART is based on the use of combination regimens that typically include two NRTIs combined with a third agent from the INSTI, NNRTI, or PI class [27]. Contemporary international guidelines, including those from WHO and the European Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS) Clinical Society (EACS), now recommend INSTI-based regimens as the preferred first-line approach for most treatment-naïve adults and adolescents [29], which is primarily due to the superior efficacy, high genetic barrier to resistance, minimal drug–drug interactions, and favorable safety profiles [28]. Commonly used combinations include BIC/FTC/TAF and DTG/ABC/LMV, which are administered once daily and support high levels of adherence due to their simplicity. Dual-drug regimens have also emerged as viable options, particularly in virologically suppressed patients [28]. Interestingly, recent evidence has demonstrated that certain two-drug regimens are noninferior to traditional three-drug regimens in treatment-naïve individuals. The GEMINI-1 and GEMINI-2 phase 3 clinical trials, which collectively enrolled over 1,400 participants, evaluated the efficacy of the dual regimen DTG/LMV compared to the standard triple regimen DTG/TAF/FTC. At week 48, the proportion of participants achieving HIV-1 RNA <50 copies/mL was 86.0% in the DTG/LMV group and 89.5% in the DTG/TAF/FTC group, confirming noninferiority. Importantly, no increased risk of treatment-emergent resistance was observed in the two-drug arm, supporting the use of DTG/LMV as a viable and well-tolerated initial therapy for treatment-naïve individuals with HIV-1 infection, particularly those without baseline resistance [30]. These simplified approaches may be particularly beneficial for individuals with comorbidities, polypharmacy, or long-term treatment exposure. However, careful patient selection is required, as these regimens are not suitable for individuals with known or suspected resistance mutations or those co-infected with hepatitis B virus (HBV). In addition to these mainstay therapies, several newer agents with novel mechanisms of action have expanded treatment options for individuals with multidrug-resistant (MDR) HIV. FTR is administered orally twice daily and is approved for use in heavily treatment-experienced individuals with limited remaining options. The BRIGHTE phase 3 trial evaluated the efficacy and safety of twice-daily FTR and optimized background therapy in heavily treatment-experienced individuals with MDR HIV and limited therapeutic options. The study demonstrated that virologic and immunologic responses, as well as the safety profile, were consistent across diverse patient subgroups, including those with advanced immunosuppression and prior extensive treatment failure. These findings support fostemsavir as a valuable therapeutic option in salvage regimens for individuals with limited antiretroviral susceptibility [31]. Similarly, IBA provides an option for patients with MDR HIV. In a 25-week study, Emu et al. demonstrated that IBA, a CD4-directed post-attachment inhibitor, exhibited significant antiviral activity in individuals with MDR HIV-1 infection, advanced disease, and limited remaining treatment options. While the results underscore the potential of ibalizumab in salvage therapy, the small sample size (n = 31) and the occurrence of a serious adverse event in one participant highlight the need for larger, controlled studies to further evaluate its long-term safety and efficacy [32]. Despite the expansion of therapeutic options and improvements in regimen tolerability and convenience, ART is not without limitations. The development of resistance-associated mutations (RAMs), particularly in patients with poor adherence or prior treatment failure, remains a critical challenge [32]. Additionally, adverse effects, ranging from weight gain associated with INSTIs to lipid abnormalities from PIs, continue to impact long-term treatment sustainability [31]. Moreover, NNRTIs are susceptible to cross-resistance and have interactions via the cytochrome P450 3A4/5 pathway, which must be carefully managed in patients on concomitant medications [33]. Accessibility, affordability, and healthcare infrastructure disparities also limit the universal implementation of optimal ART regimens in low- and middle-income countries [31]. Additionally, supply chain instability, cost constraints, healthcare worker shortages, and stigma associated with HIV may hinder consistent access to care [32].

Long-acting injectable therapies: a new era in HIV treatment

LAI therapies represent a major advancement in HIV treatment, offering an alternative to daily oral ART by providing prolonged drug exposure and improving adherence. These therapies are particularly beneficial for populations where adherence to daily medication can be challenging, such as children, men who have sex with men (MSM), individuals with substance use disorders, and those experiencing mental health issues. For instance, Crim et al. reported that ART adherence was lower among MSM and those facing socioeconomic challenges, with adherence rates of 71.6% among individuals experiencing poverty, 67.2% among those reporting drug use, and 66.3% among those with depression [34]. Additionally, concerns about stigma and confidentiality can lead to suboptimal adherence, with some individuals hiding their medication or missing doses to avoid disclosing their HIV status [33–36]. CAB is administered intramuscularly every eight weeks and has a half-life of 5.6 to 11.5 weeks, while RPV can be given every four to eight weeks, with a half-life of 13 to 28 weeks. In contrast, their oral counterparts have significantly shorter half-lives of 41 and 45 hours, respectively, highlighting the extended drug exposure provided by the injectable formulations [33, 39, 40]. Clinical trials have extensively demonstrated the efficacy of CAB+RPV therapy. In a phase 3, open-label, multicenter trial, the authors reported that injectable CAB+RPV therapy was noninferior to oral therapy, with 92.5% of participants in the CAB+RPV group maintaining HIV-1 RNA levels of <50 copies/mL at week 48 [41]. While the therapy was generally well tolerated, adverse events were more common in the long-acting therapy group, primarily injection-site pain, which affected 75% (231 recipients) of those receiving long-acting therapy, although most cases were mild or moderate in severity [41]. The ATLAS-2M study, which included 1,045 participants, compared CAB+RPV dosing intervals of every 8 weeks (Q8W) versus every 4 weeks (Q4W). By week 152, 87% of the Q8W group and 86% of the Q4W group maintained HIV-1 RNA levels of <50 copies/mL, confirming the noninferiority of the Q8W regimen. These findings highlight the potential of LAI therapy to enhance adherence, particularly in populations that struggle with daily oral medication [42]. The FLAIR study further confirmed the noninferiority of CAB+RPV LA compared to standard oral regimens after 96 weeks of treatment in virologically suppressed patients. At week 96, only nine participants (3%) in each group had HIV-1 RNA levels of 50 copies/mL or more. Adverse events leading to treatment withdrawal were infrequent, occurring in 5% of the LA group and 1% of the standard care group. The 96-week results reaffirmed the 48-week findings, demonstrating that LA CAB+RPV remained effective in maintaining viral suppression [43]. These findings highlight the durability and long-term efficacy of CAB+RPV LA, reinforcing its role as a viable therapeutic option for virally suppressed individuals over nearly two years of treatment. Another LA drug, lenacapavir, is a first-in-class capsid inhibitor that was designed for patients with MDR HIV-1, and it has shown promise in both LA HIV therapy and pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP). Its unique mechanism targets multiple stages of the HIV replication cycle, offering a novel approach to viral suppression [44]. Administered subcutaneously, lenacapavir follows a two-phase absorption profile, beginning with an initial fast-release phase followed by a slow-release phase, which ensures prolonged drug activity and supports its LA nature [45]. In a clinical trial involving 72 patients with MDR HIV-1, 82% achieved HIV-1 RNA levels below 50 copies/mL after 26 weeks of treatment. Additionally, patients experienced significant increases in CD4+ cell counts, with mean gains of +75 and +104 cells/mm3, highlighting the therapy’s effectiveness in restoring immune function [46]. The PURPOSE-1 clinical trial, involving 5,338 participants, was conducted among adolescent girls and young women in South Africa and Uganda to evaluate lenacapavir as a LA PrEP option. The study found that lenacapavir injections every 26 weeks significantly reduced HIV incidence, with an infection rate of 0 per 100 person-years, compared to 2.02 per 100 person-years in the daily oral emtricitabine-tenofovir alafenamide (F/TAF) group and 1.69 per 100 person-years in the daily oral emtricitabine-tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (F/TDF) group. Adherence to F/TAF and F/TDF was low, further emphasizing the advantage of lenacapavir as an LA alternative. No safety concerns were identified, although injection-site reactions were more common in the lenacapavir group (68.8%) compared to the placebo injection group (34.9%). With just two injections per year, lenacapavir offers a promising strategy to improve adherence while achieving a remarkable reduction in HIV incidence [47]. Similarly, the PURPOSE-2 trial evaluated lenacapavir injections compared to daily F/TDF in a diverse population of men and gender-diverse individuals, including transgender men and women, where adherence to daily medication may be lower compared to cisgender individuals [48]. Among 3,265 participants, the HIV incidence in the lenacapavir group was 0.10 per 100 person-years, which is significantly lower than the 0.93 per 100 person-years observed in the F/TDF group (P < 0.001). The background HIV incidence in the screened population of 4,634 participants was 2.37 per 100 person-years, further emphasizing lenacapavir’s effectiveness. The incidence of HIV infection in the lenacapavir group was significantly lower than both the background incidence (P < 0.001) and the incidence in the F/TDF group (P = 0.002). Importantly, no safety concerns were identified, reinforcing lenacapavir as a promising LA PrEP option for populations at higher risk of poor adherence to daily oral therapy [49]. In a recent phase 1 open-label study, Jogiraju et al. investigated the feasibility of once-yearly intramuscular lenacapavir administration. The study demonstrated that median plasma concentrations remained above the threshold associated with efficacy in phase 3 trials of twice-yearly subcutaneous lenacapavir for PrEP for at least 56 weeks. While these findings suggest a potential breakthrough in LA HIV prevention and treatment, the study was limited by a small sample size of 20 participants, highlighting the need for further large-scale clinical trials to confirm its safety, efficacy, and long-term viability [50]. Islatravir (ISL) is a nucleoside reverse transcriptase translocation inhibitor with a unique mechanism that allows it to bind and incorporate into the growing HIV-1 DNA strand [51]. Its plasma half-life ranges from 49 to 73 hours, while its intracellular active form (islatravir-triphosphate) has an extended half-life of 118 to 209 hours, supporting less frequent dosing intervals [52]. A phase 1, placebo-controlled trial demonstrated that an ISL subdermal patch administered every 12 weeks was well tolerated with a good safety profile [53]. Another phase 1 trial conducted by Matthews et al. found that an ISL-eluting implant was also well tolerated, with only implant-site reactions reported [54]. On the other hand, RAMs pose a challenge, with RPV RAMs being more prevalent than CAB RAMs, underscoring the need for pre-transition resistance testing to prevent virologic failure [55]. Benefits and challenges of long-acting injectable therapies

The primary advantage of LAI is the reduced dosing frequency and elimination of daily pill burden, which helps lower the risk of treatment failure due to missed doses [61, 62]. Patients taking LAI are less likely to discontinue their treatment due to adverse events or unsuppressed HIV. Studies showed that the likelihood of discontinuing the assigned regimen was 10% among people taking long-acting ART, versus 26% for daily oral ART [63]. This is particularly relevant for individuals with dysphagia, cognitive or memory impairments, unstable housing conditions, or comorbid substance use disorders, where maintaining consistent adherence to daily oral regimens poses significant challenges. By removing the need for daily oral regimens, LAI can also minimize HIV-related stigma while enhancing convenience and privacy [64]. The FLAIR and ATLAS clinical trials have demonstrated that LAI therapy can effectively maintain viral suppression in individuals who previously relied on daily oral therapy [65]. Currently available LAI therapies, including the combination of CAB/RPV and the capsid inhibitor lenacapavir, have demonstrated a favorable tolerability profile, with most adverse events reported as mild and self-limiting [66]. The parenteral route of administration offers additional pharmacokinetic advantages by minimizing drug–food and drug–drug interactions [66]. Clinical studies have confirmed the efficacy of CAB/RPV in maintaining viral suppression among individuals with low detectable HIV-1 RNA at treatment initiation, as well as its potential role in the prevention of incidental HIV infection [67]. LAI has also shown a favorable safety profile in patients with renal impairment, and lenacapavir has demonstrated tolerability in those with mild-to-moderate hepatic dysfunction [66]. Currently, lenacapavir is not recommended for use during pregnancy or breastfeeding, and effective contraception is advised throughout treatment to mitigate potential risks [62]. However, a recent case report involving a pregnant woman suggests that a regimen incorporating lenacapavir injections, preceded by an oral lead-in and administered alongside other antiretrovirals, may represent a promising strategy for preventing vertical transmission of HIV while maintaining both maternal and neonatal safety [68]. Cabenuva, the co-formulated CAB/RPV product, has received FDA approval for use in pediatric populations aged 12 years and older and weighing at least 35 kg. Prior to initiating the injectable regimen, an oral lead-in period with CAB and RPV is recommended to evaluate drug tolerability. Once tolerability is confirmed, the long-acting injectable formulation can be administered either monthly or every two months, providing a more convenient and adherence-friendly option for pediatric and adolescent patients [69]. Findings from an ongoing clinical trial evaluating the combination of BIC and lenacapavir in children and adolescents living with HIV are anticipated to provide important insights into the safety and efficacy of this regimen in pediatric populations [70]. Despite these advantages, LAI therapy presents several challenges across economic, social, logistical, and medical domains. The known side effects are generally mild, with the most commonly reported adverse events being injection site pain and transient, self-limiting fever following administration. Despite the favorable safety profile, patient eligibility for long-acting injectable therapy extends beyond virological parameters and includes considerations of individual adherence patterns and psychosocial factors. Importantly, this treatment is currently indicated only for individuals who have already achieved viral suppression, thereby excluding those with detectable viremia or documented resistance to the included antiretroviral agents [71]. Expanding access to LAI therapy necessitates comprehensive adaptations in healthcare infrastructure to ensure equitable distribution and effective implementation. Special attention is required to extend care to underserved populations, including individuals in prisons and long-term care facilities, where access to consistent medical supervision may be limited [62]. Additionally, mentorship programs for adolescent patients could enhance adherence and long-term treatment success by addressing age-specific barriers to care [62]. Needle phobia has been identified as an additional barrier to the uptake of LAI therapies. Given the discomfort some patients experience with injections, the use of smaller-gauge needles may help reduce pain and improve tolerability. Furthermore, future advancements in alternative administration techniques could enhance patient comfort and expand acceptability of LAI therapies [62]. Hesitancy toward LAI use is also influenced by substantial knowledge gaps regarding their side effects, efficacy, and availability. A lack of transparent, accessible information on the development and regulatory approval processes of LAI treatments can erode patient trust. Moreover, there is concern that individuals living with HIV who initiate LAI therapy may misinterpret it as a cure and consequently reduce consistent condom use, underscoring the critical need for clear, patient-centered education to support informed decision-making. From the healthcare provider perspective, additional concerns have been raised, including the potential emergence of integrase inhibitor resistance, increased staffing requirements, and the added strain on already overburdened clinical teams. These operational and clinical challenges must be addressed through targeted training, infrastructure support, and strategic workforce planning to ensure the successful integration of LAI therapies into routine HIV care [72]. Clinical studies have identified an increased risk of LAI therapy failure in individuals with a body mass index (BMI) ≥30 kg/m2 or those experiencing significant weight fluctuations during treatment. These factors may alter pharmacokinetics and compromise therapeutic drug levels, thereby reducing treatment efficacy. Additionally, suboptimal dosing represents a critical concern, as it may promote viral replication and the emergence of drug-resistant mutations, emphasizing the importance of personalized dosing strategies and close clinical monitoring. Furthermore, potential drug-drug interactions must be carefully considered. Notably, co-administration of lenacapavir with rifampin, a key agent in tuberculosis treatment, is contraindicated due to significant reductions in lenacapavir plasma concentrations, which may lead to therapeutic failure [73].

Methods of immunotherapy for HIV management

Immunotherapeutic strategies for HIV treatment encompass monoclonal antibodies, chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell therapy, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Several immunotherapeutic approaches, including broadly neutralizing antibodies (BNAbs), multi-affinity antibodies, CAR-T therapy, therapeutic vaccines aimed at enhancing HIV-specific cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses, and immune modulators such as ICIs and CD152 antibodies, have been explored in clinical trials. These strategies hold particular promise in managing central nervous system complications associated with HIV infection [74]. Initially developed for cancer therapy, ICIs are now being investigated as HIV latency-reversing agents. By blocking checkpoint protein binding, ICIs can counteract T-cell exhaustion, a key contributor to disease progression, and may restore HIV-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell function [75]. Anti-PD-1 (programmed cell death protein 1) therapy has been shown to reverse HIV latency and restore the function of exhausted HIV-specific T cells, potentially enhancing immune control over the virus [76]. In a retrospective study involving 390 patients living with HIV and cancer, El Zarif et al. reported that 94% of participants achieved an HIV viral load of <400 copies/mL, demonstrating that ICIs exhibit differential activity across various cancer types without causing excess toxicity [77]. Unlike classical ART, which directly inhibits viral replication through enzyme or entry inhibition, immunotherapeutic strategies aim to suppress HIV indirectly by modulating or enhancing host immune responses. This fundamental mechanistic difference positions immunotherapy as a potential adjunct rather than a replacement for ART in current clinical paradigms [74]. BNAbs are being investigated both as a complement to ART and as standalone maintenance therapy to induce long-term HIV-1 remission. Clinical trials have demonstrated their ability to maintain viral suppression, although coverage remains incomplete across all HIV-1 strains [78]. In comparison to ART’s broad efficacy against diverse viral strains, BNAbs require careful selection and may necessitate combination antibody regimens to prevent viral escape mutations [78]. Recent studies highlight the potential of BNAbs in both treatment and prevention, with combination therapy using non-overlapping BNAbs delaying viral rebound and minimizing escape mutations [79]. CAR-T therapy, widely used in hematologic malignancies, is now being adapted for HIV treatment, particularly through CD8+ T-cell modifications. However, challenges remain in preventing CAR-T cell infection, mitigating immune escape, and effectively targeting the viral reservoir [80]. While ART suppresses active replication, CAR-T therapy offers a theoretical path to eradication of infected cells. Nonetheless, risks such as off-target effects, viral escape, and manufacturing challenges need resolution before clinical scalability is achieved [81]. While clinical trials in this area are still limited, preliminary data confirm the safety and feasibility of CAR-T therapy for HIV, underscoring its potential as a novel therapeutic approach [81].

Medicinal vaccines for HIV treatment

Research on HIV-1 vaccines aims to either prevent infection or enhance immune responses in infected individuals, ultimately reducing viral loads to undetectable levels and potentially eliminating the need for ART [82]. However, the development of an effective vaccine remains challenging due to the virus's high genetic variability, driven by retroviral recombination and a high mutation rate. This variability is particularly pronounced in the highly immunogenic Env proteins gp160, gp120, and gp41, which complicates the design of broadly effective vaccines [83]. Clinical trials for an HIV-1 vaccine began in 1987, with the first notable success emerging from the RV144 trial, initiated in 2003 [84]. This trial employed a prime-boost strategy, combining a recombinant canarypox vector vaccine (ALVAC-HIV) with booster doses of a recombinant gp120 subunit vaccine. Over a 3.5-year period, the vaccine demonstrated 31% efficacy in preventing HIV infection [84]. Despite these promising results, subsequent trials such as HVTN 702, which modified adjuvants and dosing regimens, failed to replicate the RV144 findings and were discontinued in 2020 due to lack of efficacy [85]. Current vaccine research is shifting toward BNAbs, which could neutralize a wide range of HIV strains. Although 10-30% of HIV-infected individuals naturally produce BNAbs, their levels are typically insufficient to prevent infection [83]. Studies have shown that BNAb infusions can provide protective immunity against HIV, supporting efforts to develop vaccines that stimulate B cells to produce these antibodies [86]. Phase 1 trials have demonstrated that germline-targeting vaccines can successfully induce BNAb precursors, marking a critical step toward developing an effective HIV vaccine [87, 88]. In contrast to ART, which suppresses viral replication through pharmacological mechanisms, therapeutic vaccines aim to enhance host immune memory and restore effective antiviral immune responses. Although current evidence indicates that vaccines alone are insufficient to replace ART completely, they hold significant potential as adjunctive therapies. Future strategies are likely to involve combination approaches incorporating therapeutic vaccines with BNAbs or ICIs to promote durable viral remission [87]. Additionally, vaccine-based interventions may reduce dependence on lifelong daily medication, mitigate cumulative ART-associated toxicity, and offer a viable alternative for at-risk populations with limited access to or tolerance of conventional ART. While significant challenges remain, ongoing advancements in immunogen design, BNAb-based strategies, and T-cell-targeting approaches continue to drive progress toward the development of a safe and effective HIV-1 vaccine.

Conclusions and future directions

Significant advancements in HIV treatment have transformed the disease from a life-threatening condition to a manageable chronic illness. The evolution of ART, particularly the introduction of LAI, has improved adherence and treatment outcomes, especially for populations facing barriers to daily oral regimens. The integration of BNAbs, ICIs, and CAR-T cell therapy represents a promising shift towards immunotherapeutic strategies with the potential to induce long-term viral remission. However, challenges remain, including drug resistance, accessibility, and long-term safety concerns associated with new treatment modalities. Future research should focus on optimizing LAI regimens, addressing resistance-associated mutations to prevent virologic failure, and expanding clinical trials to include diverse, currently underrepresented populations, such as children, pregnant individuals, and those with significant comorbidities. Large-scale, multi-region clinical trials are urgently needed in these key populations to comprehensively evaluate the pharmacokinetics, safety, and efficacy of LAI therapies and inform evidence-based guideline development. Moreover, further research is necessary to define optimal monitoring strategies for resistance-associated mutations, particularly those arising during the transition from oral ART to LAI, and to assess the broader impacts of LAI on patients’ quality of life, stigma reduction, and long-term adherence. From a public health perspective, implementation science should investigate the cost-effectiveness and logistical feasibility of integrating LAI into diverse healthcare settings, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where HIV remains a major public health challenge. Equitable access could be promoted through innovative delivery models, including mobile clinics, community-based administration, and targeted education campaigns aimed at populations disproportionately affected by HIV. Furthermore, combination approaches that integrate LAI with immunotherapeutic modalities should be evaluated in clinical trials to explore potential synergistic effects in achieving durable viral remission or cure. Ultimately, collaborative, multidisciplinary research bridging pharmacological innovation, immunotherapy, and public health delivery will be essential to advancing toward an accessible, sustainable, and potentially curative HIV treatment paradigm.
