Regorafenib pulmonary toxicity: A case of cavitary lung disease and ARDS treated with infliximab
Authors: Michael Dong, Mackenzie Kramer, Tanmay Gandhi, Gautam George
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Regorafenib-induced pneumonitis and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) are rare and poorly characterized toxicities. We describe a 43-year-old male with metastatic colorectal cancer who developed hypoxic respiratory failure after two cycles of regorafenib. Imaging revealed cavitary lung lesions and diffuse ground glass opacities. Despite antibiotics and high-dose corticosteroids, his condition deteriorated, requiring ICU admission and mechanical ventilation. Bronchoalveolar lavage revealed bacterial pathogens, and he was treated for pneumonia. After multidisciplinary discussion, he was assessed to have regorafenib-induced pneumonitis as a major driver of his respiratory failure, and after limited steroid response, infliximab was administered with marked respiratory improvement. However, he later developed fungal sepsis and died from refractory shock. This is the first reported case of regorafenib-induced pneumonitis and ARDS treated with infliximab and corticosteroids. The patient's transient response suggests overlap with immune-related pneumonitis mechanisms seen in tyrosine kinase and immune checkpoint inhibitor therapies. Further research is needed to clarify regorafenib's immunotoxicity and the role of advanced immunosuppressive therapies in managing pneumonitis.

Introduction

Regorafenib, a multikinase inhibitor, is used for treatment of various cancers. Drug-induced pneumonitis reaction to regorafenib is a rare and poorly understood disease entity [1]. While pulmonary toxicity has been more frequently described with other targeted therapies such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors, the pulmonary side effects of regorafenib remain underreported [2,3]. These respiratory complications pose significant diagnostic and management challenges. We describe a case of regorafenib-induced pneumonitis, cavitary lung lesions, and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) treated with infliximab and pulse-dose corticosteroids.

Case report

A 43-year-old male with metastatic colorectal cancer, previously treated with surgery and several lines of chemotherapy and targeted therapy, was started on regorafenib two months prior to admission due to disease progression. After two cycles, he developed dyspnea and cough, prompting admission to a tertiary care center.

On arrival, he was hypoxic, requiring 6 L/min of supplemental oxygen. Physical examination revealed diffuse rales and tachycardia. Labs showed leukocytosis (26,800/μL), negative testing for SARS CoV-2, influenza/parainfluenza, and respiratory viral pathogen panel, and a normal transthoracic echocardiogram. Computed tomography (CT) of the chest revealed multiple large cavitary lung lesions that correlated with prior tumor, with multifocal areas of ground glass opacities and consolidation (Fig. 1). Empiric vancomycin, piperacillin-tazobactam, and methylprednisolone (60 mg twice daily) were initiated for suspected bacterial pneumonia versus drug-induced pneumonitis. Three days later, he developed acute respiratory failure requiring ICU transfer, intubation, and mechanical ventilation. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) showed 88 % neutrophils, and cultures grew Staphylococcus aureus and Klebsiella aerogenes. He was extubated to high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) but showed no improvement despite continued broad spectrum antibiotics and a three-day trial of methylprednisolone (250 mg twice daily). After multidisciplinary discussion with Pulmonology, Oncology, and Infectious Diseases, he was assessed to have regorafenib induced pneumonitis as a major driver of his respiratory failure, and infliximab (5 mg/kg) was initiated for treatment.

Over five days, his respiratory function improved significantly, allowing de-escalation from 60L HFNC to 6 L/min nasal cannula. He was transferred to the wards on a 14-day antibiotic course, prednisone (50 mg twice daily), and a second infliximab dose at day 14.

After 20 days on the oncology wards, he was readmitted to the ICU with acute hypoxic and hypercapnic respiratory failure. Repeat BAL revealed neutrophil predominance, budding yeast, and gram-positive bacilli. Blood cultures later grew Candida albicans. He developed severe refractory septic shock and died.

Discussion

Pneumonitis reactions and ARDS due to regorafenib are rarely described, and as such management of this condition is poorly understood [[1], [4]].

Pneumonitis reactions due to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) have been better described in the literature. Regorafenib has a broad inhibition profile targeting anti-angiogenesis and remodeling of the tumor microenvironment, but exhibits mechanistic overlap with TKIs such as lenvatinib and sorafenib [5]. For example, the upregulation of HLA class I expression via the IFNy/STAT1 signaling pathway is a mechanism it shares with other TKIs [6]. Regorafenib and sorafenib both inhibit multiple protein kinases involved in tumor angiogenesis, oncogenesis, and microenvironmental signaling, with notable targets including VEGFR1-3, PDGFR, KIT, RET, and RAF kinases [5,7,8]. In addition, regorafenib resembles ICIs in its ability to modulate the tumor microenvironment by inhibiting PD-L1 and IDO1 expression, thereby enhancing the efficacy of immune inhibitors[[9], [10], [11]].

The incidence of regorafenib-induced pneumonitis is not well defined but appears to be rare. In a phase I trial evaluating safety and dosing, pneumonitis occurred in only one of ten patients in the dose optimization cohort [12]. Reports of regorafenib-associated pneumonitis or interstitial lung disease remain limited to isolated case reports [1,13]. The specific pathophysiology of regorafenib-induced pneumonitis also remains uncertain. While there may be some mechanistic overlap with ICI and TKI as described above, it is unclear whether treatment strategies for ICI- or TKI-related pneumonitis are applicable to regorafenib. Interstitial pneumonia due to multi-kinase inhibitors such as regorafenib, lenvatinib, sorafenib, and pazopanib therapy has been successfully treated with discontinuation of the drug and various dosed steroid therapies (Table 1) [1,[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. However, clinicians often face uncertainty regarding the appropriate degree of immunosuppression, including optimal steroid dosing and the potential role of advanced therapies such as biologics. This is the first case to describe the use of infliximab for treatment of regorafenib induced pneumonitis and ARDS. Our patient's life-threatening respiratory failure was refractory to intravenous pulse steroid therapy, but improved remarkably with infliximab. In ICI related pneumonitis, there is evidence supporting the use of infliximab, mycophenolate mofetil, and intravenous immunoglobulin in steroid refractory pneumonitis and its use is also supported by the American Society of Clinical Oncology guidelines [21,22]. While there is less evidence in TKI-related pneumonitis, infliximab and mycophenolate mofetil has been described in similar refractory cases[23].

The significant improvement initially seen in our patient's case with infliximab suggest mechanistic similarity in the pathogenesis of the pneumonitis caused by Regorafenib, TKIs, and ICIs. His clinical trajectory, which had been progressively worsening prior to the infliximab, shifted dramatically with oxygen support de-escalating from 60L to 6L. Unfortunately, later after the second dose of infliximab and weeks on steroids, his course was further complicated by the presence of bacterial and fungal pneumonia and fungemia, which ultimately led to the patient's death. The timing and impact of immune suppression in this setting of pneumonitis and infection is unclear. Clinicians must carefully weigh the risks of advanced immunosuppression in the context of active or suspected infection, and early multidisciplinary collaboration with oncology and infectious diseases is essential. Another unanswered question is the impact of infliximab on the cavitary lung lesions left from the lung metastases necrosis. Further research is needed to investigate whether other treatments, such as mycophenolate, tocilizumab [24], and IVIG that may be effective in TKI and ICI pneumonitis, would be effective in regorafenib related disease. Given the rarity of this complication, prospective data may remain limited, but pooled case reports and pharmacovigilance studies may help identify patterns in presentation, treatment, and outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, pneumonitis and ARDS is a rare complication of regorafenib. We report the first case of treatment of this disease process with high dose methylprednisolone and infliximab.
