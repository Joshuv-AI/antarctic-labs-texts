A structured framework of cytoskeletal proteins and noncentrosomal microtubules promotes the initiation and elongation of invadopodia
Authors: Mark Garewal, Pedro Ramos, Kenneth A Myers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer deaths are largely attributed to the dissemination of cancer cells from a primary tumor to a secondary metastatic site. The metastatic cascade is initiated by cancer cell invasion that is facilitated by cytoskeletal remodeling to produce ventral cell protrusions, termed invadopodia, that degrade the extracellular matrix to promote motility. Conventional invadopodia studies rely on techniques with embedded cells in 3D matrices to observe and determine protein behavior, which often utilize immunolabeling strategies and struggle to visualize individual invadopodia, thereby limiting investigations of protein and invadopodia dynamics. Here, the design and utilization of an axial invasion chamber is described for live-cell imaging of elongating invadopodia in 3D. Results identify that cytoskeletal and microtubule-associated proteins within invadopodia exist in an organized framework, and determine the functional contribution by which noncentrosomal microtubules promote cancer cell invasion and migration.

INTRODUCTION

Metastasis is the primary cause of cancer death, with a significant unmet medical need for metastatic targeting therapies (Chaffer and Weinberg, 2011; Lambert et al., 2017). Initiation of cancer cell metastasis is achieved by an epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition state, followed by cell invasion and migration to intravasate a local blood vessel (Thiery, 2002; Wyckoff et al., 2007; Kalluri and Weinberg, 2009). Cancer cells form actin-rich ventral protrusions, termed invadopodia, that degrade the extracellular matrix (ECM) by proteolytic activity (Murphy and Courtneidge, 2011; Eddy et al., 2017). Invadopodia are structures critical both for cancer cell intravasation and extravasation, and understanding their dynamic behaviors is key to developing therapies capable of targeting metastasis (Leong et al., 2014). Unique biomarkers of invadopodia are the matrix-metalloproteases (MMPs), such as MMP2/9, MMP14, MMP17, and others, that are trafficked to invadopodia to facilitate proteolytic activity (Jacob and Prekeris, 2015; Yan et al., 2020). The combination of invadopodia elongation and MMP activity at and near the elongating invadopodia tip enables remodeling of the stromal ECM necessary to provide the physical space for cancer cell migration to a nearby blood vessel (Nakahara et al., 1997; Poincloux et al., 2009).

More than 50 proteins are known to reside within invadopodia and work functionally to facilitate three main stages of invadopodia behavior. During the first stage, invadopodia coordinate core assembly and recruit proteins including cortactin, cofilin, N-WASP, and Arp2/3 to polymerize F-actin, and scaffold adaptor proteins such as tyrosine kinase substrate 5 (TKS5; Yamaguchi et al., 2006; Oser and Condeelis, 2009; Perrin and Gligorijevic, 2022). TKS5 is the quintessential biomarker and is critical throughout all stages of invadopodia-mediated metastasis (Saini and Courtneidge, 2018; Zagryazhskaya-Masson et al., 2020). The second stage, initiation of ECM degradation and invadopodia elongation, involves TKS5 anchoring to the plasma membrane along with recruitment of β1 integrin, intermediate filaments, microtubules (MTs), Rab GTPases, and exocyst complexes to facilitate MMP delivery for ECM degradation and to provide structural support (Schoumacher et al., 2010; Beaty et al., 2013; Monteiro et al., 2013). The third stage, invadopodia dissolution, is the retraction of invadopodia by Rac1 GTPase, TrioGEF, and PAK1 that results in the phosphorylation and dissociation of cortactin from F-actin (Moshfegh et al., 2014).

MTs are key regulators of cell polarity and are required to promote directed cell motility in a variety of cell types (Kaverina et al., 1999; Akhmanova et al., 2001; Watanabe et al., 2005). Within invadopodia, MTs provide a physical roadway along which molecular motor proteins traffic cargo, in particular MMPs, to facilitate ECM remodeling and cancer cell invasion (Marchesin et al., 2015; Saji et al., 2022). MTs are also known to be present within invadopodia during the elongation phase, and yet their functional contribution to regulating the process of invadopodia elongation has not been investigated.

More recent studies have identified that at least two different subsets of MTs exist in cells, distinguished by their location of nucleation, known as the microtubule-organizing center (MTOC). Centrosomal MTs nucleate from a centrosomal MTOC, while non-centrosomal MTs (NC-MTs) nucleate from a MTOC distinct from the centrosome, with both subsets serving different functions of cell division and cell polarity, respectively (Tillery et al., 2018; Gillard et al., 2021). For example, NC-MTs facilitate cancer cell migration, while centrosomal MTs are unable to perform this function (Nishita et al., 2017; Zahn and Lundin-Schiller, 2024). Despite these findings, the role of centrosomal versus NC-MTs within elongating invadopodia is not understood.

Conventional 3D invadopodia studies involve fluorescently labeled proteins within tumor spheroids or cells sandwiched and embedded in an ECM mixture to study protrusions by immunolabeling (Wolf et al., 2007; Li et al., 2010; Carey et al., 2016; Remy et al., 2023). Both 3D single-cell and spheroid studies present limitations because the orientation of invadopodia is inherently random, while the imaging apparatus is limited to a narrow range of focal length, resulting in few chance observations of invadopodia that are visible within the focal plane of the microscope. Additional challenges include the forfeiture of protein and invadopodia dynamics caused by the immunolabeling approach, and the inability to isolate invadopodia dynamics from whole cell migration. Here, the design and utilization of an axial invasion chamber (AIC) to visualize 3D live-cell invadopodia and protein behavior is described. Data reveal a structured framework of cytoskeletal and MT-associated protein organization, and further identify the functional contribution by which NC-MTs promote cancer cell invasion and migration.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Design and construction of an axial invasion chamber (AIC)

The axial invasion chamber (AIC) allows 3D imaging of invadopodia dynamics, inhibits whole cell migration by pore size restriction, and utilizes a physiological ECM substratum of Geltrex and Laminin to isolate and evaluate the dynamics of invadopodia and the proteins within invadopodia. The AIC permits live-cell imaging with conventional inverted microscopes and an objective lens with a minimum working distance of 310 μm, and was assembled with a standard 35 mm glass bottom dish amenable to most microscope stages and holders.

AIC assembly began with a chemoattractant bottom layer of cold 5μL of 10% Geltrex, 73% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 8% cell culture media, and 9% human epidermal growth factor (hEGF) that was applied onto a cold 35 mm glass bottom dish. A hydrophilic polyester track etch (PETE) membrane of 3.0 μm pore size and 12 μm thickness was subsequently placed on top of the chemoattractant bottom layer. This pore size was sufficient to inhibit cell body movement through the pore, while allowing for optimal formation of invadopodia. A soft adhesive containing a 1:1:1 mixture of Vaseline, Paraffin, and Lanolin (VALAP) was then applied at 121°C to seal the edges of the membrane and the contents of the bottom layer. Next, the top of the membrane was coated with cold 10% 1:1 Laminin:Geltrex in cell culture media and incubated at 2–8°C for 1 h. Following incubation, the 35-mm dish was wrapped with parafilm and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Transfected MDA-MB-231 cells were placed on top of the coated membrane and incubated at 37°C for 24 h in 200 μL of cell culture media. Before imaging, 2 ml of cell culture medium with 30 μM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)piperazine-1-ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) was added to the dish (Figure 1, A and B). AIC images obtained by confocal spinning disk fluorescent microscopy revealed visible pores at the bottom of the membrane, and a fluorescent cell cultured on top of the ECM-coated membrane (Figure 1C).

Invadopodia contain a structured framework of cytoskeletal protein organization

Investigations have revealed numerous protein players involved in signaling cascades and required for the initiation and elongation of invadopodia (Beaty and Condeelis, 2014; Hao et al., 2025). Despite these findings, the position of individual cytoskeletal proteins and the overall architecture of the MT-associated cytoskeletal network within mature invadopodia remains unknown, and is challenging to observe with traditional assays.

To determine protein localization within invadopodia, the AIC was used to image MDA-MB-231 cells expressing fluorescently labeled cytoskeletal proteins (Figure 2 and Supplemental Video 1). TKS5 is a biomarker that exclusively localizes to invadopodia and is required for the formation and maturation of invadopodia (Blouw et al., 2015). Furthermore, when imaging MDA-MB-231 invadopodia using the AIC, the position of TKS5 remained statistically invariable when expressed alone or in co-expression experiments (mean maximum depth = 13.45 ± 0.28 μm). Therefore, the maximum depths of cytoskeletal proteins were compared relative to the position of TKS5, in three dimensions (XY, YZ, and XZ; Figure 2, A–F). Experiments revealed that cells expressing Paxillin, CLASP1, and Septin-7 did not display any ventral protrusions and therefore were never found within invadopodia, despite localizing as expected within the cell body to focal adhesions (Paxillin) and MTs (CLASP and Septin-7). Furthermore, overexpression of Paxillin showed cells with a more spread, lamellar morphology, an outcome that may be due to increased adhesion number and proximity at the cell-ECM interface (Chan et al., 2009; Figure 2G). These findings support previous work showing that knockdown of CLASP1/2 does not influence MT-associated degradation of the ECM by invadopodia (Chanez et al., 2021), and suggest that the canonical Septin 2,6,7 hetero-complex filaments are not critical within invadopodia during the elongation phase of invasion (Nakos et al., 2019a).

The remaining proteins examined were identified within invadopodia, and measurements of their positions revealed that MMP14 had the greatest depth from the cell body, while MAP4 showed the least depth when compared with TKS5, with a mean depth range of 0.59 μm to 5.93 μm for all proteins. Septin-9, F-Tractin, and EB3 measured depths of 2.41 μm, 2.69 μm, and 4.78 μm, respectively, each significantly increased compared with TKS5 (Figure 2H). These findings highlight that when MT-associated proteins enter invadopodia, they are positioned in a coordinated and structured array relative to their neighboring protein counterparts.

Cytoskeletal protein architecture regulates invadopodia elongation

To determine the effects of protein architecture on invadopodia elongation, MDA-MB-231 cells were co-transfected with a fluorescently-labeled marker of cell membranes (Green Fluorescent Protein (GFP-) or Red Fluorescent Protein (RFP-) CAAX; Dai et al., 1998; Madugula and Lu, 2016), along with fluorescently-labeled Septin-9, TKS5, MAP4, F-Tractin, MMP14, or EB3. AIC microscopy revealed that each of the cytoskeletal proteins investigated was arranged in an organized pattern with respect to the position of the distal tip of the invadopodia (Figure 3, A–F), and in the same pattern as measured relative to TKS5 (refer to Figure 2). Distance measurements from the invadopodia tip revealed that TKS5, Septin-9, MAP4, and F-Tractin were statistically similar and were positioned close to the tip of invadopodia, within a range of 0.97 μm to 1.23 μm, while MMP14 and EB3 were approximately twice the distance from the invadopodia tip, on average 1.89 μm and 2.26 μm, respectively (Figure 3G).

Analysis of invadopodia elongation revealed that cells expressing MMP14 had the greatest elongation of any single protein, which may be due to increased MMP14 activity at the ECM/invadopodia interface, a well-known requirement for cancer cell invasion (Steffen et al., 2008; Poincloux et al., 2009; Niland et al., 2022). Expression of Septin-9, F-Tractin, or EB3 resulted in significant increases in invadopodia elongation that were similar to one another, yet statistically longer than TKS5 (Figure 3H). Septin-9 is known to bind to both actin filaments and MTs, as well as the plasma membrane, to perform a variety of functions (Szuba et al., 2021; Kuzmić et al., 2022), which may explain why these proteins localize in close proximity within invadopodia. Measurements revealed that cells expressing MAP4 had the least elongation and were statistically similar to TKS5 (Figure 3H). These results support published evidence suggesting that MAP4 functions to inhibit MT catastrophe as well as to inhibit cargo transport along MTs (Nguyen et al., 1999; Holmfeldt et al., 2003; Semenova et al., 2014). Together, these data reveal a structured cytoskeletal protein architecture that regulates the depth of MDA-MB-231 invadopodia elongation.

Septin-9 and MAP4 are both MT-associated proteins capable of regulating the dynamic instability of MTs (Bustos-Morán et al., 2017; Nakos et al., 2019b; Moore et al., 2020). Published evidence highlights that Septin-9 functions to regulate MAP4 accessibility and binding to MTs, and that Septin-9 suppresses MT catastrophe and enhances the growth of MT plus-ends (Nakos et al., 2019b). To gain an understanding of how these two proteins might collaborate, co-expression experiments were performed and revealed increased invadopodia elongation greater than either protein alone, with a mean elongation of 23.04 μm (Figure 3H). These results suggest that elevated levels of Septin-9, in combination with MAP4, are sufficient to overcome the inhibitory effects measured for MAP4, and thereby promote invadopodia elongation.

Noncentrosomal microtubules promote the elongation of invadopodia

Published investigations focusing on the behaviors of two distinct subsets of MTs, centrosomal and Golgi-derived noncentrosomal MTs (NC-MTs), have so far identified that cells utilize these two separate MTOCs to perform different essential cellular functions (Wu and Akhmanova, 2017; Gavilan et al., 2018; Merenich et al., 2022). For example, centrosomal MTs are typically organized into a radial array and are critical to maintain cell shape, spindle organization, and transport of specific cargos (Sanchez and Feldman, 2017); while NC-MTs are typically organized in an asymmetric network and are required for cell polarity and movement (Martin et al., 2018). Indeed, overlap in these functionalities can occur, in particular when cells are experimentally manipulated to eliminate one or the other MTOC (Muroyama and Lechler, 2017; Wu and Akhmanova, 2017; Martin et al., 2018). To determine if either one or both MTOCs are necessary for the elongation of invadopodia in MDA-MB-231 cancer cells, co-expression of fluorescently labeled EB3 and CAAX in cells with All MTs was compared with cells with only NC-MTs and only centrosomal MTs. The methodologies for eliminating centrosomal versus NC-MTs have been previously published (Choi et al., 2010; Wong et al., 2015; refer to Materials and Methods).

Measurements revealed that MTs were significantly further from the invadopodia tip in cells with All MTs than either NC-MTs or centrosomal MTs, with the NC-MTs and centrosomal MTs statistically similar (Figure 4A). Measurements of invadopodia elongation revealed a significant decrease for centrosomal MTs compared with the All MTs and NC-MTs, which were statistically similar (Figure 4B). AIC experiments also revealed a distinct change in the morphology of the cell body of NC-MT cells, which showed a more spread and lamellar shape typical of mesenchymal cells, compared to cells with All MTs or centrosomal MTs, which appeared tall and rounded with a more epithelial morphology (Figure 4C). While each group of cells was capable of facilitating invadopodia elongation, this change in morphology may indicate a role for centrosomal MTs in maintaining cell body shape that is also present in cells with All MTs, but is distinct and nonoverlapping with the function of NC-MTs. Taken together, these data suggest that both centrosomal and NC-MTs are present within elongating invadopodia, while NC-MTs increase invadopodia elongation, similar to All MTs.

Noncentrosomal microtubules promote invadopodia initiation and drive invasion-facilitated cancer cell migration

The formation of invadopodia occurs through a coordinated progression by which cancer cells must first initiate successful degradation of the ECM and subsequently establish and elongate invadopodia, before intravasation of the cell body during metastasis (Leong et al., 2014; Tokui et al., 2014; Williams et al., 2019). The finding that NC-MTs increase invadopodia elongation (Figure 4B), coupled with the observed morphological anomalies in cells with only NC-MTs (Figure 4C), prompted experiments to measure the effects of MTOCs on invadopodia-mediated ECM degradation, and subsequently the effects of MTOCs on cancer cell migration (Figure 5). To measure invadopodia degradation, the mean area of degradation was calculated (Figure 5A). Data revealed that cells with NC-MTs had a significantly greater area of degradation compared with All MTs and centrosomal MTs (All MTs = 82.97 μm; NC-MTs = 175.66 μm; centrosomal MTs = 78.70 μm; Figure 5B). Analysis of invasion revealed a significant reduction in cells with centrosomal MTs compared with All MTs and NC-MTs (3.3-fold reduction; Figure 5C), and analysis of transwell migration revealed that NC-MTs significantly promoted invasion-facilitated cancer cell migration (Figure 5D). Taken as a whole, experimental analysis of MDA-MB-231 cancer cells cultured in the AIC revealed that NC-MTs and centrosomal MTs accomplish both independent and complementary effects that are critical to initiate invadopodia-mediated degradation and elongation, and provide an architectural map of the relative positions of various families of MT-associated proteins that regulate MT contributions to invadopodia dynamics (Figure 5E).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Request a protocol through Bio-protocol

Cell culture and transfections

MDA-MB-231 cells were purchased from ATCC (HTB-26) and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) high glucose (Cytiva #SH30284.01) with L-glutamine supplemented with 10% FBS (Corning #35–015-CV), Sodium Pyruvate (Cytiva #SH30239.01) penicillin–streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich #P4333–100 ml) and maintained at 37°C in 5% CO2. All transfections were performed in either Mirus Nucleofection buffer (Mirus Bio #MIR 50117) using a Lonza Nucleofector Device, setting A-013 or Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo-Scientific L3000008) per manufacturer’s instructions. MDA-MB-231 cells were plated on AICs at ~100,000 cells per dish (Cellvis Catalogue #: D35-14-1.5N). Cells were imaged 24 h after experimental cDNA transfections or 48 h for transwell migration assays.

Expression constructs

cDNAs, labeled with GFP- (CLASP1, EB3, and Paxillin), RFP-CAAX, and td-Tomato-F-Tractin were generated by the Michael Davidson lab (Florida State University) and provided by the Clare Waterman lab (National Institutes of Health/National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, Bethesda, Maryland). GFP-F-Tractin and mCherry-MAP4 were provided by the Schell lab (Johns Hopkins Medical Institutions, Baltimore, Maryland). mCherry-Septin-7, −9, and mCherry-TKS5 constructs were provided by the Spiliotis lab (University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA). TheTKS5 construct was generated by Sara Courtneidge (Oregon Health and Science University, Portland, OR). GFP-CAAX, GFP-Septin-9, and BFP-CAAX were obtained from Addgene (Plasmid # 86056, #71609, and #116856). All cDNA used for transfection was prepared using the Plasmid Mini or Maxi kit (IBI Scientific; Catalogue #: IB47180 and IB47120). Visual analysis was performed to verify that all fluorescent cytoskeletal protein constructs localized to the expected cytoskeletal structures within the cell body before conducting imaging experiments of invadopodia.

NC-MT and centrosomal MT preparation

MDA-MB-231 cells were incubated in 2.4 μM centrinone (Plk4 inhibitor that prevents centriole duplication and leads to centrosome depletion; Wong et al., 2015; Luo et al., 2019; Cayman Chemical Company #24684) for 5 d before use. Cell culture media containing centrinone was changed every day for the duration of 5 d, and cells were subsequently transfected on the 5th day. Visual analysis of centrosome depletion was performed via fluorescence imaging of gamma-tubulin and revealed an 89.1% reduction in centrosome number in the centrinone-treated group compared with the untreated control. For the NC-MT preparation, cells were transfected with 1.1 μg/μL γ-TuNA inhibitor plasmid DNA construct, pCMV2-FLAG-CDK5RAP2 F75A (51–100; a gift from R. Qi, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology; Choi et al., 2010; Sanders et al., 2017). Cells were allowed to express the γ -TuNA inhibitor for 24 h before imaging to ensure full Golgi-derived MT knockdown. Visual analysis of NC-MT depletion was determined via expression of fluorescent CAMSAP2, which specifically labels the minus-ends of Golgi-derived NC-MTs and is reduced when NC-MTs are depleted (Merenich et al., 2022).

Axial invasion chamber (AIC) construction

AICs were constructed in a biosafety cabinet with a 3.0 μm pore size, transparent type, 12 μm thickness, 6E5 pores/cm2, 13 mm diameter polyester track etch (PETE) membrane (1300025; Sterlitech), a 35 mm glass bottom dish (Cellvis Catalogue #: D35-14-1.5N), and a soft adhesive containing a 1:1:1 mixture of Vaseline, Paraffin, and Lanolin (VALAP). A 35-mm dish was placed on ice for 20 min, and a cold (2–8°C) 5 μl (bottom layer) mixture of 73% FBS, 8% cell culture media, and 9% hEGF (Sigma E9644) was applied to the middle of the glass-bottom dish. The dish was placed on ice whenever possible. Following the application of the 5 μl bottom layer, a single PETE membrane was placed carefully on top with tweezers so as not to disturb the bottom layer, but rather flatten it and not move the membrane after being placed. A pipette tip that was cut approximately 1/16-inch was used to withdraw a 10 μl volume of heated VALAP (121°C, from aliquoted Eppendorf tubes on a hot plate), and the VALAP was spread circularly around the membrane to secure and seal the bottom layer ECM mixture underneath. The dish was placed back on ice for a minimum of 10 min before proceeding. Using another cut pipette tip, 6 μl of 1:1 Geltrex (Thermo Scientific A1413301) to Laminin (Sigma-Aldrich L2020) mix diluted 1:10 with cell culture media was spread by pipette to coat the PETE membrane. The dish was incubated at 4°C for 1 h. Following cold incubation, 3 layers of parafilm wrap were applied around the circular dish. The dish was then incubated at 37°C for 30 min before removing the parafilm and plating cells on top of the ECM-coated PETE membrane in 200 μl of media. Dishes were incubated for 24 h at 37°C and 5% CO2, and 2 ml of cell culture media containing 30 mM HEPES was added before imaging. Limitations of the AIC imaging system included decreased resolution in the Z plane due to fluorescent light scattering through the ECM, along with rapid photobleaching, a limited cell culture area defined by the bottom well dish diameter, and the required use of a long working distance objective lens.

Live-cell imaging

All imaging was performed on a spinning disk (Yokogawa CSU-X1; Andor Technology) confocal microscope using a 60× 1.2 NA water immersion objective lens on a TiE microscope (Nikon) equipped with an electronic shutter (Smart shutter; Sutter Instruments) for transmitted illumination, TI-S-ER motorized stage with encoders and Ti/Ti2 piezo stage insert (Nikon), and a multi bandpass dichromatic mirror (Semrock) and bandpass filters (Chroma Technology) in an electronic filter wheel for selection of DAPI, GFP, or Texas red emission. Laser illumination (405, 488, and 561 nm) was provided by a Nikon LUN-F XL 405/488/561/640 nm laser combiner (50/60/50/40mW) that was shuttered with electronic shutters and directed to the confocal scan-head via an FC-PC8 output fiber (Nikon). AIC images of BFP, GFP, RFP, td-Tomato, and mCherry were acquired using an ORCA-Fusion BT Digital CMOS camera (Hamamatsu) operated in 14-bit mode. Z-stacks were acquired at 0.5 μm step intervals over a 90 μm range from top to bottom using 400–500 ms exposure times. Microscope system automation was controlled with NIS Elements software (Nikon). After acquisition, image processing involved optimization of image brightness and contrast using the Nikon Elements software package.

Transwell migration assay

MDA-MB-231 cells were plated in a 12-well plate (Celltreat PN:229112) at 250,000 cells per well overnight before transfection. The following day, cells were transfected with cDNA constructs of either GFP or GFP + γ -TuNA with Lipofectamine 3000 per the manufacturer’s instructions, with a 4 h incubation at 37°C in 5% CO2. The transwell migration assay was prepared using cell inserts of 8.0 μm pore size (Celltreat 230619) that were coated with 25 μl cold, 1:1 Geltrex to Laminin diluted 1:10 in cell culture media, followed by placing the cell inserts into a 12-well glass-bottom plate (Cellvis P12–1.5H-N). The 12-well plate was then incubated at 4°C for 1 h. After incubation, previously transfected cells were trypsinized with 0.05% Trypsin and pooled such that 400,000 cells were plated on the coated 8.0 μm pore insert and filled with 1 ml cell culture media, while the bottom well was filled with 1.5 ml cell culture media with chemoattractant of 22 nM hEGF. Cells were incubated for 48 h at 37°C and 5% CO2 to allow for transwell migration. Before imaging transwell migration, the cell insert was discarded and cells that migrated to the bottom well were imaged and counted (modified from Justus et al., 2023).

Image analysis

To measure protein localization within invadopodia, AIC Z-stack images were analyzed using the “slices view” function in NIS Elements software to determine the distance of maximum depth and distance from the distal tip of the plasma membrane for each construct. The 12 μm–thick PETE membrane location was defined as the Z-stack position when the membrane pores are first in focus on the bottom of the membrane. The Z-stack position at 12 μm above the PETE membrane location was defined as the interface of the cell base and the top of the membrane. The Z-stack position of maximum depth of each protein was measured as the distance from the cell base. The distance from the distal tip of the plasma membrane was measured as the difference between the maximum depth of the plasma membrane marker (CAAX) and the maximum depth measurement for each protein evaluated. To measure the degradation area, AIC images were analyzed by defining a binary threshold and calculating the area using the “automated measurement results” function in NIS Elements software. To measure invasion, AIC images were analyzed by counting the total number of RFP-CAAX–expressing cells per dish and counting the total number of RFP-CAAX invading cells, and then calculating and comparing the fold change for each group. To measure cell migration in the transwell migration assay, fluorescent cells on the bottom plate were counted by defining a binary threshold and using the object count function in NIS Elements software. All results were compiled with the Microsoft Excel data extrapolation function in NIS Elements and subsequently transferred to GraphPad Prism software v10.5 for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Invadopodia depth of elongation, distance from tip, and area degradation were measured with the number of replicates (N) for each group listed in the corresponding figure legends, and displayed as mean ± SEM. The percentage of cells invading AIC and fluorescent cells found on the bottom was determined from three separate experiments. An ordinary one-way analysis of variance test (ANOVA) was performed to determine statistical significance between the control groups and between each experimental group. For statistical significance, α was set to 0.05, yielding a 95% confidence level, and p values for significance were displayed as: p (*) < 0.05, p (**) < 0.005, p (***) < 0.0005, and p (****) < 0.0001. GraphPad Prism software v10.5 was used for all statistical analyses.
