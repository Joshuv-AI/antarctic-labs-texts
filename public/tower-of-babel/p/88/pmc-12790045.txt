Supragingival Plaque Microbial Signatures Between Radiation Related Caries and Common Caries
Authors: Rui-huan Gan, Jing Qu, Li-qing Lan, Da-li Zheng, You-guang Lu, Ming Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe clinical manifestations of radiation-related caries (RRC) differ considerably from those of common caries. This study aims to investigate potential differences in the microbial compositions of supragingival plaque between RRC and common caries.MethodsSupragingival plaque samples from 10 RRC and 10 patients with common caries were analyzed using Type IIB Restriction-site Associated DNA sequencing for Microbiome (2bRAD-M). Analyses of microbial diversity, relative abundance and composition were performed. LEfSe analysis was employed to identify differentially abundant species between the 2 groups. Additionally, 24 RRC and 33 common caries samples were examined using qRT-PCR. Species indicators of RRC were identified through random forest analysis. KEGG and COG functional prediction analyses were conducted to assess microbial functional pathways.Results2bRAD-M sequencing revealed a significant enrichment of Prevotella conceptionensis, Treponema vincentii, and 4 Nanoperiomorbus species in the RRC group. These findings were further validated by LEfSe and qRT-PCR analyses, which confirmed the dominance of P. conceptionensis in RRC. Random forest analysis identified P. conceptionensis as a potential biomarker for RRC. The bacterial abundance in RRC samples was higher than in common caries, particularly in pathways related to glucose metabolism, including 6-phosphofructokinase activity and sugar transferases involved in LPS biosynthesis.ConclusionsThis study uniquely identifies distinct microbial signatures in supragingival plaque associated with RRC compared to common caries. Prevotella conceptionensis was identified as a specific pathogenic bacterium linked to RRC. The underlying mechanisms may involve enhanced microbial glucose metabolism and an inflammatory response mediated by ferroptosis.

Introduction

Head and neck cancer (HNC) is the seventh most prevalent cancer worldwide.1 One of the primary treatment strategies for HNC is radiation therapy.2 Despite its effectiveness, radiation therapy is associated with several adverse effects, including radiation oral mucositis (OM), radiation-related caries (RRC), and radiation osteomyelitis.3,4 Among these, RRC affects approximately 29% to 37% of HNC patients after radiotherapy,5, 6, 7 making it a major complication. Compared to common caries, RRC is characterized by rapid progression, widespread distribution, and a tendency to affect the cervical regions and cusps of the teeth.8 Diagnosis is typically based on clinical presentation and radiotherapy history. The underlying causes of the differences between RRC and common caries are complex and remain controversial. Potential contributing factors include decreased salivary flow,9 direct damage to tooth enamel10,11 and alterations in the oral microbiome.12,13

Microorganisms play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of dental caries. Previous studies have mainly focused on comparing microbial composition in the oral cavity before, during, and after radiotherapy.14 Following intensity-modulated radiation therapy, reductions in microbial alpha diversity and species richness have been observed.15 Moreover, 16S rRNA sequencing studies have demonstrated that radiation can alter microbiome diversity, often leading to an increased abundance of Streptococcus.16,17 However, some reports found no significant differences in salivary microbiota between patients with and without RRC,18 while others noted a resurgence of beneficial taxa and a decrease in pathogenic bacteria post-radiotherapy.19 At the same time, changes in the abundance of common caries-related pathogens—such as Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, and Prevotella—have also been documented after radiotherapy.7,20 However, studies that directly compare the dental plaque between RRC and common caries are still lacking.

In addition, most existing studies rely on 16S rRNA sequencing, which is cost-effective and rapid but primarily identifies microbes at the genus level and lacks species-level resolution. While whole metagenome shotgun sequencing provides species-level insights, it is more expensive and requires a high amount of DNA input. Recently, Type IIB Restriction-site Associated DNA sequencing for Microbiome (2bRAD-M) has emerged as a cost-effective, culture-independent approach that enables high-resolution profiling of microbial communities at the species level.21, 22, 23 This technique requires only 1 pg of DNA per sample and is highly sensitive to low DNA concentrations,23 making it ideal for samples such as supragingival plaque, which often yield low DNA quantities. 2bRAD-M has been applied across various fields. For instance, researchers used this method to examine the oropharyngeal cancer (OPC) microbiome and found a higher relative abundance of Selenomonas sputigena, Nocardia farcinica and other species in OPC patients compared to those with tonsillitis.24 Liang et al. employed 2bRAD-M to study colorectal cancer and identified species such as Bosea lupini, Ralstonia mannitolilytica, and Porphyromonas pasteri as potential indicators of microbial translocation into the bloodstream.25 Similarly, Lim et al. used 2bRAD-M to analyze prosthesis plaque and discovered a significant enrichment of pathogenic bacteria in uncleaned prosthetic devices.26 Given its sensitivity to low DNA concentrations and its ability to resolve species-level microbial profiles, we selected 2bRAD-M to investigate the microbial differences between RRC and common caries.

We hypothesized that specific pathogenic microorganisms are present in RRC that contribute to its rapid progression and site-specific occurrence. In particular, we aimed to demonstrate that Prevotella conceptionensis may act as a specific pathogenic agent in RRC, potentially explaining its aggressive nature and treatment challenges. Validating this hypothesis is critical for elucidating the pathogenesis of RRC and developing targeted therapeutic strategies for affected patients.

Participants rinsed their mouths with sterile 0.9% sodium chloride solution. Teeth were isolated with cotton rolls and air-dried. Supragingival plaque was collected from multiple dentin-active caries lesions using a sterile spoon excavator, placed into a 1.5 mL EP tube (Axygen, USA) containing 200 μL of sterile PBS, and transported on ice. Samples were stored at −80°C, and DNA was extracted within 1 month.

DNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing

DNA was extracted from supragingival plaque samples using the TIANamp Micro DNA Kit (Tiangen, China), following the manufacturer's instructions, and stored at −20°C. Library preparation for 2bRAD-M sequencing was based on the protocol by Wang et al.,22 with minor modifications.

Briefly, 1 pg to 200 ng of DNA was digested with 4 U of BcgI restriction enzyme (NEB, USA) at 37°C for 3 hours. The digested products were ligated and then PCR-amplified. Amplified fragments were separated on an 8% polyacrylamide gel, and bands of approximately 100 bp were excised. DNA was eluted in nuclease-free water at 4°C for 6-12 hours.

Sample-specific barcodes were introduced using a second round of PCR with platform-specific barcode-bearing primers. Each 20 μL PCR reaction included 6 μL gel-purified DNA, 0.2 μM of each primer, 0.3 mM dNTPs, 1× Phusion HF buffer, and 0.4 U Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (NEB, USA). The PCR cycling conditions were 98°C for 5 s, 60°C for 20 s, and 72°C for 10 s, repeated for 16-28 cycles.

PCR products were purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Germany) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq PE150 platform. All 2bRAD-M sequencing and library construction were performed at Qingdao OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China).

2bRAD microbial database construction

A total of 404,199 microbial genomes, including bacterial, fungal, and archaeal sequences, were obtained from the GTDB and Ensembl databases. These genomes were subjected to in silico digestion using 16 Type IIB restriction enzymes. Unique species-specific 2bRAD tags—non-redundant markers that do not overlap with other species within the same taxonomic group—were identified. These species-level markers were compiled to construct a comprehensive 2bRAD microbial genome database.21,29

Alpha diversity was calculated using the “vegan” package. Beta diversity was estimated using the “vegan” package and displayed as Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) scatter plots. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25 and R software (4.2.1). For alpha diversity and functional prediction (COG and KEGG) were assessed by the Wilcoxon test. For beta diversity by permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). T-test analysis was used to compare the differences in microbial communities between NC and RC. The qRT-PCR data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The clinical manifestations of RRC differ significantly from those of conventional caries. These differences are attributed not only to reduced salivary flow and radiotherapy-induced damage to tooth hard tissues, but also to alterations in the oral microbiome, as reported by several studies.13,31 This study is the first to employ 2bRAD-M sequencing to investigate species-level differences in supragingival plaque microbiota between RRC and common caries. Alpha diversity analysis revealed no significant difference between the 2 groups, likely due to similar overall richness and evenness of bacterial species within each sample. However, beta diversity analysis showed distinct microbial community structures between groups, suggesting differences in community structure between the 2 groups. This finding aligns with results from Lu et al.,32 who also reported no change in alpha diversity but a dispersed microbiota pattern in post-radiotherapy patients based on beta diversity.

Further abundance analysis identified Corynebacterium, Prevotella, Streptococcus, and Actinomyces as dominant genera in both groups. These genera have been consistently implicated in caries pathogenesis in previous studies.33, 34, 35 The genus Capnocytophaga was found to be more abundant in supragingival plaque of caries-active patients than in caries-free individuals.36 Furthermore, Actinomyces,37 Corynebacterium,32 and Streptococcus38,39 have all been reported to increase in the oral or nasal mucosa of patients following radiotherapy. Similarly, an increased abundance of Veillonella has also been noted in post-radiotherapy patients.40,41

In our study, Prevotella species were more abundant in the RRC group than in the control group. Prevotella is known to play a dual role in the oral microbiome, often associated with both health and disease.42,43 Many Prevotella species contribute to oral inflammation and are frequently detected in dysbiotic biofilms associated with periodontal disease, carious lesions, endodontic infections, and other oral conditions.44 Different Prevotella species have also been used to distinguish between caries-active and caries-free individuals.45 To our knowledge, this study is the first to report elevated levels of Prevotella conceptionensis in RRC, identifying it as a dominant pathogenic species. This is consistent with previous findings where increased Prevotella abundance was observed in patients with rampant caries.46,47 Prevotella conceptionensis is a newly identified, uncultivable anaerobe, notable for its resistance to several antibiotics, including ampicillin, clindamycin, tetracycline, and moxifloxacin.48 It was initially detected in osteoarticular samples using direct 16S rRNA gene amplification,49 and later isolated from sinus biopsies, consistent with the buccal niche of many Prevotella species.50 To date, no studies have reported the presence of P. conceptionensis in the oral cavity. However, it has been suggested that complex, novel, and uncultivable Prevotella species and Prevotella-like organisms may dominate microbial communities in advanced caries.51 In addition to P. conceptionensis, the RRC group also exhibited higher abundances of Treponema vincentii and 4 species belonging to the genus Nanoperiomorbus. Since RRC lesions predominantly originate at the cervical region and progress rapidly, frequently extending to the root surface, root caries prevalence was significantly higher in the RRC group. In contrast, common caries primarily affected coronal surfaces, with a relatively low rate of root caries. However, for patients with common caries, we preferentially selected those with smooth surface caries located near the gingival margin. The Treponema has been previously reported to be associated with root caries,52 with a detection rate of only 1.8% in elderly root caries lesions.53 However, a comparison between coronal caries-active and caries-free populations in permanent teeth found a higher proportion of Treponema in the caries-active group.54 Interestingly, another study reported that the proportion of this genus was higher in coronal caries-free children than in children with coronal caries.55 In periodontitis, T. vincentii is more prevalent in shallow subgingival pockets.56 In our pilot qRT-PCR assays, primers targeting T. vincentii failed to yield detectable Ct values due to the low abundance; therefore, these data were omitted from the final analysis. In addition, there was no significant difference in the abundance of Treponema D buccale between the 2 groups based on both qRT-PCR and 2bRAD-M assays. Collectively, the contribution of Treponema to the present findings appears to be minimal and needs more research. Nanoperiomorbus, a genus within the phylum Candidatus Saccharibacteria (TM7), was first identified in patients with severe periodontitis.57 TM7 belongs to the Candidate Phyla Radiation (CPR) and is considered part of the so-called microbial "dark matter" due to its elusive nature.58,59 Subsequent studies have proposed that Candidatus Saccharibacteria may be a core component of the oral microbiome.60,61 The presence of periodontitis-associated taxa such as Treponema and Nanoperiomorbus in the RRC group may partly explain the clinical predilection of RRC for the cervical areas of teeth. However, this hypothesis requires further experimental validation.

Functional prediction analysis using the KEGG and COG databases revealed that several metabolic pathways were significantly upregulated in the RRC group compared to the NC group. Many of these pathways are closely associated with bacterial pathogenicity. Supporting our findings, previous studies have reported that alterations in Prevotella62 and Streptococcus63 abundance in the oropharyngeal microbiota of patients with COVID-19 or influenza A virus pneumonia were accompanied by changes in sphingolipid metabolism. Additionally, oral health studies have found that a common sphingomyelin metabolite exhibits a negative correlation with gingivitis and carotid intima-media thickness.64 Ferroptosis-related pathways, also elevated in our study, have been shown to be upregulated in patients with periodontitis.65,66 The enzyme 6-phosphofructokinase may serve as a key differential factor in the catabolism of fructose and sucrose by the gut microbiota. It also plays a role in the biosynthesis of lipopolysaccharides (LPS) and glycoconjugates essential for bacterial viability, particularly in pathogens such as Porphyromonas gingivalis.67,68 Notably, LPS biosynthesis has been found to be elevated in subgingival plaques of patients with periodontitis and gingival squamous cell carcinoma.69 Therefore, the higher microbial metabolic activity observed in the RRC group may help explain the more aggressive clinical manifestations of RRC compared to common caries.

This study identified distinct microbial profiles between RRC and common caries. The RRC group was enriched with Actinomyces and Prevotella, particularly Prevotella conceptionensis. Species-level analysis using 2bRAD-M revealed a significant enrichment of P. conceptionensis, 4 Nanoperiomorbus species, and Treponema vincentii in the RRC group. Conversely, Porphyromonas pasteri, Aggregatibacter sp000466335, Capnocytophaga periodontitidis, and Cardiobacterium hominis were more abundant in the NC group. qRT-PCR validation confirmed the higher expression of Prevotella conceptionensis in RRC samples. As a newly identified species in the oral cavity, P. conceptionensis presents a novel insight into the microbial pathogenesis of RRC. Additionally, the RRC group showed increased abundance in glucose metabolism–related pathways, including 6-phosphofructokinase and sugar transferases involved in LPS biosynthesis, suggesting that enhanced glucose and LPS metabolism may contribute to the rapid progression of RRC. Future studies could investigate the role of P. conceptionensis in accelerating RRC progression using in vivo and in vitro models. Moreover, P. conceptionensis shows promise as a plaque-derived biomarker, enabling early risk stratification and prompt intervention. Distinct microbiota signatures also differentiate RRC from common caries, supporting etiology-specific therapies, such as targeted antibiotics or probiotics against P. conceptionensis. Although microbial screening remains pre-clinical, our results underpin the development of point-of-care tools, such as Prevotella-specific test strips, for high-risk populations.

This study has several limitations that warrant discussion. First, the small sample size for sequencing analysis (n=10) limits statistical power to detect subtle differences. However, all specimens underwent rigorous screening against predefined inclusion criteria. As a pilot investigation, this study aimed to identify preliminary microbial community disparities, thereby laying the groundwork for larger-scale follow-up studies. To enhance data reliability within this constrained sample size, we employed 2bRAD-M technology. Comparative validation studies support its efficacy: 2bRAD-M achieves species-level resolution comparable to whole-metagenome shotgun sequencing (WMS), even with minimal samples (n = 3).21 Furthermore, the integrated MAP2B algorithm improves microbial identification accuracy by leveraging machine learning to reduce false positives.29 This methodological strength is evidenced in studies with similarly small cohorts (e.g., n = 5).70 Additionally, the relatively high abundance of Prevotella and P. conceptionensis in our samples may mitigate some negative effects of the limited size. To further address potential false positives, we validated differential species detected by 2bRAD-M using real-time PCR on a larger sample set (NC: n=33; RRC: n=24). Second, potential baseline heterogeneity between RRC and common caries groups should be acknowledged. Due to practical constraints during sample collection—notably, the logistical challenges of recruiting RRC patients coming from distant regions for follow-up visits who could not undergo prolonged oral examinations—comprehensive intraoral data (e.g., oral hygiene indices, periodontal status) were not systematically recorded. However, major confounders (age, sex, DMFT) were included to ensure group comparability. Future studies will implement standardized clinical data protocols to minimize inter-group variability. Third, while oral mucositis (OM), a common radiotherapy complication known to be influenced by oral microbiota,12 was recorded as present or absent post-radiotherapy, this binary assessment may introduce bias. The impact of radiotherapy and OM on specific bacteria, such as Prevotella, remains inconsistently reported.71, 72, 73 Crucially, studies suggest OM significantly affects salivary microbiota, with potentially less pronounced effects on dental plaque communities.74 Given that our samples were collected from carious plaque 1 year after radiotherapy—a time point when oral microbiota composition is thought to resemble pre-radiotherapy states14—the impact of OM status on our findings is likely minimal. Future studies incorporating rigorous OM phenotyping and grouping would be valuable to definitively assess its impact.
