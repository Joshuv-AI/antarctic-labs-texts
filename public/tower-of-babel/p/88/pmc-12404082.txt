The potential of teleaudiology in remote areas as the Arctic: a scoping review of treatment and rehabilitation in remote and low-resource settings
Authors: Katrine Rosenquist Kirk, Michael Bille, Ramon Gordon Jensen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHearing disability constitutes a major health care issue especially among indigenous populations and in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC). The authors are engaged in organising audiological services in Greenland, where the geographical challenges highlight the potential of tele-audiology. The search strategy was created in the PubMed database combining keywords and MeSH Terms with “OR” in each domain: “Hearing” and “Telemedicine” and then combined using “AND”. Telerehabilitation was defined as: Rehabilitation of patients with hearing loss enabled by one or more consultations with a specialist over a remote connection. The search was reproduced in Cochrane. The final search resulted in a total of 2,273 articles. After screening, 32 articles were reviewed. The included studies cover a broad variety of study designs, age groups, and countries. All included studies were analysed and summarised according to themes of relevance to an arctic setting. The review concludes that the technological development in tele-audiology has come far. Importantly, none of the studies reviewed provided clear evidence that tele-audiology delivers an inferior service, though further high-quality research is needed to confirm this. However, the evidence level in the studies was generally low. The usability of tele-audiology in cross-cultural settings and LMICstill need further investigation.KEYWORDS: Greenland, arctic medicine, indigenous populations, telemedicine, audiology, rehabilitation, remote areas, treatment, LMIC, hearing impairment

Introduction

Rationale

Hearing disability constitutes a major health care issue especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC). Thus, more than 5% of the world’s population (approximately 430 million people) has a hearing disability that requires some degree of rehabilitation [1]. 80% live in LMIC having low access to hearing specialists [1,2]. It is estimated that 93% of the population in LMIC have fewer than 1 audiologist per million citizens [2]. Hearing impairment affects the ability to communicate with others and thereby impact cognitive, physical, and mental function, and hearing-impaired individuals experience a lower health-related quality of life [3]. Device treatment such as a cochlear implant or a conventional hearing aid has shown to increase mental health quality of life significantly [3]. The sense of hearing plays various roles in different age groups [4,5]. Being an older adult suffering from age-related hearing loss is associated with social isolation, loneliness, and depression as well as general frailty and cognitive impairment [5]. Hearing loss among children has consequences for normal development and can entail delay in speech and language development and is associated with lower quality of life by affecting social functioning and cognitive abilities [6,7]. Early diagnosis and treatment of hearing loss is crucial for the avoidance of negative consequences no matter the age group.

The high demand for audiology makes it necessary to continuously optimise clinical practices to treat as many individuals with hearing loss as possible. Apps for self-testing and school-screenings for hearing loss have already optimised the diagnostics of hearing loss. However, knowledge of tele-audiology treatment and rehabilitation is lacking behind. It is important that possibilities in new technologies are being investigated to make sure that new and more effective ways of treating and rehabilitating are not being overlooked. Therefore, the focus of this review is tele-audiology studies on rehabilitation and hearing loss treatment.

Telemedicine has proven effective when compared to face-to-face visits when used in several other chronic conditions such as chronic lung diseases, diabetes management, chronic heart conditions, and mental health treatment [7]. Furthermore, research has shown that telemedicine reduces number of appointments cancelled due to illness, saves time and expenses on transportation, reduces time to follow up from time of diagnosis, and can enable specialist treatment in remote and rural areas with otherwise low access to specialist treatment [7–10].

The authors of this review are engaged in creating new audiological services in Greenland where earlier studies have shown a high prevalence of untreated hearing loss in school children [11]. There are currently no specialists, technical audiologists, audiology assistants, or educational staff dedicated to audiology in Greenland. Since 1962, the provision of audiological care has been managed by Danish audiology departments, which have conducted biannual outreach visits. These visits have typically included physician audiologists and audiology assistants. Nuuk has been visited annually, while larger coastal towns have generally been visited every second year.

Hearing loss among children and adolescents in Greenland is closely linked to otologic pathology, particularly due to a high prevalence of chronic otitis media. Additionally, there is a high prevalence of noise-induced hearing loss across all age groups, largely due to hunting-related noise exposure. Consequently, both conductive and sensorineural hearing losses are widespread across the population.

Greenland is an autonomous territory of the Kingdom of Denmark and comprises 2,166,086 square kilometres, with 80% of the country covered with ice [12]. The total population is 56,421 people (Jan 2021) and this makes it the country with the world’s lowest population density with only 0.3 people per square kilometre when exclusively counting the ice-free areas of the country [12]. All cities and settlements are located along the coastline, mostly on the southern part of the west coast. No roads connect the cities, and travel is only possible with either ship or airplane [12]. A survey from 2017 showed that 88% of citizens in Greenland have access to internet and 89% have access to a smartphone at home [13]. Audiology services in Greenland are generally limited as there are no specialists in Greenland, and with the extreme weather conditions and infrastructural challenges it is time-consuming and expensive for travelling specialists from Denmark to get around the country. Tele-audiology is, therefore, highly relevant to consider when organising audiology treatment in Greenland. Thus, the content of this article will focus on intervention studies to explore the existing knowledge of tele-audiological treatment in remote areas to address the insufficiencies of current audiological treatment in Greenland.

Tele-audiology services can be delivered through synchronous or asynchronous models. Synchronous tele-audiology involves live communication between the patient and audiologist, allowing for immediate feedback and interaction. However, this approach requires both parties to be available simultaneously and depends on a stable internet connection. In contrast, asynchronous tele-audiology does not involve real-time interaction. Instead, data is collected from the patient, reviewed by the audiologist, and adjustments, such as hearing aid modifications, are made, and sent back to the patient. This model offers greater flexibility and eliminates the need for a stable internet connection but limits direct communication between the patient and audiologist [14].

The intent of this review is to create an overview of existing literature from original studies, including case studies and case series, focusing on teleaudiological intervention or rehabilitation in indigenous populations and remote or low-resource settings. Despite differences in economic classification LMICs and remote areas – whether within high-income or lower-income settings – often face similar healthcare challenges, including limited access to facilities, shortages of audiologists, and significant geographic barriers. We wish to explore whether remote hearing aid fittings and tele-audiological services are as effective as traditional in-clinic services.

The purpose of the article is to provide guidance regarding the opportunities offered by remote audiology to the target audience of clinicians and policy makers wishing to create remote services in areas, such as Greenland, where conventional audiology services are insufficient or non-existing.

Method

Eligibility criteria

The focus of this review was on the usability and limitations of telerehabilitation in audiology in a clinical setting. Telerehabilitation was defined by the following: Rehabilitation of patients with hearing loss enabled by one or more consultations with a specialist over a remote connection.

Subjects described in the results were primarily defined before reviewing the articles, while some additional subjects were continuously added as new relevant subjects were discovered in the articles in the reviewing process. Subjects defined prior to examining the articles were as follows: usability of tele-audiology in cross-cultural settings, user attitudes towards tele-audiological consultations, audiologist attitudes, logging data, rural areas, apps, cost effectiveness, general study qualities and bias assessments. Subjects that were added during the reviewing process were as follows: remote clinic setups with local facilitators, technical challenges, data safety, remote patient education and patient-to-patient interaction, auto- and self-fitting hearing aids.

Information sources

The databases PubMed and Cochrane Library were searched using relevant search terms indicated below, and the search in PubMed and Cochrane was performed in collaboration with a librarian from Royal Danish Library.

Search strategy

Titles and abstracts were initially screened by one reviewer to remove clearly irrelevant studies. Full-text articles were then independently screened by two reviewers using the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any disagreements were resolved through discussions, and when consensus could not be reached, the senior reviewer was consulted to make the final decision. The search was performed in PubMed initially (Table 1 and reproduced in Cochrane later. From the Cochrane search all duplicates were removed and the remaining articles were then screened according to the same steps as described above.

Inclusion criteria

Studies performed in LMIC, according to the definition by The World Bank [15], countries with indigenous populations, or remote areas in high-income countries were included if they were addressing tele-audiological intervention, treatment, and rehabilitation. Studies of both quantitative, qualitative or mixed-methods approaches were included.

Exclusion criteria

(i) Articles written in other languages than English, (ii) articles concerning diagnostics such as tele-otoscopies, hearing tests and screenings, apps for audiometry etc., (iii) articles about usage of telephones and television for hearing disabled, (iv) articles concerning other diseases than hearing disability, e.g., stutter and other speech disabilities, vertigo, tinnitus etc., (v) articles not based on original studies, e.g. other scoping reviews or systematic reviews, (vi) articles concerning cochlear implants if not used in a low resource setting.

General description of the included studies

The 32 articles included in this review were predominantly from the USA (n = 12, 38%), Australia (n = 8, 25%) and South Africa (n = 7, 22%). Nine studies originated from the LMIC. Eight studies were conducted in rural areas: two in rural communities in Arizona near the Mexican border, one in Alaska, one in Australia, one in Brazil, one in South India, and two in rural Saudi Arabia. Most studies included adult participants exclusively (n = 24, 75%), one study included patients aged five years and above (n = 1, 3%), and the remaining 7 studies included children and/or young adults from 0–23 years of age. The main part of the studies was conducted between 2020 and 2022 (n = 11, 34%) and between 2023 and 2024 (n = 11, 34%), and the oldest study was from 2012. The number of participants across the studies ranged from 3 to 42,697. Most studies had participant numbers between 11 and 100 (n = 16, 50%), followed by seven studies with 1 to 10 participants (n = 7, 22%) and seven studies with 101–400 participants (n = 7, 22%). Additionally, two studies included more than 1,000 participants. The most used study designs were case series (n = 6, 19%), randomised controlled trials (n = 6, 19%), and cross-sectional surveys (n = 6, 19%), other study designs like case studies, feasibility studies, cross over studies, cluster-randomised controlled trials, retrospective case control studies, and different qualitative methods designs like focus group discussions and interviews were also used. Seven studies included audiologist’s or other health care providers (n = 7, 22%), 24 studies included patients (n = 24, 78%) and one study included both health care professionals and patients (n = 1, 3%).

Remote clinic setups with local facilitators

Seven studies described the feasibility and efficiency of a setup using a remote clinic close to the hearing aid user that could facilitate a consultation with an audiologist [17–23]. The setup in all studies was that the remote clinic supplied the equipment and a facilitator, and the audiologist could make either follow-up consultations or hearing aid fittings remotely with the help of the facilitator. All four studies concluded that the setups were usable even though study design variated.

A similar cross-over study using remote consultations with a facilitator making video call contact with an audiologist was performed in South Africa by Tao et al. [18]. This study included all types of hearing losses including sensorineural hearing loss with a lower age limit was 18 years and no upper limit. 56 patients participated in the study and were divided into two groups each consisting of 14 experienced and 14 new hearing aid users, respectively. All participants initially received a conventional face-to-face hearing aid fitting, and subsequently the participants were divided into two groups. One group received a follow-up consultation remotely followed by a face-to-face follow-up, and the other group received the same consultations in opposite order. The remote consultations used two laptops by the audiologist: one to access the remote software allowing the hearing aids to be fitted remotely and one to supply video and audio communication with the patient. At the remote consultations the patients were located at the clinic or at a local healthcare facility and the audiologist conducted the fitting from a remote site. Face-to-face consultations and remote consultations did not vary significantly regarding duration with the median time being 43 minutes for the remote consultations and 41 minutes for face-to-face consultations. The study used the following validated questionnaires to quantify the effectiveness and the quality of the consultations: the International Outcome Inventory for Hearing Aids (IOI-HA) [24], Hearing Aid Users’ Questionnaire (HAUQ) [25] and the Client Oriented Scale of Improvement (COSI) [26]. The study concluded that: “Teleaudiology follow-up consultations in this study were of similar effectiveness, quality, length of time, and can deliver similar results as face-to-face consultations when implemented into routine practice settings for new or experienced HA users” [18]. In addition, the study showed that a blended follow-up service consisting of both remote and a face-to-face consultation can provide an effective and high-quality service without any influence of the order of the services.

Another design was used by Pross et al. in a retrospective case-control study with 42,697 veterans using hearing aids where a remote clinic setup for hearing aid fitting was tested [17]. The intervention group received hearing aid services via video connection with an audiologist assisted by a local facilitator, typically being a staff member or technician trained to support teleaudiology consultation. During the remote fitting the audiologist could make hearing aid adjustments and programming using remote software and provide patient education and counselling on the real-time. 1,009 of the veterans received tele-audiological (TA) follow-up, and 41,688 received in-person (IP) care. Based on the International Outcome Inventory for Hearing Aids questionnaire (IOI-HA) [24], the study concluded the following: “[…] providing hearing aids to veterans through TA is not inferior to IP as both delivery models demonstrate equally high levels of effectiveness” [17]. Pross et al. assessed possible biases in the study. The decision on who to include in each group was not randomised which created a potential for selection bias. Moreover, a risk of potential response bias was reported caused by a low participation in the International Outcome Inventory for Hearing Aids (IOI-HA) [24] survey where only one third of the participants responded.

In a case study from Brazil by Penteado et al. with three patient cases was reported where remote hearing aid fittings were tested using a setup with a local facilitator connected with online video and audio connection to an audiologist in a specialised centre. First, the facilitators were trained to assist in the remote hearing aid fittings by real-time video and audio connation with an audiologist they were trained in placing the hearing aids correctly on the patients, how to adjust the hearing aids, and how to give hearing aids specific guidance to the patients regarding how to clean the hearing aids. Second, the three participants had their hearing aids fitted remotely with the assistance of the trained local facilitators. The participants were adults with moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss in one or both ears. In the study, all three patients were fitted remotely, and the duration of the fitting was between 15 and 20 minutes. Challenges in the study were mostly caused by issues with internet connection but the study found that the remote fitting process, aided by video and audio technology, was effective and satisfying for both patients and audiologists. This suggests, according to the authors, that a setup with a remote clinic and a local facilitator could be a feasible model for hearing aid fittings, especially in remote areas with lower access to audiology treatment [19].

Remote Setups without Local Facilitators

Remote consultation setups without any professionals assisting at the patient’s side was seen in seven studies [7,8,27–31]. These setups will, however, be described in detail under some of the other themes based on the rest of the content in the articles.

Usability of tele-audiology in cross cultural settings

This predefined subject was not examined in the articles of this review indicating that this subject still needs to be further investigated.

User attitudes towards tele-audiological consultations

Eight studies mention user attitudes when assessing the usability of remote solutions regarding audiological rehabilitation [7,8,21,27,30,32–35].

In a two-phase study from The United States, Angley et al. evaluated user attitudes towards tele-audiological haring aid follow-up appointments [27]. The first phase included 50 hearing aid users receiving remote follow-up appointments while located at the clinic. Participants had to instal a distance support (DS) software using a guide and would then connect to an audiologist located in a different room in the clinic. In phase two, 21 of the patients (mean age: 64.4) from phase one and one additional patient received a remote follow-up appointment having to instal the DS software using the guide and then connect to an audiologist located at the clinic. Most patients succeeded in downloading the software, but older patients and patients with lower technological confidence needed more support. The patients from phase one felt the possible strengths from the DS appointments as being: “[…] time saving (48%), convenience (38%), and a reduction in travel cost (20%)” [27]. From phase one, some of the weaknesses addressed by the patients were: “[…] lack of face to face time with their provider (22%) and possible computer and software issues (12%)” [27]. After the second phase of the study, 60% of the participants answered that they would prefer a DS appointment in the future over face-to-face appointments.

A case study of ten families with small children (age 30–79 months) compared tele-audiological and face-to-face sessions as early interventions [30]. In this study by Havenga et al., parents mention similar advantages with remote sessions as reported in Angley et al., namely travel time and expenses. Another positive aspect mentioned by the parents was that having the consultations at home makes the children appear more comfortable. Nickbakht et al. [32] interviewed 17 families all having children with congenital hearing loss and 11 professionals working with the families on their attitudes towards using tele-audiology such as video conferences. These interviews revealed that tele-audiology can be a viable solution for early hearing support services to families of children with hearing loss if barriers are addressed properly, these being the culture of face-to-face services, motivation, and stakeholder’s knowledge.

Arnold et al. [8] evaluated effect and patient satisfaction of a model in a case series study. The study combined an initial conventional audiology consultation with two following appointments made remotely using an app. In this study, part of the outcome measurements were total scores on the Telehealth Acceptance Questionnaire (TAQ) [36] which evaluate general acceptance of telehealth using eight questions scored from 0 to 10 in level of satisfaction. This study included 10 first-time hearing aid users ( >70 yrs), and they completed the TAQs before and after the remote follow-up appointments. TAQ answers revealed an improvement in telehealth attitude from a mean score of 5.49 (SD = 0.92) at baseline to a mean at 8.79 (SD = 1.48) after the two remote appointments. This could indicate that the users’ attitude towards telehealth get more positive when they get more experienced [8].

In another case series, Muños et al. [7] had two audiologists performing remote appointments with four families of children ( < 5 yrs) with permanent hearing loss (HL) and hearing aids (HAs). In general, parents were positive towards virtual appointments both before and after the trial. Families described advantages as saving time on transportation, ease of access to consultations, and the possibility of attending an appointment despite illness in the family. No negative attitudes towards virtual visits were mentioned by the families in the questionnaires. This might, however, be based on the fact that the families received a combined treatment with virtual visits and in person visits which could compensate for potential inadequate parts of tele-audiology [7].

Audiologist attitudes

To determine the possibilities in tele-audiology, the audiologist’s own attitudes towards the method’s usability and limitations are also important to clarify. Most studies indicate a positive attitude from the audiologists [27,34,35,37–39]. The study by Angley et al. [27] showed an “[…] audiologist satisfaction with 82% of the completed appointments” and in Winter et al. [34]. with a “[…] 82% in agreement that the service was more efficient than a traditional in-person model and 14,3% unsure”. Likewise, Muños et al. [7] observed a positive attitude towards remote appointments from two audiologists before and after providing remote services to four families. A risk of bias must, however, be considered on this matter assuming most audiologists attending trials regarding remote audiology have a positive opinion towards it beforehand.

Some concerns were raised by audiologists in other studies, like the study by Arnold et al. [8], where patients’ ability to work the technology (Wi-Fi and Bluetooth) or having communicational challenges with the patients on the remote connection is mentioned as potential challenges. In the study by Govender et al. [22] from South Africa audiologists were very willing to use teleaudiology but also expressed concerns especially with feasibility of implementing tele-audiology because of resource-related – and technical barriers with limited access to the necessary equipment and internet connectivity. Audiologists mention potential barriers about implementing tele-audiology services in rural areas in a study performed in India by Ramkumar et al. [40] because of the general lack of professional staff and appropriate equipment.

Glista et al. [41] investigated audiologist attitudes towards the potential of using apps for adolescent patients (aged 12–17 years). Through virtual focus groups with 12 audiologists the topic was being discussed. In general, they saw potential in medical health apps in audiology to enhance patient engagement and autonomy in hearing aid management. For some of the participating audiologists their own comfort using technology could be a barrier and they stressed the importance of structured training if tele-audiology should be implemented successfully.

Technical challenges

As mentioned earlier, some studies indicated worries from audiologists regarding technical demands imposed on the hearing aid users regarding remote consultations. Havenga et al. [30] observed technical difficulties such as video and audio quality due to the strength of the internet connection and difficulties with hard- and software. In a case study by Steuerwald et al. [31], two paediatric HA users received follow-up appointments remotely, and in one out of the two cases the family had technical difficulties downloading the software and had to contact the support desk in the telehealth department of the hospital to successfully download the software. However, no technological difficulties were described during the remote session [31]. In the qualitative study by Ramkumar et al. [40] from India issues with internet connectivity, especially in the remote areas of India, was reported from different health care providers based on semi-structured interviews and focus group discussions. In the study by Govender et al. [22] from South Africa internet connectivity was also mentioned as a significant barrier in supplying tele-audiology services especially in rural and underserved areas.

Logging data

Few measurement tools are available for extracting objective and quantitative data in the field of audiology. Logging data from devices is one way to quantify HA use without bias. The tool was used in four studies to either measure effectiveness of remote sessions or for comparison of tele-audiological appointments with conventional face-to-face appointments [7,8,20,42].

In a case study of four families, Muñoz et al. [7] used logging data to investigate the effects of remote follow-up appointments. The participating families had children ( <5 years) with a permanent hearing loss treated with hearing aids. In this study, logging data showed an average increase of 3.5 hours of the daily hearing aid use of the children. Baseline hearing aid use was 7 hours a day, and after the 6 months study period the average daily use reached 10.5 hours. The average number of visits in total were 14 for each family of which 69% were virtual and the rest in-office visits. The study shows positive effects on the use of hearing aids and positive attitudes of both families and audiologists towards the tele-audiological appointments. Whether the results specifically tell anything about effectiveness of tele-audiological appointments seems unclear. Fourteen visits in 6 months are, however, probably more than what families normally receive, and the logging data results could be a consequence of an increase in appointments resulting in more family-focus which improves HA use.

Remote patient education and patient to patient interaction

Remote audiology can enable more frequent contact with audiologists for follow-up sessions and remote fittings of hearing aids. Another potential in online services is to educate patients with interactive training programs to make them more confident in the usage of hearing aids.

A hybrid tele-audiology method was tested in the study by Khatib et al. [43] with a 4-week online auditory training (AT) program for hearing rehabilitation combined with face-to-face meetings, WhatsApp texts and phone calls with a clinician. The AT-program consisted of an online training platform called the Listening and Communication Enhancement (LACE) [44] with online sessions training word memory, rapid speech, speech in noise and competing speaker. LACE consisted of 11 sessions lasting from 25 to 45 minutes and the patients finished a mean of 9,3 sessions a week during the 4-week program. The study by Khatib et al. [43] concluded that “[…] a hybrid tele-audiology model to provide audiological rehabilitation (such as online AT) may be feasible for implementation in low-resourced contexts like South Africa, with minimal additional costs, workload and time (face-to-face clinical contact) implied in current services”.

Rural areas

Tele-audiology provides the unique possibility of reaching remote areas with low or no access to specialised intervention. Studies in different remote areas of Australia, Alaska, Brazil, South India and rural communities in Arizona near the Mexican border investigate the benefits and challenges of teleaudiology when applied to a remote location [9,10,19–22].

A study in rural and remote areas of Australia by McCarthy et al. [9] tested videoconferences lasting more than one hour as a rehabilitative intervention for hearing and vision disabled children. 156 children aged up to 18 years were included in the study. 97 surveys were sent out to families. However, only 52 questionnaires were returned introducing a risk of response bias to the study. Among the families participating in the survey, their attitude towards the teleaudiology program was generally described as positive.

In a study performed in Alaska by Emmett. et al. [10], telemedical follow-up after a positive school hearing screening was compared to conventional follow-up. The study design was a cluster-randomised study covering 15 Alaskan communities in USA. Seven communities with 510 children were randomised to conventional referral to a primary care provider who would then determine if a referral to a specialist was needed. The other eight communities with 481 children were randomised to the intervention receiving telemedical referral directly to a specialist bypassing the traditional primary care referral process. The telemedical follow up consisted of live a video consultation and remote review of the data collected during the screening process on the school. Less time from referral to follow-up was reported in the intervention group. Mean time to follow-up on was 41.5 days in the communities receiving tele-medical referrals compared to 92.0 days in the primary care referral pathway. The study shows potential of using teleaudiology in rural communities: “This study has implication for improving access to specialty care in low-resource settings globally where specialists are typically located in cities and are not easily accessible in rural communities” [10]. The article also describes that the Alaskan region where the study was conducted already has telemedical infrastructure and that telemedicine is a routine part of the health care system which limits the generalisability of the study in terms of other communities.

Brazil has a small number of specialised centres for hearing health services (approximately 140) considering the size of the country [19]. Tele-audiological treatment could prove beneficial by allowing a broader and more efficient coverage of the whole country concerning availability of audiological treatment. A case study of three adults performed by Penteado et al. [19] tested the feasibility of tele-audiological hearing aid fittings made by a local facilitator connected with video and audio to an audiologist in a specialised centre. According to the authors, all three cases were successfully fitted suggesting that tele-audiology could be feasible in Brazil.

In a study by Ramkumar et al. [40] health care services in rural areas of South India are being discussed. The study focusses on rehabilitation of children with hearing and speech-language disorders through semi-structured interviews and focus group discussions with 50 public sector health care providers. This article states that that rural areas in India receive fewer health care services because of deficiency in both health care personnel and infrastructure. If the services in these areas potentially are available the access is complicated due to limited public transportation, poor road connectivity and long distances and travel time [40]. The authors state a need for strengthened services in rural areas and highlight a potential in the use of tele-practice to provide it. Similar challenges are described in rural areas of South Africa in the article by Govender et al. [22] with under-resourced areas with limited access to healthcare, long distances and limited public transportation options and lack of access to reliable internet.

Data safety

When visits are performed remotely, patient-sensitive data is exchanged on an online connection highlighting the need to secure data safety. In an article by Havenga et al. [30], Skype was used to facilitate consultations on virus-free computers were used with anti-virus software. Havenga et al. [30] also used audit controls to monitor how often data was accessed or released to monitor the data. Another study, performed by Penteado et al. [19], used the “Avast” antivirus program in their tele-consultations where Skype was also used to enable the contact between patient and audiologist. Both studies recognise the need for protecting patient data in telemedicine settings, especially since there is a rising use of telemedicine worldwide [19,30].

Apps

Some older studies (2011–2016) use computer programs to obtain communication between audiologist and patient [17,19,45]. More recent studies (2019–2022) use smartphone apps to supply the remote communication for synchronous tele-audiology setups [8,29,46]. Use of smartphones and apps is very common, and smartphones with mobile networks make people able to access apps almost everywhere. Several studies testing apps for different purposes in hearing rehabilitation have been reported.

Convery et al. [29] investigated an app that enables patients to contact an audiologist and communicate in real-time and receive new settings on their hearing aids directly – a synchronous hearing aid fitting model. The study tested efficacy and potential to improve hearing care access in a randomised controlled trial with 30 adults. All participants attended and initial face to face consultation with an otoscopy and a pure tone air- and bone-conduction audiometry. All patients also received hearing aid fittings bilaterally approximately 2 weeks after the initial measures. Next, half were allocated to a control group receiving face-to-face hearing aid adjustments in the following six weeks, while the other half were allocated to the intervention where they had to report any hearing aid problems in the app in the same period. The study showed that almost half of the requests made in the app required physical assistance or other modifications that could not be handled through the app. The study thereby illustrates that apps and remote audiology in many cases cannot replace face-to-face consultations completely but might work in a blended model.

An app for self- and auto-fitting was tested on 10 people with normal hearing in the study by Patel et al. [46]. In the study, they developed an app with a default compression system to improve sound quality by amplifying soft sounds and preserving sound levels of loud sounds. The user could also change the parameters of the hearing aid manually in the app for personal preferences. In a survey after a hearing test, the study participants’ mean opinion scores on the sound quality improved when they used the app compared to when using a hearing aid without the app. In the study by Sousa et al. [47] performed in South Africa and app was also used to adjust hearing aids. In this setup, the app was used to enable the participants to adjust their own hearing aids. The study will be described in further detail in the next section.

Auto- and self-fitting hearing aids

New technology has made it possible to develop self-fitting hearing aids (SFHAs). SFHAs enable users to set up hearing aids that fit individual hearing losses without the need for assistance from an audiologist. The devices can perform automated in situ hearing threshold tests thereby determining personalised settings of hearing aids, and additional fine tuning can be done manually by using an app. Conventional hearing aid models require the fitting procedure to be performed by an audiologist fitting the hearing aids manually in the clinic [48].

A commercial SFHA (“The Companion” from Sound World Solutions) was tested on 27 experienced hearing aid users and 25 new hearing aid users in a study by Keidser et al. [48]. Twenty-two experienced and 16 new hearing aid users succeeded the self-fitting using an instructional guide provided and available help from a clinical assistant. Fourteen participants ended up having clinician-driven fittings. At inclusion, the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) was performed, and it was concluded that poorer cognitive function was connected to more difficulty handling the SFHAs. The study showed no significant difference on different outcomes like speech recognition in noise, self-perceived outcomes from validated questionnaires, Abbreviated Profile of Hearing Aid Benefit (APHAB) [49] and Satisfaction with Amplification in Daily Life (SADL) [50], and coupler measurements between successfully self-fitted and clinician-fitted hearing aids.

A smartphone-based hearing system called Hearing Aid Learning and Inference Controller (HALIC) was developed and tested in a study by Aldaz et al. [42]. The HALIC system could be trained to learn user preferences, and 16 hearing impaired adults tested the app in a six-week trial period. The system was connected to a smartphone, and sensor data were recorded including environmental sound classification, general sound level, and location of the device. The app was evaluated by study participants with a blinded listening evaluation comparing sounds by the trained system to sounds from the hearing aids’ untrained systems. The test showed that participants preferred the trained settings in 49% of the time suggesting that the auto fitting HALIC system still needs further development.

Another self-fitting hearing aid device was tested in a study by Chang et al. from 2023 [51]. 30 mandarin-speaking participants in the age span between 23 and 64 years and a mean age of 46 years participated in the study testing an iPhone app connected to a hearing aid using Bluetooth. Participants adjusted the volume using an app and their mean sentence reception threshold (SRT) was tested. Results were compared to the same patient’s SRT’s using unaided devices and preset program-aided devices, programmed from hearing tests. The study concludes that “Although both the self-fitting program-aided and preset program-aided conditions offered benefits relative to the unaided condition in quiet environments, only the self-fitting program-aided condition conferred substantial benefits in environments with 90° noise” [51]. Based on a questionnaire by the authors another important factor for the willingness from patients to learn to use the app is age [51]. This suggests that younger age groups might be more likely to benefit from self-fitting devices.

In South Africa, Sousa et al. [47] tested the effectiveness of an over the counter (OTC) self-fitting hearing aid compared to hearing aids fitted by audiologists following best practices in a 6-week study period. The study included 68 adults with self-perceived mild to moderate hearing loss and the participants were divided into two groups. One group receiving self-fitting hearing aids provided with instructional material and an app for smartphones, where they could adjust the hearing aids themselves. The control group received audiologist fitted hearing aids within person fittings adjusting sound, comfort adjustments and provided education and counselling. After 2 weeks of trial, the two groups were offered adjustments either remotely in the self-fitting group or in person in the audiologist fitted group. Validated questionnaires Abbreviated Profile of Hearing Aid Benefit (APHAB) [49] and the International Outcome Inventory for Hearing Aids (IOI-HA) [24] were used to measure self-reported benefits after 2 and 6 weeks. After 2 weeks the participants in the self-fitted group reported slightly more benefit and satisfaction compared to the audiologist fitted group. After 6 weeks the two groups reported the same level of satisfaction and benefit. Speech recognition testing was performed after 2 and 6 weeks and showed no statistically significant difference between the two groups at any time point. The study, by Sousa et al. [47], indicates that the self-fitting hearing aids provide comparably good sound amplification compared to the hearing aids fitted by audiologists.

In the study by Knoetze et al. [52] self-fitted and auto-fitted hearing aids were compared in a study also performed in South Africa. Twenty-eight participants with self-perceived mild-to-moderate hearing loss were included in the study. All participants tried the self-fitting strategy for 4 weeks and the auto-fitting strategy for another 4 weeks. The self-fitting strategy allowed the participants to adjust the hearing aids using an app and the auto-fitting strategy relied on an automatically adjusted hearing aids according to in-situ audiometry. In this study outcomes were also measured using speech recognition testing and the validated questionnaires: the Abbreviated Profile of Hearing Aid Benefit (APHAB) [49] and the International Outcome Inventory for Hearing Aids (IOI-HA) [24]. There was an overall satisfaction and no statistically significant differences in speech recognition testing in the two fitting strategies suggesting that both could work as sufficient hearing aid fitting strategies.

Cost effectiveness

Steuerwald et al. [31] developed and tested a telehealth program for audiological patients at the Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center. It was reported that the biggest expense in telehealth setups usually is video conference equipment. Other expenses described in the study are headphones and webcams for each provider, software licence for each provider, and support from the hospital’s telehealth department. The study mentions that teleintervention can create savings by more effective use of audiologists’ time, saving patients and families from travel time and travel-associated costs, and not having to get off from work to go to an appointment. Allegedly, costs due to equipment expenses, personnel, and software will be larger than any economic gains by telehealth solutions. However, most of these expenses are upfront and savings from travel, etc., will accumulate over time.

A randomised controlled trial, by Blaiser et al. [28], evaluate the cost-effectiveness of tele-intervention. Twenty-seven families with infants or toddlers with hearing loss were included in the study. The intervention group received services through teleintervention, and the control group received in-person home visits. Outcome measurements were family and health care professional satisfaction, quality of the home-visits and teleintervention consultations (HOVRS-A+) [53], children’s language development (SKI-HI Language Development Scale) [54], and costs: “The cost analysis provided evidence that, given an average of three to four intervention sessions per month, the cost savings from teleintervention over time could be substantial” [28]. Other outcomes showed tele-intervention was either equivalently good or better than home visits. The authors conclude that tele-intervention in this trial proved to be a cost-effective method to deliver audiology sessions without compromising the quality of the services.

Discussion

General study quality and bias Assessment

In general, few of the studies reviewed are randomised leading to the risk of selection bias in terms of participants. Blinding is difficult to conduct because of the obvious nature of remote services compared to face-to-face services, and this can lead to detection bias where the effect of the intervention may be both over- and underestimated [18]. Outcome measurements used in the studies are rarely objective leading to risk of interpretation bias. Low participation rate was seen in some studies’ follow-up questionnaires which might lead to a risk of response bias as in Pross et al. where only one third of the participants filled the IOI-HA questionnaire [17,24]. Other studies introduced selection bias by inclusion criteria with hearing aid experience or cognitive testing scores like the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [48].

Despite the varying quality of the studies reported in the area, including bias risk, the findings reported give an impression of the feasibility, benefits, and main challenges of implementing telemedical treatment in audiology.

Successful implementation of telemedical treatment in audiology

Based on this review, the available literature points towards some key elements required for successful implementation of telemedical treatment in audiology: positive attitudes from patients and audiologists, a certain level of technological skills and confidence of using it, and technology that is easy and safe to use.

Attitudes of patients and audiologists are measured in many of the reviewed studies using different questionnaires for the purpose. In general, the studies reviewed present positive attitudes from users towards remote audiology services [7,8,27]. However, the validity of these findings can in many cases be questioned because of response and selection bias due to non-blinded or non-randomised study setups, few study participants, and low participation rate in follow-up questionnaires. Audiologist attitudes described in the studies are generally of positive character as well, even though some concerns are raised regarding technology skills of patients or communicational challenges [7,8,27]. These results lead to a risk of bias caused by the selection of participating audiologists probably not being representative of the general field. In a systematic review of telehealth applications in audiology from 2010 by Swanepoel et al., limited information on patient attitudes towards remote audiology was described and not a single report on audiologist attitudes was found [55]. As indicated above, the number of studies investigating the field has increased over the last decade improving knowledge in the field. Even though the generalisability of the findings on patient and audiologist attitudes should be challenged, indications point towards an overall positive attitude of both groups towards remote audiology services.

Technical challenges represent another potential obstacle regarding usability of telemedicine in audiology. A few studies observed that some patients experienced technical difficulties mostly with downloading the required software, but problems were easily overcome with the necessary assistance [30,31]. When systems are designed, antivirus software and data protection should also be considered to guard patient-sensitive data [30,31]. However, modern technology will probably develop more in the direction of apps making antivirus software more unnecessary.

Confidence in using remote technologies has experienced a general boost in the global society over the very last few years triggered by the COVID-19 pandemic that forced many employees to work from home using video conferencing systems on computers and as apps on mobile devices. The pandemic also accelerated the use of telemedicine, and patients and health care professionals suddenly had to adapt to a new way of receiving and facilitating health care services [56–58].

When implementing telemedicine treatment in audiology, it is relevant to learn from experiences generated in other areas of therapy where the technology has a longer history of use. Thus, use of telemedicine seems to be more well described in other chronic diseases, especially chronic heart failure and diabetes [59]. In a systematic meta-review by Hanlon et al. from 2017 [59], effects of telehealth in self-management of diabetes, heart failure, asthma, chronic obstructive lung disease, and cancer were studied. The study concluded that telehealth interventions deliver a safe option for self-management in chronic diseases with no negative effects reported, particularly in type 2 diabetes and chronic heart failure where the evidence base was most sufficient.

A Cochrane review by Inglis et al. from 2015 reviewed 41 articles about remote rehabilitation for people with heart failure [60]. The effect of tele-rehabilitation in this patient group indicates that intensive telehealth service has the potential to benefit patient outcomes in chronic diseases extensively and must be investigated further in health areas with less current evidence.

Pros and cons of implementing telemedical treatment in audiology

Obvious advantages of remote audiology were mentioned such as increased treatment accessibility, saving time and expenses on travel, no inconvenience from getting off from work, and less absence from appointments due to illness or other reasons. Also, apps create an easy way to self-adjust hearing aids and a potentially flexible and easy access to consult an audiologist. In remote areas, the advantages are clear: with few or no nearby audiologists, teleaudiology can provide treatment to people who would otherwise not get access to treatment or would have to travel far to get it [9,10,19].

Challenges that must be addressed when creating a remote health service were mentioned earlier. However, some parts of health care services might not be suited for remote consultations. Placing a hearing aid correctly or how to manage the equipment when changing batteries, etc., might require in-person care in some cases. Treatment offers with blended services could be a cost-effective way of providing audiology in future clinical setups without compromising on quality of the service provided.

In general, measurement tools in the field of remote audiology are limited and lack international standardisation. In the studies included in this review, 31 different measurement parameters were used showing a broad variety. Nineteen different standardised questionnaires were used, only four standardised questionnaires were used in more than one study, and seven studies used self-developed questionnaires. This illustrates that this field is not very streamlined when measuring outcomes, and this could indicate the need for more international guidelines. However, questionnaires are not necessarily standardisable across different countries and cultures because questions measuring hearing loss should be adapted to relevant everyday situations. Living with a hearing loss e.g. in a big western city compared to a small settlement in Greenland creates different limitations and experiences that cannot necessarily be measured using the same questionnaire.

In a country like Greenland, tele-audiology can change the availability of hearing health care by providing access in the most remote parts of the country where audiological treatment and rehabilitation services are either non-existing or very limited today. Technically, remote solutions should be possible; however, undiscovered challenges might influence the effectiveness of tele-audiology, and this field has not yet been studied. The health care system in Greenland is founded by Denmark, and little is known about usability of tele-audiology in cross-cultural settings. Thereby, it is hard to predict how these conditions might influence remote setups. Tele-audiology in Greenland has great potential to improve access, but the field needs further investigation with studies to uncover the actual usability in clinical practice.

The central question addressed in this review is whether remote hearing aid fittings and tele-audiological services are as effective as traditional in-clinic services. The reviewed studies suggest that tele-audiology has the potential to eventually replace conventional in-person audiology services partially, particularly in areas with limited access to audiologists. However, further research is required to identify the most effective setup. In general, remote services appear to be effective, especially when combined with in-person consultations. Continued research and advancements in hearing aid technology and software for remote rehabilitation could facilitate the integration of tele-audiology into healthcare systems, increasing accessibility to audiology services for a broader range of patients, such as those in Greenland.

Conclusively, tele-audiology has come far and seems highly relevant especially in specific clinical settings. Importantly, none of the studies reviewed implied that tele-audiology delivers an inferior service compared to face-to-face audiology and no factors have been identified speaking against implementing tele-audiology as a part of treatment and rehabilitation. In LMIC with geographical challenges, such as Greenland and other Arctic areas with Inuit populations, implementation can offer the potential to improve the level of treatment and rehabilitation significantly. However, no studies have so far been performed in these areas to confirm this hypothesis. Accordingly, the area is still in its infancy and calls for further investigation. Apart from more general studies, specific research questions could focus on challenges that are particularly relevant in these regions such as cross-cultural, language- and discrimination challenges.
