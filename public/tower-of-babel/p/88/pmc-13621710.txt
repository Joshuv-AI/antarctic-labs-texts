Explanatory model for “becoming a mother” among first-time mothers who experienced high-risk pregnancies
Authors: Hyunjin Cho, Sukhee Ahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh-risk pregnancies can disrupt the transition to motherhood by limiting maternal–infant interaction, increasing psychological distress, and undermining confidence in assuming the maternal role; however, few studies have modeled how relational, social, and health-related factors jointly influence this process. Guided by Mercer’s theory of maternal role attainment, this cross-sectional study developed and tested an explanatory structural equation model that described the process of becoming a mother among first-time mothers with a history of high-risk pregnancies.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, we tested a hypothesized structural equation model for the process of becoming a mother among first-time mothers with high-risk pregnancies. A total of 320 women within 6 months postpartum who had a physician-confirmed high-risk singleton pregnancy and lived with a spouse completed an online survey. Assessed variables included the mother–infant relationship, spousal support, social support, maternal health, maternal role confidence, and the process of becoming a mother.ResultsA total of 320 mothers were included in the final analysis. First-time mothers who had experienced high-risk pregnancies demonstrated a stronger sense of becoming a mother when they reported a more positive mother–infant relationship (β = 0.64, p = .001), a more supportive spousal relationship (β = 0.10, p = .003), higher levels of social support (β = 0.36, p = .008), better maternal health (lower scores indicating poorer health; β = −0.52, p = .002), and higher maternal role confidence (β = 0.42, p = .003).ConclusionsWomen who had experienced high-risk pregnancies were both directly and indirectly influenced by their mother–infant and spousal relationships, social support, maternal health, and maternal role confidence. These findings suggest that perinatal care for primiparas with a history of high-risk pregnancy should include brief, structured interventions that strengthen mother–infant bonding and spousal support, proactively address physical and emotional symptoms, and provide targeted opportunities to build confidence in assuming the maternal role.Keywords: Pregnancy, High-Risk, Mother-Child Relations, Spouses, Social Support, Maternal Health, Self-Efficacy, Cross-Sectional Studies

Background

Becoming a mother is a dynamic psychological and physical process through which a woman develops a maternal role, shaped not only by the woman herself but also by the infant, spouse, and surrounding environment [1, 2].

High-risk pregnancies substantially disrupt the typical process of becoming a mother. Medical complications, prolonged hospitalization, preterm birth, and neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission limit early maternal–infant contact, reduce opportunities for caregiving, increase psychological burden, and disrupt interactions within the maternal microsystem [3–6]. Mothers of preterm infants experience a dual transition—entry into parenthood and the transition from hospital to home—and frequently report fear, reduced maternal role confidence, and uncertainty regarding the care of medically fragile infants [4, 5]. After NICU discharge, frequent clinic visits for rehabilitation needs and a rehospitalization rate of approximately 20% further increase emotional, physical, and economic burdens, thereby complicating maternal adjustment [6]. Overall, maternal role attainment in high-risk contexts occurs amid uncertainty, limited maternal–infant proximity, and disrupted opportunities for consistent attachment.

Attachment to the fetus and infant—typically facilitated by holding, gazing, and breastfeeding—supports the infant’s physiological and emotional needs and contributes to the mother’s maternal identity formation [7]. However, high-risk pregnancies often involve medical separation or NICU-related restrictions that delay or disrupt attachment formation [8, 9]. Early disruptions in attachment are associated with caregiving difficulties, maternal depressive symptoms, and delayed infant development [7, 10, 11], which interfere with the consolidation of maternal identity, a process involving internalizing caregiving roles and integrating maternal thoughts, behaviors, and perceptions [12–16].

Spousal relationship constitutes a critical determinant of mothers’ adjustment to parenthood. Positive spousal relationships are associated with reduced maternal stress and depressive symptoms, particularly in high-risk pregnancy contexts [6, 11]. Mercer’s theory highlights spousal support as a key component of the maternal microsystem that fosters emotional stability, identity formation, and maternal role attainment [17]. However, parents experiencing high-risk pregnancies often face prolonged caregiving demands, substantial financial and time burdens, and heightened psychological stress, which may intensify marital conflict or contribute to relationship strain [7, 8]. These challenges underscore the increasing importance of spousal support in high-risk contexts; however, empirical evidence regarding how marital intimacy influences becoming a mother during NICU hospitalization and the transition to home remains limited.

Women with high-risk pregnancies report greater need for social support, which has been associated with reduced anxiety, stress, and postpartum depression, as well as enhanced maternal confidence, positive parenting perceptions, and maternal role development [10–12]. Maternal health directly and indirectly shapes the process of becoming a mother, and more than two-thirds of mothers with high-risk pregnancies experience persistent postpartum symptoms, such as fatigue, headaches, abdominal discomfort, urinary problems, and bowel irregularities, for up to 9–12 months after birth [13]. Poor physical health is associated with increased maternal role difficulty [7], and mothers who experienced high-risk pregnancies have a higher likelihood of postpartum depression than mothers with low-risk pregnancies [15], which in turn negatively affects maternal adjustment and caregiving [12, 14].

Maternal role confidence, which develops during pregnancy and continues into the postpartum period, is a core predictor of maternal role attainment [16]. High-risk pregnancies often undermine this confidence by limiting opportunities to learn and practice caregiving behaviors, and first-time mothers frequently report feeling unprepared for infant care, resulting in lower maternal role confidence and reduced satisfaction with the transition to motherhood [17–19]. Although qualitative studies in South Korea have examined experiences of high-risk pregnancy, postpartum adjustment, maternal identity, attachment, and depression [7, 18], explanatory models of becoming a mother have primarily focused on women with low-risk pregnancies [6].

Mercer’s theory of becoming a mother (BAM) conceptualizes this transition as a dynamic and lifelong process rather than a fixed endpoint, highlighting that maternal identity continually evolves as women respond to new developmental challenges and caregiving demands [2]. In her theoretical and empirical work, Mercer describes BAM as a staged yet fluid process that begins with commitment, attachment, and preparation during pregnancy; progresses through acquaintance, learning, and physical restoration in the early postpartum period; and continues as mothers move toward a new normal and experience an emerging sense of maternal identity around 4 months after birth, with substantial individual variation [4]. She further shows that key constructs in this process—attachment to the infant, perceived parental competence, gratification in the maternal role, and maternal identity—are interrelated and influenced by maternal, infant, and contextual factors, including pregnancy risk status, social support, and maternal health. Against this background, applying Mercer’s becoming-a-mother framework to women with high-risk pregnancies provides a useful lens for examining how infant attachment, relationship with a spouse, maternal role confidence, and maternal identity develop under conditions such as preterm birth, low birth weight, and NICU hospitalization.

Methods

Aims

We aimed to develop and evaluate a model derived from Mercer’s theory of becoming a mother and prior literature [11–16, 18] to identify factors influencing the postpartum process of becoming a mother among first-time mothers who experienced high-risk pregnancies (Fig. 1).

(A) HypothethEtical model (B) Modified model

Study design

This cross-sectional study tested a hypothesized structural model explaining the process of becoming a mother among first-time mothers who experienced high-risk pregnancies. The model was based on Mercer’s theory of becoming a mother and the findings of previous studies. In addition, we aimed to assess model fit and test the proposed research hypotheses.

Study participants

The participants were first-time mothers within 6 months postpartum who had experienced high-risk pregnancies. The inclusion criteria were as follows: obstetrician diagnosis of at least one of 19 specific high-risk conditions (e.g., preterm labor, severe toxicosis, or placenta previa) between 20 and 37 weeks of gestation; age of 20 years or older; first singleton birth; cohabitation with a spouse; direct involvement in infant care; and ability to complete a questionnaire in Korean. We excluded those without a confirmed high-risk condition, mothers of multiple births, and those not living with a spouse or participating directly in childcare for their babies. Of the mothers screened for eligibility, those who did not meet these criteria or declined to participate were excluded, resulting in a final analytic sample of 320 first-time mothers with high-risk pregnancies.

High-risk pregnancy status was verified by requiring participants to upload a diagnostic document showing only the high-risk pregnancy diagnosis. Participants were instructed to mask all personal identifying information (e.g., name, resident registration number, and hospital registration number) so that only the diagnosis remained visible. The research team confirmed eligibility by reviewing only the diagnostic label without collecting any identifiable medical information. All uploaded files were used exclusively for eligibility verification and were deleted immediately after confirmation to prevent any linkage to survey response data.

Consent process

The study aims and procedures were explained to all participants. The first author informed the participants that their decision to participate or decline participation would have no impact on their lives. Additional information was provided regarding the data collection procedures, right to provide or withdraw consent, confidentiality protection, and the potential risks and benefits of participation. All participants provided informed consent.

Ethical considerations

This study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of C University (approval number: 2022##--##-###-##), and all procedures complied with relevant ethical guidelines. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was obtained from all participants electronically before data collection.

Instruments

The process of “becoming a mother” was assessed using measures of maternal identity and maternal adaptation. Maternal identity was assessed using the Semantic Differential Scale (SD-Self, SD-Baby) [20], which was adapted into Korean [21] and demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.79–0.83). Adaptation to becoming a mother was evaluated using a Korean-translated tool [22, 23] (α = 0.81–0.83).

Maternal–infant attachment was assessed using the Korean version of the Maternal Attachment Inventory [24, 25] (α = 0.98). Relationship with spouse was measured using the Korean-translated Dyadic Adjustment Scale [26, 27] (α = 0.89). Social support was measured using the Perinatal Infant Care Social Support Scale [28, 29], with subscale reliabilities of 0.73 for informational support, 0.91 for supportive presence, and 0.87 for practical support. Postpartum depressive symptoms were measured using the validated Korean version of the Edinburgh Postpartum Depression Scale [30, 31] (α = 0.82). Physical health was measured using the Jones and Park Physical Health Checklist [32], as adapted by Ahn [33] (α = 0.64). Maternal role confidence was measured using Pharis’ self-assessment tool [34].

The physical health scale encompasses a broad range of postpartum physical symptoms, including fatigue, headache, urinary discomfort, abdominal pain, and sleep disturbance, which likely reduce inter-item correlations and result in a Cronbach’s α in the 0.60 range.

Data collection

Data were collected from February to July 2023 through online mother-focused community forums. Participants who provided informed consent completed a 20–30-min online questionnaire. Participant anonymity was guaranteed, and incentives in the form of coffee gift coupons were provided to acknowledge voluntary participation and attentive questionnaire completion.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 and AMOS version 26.0. IBM SPSS was used to compute descriptive statistics and correlations and evaluate internal consistency reliability. AMOS 26.0 was used to test the hypothesized structural model.

Normality was evaluated using skewness, kurtosis, and Mardia’s multivariate kurtosis. Structural equation modeling was conducted using maximum likelihood estimation, which is appropriate when normality assumptions are satisfied [35]. Rather than creating composite scores, each latent variable (relationship with the infant, maternal health, and maternal role confidence) was modeled using all scale items as indicators, consistent with prior validation studies, while preserving the multidimensional structure of the constructs.

Independent-samples t-tests were conducted to examine group differences in key study variables (e.g., infant gestational age or NICU admission status). For each comparison, we used two-tailed tests, reported t values and p values, and verified the homogeneity of variance using Levene’s test, applying equal- or unequal-variance t-test results as appropriate.

Model evaluation included chi-square (χ²), goodness-of-fit index (GFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), comparative fit index (CFI), parsimonious normed fit index (PNFI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). The significance of direct, indirect, and total effects was tested using 10,000 bootstrap samples, providing bias-corrected confidence intervals. Squared multiple correlations (R²) were used to assess the explanatory power of endogenous variables.

Results

General characteristics of the participants

The participants had a mean age of 33.07 ± 3.50 years, with 34.6% aged ≥ 35 years and 62.1% reporting monthly household incomes exceeding 4 million won. The most common high-risk diagnoses were preterm labor (49.1%), labor-related hemorrhage (11.6%), and polyhydramnios (10.6%). The infants had a mean age of 5.2 ± 2.83 months, 51.2% were female, and 51.2% of mothers had vaginal deliveries. The mean gestational age was 35.71 ± 3.68 weeks, and the mean birth weight was 2.97 ± 0.43 kg. Pregnancy hospitalization occurred in 38.4% of participants, and 41.5% of infants were admitted to the NICU (Table 1).

Significant group differences were identified. Mothers with a college degree or higher showed greater self-identification (t = 3.20, p = .001) and maternal role adaptation (t = 2.11, p = .039). Mothers of male infants reported higher maternal identity (t = 3.93, p < .001), self-role adjustment (t = 2.77, p = .006), and overall maternal role adjustment (t = 3.22, p = .001). Maternal identity was positively associated with vaginal birth (t = 3.03, p = .003) and negatively associated with preterm birth (t = − 1.66, p = .043). Both maternal identity and role adjustment were positively associated with infants weighing < 2,500 g (t = 2.22, p = .027). Lower maternal role adjustment was associated with a history of NICU admission (t = − 2.17, p = .031) (Table 1).

Verification of the hypothesis model

Higher scores on the physical health checklist indicate poorer physical health; therefore, negative path coefficients (e.g., β = −0.52) reflect the expected inverse relationship between poorer maternal health and higher levels of the process of becoming a mother. This scoring direction was considered when interpreting the structural paths in the hypothesized and modified models.

Test of the hypothesized model and goodness-of-fit of the modified model

Higher scores on the physical health checklist indicate poorer physical health; therefore, negative coefficients (e.g., β = –0.52) reflect the expected inverse relationship between poorer maternal health and lower levels of the process of becoming a mother.

The hypothesized model showed a poor fit (χ²/df = 4.39, GFI = 0.91, SRMR = 0.09, RMSEA = 0.10); therefore, a modified model was constructed by incorporating theoretically supported pathways identified in prior studies. Four additional pathways were incorporated into the model.

First, postpartum depression was found to influence the process of becoming a mother, as documented in prior research [8].

Second, the quality of the maternal relationship with the infant affected maternal role confidence [18]. Third, social support contributed to enhanced maternal role confidence [19]. Finally, social support influenced the process of becoming a mother [11]. Newborn birth weight was also included as a predictor because of its established association with the process of becoming a mother [12].

Each added path was grounded in empirical evidence derived from research on high-risk pregnancies. Postpartum depression directly influences the process of becoming a mother because high-risk mothers exhibit elevated depressive symptoms associated with infant vulnerability [36, 37]. Maternal–infant attachment affects maternal role confidence because attachment disruptions—common among mothers of preterm or medically fragile infants—delay the development of confidence [38]. Social support influences both maternal role confidence and the process of becoming a mother by enhancing maternal self-efficacy and identity integration in high-risk contexts [39]. Birth weight was included because lower infant birth weight is associated with increased maternal anxiety and delayed maternal role attainment [40]. These additions strengthened the theoretical alignment with Mercer’s framework and reflected the characteristics of high-risk postpartum transition, although the model remains exploratory.

The modified model demonstrated a substantially improved fit (χ²/df = 2.95, SRMR = 0.07, CFI = 0.96, PNFI = 0.62), with the GFI exceeding that of the initial model. Cross-validation (Δχ² = 126.84, p < .001) confirmed a statistically significant improvement in model fit (Table 3).

Discussion

We explained the process of becoming a mother among first-time mothers with high-risk pregnancies through the development of an explanatory model grounded in Mercer’s theory of becoming a mother. Becoming a mother was defined as the final outcome variable, and the model demonstrated an explanatory power of 60%, which is higher than the 53% reported among low-risk mothers [6]. Mercer conceptualized becoming a mother as a dynamic transition in which women move through stages of commitment and preparation during pregnancy, acquaintance and physical recovery in the early postpartum period, moving toward a new normal over approximately the first 4 months, and achieving a relatively stable maternal identity, with maternal identity, attachment, and perceived competence evolving in response to infant characteristics and contextual demands [4]. This difference may reflect the characteristics of high-risk pregnancies, in which maternal physical discomfort, postpartum depression, and fatigue interact with relational and psychosocial variables. Our findings suggest that within the Korean context, where intensive maternal involvement and self-sacrifice are culturally valued, these physical and emotional burdens may be particularly salient in shaping how women negotiate this developmental transition [41]. The model confirmed that maternal–infant attachment, spousal relationship quality, social support, maternal health, and maternal role confidence exert both direct and indirect effects on the process of becoming a mother.

Among the 12 hypothesized paths, 11 were supported, validating Mercer’s theory within high-risk pregnancy contexts. Mercer described maternal identity as developing through continuous interaction with the infant, spouse, and a broader microsystem. In her longitudinal work with mothers from adolescence to their forties, Mercer followed women for up to 8–12 months postpartum and showed that perceived maternal competence, attachment to the infant, and role strain are key predictors of maternal identity across age and risk groups, with health status and social support repeatedly emerging as important contextual factors [4]. The findings extend this framework by demonstrating that mothers with high-risk pregnancies follow similar developmental processes but under intensified physical and emotional demands. Higher maternal–infant attachment, stronger spousal relationships, greater social support, higher maternal role confidence, and lower levels of postpartum depression and physical discomfort contributed to more positive maternal transitions. These results echo earlier studies in both low- and high-risk populations that showed that maternal identity is strengthened when women receive adequate practical and emotional support and have opportunities to practice caregiving. However, our findings indicate that such conditions may be harder to achieve for Korean mothers managing complications after a high-risk birth [6–8, 13, 14].

Maternal attachment had a mean score of 88.16 out of 104, indicating moderate to high levels. Previous research involving mothers of preterm infants reported even higher attachment scores (95.01) [12] based on a cross-sectional survey of NICU mothers in a tertiary hospital setting using the same attachment scale, suggesting that mothers of medically fragile infants may show particularly intense early attachment despite limited physical contact. This suggests that mothers with high-risk pregnancies may form strong attachment patterns depending on infant birth weight, medical complications, or congenital anomalies [12]. Longitudinal comparisons of high-risk and low-risk mothers during the first 8 months postpartum have shown that women hospitalized for high-risk pregnancy sometimes report higher early attachment than low-risk women, interpreted as reflecting heightened vigilance and commitment to medically vulnerable infants [42]. Although high-risk pregnancies often involve early separation and uncertainty, strong maternal–infant attachment has been associated with positive maternal identity [7]. The strong association between attachment and the process of becoming a mother aligns with Mercer’s view that maternal–infant relationships are fundamental, and heightened attachment may reflect increased emotional investment in vulnerable infants.

Spousal relationships scores averaged 29.70 out of 51, which is lower than those reported in previous studies of high-risk pregnancies (38.19) [14]. Spouses provide essential emotional and physical support during high-risk pregnancies. Prior studies of women with high-risk or medically complicated pregnancies, often conducted in tertiary hospitals or perinatal centers using cross-sectional or short-term longitudinal survey designs, have shown that partner support and marital satisfaction are important predictors of lower levels of depression, stronger maternal–infant attachment, and better adaptation to the maternal role [6]. Close spousal relationships have been shown to reduce postpartum depression, promote maternal–infant attachment, enhance maternal confidence [18], and serve as important predictors of maternal role development [11]. Consistent with Mercer’s framework, we found that spousal relationships indirectly influenced the process of becoming a mother through maternal health, highlighting the critical role of spousal support in high-risk pregnancies.

Social support averaged 69.63, which is higher than the mean of 66.40 observed among low-risk mothers, supporting earlier findings that high-risk mothers require greater social support [6, 7]. Previous studies have shown that social support reduces anxiety, depression, and stress [15] and enhances maternal role development by increasing maternal confidence [19]. However, in this study, social support directly influenced depression, but not maternal role confidence. This pattern may suggest that, among mothers with high-risk or medically complicated pregnancies, perceived support primarily functions as an immediate emotional buffer, reducing depressive symptoms, whereas its effects on maternal role confidence are more indirect and may operate through intermediaries, such as reduced parenting stress, increased psychological resources, or improved marital relationships. In addition, during the early postpartum period, especially in high-risk contexts, women may be more sensitive to emotional relief than to changes in their self-evaluation as mothers; therefore, the confidence dimension requires more time and cumulative caregiving experiences to be meaningfully affected [12].

This difference may be related to differences in measurement timing; earlier research assessed mothers during NICU hospitalization [10], whereas the present study included high-risk mothers caring for their infants at home, regardless of prior NICU experience. Significant indirect effects indicate that social support may improve maternal role development primarily through physical and emotional well-being rather than directly increasing confidence, a pattern that differs from findings reported among low-risk mothers. In Korea, social support is often delivered through extended family networks and postpartum care centers, which tend to prioritize instrumental help and prescriptive advice. Such support may alleviate fatigue and depressive symptoms, but creates pressure to meet idealized standards of motherhood, thereby buffering distress without necessarily enhancing women’s sense of competence [43].

Maternal health accounted for 56% of the variance in maternal role confidence. Physical discomfort averaged 11.30 symptoms, which was considerably higher than the mean of 4.80 reported among low-risk mothers at 3–4 months postpartum [8], supporting previous findings that high-risk mothers experience greater physical discomfort [6]. Postpartum depression scores among high-risk mothers (12.30–15.40) were comparable to those reported in earlier studies [15, 16], while the mean score among low-risk mothers was 14.93 [14]. These results indicate that physical discomfort and postpartum depression significantly influence the process of becoming a mother [13, 14, 19]. Poor physical health showed negative associations, reflecting its expected adverse impact on maternal confidence and development. Mercer’s longitudinal research similarly noted that health status and role strain can delay or disrupt maternal identity, particularly when women struggle to regain functional control of their bodies and balance maternal, marital, and work roles over the first postpartum year [2].

Maternal role confidence had a mean score of 60.40. High-risk pregnancies often reduce maternal confidence because opportunities to acquire caregiving skills depend on infant health status, maternal recovery, and hospitalization experiences [4]. In this study, maternal role confidence directly influenced the process of becoming a mother, which is consistent with previous findings indicating that confidence is central to adaptation to the maternal role [8, 16]. Prospective studies of first-time mothers in North America and Europe, typically following women from late pregnancy to 3–12 months postpartum, have consistently shown that parenting self-efficacy increases as mothers gain experience, and that higher self-efficacy is linked to more sensitive mother–infant interaction, better marital satisfaction, and fewer depressive symptoms.

Our results indicate that similar mechanisms operate in Korean high-risk mothers, but opportunities for practice may be constrained by infant medical vulnerability and reliance on grandparents or professional staff in postpartum care centers, which can limit mothers’ hands-on caregiving and delay confidence building.

The mean score for becoming a mother was 62.29, which was above average but lower than the range of 84.30–87.30 reported among low-risk mothers. Maternal identity scores were similar to those reported in earlier studies on high-risk mothers (61.30) [6]. Maternal health had the strongest direct effect on the process of becoming a mother, followed by maternal role confidence. The mother–infant relationship showed the strongest indirect effect, followed by spousal relationships and social support. These patterns are congruent with Mercer’s description of becoming a mother as a process in which identity is achieved when women feel competent, confident, and emotionally connected to their infant, but remain vulnerable to disruption when health problems, role strain, or relational conflicts are present [41].

The experiences of high-risk mothers in South Korea are shaped not only by clinical and psychological factors but also by strong sociocultural expectations regarding motherhood. Although paternal involvement has increased, mothers continue to perceive themselves as primarily responsible for childcare. Previous studies have indicated that Korean first-time mothers experience tension between postpartum recovery and cultural expectations of competence and self-sacrifice [6–8], which adds a psychological burden for those already coping with medical uncertainty. From the perspective of transition and identity theories, these expectations intensify the work that Korean women must do to “move toward a new normal,” as they attempt to reconcile internal feelings of vulnerability with external norms of being a devoted, efficient, and emotionally stable mother [6–8]. Korea’s postpartum care center system also plays a significant role in shaping early maternal experiences. Although mothers expect to acquire childcare skills in these centers, research indicates that programs often lack practical relevance, and peer networks may provide support while also exposing mothers to inconsistent or overwhelming information [10, 13], which can negatively affect maternal identity and confidence.

Overall, the explanatory model of becoming a mother after a high-risk pregnancy highlights how maternal health, role confidence, infant attachment, social support, and spousal relationships collectively shape maternal development and underscores the need for tailored support for high-risk mothers. Our findings suggest that interventions in South Korea should focus on symptom management and psychological screening, and also on enhancing fathers’ involvement, restructuring postpartum care programs to provide more hands-on skill-building, and explicitly addressing cultural narratives of ideal motherhood so that high-risk women can come to understand the process of becoming a mother as one that accommodates both their limitations and strengths [6–8, 10, 13, 19].

Strengths and limitations

We investigated the process of becoming a mother among women with high-risk pregnancies to inform the development of educational and supportive interventions that facilitate a positive transition to motherhood [16]. This study contributes both theoretically and clinically to the understanding of maternal adaptation among first-time mothers who experienced high-risk pregnancies by constructing and validating an explanatory model based on Mercer’s theory. The inclusion of mothers with medically verified high-risk conditions—an often under-represented group in the literature—strengthens the relevance and applicability of the findings. Furthermore, the use of structural equation modeling allowed the examination of both direct and indirect effects, providing a comprehensive depiction of how maternal health, infant attachment, spousal relationships, social support, and role confidence collectively shape the process of becoming a mother.

However, certain limitations of this study should be acknowledged.

First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, as temporal ordering cannot be confirmed; therefore, the observed relationships should be interpreted as associations. Second, the use of self-report questionnaires introduces potential response and recall bias, and mothers experiencing physical or psychological burdens may have misestimated their health status, attachment, or perceived social support. In addition, some instruments, particularly the physical health scale, showed relatively low internal consistency (Cronbach’s α in the 0.60 range), likely due to the broad and heterogeneous nature of postpartum physical symptoms. This may have limited the precision with which physical health status was measured in high-risk mothers. Recruitment through online mother-focused community forums may also have introduced a selection bias by overrepresenting digitally active or socioeconomically advantaged mothers, thereby limiting generalizability.

Future research should include longitudinal studies to examine changes in maternal identity, role confidence, and psychological well-being across pregnancy and the postpartum period, which would allow the assessment of causal directions and pathway stability. Interventional studies evaluating postpartum education, spousal support programs, or structured support networks are also needed to strengthen maternal adaptation and further validate the explanatory model, thereby contributing to culturally tailored care for high-risk mothers.

Conclusions

Women who experienced high-risk pregnancies were influenced by a combination of factors, including infant attachment, spousal relationship, social support, maternal health, and role confidence, which collectively shaped their progression toward higher levels of motherhood. Strategies to support these first-time mothers in their transition to motherhood should prioritize fostering strong relationships with their infants and spouses, enhancing social support systems, improving maternal health, and increasing confidence in assuming the maternal role. Based on these findings, perinatal healthcare providers should implement structured educational and counseling interventions that promote infant–mother and spousal bonding, enhance access to formal and informal support resources, and systematically assess and manage physical and emotional symptoms to strengthen maternal role confidence among high-risk first-time mothers. In addition, these results highlight the need for future longitudinal and intervention studies to examine whether such targeted educational, spousal support, and social support programs can sustain improvements in maternal identity, role confidence, and psychological well-being in high-risk populations.
