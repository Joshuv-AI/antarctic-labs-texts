RNAi‐Mediated Attenuation of CsCalS7‐Related Transcripts Mitigates Callose Accumulation and Huanglongbing Symptoms
Authors: Laís Moreira Granato, Nathália da Roz D’Alessandre, Raquel Luciana Boscariol‐Camargo, Marcos Antonio Machado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuanglongbing (HLB) is the most destructive citrus disease worldwide and is characterised by excessive callose deposition at sieve plates, which has been associated with impaired carbohydrate transport, starch accumulation and severe symptoms. CsCalS7, a phloem‐associated callose synthase, is induced during infection and involved in sieve pore occlusion. Citrus sinensis contains at least three CalS7 genes (CsCalS7a, CsCalS7b and CsCalS7c). Here, RNA interference (RNAi) was employed to reduce the CsCalS7‐related transcript levels. Similarity analyses showed the hairpin‐RNAi (hpRNAi) perfectly matched CsCalS7a and shared similarity with CsCalS7b and CsCalS7c. siRNA‐level analysis predicted strong silencing of CsCalS7a and possible moderate effects on CsCalS7b and CsCalS7c, supporting modulation of multiple paralogs. Reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR confirmed partial transcript reduction (10%–62%) across independent lines, and microscopic analysis revealed a significant reduction of phloem callose deposition in RNAi lines. After ‘Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus’ (CLas) inoculation, several RNAi lines showed reduced starch accumulation and attenuated HLB symptom severity compared to non‐transgenic controls. Bacterial levels did not differ significantly among genotypes at 5 months post‐inoculation in the evaluated plant tissues. Together, these results demonstrate that moderate suppression of CsCalS7‐related transcripts reduces callose deposition and possibly mitigates starch overaccumulation and symptom severity. The observed effects of partial RNAi‐mediated suppression suggest that modulating callose synthase activity can improve host tolerance to HLB.

Introduction

Since huanglongbing (HLB) was detected for the first time, citriculture has been facing a big challenge that keeps growing every year (Coletta‐Filho et al. 2004). The decline in fruit production has resulted in substantial annual economic losses to the citriculture. In Brazil's citrus belt (São Paulo and West‐Southwest Minas Gerais), the incidence of orange trees with HLB symptoms in 2023 was 38%, which corresponds to approximately 72.22 million diseased trees out of the total of 202.88 million orange trees throughout the park (FUNDECITRUS 2023). The annual expansion of affected areas has led to a significant reduction in orange production, impacting both the fresh fruit market and the concentrated orange juice sector. The production decline has affected exports, causing challenges to the citrus industry's viability.

HLB is caused by the phloem‐limited bacterium ‘Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus’ (CLas) with transmission by the Asian citrus psyllid (ACP, Diaphorina citri ) (Coletta‐Filho et al. 2004; Bové 2006). CLas colonises the phloem sieve tubes (Jagoueix et al. 1994; Aritua et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2017), leading to phloem sieve element plugging and necrosis, which has been related to the abundant callose and phloem protein deposition (Achor et al. 2010; Koh et al. 2012; Albrigo et al. 2014). Sieve element collapse leads to a reduction of the phloem functionality, interfering systemically with the transport of carbohydrates from the sources (e.g., mature leaves) to sinks (e.g., roots) (Nishimura et al. 2008; Etxeberria and Narciso 2015). Consequently, as a result of the obstructed photosynthate transport, large quantities of starch granules accumulate in the aerial organs (e.g., leaves) (Folimonova and Achor 2010; Gonzalez et al. 2012).

The phloem transport disturbance in HLB‐affected plants has been considered responsible for the disease symptoms (Folimonova and Achor 2010; Koh et al. 2012; Wang and Trivedi 2013). HLB symptoms include blotchy chlorosis and mottling of leaves, yellow shoots, vein corking, stunted growth, suppression of new root growth, and production of small, green, lopsided fruits with aborted seeds and mature fruits dropping (Bové 2006; Tang and Vashisth 2020; Yang et al. 2024).

Callose accumulation plays a crucial role in citrus phloem dysfunction and the progression of HLB symptoms. Callose is a 1–3‐linked β‐glucan polymer biosynthesized by the enzyme class Callose Synthase (CalS) (Richmond and Somerville 2000; Verma and Hong 2011). Callose synthase genes have been identified and characterised in several plants, including Arabidopsis and Citrus (Richmond and Somerville 2000; Hong et al. 2001; Feng et al. 2021; Niu et al. 2022; Granato et al. 2019; Yao et al. 2024). Among them, CalS7 has been associated with callose synthesis in the phloem sieve plate (Xie and Hong 2011). Arabidopsis calS7 mutants exhibit reduced callose deposition in the phloem, leading to impaired phloem transport and altered plant growth patterns (Xie et al. 2011; Barratt et al. 2011).

The importance of CalS7 in plant defence is further highlighted by its role in response to CLas infection and HLB symptoms. Studies have shown that the Citrus sinensis callose synthase (CsCalS7) expression significantly increases in Citrus plants affected by HLB compared to healthy plants (Granato et al. 2019; Curtolo et al. 2020; Achor et al. 2019). A recent functional study of the CsCalS7 family reported that C. sinensis harbours at least three homologues of the Arabidopsis CalS7 gene (CsCalS7a, CsCalS7b and CsCalS7c) and that loss‐of‐function of specific transcripts leads to phloem malformation and HLB‐like symptoms (Huang et al. 2025). These results indicate that CsCalS7 function is distributed among multiple paralogs and that complete inactivation of certain family members may be detrimental to phloem integrity.

In this context, RNA interference (RNAi)‐based approaches aimed at modulating phloem callose deposition may unbalance suppression of multiple CsCalS7‐related transcripts. However, a moderate reduction in CsCalS7 expression can alleviate excessive callose accumulation while preserving basal phloem function, potentially attenuating the HLB‐associated physiological disruptions and symptoms development.

In this study, we investigate the RNAi‐mediated downregulation of CsCalS7‐related transcripts in C. sinensis , with CsCalS7a contributing preferentially, but not exclusively, and the consequences for phloem callose deposition, starch accumulation and host symptoms following CLas challenge. By integrating analyses of host responses under conditions of reduced CsCalS7‐related expression, we aim to clarify how moderated callose synthesis contributes to HLB‐associated symptom development.

Results

In Silico Evaluation of RNAi Target Coverage and Primer Specificity Across CsCalS7 Paralogs

To support the design of a gene‐silencing construct with potential coverage of the CsCalS7 family members, the selected RNAi target sequence was aligned in silico against the three predicted C. sinensis callose synthase 7‐like transcript variants. BLASTn analysis revealed a perfect match with transcript variant X1 (CsCalS7a; XM_006448823.4), showing 100% identity over a 598‐bp region. High levels of sequence similarity were also observed with transcript variant X2 (CsCalS7b; XM_052439635.1), which exhibited 89% identity (529/592 bp), and with transcript variant X3 (CsCalS7c; XM_052439472.1), which showed 91% identity (544/601 bp) (Figure 1). These results indicate that the RNAi sequence designed against CsCalS7a retains substantial homology with CsCalS7b and CsCalS7c, suggesting it can simultaneously target multiple CsCalS7 paralogs rather than being strictly gene specific.

The reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) primer pair sequences used to verify CsCalS7 expression were also aligned against the three paralog sequences, showing a complete match to CsCalS7a (XM_006484823.4), with no mismatches detected in either the forward or reverse primer‐binding regions. In contrast, alignments to CsCalS7b (XM_052439635.1) revealed multiple nucleotide mismatches within the forward primer binding site and at least one mismatch in the reverse primer region, while alignments to CsCalS7c (XM_052439472.1) showed three mismatches in the forward primer binding site together with additional mismatches in the reverse primer site (Appendix S1). Although the predicted amplicon length was identical (170 bp) across all three transcripts, the presence of mismatches in both primer annealing regions for CsCalS7b and CsCalS7c is expected to reduce, but not necessarily abolish, amplification efficiency for these paralogs under the qPCR conditions employed. Therefore, while these in silico results are consistent with a preferential amplification of CsCalS7a, they do not support a definitive conclusion regarding exclusive paralog specificity.

siRNA‐Level Off‐Target Across CsCalS7 Paralogs

In silico siRNA target analysis revealed differential targeting across members of the CsCalS7 gene family. The highest number of predicted siRNA hits was observed for CsCalS7a (LOC102612996, transcript variant X1), with 578 total hits, of which 294 were classified as efficient, indicating strong sequence complementarity to the RNAi construct. In contrast, CsCalS7c (LOC102620841, transcript variant X3) displayed 103 total hits and 48 efficient hits, while CsCalS7b (LOC102610094, transcript variant X2) showed 71–87 total hits and 35–41 efficient hits across multiple predicted CDS entries (Figure 2 and Appendix S2). Although the total number of hits differed substantially among paralogs, the proportion of efficient siRNAs relative to total hits remained relatively consistent (approximately 47%–51%). These results indicate that the RNAi construct is predicted to strongly target CsCalS7a, while still retaining moderate silencing potential toward CsCalS7b and CsCalS7c, supporting the likelihood of multiparalog modulation rather than strict paralog specificity.

Genetic Transformation and Silencing of CscalS7 ‐Related Transcript in Sweet Orange

The pCambia_hpCsCalS7a was successfully constructed by cloning the hpRNAi construction (Appendix S3A,B) under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter (approximately 4000 bp) onto vector pCambia2301 (Appendix S3C). Agrobacterium tumefaciens ‐mediated transformation was accomplished, generating 15 transgenic lines confirmed by β‐glucuronidase (GUS) assay and PCR. After grafting onto Rangpur lime, only seven lines survived (RNAi1, RNAi2, RNAi3, RNAi5, RNAi7, RNAi9 and RNAi15) and were maintained in the restricted‐access greenhouse. However, the growth of the RNAi5 line was compromised, and it was not used in further evaluations.

Using RNAi, a significant reduction in CsCalS7‐related transcript abundance, possibly predominantly due to CsCalS7a suppression, was observed in five independent RNAi lines, with decreases ranging from 10% to 62% relative to non‐transgenic controls (wild type [wt] and DsRed). Among these, three lines (RNAi1, RNAi3 and RNAi15) exhibited statistically significant downregulation, showing reductions of 51.4% (RNAi1), 53.0% (RNAi3) and 62.0% (RNAi15) compared with controls (Figure 3). Given the 100% identity of the RNAi harpin (Appendix S2) and RT‐qPCR primers to CsCalS7a (Appendix S1), besides the reduced but non‐zero homology to CsCalS7b and CsCalS7c (Figure 1), these measurements are interpreted as primarily reflecting CsCalS7a transcript suppression, but with a potential contribution from partial co‐silencing of other CsCalS7 paralogs. The DsRed control line, which is also GUS‐positive, did not show a reduction in CsCalS7‐related transcript levels, indicating that insertion of the pCAMBIA vector on its own does not affect expression of CsCalS7 family members.

Reduced Callose Deposition and Phenotypic Alterations in CsCalS7 ‐RNAi Citrus Plants

To verify if knocking down CsCalS7 reduces callose deposition in the phloem of sweet orange, cross‐section samples of leaf petioles stained by aniline blue were evaluated by microscopy in primary plants. Microscopic analysis of the phloem revealed a significant reduction in callose accumulation of CsCalS7‐RNAi plants compared to wt plants (Figure 4A). Quantitative measurements showed a significant decline in callose levels in the leaf petioles from RNAi1, RNAi3, RNAi9 and RNAi15, dropping from 12.354 pixels/cm in wt controls to 5.124 in RNAi1, 5.507 in RNAi3, 6.993 in RNAi9 and 6.812 in RNAi15 (Figure 4B).

The primary RNAi lines also exhibited visible growth alterations compared with wt plants. Although no systematic quantification of vegetative growth parameters was performed, differences in plant size can be visually assessed in Figure 5, as all plants were cultivated in plastic containers of identical dimensions. Three CsCalS7‐RNAi lines (RNAi3, RNAi10 and RNAi11) showed marked affected growth, and three lines demonstrated chlorotic spots on leaves (RNAi2, RNAi3 and RNAi9) (Figure 5). The chlorotic spots on leaves were not observed in the propagated RNAi plants at the time of evaluation.

CsCalS7 Knockdown Reduces Starch Accumulation and HLB Symptom Severity

Five months post‐inoculation, the six propagated biological replicates of each RNAi line were evaluated based on bacterial quantification, starch concentration and HLB symptom severity.

One‐way ANOVA revealed a significant overall effect of genotype on CLas accumulation (F = 3.24, p = 0.0205). However, Tukey's post hoc test found no statistically significant pairwise differences among genotypes (p > 0.05), consistent with the high variability observed in transgenic plants, particularly in the RNAi1 and RNAi2 lines. Although RNAi1 and RNAi2 exhibited higher CLas titres than wt and DsRed controls, RNAi3, RNAi9 and RNAi15 showed bacterial loads comparable to or slightly lower than wt plants (Figure 6A). These results indicate that RNAi‐mediated silencing of CsCalS7‐family members did not consistently reduce CLas levels in petiole and midrib tissues across RNAi lines under the conditions tested.

To further assess host physiological responses, we next examined starch accumulation, a downstream indicator of impaired phloem transport during HLB in the propagated plants. Although Tukey's post hoc test did not detect statistically significant differences between the RNAi lines and the wt control, leaf starch accumulation varied among genotypes and among individual plants at 5 months post‐inoculation, resulting in substantial variance. The lines RNAi1 and RNAi15 accumulated approximately half the starch content of wt (12.43 and 13.73 mg g−1, respectively), whereas RNAi9 showed a more pronounced reduction, with mean values of 7 mg g−1, nearly fourfold lower than wt. RNAi3 and RNAi2 showed starch levels comparable to wt (27.4 mg g−1) and DsRed (51 mg g−1) control plants (Figure 6B).

Given that starch accumulation is closely associated with HLB symptom development (Granato et al. 2019; Curtolo et al. 2020; Achor et al. 2019; Huang et al. 2025), the disease severity was also evaluated in the propagated plants. Symptom severity for HLB was rated on a scale from 0 to 4, with no plant showing HLB‐related foliar symptoms on more than 80% of its foliage at the time of the evaluation. Considerable variation in symptom severity was observed among individual plants, including both controls and RNAi lines. The wt control group showed a rate of 27%, while the DsRed control plants exhibited a similar infection index of 25%, indicating comparable symptom development in the controls. In contrast, transgenic plants with reduced CsCalS7‐related transcript levels showed lower and more variable HLB severity indices, ranging from 1% in the RNAi3 and RNAi9 lines to 22% in the RNAi1 line (Figure 7).

Discussion

Previous studies have established that callose deposition is a central component of the citrus response to huanglongbing (HLB) and that members of the callose synthase (CalS) gene family are differentially regulated during infection (Curtolo et al. 2020; Achor et al. 2019; Pandey et al. 2023). Our previous work identified nine callose synthase genes in C. sinensis based on homology to Arabidopsis thaliana CalS proteins and demonstrated that CsCalS7 was significantly upregulated in HLB‐positive plants (Granato et al. 2019). A subsequent genome‐wide survey expanded this family to 11 CsCalS genes, seven of which showed one‐to‐one orthologous relationships with Arabidopsis AtCalS (Yao et al. 2024). More recently, Huang et al. (2025) demonstrated that C. sinensis encodes at least three AtCalS7 homologues (CsCalS7a, CsCalS7b and CsCalS7c) and that the loss‐of‐function of specific paralogs demonstrated that phloem callose deposition causes phloem cell death and HLB‐like symptoms. Together, these studies highlighted the importance of CsCalS7‐related transcripts in response to CLas infection, because the increased callose deposition led by CsCalS7 activity in the citrus phloem during CLas infection contributes to phloem sieve pore obstruction, reducing the transport of photosynthates and impairing the citrus plant's health.

In this study, the RNAi construct exhibited perfect sequence identity with the CsCalS7a paralog, while retaining substantial but incomplete homology to CsCalS7b and CsCalS7c (Figure 1). In addition, in silico siRNA‐level analysis (Figure 2 and Appendix S2) indicated that the hairpin‐derived small RNAs are predicted to predominantly target CsCalS7a, while retaining moderate targeting potential toward CsCalS7b and CsCalS7c. Importantly, no substantial off‐target hits were detected outside the CsCalS7‐related transcripts under the applied prediction parameters, suggesting that large‐scale unintended silencing of unrelated genes is unlikely. Although computational predictions cannot fully exclude secondary or low‐affinity interactions in planta, these results support the interpretation that the observed phenotypes are primarily associated with modulation of CsCalS7‐related transcripts rather than broad off‐target effects. Nevertheless, future transcriptome‐wide analyses would be required to definitively exclude unintended silencing events and to refine our understanding of the specificity of the RNAi construct.

Based on these analyses and on the evaluation of the RT‐qPCR primer pair sequences (Appendix S1), the reduction in transcript levels most likely reflects predominant suppression of CsCalS7a, with a possible contribution from partial co‐silencing of other CsCalS7 paralogs. However, definitive determination of whether additional paralogs are affected would require expression analysis using paralog‐specific primer pairs designed to uniquely amplify CsCalS7a, CsCalS7b and CsCalS7c. It represents an important follow‐up approach that could clarify the contribution of individual paralogs in future investigations.

RNAi‐mediated knockdown varied across independent transgenic lines, with three lines (RNAi1, RNAi3 and RNAi15) exhibiting statistically significant reductions in CsCalS7‐related transcript abundance exceeding 50% (Figure 2A). Although this threshold appeared to be associated with measurable phenotypic effects, the RNAi9 line did not show a statistically significant reduction in overall CsCalS7 transcript abundance compared with wt plants (Figure 2A), whereas callose deposition (Figure 4) and starch accumulation (Figure 6B) were reduced. Therefore, in this line, the magnitude of transcript reduction detected in bulk leaf tissue did not directly parallel the observed anatomical and physiological phenotypes. Similar discrepancies between transcript abundance measured in whole tissues and localized cellular phenotypes have been reported previously (Xie et al. 2011; Barratt et al. 2011). In the present study, multiple independent RNAi lines (RNAi1, RNAi3, RNAi9 and RNAi15) exhibited significantly lower levels of phloem callose deposition relative to wt plants (Figure 4), supporting an association between RNAi‐mediated modulation of CsCalS7‐related transcripts and reduced callose accumulation. However, the specific molecular basis underlying the RNAi9 phenotype cannot be determined from the current dataset and warrants further investigation.

Callose deposition in sieve elements is a tightly regulated process required for maintaining basal phloem function and structural integrity (Xie et al. 2011). It has a role in sustaining sieve pore architecture and long‐distance photoassimilate transport (Sjölund 1997; Braun and Slewinski 2009; Barratt et al. 2011), and its excessive reduction is likely to compromise developmental performance, particularly in perennial species such as citrus. Some independent primary RNAi lines evaluated in this study exhibited visibly reduced vegetative growth, leaf yellowing and corky veins (Figure 5). Although quantitative assessments of growth parameters such as plant height, biomass accumulation or chlorophyll content have not been performed, differences in plant size can be visually assessed in Figure 5, as all plants were cultivated in plastic containers of identical dimensions. These phenotypes are similar to those reported by Huang et al. (2025) and may indicate that severe suppression of CsCalS7‐related transcript expression is associated with measurable fitness penalties, highlighting the need to balance callose attenuation with the maintenance of essential phloem functions.

Notably, these developmental alterations did not show a strict linear relationship with the extent of CsCalS7 transcript reduction, as lines with similar levels of silencing displayed contrasting growth responses. This variability suggests that factors such as transgene insertion‐site effects, spatial heterogeneity in silencing within phloem tissues or differential co‐silencing of CsCalS7 paralogs may contribute to the observed line‐specific phenotypes. Besides, the use of the constitutive CaMV 35S promoter may also have contributed to these developmental effects, as CsCalS7 expression is normally tightly regulated and largely confined to phloem tissues. From an applied perspective, future strategies to manipulate callose deposition for mitigating HLB symptoms would benefit from phloem‐specific or inducible promoters. Such regulatory elements could restrict CsCalS7 suppression to relevant tissues or infection contexts, thereby minimising unintended growth penalties while preserving the beneficial effects on phloem conductivity.

Callose deposition is also a well‐established component of the plant immune response and is strongly induced during CLas infection, together with reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and salicylic acid‐dependent defence signalling (Torres et al. 2006; Oliveira et al. 2019; Mohammadi et al. 2021; Ma et al. 2022). CLas‐induced callose accumulation results in a plugging mechanism that reduces sieve pore diameters and restricts photoassimilate transport along the phloem (Granato et al. 2019; Achor et al. 2019; Kalmbach et al. 2023). This impaired transport has been directly associated with starch accumulation in infected leaves (Achor et al. 2010; Koh et al. 2012; Granato et al. 2019), leading to chloroplast dysfunction and leaf yellowing. Here, RNAi lines displayed reduced starch accumulation following CLas infection (Figure 6B). This result supports the hypothesis that the level of callose reduction in sieve pores of RNAi lines may have maintained the pore conductivity and sustained sucrose transport required for normal plant development (Braun and Slewinski 2009; Barratt et al. 2011) and for not aggravating HLB symptoms (Figure 7). Direct evaluation of this mechanistic model requires additional experiments, such as phloem assimilate transport (exudate analyses) or ultrastructural characterisation of phloem sieve plates, which were not addressed in the present study but represent important directions for future work.

Despite reduced callose deposition and starch accumulation, no corresponding decrease in bacterial titres was observed in the evaluated tissues at 5 months post‐inoculation (Figure 6A). Because the analysis was conducted at only one time point and in a single tissue, effects occurring in other tissues or at different stages cannot be excluded. Even so, our results align with previous reports indicating that CLas can pass through partially occluded sieve pores due to its flexible, pleomorphic morphology (Pandey et al. 2022) or bypass obstructed conduits by using alternative phloem pathways (Achor et al. 2019). Despite similar bacterial titres, RNAi lines showed significantly lower HLB severity indices than wt plants (Figure 7). This finding supports the view that HLB symptoms are largely a consequence of host physiological disruption, such as starch‐associated chloroplast damage (Achor et al. 2010) and of phloem cell death linked to host immune responses, rather than being directly proportional to bacterial biomass (Ma et al. 2022).

In conclusion, this study provides functional evidence that RNAi‐mediated reduction of CsCalS7‐related transcripts, predominantly affecting CsCalS7a, with possible co‐silencing of additional paralogs, alters physiological responses associated with HLB. Partial suppression of CsCalS7 was associated with reduced callose deposition in healthy plants, lower starch accumulation and decreased symptom severity in a subset of RNAi lines following CLas infection.

These results support a threshold‐dependent model in which CsCalS7 activity must be maintained within an optimal range to preserve the balance between phloem integrity and conductivity. By allowing partial rather than complete suppression of gene function, RNAi provides a useful strategy to investigate dose‐dependent effects of callose synthase genes in perennial species like Citrus and to explore approaches aimed at reducing HLB‐associated phloem dysfunction while maintaining essential vascular function.

Experimental Procedures

In Silico Specificity and Off‐Target Analysis of the CsCalS7 RNAi Target Sequence

The nucleotide sequence selected for gene silencing was evaluated for specificity and conservation within the C. sinensis callose synthase 7‐like gene family. The target sequence was aligned, using BLASTn (NCBI) with default parameters, with the three predicted CsCalS7 transcript variants: X1 (LOC102612996; XM_006448823.4; CsCalS7a), X2 (LOC102610094; XM_052439635.1; CsCalS7b) and X3 (LOC102620841; XM_052439472.1; CsCalS7c). For each alignment, percentage identity, alignment length and the occurrence of mismatches and gaps were recorded. These data were used to assess sequence conservation among the variants.

To assess whether the knockdown may be paralog‐specific or broad, the RT‐qPCR primer sequences were aligned against the CsCalS7b and CsCalS7c transcripts.

To evaluate whether small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) derived from the hpRNAi construct could potentially target additional callose synthase genes or unrelated transcripts, an in silico off‐target analysis was performed against the C. sinensis genome (GCA_022201045.1). RNAi off‐target prediction was performed using the siRNA‐Finder (si‐Fi) software (Lück et al. 2019), using the default parameters for plant siRNA prediction.

Construction of hpRNAi Expression Vector

To silence CsCalS7 (XM_006448823.4), primers were designed within the coding sequence. Approximately 590 bp fragments of CsCalS7 were PCR‐amplified using the primer pairs CscalS7a_XhoI/CsCalS7a_KpnI and CsCalS7a_HindIII/CsCalS7a_BamHI (Appendix S4). The PCR products were individually cloned into the pGEM‐T Easy vector (Promega). The presence of the CsCalS7aF1 fragment was confirmed by restriction digestion with XhoI and KpnI, whereas CsCalS7aF2 was verified using HindIII and BamHI. Both 590‐bp fragments were further validated by sequencing and subsequently subcloned into the pHANNIBAL vector (5824 bp) in opposite orientations. Subsequently, the hpRNA cassette (Appendices S3D and S4), driven by the CaMV 35S promoter (approximately 4000 bp), was excised by digestion with SacI and PstI and ligated into the corresponding SacI and PstI sites of the binary vector pCAMBIA2301 (11,634 bp).

The resulting hpRNAi construction (pCambia_hpCsCalS7) was transformed into the Agrobacterium tumefaciens EHA105. When the construction is expressed in plants, a hpRNAi spliced with the PDK intron (767 bp) is produced.

Citrus Transformation

Epicotyl segments of Hamlin sweet orange in vitro‐germinated seedlings were used as explants for citrus transformation with A. tumefaciens EHA105 carrying pCambia_hpCsCalS7, following the methodology described by Boscariol‐Camargo et al. (2016). Shoots were regenerated in MS basal medium (Murashige and Skoog 1962) supplemented with 30 g/L sucrose, 12 g/L agar and 100 μg/mL kanamycin. The regenerated shoots were first tested for GUS activity and blue tissues were considered positive for transformation. Well‐developed shoots were then confirmed by PCR using specific primers designed to anneal to the kanamycin resistance (kan) gene embedded in the pCambia_hpCsCalS7 vector. Shoots with GUS assay and PCR positives were grafted onto 6‐month‐old Rangpur lime (Citrus limonia) rootstocks and transferred to a restricted access greenhouse with an average temperature of 28°C.

Transgenic Lines, Controls and Plant Replication

Eight transgenic lines (RNAi), one wild‐type—non‐transgenic control ( C. sinensis ‘Hamlin’) cultivated in vitro, and one control RNA‐interference—DsRed RNAi ( C. sinensis mutant ×11), grafted onto Rangpur lime (Citrus × limonia), were cultivated in the restricted access greenhouse. The control DsRed RNAi is a transgenic plant that carries the hairpin‐expressing vector (pCambia2301_hairpinDsRed) to silence a Colletotrichum abscissum mutant expressing the DsRed fluorescence protein (Goulin et al. 2022). CsCasl7‐related RNAi lines and control plants were propagated using the grafting technique, obtaining six replicates from each transgenic line and six replicates from each control.

CscalS7 ‐Related Transcripts Silencing Evaluation in the RNAi Plants

RT‐qPCR was used to confirm if the hpRNA produced could silence the CsCalS7a gene in transgenic plants. Five fully expanded mature leaves (including midrib and petiole) obtained from each transgenic line and each control plant (wt and DsRed hairpin‐expressing line) were evaluated.

First, total RNA from control and RNAi leaves was isolated from each independent leaf using the Direct‐Zol RNA Miniprep kit (Zymo Research) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA quality was verified by separation on 1% agarose gel, and RNA concentration was determined using a NanoDrop ND‐8000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Reverse transcription was performed on 1 μg of total RNA using the iScript cDNA Synthesis kit (Bio‐Rad) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

For the expression assay, a specific primer pair (Appendix S4) was designed for CsCalS7 based on the sequence outside the region used to construct the hpRNAi vector. This primer sequence was also used to assess percent identity with other CsCalS7 paralogs and to identify potential off‐targets (Appendix S5).

qPCR was performed using the GoTaq qPCR Master Mix (Promega). Amplification was performed on three technical replicates of each biological sample with appropriate negative controls on an ABI PRISM 7500 SDS (Applied Biosystems) thermal cycler. The resulting data from each technical replicate were normalised with Fbox (encoding a member of the F‐box family) and ubiquitin genes (Mafra et al. 2012). Relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). Gene expression was shown as a fold change of the transgenic lines compared to the wt plants. A Python script executed in Google Colaboratory (Google 2023) was used to perform a Student's t‐test (p < 0.05), and it is available upon request. Graph bars represent the mean of replicates (n = 5) ± standard deviation and asterisks indicate significant downregulation (p < 0.05).

Callose Staining Measurement and Microscopy

Callose detection was performed on the petioles of leaves at similar developmental stages in the primary plants, following previously published protocol (Boava et al. 2017; Granato et al. 2019; Curtolo et al. 2020). Petioles from 6‐month‐old CsCalS7 RNAi and non‐transgenic plants were collected and immediately transferred to FAA solution (50 mL 37% formaldehyde, 50 mL glacial acetic acid, 900 mL 70% ethanol) (Koh et al. 2012). After 72 h of incubation in FAA solution at room temperature, the samples were maintained in 70% ethanol. 10‐μm‐thick transverse sections were obtained from each plant using an automatic sliding microtome (Leica; SM2010R). The sections were stained with blue aniline and examined on an Olympus BX61 fluorescence microscope using a 355–375 nm excitation filter, a 400‐nm dichromatic mirror and a 435–490 nm emission filter. Images were captured at 10× magnification. All genotypes were processed identically; the samples were analysed immediately after sectioning, and identical imaging parameters were applied across all treatments.

Two leaves from each plant were sampled, and 15 transversal sections from each leaf sample were examined and imaged. The blue aniline fluorescence intensity was measured using ImageJ (v. 1.53c) (Schroeder et al. 2021). For quantification, measurements from individual images were first averaged to generate a section‐level value. Section‐level values were then averaged to obtain a single plant‐level mean. These plant‐level means were subsequently used for statistical analysis. The statistical methods used are described in the Section 4.11.

CLas Inoculation

After 6 months of propagation, six replicates from the non‐transgenic control (wt), six from the DsRed RNAi control and six from each of the confirmed CsCalS7 RNAi silenced lines (RNAi1, RNAi2, RNAi3, RNAi9 and RNAi15) were inoculated with CLas by grafting. Two CLas‐infected budwoods obtained from a positive HLB plant were grafted on the opposite side of the plant's primary stem. Infected budwoods were kept on the plants, but any shoots emerging from them were removed after sprouting.

CLas Quantification

CLas detection was performed 5 months after the bacterial inoculation for all six propagated replicates from CsCalS7 RNAi and non‐transgenic (wt). For each plant, five fully developed leaves were randomly collected from different sections. Petioles and midribs from the five leaves were pooled and macerated using a TissueLyser II (Qiagen). DNA extraction was performed using the CTAB method, and DNA concentration was determined with a NanoDrop 8000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) and adjusted to 100 ng μL−1. CLas was quantified by qPCR using primers for amplification of the 16S rDNA gene and a FAM/Iowa Black FQ label probe (IDT), as previously described (Li et al. 2006; Boava et al. 2015). To determine the copy number (CN) of the CLas 16S rDNA gene, a standard curve was established using serial dilutions of a pGEM‐T (Promega) vector cloned with the 16S rDNA gene. The number of 16S rDNA copies per microlitre was estimated according to the formula: CN = (M × N)/(L × D), where M is the minimum concentration of DNA detected (g/mL); N is Avogadro's number; L is the length of DNA in kb compared to the plasmid size (3.05 kb); and D is a conversion factor to transform 1 kb of DNA into Daltons (6.6 × 105). The results are presented as log CN. The statistical methods used are described in the Section 4.11.

Starch Assays

All six propagated replicates from CsCalS7 RNAi and non‐transgenic (wt) were evaluated 5 months after the bacterial inoculation. Starch content was assessed using leaf blades from five leaves collected per propagated plant. Quantification was performed according to previously published protocols (Boava et al. 2017; Granato et al. 2019; Curtolo et al. 2020). The samples were dehydrated in an oven at 70°C for 48 h and ground using beads (3 mm in diameter) at 30 Hz for 120 s in a TissueLyser II (Qiagen). The starch content was estimated for each biological replicate by enzymatic analysis according to Amaral et al. (2007), using 10 mg from a pool of the five dehydrated leaves from each plant. Absorbance was measured in 96‐well microtitre plates using a Microplate Reader (Model 3550; Bio‐Rad) at 490 nm with glucose (Sigma Aldrich) as the standard. Regression was calculated to estimate the glucose values according to absorbance. For this purpose, a standard curve was generated using a glucose solution at concentrations of 0, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 2.5, 5, 7.5 and 10 μg mL−1. The statistical methods used are described in the Section 4.11.

All six propagated plants maintained in the greenhouse were evaluated using a grading scale to assess field plants infected with HLB severity (data not published). This scale was previously adapted to evaluate smaller plants and was divided into six grades (0 to 5), with key points indicating increasing. Statistical Analysis

A Python script executed in Google Colaboratory (Google 2023) was used to perform statistical analysis and create a bar graph, and it is available upon request. The Shapiro–Wilk test verified the normality of the data from each assay.

After the normality test, the quantitative data for callose deposition, CLas levels and starch content were analysed using a one‐way ANOVA (p < 0.05) and multiple comparisons with the Tukey's post hoc test to identify pairwise differences (p > 0.05). Lines that share at least one letter in common do not differ significantly (p > 0.05).
