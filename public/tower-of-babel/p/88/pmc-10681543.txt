A unified DNA‐ and RNA‐based NGS strategy for the analysis of multiple types of variants at the dual nucleic acid level in solid tumors
Authors: Huijuan Chen, Bing Wang, Yiran Zhang, Yingshuang Shu, Henan Dong, Qian Zhao, Chunyan Yang, Jianji Li, Xiaohong Duan, Qiming Zhou
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTargeted next‐generation sequencing (NGS) is a powerful and suitable approach to comprehensively identify multiple types of variants in tumors. RNA‐based NGS is increasingly playing an important role in precision oncology. Both parallel and sequential DNA‐ and RNA‐based approaches are expensive, burdensome, and have long turnaround times, which can be impractical in clinical practice. A streamlined, unified DNA‐ and RNA‐based NGS approach is urgently needed in clinical practice.MethodsA DNA/RNA co‐hybrid capture sequencing (DRCC‐Seq) approach was designed to capture pre‐capture DNA and RNA libraries in a single tube and convert them into one NGS library. The performance of the DRCC‐Seq approach was evaluated by a panel of reference standards and clinical samples.ResultsThe average depth, DNA data ratio, capture ratio, and target coverage 250 (×) of the DNA panel data had a negative correlation with an increase in the proportion of RNA probes. The SNVs, indels, fusions, and MSI status were not affected by the proportion of RNA probes, but the copy numbers of the target genes were higher than expected in the standard materials, and many unexpected gene amplifications were found using D:R (1:2) and D:R (1:4) probe panels. The optimal ratio of DNA and RNA probes in the combined probe panel was 1:1 using the DRCC‐Seq approach. The DRCC‐Seq approach was feasible and reliable for detecting multiple types of variants in reference standards and real‐world clinical samples.ConclusionsThe DRCC‐Seq approach is more cost‐effective, with a shorter turnaround time and lower labor requirements than either parallel or sequential targeted DNA NGS and RNA NGS. It is feasible to identify multiple genetic variations at the DNA and RNA levels simultaneously in clinical practice.Keywords: DNA‐based NGS, genomic variations, next‐generation sequencing, RNA‐based NGS

INTRODUCTION

Multiple types of somatic genomic alterations, including small insertions and deletions (indels), single nucleotide variants (SNVs), copy number variations and amplifications (CNVs), and gene fusions and rearrangement have a pivotal role in precision cancer medicine for the rapidly increasing number of approved gene‐targeted therapies and immune‐targeted approaches. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Numerous genomic alterations have been identified that impact tumor therapy selection. Detecting a targetable genomic alteration profile is a prerequisite for the effective application of targeted therapies. For example, in non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), the established predictive molecular biomarkers include mutations in EGFR, BRAF, K‐RAS, and HER‐2; gene rearrangements in ALK, ROS1, and RET; NTRK1/2/3 gene fusions; 5 and a MET exon 14 skipping mutation. Moreover, a high level of MET amplification 6 , 7 and HER2 amplification 8 are regarded as emerging biomarkers for novel targeted therapies. Broad molecular profiling that identifies all the classic actionable biomarkers and the emerging biomarkers is recommended by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines for NSCLC therapeutic options.

As one of the recommended testing techniques by the NCCN guidelines for tumor genomic profiling, targeted next‐generation sequencing (NGS) is a powerful and suitable technique to comprehensively evaluate the ever‐growing list of biomarkers for cancer in daily clinical practice. Based on a limited number of actionable biomarkers, it requires a lower initial volume of samples, shorter turnaround time, easier data analysis, and simpler results interpretation compared to whole genome sequencing (WGS) and whole transcriptome sequencing (WTS). 9 , 10 In recent years, DNA‐based targeted NGS approaches have been widely applied in routine clinical practice to guide precision medicine. Multiple custom and commercial targeted DNA panels covering different targets have been designed and broadly used for the analysis of SNVs, indels, CNVs, structure variants (SVs), gene fusions, and exon‐skipping in tumor specimens. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Several of these panels have been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) or the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) for analyzing tumor genomic profiling or as a companion diagnostic to guide targeted therapy, such as MSK‐IMPACT (468 cancer‐associated genes), FoundationOne CDx (324 genes), and Guardant360 CDx (61 genes). Although large DNA panels consisting of hundreds or thousands of cancer‐related genes are powerful for evaluating the mutation landscape, it still is a great challenge for DNA‐based assays to identify gene fusions and exon‐skipping accurately, 15 , 16 , 17 particularly novel gene fusions. Fusion breakpoints that often occur within introns, large intergenic regions, and highly repetitive regions in DNA will impact the performance of the DNA‐based assays because of the size limitations of hybridization‐capture probes or the difficulty of tiling in a hybridization‐capture assay. 18 In addition, DNA‐based assays cannot evaluate the expression state and level of the fusion genes.

RNA‐based targeted NGS approaches have been found to be effective in identifying gene fusions and exon‐skipping and are more accurate compared with DNA‐based assays. 17 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 In several reports, additional RNA‐based NGS assays were suggested to detect targetable kinase gene fusions and exon‐skipping in samples without detectable pathogenic driver mutations or with a failure to yield optimal outcomes by DNA‐based assays. 23 , 24 , 25 Moreover, RNA‐based NGS has been recommended by the NCCN guidelines for gene fusion and exon‐skipping analysis. However, RNA‐based NGS is rarely used for somatic SNV or indel identification because RNA is unstable, has varying expression levels, and lacks double‐stranded context and stable evidence. Combined targeted DNA‐based NGS with RNA‐based NGS systems are increasingly used in molecular diagnostic laboratories to provide a full landscape of genomic profiling of tumors. Nearly all molecular diagnostic laboratories apply RNA‐based NGS assays as a complementary test after mainstream DNA panels or perform paired DNA‐ and RNA‐based NGS assays in parallel or sequentially. 24 , 26 , 27 , 28 These strategies are expensive, labor‐intensive, and require a large amount of input material and, therefore, are not practical in real‐world clinical practice.

In this study, we combined two split assays, a DNA‐based NGS panel and RNA‐based panel, into a single assay to analyze both DNA and RNA templates in a streamlined procedure, allowing us to detect multiple types of variants in tumor specimens easily, cost‐effectively, and feasibly in daily clinical practice.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample types

All of the clinical samples in this study were collected from patients who underwent clinical NGS testing at ChosenMed Clinical Laboratory. Sample types in this study included surgical and biopsy specimens prepared as formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks. Tumor cellularity was evaluated by board‐certified pathologist through hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining under an optical microscope. Specimens with ≥10% tumor tissue and adequate tissue for the experiment were selected. Written informed consent following approved guidelines was obtained from each patient. Standard references were purchased from Horizon Discovery Group PLC.

Nucleic acid preparation

Genomic DNA was extracted from FFPE tissue sections using a Concert FFPE DNA kit (ConcertBio), and RNA was extracted using a Concert FFPE total RNA kit (ConcertBio) following the manufacturer's instructions, and after manual microdissection, if necessary. The concentrations of the extracted DNA and RNA were quantified using a Qubit fluorometer 3.0 (Life Technologies). The quality was confirmed using a QIAxcel advanced system (Qiagen).

DNA‐based NGS

Hybrid capture‐based targeted DNA NGS was conducted using a custom‐designed ChosenOne NGS gene panel (ChosenMed). The panel was 2.094 M bp in size and contained 18,910 probes against 1123 genes listed in Table S1. At least 100 ng of isolated DNA was used for pre‐capture library preparation using a Rapid MaxDNA Lib Prep kit (ABclonal Technology) through the following sequential steps: ultrasonic fragmentation, end repair, ligation, and low‐cycle PCR amplification. Then, the pre‐capture library was hybridized and captured using the designed DNA probe panel following the Twist Fast hybridization and wash kit (Twist Bioscience) manufacturer's instruction. The indexed sequencing libraries were pooled and sequenced using an MGISEQ‐2000 sequencing platform (BGI) with 100‐bp paired‐end cycles.

RNA‐based NGS

A custom‐designed RNA panel 295.598 kb in size, which consisted of 5256 probes covering the entire transcript sequences of 89 genes (Table S1), was used for analyzing gene fusions and exon‐skipping. At least 100 ng of total RNA was used for the pre‐capture library construction using a Fast RNA‐seq Lib Prep kit V2 (ABclonal Technology) through the following sequential steps: enzymatic fragmentation, fragmented RNA to cDNA conversion, second strand synthesis, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification. As a DNA‐based NGS, the pre‐capture library was captured using the designed RNA probe panel to generate the sequencing libraries. The final libraries were pooled with DNA‐based libraries and sequenced simultaneously.

Total nucleic acid (TNA)‐based NGS assay

DNA and RNA extraction and DNA‐based and RNA‐based pre‐capture library preparations were conducted as described in Sections 2.3 and 2.4. The two pre‐capture libraries were mixed according to the following equation to generate a new pre‐capture library: Amount of pre‐capture RNA library (ng) = amount of pre‐capture DNA library (ng) × [the average insert size of the pre‐capture RNA library (bp)/the average insert size of the pre‐capture DNA library (bp)] × [expected sequencing data volume of the post‐capture RNA library (Gb)/expected sequencing data volume of the post‐capture DNA library].

Then, the new library was captured using a mixture of designed DNA and RNA panel probes, which were mixed in defined proportions. The captured fragments were pooled and sequenced on an MGI 2000 as described in Section 2.3. To assess the optimal ratio of the DNA panel probes and RNA panel probes, the ready‐to‐use DNA panel probe was mixed with the RNA panel probe at the following ratio: DNA probe:RNA probe, 1:1, 1:2, 1:4, 1:8, 1:16, and 1:32, designated D:R (1:1), D:R (1:2), D:R (1:4), D:R (1:8), D:R (1:16), and D:R (1:32), respectively. Six reference materials with six different levels of EGFR, ERBB2, and MET gene copy numbers were designated levels A, B, C, D, E, and F for CNV detection.

NGS data processing

An in‐house bioinformatic analysis system was used for data analysis. Briefly, the raw data were filtered using Fastp (v0.22) to generate clean sequence data. The clean data were aligned to the human reference genome (Hg19/GRCh 37) by Burrows‐Wheeler Aligner (BWA, v0.7.17)‐MEM. 29 Duplicate reads were marked using Picard (v1.119), and quality score base re‐calibration and indel realignment were performed by GenomeAnalysisTK (GATK, v4.2.6.1). 30 Vardict (v1.8.2) and VarScan 31 were used for identifying the SNVs and indels, and CNVKit and FusionMap were used for the analysis of copy number variants (CNVs) and gene fusions, respectively. For RNA‐based data, the transcript sequence of a corresponding BED file containing the designed target regions was used as a reference for calling gene fusions. STAR, FusionCatcher, and STAR‐fusion were used for the RNA sequencing data analysis. The MSI status was identified using MSIsensor. 32

Additional data analysis and statistics

The multiple types of calling variants were further verified using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV version 2.5.14), including manual verification if necessary. Prism 8 for Windows (GraphPad Software) was used for statistical analysis and diagram drawing.

RESULTS

Unified capture procedure for DNA‐ and RNA‐based libraries (TNA‐based NGS)

The DNA/RNA co‐hybrid capture sequencing (DRCC‐Seq) approach was designed so that the pre‐capture DNA and RNA libraries were captured by a combined probe panel in a single tube, and converted to one NGS library without splitting the DNA and RNA panels during the clinical laboratory processing (Figure 1).

Assay workflow of DNA/RNA co‐hybrid capture sequencing approach. Genomic DNA was processed to pre‐capture the DNA NGS library through fragmentation, adaptor ligation, and PCR amplification. The total RNA was subjected to fragmentation and reverse transcription to generate the double‐stranded, fragmented cDNA. The pre‐capture RNA library was prepared through adaptor ligation and PCR amplification. The different indexes or barcodes were used during the DNA and RNA library preparation process to identify the DNA and RNA data from the same sample. The pre‐capture DNA and RNA libraries were mixed according to the empirical equation in section 2. Materials and Methods. Then, the library mixture was captured using the combined custom DNA and RNA panel probes in a single tube and converted to one sequencing library.

To identify the optimal ratio of DNA and RNA probes in the combined probe panel, a series of mixtures of DNA panel probes with RNA panel probes were generated (see section 2. Materials and Methods), and four clinical samples with known SNVs and indels, gene fusions, and MSI statuses were selected. They were previously analyzed by our separate DNA and RNA probe capture assays (see section 2. Materials and Methods). For DNA panel data, the average depth, DNA data ratio, capture ratio, and target coverage 250 (×) had no significant differences between DNA + RNA (separately), D:R (1:1), D:R (1:2), or D:R (1:4). However, these parameters decreased significantly as the proportion of RNA probes increased from D:R (1:8) to (1:32) (Figure 2A and Table S2). For the RNA panel data, the RNA data ratio and the panel gene count were positively correlated, and the rRNA ratio was negative correlated with the proportion of RNA probes (Figure 2B and Table S3). The composition ratio of DNA and RNA panel probes had no effect on the on‐target ratio of the DNA and RNA panel data (Figure 2A,B), SNV and indel (Figure 2C), gene fusion (Figure 2D), or MSI status (Figure 2E) calling.

RNA data ratio and panel gene count were positively correlated with the proportion of RNA probes. (C) SNVs and indels. (D) Gene fusions. (E) MSI status. (F) Copy number variants. To determine how the composition ratio of DNA and RNA panel probes affected CNV detection, a series of reference materials with different EGFR, ERBB2, and MET copy numbers were analyzed (see section 2. Materials and Methods). The DNA + RNA (separately), D:R (1:1), and D:R (1:2) analyses detected similar EGFR, ERBB2, and MET copy numbers with the expected results. EGFR, ERBB2, and MET copy numbers detected by D:R (1:4) were higher than expected in the high‐level standard materials (levels C, D, E, F) (Figure 2F). Notably, many unexpected gene amplifications were detected by D:R (1:2) and D:R (1:4) in the high‐level standard materials (level D, E, F) (Table S4). Upon further investigation, we found that there were probes in the designed RNA panel targeting all of these unexpected amplified genes. Therefore, the optimal ratio of DNA and RNA panel probes in the combined probe panel was 1:1 in our study.

Proof‐of‐concept study identified multiple types of cancer‐related variants

A cohort of six reference standards and six FFPE tissue samples with the results of parallel DNA‐ and RNA‐based panel analyses were selected to evaluate the feasibility and reliability of the designed DRCC‐Seq approach using a D:R (1:1) combined probe panel. In total, 34 SNVs in eight different genes, six indels in two genes, 12 gene fusions, 8 CNV of 5 different genes, and 2 MSI‐H were involved (Table S4). All the variants were identified by our DRCC‐Seq approach, without false‐positive calls (Table S5). Detected mutant allele frequencies (MAF) and gene fusion frequencies were highly correlated to the nominal MAFs and gene fusion frequencies of the reference standards (Figure 3). These results demonstrated that the DRCC‐Seq approach was indeed feasible and reliable.

Performance of the DRCC‐seq approach in real‐world clinical samples

To test the usability of the DRCC‐Seq approach, FFPE samples from 40 patients were selected, including 20 patients with lung cancer, 12 patients with gastric cancer, and 8 patients with colorectal cancer (CRC). All 40 samples were successfully sequenced and analyzed, with sufficient acceptable DNA and RNA reads. The average depth, capture ratio, and on‐target ratio were 1590×, 51.28%, and 97.12%, respectively. Overall, 39/40 (97.5%) of samples were found to harbor at least one SNV or indel. Among these samples, a total of 17 CNVs were detected in 11/39 (28.20%) samples. Fifteen gene fusions were identified in 12/39 (30.76%) samples, and 6 of 15 gene fusions detected in the DNA‐based panel data were found in the RNA‐based panel data simultaneously, while 9 other gene fusions were observed only in the RNA‐based panel data. Notably, MET exon 14 skipping was found at the RNA level in one lung cancer sample but not at the DNA level. All 39 samples were MSS except for one that was MSI‐L (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

Targeted NGS, with the ability to concurrently sequence a panel of genes commonly altered in solid tumors and hematopoietic cancers, has become increasing common in clinical practice. 33 , 34 Currently, in many clinical laboratories, DNA‐based targeted NGS analysis has become a routine approach for mutation detection instead of multiple different traditional molecular assays, such as Sanger sequencing and qPCR. Recently, several studies demonstrated that RNA NGS was a valuable addition to identify fusions and exon‐skipping events, especially for novel or unusual gene fusions. 25 , 28 , 35 , 36 , 37 A study by Benayed et al. found that among 254 driver‐negative lung cancer cases analyzed by a large DNA panel (MSK‐IMPACT), 14% (36/254) of cases were identified as previously undetected alterations, most of which (33/36) were actionable alterations. In NSCLC, an RNA‐based approach detected a higher proportion of MET exon 14 skipping cases than did DNA‐based analysis (4.2% versus 1.3%). 17 Another study showed that treatment options were changed by adding RNA NGS analysis in 5% of all NSCLC patients, in which 22% never smoked. 24

Although the value of RNA‐based NGS analysis is increasingly being realized and has been recommended by NCCN guidelines for gene fusion and exon‐skipping analysis, the practical feasibility in real‐world daily clinical practice is still unclear due to the high cost and long turnaround time (TAT) of parallel DNA NGS and RNA NGS or a sequential approach (RNA NGS only performed in cases in which no mutations were detected by DNA NGS). The average targeted DNA‐based NGS costs were estimated at €607 (±207) in large‐scale, cost‐effectiveness analyses. 38 The parallel DNA NGS and RNA NGS approach is approximately twice that of the DNA NGS cost. The average costs of the sequential approach may be reduced by omitting RNA NGS analysis in cases with clear mutations in DNA NGS. However, the median TAT of the sequential approach was 15 days, versus 7 working days for the parallel approach. 24 Therefore, we developed a streamlined procedure for DNA/RNA co‐hybrid capture sequencing (DRCC‐Seq), which converted the pre‐capture DNA and RNA libraries into one NGS library (Figure 1). Compared with the parallel hybrid capture‐based DNA and RNA NGS approach in our laboratory, the TAT, cost, and labor requirements of the newly designed DRCC‐Seq approach were shorter, cheaper and less than those in the previous parallel approach.

In our study, we found that the average depth, DNA data ratio, capture ratio, and target coverage 250 (×) of DNA panel data had a negative correlation with the increase in the proportion of RNA probes. This may be caused by DNA and RNA panel probes competitively binding at the same target areas. The SNVs and indels, fusions, and MSI status were not affected by the proportion of RNA probes, but the copy numbers of the target genes were higher than expected in the standard materials, and many unexpected gene amplifications were found using the D:R (1:2) and D:R (1:4) probe panels. This may be associated with the additional sequences captured by RNA probes because all detected unexpected amplified genes had the corresponding probes in the designed RNA panel.

Our DRCC‐Seq approach is feasible and reliable in real‐world clinical practice. In a small validated panel sample, including reference standards and clinical samples with known genetic alterations, 100% concordance was found between multiple types of variants detected by the DRCC‐Seq approach, and no false‐positive alterations were detected. In real‐world clinical samples, 6 of 15 gene fusions were identified at both the DNA and RNA level, and 9 were only found at the RNA level. More importantly, MET exon 14 skipping was found at the RNA level but not at the DNA level. These results further confirmed the value of RNA‐based analysis as previously reported. In real‐world lung cancer samples, EGFR mutations were the most prevalent events, and several well‐annotated genetic alterations, such as K‐RAS mutations, ALK fusions, and ROS1 and NTRK fusions, were found in this study. The prevalence of alterations was similar to other studies with Eastern Asian populations. 39 , 40 In gastric cancer, TP53 point mutations were the most common events, and EGFR, ERBB2, CCND1, and TOP2A gene amplifications were detected in samples, but no gene fusions were found. These results were largely consistent with published literature. 41 In CRCs, K‐RAS point mutations, and SNVs and indels in APC were detected frequently. No CNVs or gene fusions were identified. The different genomic profiling of different tumors indicated that DRCC‐Seq could reliably identify the genetic alterations in various types of tumors.

In conclusion, our study presented a reliable, feasible, and streamlined approach, DRCC‐Seq, to identify multiple types of genetic variants at the DNA and RNA level simultaneously. It is more cost‐effective with a faster turnaround time and lower labor requirements than either parallel or sequential targeted DNA and RNA NGS approaches. This approach holds further clinical and investigational potential, which could benefit the majority of patients with potential actionable genomic alterations, and also those with rare and novel variants.
