Influence of Pre-treatment CA 125 Levels on Survival Outcomes Across Ethnic Groups in Ovarian Cancer Patients

ABSTRACT
AimThis study evaluates demographics, clinicopathological features, and mortality influencers among 16,466 US patients with ovarian cancer diagnosed between 2010 and 2017, focusing on CA 125 levels and racial disparities affecting outcomes.MethodsWe analyzed SEER data for 16,466 patients diagnosed from 2010 to 2017. Demographic, clinical, overall mortality (OM), and cancer-specific mortality (CSM) variables were assessed. Factors with P < 0.1 on univariate Cox regression were entered into multivariate Cox models to identify independent prognostic factors, with hazard ratios greater than one indicating adverse prognosis.ResultsAmong 16,466 patients with ovarian cancer diagnosed between 2010 and 2017, most were aged 40–79 years and presented with distant-stage disease. The majority of patients were non-Hispanic White, followed by Hispanic and non-Hispanic Black patients. Serous carcinoma was the most common histologic subtype, and most tumors were poorly differentiated or undifferentiated. Multivariate analysis identified age, marital status, tumor stage, race, tumor subtype, tumor grade, CA-125 level, chemotherapy, and surgery as significant predictors of survival. Hispanic patients with borderline CA-125 levels demonstrated higher mortality risk, whereas elevated CA-125 levels were not significantly associated with increased mortality among non-Hispanic Black patients.ConclusionThese findings emphasize early detection, personalized treatment, and social support to improve outcomes. Persistent racial disparities, independent of CA 125 levels, highlight the need for targeted research and interventions. This study adds to existing literature and supports continued efforts to enhance screening, treatment, and supportive care for ovarian cancer patients, particularly to address inequities in survival nationwide. Future studies should integrate biomarker stratification with equity-focused clinical strategies.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer remains one of the most challenging gynecological malignancies to diagnose and treat, with survival outcomes varying significantly among different ethnic groups. CA-125 is a tumor marker commonly associated with ovarian cancer and is primarily used for monitoring treatment response, assessing disease progression, and evaluating prognosis following tissue diagnosis. Racial disparities have been shown to influence the survival outcomes of women affected by ovarian cancer. Research indicates that CA 125 levels at diagnosis can vary significantly among ethnic groups.1 However, to the best of our knowledge, there is currently a paucity of studies addressing the influence of CA 125 on survival outcomes among different ethnic groups.

Research indicates that pretreatment CA-125 levels may vary significantly among ethnic groups and may reflect differences in tumor burden, disease biology, and clinical outcomes. A study by Smith et al. found that African American women often present with higher CA 125 levels than their Caucasian counterparts, potentially reflecting more advanced disease at diagnosis or differences in tumor biology.2 Similarly, Lee and colleagues (2012) reported that Asian women had lower initial CA 125 levels but did not necessarily have better survival outcomes, suggesting that CA 125’s prognostic value might vary by ethnicity.3

The variability in CA 125 levels extends beyond diagnosis into treatment monitoring and recurrence surveillance. Morales-Vasquez et al. observed that Hispanic women had a slower decline in CA 125 levels during first-line chemotherapy, which correlated with a longer progression-free survival.4 Socioeconomic and access-to-care factors also significantly impact the utility of CA 125 in managing ovarian cancer across ethnic groups. According to the study by Hernandez et al., disparities in healthcare access and utilization can lead to delayed diagnosis and treatment initiation, affecting the marker’s effectiveness as a monitoring tool.5 Moreover, cultural beliefs and mistrust in the healthcare system may lead to underutilization of CA 125 testing in certain ethnic populations, further complicating efforts to use this marker effectively across diverse groups.6,7

The impact of CA 125 on survival outcomes in ovarian cancer patients varies significantly among different ethnic groups, influenced by a combination of biological differences, socioeconomic factors, and access to care. Some ethnic groups have also been found to be a poor prognostic factor 8–|-10. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are no studies evaluating the impact of CA 125 on ovarian cancer survival among different ethnic groups. This study aims to analyze independent prognostic factors of women diagnosed with ovarian cancer over the past 2 decades with a particular focus on the interaction between the CA 125 level and different ethnic groups.

Methods

2.1. Study design

We conducted a population-based retrospective cohort study on patients with ovarian cancer, drawing data from the SEER (Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results) research database, specifically utilizing the 18 registries included in the November 2020 submission (http://www.seer.cancer.gov). Funded by the United States National Cancer Institute, the SEER Program stands as one of the most comprehensive and reliable sources of cancer data in the United States. The SEER 18 database compiles information on cancer incidence, clinicopathological characteristics of patients, and survival data from 18 population-based cancer registries, representing close to 28% of the U.S. population.11 Given that the dataset is de-identified and publicly available, this study was not subject to review by an Institutional Review Board.

2.2. Patient selection

2.2.1. Inclusion criteria

Patients diagnosed with ovarian cancer between 2010 and 2017 were selected based on criteria established in prior research.12 Utilizing ICD-O-3, site and morphology codes for histology/behavior, we identified individuals with ovarian cancer. The ICD-O-3 site and histology codes used for case identification. Our study was limited to those whose ovarian cancer was the primary tumor, confirmed through positive histological findings, immunotherapy results, or genetic testing. This approach enhanced the precision of our results and reduced the likelihood of including false-positive diagnoses.

2.2.2. Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded from our study if they had unknown age at diagnosis, indeterminate tumor stage, unspecified tumor location, or unrecorded racial background. Additionally, individuals diagnosed posthumously via autopsy were also omitted from the analysis. After applying the exclusion criteria, 16,466 patients were included in the final analysis.

2.3. Study variables

2.3.1. Main exposures

All variables and their interaction were the main exposures of interest.

2.3.2. Sociodemographic and tumor characteristics

For the study, we assessed variables including age at diagnosis, tumor stage, annual income, marital status, race, residential area, tumor subtype, tumor grade, pretreatment CA-125 levels at diagnosis, use of radiation therapy, year of diagnosis, surgical resection, and chemotherapy treatment.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Crude and adjusted Cox proportional hazard regression analyses were conducted to examine the effects of CA125 levels and ethnicity on mortality from ovarian cancer. Variables displaying a value of less than 0.1 in the univariate Cox regression analysis were subsequently included in the multivariate Cox proportional hazard analysis to identify independent prognostic factors influencing overall mortality (OM) and cancer-specific mortality (CSM). A hazard ratio (HR) greater than 1 indicated negative prognostic factors. The significance of all tests was determined using a two-sided approach, with a 95% confidence interval and a P value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were carried out using STATA18 software.

Results

After application of the exclusion criteria, 16,466 patients diagnosed with ovarian cancer between 2010 and 2017 were included in the final analysis. Most patients were aged 40–79 years, and the majority presented with distant-stage disease at diagnosis (61.0%). Non-Hispanic White patients represented most of the cohort (68.2%), followed by Hispanic patients (14.7%). Serous carcinoma was the predominant histologic subtype (65.1%), and most tumors were poorly differentiated or undifferentiated. Elevated pretreatment CA-125 levels were present in 88.6% of patients at diagnosis. Most patients underwent surgery (99.3%) and chemotherapy (81.1%) (see Table 1).

Crude Cox proportional hazard regression analysis demonstrated several factors associated with overall mortality (OM) and cancer-specific mortality (CSM) (Table 2). Increasing age was associated with higher mortality risk. Compared with patients aged 00–39 years, the hazard ratio for OM was 1.62 (95% CI 1.42–1.85) for patients aged 40–59 years, 2.66 (95% CI 2.34–3.03) for those aged 60–79 years, and 4.67 (95% CI 4.04–5.40) for patients aged ≥80 years. Marital status was also associated with mortality. Compared with married patients, widowed individuals had higher mortality (OM HR 1.68, 95% CI 1.58–1.79), as did divorced or separated individuals (OM HR 1.25, 95% CI 1.17–1.33).

Tumor stage showed a strong association with mortality. Compared with localized disease, distant-stage disease had a hazard ratio of 7.79 (95% CI 6.97–8.69) for OM and 12.01 (95% CI 10.42–13.84) for CSM. Racial differences were also observed. Compared with non-Hispanic White patients, non-Hispanic Black patients had higher mortality (OM HR 1.35, 95% CI 1.24–1.47), while Hispanic patients had slightly lower mortality (OM HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.86–0.97). Tumor subtype and grade were significantly associated with mortality. Carcinosarcoma demonstrated one of the highest risks (OM HR 6.86, 95% CI 5.96–7.88), and poorly differentiated tumors were associated with increased mortality (OM HR 5.37, 95% CI 4.74–6.10). Elevated CA-125 levels were associated with increased mortality compared with normal levels (OM HR 2.73, 95% CI 2.49–2.99). Surgical treatment was associated with reduced mortality (OM HR 0.30, 95% CI 0.24–0.37).

Multivariate Cox proportional hazard regression analysis identified several independent predictors of overall mortality (OM) and cancer-specific mortality (CSM) (Table 3). Increasing age remained significantly associated with higher mortality, particularly among patients aged ≥80 years (OM HR 2.59, 95% CI 2.22–3.02; CSM HR 2.16, 95% CI 1.83–2.54). Advanced tumor stage was strongly associated with mortality, with distant-stage disease demonstrating the highest risk (OM HR 6.57, 95% CI 5.79–7.47; CSM HR 9.48, 95% CI 8.09–11.10). Non-Hispanic Black patients had higher mortality compared with non-Hispanic White patients (OM HR 1.29, 95% CI 1.18–1.40; CSM HR 1.31, 95% CI 1.20–1.43). Elevated pretreatment CA-125 levels were independently associated with increased mortality (OM HR 1.50, 95% CI 1.37–1.66; CSM HR 1.57, 95% CI 1.41–1.75). Chemotherapy and surgery were associated with reduced mortality risk.

Interaction analysis between race and pretreatment CA-125 levels demonstrated significant differences in mortality risk across racial groups (Table 4). Hispanic patients with borderline CA-125 levels had significantly increased overall mortality (HR 5.71, 95% CI 1.49–21.94) and cancer-specific mortality (HR 7.05, 95% CI 1.70–29.27). Elevated pretreatment CA-125 levels were not significantly associated with increased mortality among non-Hispanic Black patients.

Discussion

The comprehensive analysis of 16,466 US patients diagnosed with ovarian cancer from 2010 to 2017 provides a deep dive into the demographics, clinicopathological features, and mortality influencers of this disease. Primarily affecting women aged 40 to 79, the study highlights the predominance of ovarian cancer in middle-aged and older women, with a significant number diagnosed at a late stage, emphasizing the disease’s insidious nature. The data reveal a majority of non-Hispanic white patients, though significant numbers of Hispanics and non-Hispanic blacks indicate the disease’s wide impact, possibly affected by genetic and socioeconomic factors. Urban residency and higher economic status correlate with higher detection rates, suggesting better healthcare access. The predominance of serous carcinoma and high-grade tumors reflects the disease’s aggressiveness. Mortality analysis through multivariate Cox proportional hazard regression indicates age, marital status, tumor stage, racial disparities, tumor biology, and treatment modalities as significant factors influencing survival. The findings underscore the critical need for early detection, personalized treatment approaches, and the importance of social support, alongside ongoing research to improve outcomes for ovarian cancer patients in the US. The interaction between CA 125 levels and race in this cohort shows that Hispanic patients with borderline CA 125 levels face significantly higher mortality risks. In contrast, elevated CA 125 levels do not significantly change the risk for non-Hispanic Black patients.

The age distribution of ovarian cancer patients, as depicted in the provided data, is consistent with established literature, indicating a higher incidence among women aged 40 to 79, with the peak occurrence in the age group of 60–79 years. This aligns with previous research emphasizing the heightened risk associated with advancing age.13 Furthermore, the challenge of late-stage diagnosis echoes a common finding in ovarian cancer literature, underscoring the difficulty in early detection due to the asymptomatic nature of the disease until it has progressed significantly.14 These findings highlight the critical need for improved screening strategies to enhance early detection and subsequent treatment outcomes for ovarian cancer patients.

In examining ethnic disparities, the data reflects patterns seen in prior studies, with non-Hispanic whites comprising the majority of cases but significant representation from other ethnic groups. This suggests a multifaceted interplay of genetic and socioeconomic factors influencing disease prevalence.13 Conversely, the emphasis on marital status and its correlation with ovarian cancer diagnosis, as noted in the provided data, diverges from conventional literature, offering a fresh perspective on demographic characteristics.15 Similarly, the observation that urban residency correlates with higher detection rates and the association between higher income brackets and diagnosis contrasts somewhat with existing literature, which often highlights disparities in outcomes based on race and insurance status.16 Additionally, while the treatment landscape aligns with standard protocols emphasizing chemotherapy and surgery, the minimal role of radiation therapy presents a departure from some studies exploring its potential efficacy, signaling a potential area for further investigation and optimization of treatment strategies.17

Consistent with prior studies, age emerges as a significant predictor of prognosis, with older patients facing heightened mortality risks, potentially due to age-related comorbidities and disease aggressiveness. This finding underscores the importance of considering age-related vulnerabilities in treatment planning and survivorship care.18 Similarly, the association between marital status and mortality risk resonates with previous research highlighting the impact of social support on cancer outcomes.19,20 However, while tumor stage at diagnosis remains a crucial prognostic factor, the lack of a significant effect of the year of diagnosis on mortality rates contrasts with some literature suggesting improvements in survival over time due to advancements in detection and treatment.21

Moreover, racial disparities in mortality risks observed in the analysis diverge from some existing literature. While the data indicates that non-Hispanic blacks experience higher mortality risks compared to non-Hispanic whites, the underlying factors contributing to these disparities warrant further investigation. Previous studies have documented racial inequities in ovarian cancer outcomes, often attributing them to differences in access to care, tumor biology, and socioeconomic status.22 The influence of geographic living areas and socioeconomic status on mortality, while less directly impactful in the adjusted analysis, echoes broader discussions on social determinants of health and their implications for cancer disparities.23 Our findings underscore the need for targeted interventions addressing systemic barriers to care and disparities in access to treatment resources and quality care.

The analysis of the interaction between tumor markers and race among US ovarian cancer patients offers both similarities and differences compared to existing literature. Similar to previous studies, the examination of CA 125 levels reveals its prognostic value in assessing mortality risks among ovarian cancer patients.24 However, the findings diverge concerning the impact of race on the association between CA 125 levels and mortality. While Non-Hispanic Black patients exhibit no significant alteration in mortality risks based on CA 125 levels, Hispanic patients with borderline CA 125 levels experience a notable increase in mortality risks. This study unveils an extremely important factor, as non-Hispanic blacks have higher mortality compared to their non-Hispanic white counterparts regardless of the pretreatment CA 125 level. This can be explained by a delay in the treatment of this ethnic group as evidenced in other cancers.25,26 Furthermore, Hispanics with a borderline CA 125 value were found to have a higher mortality. This can be explained by a low sample size of Hispanics and patients with a borderline CA 125 level. Ovarian carcinosarcoma had the highest mortality rate among all tumor subtypes. These findings mirror the current literature.27

Some limitations must be taken into account while analyzing this data. The retrospective nature of the study inherently limits control over unmeasured confounders, potentially skewing results due to the reliance on existing data. Selection bias may arise from the inclusion criteria based on the availability of CA 125 data, possibly excluding relevant cases and affecting generalizability. An additional limitation is the very small number of patients with borderline CA-125 levels (n = 27), particularly within specific racial subgroups such as Hispanic patients. This limited sample size likely contributed to the wide confidence intervals observed in the interaction analysis and therefore these findings should be interpreted with caution. Over the decades-spanning SEER database, changes in diagnostic criteria and treatment approaches introduce temporal bias that can impact study outcomes. The database’s racial and ethnic classifications may not accurately capture the complex biological diversity, leading to misclassification bias. Furthermore, the lack of comprehensive clinical information in SEER, including comorbidities and lifestyle factors, limits the depth of analysis on CA 125 levels and cancer outcomes. Consequently, the study’s findings may not be generalizable beyond the US population. However, our study has provided important epidemiological insights into how CA 125 levels interact with racial factors in ovarian cancer outcomes, offering a foundation for developing more personalized and effective cancer care strategies.

Conclusion

This population-based analysis of 16,466 patients with ovarian cancer identified age, tumor stage, race, tumor subtype, tumor grade, pretreatment CA-125 level, chemotherapy, and surgery as independent predictors of survival. Non-Hispanic Black patients experienced higher mortality, while Hispanic patients with borderline CA-125 levels demonstrated particularly poor outcomes. These findings highlight persistent racial disparities in ovarian cancer survival and support the need for earlier detection, equitable access to care, and personalized treatment strategies.
