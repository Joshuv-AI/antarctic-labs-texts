An Outbreak of Vitamin D Toxicity in a Family: Report of 3 Cases From Unregulated Fortification
Authors: Vivek Jha, Rahul Rouniyar, Sudhaker Dhanwada Rao, Ajit Dua, Sanjay Kumar Bhadada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVitamin D toxicity is a rare but potentially life-threatening cause of PTH-independent hypercalcemia. While most cases result from inappropriate supplement use, food-related exposures are increasingly recognized. We report a cluster of 3 adults from the same household who presented within 3 days with severe hypercalcemia and acute kidney injury. All had markedly elevated 25-hydroxyvitamin D, suppressed intact PTH, and a shared history of consuming locally sourced, unpackaged cooking oil. Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry analysis of the retained oil confirmed toxic vitamin D3 contamination at 1286 μg/mL (SI: ∼3.34 × 106 nmol/L). Treatment included IV fluids, calcitonin, and denosumab; 1 required dialysis. All recovered fully. This case series highlights the need to consider dietary sources in familial hypercalcemia and underscores the importance of regulating food fortification and edible product safety.Keywords: vitamin D toxicity, hypercalcemia, cooking oil contamination, community outbreak, food fortification

Introduction

Vitamin D, a fat-soluble prohormone, plays a key role in calcium-phosphate homeostasis, bone health, and immune regulation. It is mainly synthesized in the skin via ultraviolet B exposure and obtained through diet or supplements Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] levels of 20 to 50 ng/mL are considered adequate, values >100 ng/mL are excessive, and levels >150 ng/mL are typically toxic [1, 2].

Vitamin D toxicity (VDT), though uncommon, can be serious and usually results from prolonged high-dose intake due to supplement misuse or fortification errors [1]. It presents with hypercalcemia, suppressed intact PTH (iPTH), and markedly elevated 25(OH)D. Clinical features range from mild symptoms to severe complications like acute kidney injury (AKI) and nephrocalcinosis [1, 3].

The incidence of VDT is rising due to unregulated access to high-dose preparations. An Indian study found 1.2% of samples had 25(OH)D >100 ng/mL, and 0.1% had levels >150 ng/mL, though many were asymptomatic [4]. Another study reported VDT in 0.3% of hospitalized hypercalcemia cases, often linked to unsupervised supplement use or fortified foods [5].

We report a rare cluster of VDT due to contaminated cooking oil, causing hypercalcemia and AKI in 3 adults from the same household. This case highlights the need to evaluate shared exposures in familial hypercalcemia and underscores the importance of regulating food fortification to prevent such outbreaks.

Case Presentation

Three members of the same household presented over 3 days to our tertiary care center with symptoms consistent with hypercalcemia. The index case, a 26-year-old previously healthy man, reported 3 days of persistent vomiting, generalized weakness, polyuria, and worsening sensorium. He had reduced urine output in the preceding 24 hours, signs of dehydration, conjunctival redness, and anorexia. There was no history of fever, gastrointestinal infection, drug intake, or toxic exposure.

On the same day, his 22-year-old brother presented with diffuse abdominal pain, vomiting, polyuria, malaise, and conjunctival congestion. He denied any recent travel, fever, or substance use. Both were nonsmokers, consumed minimal alcohol, and had no comorbidities or hospitalizations.

Their father, in his early 50s, was evaluated subsequently despite lacking acute symptoms. He noted progressive fatigue, loss of appetite, and mild lethargy for the past week, without vomiting, constipation, altered mental status, or polyuria.

The index case, younger brother, and father had body mass index values of 22.8, 23.4, and 24.1 kg/m², respectively, all within the normal range.

All 3 had been using the same unpackaged, locally sourced cooking oil exclusively for the past 2 months. None of the patients had consumed vitamin D or calcium supplements, multivitamins, herbal products, fortified foods, or any medications known to affect calcium or bone metabolism. Occupational exposure to vitamin D compounds was absent. Family history was noncontributory, with no known hypercalcemia, endocrine disorders, nephrolithiasis, sarcoidosis, or autoimmune conditions. There were no symptoms suggesting thyroid, adrenal, or metabolic disorders or evidence of systemic infections.

On examination, all were conscious and oriented except the index patient, who was confused (Glasgow coma scale: E4V3M5). Conjunctival injection was seen in all. Both sons had signs of dehydration (dry mucosa, decreased skin turgor), while the father was euvolemic. No organomegaly, lymphadenopathy, neurological deficits, or skeletal abnormalities were noted.

The clustering of cases, shared dietary exposure, and consistent biochemical findings strongly pointed toward a common environmental source of vitamin D toxicity.

Diagnostic Assessment

All 3 patients exhibited significantly elevated albumin-adjusted serum calcium concentrations (14.2-17.3 mg/dL [SI: 3.55-4.32 mmol/L]) (reference range, 8.5-10.5 mg/dL [SI: 2.12-2.62 mmol/L]), highest in the index case, followed by his younger brother and father (Table 1). All had AKI, with serum creatinine ranging from 2.6 to 4.9 mg/dL (SI: 230-433 µmol/L) (reference range, 0.7-1.2 mg/dL [SI: 61.9-106.1 µmol/L]). Serum phosphate was low in both younger patients (2.3-2.4 mg/dL [SI: 0.74-0.77 mmol/L]) (reference range, 2.5-4.5 mg/dL [SI: 0.81-1.45 mmol/L]) despite suppressed iPTH levels; the father's phosphate remained normal. Hypomagnesemia and hypokalemia, along with granular casts on urine microscopy, suggested hypercalcemia-induced tubular injury in the sons. Serum albumin levels were normal in all, ruling out pseudohypercalcemia.

Serum alkaline phosphatase was mildly elevated (158-210 U/L [SI: 2.6-3.5 μkat/L]) (reference range, 44-147 U/L [SI: 0.7-2.5 μkat/L]), likely reflecting high bone turnover. Plasma 25(OH)D, measured using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry was markedly elevated at 988, 976, and 886 ng/mL (SI: 2470, 2440, and 2215 nmol/L, respectively) (reference range, 30-50 ng/mL [SI: 75-125 nmol/L]), confirming vitamin D toxicity (reference: 30-50 ng/mL). Correspondingly, iPTH was suppressed (1.2-6.1 pg/mL [SI: 0.13-0.67 pmol/L]) (reference range, 15-65 pg/mL [SI: 1.6-6.9 pmol/L]), consistent with a PTH-independent mechanism.

Twenty-four-hour urinary calcium excretion was elevated in all (298-452 mg/day [SI: 7.45-11.3 mmol/day]) (reference range, <300 mg/day [SI: <7.5 mmol/day]), with normal urinary creatinine, confirming accurate sampling. Ultrasonography showed bilateral increased cortical echogenicity in the sons, suggestive of parenchymal damage; the father's scan was normal. Chest radiographs were unremarkable.

Electrocardiogram in the index case showed shortened corrected QT interval of 340 ms (reference: 360-450 ms), likely due to hypercalcemia; electrocardiograms in the others were normal. A comprehensive metabolic and paraneoplastic workup excluded other causes of PTH-independent hypercalcemia: serum protein electrophoresis, free light chains, kappa-to-lambda ratio, and serologies for human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis B surface antigen, and antihepatitis C virus were normal. Angiotensin-converting enzyme, TSH, free T3, morning cortisol, and lactate dehydrogenase levels were all within reference limits.

Analysis of the retained cooking oil using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry revealed vitamin D3 at 1286 μg/mL (SI: ∼3.34 × 10⁶ nmol/L), confirming it as the exogenous source of toxicity.

Treatment

All 3 patients were admitted and managed with aggressive IV hydration using isotonic saline to promote calciuresis and correct volume depletion. For initial control of hypercalcemia, each received IV calcitonin for 48 hours, providing transient inhibition of osteoclastic activity and a modest reduction in serum calcium.

To achieve sustained calcium lowering, all were administered a single subcutaneous dose of denosumab (60 mg). Denosumab was preferred over bisphosphonates due to AKI in the index case and his brother, making bisphosphonates relatively contraindicated. Although the role of antiresorptive in vitamin D toxicity remains debated, the decision was guided by persistent hypercalcemia and unclear etiology at presentation.

The index case, who had the most severe presentation, received the same pharmacologic treatment but required intermittent hemodialysis starting on day 2 due to worsening clinical status. Dialysis was conducted using low-calcium dialysate (1.25 mmol/L), aiming for gradual correction of hypercalcemia without inducing hypocalcemia. Two sessions (4 hours each) led to improvement in mental status, normalization of sensorium, and progressive renal recovery.

No patient received corticosteroids or bisphosphonates during hospitalization. Serum electrolytes (potassium, magnesium, phosphate) were serially monitored in all, though none required supplementation.

Once serum calcium declined and renal function improved, all 3 were shifted from the high-dependency unit to general medical wards for continued care.

Outcome and Follow-up

All 3 patients showed marked clinical and biochemical improvement following treatment. Serum calcium levels normalized within 5 to 7 days, and renal function recovered in all, with serum creatinine returning to baseline by the end of the first week. The index case, who had required 2 sessions of hemodialysis, demonstrated complete neurological recovery by day 4 and renal recovery by day 7.

Serum phosphate, initially low in the 2 younger individuals, normalized alongside improvements in calcium levels and renal function. Conjunctival injection, noted at presentation in all 3, resolved with correction of dehydration and hypercalcemia, suggesting a reversible vasomotor response.

At 4 months follow-up, all patients maintained normal serum calcium (Fig. 1) and creatinine. While 25(OH)D levels remained markedly elevated at 4 weeks, they declined progressively and approached the upper limit of normal by 4 months in all patients (index case 62 ng/mL [SI: 155 nmol/L], younger brother 58 ng/mL [SI: 145 nmol/L], father 56 ng/mL [SI: 140 nmol/L]), consistent with the long half-life of vitamin D and removal of the toxic source (Fig. 2).

All patients were advised to avoid unsupervised vitamin D supplementation and received nutritional counseling. The contaminated oil was reported to public health authorities, leading to investigation of its distribution and recall.

Discussion

VDT, though uncommon, is increasingly reported, especially in regions with poorly regulated supplement or food fortification. While most cases stem from chronic high-dose intake, contaminated food is a lesser-known but serious cause. We report a unique familial cluster of VDT in 3 adults who developed severe hypercalcemia and AKI after consuming vitamin D3-contaminated cooking oil from a local roadside vendor.

All 3 patients showed a biochemical profile typical of VDT: markedly elevated 25(OH)D, suppressed iPTH, and significant hypercalcemia. While laboratory findings made the diagnosis clear, identifying a common source was challenging. Chemical analysis confirmed toxic levels of vitamin D3 in the cooking oil, which had been purchased from an unlabeled batch. None of the patients had previously used this source or consumed any branded fortified oils during the exposure period.

Interestingly, 2 patients had low serum phosphate despite suppressed iPTH, attributed to renal phosphate wasting from hypercalcemia-induced AKI. The diagnostic workup systematically excluded other causes of PTH independent hypercalcemia, including malignancies (eg, PTH–related peptide-producing tumors, osteolytic metastases), granulomatous diseases, vitamin A toxicity, and prolonged immobilization [6]. Markedly elevated 25(OH)D levels, low iPTH, and confirmed exposure to a toxic dietary source established the diagnosis of vitamin D toxicity.

Foodborne VDT outbreaks are rare globally, with most reports involving dairy products [7, 8]. One incident in Massachusetts involved hypercalcemia from overfortified milk [7], while another in Turkey reported pediatric cases due to a multivitamin error [8]. Our report is unique due to the novel source—contaminated cooking oil—and simultaneous toxicity in multiple nonsupplementing adults from the same household. To our knowledge, such cases are rarely documented.

A peculiar clinical sign observed in all 3 patients was conjunctival redness, which resolved completely with normalization of serum calcium. This likely reflected reversible vascular congestion or superficial vasodilation due to dehydration, hemoconcentration, or hypercalcemia-induced endothelial dysfunction [9]. Though underrecognized in modern literature, similar signs have been historically described and may serve as a subtle clinical clue to VDT.

Renal injury in vitamin D toxicity results from vasoconstriction, calcium deposition, and tubular damage. In our patients, AKI was predominant, with granular casts, electrolyte abnormalities, and cortical echogenicity in younger cases; 1 required dialysis, but all recovered with supportive care.

Two patients developed hypophosphatemia despite suppressed iPTH. While chronic kidney disease usually causes hyperphosphatemia, AKI with severe hypercalcemia can instead lead to phosphate wasting. Hypercalcemia impairs proximal tubular function, resulting in inappropriate renal phosphate loss, polyuria, and electrolyte wasting—explaining the paradoxical hypophosphatemia observed.

Younger family members had more severe biochemical abnormalities and renal impairment, likely reflecting higher calcium absorption, greater metabolic demand, or lower adipose sequestration of vitamin D. Age-related renal handling or genetic variations in vitamin D metabolism (VDR, GC, CYP2R1, CYP27B1, CYP24A1) may also play a role [10, 11].

All family members reported using the same contaminated cooking oil daily as their sole cooking medium for the past 2 months. There was no significant difference in the amount or frequency of oil consumption between the 3 severely affected patients and other household members who developed milder symptoms.

Management included IV hydration, calcitonin, and denosumab, with bisphosphonates avoided due to renal dysfunction. Dialysis was required only in the index case. All patients recovered without sequelae.

At follow-up after recovery, the 3 severely affected patients did not exhibit persistently higher serum calcium or 25(OH)D levels compared with their mother and cousin, making a genetic predisposition less likely; however, as genetic testing was not performed, this possibility cannot be completely excluded.

Beyond the 3 hospitalized cases, 2 family members—the mother and a cousin—had milder symptoms, and several neighbors who consumed the same oil also developed hypercalcemia, prompting a public health inquiry. The oil contained toxic vitamin D3 levels, likely from erroneous or unregulated fortification—a known risk in settings where informal vendors bypass regulatory oversight.

This series underscores the public health risks of unregulated fortification, where inadvertent exposure to fat-soluble vitamins can occur. Clinicians should assess shared dietary sources in clustered hypercalcemia and advocate stronger food safety oversight.
