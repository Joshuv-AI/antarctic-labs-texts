Pulp chamber debridement using various irrigant activation techniques in teeth with truss endodontic access – Using a new in vitro tooth model
Authors: Rajeswari Sivalingam, Smita Surendran, Velmurugan Natanasabapathy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and Aim:This study aimed to assess the effectiveness of different irrigant activation techniques in dissolving pulp tissue in the chamber of mandibular molars with Truss endodontic access, using a novel in vitro tooth model.Materials and Methods:Twenty-four freshly extracted human mandibular molars were selected, sectioned longitudinally, and standardized volume of bovine pulp tissue was placed into the pulp chamber. The split halves were then reassembled, sealed, and subjected to biomechanical preparation. The samples were randomly assigned to three groups based on the irrigation technique used: Conventional needle irrigation (CNI), modified endovac irrigation (MEV), and passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI). Tissue dissolution was evaluated using stereomicroscopic imaging at ×10–×22 magnification. Statistical analysis was done using one-way ANOVA and post hoc tests.Results:The PUI group exhibited the highest mean percentage of bovine pulp tissue dissolved, followed by the CNI and MEV groups. However, the differences among the three groups were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). None of the techniques was able to achieve complete dissolution of the pulp tissue.Conclusion:All three irrigant activation techniques, CNI, MEV, and PUI, were similar in terms of bovine pulp tissue dissolution while using the truss access preparation in the new in vitro tooth model.

INTRODUCTION

Conventional access cavity designs involve greater removal of dentin in order to gain straight-line access to the root canal system. The alternative cavity design suggested by Clark and Khademi[1] proposes conservation of tooth structure during access cavity preparation. Some of the modern access cavity designs are ultra-conservative access, Truss access, caries-driven access, and restoration-driven access. Truss access which is otherwise also known as orifice directed dentin design, involves accessing the root canals via two separate openings. The preservation of the bulk of dentin may help maintain the structural integrity of the endodontically treated tooth, in addition to improving the biomechanics of force distribution on masticatory loading.[1] But studies on fracture resistance, with conservative access, have produced controversial results, with few indicating that it improves the strength, while few others report the opposite.[234567]

With regards to the truss access, the major limitation could be in tissue debridement, as it has been found that there is more remnant pulp tissue with this type of access cavity.[3] Difficulty in identifying root canals and tissues during obturation is another limitation with truss access. Previous studies on pulp dissolution have been largely done by splitting the tooth which could cause a considerable amount of damage to the tissue within the chamber. The amount of pulp tissue prior to debridement is not quantified; hence, the results will not be an accurate estimate of the pulp tissue that was dissolved. Hence, there is a need to develop a new model that would accurately evaluate the amount of pulp tissue dissolved in the chamber. Hence, we are suggesting a novel in vitro tooth model to assess the pulp chamber tissue dissolution.

Different irrigant agitation systems like passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI), continuous ultrasonic irrigation, and apical negative pressure irrigation have been used as adjuncts, with the aim of improving the cleanliness and debridement of pulp tissue remnants.[4] In an earlier study, it was established that the modified EndoVac (MEV) technique, which involves the delivery of irrigants in one of the mesial root canals using a regular syringe and needle, while aspirating the solution from the neighboring canal, with the macro cannula of the EndoVac. MEV was efficient for debriding the root canal isthmus.[8] This technique could also be useful in clearing the tissue remnants of the pulp chamber in teeth with truss access. Hence, the aim of this study is to evaluate the pulp chamber tissue dissolution using different irrigant activation systems in Truss access of mandibular molar teeth using this new model.

Sample size estimation

G*Power software (version 3.1.9.2) (Heinrich Heine Universitat Dusseldorf, Dusseldorf, Germany) was used for sample size calculation. The sample size for the study was calculated based on a pilot study which was conducted among four samples each. Based on the effect size (0.83) obtained from the pilot study, the total sample size for the main study was calculated as 24 (8 per group, i.e., 8 × 3 = 24) by fixing the power of the study at 90% and the level of significance at 5%.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Twenty-four freshly extracted human permanent mandibular molars that were collected for a purpose not related to this study were used for the study, after obtaining institutional ethical clearance (Institutional Review Board of Meenakshi Ammal Dental College and Hospital) MADC/IEC/I/81/2025. A preoperative intraoral periapical was used to exclude teeth with pulp stones, pulp chamber calcifications, and any other obstructions within the pulp chamber that might impede the placement and clearance of the pulp tissue.

Proposed irrigation tooth model

All the selected teeth were mounted individually in Plaster of Paris with wax simulating the periodontal Space and the plaster simulating the bone. Truss access cavities were prepared in all the teeth using a No 2 Round bur [Figure 1a], and bio-mechanical preparation was done till 25/0.02 Rotary Ni-Ti Files after working length determination in all the samples. The teeth were removed from the plaster mould after setting and sectioned longitudinally into two halves using a diamond disc and chisel to split them into two halves such that they could be re-approximated [Figure 1b].

(a) Truss access cavity preparation, (b) Sectioned halves of tooth sample, (c) Pulp extripated from bovine teeth, (d) Stained pulp with acid fushion dye

The pulp chamber was cleaned of all debris by soaking it in NaOCL and washing it under running water. After soft tissue removal, the split halves were ultrasonically cleaned and then dried prior to placement of the bovine pulp tissue.

Bovine tissue preparation

The bovine pulp tissue for placement in the samples was prepared based on the method described by Al-Jadaa et al.[9] The pulp tissue was removed from the anterior teeth of bovine mandibles that were raised and slaughtered for food production [Figure 1c]. Access opening was done in the bovine teeth, pulp was removed using barbed broaches and stainless-steel H-Files (Mani, Tochigi, Japan), and the tissue was collected in Eppendorf tubes containing saline and stored at – 20°C. Multiple cold saline washes were used to remove excess blood.

The bovine tissue was thawed to room temperature and dried with tissue paper. Prior to the placement of the tissues into the pulp chamber, they were cut and weighed to 0.2 cc, for each half of the chamber.

The stained and preweighed minced bovine pulp tissue was packed into the pulp chamber area after staining with acid fuchsin dye (SRL-India) [Figure 1d]. The root canal orifices were blocked out using polytetrafluoroethylene tape. Prior to approximation, images of the chamber with bovine tissue were taken using a stereomicroscope (Lawrance and Mayo NSZ-606, India) with × 10 × 22 magnification [Figure 2c and d]. Once this was done, the split halves were re-approximated and sealed with flowable composite. The sealed assembly was then placed back into the respective moulds for further experimentation. Biomechanical preparation was done till 25/0.04 in all samples with rotary NiTi (Protaper gold Densply Sirona) along with irrigation. During instrumentation, 5 ml of 5.25% NaOCL, 5ml of 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), and saline were used as irrigating solutions. The samples were randomly divided into three groups, following which the final irrigation protocols were done.

Needle irrigation protocol

A 27-gauge open-ended needle (Unolok, Hindustan Syringes and Medical Devices, India) was placed at the level of each truss access root canal orifice, and irrigants were flushed into the pulp chamber in the following sequence:

Five milliliter of 5.25% NaOCl (Neelkanth Chemicals, Gujarat, India) delivered at a flow rate of 1.5 ml/min; 5 ml of saline (1 min); 5 ml of 17% EDTA (1 min) (Prime Dental Products, Mumbai, India) followed by a final rinse with 5 ml of normal saline.

Modified endovac irrigant group

The macro-cannula was placed in the distal portion of the Truss access when the irrigant was delivered in the mesial portion of the Truss cavity and vice versa [Figure 2b].

2.5 ml of NaOCL was delivered into the mesiobuccal (MB) canal using a syringe and needle at the rate of 1.5 ml/min, followed by 2.5 ml of saline, 2.5 ml of EDTA, and a final flush by another 2.5 ml of saline. As the irrigants were delivered in the MB root canal, the macro-cannula of the EndoVac connected to the suction was used simultaneously in the ML root canal. The same procedure was repeated using the irrigant delivery in the ML canal and the macro-cannula in the MB canal. A total of 5 ml of NaOCL, 5ml of EDTA, and 10ml of saline was used.

Total of 5 ml of 5.25% NaOCl delivered at a flow rate of 1.5 ml/min; 5 ml of saline (1 min); 5 ml of 17% EDTA for 1 min, and a final rinse with 5 ml of saline.

Passive ultrasonic irrigation group

The irrigation tip was inserted passively into the pulp chamber without touching the walls. 2.5 ml of 5.25% NaOCL, 2.5ml of 17% EDTA, and 5ml of saline were all used in two cycles for 10 seconds each in both MB and ML canals.

The volume of irrigant was standardized to 20 ml in all three groups.

After irrigation, each sample was gently split into two halves near the bonded area using a chisel and mallet. Each half was examined under a stereomicroscope at magnifications ranging from ×10 to ×22 to evaluate for pulp chamber dissolution [Figure 2e and f].

Evaluation criteria

The evaluation was performed by dividing the pulp chamber into five parts using lines V1–V4 (vertical lines) and H1–H3 (horizontal lines) [Figure 2a]. Scoring was evaluated by measuring the percentage of tissue dissolved in each part by comparing the pre-and post-irrigation stereo microscopic images. We developed a new scoring system based on the percentage of remaining pulp tissues postirrigation. A score of 5 was assigned when 100% of the tissue remained, 4 for 75%–99%, 3 for 50%–74%, 2 for 25%–49%, 1 for 10%–24%, and 0 for 0%–9% remaining tissue, respectively. Two trained, independent evaluators, blinded to the group assignments of each specimen, assessed and scored the samples using these criteria.

The data obtained was entered into Microsoft Excel spreadsheets and subjected to statistical analysis. All analyses were done using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS version 26, IBM, Chicago, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. As samples were chosen randomly, a parametric test of significance was used. The level of significance was set at 5%.

DISCUSSION

The current study introduces a novel in vitro model to quantify pulp tissue dissolution in the pulp chamber of mandibular molars treated with Truss endodontic access cavities. The rationale stems from the growing interest in conservative access designs that aim to preserve pericervical dentin, thereby improving the biomechanical performance of endodontically treated teeth. However, literature reports conflicting outcomes regarding the efficacy of cleaning and shaping with such designs. Neelakantan et al. reported that dentin-directed conservative designs, such as Truss access, retained significantly more pulp tissue within the chamber and canal systems due to limited irrigant penetration and restricted visibility.[2] Hence, this study was designed to assess the efficacy of three irrigation techniques, CNI, MEV, and PUI, in a simulated clinical scenario using bovine pulp as a histologically comparable alternative to human pulp tissue.

The newly developed model involved placing preweighed, stained bovine pulp into the split pulp chamber and reassembling the halves, which allowed for stereomicroscopic quantification of tissue dissolution. This approach addressed a significant limitation in previous studies where either qualitative assessments or variable tissue amounts were used. While the use of bovine pulp and split models introduces some anatomical discontinuity, the reproducibility and ability to standardize the pulp tissue volume represent major methodological strengths. Similar models using bovine pulp, tendon, and muscle have been successfully employed in prior investigations for irrigant efficacy.[1011]

Complete dissolution of pulp tissue was not achieved in any of the tested groups, despite the PUI group exhibiting the highest mean percentage of tissue dissolved. However, the differences observed among the PUI, CNI, and MEV groups were not statistically significant. This outcome is consistent with the findings of Neelakantan et al., who reported residual tissue in the pulp chamber even after cleaning and shaping, particularly when conservative access designs were used.[2] Although PUI is known for its lateral cleaning ability, remnants of pulp tissue were still observed, underscoring its limitations in achieving complete debridement.

In only one earlier published study on the MEV technique by Thomas et al., demonstrated that the MEV technique effectively debride the canal isthmus.[8] However, their study debris used was not standardized, and hence, that result cannot be directly compared to the current study, fresh, preweighed bovine pulp tissue. Moreover, while their study focused on isthmus debridement, the current study aimed to evaluate pulp tissue dissolution. This fundamental methodological difference may explain the discrepancy in the observed results.

Among the irrigation techniques tested, PUI (2.36 ± 0.91) demonstrated the highest mean percentage of tissue dissolved, followed by CNI (2.35 ± 1.32) and MEV (1.21 ± 1.08). Although the differences were not statistically significant, the trend supports findings from other studies. Siu et al.,(2010) demonstrated that EndoVac achieved significantly less debris at 1 mm from the working length compared to needle irrigation.[12] Townsend and Maki et al., and Chen et al., showed that PUI enhances smear layer removal, tissue dissolution, and irrigant dynamics via acoustic streaming and cavitation effects.[1314] These benefits are especially relevant in access-limited designs where conventional methods may be less effective. In addition, Al-Jadaa et al. reported significantly higher necrotic pulp dissolution in accessory canals when PUI was employed compared to static delivery methods.[9] In our study, however, none of the techniques achieved complete dissolution, highlighting the challenge of tissue removal in conservative access scenarios, even when advanced irrigation systems are employed.

The primary strength of the study lies in its experimental model, which allows accurate quantification of tissue dissolution, a critical advancement over traditional qualitative or in vivo assessments. Moreover, the model is compatible with conservative access cavity designs, making it highly relevant to current clinical trends. However, key limitations must be acknowledged. The simulated pulp chamber was formed by approximating two split halves, which, while structurally aligned, may not replicate the true anatomic continuity of pulp tissue. In addition, the study did not include radicular pulp or evaluate irrigant dynamics beyond the chamber, an area that significantly affects overall cleaning efficacy and treatment outcomes.

Clinically, this study emphasizes a growing concern: while minimally invasive access designs may preserve tooth structure and improve resistance to fracture,[1] they may also hinder effective disinfection. Without adjunctive irrigant activation strategies such as PUI or MEV, the risk of incomplete debridement and persistent infection may compromise long-term success.

Therefore, future research should focus on enhancing irrigation efficacy through increased concentrations of sodium hypochlorite and incorporating high-pressure advanced irrigant activation methods such as laser-activated irrigation to optimize pulp dissolution and canal disinfection.

CONCLUSION

Bovine pulp tissue dissolution in the chamber was the same in all the tested irrigant activation groups, and complete dissolution was not possible in any of the groups in teeth with Truss endodontic access, using the new model.
