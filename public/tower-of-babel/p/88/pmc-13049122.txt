Design and characterization of Zn-integrated cellulose nanoparticle derived from sugarcane bagasse for biomedical applications
Authors: Abhishek Verma, Gaurav Singh, Ankit Srivastava, Subash Chandra Gupta, Dalia Dasgupta Mandal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The valorization of agricultural residues for synthesizing advanced nanomaterials has garnered significant attention in recent years. This study reports the environmentally benign and cost-effective extraction of cellulose from sugarcane bagasse (SCB), a prevalent agro-industrial byproduct for the synthesis of nanoparticles integrated with Zn (CNP-Zn). Cellulose was extracted from SCB through iterative alkali treatment and bleaching, followed by in situ zinc incorporation during nanoparticle formation via acid hydrolysis and physicochemically characterized. Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) analysis indicated an average particle diameter of 341.52 nm (PDI 0.4786), whereas Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) and High-Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HR-TEM) images demonstrated quasi-spherical morphology (< 100 nm). The Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) confirmed the presence of characteristic cellulose functional groups. X-ray Diffraction (XRD) patterns revealed a crystalline structure. Antioxidant activity, assessed via the DPPH assay, demonstrated CNP-Zn’s free radical scavenging capacity. Hemolytic analysis (10–500 µg/ml) showed less than 2% red blood cell lysis, supporting the cytocompatibility of CNP-Zn. These nanoparticles exhibited moderate anticancer activity compared to their control (IC50 557.61 µg/ml) in the triple-negative breast cancer cell line MDA-MB-231. Apart from this, bio-stimulant and favourable biocompatibility have been monitored in plant assays (250–2500 µg/ml) on Vigna radiata. These nanoparticles also revealed moderate broad-spectrum UV protection with a Sun Protection Factor (SPF) of 13.54; CNP-Zn represents its good UV-protecting feature. These studies thus highlight the ability of agricultural waste-derived CNP-Zn as a sustainable nanomaterial for biomedical applications.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04540-7.

Introduction

Recent estimates indicate that the global annual output of lignocellulosic biomass (LCB) is approximately 181.5 billion tons, with primary sources including forests and cultivated crops (43%), grasslands (42%), and agricultural residues (15%) [1]. This vast resource base offers significant potential as a renewable feedstock for the sustainable generation of fuels and high-value chemicals. A comprehensive review of the transformation of LCB into such products has been published recently [2]. Sugarcane, a prominent example of LCB, is cultivated extensively worldwide for sugar extraction. In 2021, global sugarcane production approached 1.9 billion tons, with Brazil, India, and China leading as the top producers [1]. Ecuador has also experienced a steady increase in sugarcane cultivation to meet domestic requirements. Beyond sugar production, sugarcane’s juice is also used to produce distilled spirits through fermentation processes [3]. Sugarcane bagasse (SCB), a significant byproduct of the sugar industry, is the fibrous material that constitutes roughly 30% of the total mass of harvested sugarcane with an annual generation of around 540 million tons [1, 4]. These fibrous by-products are rich in cellulose (26–47%), hemicellulose (19–33%), lignin (14–32%), and some extractives (6–13%), including waxes, lipids, and soluble sugars [5, 6]. SCB has been studied for its use in extracting residual sugars (cellulose and hemicellulose), dye removal, anaerobic digestion, methane production, and composite manufacturing [7–11]. The utilization of cellulose from SCB not only increases the value of this agricultural waste but also highlights its potential as a valuable resource for sustainable bioproducts and waste management [12].

Cellulose is a naturally occurring biopolymer on Earth, serving primarily as a component of plant cell walls. It is made up of linear chains of β-1,4-linked glucose units that form a fibrous, crystalline structure that imparts considerable strength and durability [13]. In LCB, cellulose coexists with lignin and hemicellulose. So, the initial step for the isolation of cellulose involves the removal of lignin and hemicellulose from LCB. On an industrial scale, cellulose pulp production typically comprises two main stages: pulping and bleaching [12]. The Kraft process is the most widely implemented method due to its high efficiency and pulp yield; however, it produces sulfur-containing by-products that can pose environmental challenges during waste disposal. Organosolv methods have been developed, offering a sulfur-free approach that utilizes organic solvents under elevated temperature and pressure to selectively solubilize lignin, aided by the low viscosity of the solvent system, which enhances fibre penetration and lignin extraction [14]. Significantly, the solvent can be recovered post-process via distillation. Following pulping, residual lignin is typically eliminated through oxidative bleaching, a stage historically dominated by chlorinated reagents such as chlorine gas, chlorine dioxide, and sodium hypochlorite. Recent advancements in cellulose research have concentrated on engineering cellulosic materials at the micro and nanoscale, as reducing the dimensions of cellulose fibres to these scales can significantly alter their physical and chemical properties. Such micro and nanocellulose materials have demonstrated considerable potential across a wide range of fields, including biomedicine, pharmaceuticals, and materials science [15–20]. Nanomaterials are important in biomedicine due to their distinct physicochemical characteristics, including a high surface area-to-volume ratio, flexible size, shape, and surface chemistry, which enable advancements in targeted drug delivery, diagnostic imaging, tissue engineering, and cancer therapy [21, 22]. These characteristics improve drug solubility, bioavailability, and controlled release, lowering systemic toxicity and enabling targeted interventions such as gene therapy, biosensors, and regenerative scaffolds that resemble extracellular matrices [23–25]. The fabrication of nanocellulose can be achieved through various methods: mechanical processes such as high-pressure homogenization and grinding [26–28], chemical treatments including acid hydrolysis [29, 30], enzymatic hydrolysis, or combinations of these approaches [31, 32]. Nanocellulose exhibits superior reinforcement in biodegradable packaging and composite films, markedly enhancing mechanical properties through its biocompatibility, strength, and nanoscale characteristics. These attributes position them ideally for wound dressings, cartilage regeneration, and neural tissue scaffolds, while ongoing research explores novel applications in food packaging, biomedicine, and materials engineering [25, 33, 34]. Recently, many attempts have been made to easily fabricate these nanocellulose using acid hydrolysis, mechanical treatments, and chemical processes that are eco-friendly to nature. Nanocellulose, which includes nanocrystals (NCs) and nanoparticles (NPs), is suitable for biomedical scaffolds, wound dressings, and drug carriers due to its biocompatibility, low cytotoxicity, high biodegradability, and low immunogenicity when surface-modified. Typical biocompatibility assessment models for nanocellulose include in-vitro cytotoxicity tests (e.g., MTT), hemocompatibility tests that assess protein adsorption and hemolysis, and in-vivo studies involving subcutaneous implantation, intravenous administration, or bone defect models in rodents to track inflammation, tissue integration, and long-term degradation [35–37]. The development of nanoparticles from sugarcane bagasse (SCB) directly supports multiple United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [38]. Valorizing SCB agricultural waste promotes SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production) by fostering circular bioeconomy principles. Eco-friendly green synthesis innovates sustainable materials, aligning with SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation, and Infrastructure). Renewable multifunctional nanomaterials contribute to SDG 13 (Climate Action) [38, 39].

Many studies suggest that the synthesis of cellulose nanomaterials is very difficult due to their strong intermolecular hydrogen interactions, which aggregate and make their characteristics difficult to retain and scale up. Integrating these nanomaterials with metals, particularly zinc, can act as a stabilizing scaffold that physically separates particles and reduces strong hydrogen bonding, improving dispersion, mechanical strength, and added functionalities such as antibacterial and UV-blocking properties [40–44]. Zinc (Zn) metal plays an essential role in biomedicine for enzymatic functions, immunological modulation, and wound healing. Amalgamation of Zn into biopolymer nanoparticles is essential to enhance these benefits over bulk forms due to higher bioavailability, high surface area, and ROS-mediated antioxidant and anticancer properties [45, 46]. This combination overcomes solubility restrictions, improves pH-responsive drug delivery, photodynamic treatment, and biofilm suppression in multidrug-resistant illnesses [47]. So, Zn has been selected for this study over other metals due to its biocompatibility, affordability, and synergy with biomass-derived nanocellulose for sustainable therapeutics. Despite of huge interest in transforming agricultural waste into high-value nanomaterials, there are few investigations on zinc-integrated cellulose nanoparticles (CNP-Zn) sourced from agricultural waste. This study aims to bridge the gaps by employing chemical treatment of bagasse for the extraction of cellulose and its conversion into Zn integrated nanoparticles via a single-step synthesis model, thoroughly characterizing their properties using advanced instrumentation, demonstrating superior SPF, anticancer, and antioxidant properties. The innovative aspect of this research is the integration of extensive material characterization with biological and agricultural performance tests, including, for the first time, the demonstration of the positive impact of CNP-Zn on Vigna radiata root growth. The cytotoxicity of the CNP-Zn was studied against the MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cell line. This approach highlights the untapped potential of sugarcane bagasse for the synthesis of CNP-Zn and its diverse applications across biomedical, environmental, and agricultural sectors.

Materials and methods

Reagents and materials

Sugarcane bagasse and Vigna radiata were purchased from the local market in Durgapur, West Bengal, India. 2,2-diphenyl-1-picryl-hydrazyl-hydrate (DPPH) and Whatman filter paper—Grade 1 were obtained from Sigma Aldrich. Crucial reagents, including sodium hydroxide, nitric acid, ammonia solution, and others, were obtained from Merck. HiMedia supplied sodium sulfite, thiourea, urea, and a dialysis bag (pore size—135,000 Da). All experiments utilized Milli-Q water produced by a Millipore Type 1 Ultra Pure Water system. Filtration was achieved through a vacuum filter setup utilizing polycarbonate filters alongside Millipore filter paper. The Human embryonic kidney (HEK-293) and breast cancer cell lines (MDA-MB-231) were procured from the National Centre for Cell Science, Pune, India.

Pretreatment of SCB biomass and extraction of cellulose

The SCB was rinsed with distilled water to remove debris and dried at 65 °C. This dried biomass underwent milling using an electric mixer and grinder, subsequently being sieved to obtain a consistent size of 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm. The treated SCB was subjected to NH4OH at a 1:10 ratio for a duration of 24 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the cellulose extraction process was done by following the protocol reported by Singh et al. (2025) [48, 49]. The solid fraction of sugarcane bagasse treated with NH4OH underwent extensive rinsing with Milli-Q water to remove any remaining impurities before the fractionation process. Hemicellulose was extracted through a hot-water extraction process at 75 °C for 2 h, utilizing a mass ratio of 1:10 with water, and subsequently filtered with Whatman No.1 to yield an insoluble cellulose-lignin structure. Alkaline delignification was employed to extract lignin using 15% NaOH. The remaining solid cellulose-rich fraction underwent oxidative bleaching with 0.7% NaClO2 (pH 4) at boiling temperature for 4 h (1:50 fibre: liquor ratio). A secondary treatment with 5% sodium sulfite solution was used to yield purified cellulose and complete lignin removal. It was dried at 40 °C. Characterization of extracted cellulose was performed through FESEM, XRD, and FTIR for morphological evaluation and purity analysis.

Preparation and characterization of cellulose nanoparticles with Zn (CNP-Zn)

Cellulose obtained from SCB was dissolved in a specialized solution containing NaOH, Thiourea, Urea, and Water, and filtered using a 0.45 μm syringe filter [48]. To this, 2 mg/ml of Zinc salt (ZnSO4.7H2O) was added and stirred (800 rpm) at room temperature for 2 h. Post-filtration, an equal volume of 0.025 M HNO3 was added to the filtrate at a rate of 0.5 ml/min while continuously stirring at 800 rpm, and the mixture (pH 10) was allowed to stir at room temperature for 2 h. Thereafter, the product was dialyzed with deionized water at room temperature for 72 h, during which water was changed at equal intervals (6 h) to eliminate any remaining reactants and by-products. The dialyzed product (pH 7) was washed with deionized water, dried at 50 °C. This final product was designated as CNP-Zn. Characterization of CNP-Zn was performed through DLS, FESEM, and HR-TEM for size and morphological evaluation, XRD for crystallinity assessment, and FTIR for functional group analysis.

Hemocompatibility assessment assay

The impact of CNP-Zn and only cellulose nanoparticles (CNP) on the blood vascular system was investigated using the in vitro hemolysis assay [49]. In brief, samples were obtained from volunteers (with their consent and as per the Institute’s ethical committee clearance) who were in good health. A 2% suspension of hematocrit was prepared and subjected to different concentrations of the nanoparticles (ranging from 10 to 500 µg/ml). After 30 min of incubation at 37 °C, the samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min. The resulting mixture underwent analysis for hemoglobin levels using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 541 nm. Control tubes were prepared with 0.5% Triton-X100 and normal saline to assess the percentage of hemolysis [50].

Antioxidant evaluation: DPPH assay

The DPPH assay was performed on the CNP-Zn to assess its antioxidant properties. For this, 3 ml of CNP-Zn at varying concentrations was mixed with 1 ml of a 0.1 mM DPPH stock solution, which had been stored in the dark for 30 min. Positive and negative control tubes were made using 0.5% Triton-X100 and normal saline, respectively. The absorbance was subsequently recorded at 517 nm. The IC50 value and the percentage of radical scavenging were evaluated [49, 51].

In vitro toxicity assessment on HEK-293 cells: MTT assay

To assess the effect of CNP-Zn on the survival of HEK-293 cell lines, a mitochondrial reductase activity assay was conducted utilizing MTT. Cells were placed in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in DMEM enriched with 1% FBS. Following 24 h, the cells underwent washing and were subsequently treated with CNP-Zn at concentrations ranging from 25 to 1000 μg/ml. After a 2 h incubation at 37 °C, 0.1 ml of extraction buffer (20% SDS, 50% dimethylformamide) was introduced, and the cells were allowed to incubate overnight at 37 °C. Measurements of absorbance were conducted at 570 nm utilizing a microplate reader, with the extraction buffer acting as the blank reference, and compared with the respective untreated cell line [48, 52]. PBS was taken as a negative control, while Ethyl Methylsulfonate (EMS) was used as a positive control. Potential to combat cancer in the MDA-MB-231 lines

The MTT test was performed using a variety of concentrations (25–1000 μg/ml) of Zn integrated cellulose nanoparticles (CNP-Zn), on the triple-negative MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cell line, to determine their role as an anticancer agent. PBS was taken as a negative control, while Ethyl Methylsulfonate (EMS) was used as a positive control [48, 53].

Assessment of sunscreen protection factor

A sample solution was prepared in Milli-Q water to evaluate the sun protection factor (SPF) of CNP-Zn and was subsequently analyzed using spectrophotometry. The absorbance of the solution was meticulously recorded at wavelengths spanning from 290 to 320 nm, with precise measurements taken at 5 nm intervals. The SPF value was determined through the application of the Mansur mathematical method, which integrates absorbance data along with standardized values for the erythemal effect and solar intensity across each wavelength. Effect on plant growth: Vigna radiata plant bioassay

The seeds of Vigna radiata were surface sterilized using 0.1% HgCl2, followed by 70% ethanol and distilled water. Eight seeds were carefully arranged in a petri dish measuring 15 cm. The dish was lined with a Whatman No. 1 filter paper, which was subsequently moistened with 5 ml of various concentrations of CNP-Zn solutions in double-distilled water and incubated in a plant growth chamber set at a temperature of 22 °C, with alternating periods of light and darkness lasting 14 and 10 h, respectively. Double-distilled water was used as a control. The root length of V. radiata was measured using a thread and a scale on the fifth day of exposure [56].

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and the data represented are mean ± SD. Differences among groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Extraction of cellulose and its characterization

Alkaline extraction is a well-established technique for isolating lignin and cellulose from plant-based residues [57]. In the present investigation, sugarcane bagasse (SCB) subjected to NH4OH pre-treatment yielded cellulose at a rate of 0.56 ± 0.01 g/g of dry biomass. This yield aligns closely with earlier studies, which have reported cellulose recoveries of 0.5 g/g and 0.398 g/g by Jimenez (2009) and Yu (2018), respectively [58, 59]. These results underscore the reliability and efficiency of the alkaline pre-treatment approach for fractionating lignocellulosic components from agricultural wastes. Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) analysis of extracted cellulose from SCB revealed a crystalline morphology with irregular structural organization (Fig. 1a). Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was utilized to analyze the functional groups of extracted cellulose (Fig. 1b). Cellulose-specific peaks were clearly noticed at 3445.16 cm−1 (stretching of -OH groups), 2892.56 cm−1 (stretching of -CH groups), 1639.76 cm−1 (symmetric stretching vibrations of the glycosidic bond), 1077.96 cm−1 (stretching vibrations of the glucopyranose ring), and 888.76 cm−1 (vibrations of the glucopyranose ring), confirming the cellulose presence [48, 60–62]. The extracted cellulose from chemically treated SCB is crystalline in nature due to the removal of lignin and hemicellulose (Fig. 1c). Preparation of zinc-integrated cellulose nanoparticle (CNP-Zn) and its characterization

In the study, the yield of Zinc-cellulose nanoparticles (CNP-Zn) was achieved at 67.8 ± 1.3% through a urea/thiourea/NaOH aqueous system, which is found to be better than cellulose nanocrystals (< 20%) [64], Au/cellulose fibers (43%) [65], and nanocrystals (25 and 40%) [66]. This solvent system disrupted hydrogen bonding in cellulose, enabling consistent dispersion and accessibility of hydroxyl groups for Zn2⁺ coordination. During the synthesis of CNP-Zn, the process of acidification using HNO3 may lead to a decrease in cellulose solubility, resulting in precipitation and successfully capturing Zn2⁺ within the polymeric matrix. The protonation process also facilitated crosslinking and hydrogen bonding, resulting in the hardening of these nanoparticles [67]. Moreover, the rearrangement of cellulose chains with amphiphilic properties under these ionic conditions promoted the self-assembly into CNP-Zn. The consistent integration of Zn and the effective formation of particles underscore their promise in applications related to antimicrobial and biomedical fields.

Hydrodynamic size distribution of CNP-Zn: DLS study

Figure 2 represents the distribution of particle sizes for the synthesized nanoparticles, highlighting the proportion of particles based on their hydrodynamic diameters. The measured average particle diameter for CNP-Zn was found to be 341.52 nm, with a polydispersity index (PDI) recorded at 0.4786. A PDI of 0.4786 indicates moderate-to-high polydispersity, approaching the upper limit of acceptability for biomedical applications. While this reflects some size heterogeneity in the sample, the value remains within the practical range (0.2–0.5) for drug delivery systems [68]. The results closely correspond with previous research conducted by multiple investigations that have documented the hydrodynamic size of nanocellulose. According to the research conducted by Adsul et al. (2012), the measurement of nanocellulose was determined to be 400 nm. Recent reports indicated that the size could range from 157 to 240 nm according to Fuster et al. (2023), between 59 and 100 nm as noted by Samsudin et al. Evaluation of colloidal stability by zeta potential assessment

The CNP-Zn nanoparticles displayed a zeta potential of around −21.3 mV (pH 4), −25.85 mV (pH 7), and −38.68 mV (pH 9) (Supplementary Fig. 1). This trend underscores robust colloidal stability, particularly at neutral and alkaline pH, where values exceeding ± 25 mV generally undergo adequate electrostatic repulsion to avert aggregation [73]. Structural morphology of CNP-Zn: FESEM and HR-TEM study

The morphology and size distribution of the synthesized CNP-Zn nanoparticles were characterized by FESEM and HR-TEM (Fig. 3), revealing predominantly quasi-spherical particles with smooth surfaces and diameters below 100 nm. This morphology is consistent with earlier research on biopolymer-based nanoparticles [48, 75–77]. The quasi-spherical shape of CNP-Zn enhances its compatibility with biological systems and facilitates medical applications such as precise drug delivery and imaging techniques [46, 78]. Previous studies also suggest that nanoparticles sized between 10 and 200 nm demonstrate improved biodistribution and prolonged circulation times, which can enhance their therapeutic efficacy and targeting capabilities [79–81]. The apparent size discrepancy between DLS (341.52 nm) and microscopy (< 100 nm) reflects different measurement principles. DLS measures the hydrodynamic diameter, including the hydration layer and potential aggregates in solution, whereas FESEM and HR-TEM reveal the actual core particle dimensions in the dried state. Elemental analysis: EDAX study

The EDAX analysis of zinc-integrated cellulose nanoparticles (CNP-Zn) reveals the elemental composition (wt%): carbon (C) at 21.70%, oxygen (O) at 28.00%, nitrogen (N) at 12.78%, zinc (Zn) at 33.42%, and sulfur (S) at 3.91%. The relatively high zinc content (33.42 wt%) suggests CNP-Zn represents a cellulose-zinc composite rather than traditional doping, where the zinc component plays a substantial structural and functional role. Sulfur was identified in smaller amounts, probably due to leftover reagents or surface functional groups. The significant presence of carbon and oxygen validates the retention of a cellulose matrix abundant in hydroxyl groups, which function as binding sites for zinc ions. The elevated zinc proportion indicates a successful incorporation of Zn into the cellulose framework, consistent with amalgamation instead of mere surface adsorption. The elemental distribution aligns with earlier studies on zinc-doped biopolymer nanocomposites, suggesting that zinc content varies from 5 to 15 wt% depending on synthesis conditions [44, 67]. The EDAX results validate the effective integration of zinc and uniform dispersion within the cellulose nanoparticles, highlighting their potential for advanced applications such as drug delivery and antimicrobial agents.

Analysis of functional groups in CNP-Zn

The FT-IR spectrum of CNP-Zn exhibits multiple distinct peaks, signifying zinc's successful formation and incorporation (Fig. 4). The significant absorption at 3438 cm⁻1 is attributed to –OH stretching vibrations, highlighting the abundant hydroxyl groups in cellulose involved in nanoparticle formation and possible Zn2⁺ coordination. The peak at 2906 cm⁻1 corresponds to –CH₂ stretching, a feature typical of the cellulose backbone. The band at 1634 cm⁻1 is linked to O–H bending of adsorbed water and contributions from C = O stretching, suggesting intermolecular hydrogen bonding and possible structural rearrangement. A notable signal at 1052 cm⁻1 suggests C–O and C–O–C stretching, typical of glycosidic linkages found in cellulose [60–62]. Peaks at 524.27 cm⁻1 and 446.45 cm⁻1 in the FTIR spectra indicate Zn–O stretching vibrations, thus affirming the effective integration of zinc into the cellulose matrix [82]. Thermogravimetric analysis: thermal stability study

The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) thermogram for CNP-Zn highlights superior thermal resilience imparted by ZnO incorporation, with decomposition initiating later than in native cellulose nanoparticles (Fig. 5). Initially, CNP-Zn exhibited an initial minor mass loss of approximately 5% up to 150 °C, attributed to the evaporation of adsorbed moisture and low-molecular-weight volatiles inherent to the hydrophilic cellulose matrix [86, 87]. The primary degradation phase (45–55% loss) spanned 150–400 °C, linked to cellulose backbone depolymerization and volatile evolution. A secondary shoulder (15–25% loss) was observed between 400 and 600 °C, indicating char oxidation [88]. So, according to TGA analysis, 80% of the synthesized CNP-Zn nanoparticles are stable up to 300 °C. Our findings are consistent with previous findings by Onyszko et al. (2022) and Bakkardouch et al. (2021), which reported parallel yields and stability improvements [86, 89].

Antioxidant efficiency of zinc- cellulose nanoparticles

The antioxidant activity of CNP-Zn showed a notable improvement when compared to normal cellulose nanoparticles (CNP), with IC50 values recorded at 250.35 ± 0.25 μg/ml and 556.83 ± 3.84 μg/ml, respectively. These results are consistent with earlier research on metal-doped nanoparticles [90–92]. CNP-Zn also showed a better IC50 value than other metallic nanoparticles like AgNPs using Clinacanthus nutans leaf (IC50 417.05), Achillea biebersteinii flower (IC50 261.84), and Chaenomeles sinensis fruit (IC50 725.93) [93, 94]. The IC50 of common antioxidants like quercetin (1.5) and ascorbic acid (0.92) were also examined. The incorporation of zinc into cellulose nanoparticles creates extra active sites for radical scavenging, enhancing the overall antioxidant capabilities. This collaborative influence underscores the essential function of zinc in improving the physicochemical interactions within the cellulose matrix. The findings highlight CNP-Zn as a promising natural antioxidant, suggesting its potential use in biomedical and environmental areas where neutralizing free radicals is crucial [95]. The trend corresponds with comparable findings in zinc-doped nanocomposites, where the inclusion of metal ions markedly enhances antioxidant effectiveness via synergistic mechanisms [88, 96].

Hemocompatibility assessment of zinc- cellulose nanoparticles

The hemolysis of CNP-Zn is minimal, recorded at under 2% at a concentration of 500 μg/ml (Supplementary Fig. 3). This value is considerably below the hemocompatibility threshold commonly recommended for biomedical nano-formulations, which is generally accepted as non-hemolytic and safe [49, 97]. The observed minimal hemolytic activity indicates the hemocompatibility of CNP-Zn, suggesting its significant potential for various medical and therapeutic applications involving direct blood contact [98, 99].

In vitro toxicity studies of CNP-Zn on HEK-293 cell line

The cytotoxicity of cellulose nanoparticle-zinc (CNP-Zn) composites on HEK-293 cell lines was evaluated via MTT assay. Cells were treated with CNP-Zn at 25 to 1000 µg/ml for 24 h. No significant reduction (< 25%) in viability was observed in HEK-293 cell lines after CNP-Zn treatment, indicating low cytotoxicity and a suitable candidate for biomedicine applications (Supplementary Fig. 4). At 100 µg/ml, CNP-Zn exhibited superior cell viability of 98.2 ± 0.4%, outperforming ZnO-NPs (63.24%) and Ag/ZnO-NPs (22.47%). Even at 50 µg/ml, Ag/ZnO-NPs showed only 31.89% viability, underscoring CNP-Zn's enhanced biocompatibility [100, 101].

In vitro anticancer potential on MDA-MB-231 cell line

The evaluation of anticancer activity for CNP-Zn, which contains over 30% zinc, was conducted using MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells (Fig. 6). The findings revealed an apparent dose-dependent decline in cell viability (Supplementary Fig. 5), beginning with roughly 87.35 ± 0.62% viability at 25 μg/ml and dropping significantly to around 26.23 ± 0.71% at 1000 μg/ml. CNP-Zn exhibited dose-dependent cytotoxicity with an IC50 of 557.61 ± 0.95 µg/ml, indicating moderate-to-weak anticancer activity that was nevertheless improved compared to normal cellulose nanoparticles (724.4 ± 1.8 µg/ml) [48]. However, our findings are consistent with bimetallic B2O3-ZnO NPs (IC50 569.2 μg/ml), while biomass-derived ZnO Nanoparticles exhibited cytotoxic IC50 values of 476.9 μg/ml [41, 102]. Compared to alternative zinc-based nanoparticles, this heightened cytotoxicity could be linked to elevated zinc levels, leading to oxidative stress and mitochondrial impairment in cancer cells. Based on the literature, the cytotoxic effect may be attributed to zinc ion release, which has been reported to induce oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in cancer cells [103, 104]. Future mechanistic studies are needed to confirm these pathways in our CNP-Zn system. Determination of SPF

The SPF activity of CNP-Zn nanoparticles exhibited a significant increase with concentration, moving from 1.63 ± 0.07 at 20 μg/ml to 13.54 ± 0.57 at 200 μg/ml, whereas the normal CNP demonstrated only a slight enhancement from 0.046 ± 0.002 to 1.34 ± 0.067 within the same range (Fig. 7). The SPF value of CNP-Zn nanoparticles (13.54 ± 0.57) are better than Chitosan/tripolyphosphate nanoparticles (2.4 ± 0.4), sunscreen with 15 wt% TX-85 (10.73 ± 1.77), cream with 5 wt% Cin-CNCs/SiO2 (4.51 ± 0.71) and DES-grafted CNC (5.03) [106–108]. The nonlinear increase in SPF, which depends on concentration, highlights the enhanced UV-blocking ability of CNP-Zn nanoparticles compared to their normal CNP. This notable enhancement is due to the in-situ creation of ZnO within the composite matrix, which provides strong UV absorption and scattering capabilities. At every tested dose, the SPF values for CNP-Zn were notably elevated compared to those for CNP, highlighting the synergistic effect of zinc integration. The inclusion of ZnO significantly enhanced crystallinity and promoted optimal dispersion of nanoparticles on substrates, leading to improved film formation and overall performance in photoprotection [109]. Effect of CNP-Zn on plant growth

The assessment of nanoparticle biocompatibility is crucial for ensuring their safe use in agricultural practices. Due to its sensitivity and quick germination, Vigna radiata is frequently utilized as a model for evaluating phytotoxicity and growth stimulation [56]. The present study revealed that administering CNP-Zn at increasing concentrations (500–2500 μg/ml) brings about a bio-stimulant effect on root growth compared to the untreated control (Table 1). The control seeds exhibited the shortest root lengths (Supplementary Fig. 6). The most distinct root elongation occurred with CNP-Zn at 2500 μg/ml (121.2%), which outperformed CNP (105.38%) and cysteine-capped ZnO NPs (90%) [48, 110]. The observed effect arises from zinc ions that boost crucial enzymatic processes related to cell division and expansion, in conjunction with the cellulose nanoparticle matrix that improves water retention and nutrient absorption at the seed surface [21, 111, 112]. The antioxidative property of CNP-Zn reduces oxidative stress during germination, thus aiding in cellular expansion [56]. The noted growth in root length associated with higher CNP-Zn levels is consistent with earlier research suggesting that metal-doped and polymeric nanoparticles enhance root development by facilitating micronutrient delivery and refining the microenvironment [85, 113]. Conclusion

This study aimed to develop a method for producing zinc-cellulose nanoparticles (CNP-Zn) using sugarcane bagasse, a plentiful agricultural byproduct. Employing an eco-friendly approach that incorporates urea/thiourea/NaOH treatment along with zinc addition, nanoparticles exhibiting a high yield, quasi-spherical shape, with particle sizes as per HR-TEM analysis under 100 nm. Thorough analysis validated the effective integration of zinc ions within the cellulose structure, improving crystallinity and physicochemical stability. Biological evaluations revealed its antioxidant activity, anticancer effectiveness against MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells, and significant hemocompatibility, highlighting its potential in the biomedical field. Remarkably, CNP-Zn demonstrated a dose-dependent capability for UV protection with significant SPF values indicating its role as a UV-protecting agent, which may be utilized for different purposes. Furthermore, plant assays demonstrated dose-dependent root growth stimulatory properties on Vigna radiata, highlighting potential agricultural applications. Although the variation in nanoparticle size poses a challenge, these findings together revealed CNP-Zn as highly promising multifunctional materials with potential applications in biomedicine, cosmetics, and sustainable agriculture, in line with principles of green chemistry and goals of a circular bioeconomy. The major challenge is that sequential chemical treatments for zinc incorporation and cellulose isolation from sugarcane bagasse remain lab-scale, posing hurdles for industrial production despite being environmentally benign and cost-effective. So, in the future, additional investigations into release kinetics and in vivo safety are essential to enhance both clinical and environmental applications. Significant antioxidant activity and potent anticancer effects mechanisms will be studied on MDA-MB-231 cells to position CNP-Zn for drug delivery, and other cytocompatibility therapies.
