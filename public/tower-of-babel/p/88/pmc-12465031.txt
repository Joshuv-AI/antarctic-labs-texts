Cholesterol crystal embolization syndrome: Systemic and end-organ injury☆
Authors: Akanksha Mehla, Yash Patel, Majid Yavari, Mahmoud Khairy, Kevin Watat, F.N.U. Parul, Karuna Rayamajhi, Britni Smith, George S. Abela, Christopher A. Hanson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cholesterol embolization syndrome (CES) is characterized by the embolization of cholesterol crystals from atherosclerotic plaques into small and medium-size arteries. It predominantly occurs spontaneously in individuals with severe atherosclerosis or following vascular interventions. The dislodgment of cholesterol crystals triggers an inflammatory cascade in the affected vessels, leading to tissue ischemia and multi-organ dysfunction. Commonly affected are the kidneys, skin, and gastrointestinal tract, although any affected, though any organ system may be involved with varied presentations often making the diagnosis challenging. CES is diagnosed based on clinical suspicion supported by laboratory findings, such as eosinophilia, elevated inflammatory markers, imaging and/or histopathological evidence of cholesterol emboli. Treatment focuses on supportive care, addressing end-organ damage, and minimizing further embolization by optimizing atherosclerotic risk factors. Anticoagulation is generally avoided unless there is an alternative indication. Statins are commonly used for their pleiotropic effects on inflammation and plaque stabilization. In conclusion, CES is an underrecognized condition with significant morbidity. A high index of suspicion is essential for early diagnosis and intervention.

Introduction

Cholesterol-embolization syndrome (CES) is a systemic condition caused by the release of atherosclerotic plaque debris, mainly cholesterol crystals (CCs), from the aorta and its major branches into the distal circulation [1]. This results in ischemic and inflammatory damage to various organs [1]. CES must be distinguished from the more common form of arterial embolization where the abrupt dislodgement of a thrombus from an atheromatous plaque leads to acute ischemia and infarction of the affected organ. In contrast, CES involves the embolization of smaller cholesterol crystals, which leads to slower, progressive organ damage through both ischemic and inflammatory processes. While CES has historically been underdiagnosed, its detection has increased in recent years, likely due to greater clinical awareness, longer life expectancy in patients with atherosclerosis, and an increasing number of intravascular procedures [1].

Epidemiology

Despite numerous reports on the risk of plaque rupture resulting in cholesterol embolization, the true incidence and prevalence of cholesterol embolism remain unknown. This is primarily due to several factors, principally, the absence of a specific diagnostic test to detect cholesterol embolism antemortem, as well as the implication that the effects of atheroembolism typically become evident only after clinically significant end-organ damage has occurred. Additionally, it is likely that many atheroembolic episodes go unrecognized clinically, and those that are identified represent only a small fraction of the total occurrences of atheroembolism [2].

Early reports suggest that spontaneous atheroembolism has an approximate incidence between 0.79 % to 3.4 % in previously established autopsy studies [3,4]. In contrast, the incidence of atheroembolism related to cardiac catheterization has been reported to range from as low as 0.06 % for clinically evident embolization, 0.38 % for a combination of histologically and/or clinically diagnosed cholesterol crystal embolization (CCE), and up to 1.9 % for clinically significant end-organ damage [[5], [6], [7]]. Despite these reported incidences, it is estimated that nearly 80 % of cholesterol emboli are a consequence of an intra-arterial procedure [8]. However, more recent studies using non-obstructive general angioscopy (NOGA) have demonstrated that 80 % of aortic plaque rupture spontaneously but most of these events are asymptomatic [9,10] Abdominal aortic aneurysm repair ranks highest in the procedural incidence of embolization at incidence of approximately 77 % [11]. Given the challenges in diagnosing the condition and heterogeneity of presentation, current estimates and understanding of disease burden is inconsistent, and the true incidence remains unknown.

Pathophysiology

Cholesterol embolization syndrome develops under six critical conditions:1.Presence of a vulnerable plaque in a proximal, large-caliber artery, such as the aorta or the internal carotid, subclavian and iliac arteries.2.Rupture of the plaque, either spontaneously, through trauma, or due to interventional procedures.3.Embolization of plaque debris, including CCs and other harmful components.4.Lodging of these emboli in small to medium arteries, resulting in mechanical obstruction.5.Inflammatory response to the cholesterol emboli.6.End-organ damage caused by the effects of circulatory obstruction and inflammation.

Atherosclerotic plaque

Atherosclerosis is a chronically progressive condition characterized by unresolved inflammation and the accumulation of cholesterol deposits within the subendothelial layer of the vascular wall [12]. The formation of atheromas is thought to initiate with endothelial dysfunction or activation, which is a key factor in the pathophysiology of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [13]. Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) plays an integral role by accumulating in the arterial intima and inducing a local inflammatory response, eventually resulting in plaque formation [12]. The composition of these resultant atheromatous plaques typically consists of platelets, fibrin, necrotic cell debris, and CCs [14]. Early in plaque development, studies suggest a significant alteration in endothelial function, marked by increased permeability to atherogenic LDL particles. In vitro analyses of the vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) secretomes reveal that VSMCs predominantly generate lipid-retentive extracellular matrix components, further stabilizing plaque structure [15]. Despite these insights, the precise molecular and cellular mechanisms by which lipoproteins initiate and propagate atherosclerotic plaques are not fully elucidated [16].

Plaque rupture

Plaque rupture may occur spontaneously or as a peri-procedural complication. Unstable atherosclerotic plaques—those prone to rupture—are characterized by a lipid-rich necrotic core, a thin fibrous cap, and inflammation [17]. At the periphery of advanced plaques, increased concentrations of esterified lipids, inversely related to cap thickness, may reflect cell activity and a greater susceptibility to rupture [18]. Inflammation is a major factor in the formation of plaque and eventual instability. Many studies have demonstrated the role of inflammatory cytokines early in the formation of plaques and its eventual instability to rupture [17]. Specifically, ICAMs and VCAMs are essential in the internalizing of macrophages and leukocytes into the arterial intima leading to stimulation of smooth muscle migration and growth [19,20]. Leukocytes contribute to the inflammation by formation of extracellular traps that trigger cytokine production by macrophages [21]. Macrophages and lymphocytes within the plaque secrete metalloproteinases that can weaken the fibrous cap rendering the plaque vulnerable to rupture [22]. Moreover, the formation of cholesterol crystals noted in early plaque development further triggers inflammation via inflammasomes activating IL-1β as well as the complement pathway [[23], [24], [25]]. This inflammatory activity is then culminated by mechanical events where cholesterol crystallization within the plaque core expands to occupy a larger volume leading to stretching and thinning of the fibrous cap causing hemorrhage by traumatizing the vas vasorum with sharp-tipped CCs that then perforate the intima and trigger rupture and thrombosis [26]. Changes in blood flow, inflammation, and intraplaque hemorrhage—either spontaneous or resulting from invasive procedures—can lead to plaque erosion and rupture of contents [1]. CCs released into the circulation may cause downstream arteriolar obstruction - in vessels with diameters ranging from 150 to 200 micrometers [27]. Beyond mechanical blockage, CCs provoke inflammation: rabbit models show early panarteritis (24–72 h) with immune cell infiltration, followed by endothelial proliferation, fibrosis, and persistent vascular damage up to 160 days [28]. CCs also mechanically abrade the endothelium, impair vasodilation, and resist macrophage clearance due to insolubility, exacerbating vascular injury injury [29,30]. In humans, angioscopy of the abdominal aorta with NOGA revealed CCs being released from atherosclerotic plaques triggering inflammation [9,10]. Cholesterol atheroembolism in specific cardiovascular procedures

Cardiac catheterization & angioplasty

CES seems to be rare in cardiac catheterization procedures. In a prospective study, Fukumoto et al. found that of patients who underwent left-heart catheterization, only 1.4 % had complications of CES, including digital gangrene, blue toe syndrome, and livedo reticularis. The ascending aorta is the primary embolic source, with no difference between femoral and brachial access [31]. However, CCs can also be implicated in the no reflow phenomenon following coronary interventional procedures [29,30].

Cardiac surgery

In an autopsy study by Blauth et al., 221 patients (mean age 66 years; 58.8 % male) who underwent myocardial revascularization or valve surgery between 1982 and 1989 revealed cholesterol embolization or related abnormalities in 48 cases (21.7 %), while thromboembolic events were found in 14 patients (6.3 %). Cholesterol embolization was observed three times more frequently after coronary revascularization (26.1 %) compared to valve surgery (8.9 %). The likelihood of cholesterol embolization increases significantly with the severity of atherosclerosis in the ascending aorta, which increases with patient age. The brain was the most common site for cholesterol emboli, found in 8 out of 48 patients. Other sites included the spleen and kidneys with 5 cases and the pancreas with 3 cases. Multiorgan involvement was noted in 30 of the 48 patients [32]. However, off-versus on-pump CABG has demonstrated a reduction in cerebrovascular events [33].

Carotid interventions

CES has been observed after both surgical carotid endarterectomy and percutaneous carotid stenting. Major trials indicate that the rate of perioperative stroke and death ranges from 2.0 % to 7.5 %, with the majority being mild to moderate ischemic strokes due to cholesterol embolization or hypoperfusion linked to carotid clamping [34]. Carotid stenting has emerged as an alternative for patients with significant carotid artery stenosis; however, the procedure can result in the release of microemboli. Prospective studies have shown a high incidence of new ischemic brain lesions on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) after carotid revascularization; however, these studies were not randomized, limiting their validity [35]. The international carotid stenting trial (ICSS), which compared stenting versus endarterectomy in 1713 patients, found similar rates of fatal or disabling strokes in both groups (6.4 % vs. 6.5 %). In the MR sub-study of ICSS involving 231 patients, 50 % in the stenting group exhibited new DWI lesions compared to 17 % in the endarterectomy group, though most cases were asymptomatic [36]. The CREST trial found that the highest stroke risk following stenting occurs during or immediately after the procedure, with a median time to stroke of 0 days [37]. A recent meta-analysis revealed that 37.4 % of patients experienced DWI lesions after stenting, compared to 13.0 % after endarterectomy [38]. Furthermore, clinical strokes and transient ischemic attacks (TIAs) were higher after stenting (4.1 % vs. 1.8 %) [38]. However, recent use of protection devices has reduced the risk of neurological events with stenting [39].

Anticoagulant therapy

The potential association between anticoagulation therapy and cholesterol embolization syndrome manifesting as plaque hemorrhage, rupture, or microembolization remains uncertain due to insufficient evidence [40]. A retrospective analysis of 519 individuals with severe thoracic aortic atherosclerosis revealed no significant disparity in CES incidence between warfarin-treated and non-warfarin groups, with only five cases reported (1 % in each cohort: 3 on warfarin, 2 without) [41]. It is hypothesized that anticoagulation weakens the thrombus covering a ruptured plaque allowing more release of CCs from the plaque into the circulation. This continued efflux of CCs from a ruptured plaque can persist for several days or even weeks following rupture as has been demonstrated [9]. However, two trials indirectly explored this relationship: The French Study of Aortic Plaques in Stroke (FSOAPS), involving 331 elderly patients with prior brain infarction, documented a single CES case in a non-warfarin user [42], and the Stroke Prevention and Atrial Fibrillation trial (SPAF-III) compared adjusted-dose warfarin to combined low-dose warfarin/aspirin therapy. Both trials failed to establish a strong causation relationship between warfarin use and CES. Researchers concluded the CES risk with adjusted-dose warfarin in elderly atrial fibrillation patients with aortic plaque is low.

Current evidence can neither confirm nor disprove anticoagulation as a causal factor for CES. Coumadin (warfarin) prescribing information acknowledges a potential increased risk of cholesterol microembolization and advises discontinuation if CES occurs. One case report found that Edoxaban was implicated in cholesterol crystal embolization (CCE), otherwise no large-scale reports have linked DOAC use with CCE [43].

Thrombolytic therapy

CES as a result of thrombolytic therapy is an uncommon yet serious complication that could carry a significant risk of morbidity and mortality. Its clinical manifestations can vary widely, often leading to diagnostic delays with limited effective treatments.

The first documented case of thrombolytic therapy-induced cholesterol embolization syndrome was reported in 1984 [44]. Between 1980 and 2007, a literature review identified 30 cases associated with thrombolytic agents, primarily for acute myocardial infarction and deep vein thrombosis [45]. While these reports may suggest a causal relationship between cholesterol embolism and thrombolytic therapy, large clinical trials on thrombolytics such as ISIS-3 and GUSTO, did not demonstrate CES as a specific complication, thus limiting the understanding of its occurrence in these settings [46].

Diagnosis (Table 1)

As previously discussed, the definitive diagnosis of CES remains challenging due to the lack of reliable non-invasive modalities to confirm the presence of CCs. The crucial element in distinguishing a definitive diagnosis of CES from other conditions with similar presentations is a thorough history and physical examination, including recent procedures should be considered. Skin changes should be carefully examined, and new visual changes should prompt a fundoscopic ophthalmological exam [47]. Laboratory and imaging findings can support a diagnosis of CES, biopsy remains the gold standard as we would be able to clearly visualize evidence of CCs. Though CES may have multisystem involvement, biopsy of skin is considered safer than biopsy of other organ systems to evaluate for CCs. On histology they appear as biconvex and needle shaped spaces and if prepared under liquid nitrogen one can visualize double refraction under polarized light. Other sites such as the kidney can be biopsied, however, they have lower sensitivity due to patchier distribution resulting in lower yield [[49], [50], [51]].

Tissue biopsy is not always an option due to a multitude of factors, and so we must rely on alternative tools such as laboratory studies and various imaging modalities. CES often triggers an inflammatory response, which may be observed in some cases. Eosinophilia (>500 /ul) is more commonly noted post cardiac catheterization in patients with CES [31]. Elevated C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate may also be present in CES [31]. Alternative lab abnormalities may suggest organ-specific involvement in CES or the presence of significant pathology, such as elevated lactate levels indicative of bowel infarction [52]. Imaging is directed at the site of injury. For suspected stroke, a typical workup involves CT angiogram, MRI, ultrasound and echocardiography. For kidney injury, renal ultrasound and CT angiogram may be considered [28]. For aortic disordered CT, MRI is a useful first step and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) can be considered when there are contraindications to CT/ MRA. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) and Micro OCT and NOGA can be utilized to visualize CC build up as well [9,53]. Continuous clinical and scientific validation remains essential to establish OCT as a reliable diagnostic tool. Overall, diagnosis requires correlating clinical signs, laboratory findings, and patient history. The aforementioned workup also helps distinguish CES from other embolic etiologies such as endocarditis, thromboembolism or vasculitis.

Systemic involvement

Plaque embolization from distinct anatomic regions tends to preferentially damage specific end organs. Aortic arch plaques typically affect the brain, eyes, and upper extremities, while plaques in the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta embolize to the gastrointestinal tract and lower extremities. Commonly involved organs include the skin, kidneys, coronary arteries, central nervous system, and gastrointestinal tract, with their clinical manifestations outlined below:

CNS

CNS involvement is especially prevalent after carotid artery interventions, a major source of embolization to the cerebral and retinal vasculature. Less commonly, CNS involvement has also been reported after coronary interventions [48,54]. Imaging findings in cases of CES often reveals multiple small ischemic lesions as well as border zone infarcts in some instances [48,55]. Transcranial doppler ultrasonography (TCD) can detect thousands of microemboli after carotid endarterectomy or angioplasty [56]. A study of 41 patients evaluated with TCD and MRI demonstrated new brain lesions on MRI brain in 8 patients, although only 3 patients showed clinical symptoms consistent with stroke, suggesting the true burden of the syndrome is likely underreported [57]. Frequent events of emboli have been reported especially with transcatheter aortic-valve replacement (TAVR) with CCs released to the brain [58]. The clinical presentation of CES with CNS involvement is diverse, including transient ischemic attacks (TIAs), stroke, confusion, headache, dizziness, paraparesis, mononeuropathy, and spinal cord infarction [1]. Emboli in the cerebral circulation disrupts the blood-brain barrier, triggering an acute inflammatory response. Over time, CES often causes diffuse brain injury, characterized by memory loss, confusion, and cognitive decline, in contrast to the focal deficits typical of thromboembolic syndromes.

Eyes

Ophthalmologic findings in CES are due to retinal emboli, which may cause monocular vision loss or field defects, and secondary vasculitis, which can lead to diplopia or central neuro-ophthalmologic symptoms from lesions in the visual pathways [59]. Fundoscopic examination may be an effective tool for the evaluation of CES as in up to 10–25 % of cases, retinal emboli can be found [59]. A hallmark of CES is the presence of Hollenhorst plaques, seen on fundoscopy as bright, refractive retinal lesions commonly linked to carotid plaques. While these plaques may not always aid in diagnosing acute CES, they suggest prior embolic events and are considered poor predictors of future episodes [60].

Coronary arteries

Coronary involvement in CES is a rare but serious complication, with limited literature primarily consisting of case reports [61]. Currently there are no diagnostic tests of standard procedures to reliably distinguish CES from acute thrombus, likely explaining the low rates of CES identified in the coronary arteries. The dissolution of CCs by ethanol during tissue processing further complicates detection [26,62]. Distal embolization from ruptured plaques during coronary interventions is thought to cause distal perfusion defects [34]. Plaque rupture releases CCs, platelets, cell debris, fibrin, and red blood cells, with CCs and platelets as key drivers of the inflammatory response [63]. A recent case report highlighted the development of acute MI secondary to distal embolization of a proximally ruptured plaque in the RCA [43]. Additionally, a study on saphenous vein grafts in patients who underwent CABG found a tenfold increased risk of complications, including acute MI, death, or repeat CABG, associated with distal embolization [64].

Kidney

Atheroembolic renal disease (ARD) can develop spontaneously in 40–69 % of cases, or from invasive vascular procedures due to the close proximity of the renal arteries to the abdominal aorta [65,66]. In patients who develop ARD following angiographic or vascular surgical procedures, the onset of renal symptoms may vary significantly, occurring anywhere from days to weeks or even months after the inciting factor. One study showed a mean interval of 5.3 weeks [49]. ARD can lead to acute, subacute, and chronic renal failure. The acute form results from massive cholesterol emboli, while the subacute form is from cyclic cholesterol crystal occurrences [67]. Chronic forms are likely due to spontaneous low-grade release of cholesterol emboli over long periods [67]. Characteristic lab findings include elevated serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogens, and hematuria noted in 33–40 % of cases, non nephrotic range proteinuria in 50–60 % [65,68,69]. ARD is a clinical diagnosis that should be suspected in the setting of precipitating events, acute, or subacute renal failure and clinical sequelae of peripheral cholesterol embolization [70]. Renal biopsy is the most definitive diagnosis (Fig. 3). Renal outcomes in patients with atheroembolic renal disease may vary; some patients experience further deterioration or remain dialysis-dependent, while others may show improvement or continued chronic kidney impairment. Cutaneous

Cutaneous manifestations are reported in 35 % to 96 % of cholesterol embolization syndrome (CES) cases [63], reflecting microvascular ischemia that can result in significant tissue damage. Key findings include livedo reticularis (49 %), gangrene (35 %), and cyanosis (28 %) [71]. The lower extremities are commonly affected, often bilaterally, except in cases of gangrene [64]. Livedo reticularis presents as a net-like red-blue discoloration of the skin, representing a cyanotic response to small vessel intravascular obstruction [71]. This condition typically develops suddenly, spreads extensively, and is observed in 16 % to 96 % of cases [71]. Gangrene, characterized by necrosis of tissue secondary to impaired blood flow, occurs in 15 % to 27 % of cases. Its etiology may include embolic, vasculitic, or coagulopathic processes. Management typically involves antibiotic therapy followed by surgical intervention, such as amputation [71]. Cyanosis, often presenting as a blue or purple discoloration of the toes, is considered pathognomonic for CES diagnosis [63]. These manifestations collectively indicate vascular dysfunction. Given the broad differential diagnosis, which includes conditions such as endocarditis, vasculitis, and coagulopathies, a skin biopsy is recommended as part of the diagnostic workup, with a reported sensitivity of approximately 92 % [72] (Fig. 3).

Gastroenterology

GI manifestations are observed in approximately 18.6 % to 48 % of CES cases [72]. The presentation of CES ranges from chronic or indolent disease states to more acute multiorgan involvement [63]. Common clinical symptoms include abdominal pain, diarrhea, malabsorption, and GI bleeding [71]. Bleeding is often attributed to colonic ischemia leading to ulceration, pseudopolyps and potential intestinal perforation [2]. The lesions associated with CES are typically microscopic and demonstrate rapid healing, thereby rendering endoscopic visualization difficult [72]. Although rare, complications such as acalculous necrotizing cholecystitis and acute pancreatitis are clinically significant, particularly in elderly patients [63]. Consequently, CES should be considered in the differential diagnosis for elderly patients with underlying atherosclerotic disease who present with GI bleeding [71].

Musculoskeletal

Musculoskeletal manifestations of CES typically involve muscle tenderness and are often accompanied by cutaneous lesions [71]. Rare cases of isolated muscle involvement without skin manifestations have been reported. One such case involved a patient presenting with bilateral calf tenderness, elevated creatine kinase levels, albeit with an unremarkable inflammatory and autoimmune profile. Muscle biopsy demonstrated injury and necrosis, and an ulcerating plaque was observed in the distal abdominal aorta [72]. Additionally, a study utilizing PET/CTA identified muscle damage induced by cholesterol crystal emboli in a white rabbit model, even in the absence of ischemia and skin lesions [28]. Treatment

Although, it depends on the organ(s) involved and burden of emboli, the prognosis for patients with CES is generally poor, emphasizing the importance of early recognition and intervention. Treatment is mainly geared towards risk factor modification, supportive care for end organ damage and secondary prophylaxis. Smoking cessation along with optimal blood pressure and cholesterol control should be strongly advised. In patients with renal involvement in hospital mortality may reach up to 16 % [73]. A cornerstone of CES management is medical therapy with statins and antiplatelet agents, which help with plaque stabilization and reduce risk of CCE [74,75]. Statins offer several benefits, including lowering LDL-C, preventing plaque buildup, and enhanced plaque stability [75]. They also have demonstrated anti-inflammatory properties by decreasing CRP levels [76]. Antiplatelet agents, such as low-dose Aspirin 81 mg, help prevent adverse cardiovascular events like myocardial infarction. While there is no direct evidence supporting aspirin's benefit in CES, it may be considered due to the common comorbidities in patients with CES [76]. Studies have shown that statins (in vitro and in vivo) and aspirin (in vitro) dissolve CCs directly (Fig. 5) [75,76]. Due to the uncertain effects of thrombolytic and anticoagulant therapies, initiating such treatments in patients with CES is generally not recommended. Summary

Unlike thromboembolism, CES progresses gradually and involves smaller emboli. The increase in diagnosis of CES is due to greater clinical awareness and the rise in intravascular procedures. However, the true incidence of CES remains unknown due to the lack of a specific diagnostic test. Diagnosis relies primarily on clinical history, physical examination, laboratory findings (e.g., eosinophilia, inflammatory markers), and imaging but biopsy remains the gold standard. Imaging modalities such as CT, MRI, angioscopy and OCT help assess aortic plaque burden as an associated risk. Treatment focuses on lipid lowering, blood pressure control and lifestyle modification as well as supportive care. Anticoagulation and thrombolytic therapy is generally avoided unless indicated by other required conditions.
