A Modified Technique for Converting Retrograde Femoral Artery Access to Antegrade Access With the Assistance of a Long Sheath
Authors: Guangfeng Zheng, Ze Wang, Chuanyong Li, Qiang Zhang, Zhichang Pan, Shuming Shi, Jianjie Rong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPercutaneous femoral access serves as the cornerstone of endovascular therapy of lower extremity arterial disease. In complex cases, the arterial disease of lower extremity are involve in ipsilateral iliac artery pathology, which conventional treatment strategies typically require multiple punctures or staged procedures.AimsThis study aimed to explore the bilateral lower extremity endovascular intervention of patients with coexisting ipsilateral iliac artery stenosis using the long sheath‐assisted access conversion technique (LS‐ACT) via a single puncture.MethodsThis is a retrospective cohort study of 18 patients with arteriosclerosis obliterans of the lower extremity who were treated between April 2023 and July 2025. The cohort included 13 males and 5 females, with a mean age of 70.4 ± 7.2 years. After retrograde femoral access, contralateral interventions were performed using the LS‐ACT protocol.ResultsThe LS‐ACT was performed in all 18 patients, achieving successful transition from femoral retrograde to antegrade access. Seven of these patients underwent simultaneous bilateral lower extremity arterial interventions. In 11 patients, the LS‐ACT was utilized following failed antegrade puncture due to obesity, scarred puncture sites, or a high bifurcation of the profunda femoris artery. Notably, 8 patients presented with hemodynamically significant improvement of ipsilateral iliac artery stenosis, including 2 who had undergone prior stenting. No LS‐ACT relevant complications occurred.ConclusionThe LS‐ACT demonstrates substantial clinical utility in real‐world practice. It is particularly valuable for patients with ipsilateral iliac artery stenosis or those requiring single‐session bilateral lower extremity interventions, and represents a reliable alternative in cases of failed antegrade puncture or when managing bilateral disease.Keywords: antegrade, conversion technique, femoral access, long sheath‐assisted access conversion technique, retrograde

Introduction

The incidence of peripheral artery disease (PAD) ranks among the top three atherosclerotic conditions worldwide, following coronary artery disease (CAD) and cerebral infarction (CI) [1, 2]. PAD clinical manifestations include intermittent claudication, skin ulcers, bluish skin, cold skin, muscle atrophy, decreased or absent pulse, abnormal nail and hair growth in the affected limb [3, 4]. Lifestyle changes like no smoking, medicine, revascularization, and therapeutic underlying diseases are major methods for PAD treatments [5, 6, 7]. While medical therapy remains fundamental, endovascular treatment has demonstrated great efficacies in improving functional status in patients with intermittent claudication and reducing amputation risks in those with chronic limb‐threatening ischemia (CLTI) [8, 9]. In endovascular interventions, selecting optimal access is critical for technical success [10], which retrograde access (RA) and antegrade access (AA) of the common femoral artery (CFA) are the two primary approaches. RA allows contralateral intervention via the “crossover” technique because it utilizes longer device trajectories and directional changes, but increasing procedural complexity. In contrast, AA provides a more anatomically direct path to ipsilateral disease, enhancing device control and pushability. However, AA has carried a steeper learning curve [11] and met technical challenges in those patients with obese, scarred groins, or cases of high profunda femoris bifurcation (PFA) [12, 13]. For patients with bilateral femoropopliteal disease requiring single‐session intervention, bilateral CFA punctures significantly increase trauma, complication risks, and postoperative recovery burden. These limitations highlight the need for innovative access strategies.

Recent report showed that the retrograde femoral access flipped antegrade (RAFA) technique has great clinical benefits over either RA or AA alone [14], which conventional methods require catheter looping through the ipsilateral iliac system. However, this technique has driven the procedure challenging or contraindicated in cases of iliac stenosis, occlusion, or prior stenting [14, 15, 16]. To overcome these constraints, our center has developed and refined a modified long sheath‐assisted access conversion technique (LS‐ACT) to minimize vascular trauma during access conversion. The LS‐ACT offers distinct procedural and clinical advantages [1]: it enables safe application in patients with ipsilateral iliac artery, a population traditionally excluded from conventional RAFA [2]; it supports single‐session bilateral interventions through on access site, streamlining procedural workflow and minimizing patient trauma; and [3] it provides a reliable salvage option when antegrade femoral access fails or is anatomically unfavorable. By enhancing both the safety profile and versatility of femoral access conversion, LS‐ACT represents a meaningful advance in the endovascular management of complex peripheral arterial disease.

Methods

Study Population

A retrospective analysis was conducted on 18 treated patients with lower‐extremity atherosclerotic occlusive disease in the Department of Vascular Surgery, Suzhou Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, between November 2023 and July 2025. The cohort study included 13 males and 5 females with a mean age of 70.4 ± 7.2 years following inclusion criteria [1]: all patients were diagnosed as PAD (Rutherford category 3,4,5) following the American Heart Association (AHA) recommended guidelines [2, 17]; patients had bilateral femoropopliteal lesions requiring endovascular intervention and suitable for single‐session treatment [3]; presented with coexisting ipsilateral iliac artery stenosis amenable to concurrent intervention [4]; exhibited chanllengling or failed antegrade CFA puncture duo to obesity, groin scarring, or high biburcation of the profunda femoris artery. Exclusion criteria included [1]: acute limb ischemia [2]; significant stenosis or vulnerable plaque in the accessed CFA [3]; Contraindications to antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy [4]; Incomplete preoperative imaging data.

Among these patients, there were the following underlying diseases: hypertension presented in 15 patients (15/18, 83.3%), hyperlipidemia in 14 (14/18, 77.8%), and diabetes mellitus in 11 (11/18, 61.1%). Six patients (33.3%) were active smokers. Seven patients (7/18, 38.9%) had bilateral lower extremity arterial disease and underwent simultaneous bilateral intervention. Eleven patients (11/18, 61.1%) required conversion from retrograde to AA due to failed antegrade puncture secondary to obesity, groin scarring, or high PFA. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Sushou Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital (approval number: 2026‐049) in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent prior to their inclusion in the study.

Procedural Technique and Patient Evaluation

Preoperative diagnosis was confirmed using bilateral lower extremity computed tomography angiography (CTA) via a GE Revolution 256‐slice CT scanner, with reconstructed images processed on a GE ADW4.7 workstation.

Under ultrasound guidance, the CFA was first identified and the PFA was marked on the skin. Then, retrograde puncture was performed 2–3 cm above the bifurcation using a modified Seldinger technique [18] with a needle angle ≥ 60° relative to the skin. In obese patients, a near‐vertical approach was employed with subcutaneous tissue dilation using vascular forceps. For unilateral disease, a steeper angle was preferred to minimize subcutaneous tract length. (All procedures were performed by attending vascular surgeons).

LS‐ACT Surgical Procedures

Following sheath placement, patients with significant ipsilateral iliac artery stenosis underwent initial revascularization using appropriate guidewires and catheters to traverse the stenotic segment, followed by balloon angioplasty and/or stent implantation. Selective catheterization of the contralateral iliac artery was achieved using diagnostic catheters (e.g., pigtail, Cobra), through which a long sheath (TERUMO, Model 54‐64501, 6F, 45 cm) was advanced put. For patients requiring concurrent bilateral intervention, contralateral lower extremity procedures were first completed via the crossover approach.

The initial steps of the LS‐ACT were following: (A) Positioning the catheter tip in the contralateral CFA with the guidewire and sheath tip below the aortic bifurcation; (B) Simultaneous clockwise catheter rotation and proximal advancement to establish a stable loop configuration within the aortic lumen, followed by selective guidewire cannulation of the ipsilateral superficial femoral artery (SFA); (C) Controlled catheter withdrawal under fluoroscopic guidance while ensuring continuous SFA wire positioning; (D, E) Sequential retraction of the sheath to the CFA access site followed by gradual wire withdrawal until complete loop resolution; (F) Final antegrade sheath advancement over the non‐looped wire segment to establish definitive working access (Figure 1).

Clinical Applications in Patients With Complex Iliac Anatomy

The LC‐ACT clinical utility was demonstrated through two representative cases with challenging iliac anatomy. In case 10, despite significant tortuosity and moderate stenosis (exceeding 50% diameter reduction) of the ipsilateral iliac artery, conversion was successfully achieved through optimized wire navigation. In case 15, the technique proved equally effective in a patient with prior bilateral external iliac stenting, confirming its applicability in post‐stenting scenarios. These cases collectively validate the technique's value in managing patients with coexisting iliac artery pathology (Figures 2 and 3).

The remaining 11 patients (11/18, 61.1%) required conversion after failed antegrade puncture due to obesity (n = 4), scarred groin (n = 4), or high bifurcation of the profunda femoris artery (n = 3). The ipsilateral iliac artery stenosis exceeding 50% diameter reduction was presented in eight patients, including two patients with prior iliac artery stenting (Table 2).

All patients have undergone balloon angioplasty (18/18, 100%), including 16 cases that used drug‐coated balloons. Adjunctive atherectomy was performed in five patients, and arterial stenting was implemented in two cases (Table 2). Postoperative assessment demonstrated significant hemodynamic improvement in all treated limbs.

Hemostasis was achieved using a vascular closure device (Cordis Exoseal) followed by manual compression for over 15 min and 24‐h elastic compression bandaging. Dual antithrombotic therapy consisting of aspirin (100 mg daily) and rivaroxaban (10 mg daily) was administered postoperatively.

Hemodynamic improvement was objectively evaluated through Rutherford category assessment. Patients presented with Rutherford category 3 (n = 4), 4 (n = 8), or 5 (n = 6) preoperatively. All 18 patients demonstrated an improvement of at least two Rutherford categories postoperatively. Specifically, all 8 patients with preoperative ischemic rest pain (category 4) achieved complete symptom resolution, and all 6 patients with minor tissue loss (category 5) showed complete or substantial ulcer healing (Table 1).

Perioperative duplex ultrasound evaluation of the accessed femoral and iliac arteries revealed no technique‐specific complications such as plaque embolism or arterial injury. Access site complications included two cases of local hematoma and one pseudoaneurysm, all of which resolved with conservative compression therapy (Table 2).

Discussion

This study analyzed the LS‐ACT that builds upon existing methods by enabling application in patients with ipsilateral iliac artery stenosis or prior stenting. This technique facilitates single‐session endovascular treatment for bilateral iliac artery and lower extremity arterial diseases. For patients with challenging or failed CFA antegrade puncture, it offers an alternative approach by converting RA into AA, demonstrating significant clinical advantages.

Since Kadir et al. first described the conversion from retrograde to antegrade femoral access using a looped Cobra catheter in 1981 [15], this fundamental principle has been extensively applied for managing ipsilateral lower extremity lesions. However, this classic technique has its limitations: it relies on catheter looping within the aortoiliac system and requires passage of the looped catheter through the ipsilateral iliac artery [15]. Subsequent refinements using pre‐shaped catheters like Simmons catheter [19] and SOS catheter [20] simplified the procedure, and the smaller loop configuration reduced vascular wall injury risks. Nevertheless, the looped catheter still must traverse the iliac artery, carrying risks of plaque dislodgement and catheter kinking. Ysa A. et al. introduced a pullback technique [13], which innovated the looping mechanism by offering simplicity and cost‐effectiveness. However, the 180‐degree catheter rotation within the artery poses risks of plaque disruption or arterial dissection, and it has not been applied clinically in patients with ipsilateral iliac stenosis or prior stenting.

In conventional conversion techniques, severe aortoiliac atherosclerosis, stenosis, or prior stenting are considered relative contraindications [14, 20]. These patients represent precisely the population requiring intervention, as they are often unsuitable for standard crossover approaches due to contralateral access difficulties or an acute aortic bifurcation angle. Even crossover is feasible, technical challenges—particularly in revascularizing occluded segments‐ exist in long‐segment femoropopliteal disease. Some patients also have concurrent infrapopliteal lesions that cannot be addressed in the same session due to device length limitations. In case 15 (Figure 3) of this study, the patient presented with bilateral lower limb ischemic symptoms, more severe on the right, bilateral significant external iliac artery stenosis, left‐sided long‐segment SFA occlusion, localized severe right SFA stenosis, and three‐vessel infrapopliteal disease on the right. A left‐sided crossover approach could have treated the bilateral iliac artery stenosis and right SFA lesions but would have failed to address the right infrapopliteal disease, necessitating a staged procedure—or even repeated interventions—for the left SFA occlusion. Using the LS‐ACT, we managed all these lesions in one session, promptly improving perfusion to both limbs while reducing hospital stays and costs. In case 10 (Figure 2), the accessed iliac artery was tortuous with moderate stenosis, while contralateral CFA and SFA stenosis precluded crossover access. Using our LS‐ACT technique, we firstly performed balloon angioplasty for the left SFA, then atherectomy plus angioplasty for the right SFA, achieving bilateral hemodynamic improvement. Both case 15 (prior stenting) and case 10 (tortuous stenotic iliac) would have been unsuitable for conventional conversion techniques, yet the LS‐ACT successfully overcame these limitations, demonstrating unique clinical benefits.

The LS‐ACT represents an evolution of Kadir's method [15]. After the looped catheter directs the guidewire into the ipsilateral SFA, the catheter is withdrawn using the support of the long sheath tip within a healthy segment of the iliac artery or aorta. This avoids traversing stenotic or stented iliac segments, minimizing mechanical irritation to the vessel wall and reducing risks of plaque embolization, dissection, catheter kinking, or stent displacement. Moreover, this technique primarily employs a hydrophilic guidewire to form a loop and cross the ipsilateral iliac segment. The hydrophilic wire's lubricity, flexible tip, and track ability help mitigate arterial injury—advantages acknowledged in prior studies [13, 20] and fully leveraged in our approach.

Another strength of the LS‐ACT lies in its utility for simultaneous bilateral lower extremity intervention. While ultrasound and CTA are valuable for evaluating lower extremity arterial disease, digital subtraction angiography (DSA) remains the gold standard, particularly for infrapopliteal arteries. Selective DSA is the optimal method for assessing small‐caliber runoff vessels [21]. In such scenarios, single‐puncture bilateral angiography via retrograde‐to‐antegrade conversion reduces trauma. Although upper extremity access can be used for iliofemoral interventions and theoretically allows bilateral treatment, it carries higher complication rates and requires longer devices, serving only as an alternative in select cases [16]. CFA access remains the mainstream approach for lower‐extremity endovascular therapy. For patients with bilateral disease, the retrograde‐to‐antegrade technique is a valuable option: through a single, safe ipsilateral retrograde puncture, operators can first address iliac or contralateral femoral lesions in a standard retrograde fashion, then establish a stable antegrade working channel for treating ipsilateral femoropopliteal and infrapopliteal disease. This “one‐stop” strategy avoids a second arterial puncture for contralateral disease, reducing puncture‐related complications, potentially improving procedural efficiency, and decreasing the number of interventions—particularly beneficial for patients with advanced PAD requiring multilevel treatment.

Antegrade CFA access remains the preferred approach for infrapopliteal interventions due to its short working distance and enhanced catheter control, minimizing repeated adjustments and complex manipulations. However, antegrade puncture carries risks of improper puncture site selection; excessively high puncture or suboptimal needle angles increase retroperitoneal hemorrhage risk, especially in obese patients [12]. Limited space between the CFA entry point and profunda femoris origin, combined with puncture angle issues, often leads to unintended wire entry into the profunda artery [22], requiring time‐consuming redirection and risking access loss. In our study, patients with failed antegrade puncture due to obesity, groin scarring, or high profunda bifurcation were successfully converted using the LS‐ACT, aligning with findings by Nice et al. [23]. This demonstrates the technique's ability to combine the mechanical advantages of RA with the operational convenience of AA, accommodating individualized treatment needs. A recent retrospective cohort study reported high technical success rates for the RAFA approach in lower extremity interventions without significantly increasing access site complications such as hematoma or pseudoaneurysm, demonstrating safety comparable to conventional AA and RA [14]. Similarly, our series observed no technique‐related complications such as distal embolization or arterial injury, and access site complications (e.g., hematoma, pseudoaneurysm) were rare. One pseudoaneurysm case was associated with premature patient mobilization post‐procedure. This low complication profile corroborates earlier findings and underscores the technique's safety. However, our sample size only is 18 cases to limit this study's generalizability. Further reduction in access site complications will depend on enhanced clinical monitoring and continued refinement of vascular closure devices.

The LS‐ACT revealed that the catheter was early withdrawn over the sheath tip after selecting the ipsilateral iliac artery, followed by passage of a looped hydrophilic wire through the iliac segment—enables safe application in patients with severe pelvic atherosclerosis, iliac stenosis, or prior stenting. This approach overcomes traditional contraindications and fully leverages the core advantages of retrograde‐to‐antegrade conversion by combining the technical simplicity of retrograde puncture with the mechanical superiority of AA. Crucially, it provides a safe and efficient platform for managing complex bilateral lower‐extremity diseases through a single puncture site, facilitating personalized treatment.

Limitations

This study has some limitations: First, it represents a single‐center retrospective analysis with a limited cohort size, which inherently restricts the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Second, as with any retrospective design, there exists potential for selection bias, as the choice to apply LS‐ACT was at the discretion of the operating surgeon based on individual anatomy and clinical judgment. Third, the technical success observed is closely linked to appropriate patient selection, particularly the absence of CFA occlusion or prohibitive iliac disease, which may not be representative of broader, unselected populations. Finally, while the procedural and short‐term outcomes are promising, the absence of a control group and the lack of long‐term follow‐up data preclude definitive conclusions regarding comparative efficacy and durability. Therefore, the clinical value and broader applicability of the LS‐ACT technique require further validation through prospective, multicenter, and ideally randomized controlled studies with standardized protocols and longer‐term endpoints.

Conclusion

The LS‐ACT demonstrates significant clinical utility in the management of complex lower‐extremity arterial disease. By enabling safe application in patients with ipsilateral iliac artery stenosis or prior stenting, this approach effectively overcomes traditional contraindications associated with conventional conversion methods. The technique's ability to facilitate single‐session bilateral interventions through a single access site provides a valuable treatment strategy for patients with multilevel disease. While the initial experience appears promising, further validation through prospective multicenter studies is warranted to establish its broader applicability and long‐term outcomes.
