ABSTRACT
BackgroundMeasures implemented to control the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic influenced physical activity, including participation in sports. However, how long this impact persisted after lifting restrictions has not been well documented. Our purpose was to compare prevalence of outdoor sports in Spain right after the strict lockdown at the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic, which did not allow people to leave their homes to exercise, and during the new normal.MethodsNationwide population-based study in Spain (ENE-COVID). Participants were selected through two-stage stratified random sampling. Epidemiological information was gathered in the early post-lockdown (May–June 2020, n = 64,258) and in the new normal (November 2020, n = 50,159). We estimated crude and logistic model-based standardized prevalences of outdoor sports, and differences by personal and contextual characteristics.ResultsPrevalence of outdoor sports in the early post-lockdown was 36.7% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 35.8, 37.6), increasing to 53.4% (95% CI: 52.4, 54.4) in the new normal. Children, older people, and those living in provinces with higher SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence showed a more delayed recovery of activity. Among people not practicing outdoor sports in the early post-lockdown, 41% (95% CI: 39.9, 42.2) practiced them in the new normal, while discontinuation percentage was 25.3% (95% CI: 24.3, 26.4).ConclusionsParticipation in outdoor sports, abruptly interrupted during the strict home-confinement, was not resumed immediately but remained severely reduced early after the lockdown. Seven months later, baseline levels had been recovered in general, although differences were observed depending on sociodemographic and health-status factors. This highlights the importance of considering personal and contextual characteristics to plan physical activity and sport promotion strategies in contexts requiring population movement restrictions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-26072-y.

Background

Sport can be defined, in a broad sense, as any form of physical activity (PA) performed with the aim of improving or maintaining fitness and mental well-being, socializing or obtaining results in competitions [1, 2]. It is therefore a subcategory within the more general concept of PA [3]. Regular PA, including participation in sports, has been associated with multiple health benefits, supporting the importance of being physically active and meeting PA recommendations to obtain physical and mental health benefits and to reduce the risk of diseases and premature mortality [4–6]. In middle and high income countries, where sedentary jobs predominate, sport is one of the main contributors to fulfill these recommendations [7, 8]. Outdoor sports, those practiced in the open air, including but not restricted to those done in contact with nature or the natural landscape, may have additional health benefits related to exposure to green and blue spaces or the natural environment [9].

In spite of these evidences, according to the World Health Organization (WHO), in 2022 nearly a third of the world’s adult population (1.8 billion people) were insufficiently physically active [10]. In addition, if the estimated 2010 to 2022 trend continues, the global target of the WHO of reducing physical inactivity by a 15% in 2030 [11] will not be achieved [12]. This objective may be further away from being reached due to the COVID-19 pandemic [13], which in the first quarter of 2020 led most governments around the world to impose different actions to reduce transmission of SARS-CoV-2 virus, often including movement restrictions and stay-at-home orders. These conditions indirectly gave rise to a natural experiment, in which the population was exposed to different scenarios in terms of opportunities and restrictions for PA, especially outdoors. The first scenario would correspond to the period when the strict lockdowns and stay-at-home orders were in force. During this period, in many countries PA could only be done at home, disrupting any outdoor sports, which in general resulted in a reduction in total PA levels [14]. The second scenario would be the early post-lockdown phase, when people were allowed to leave their homes to engage in PA, but some restrictions on freedom of movement were still in force. This period was characterized by a great heterogeneity in terms of duration and type and intensity of restrictions across countries, and even within individual countries [15]. Finally, the third scenario would be the return to the new normal, when social distancing and restrictions to practice sports were further relaxed, returning to conditions resembling the pre-pandemic period.

Spain, one of the European countries with the highest number of cases and deaths during the first wave of the pandemic, was a clear example of these scenarios. In March 2020, the Spanish Government ordered a strict home confinement, which included the ban on leaving home to engage in any PA. This strict lockdown lasted almost two months. Afterwards, during the early post-lockdown, which began on May 1, restrictions were progressively softened. Initially, outdoor sports were allowed, but only for one hour per day, and had to be done alone or with one household member; then, gyms and sports centers reopened, although still with certain restrictions, such as mandatory mask use and training in small groups [16]. At the end of June these restrictions were lifted, moving on to the new normal period.

Several studies have tried to measure the impact of these measures in PA. Most of them found reduced PA levels [15, 17–20], even after restrictions had been lifted [17, 21]. However, there is still a scarcity of representative population-based research on the evolution and determinants of PA from the immediate post-lockdown to the following months, and more specifically on outdoor sports. The outdoors is the most common environment in which sports and other types of PA are practiced in most European countries [22, 23]. Therefore, it is important to identify the population that may be at higher risk of reducing outdoor sports and not returning to pre-pandemic levels after a period of prohibition. This knowledge would be useful for designing more targeted prevention strategies or intervention programs aimed to promote sports and PA. It would also serve to inform public health strategies in the event of future pandemics.

Early during the first wave of the pandemic, a population-based nationwide seroprevalence study, the Spanish National Seroepidemiological Survey of SARS-CoV-2 Infection (ENE-COVID), was launched in Spain [24]. This study collected sociodemographic, behavioral and epidemiological information in four different time points, providing a good opportunity to look into different behaviors, including sports, and their temporal evolution.

In this context, the present study aims to estimate the population prevalence of outdoor sports during two periods after the strict lockdown in Spain (the early post-lockdown and the first five months of the new normal), and to identify sociodemographic, COVID-19- and health-related, and geographic determinants of these prevalences and of change in the frequency of participation in outdoor sports between both periods.

Methods

The ENE-COVID was a nationwide population-based cohort study to quantify the seropositivity for SARS-CoV-2 in the non-institutionalized population of Spain. The study design has been previously described [24]. Briefly, 1,500 census tracts and up to 24 households per tract were selected through a two-stage random sampling strategy stratified by province and municipality size. Residents from the 35,885 selected households were invited to participate. Epidemiological questionnaires and serology tests results were collected in four rounds during the two first COVID-19 pandemic waves in Spain, from April 27 to November 29, 2020, under a common protocol.

To study prevalence of outdoor sports, we used data from 68,403 participants 5 years or older that took part in any of the two post-lockdown periods: a) The early post-lockdown, that included the second and third rounds of the ENE-COVID (May to June 2020), with 64,258 participants, and b) the first five months of the new normal, whose information was collected in the fourth round (November 2020), with 50,159 participants.

Epidemiological information

The epidemiological questionnaire included sociodemographic information (e.g., age [5–9, 10–14, 15–24, 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, 55–64, 65–74, 75–84, ≥ 85], sex, nationality [grouped into Spanish or Other, due to the low proportion of individual countries other than Spain], level of education [less than primary, primary, secondary, university], employment status [active working, household chores, unemployed, student, retired, or others]) and COVID-19- and health- related information (e.g. COVID-19 symptoms [fever, chills, severe tiredness, sore throat, cough, shortness of breath, headache, anosmia or ageusia, or nausea, vomiting or diarrhea], chronic diseases [diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, cancer in the past five years, chronic pulmonary, kidney or liver disease, asthma, sleep apnea, immunosuppressive therapy, or connective tissue disease], disability [no recognized disability, or recognized disability > 33%], height [in centimeters] and weight [in kilograms], tobacco use [never smoker, former smoker, or current smoker]). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters and grouped into normal weight (BMI < 25), overweight (25 ≤ BMI < 30) and obesity (BMI ≥ 30). Based on information from questionnaires and antibody test results, we classified participants according to their personal history of COVID-19 (no infection, not severe infection, or pneumonia/hospitalization), and to whether they lived with vulnerable people (i.e., people with some comorbidity or aged 75 years or older).

Additionally, we took into account some contextual variables, such as the relative income of the census tract of residence (relative to the corresponding province), municipality size, and province SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence in the first pandemic wave.

Outdoor sports

The epidemiological questionnaire included several items to collect information about the frequency of leaving home for different reasons, including sports, as a measure of the possible exposure to SARS-CoV-2 infection. The single-item question related to sports read as follows: “During this time, how often have you gone out for sports?”, and three response options were offered: 1) did not go, 2) some days (1–3 times per week), or 3) almost every day (4–7 times per week). The latter two responses were grouped into one category (at least once per week) for analyses.

Statistical analysis

Sampling weights were assigned to participants to account for the different selection probabilities by province and for the response rate differences by sex, age group and census tract relative income [24], and these weights were used in all the statistical analyses.

We estimated crude and standardized prevalence and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of outdoor sports in the early post-lockdown and the new normal periods. Taking advantage of the longitudinal design of the ENE-COVID, we also estimated crude and standardized prevalences of change, both from not practicing outdoor sports in the early post-lockdown to starting to practice them in the new normal, and vice versa. The same approach was used to characterize participants who did not practice outdoor sports in either of the two periods. In addition, standardized prevalence differences were estimated between periods as well as across categories of sociodemographic, COVID-19 and health-related variables by using logistic model-based standardization methods [25]. Prevalences and prevalence differences were standardized to the overall distribution of sex, age, education level, census tract relative income, number of chronic diseases, BMI and province SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence.

Lastly, to explore province-based geographical patterns, age and sex standardized prevalences were estimated by province, overall and stratified by wide age groups (5–17; 18–34; 35–64; and over 64 years).

All analyses were performed using Stata, version 17 (Stata Corp) and its survey module to adjust for the design effects of the survey. R Statistical Software (v4.4.2; R Core Team 2021) and packages sf (v1.0–21; Pebesma E et al. 2025 [26]), data.table (v1.16.2; Barrett T et al. 2024 [27]) and ggplot2 (v3.5.2; Hadley W et al. 2025 [28]) were used to create Fig. 3.

Results

Table 1 shows the prevalence of participants who practiced outdoor sports during the early post-lockdown and the new normal, as well as standardized proportion differences by personal and contextual characteristics. In the early post-lockdown, 36.7% (95% CI: 35.8, 37.6) of the population practiced outdoor sports in Spain. Standardized prevalence was higher in men (43.0% [95% CI: 42.0, 44.0]) and in those 15–24 years old (50.3% [95% CI: 48.5, 52.1]). Likewise, prevalence increased with education, reaching 44.2% (95% CI: 42.8, 45.7) in university graduates. Persons residing in census tracts with higher relative income also had higher prevalence (40.3% [95% CI: 38.6, 42.0]). In contrast, prevalence was lower in children 5–9 years old and adults over 65 years, especially in the group of adults older than 85 (8.6% [95% CI: 6.3, 11.0]). Standardized prevalence was also lower in persons with other than Spanish nationality, residing in provinces with higher SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence, living with vulnerable persons, having more than two chronic diseases, disability, or obesity, and in current smokers. In the new normal, the prevalence of outdoor sports increased up to 53.4% (95% CI: 52.4, 54.4), representing an overall increment of 16.8% (95% CI: 15.5, 18.2). Differences by personal and contextual characteristics were similar to those observed in the early post-lockdown, except for some age groups and province seroprevalence. Particularly, outdoor sports were no longer over the mean in the group 15–24 years old (standardized proportion difference: 1.9% [95% CI: −0.1, 3.9]). On the other hand, the 65–74 years age group showed a higher prevalence only in the new normal (8.2% [95% CI: 6.4, 9.9]), and being 5–9 years old or living in provinces with high seroprevalence were no longer related to a lower prevalence of outdoor sports (2.3% [95% CI: −3.6, 8.2] and 2.6% [95% CI: 0.1, 5.1], respectively) (Table 1). This implied that the increase from the early post-lockdown to the new normal was not homogeneous across personal and contextual characteristics, resulting in lower differences by age and province SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence in the new normal (See Supplementary Table 1 in Additional File 1, and Additional File 2). Among older people, despite their larger increase in outdoor sports, prevalence still remained under the population average, particularly in those 85 years or older (29.5% [95% CI: 25.1, 33.9]).

The higher prevalence of outdoor sports in men was observed in every age group and in both periods (Fig. 1). The pattern by age was similar in women and men in the early post-lockdown, with the highest prevalence at 15–24 year age group, which decreased from this age on. However, in the new normal the age pattern changed and some differences emerged between sexes. The peak in outdoor sports prevalence observed at 15–24 years old in the early post-lockdown became smoother, and even disappeared in the new normal period for women. The decline in outdoor sports with increasing age was also smoother in the new normal than in the early post-lockdown, and even reversed at 65–74 year age group. Indeed, this group became the highest in prevalence in both, men and women. Still, the pattern in the new normal differed between sexes, with the decline during the middle age being steeper and starting at a younger age (already noticeable at 15–24 years) among women. With respect to changes over time (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Tables 2–3 in Additional File 1), our results showed that 25.3% (95% CI: 24.3, 26.4) of people who practiced outdoor sports in the early post-lockdown stopped doing it in the new normal. Discussion

Our findings revealed that following the easing of restrictions after the strict home-confinement, the population in Spain gradually resumed outdoor sports. Only 36.7% of the population reported this practice in the first weeks post-lockdown, percentage that increased to 53.4% after five months in the new normal period. Women, people with lower education level, children and old people were less likely to practice outdoor sports in the early post-lockdown. Differences were also observed by health-related characteristics and by contextual and geographical factors, with lower prevalences in people with chronic diseases, disability, obesity, tobacco use, and those living with vulnerable persons, in more deprived areas, bigger cities or provinces with higher SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence. These differences remained fairly stable over the two periods studied, though the increase in outdoor sports was especially remarkable in children 5–9 years old, adults aged 65–84 years and people living in provinces with higher SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence. However, 25% of the population that practiced outdoor sports in the first period did not maintain this habit. This trajectory was more frequent in people over 85 years old, with low education level, with obesity and in smokers. Interprovincial variation in the prevalence of outdoor sports was noticeable in all age groups and both periods.

In the early post-lockdown, despite the softening of restrictions, prevalence of outdoor sports was still low, similar to that reported in Lebanon during its first lockdown when outdoor exercising was allowed [29], but lower than in other countries such as Canada [30]. This low prevalence was probably due to the uncertainty about the spread mechanisms of the virus and the fear of becoming infected after the severe consequences of the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic in Spain. Young people had probably a lower risk perception, which may explain the earlier recovery of outdoor sports, as suggested by our results. People 15–24 years old showed the highest prevalence in this early post-lockdown period, being this prevalence almost as high as in the new normal. An early recovery of PA among young people has also been reported in other studies [31]. During this early post-lockdown period, some control measures were still being applied in Spain, such as social distancing, mobility restrictions, scheduled hours for leaving home, closure of playgrounds, and specific regulations in sports facilities [16], which shaped a scenario with fewer opportunities for participating in outdoor sports. This was evident in the case of children, with very low prevalence in this period, coinciding with the closure of playgrounds, recreational areas, and schools, which are the main settings where children usually play sports or engage in any PA [32]. Our estimates in children and adolescents are much lower than those reported by other authors, such as Liu et al., that found that around 76% of school-age children engaged in outdoor exercise at least once a week during the first wave of the pandemic [33]. Differences in the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic and the severity of the control measures between countries, greatly limit direct comparability of the results. Older adults, people with comorbidities, disability, or obesity, and smokers, also showed reduced prevalence of outdoor sports during this period. All these factors were thought to increase the risk of critical and fatal COVID-19, at the beginning of the pandemic [34], which may have contributed to a more cautious behavior in these population groups. Overall, observations during this period highlight the need for global planning to ensure that control and containment measures do not disproportionately hinder sports and PA, especially in vulnerable groups. Future crisis management strategies should incorporate protocols for maintaining access to safe outdoor spaces and promoting PA in situations similar to the early post-lockdown.

In the new normal, when control measures were further relaxed, the prevalence of outdoor sports increased in the whole population, including in some of the abovementioned groups that had restricted their participation in outdoor sports the most. Especially noteworthy was the case of people 65–84 years old, who showed the highest participation increase. However, as this group reported very low outdoor sports during the early post-lockdown, even if this reduction was transient, it could have had negative effects in their mobility, and caused physical deconditioning or adverse health effects [18–20], especially in people with frailty. The prevalence of outdoor sports in the new normal observed in our study was higher than in the pre-pandemic era, when 46.2% of the population over 14 years old in Spain was estimated to practice sport at least once a week in 2015 (35.1% excluding those practicing only indoor) [35]. This would suggest that returning to baseline outdoor sports habits did not take a very long time after the lockdown, which is consistent with findings in other European regions [36] as well as with the Survey of Sporting Habits in Spain carried out from September 2020 to January 2021, that also found a notable increase in the percentage of the population engaged in sports compared to 2015 (54.8% vs 46.2%) [37]. However, a slight decline was observed in 2022, suggesting that the increase could have been self-limited in time [23].

Regarding outdoor sports trajectories, our results on the proportion of the population that discontinued outdoor sports is consistent with a research in England that found that 29% of adults reduced PA over time [38]. As has been discussed by other authors, this would be compatible with the perception that, just after the strict home confinement, more people practiced various forms of PA in parks and streets in Spain than usual [39], which could correspond to previously sedentary people that started practicing outdoor sports as a reaction to the confinement, but who subsequently discontinued this practice. On the other hand, the study in England found that 9% of the population increased time spent in PA [38], which is much lower than our estimation of a 41% of the population starting to practice outdoor sports. The shorter follow-up in the English study and differences in population characteristics may partially explain this discrepancy. These authors also identified a range of factors associated to PA trajectories, such as age, sex, education, income, employment status, and health [38], which in general match those found in our analysis. Among the different possible trajectories, it is interesting to give attention to the group of “early quitters”, i.e., those that reported outdoor sports in the early post-lockdown and not during the new normal. This change was more frequent in women, older adults, people with low education, living in more deprived areas, having obesity, or being smokers. On the contrary, former smokers less frequently discontinued outdoor sports, which may reflect a higher commitment to their health. These factors, together with having chronic diseases or disability, also characterized the population that did not practice outdoor sports in either of the two periods, and most of them have been consistently related to lower adherence to PA in the literature [40–44]. Thus, these population groups would deserve more attention in order to change behaviors and increase their PA level. Likewise, future studies are necessary to monitor the post-pandemic long-term PA trajectory in these groups.

It is noteworthy that in provinces with higher SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence during the first pandemic wave, prevalence of outdoor sports in the early post-lockdown was lower, which could indicate a higher risk perception of becoming infected. Therefore, a worse epidemiological context should be considered as a barrier to outdoor sports and by extension, to total PA. This may have been especially significant for older adults, in spite of the implementation of some specific measures intended to protect them (e.g., reserving certain times of the day when only the elderly were allowed to go out) [16]. However, the geographical pattern of SARS-CoV-2 infection was not entirely coincident with that of outdoor sports (Additional File 4). This could be explained by the influence of other sport-related factors with a different province distribution. Also, the pace of the de-escalation of control measures differed between provinces, depending not only on their infection prevalence, but also on other factors, including those related to health-system capacity and socioeconomic and policy context [16]. Given the potential benefits of PA on COVID-19 [45–48] and in other health conditions, one of the lessons learned from this pandemic could be the importance of an appropriate management of sports and PA related policies. For instance, when considering prohibiting access to parks or natural environments, the risk of transmission in these areas [49] should be weighed against the benefits of accessing them in terms of PA, mental health and other health outcomes.

A key strength of this study was the large and nationally representative population sample, that renders the results and conclusions representative of outdoor sports in Spain and in each province, which could serve as a reference for other countries with similar characteristics. The timing of the study and the prospective follow-up enabled to compare two critical and different periods in the first months after the lockdown and evaluate how the different epidemiological situations and control measures could have impacted outdoor sports. Likewise, the availability of sociodemographic, clinical and contextual information, and the estimation of standardized prevalences, helped to understand the factors associated with different outdoor sports trajectories. On the other hand, our study was not without limitations. It was performed in non-institutionalized people, and therefore care-home residents, and residents in other collective residences were not included. In addition, although still high, participation rate was lower in the new normal period compared to the early post-lockdown, which may reduce representativeness of the sample. Another limitation was that the epidemiological questionnaire asked specifically about the frequency of outdoor sports, implying our results cannot be directly generalized to overall PA. Particularly, physical activity at home was not considered, and the differences observed could be influenced in part by changes in the place of exercising. Also, it is possible that PA domains other than sport were not taken into account by the participants when answering to this question, and that there was some degree of variability in its interpretation, as no examples or explanations were systematically provided to the participants as to what was considered participating in sports. However, in Spain and Europe in general, unlike other regions such as the USA, the term “sport” does not necessarily imply competition, and usually includes also recreational PA such as walking or hiking [50]. Therefore, the question may have captured most of outdoor PA. In addition, self-reporting is susceptible to recall and reporting bias; however, considering the context where the information was collected, this method was the quickest and most feasible. In addition, single-item measures have been found to be reliable and valid as screening tools to discriminate populations active enough to benefit their health [51, 52]. Also, even though we were able to standardize by many determinants, residual confounding may still be present, either due to measured or unmeasured factors, such as mental health or social support. Likewise, including BMI and chronic diseases in the standardization may have led to some degree of overadjustment, given their potential role as mediators in some of the associations. However, analyses excluding these two variables from the models did not substantially change the results. Finally, the lack of a pre-pandemic outdoor sports measurement and the limited follow-up time did not allow us to show a complete picture of the impact of the pandemic on this outcome.

Conclusions

This study highlights that after easing the restrictions on outdoor activities following a strict home confinement aimed at controlling the transmission of respiratory viruses, the population gradually resumed the practice of outdoor sports, although children, people 65–84 years old and those living in regions with higher viral transmission showed a slower rise. This emphasizes the need for policies that encourage sports and physical activity in these populations in scenarios similar to those of the first COVID-19 pandemic wave. Policies should also promote safe access to sports practice, even during health crisis.

Once in the new normal period, disparities persisted according to several sociodemographic and health status factors. Female sex, age over 85 years, lower education, foreign nationality, comorbidities, disability, obesity, smoking habit, and living with vulnerable people were related to lower participation in outdoor sports. Some contextual factors, mainly a lower census tract relative income, were also related to this outcome. Taking these population characteristics into account when planning strategies to promote sports and physical activity would contribute to improve their effectiveness, and reduce health inequalities.
