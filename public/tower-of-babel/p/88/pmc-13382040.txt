Real-world diagnostic pathways and treatment patterns for suspected lung cancer in Japan: a nationwide claims database analysis
Authors: Y. Matsumoto, Y.E. Hong, H. Shim, M. Shin, H. Furuse, T. Tsuchida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung cancer (LC) poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, particularly regarding accurate and timely diagnosis. This study aimed to assess real-world diagnostic pathways and treatment patterns for suspected LC in Japan, identifying potential inefficiencies and areas for improvement.Materials and methodsA nationwide retrospective analysis was conducted using claims data from ∼28% of Diagnosis Procedure Combination hospitals in Japan, encompassing patients with suspected LC between 2008 and 2023. Diagnostic methods, biopsy utilization, treatment modalities, and intervals from suspicion to diagnosis and treatment initiation were analyzed.ResultsOf the 757 397 patients studied (mean age 66.7 years; 56.0% male), 23.0% (n = 174 006) were confirmed to have LC. Diagnostic processes primarily relied on imaging, with biopsies carried out in only 21.2% of cases. Notably, 118 463 patients received LC diagnoses based solely on imaging findings without pathological confirmation, raising concerns about diagnostic accuracy. Surgical biopsies carried out before LC confirmation showed that 24% of benign conditions were identified. Bronchoscopy was frequently used, with increasing trends observed for advanced techniques like navigational bronchoscopy and cryobiopsy. Treatment initiation was relatively rapid, occurring a median of 14 days after suspicion and 9 days after confirmation. The primary treatments were surgery (35.4%), palliative care (28.1%), radiotherapy (19.6%), and drug therapy (16.8%).ConclusionsSignificant gaps were identified in LC diagnosis in Japan, including overreliance on imaging without sufficient biopsy validation and unnecessary invasive procedures. Improving biopsy utilization and adopting advanced bronchoscopic methods, alongside reconsidering current screening practices, could enhance diagnostic accuracy, patient outcomes, and resource efficiency.

Introduction

Lung cancer (LC) is the most commonly diagnosed malignancy worldwide, accounting for 12.4% of all cancer cases and contributing to an estimated 2.5 million new cases in 2022.1 It remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally, with ∼1.8 million deaths annually. According to cancer statistics in Japan, LC is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths, followed by colorectal and pancreatic cancers.2 Projections for 2024 estimate ∼77 900 LC deaths in Japan. LC mortality is higher in men, with 54 400 deaths (the leading cause of cancer mortality), compared with 23 500 deaths in women (the second leading cause). These data highlight the need to evaluate diagnostic and treatment pathways in Japan.

Non-small-cell LC (NSCLC) accounts for ∼85%-90% of LCs. Population data show that many patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, contributing to poor long-term survival, as symptoms often appear only after the disease has progressed beyond the stage amenable to curative resection. According to a hospital-based cancer registry report from the National Cancer Center Japan, which analyzed LC cases diagnosed between 2014 and 2015, the stage distribution for NSCLC was stage I (42.2%), stage II (8.8%), stage III (16.4%), and stage IV (31.0%).2,3 The corresponding 5-year observed survival rates were 74.6%, 47.7%, 28.2%, and 8.4%. In small-cell LC, the stage distribution further shifted to the advanced stage, and the survival rates were worse across all stages. These findings highlight the need for earlier detection strategies and more effective treatments, particularly for patients with stage III-IV disease, for whom survival outcomes remain poor.

To address these survival disparities, Japan has implemented long-standing population-based screening programs. In 1987, a national chest radiography screening program for individuals aged 40 years and older was introduced, leveraging the infrastructure established through decades of tuberculosis screening. In the 1990s, the scientific basis for this program was further supported by multiple case-control studies conducted in four regions.4 These studies demonstrated a significant reduction in LC mortality, with a pooled odds ratio of 0.56 (95% confidence interval: 0.48-0.65), supporting the continued use of chest radiography as a population-based screening program. Participation rates have steadily increased, with 53.4% of men and 45.6% of women screened by 2019.5 However, this approach remains controversial internationally. Chest radiography screening is not recommended in most international guidelines based on the results of the Prostate, Lung, Colorectal, and Ovarian Cancer Screening Trial, which found no significant reduction in LC mortality.6

Another distinctive feature of the Japanese health care system is the high technological density of its medical facilities. Japan has one of the highest per capita densities of computed tomography (CT) scanners in the world, making access to imaging highly available in both hospital and outpatient settings. This accessibility facilitates a high rate of incidental findings. As a result, LCs are often detected incidentally, even outside of screening programs. These factors are considered to contribute to the early detection of LC in Japan, as reflected in national data showing a higher proportion of cases diagnosed at an early stage5 and in international comparisons indicating relatively favorable survival outcomes.7

Despite these strengths, diagnostic pathways after LC detection remain heterogeneous, and population-level data are limited. To address this gap, we used real-world data (RWD) from a nationwide claims database to examine patient pathways from suspicion to diagnosis and treatment. RWD enables evaluation of real-world clinical practices, including imaging use, biopsy modalities, and treatment initiation across diverse health care settings. As highlighted by Booth et al.8, RWD provides valuable insights into patient care, bridging the gap between controlled trial settings and everyday clinical reality. Therefore, this study aimed to characterize current diagnostic patterns and identify opportunities to improve early detection and timely treatment in Japan.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study used RWD from the Japanese Medical Data Vision (MDV, Tokyo, Japan) database, a nationwide claims database comprising inpatient and outpatient records from acute care hospitals under the Diagnosis Procedure Combination (DPC) system.9 Diagnoses were coded according to International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) and standardized disease codes defined by the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare. The database covers clinical and economic information for around 43 million patients from 495 hospitals in Japan, representing ∼28% of DPC hospitals. Because the dataset was anonymized, institutional review board approval and informed consent were waived.

Study population

Patients registered in the MDV database between April 2008 and September 2023 were included. Patients with suspected LC were identified using ICD-10 codes related to malignant, premalignant, and abnormal pulmonary findings (C34, C78.0, D02.2, D14.3, D38.1, and R91). The UTAGAIFLG column in the MDV database indicates whether each diagnosis is suspected or confirmed, which enables longitudinal tracking from suspicion to confirmation over the study period. To capture the first episode of suspected LC, patients were limited to those who underwent chest imaging and subsequently received a suspected LC diagnosis at or after the time of imaging. Patients with confirmed LC before the initial imaging episode or without relevant diagnostic records were excluded.

Variables

The variables analyzed in this study included patient demographics and health care utilization, including imaging, biopsy, and treatment patterns. Age was defined at the first appearance of the target diseases, and sex was categorized as male or female.

Chest imaging included chest radiography, CT, [18F]2-fluoro-2-deoxy-d-glucose–positron emission tomography (FDG–PET), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). ‘Chest imaging’ was defined as any imaging modality (chest radiography, CT, FDG–PET, and MRI) claimed in conjunction with chest-related diseases (e.g. LC, pneumonia, bronchitis, tuberculosis, pleural effusion, and coronavirus disease 2019).

The ‘diagnosis’ category comprised biopsy and imaging. The biopsy group included intrathoracic biopsy, extrathoracic biopsy, and genetic biomarker testing. Intrathoracic biopsy was defined as tissue sampling within the thoracic cavity targeting lung or mediastinal lesions, including transthoracic needle aspiration/biopsy (TTNA/B), transbronchial biopsy (TBB), TBB with navigation (TBB-navigation), transbronchial cryobiopsy (TBC), endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA), mediastinoscopy, thoracoscopy, open chest biopsy, and thoracentesis. Extrathoracic biopsy included laryngoscopy, bone tissue sampling, malignant skin tumor resection, intracranial tumor extirpation, and endoscopic brain tumor biopsy/removal. Genetic biomarker testing referred to analyses identifying LC driver mutations such as EGFR mutation testing.

Meanwhile, the imaging group consisted of patients who did not undergo biopsy and was further subdivided into surgical biopsy and imaging-only groups according to surgical intervention.

Patients were classified as LC only when ICD-10 code C34 (malignant neoplasm of bronchus and lung) was recorded as a confirmed diagnosis in the UTAGAIFLG column. The date of the first confirmed C34 record was operationally defined as the LC diagnosis date. Because diagnostic procedures and biomarker tests may occur in parallel during workup, some procedures could appear in claims data before this date. Patients without the C34 code were classified as non-LC. Cancer stage at diagnosis was classified according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer 8th edition LC staging system.10

The ‘treatment’ category comprised surgery, radiotherapy, drug therapy, and palliative care. Surgery included lung resection, including lobectomy, segmentectomy, partial resections such as wedge resection and bullectomy, complex resections that could not be categorized into standard surgical types, pneumonectomy, and other thoracic surgeries including chest-wall tumor extirpation, mediastinal dissection, bronchial tumor resection, and bronchoplasty. Drug therapy included cytotoxic chemotherapy, angiogenesis inhibitors, molecularly targeted agents, and immunotherapy. Detailed codes used to identify diagnoses, procedures, and treatments are provided in Supplementary Tables S1-S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100729.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the study population’s characteristics, focusing on numeric trends within the dataset. Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations (SDs), while categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Median values and interquartile ranges (IQRs) were used to represent time intervals. Group comparisons were conducted using chi-square tests, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Imaging utilization

From initial suspicion (n = 757 397) to LC confirmation (n = 174 006), patients underwent a mean of 4.69 (SD 8.36) imaging examinations. Among patients diagnosed through imaging only (n = 118 463), the mean number was 3.30 (SD 5.41). In both groups, chest radiography was the most frequently used modality (mean 2.37, SD 4.19). Among confirmed LC patients, chest radiography (75.2%, n = 130 907) and CT (74.2%, n = 129 033) were the most commonly used modalities. Similar patterns were observed in the imaging-only group, whereas MRI and FDG–PET were used less frequently.

Treatment pathways

Among patients with suspected LC (n = 757 397), 23.0% (n = 174 006) were confirmed as LC, with a median confirmation time of 14 days (IQR 1-97). Overall, stage I was most common (41%, n = 17 020), followed by stage IV (35.9%, n = 14 893) and stage III (15.3%, n = 6360). A higher proportion of patients diagnosed via biopsy had advanced stages, with 37.2% (n = 5290) at stage IV and 18.7% (n = 2653) at stage III. Similar patterns were observed in patients undergoing surgical biopsy, with 37.6% (n = 141) at stage IV and 21.1% (n = 79) at stage III.

Among 174 006 LC patients, 75.6% (n = 131 633) received treatment after LC confirmation, whereas 24.4% (n = 42 373) did not. Untreated patients either died from LC, were lost to follow-up, or had no defined treatment codes. The median time from LC confirmation to initial treatment was 9 days (IQR 2-40).

The treatment pattern was classified using a hierarchical approach—surgery, radiotherapy, drug therapy, and palliative care, in that order—to identify the definitive treatment. Surgery was the most common treatment at 35.4% (n = 46 544), followed by palliative care at 28.1% (n = 37 060), radiotherapy at 19.6% (n = 25 855), and drug therapy at 16.8% (n = 22 174).

Among surgical treatments, lobectomy (64.4%, n = 29 997) was the most dominant procedure, followed by partial resection (20.1%, n = 9348), segmentectomy (13.1%, n = 6080), complex resection (3.7%, n = 1706), and exploratory resection (2.6%, n = 1223). Other thoracic surgeries were rare (<1%). For patients who received drug therapy (n = 22 174), cytotoxic chemotherapy (51.8%, n = 11 491) was dominant, followed by immunotherapy (24.2%, n = 5376).

Discussion

This large-scale RWD-based study provides a comprehensive overview of the diagnostic and treatment patterns among patients with suspected LC in Japan. Prior studies have shown that RWD can complement evidence from trials by clarifying diagnostic performance and revealing care delivery gaps in actual practice.11,12 Our analysis identified several structural strengths within the Japanese health care system, notably the high accessibility to imaging modalities and the relatively short median time from LC confirmation to initial treatment (9 days, IQR 2-40). These factors likely support timely care delivery, particularly for early-stage cases,13 and may contribute to the favorable outcomes observed in Japan compared with other countries.7

Nonetheless, our findings also revealed several challenges that warrant attention within a broad suspected population, encompassing both malignant and nonmalignant pulmonary conditions, including patients who may not have undergone complete diagnostic workup. We identified three key weaknesses in the current diagnostic pathway: (i) heavy reliance on imaging without pathological confirmation; (ii) underutilization of biopsy; and (iii) frequent use of surgical biopsy, even in cases ultimately confirmed as non-LC. A prominent issue identified in this study was that the diagnostic pathway heavily relied on imaging modalities without confirmatory biopsy. Although radiographic tools such as chest radiography and CT play a critical role in detecting pulmonary lesions, our data showed that more than 120 000 patients received LC confirmation without preceding biopsy-related claims.

Among those undergoing surgical biopsy before LC confirmation, 24.4% were subsequently diagnosed with benign disease. This suggested that a substantial number of patients may have been overtreated for benign conditions. Notably, our findings indicated that even lobectomies and more invasive surgical procedures were carried out in some non-LC cases. Previous studies have reported that when surgery was carried out without a confirmed diagnosis, ∼10%-15% of cases ultimately proved to be benign lesions.14, 15, 16 This underscores the importance of obtaining pathological confirmation before surgical intervention.

By contrast, the low biopsy rate may reflect late diagnosis, where biopsy was not feasible due to disease progression or comorbidities. Although the predominance of stage I disease aligns with the high rates of surgery and radiotherapy observed in the database, stage IV was the second most frequent stage at diagnosis. Despite this, the proportion of patients receiving drug therapy was modest, with many patients receiving only palliative care or no treatment. These treatment patterns raise concern that limited access to biopsy may be hindering the delivery of appropriate therapeutic interventions in advanced-stage cases. These observations pointed toward a structural vulnerability in the diagnostic pathway.

Although Japan maintains a population-based screening system using chest radiography, its efficacy in reducing LC mortality remains uncertain.6 Previous studies showed limited sensitivity of radiology for detecting early-stage tumors, particularly peripheral pulmonary lesions (PPLs),17,18 and no robust evidence currently supports its use as an effective screening modality. Therefore, the low biopsy rate may not simply reflect clinical decision making, but rather a broader failure of early detection strategies to enable timely diagnostic workups.

Meanwhile, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) conducted in the United States and Europe have demonstrated that low-dose CT (LDCT) screening in high-risk populations resulted in a measurable stage shift, increasing the proportion of stage I diagnoses while significantly reducing LC-specific mortality.19,20 In the present data, although a substantial number of early-stage cases were diagnosed, a large proportion of patients presented with stage III or IV disease. Along with frequent imaging-based diagnoses without pathological confirmation, this suggests limitations in Japan’s screening infrastructure for timely detection and risk stratification. The absence of a nationwide LDCT program represents a missed opportunity to identify LC at more curable stages and to enable less invasive, guideline-concordant diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.21

Turning to the biopsy modalities carried out, TBB was the most commonly carried out intrathoracic procedure throughout the study period. This dominance is not surprising, given the history of the flexible bronchoscope, which was developed in Japan in the 1960s,22 and the fact that it remains an indispensable tool for visualizing and sampling endobronchial lesions.23 Its utility in diagnosing centrally located lung tumors is well established, and when combined with EBUS-TBNA, it offers high diagnostic accuracy for both primary lesions and nodal staging.23,24

However, since PPLs cannot be visualized via bronchoscopy, the conventional fluoroscopy-guided semi-blind approach has been limited by modest sensitivity, particularly for small nodules.25 To address this, several adjunct technologies such as radial EBUS, electromagnetic navigation, and virtual bronchoscopic navigation have been used since the early 2010s.26 However, recent studies indicate that diagnostic yield has plateaued at ∼70%.27,28 TBC has the potential to further enhance the yield, based on data of its efficacy for endobronchial lesions29 and interstitial lung diseases.30 Since its approval in Japan in 2017, its use has gradually expanded to PPLs, for which its efficacy has been reported.31,32 Reflecting these changes, part of TBB has been gradually replaced by TBB-navigation and TBC.

In parallel, the use of TTNA/B gradually declined over the same period. Its relative share dropped from >20% in 2008 to <15% by 2023. TTNA/B does not rely on existing bronchial anatomy, allowing accurate diagnosis of PPLs that are difficult to access with a transbronchial approach. This was reflected by the increasing use of TTNA/B with subsequent biopsies in patients undergoing multiple procedures. However, the need to penetrate the pleura poses a significant risk of pneumothorax; ∼25.9% of patients experience this complication, and 6.9% require chest tube placement.33 The latest RCT showed that navigation bronchoscopy was noninferior to TTNB in the diagnosis of PPLs (79.0% compared with 73.6%, P = 0.003 for noninferiority),34 which is a notable finding.

Bronchoscopy also offers the advantage of simultaneously performing nodal staging using EBUS-TBNA, a feature that has recently been emphasized. Additionally, the efficacy of advanced robotic-assisted bronchoscopy has been reported,35, 36, 37 and bronchoscopy will increasingly replace TTNA/B in the future. Beyond distinguishing malignant from benign lesions, biopsy remains essential across all stages of LC. Although screening primarily aims to identify early-stage disease amenable to curative treatment, the reality is that a substantial proportion of patients continue to present with locally advanced or advanced disease.38 In such cases, biopsy is not only required for pathological confirmation but also for establishing eligibility for drug therapy.

In the present database, diverse drug regimens were administered, including molecularly targeted agents in 26.9%, which require the identification of actionable driver oncogene aberrations to determine their application. As such, the purpose of biopsy has expanded beyond mere histological classification to ensure the suitability of tissue for comprehensive molecular profiling. This plays an essential role in determining treatment strategies in the era of precision cancer medicine, and securing high-quality tissue samples through appropriate biopsy modalities has become extremely important.39,40

This study has several limitations. Firstly, due to the retrospective design and reliance on administrative claims data, the accuracy and granularity of clinical information were limited because the data were not based on clinical pathology records. In addition, long-term follow-up and out-of-hospital mortality could not be comprehensively assessed, as the MDV database primarily captures in-hospital encounters. Information on cancer stage was also limited, with a few instances of missing data. Cancer stage was captured only at the time of diagnosis and was not tracked for posttreatment outcomes or long-term follow-up. Secondly, although the MDV database covers a large proportion of DPC hospitals in Japan, it does not include non-DPC or primary care institutions. Thirdly, some variables, such as the cause of treatment omission (e.g. patient refusal, frailty, or socioeconomic barriers), could not be assessed from claims data. Lastly, because diagnoses were based on administrative claims data and ICD-10 coding rather than pathology records, some degree of diagnostic misclassification cannot be completely excluded. Future studies should incorporate prospective designs and integrate clinical datasets to better understand patient outcomes.

Conclusions

This nationwide RWD-based study revealed significant gaps in the LC diagnostic pathway in Japan, including overreliance on imaging and underutilization of biopsy. These findings highlight the need for early detection strategies linked to timely and accurate pathological confirmation. Reforms in diagnostic practices and the potential implementation of LDCT screening could improve patient outcomes.
